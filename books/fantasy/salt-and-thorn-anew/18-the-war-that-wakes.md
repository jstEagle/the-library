## 18. The War That Wakes

* * *

The first thing the world did with its returned gods was remember how to hate.

Tomas felt it before he understood it. He was standing in the market square of Duncarrow, a town of gray slate roofs and tired sheep-farmers three days east of the Hollow's edge, when every bell in the village began to ring at once. No hand pulled them. The ropes hung slack and swaying, and the bronze mouths swung open and poured sound into the morning like something exhaling after a long sleep.

The crowd went still. Then—worse—they began to kneel.

Not all of them. But enough. A woman by the well dropped to her knees so fast she cracked them on the cobbles and didn't seem to feel it, her face turned up, tears cutting through the flour dust on her cheeks. "It heard," she whispered. "Oh, saints, it heard me. I prayed last night. I prayed for my boy to come home from the coast, and it *heard*."

Tomas's hand went to his chest, where his sister's mark had faded to a pale silver crescent since they'd fled the Court's hunters. He knew what answered prayer cost. He'd paid for one himself with the memory of his mother singing, and he still caught himself reaching for that song some mornings and finding only a smooth blank place where it used to be.

But these people didn't know. They were kneeling in the dirt with their faces shining, and above the square the air had begun to shimmer, faint as heat off summer stone, and something in that shimmer was *listening*, and it was hungry, and it was grateful, and those two things were, as far as Tomas could tell, exactly the same thing.

"Get up," he said. His voice came out too loud. "All of you—get up, don't answer it, don't let it hear your names—"

A man near him laughed, kind and pitying, the way you laugh at a child afraid of geese. "Boy, the gods are back. The gods are *back*. Whatever comes now is better than what we had."

Tomas looked at the shimmering air, and the shimmering air looked back, and he thought: *that's what everyone said before the last war too.*

He ran to find Vesper.

* * *

Vesper found the war before the war found her.

She was on the high road above the Vale of Serren, where the land folded down into green terraces and an old river ran slow and silver, and from up there she could see the banners.

They were coming out of the ground itself. That was the part that made her stomach turn over—not the armies, not yet, but the way the old sigils rose through the soil like crocuses. Along the riverbank, stones that had been boundary markers for centuries split with a sound like knuckles cracking, and out of each crack unfurled a standard of deep red silk, dry as ash despite the mud, bearing the sun-and-spear of Ossric the Unyielding. A war god. Dead, her grandmother's stories said. Dead for four hundred years.

Except nothing was dead anymore. She had seen to that.

She stood very still on the road with the wind pulling at her cloak, and she counted banners, because counting was better than thinking. Red silk to the south. To the north, past the tree line, a cold blue light pulsed under the horizon like a bruise—the banner-color of the Sisters of the Drowned Moon, whose cult had been fishermen's superstition when Vesper was born and was now, apparently, mustering an army. And far to the west, almost too distant to see, a column of smoke rose straight and black and deliberate, not wildfire but signal-fire, the old kind, the kind lit only when a kingdom meant to call its swords home.

Behind her, on the road, refugees were already moving. Not many yet—a trickle, families with carts, a drover walking beside oxen that kept shying away from the northern sky. They passed her without speaking. One old woman pressed a heel of bread into Vesper's hand as she went by, the way you feed a stray, and Vesper stood holding it long after she was gone.

*I broke the oath,* she thought. *I broke it in front of witnesses, with the right words, for the right reasons, and it was the truest thing I have ever done.*

And the seal had come down like a dam, and everything the founders had held back for six generations had come through the breach all at once, and the first thing divinity had done upon returning to the world was start handing out weapons.

The covenant's voice was quiet in her sword these days—or perhaps quieter, or perhaps she had simply stopped listening, which amounted to the same thing. But as she stood there counting the banners of a war she had unchained, she could have sworn she felt it stir against her hip, patient as sediment, and say nothing at all. It didn't need to speak. The road spoke for it. The banners spoke for it.

*This is what refusal looks like,* it might have said. *This is what mercy costs.*

"Shut up," Vesper told the empty road, and walked down toward the refugees, because someone had to tell them which passes were safe, and she was the only one left who remembered.

* * *

In the drowned city of Mereth-Nua, which had been sinking into its lagoon for a hundred years and worshipping the water that killed it, a godling named Serevain sat on the roof of a flooded cathedral and watched the faithful drown themselves willingly, one by one, into the green water below.

That was the new fashion. Answered prayer required vessels, and vessels required devotion, and devotion, freshly legal again after four centuries of silence, had gone to everyone's head.

Serevain was small, as godlings went—a gutter-flame of a thing, born from a beggar's prayer two winters ago and grown fat and quick since the seal fell. It had offered Vesper protection once, on a rain-dark road, and been refused, and had spent the weeks since telling itself the refusal stung less than it did. Now it watched the great powers carve up the world and felt the peculiar envy of the small: the Thorn Queen had her bramble throne, Ossric had his resurrected host, the Drowned Moon had her willing dead—and Serevain had a cathedral roof and forty-one worshipers, most of them deaf.

Below, another swimmer went under. The water glowed faintly where she sank, accepting her, and somewhere in the deep a voice murmured *yes, yes, I hear you, I will keep your son safe at sea,* and Serevain tasted the prayer on the wind and found it sweet and rotten, sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in the sun.

"They're going to burn it all," it said aloud, to no one, in a voice like a struck bell heard underwater. "They're going to burn it all again, and this time there'll be no Ashgrove women left to bury us."

It thought about the swordspeaker. About the look on her face when she'd refused—the exhaustion of it, the terrible arithmetic behind her eyes. Small gods survive by reading large ones, and mortals too, and Serevain had read Vesper Ashgrove plainly: here is a woman who would rather break the world than wear it.

Which meant, Serevain reasoned, settling its insubstantial haunches on the wet slate, that if anyone was going to walk into the Thorn Court's heart and try to end this, it would be her. And if anyone needed a small, quick, cowardly, indispensable guide through the bramble ways—

Well. Even a gutter-flame could carry a torch.

* * *

Brey heard about the banners in the taproom of her own inn, from a tinker who'd come up the mountain road white-faced and babbling, and she finished pulling the ale before she let herself react, because that was discipline, and discipline was all her grandmother's training had left her that still worked.

Then she set down the jug, walked into the back room, took her old campaign sword down from above the lintel where it had hung gathering rust for thirty years, and carried it out to the yard.

The blade was pitted. Her hands remembered anyway.

She stood in the grey afternoon light with her feet in the stance, and she thought about Ilse Ashgrove—Vesper's grandmother, her own shield-sister, the finest woman Brey had ever failed. They had been young together in the last years of the seal's strength, sworn to the watch none of the valley's children ever knew existed: the vigil of the Hollow, the feeding of the silence. Every Ashgrove woman, dying at thirty, her life poured into the seal like oil into a lamp. Ilse had run from it. Brey had helped her run. And the lamp had burned low for fifty years, and now the lamp was shattered entirely, and the dark outside the glass was full of teeth.

"You'd have known what to do," Brey told the sword. "You always said the oath wasn't the problem. The oath was the bandage. You said somebody would have to heal what was underneath, someday, and it would be worse than any of us could imagine, and we should leave the girl a map instead of a grave."

Ilse had left a map. Brey had burned half of it, drunk, the winter after the funeral, and lied about it ever since.

She lowered the sword. Down the valley, in the direction of the lowlands, the evening sky had taken on a strange color—not sunset, something redder and more organized, like a wound deciding where to open. Horns were sounding out there, faint and rhythmic, the muster-calls of houses that hadn't mustered in living memory. Old grievances were waking up with the gods that had sponsored them: the feud between Carreth and Lowmark over a border drawn in blood four centuries ago; the drowned-moon fisher clans and the inland temples; a hundred little hatreds, banked coals, and now overhead a sky full of hands offering bellows.

Brey slung the rusty sword across her back, went inside, and wrote a note for whoever came looking for her next:

*Gone south after the girl. If the valley burns, it burns behind me, not around her. Tell her—I don't know. Tell her the map isn't lost. Tell her I'm sorry it took a war.*

She hesitated, then added: *Tell her her grandmother never regretted running. Only what the running cost.*

Then she put on her boots and started walking toward the red sky.

* * *

Deep in the Thorn Court, where the palace grew rather than stood and the corridors were tunnels of living blackthorn, the Thorn Queen received the day's harvest.

Her courtiers brought it to her in bowls: prayers, plucked fresh from the reawakened world, still warm. Prayers for victory. Prayers for vengeance. Prayers that the enemy's sons die screaming. The seal's fall had been a famine's end, and the Queen's table groaned, and her hollow-eyed courtiers knelt in rows along the thorn hall with their bowls raised, and none of them could remember their own mothers' faces, and all of them smiled.

"The Unyielding One has chosen a champion," murmured the courtier called Wren, who had once been a man with a name longer than *Wren*. "A butcher's son from Carreth. Seventeen. He pulled the red banner from the earth with his bare hands and it didn't cut him."

"And the Moon?"

"Three champions. They drowned voluntarily. They walk out of the water now. The fishing villages are—" he consulted the bowl, tilting it so the Queen could see the light swimming in it, "—enthusiastic."

The Queen sat on her throne of braided bramble and listened, and beneath the throne, sealed in the heartwood, something vast turned over in its sleep and dreamed of Vesper Ashgrove. The Queen felt the dream move through her roots and did not flinch. She had been cultivating that dream for a generation. Let the other gods squabble over champions and banners and the petty real estate of mortal hatred. The Court had no need of armies. The Court needed only one woman, of the one line, with the one covenant sleeping in her blood—and every day the war spread, the world taught Vesper Ashgrove exactly one lesson, over and over, in burning villages and ringing bells and kneeling crowds:

*Refusal has a body count too.*

When the lesson had been repeated often enough, the Queen was confident, the swordspeaker would come to the bramble throne on her own feet, asking not to fight but to surrender. They always did, in the end. It was the kindest trap ever built: a door marked *mercy*, opening onto a crown.

"Send no emissaries," the Queen said at last, rising, thorns whispering across the floor like applause. "Send nothing. She knows the way."

* * *

Tomas found Vesper at dusk on a hill above a burning granary—the third burning they'd seen in two days, though whether it was war or panic or simple human cruelty newly licensed, nobody could say. She stood watching the fire with her arms wrapped around herself, and for once she didn't look like a swordspeaker or an heir or a vessel. She just looked tired, in the specific way of someone carrying a weight that grows heavier the longer she carries it.

"It's because of me," she said, when he reached her. Not a question. She'd been saying versions of it for days, and each time the words came out flatter, more worn smooth, like a coin rubbed between fingers.

"No," Tomas said, because he'd decided he would say that every time, forever, even if it stopped being true. Especially then.

"Tomas." She turned. Firelight moved on her face. "I broke the seal to free people. I can name the reason—it was a good reason, it was the *only* reason, Maren was dying and the Court was at the ridge and there was no third door. I've checked. I've spent every night since checking, and there was no third door." Her voice cracked, quietly, without drama. "And the world is ringing bells and kneeling in the dirt and lighting signal fires, and every single one of them thinks the gods coming back is a gift, and I'm the only person alive who knows it's a debt, and the interest is *that*—" she pointed at the burning granary, at the tiny figures of farmers beating at flames with sacks, "—and it hasn't even properly started."

Tomas didn't argue. He had learned that much on the road. Instead he sat down on the cold grass, and after a moment she sat too, and they watched the fire together the way you watch anything too big to stop.

"My sister wrote to me," he said finally. "Letter came through a tinker. The mark on her arm—it's changed shape. It's making patterns now. Maps, the tinker said. The mark is drawing maps." He swallowed. "Vesper, I think it's drawing a map to *you*. I think everything is. The banners, the champions, the Queen going quiet—all of it points at you like compass needles."

Vesper was silent a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone very calm, the calm he had learned to fear more than shouting.

"Then there's no third door here either," she said. "There's only one place all these roads lead, and it isn't a battlefield." She stood, and pulled her cloak tight, and looked south, toward the distant smudge on the horizon where the land grew black and thorned even at this distance, like a shadow that had taken root.

"The bramble throne," she said. "I'm done being hunted. If the whole world wants to point at me—if the war wants an address—then I'll give it one. And I'll stand in front of the Queen and every god she keeps, and I will make them listen if it's the last thing my blood does."

Above them, far to the north, a second sun rose briefly—some god's champion, some answered prayer, some horror with a human face—and sank again, and the night went on, and the horns did not stop.

Tomas got up and followed her south.
