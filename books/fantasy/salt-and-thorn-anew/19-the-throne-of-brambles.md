## 19. The Throne of Brambles

They let her walk in.

That was the first thing Vesper understood about the Thorn Court's heart: no gate barred her, no guard moved to stop her, because nothing that entered willingly was ever turned away. The bramble-walls parted before her like a crowd making room for a funeral, thorned branches bowing inward, and the path beneath her feet was not earth but woven root, pale and smooth as bone, breathing faintly with each of her steps.

Tomas had wanted to come. She had left him at the tree line with his sister's mark hidden under a strip of linen and one instruction repeated until he hated the sound of it: *If I'm not back by moonrise, walk east and don't look back.* He had argued. He had cried, briefly, furiously, the way boys cry when they are trying to become men in the same hour. Then he had pressed something into her hand—a river stone, gray and ordinary, from the Hollow's shallows—and said nothing at all.

She carried it now in her left hand. Her sword rode her hip, sheathed, unbound by any oath since the night she broke it. That was the point. She came unarmed in every way that mattered to them, and armed in the only way that mattered to her: she came to speak, and she would not leave until she was heard.

The road into the Court wound upward through country that had once been farmland. She could see it still—the ghost-lines of hedgerows, the sunken lanes where cart wheels had worn the soil, an orchard's worth of apple trees strangled mid-branch by bramble that grew through them like veins through flesh. Here and there stood things that had been people. She did not look closely. Looking closely was how they got their hooks into you; Serevain had taught her that much before his bargain failed, and Brey had taught her the rest over a bottle of terrible wine: *The Court doesn't kill what it wants, girl. It keeps it.*

The throne itself rose out of a hill that might have been a barrow once, or a church, or both—stone crowned with living thorn, black canes thick as her waist climbing its flanks in spirals, each one studded with blossoms that opened white and bled slowly to red as she watched. The air smelled of roses and old blood and rain that never fell. And above it all, on a seat grown rather than built, sat the Thorn Queen.

Vesper had imagined her a hundred times on the road: vast, monstrous, a thing of bark and antler. She was none of that. She looked like a woman who had been beautiful so long that beauty had become a kind of armor—dark-haired, dark-eyed, wearing a gown of layered shadow that might have been silk or might have been the space between trees at midnight. Only her hands betrayed her. They were stained green to the wrist, sap-dark, the hands of someone who gardened.

"Vesper Ashgrove." The Queen's voice was warm. That was the worst of it. It was the voice of a hostess greeting a guest who had finally accepted a long-standing invitation. "You walked three hundred miles to reach me. Sit. You must be tired."

"I'll stand."

"You'll stand, then." No anger. A small smile, indulgent, as if Vesper were a child refusing dessert. "You know, I offered your grandmother this same courtesy. She chose the window instead of the door. It cost her thirty years of running and it bought her nothing but a slower death."

"My grandmother is dead."

"Yes," the Queen said softly. "I know. I keep accounts."

* * *

The court assembled around her without anyone calling it to order. They simply arrived—courtiers stepping out of the bramble-shadow, taking their places along the hillside in tiers, dozens of them, then more than dozens. Vesper counted out of habit, the way Brey had taught her to count soldiers: which ones stood near the Queen, which ones watched the exits, which ones hadn't blinked recently.

It was the last category that made her stomach tighten. There were many in it.

She had seen hollow eyes before—in the capital, in the godling Serevain's retinue, in the faces of worshipers who gave too much. But these were different. These were not emptied by devotion. They were emptied by *use*. Each one wore a collar of braided thorn, fine as jewelry, and each collar pulsed faintly, in rhythm, all together, like a single heart distributed across a hundred bodies.

"You're staring," the Queen said pleasantly.

"What are they?"

"Courtiers. Advisors. Beloved companions." The Queen spread her sap-stained hands. "Every crown needs a court, Vesper. Mine happens to be made of everyone who ever thought they could negotiate with me."

A laugh went through the tiers—not a natural laugh, a synchronized one, a hundred mouths opening on the same beat. Vesper felt the hair rise on her arms. Somewhere under the fear, cold and clear, the swordspeech stirred in her blood like a fish turning in deep water. Not speaking yet. Waiting. It had been waiting since she broke the oath, patient as weather.

"I didn't come to negotiate," she said.

"No?"

"I came to be heard. There's a difference. Negotiation means we both want something the other has. I want nothing from you."

"Everyone wants something." The Queen leaned forward, and the light changed, going green-gold as through leaves. "You want your valley safe. You want the boy and his marked little sister to grow old. You want to sleep a whole night without hearing a dead god whisper through your sword. Shall I go on? I've read you like a letter, Ashgrove. Your grandmother's letters, actually. She wrote beautifully when she was frightened."

*Don't take the bait.* Brey's voice in her head, forty years of hard lessons distilled: *Anger is a door. Never open it for someone standing on the other side holding a gift.* Vesper breathed once, slow, and let the anger pass through her like water through a net.

"The covenant dies," she said. "That's why I'm here. Not to give myself to it. Not to give myself to you. To end it. The god stays dead. The heartstone breaks. Everything it holds—all that power, all those answered prayers—it goes back to mortals the way a song goes back to singers: as memory. As story. Nothing anyone can kneel to. Nothing anyone can feed."

The silence that followed had texture. The collared courtiers stopped pulsing, all at once, and the stillness was worse than the laughter.

Then the Queen laughed herself—alone, genuinely, a real human laugh that made everything around it seem more horrible by contrast. "Oh," she said, wiping at her eye with one green-stained finger. "Oh, you wonderful, doomed girl. Do you know how many times I've heard some version of that speech? From kings. From saints. From a woman named Ashgrove, once, standing almost exactly where you're standing, with a sword almost exactly like yours." She gestured, and the tiers leaned in. "Let me tell you what happened to her proposal."

* * *

"She was heard," the Queen said. "That was my promise then, too, and I kept it. We talked for a day and a night, your ancestor and I, back when I was only a hedge-spirit with a talent for listening, before the war made me what I am. And do you know what she concluded?" The Queen rose from her throne, descending the root-steps toward Vesper, unhurried, her shadow-gown trailing like smoke. "She concluded that mortals cannot hold divinity as memory. Because memory becomes longing. Longing becomes worship. Worship becomes hunger. And hunger—" she stopped three paces away, close enough that Vesper could smell the roses on her, sweet over something rotten underneath, "—hunger builds thrones. Always. Everywhere. Every time. I am not the disease, child. I am the scar tissue."

"You feed on answered prayer," Vesper said. "I've seen what that costs. A child's memory, bought for a miracle. Tomas paid his happiest day to save his sister. That's not scar tissue. That's the wound itself, walking around wearing a crown."

"And what will you replace it with?" The Queen's voice sharpened for the first time, and beneath the sharpness Vesper heard something that startled her: exhaustion. Ancient, bottomless tiredness. "Think it through, swordspeaker. Unbind the covenant. Shatter the heartstone. Fine. Power doesn't vanish—it *scatters*. Into every mortal alive. Fragments of godhood lodged in ten million minds, waking as dreams, as rages, as sudden certainty that they alone are chosen. You won't end the age of gods. You'll birth ten thousand of them in a single night, each one convinced it's the true heir." She smiled, thin and terrible. "I will still be here. I am very good at being here. And I will harvest the field you sowed."

"Maybe," Vesper said. "Or maybe people carry a piece of the divine the way they carry grief—as something that makes them deeper, not hungrier. Maybe memory is the only container strong enough to hold power without being devoured by it. Your way has been tried, Queen. For ten thousand years. Look at your court. Look at your orchard. My way hasn't been tried at all."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Around them the collared courtiers had begun to pulse again, faster now, agitated, a hundred hearts beating out of time with anything human.

"You sound like her," the Queen said quietly. "The first Ashgrove. It's a cruel trick of the blood. She stood there with that same stubborn chin and told me that mortals deserved better than masters, even kindly ones. Especially kindly ones." She reached out—one green finger hovering an inch from Vesper's cheek, not touching. "And then the war took her children, and her children's children, and somewhere down the generations her descendants decided that if they couldn't kill the divine, they'd seal it, starve it, wall it up in a valley and call the silence peace. They became jailers, Vesper. You know this. You read the monastery library. So tell me—which of us built the prison? Me, with my thorns? Or your family, with their silence?"

"Both of us," Vesper said. "That's the answer you're fishing for, and I'll say it plainly because I promised myself I'd leave here heard: your way and their way were the same way. Control. Walls. Feeding. The covenant, the Court, the Hollow's seal—all of it one machine with different teeth. I'm not here to defend the jailers. I'm here to tear down the jail."

"And offer yourself instead?" The Queen's eyes glittered. "Because that's the other ending, isn't it? The one the covenant whispers to you at night. The willing death. The heir's blood renewing the god. I could give you that, Ashgrove. A clean martyrdom. A monument. Every prayer answered forever in your name. Isn't that better than this gamble of yours? Better than scattering divinity like seed and hoping the harvest is kinder than the farmer?"

"There it is," Vesper said softly.

"There what is?"

"The third door. You've offered me the crown and you've offered me the grave, and you think those are the only two doors because they're the only two doors *you* can imagine. Power taken or power fed. But I broke my oath, Queen. Do you understand what that means? I am the first Ashgrove in nine generations who owes the covenant nothing. Not my blood, not my silence, not my death. I don't stand here as an heir. I stand here as a woman. And a woman can simply say *no*—to the throne, to the altar, to you—to all of it."

She drew the bone sigil from her breast and held it up between them, and the whole hillside flinched. The brambles recoiled from it like flesh from a brand. In the sigil's pale gleam Vesper saw the Queen's composure crack—not into rage, but into something rawer. Want. Terrible, bottomless want, the look of a starving creature shown bread.

"That belongs to a dead god," the Queen whispered.

"It belongs to no one. That's the point. And in one day, it will belong to pieces." Vesper closed her fist around it. "Unbind the covenant with me. Not surrender—dissolution. Your power included. Walk away from the throne. Be a memory instead of a master. You said it yourself: you're tired. I saw it the moment you complained about the war that made you. Ten thousand years of harvesting, and for what? This hill. These collars. Apples that never ripen."

"Do not—" The Queen's voice cracked like ice.

"—presume to know you? I don't. I just know hunger when I see it, because I've carried one my whole life. The hunger to matter. To be *answered*. And I'm telling you there's a way to put it down." Vesper stepped closer, close enough that the green-stained fingers could have touched her, and did not flinch. "Help me break the heartstone. Stand witness as its equal, not its rival. Let the age of thrones end with both of us walking away from them. Or refuse—and tomorrow I shatter it anyway, with or without you, and you can spend eternity harvesting a field of memories that no longer need you."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high above, a blossom opened white and began, slowly, to bleed.

* * *

When the Queen finally spoke, all the warmth had gone out of her voice, and what remained was older and colder and infinitely more honest.

"You truly believe they'll carry it well. The fragments. The memories. You believe mortals are deep enough."

"I believe they deserve the chance to find out."

"Mm." The Queen descended the last step, so near now that Vesper could see her pulse in her throat—slow, wrong, once every ten breaths. "Then hear my counteroffer, swordspeaker, and weigh it carefully, because I will only make it once. Leave the heartstone whole. Take the boy and the marked child and your broken oath and go home to your valley. Live. Grow old. I will not hunt you. I will not touch the Hollow. In exchange, the covenant remains sealed as it has always been—starving, silent, harmless—and the world goes on as it has gone on, imperfectly, survivably, for ten thousand years. Order. Peace. The devil we have already survived."

"And the ones like Tomas's sister? Born with marks? The prayers that get eaten?"

"Chaff," the Queen said simply. "Every harvest has chaff."

Vesper looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked past her—at the tiers of collared courtiers, at the pulsing throats, at the woman three rows up whose mouth was moving silently, endlessly, shaping words no sound carried, words that might have been a name, might have been a prayer, might have been *help me*, spoken for so long that it had worn smooth as a river stone.

"No," Vesper said.

The word fell into the silence like a blade into still water.

"No," she said again, louder, turning so the whole court could hear her, because she had promised herself she would leave heard. "I did not walk three hundred miles to trade one walled garden for another. I came to end it. Tomorrow, at the heartstone, I end it. Anyone who stands against me chooses the cage over the key, and I will treat them accordingly." She turned back to the Queen. "You asked what I want. Now you've been told. Now you've been *heard*. That was the only thing I came here to take, and I'm taking it with me."

The Queen regarded her for a long, long moment. And then, horribly, gently, she smiled.

"Heard," she agreed. "Yes. You've been heard, Vesper Ashgrove. Everyone heard you." She lifted one green-stained hand, and the entire hillside leaned toward her like grass before wind. "That was always the price of audience in my court. Nothing spoken here is ever forgotten. Nothing witnessed here is ever free." Her smile widened, and the blossoms on the throne began opening all at once, hundreds of them, white bleeding to red, red dripping down black thorn. "You've given my courtiers something they haven't tasted in centuries. Hope. Do you know what hope does to the collared, child? It wakes them up. All of them. At once."

Around the hillside, a hundred collars began to glow.

"And I," the Thorn Queen said, rising to her full height as the shadow-gown unfurled like night falling upward, "have never once let a harvest wake without seeing whether it will bloom—or burn."

The first collar shattered with a sound like a bell breaking, and the court screamed with one throat, and Vesper's hand found her sword hilt as the throne of brambles burst into flower and fire.
