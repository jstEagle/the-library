## 21. Aftermath of Miracles

The morning after the world ended, Tomas woke to birdsong.

That was the first strange thing. He had expected silence—the great hollow silence his grandmother used to describe from the Hollow's oldest stories, the silence that had lived in the valley for three generations. He had expected the sky to feel empty, the way a house feels after a funeral.

Instead, the birds were singing over the ruins of the Thorn Court's bramble-throne, and the light came through the shattered canopy in long gold shafts, and somewhere below him Sera was laughing.

He found her at the edge of the camp, sitting on a fallen pillar with her sleeves rolled up, showing a Thorn Court deserter how to fold a bandage properly. The mark on her wrist—Vesper had called it a seed-mark, the Court had called it a vessel-sigil—had gone quiet and pale as an old scar. But when she laughed, the scar caught the light, and for half a heartbeat Tomas saw something move beneath her skin like a fish under ice.

She wasn't the only one. That was the second strange thing.

* * *

They had all of them changed. Not greatly. Not dangerously, he told himself, though some nights he wasn't sure he believed it. But the people who had gathered in the capital in the weeks after the shattering—the pilgrims, the soldiers who'd laid down their banners mid-march, the godlings' former servants wandering homeless and blinking—all of them carried something now that they hadn't carried before.

Dreams, mostly.

Tomas dreamed of standing on a shore that no sea had ever touched, watching a tide of light come in and go out, and in the dream he understood it completely, the way you understand things in dreams, and when he woke the understanding was gone but the *shape* of it stayed, warm in his chest like a stone kept near a fire. Sera dreamed of a garden where every flower was a name. The innkeeper at the Drowned Bell, a gray-faced man who had served the godling Serevain for twenty years, dreamed every night of being very small and very loved, and woke weeping, and said he wouldn't trade it for anything.

"No more answered prayers," Vesper had said, the day after the heartstone broke, standing in the rain with ash in her hair and blood on her sword-arm that might have been hers or might not have been. "But everyone carries a piece of what was answered. Memory instead of power. Dream instead of demand." She had looked at her own hands as if she didn't quite recognize them. "I don't know if that's better. I only know it's what's left."

Tomas thought about that a lot, walking through the country afterward, because there was nothing else to do but walk and look and try to understand what they had made.

* * *

The villages were the strangest part.

In the old days—three months ago, which felt like another life—a village shrine had been a place of transaction. You brought an offering. You asked. Sometimes something answered, and the answer cost you, though you rarely knew the price until later: a memory, a year, a child's face forgotten by its own mother. Tomas had seen the miracle in the capital, before everything, the one Vesper still didn't talk about—a godling healing a lame girl while the girl's brother stood beside her unable to remember why he was crying.

Now the shrines were just shrines. Stone and moss and old wax. People still came to them, but they came to sit, mostly. To be quiet together. An old woman in Marrowford swept the shrine steps every morning and told Tomas, when he asked why, "Because someone ought to, and I remember how my mother did it."

*Remembered.* That was the word everyone used now, and it meant something different than it used to mean. People remembered things that had never happened to them. Fragments of the dead god, scattered through ten thousand mortal minds like seed thrown from a bursting pod. A shepherd outside Marrowford remembered dying in a war older than any kingdom, and had stopped sleeping alone because the dream came less often if someone held his hand. A baker remembered being worshipped, and said the memory made her gentler with her customers—"It's hard to shout at someone when you know what it's like to be prayed to," she said, laughing, and then stopped laughing, because she could see he understood.

Some grieved. There were grief-cults already, little bands who walked from ruined shrine to ruined shrine leaving offerings no one would take, praying to a silence that would never answer again, and Tomas couldn't hate them for it. He understood. When you've spent your whole life believing the sky is listening, learning that it isn't—that it never truly was, that what listened was something hungry wearing the sky's voice—is a kind of bereavement. The first week in Marrowford, a man drowned himself in the millpond with a prayer still written on a scrap of paper in his pocket. *Please. Please. Please.* Three words, and nothing left in the world to hear them.

Sera cried for that man. Tomas didn't, exactly, but he carried the scrap of paper afterward, folded small, and he didn't know why until much later.

* * *

In Highmoor they found a town celebrating.

That took adjusting. They'd ridden in wary, hands near weapons, expecting the fear that clung to most places like smoke—but Highmoor had strung lanterns across its whole main street and was roasting an entire ox, and when Tomas asked a woman with flour on her arms what the festival was for, she looked at him like he was simple.

"For the quiet," she said. "My sister gave her wedding-day to the river-god when we were girls. Sold it, for rain. She remembers getting married the way you remember a story someone else told you. Smiling in the wrong places. Last month she sat down with her husband and they talked until dawn, putting it back together between them—every piece she could find. It's not the same. But it's *hers*, now. Nobody bought it. Nobody owns it." She shrugged, embarrassed suddenly by her own joy. "So we light lanterns. For the quiet."

Tomas thought of the Hollow, sealed for three generations so that nothing could buy or sell or answer. His whole childhood in that hush, thinking it was a prison. Maybe it had been a rehearsal. Maybe the whole valley had been the world's dress rehearsal for this—the long practice of living without gods, so that when the practice ended, somebody would know the steps.

He wrote that down in the journal Vesper had given him, and then crossed it out, because it sounded too grand, and then wrote it again anyway.

* * *

The Court's remnants troubled the roads for a while—hollow-eyed courtiers who had given so much of themselves to their Queen that the shattering had left them nearly empty, wandering and asking strangers, "Do you have any of me? Do you remember me?" Most towns fed them and sent them gently on. One or two did worse. Tomas tried not to think about those.

And there were the ones who wanted the fragments back. Who had tasted divinity as servants and couldn't bear the loss of it. In the third week, at a crossroads shrine, a woman grabbed Sera's wrist and pressed her thumb against the pale scar and whispered, "Is there any left? Is there any left in you?" and Sera had gone white and pulled away, and that night she wouldn't sleep near the fire, and Tomas sat up with her until dawn while she talked about the vast warm thing that had answered her prayer two years ago, when she was nine and burning with fever.

"It loved me," she said. "That's the worst part. It genuinely loved me, the way you love bread, the way you love a thing you're going to eat." She wiped her face. "I keep waiting to miss it. I keep waiting to want it back. And I don't. Isn't that awful? Everyone else is grieving and I feel *lighter*."

"It's not awful," Tomas said. "The baker in Marrowford said the same thing. Half the people we've met say the same thing." He hesitated. "Maybe that's how you know it was never really yours. The missing."

Sera considered that for a long time, watching the fire. Then she said, "Do you miss anything? From before?"

And Tomas opened his mouth to say *no*, and found that he couldn't answer.

* * *

Because here was the thing he hadn't told anyone—not Sera, not Vesper, especially not Vesper.

When the heartstone broke, when divinity burst apart into ten thousand mortal memories, Tomas had been standing closest of anyone. Closer than Vesper, even; she'd been behind the shield of her own swordspeech, holding the words like a wall between herself and the blast. Tomas had been reaching for her, unshielded, and the wave of breaking godhood had gone *through* him like cold water through cloth.

And in that flood, for one instant, he had known everything. Every fragment. Every scattered memory of the executed god, every stolen year in every stolen life, the whole archive of answered prayer passing through him like a river through a doorway.

Most of it had washed out again. He was left with the shore-dream, the tide of light, the warm shape in his chest. That was ordinary. Thousands of people had that.

But one thing had stayed. One thing had lodged in him like a splinter, and he had recognized it instantly, and he had not known what to do with it, and so he had done nothing, for six weeks, which was cowardice, and he knew it was cowardice.

He remembered his happiest day.

Not the way he'd always remembered it—the way he'd remembered it since he was twelve, since the fever-winter, since the night he'd knelt by Sera's bed and prayed with everything in him and something vast and warm had answered. He had traded for that answer. He'd learned that later, learned it in a bramble-cell with thorns at his throat, bargaining with the Thorn Court: they had shown him the ledger of his own soul, and the entry read *one memory, freely given, the brightest*. He had assumed it was the day Sera recovered. He had assumed the hole in his past was the miracle itself, the moment the fever broke.

It wasn't.

The fragment that lodged in him during the shattering was the memory itself. Returned. Whole. Sitting in his mind now like a lamp relit in a room he'd forgotten he owned.

He was seven years old. It was the last summer before the fever-winter, and their father was still alive, and he had taken them up onto the high pasture at dusk to see the stars come out, and Sera had fallen asleep against their father's shoulder, and their father had sat down in the grass with both his children and told them the story of how he had met their mother, doing all the voices, badly, until Tomas laughed so hard he hiccupped. And their father had looked at him—at *him*, specifically, Sera being asleep—in that particular way parents look, when they think you won't remember, when they are memorizing you—and had said, "You're going to be the one who takes care of her, aren't you. Good. Good boy."

And then the stars, and the grass smell, and his father's heartbeat under Tomas's ear, steady as a drum.

That was what he had paid. That was the price of his sister's life: the last full day of his father alive, the last time his father had ever held him. The godling had taken the brightest thing he had, precisely because it was brightest, because bright things burn well, and Tomas had walked around the hole where it had been for four years, knowing something wonderful was missing without knowing what.

Now he had it back. The covenant, shattering, had returned what it had taken—or perhaps the shattering simply dissolved all debts, all ledgers, all prices, everywhere, at once. He didn't know. Vesper might know. Vesper understood the mechanics of it better than anyone now, though she claimed she understood almost nothing.

He could tell her. She would want to know—it was evidence, it mattered, it proved that the shattering had undone the old bargains, that the stolen years and sold days across the whole world might be flowing home to their owners like water finding its level. It was hope, real hope, the first structural hope the aftermath had produced.

But it was also his father's voice saying *good boy*, and it was private, and he was afraid that if he spoke it aloud it would become a fact, a data point, a thing examined, and lose the warmth.

For six weeks he kept it shut behind his teeth.

* * *

They came back to the Hollow in the autumn, following the road east through country turning gold and rust. Vesper rode ahead most days, silent, watchful, still carrying the sword even though the swordspeech was only speech now, only craft, only words in the air that did what words do and nothing more. She was quieter than before the Court. Tomas worried about her. Sera worried about her. Vesper insisted she was fine in a tone that convinced no one.

On the last ridge above the valley, they stopped, because the view demanded stopping.

The Hollow lay below in its bowl of hills, and it was open—you could see it plainly, the roads running down into it unbroken, no haze, no hush, the little town with its chimney-smoke rising straight in the still air. Fields being harvested. Children, actual children, running along the ridgeline path where Tomas had been taught his whole childhood never to go.

"First time I've seen it from up here," Sera said softly. "It's beautiful. Why did we ever think it was grim?"

"Because it was hiding," Tomas said. "You can always tell, afterward. Hiding looks like grimness from the inside."

Vesper said nothing. She was looking at the graveyards, he realized. At the small dark shapes of the cairns along the eastern slope, where the Ashgroves lay, where her grandmother lay. Her hand rested on the pommel of her sword, not gripping. Resting. Like touching an old dog.

"We'll go down tomorrow," she said finally. "Tonight I want to sit here a while."

So they sat. Sera dozed against her pack. The sun went down over the valley that had forgotten how to forget, and the first stars came out, and Tomas watched them and felt the lamp-lit room in his mind, warm, patient, waiting.

He decided.

"Sera," he said quietly, when she stirred. "Do you remember Dad? Really remember? Not stories."

Her breath caught. "Some," she whispered. "His hands. Him singing off-key. Why?"

"I remember more," Tomas said. His throat was tight. "Since the shattering. I got something back. Something I paid, a long time ago, without knowing what it was." He looked at the valley, at the lights coming on in the windows below, one by one, each one a family, each one maybe carrying its own returned fragment, its own forgiven debt, its own small resurrection. "And I think—I think maybe everyone did. I think maybe everything they took is going home."

Sera reached over and took his hand, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because some things need silence, and for once the silence was the good kind—the kind you choose, the kind that holds you, the kind that has nothing listening inside it except the people you love.

Below them, in the gathering dusk, a lamp flickered in the Ashgrove cottage—the old cottage, the one Vesper had kept shuttered for years. Someone was lighting it. Someone was home early.

And on the road below the ridge, small in the last light, a figure was climbing toward them out of the valley, walking fast, and even at that distance Tomas knew the set of the shoulders, and knew that whatever had made Vesper leave her own vigil and climb toward them in the dark was news she could not wait until morning to tell.
