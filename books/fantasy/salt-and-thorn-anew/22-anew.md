## 22. Anew

The road into the Hollow had no gate anymore.

Vesper stopped where the old boundary stones stood—those gray teeth of granite that had marked the edge of silence for six generations—and found them just stones. Moss on their shoulders. A bird's nest wedged in the crack of one. When she laid her palm against the nearest, she felt nothing but cold rock and the faint pulse of her own blood, which was, she was learning, its own kind of miracle.

Behind her, the world went on being loud. Somewhere south, a village was holding its first festival of dreams, where people traded fragments of the shattered covenant like keepsakes—one woman remembered flying, a man carried the memory of a thousand years of rain. Somewhere east, the Thorn Court's brambles were dying back to honest thorns, and children were already daring each other to touch them.

Here, the valley waited the way it always had: green and patient and smelling of cut hay.

She walked in.

The path down was longer than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply never walked it slowly before. Every other return had been a flight or a retreat—a body carried home, an oath dragging at her heels. This time she let the road take its time, and the road obliged, unspooling through alder shade and past the shepherd terraces where Tomas had once counted sheep and prayed desperate little prayers into a silence that should not have answered.

His sister's mark had faded to a pale crescent on her wrist, barely visible now, more memory than power—which was, Vesper thought, exactly as it should be. The girl wore it like a scar from a fever survived. She'd asked Vesper once, solemn as a magistrate, whether she missed it. Vesper had told her the truth: that she had never had one to miss.

* * *

The graves needed tending. They always had; that part of her inheritance required no covenant at all.

Her grandmother's stone stood in the third row of the Ashgrove plot, beneath the rowan tree that had somehow survived every hard winter the valley could devise. The carving was worn soft now—*SERA ASHGROVE* with no dates, because Serah had refused them, insisting that a woman who fled her duty shouldn't get the dignity of arithmetic. Vesper had argued with that stone for years. Had raged at it, in the raw months after learning what her grandmother had run from, what her running had cost. Thirty years, Brey had said. The Ashgrove women were meant to die at thirty, their blood feeding the seal, and Serah Ashgrove had looked at that arithmetic and chosen instead to live, to marry, to raise a daughter who raised a daughter who—

Who was kneeling in the grass with a bundle of thorn roses and a hand trowel, feeling none of the rage she had budgeted for this moment.

That was the strange thing about peace. It didn't arrive like a victory. It arrived like weather.

"I brought you roses," she said aloud. Her voice sounded odd in the open air—not because the valley was silent anymore, but because it wasn't. Wind moved through the rowan with opinions. A wren scolded her from the wall. Somewhere up-slope, Maren's great-nephew was singing badly while he mended fence, and the song carried all the way down to the graves, unbroken by any seal, unhidden by any hush. "Thorn roses. I know. You'd have said they're sentimental."

She dug the holes herself. Six of them, along the south face of the stone where the morning sun would find them first. The roots went in dark and unwilling, the way rose roots do, and she pressed the soil down with her palms until her hands were brown to the wrist.

The thorn roses of the Hollow had always been a contradiction—the Court's flower growing in sanctuary soil—but Vesper had come to think of them differently now. Everything beautiful had a defense built in. That wasn't corruption. That was just being alive in a world with teeth.

"They're blooming wild all over the ridge," she told the stone. "Since the Court fell back. Serevain swears they're harmless now, but Serevain swears a lot of things." She sat back on her heels. "I met your friend Brey's daughter last month. Did you know she kept your letters? Forty years of letters, and you never once told her what you were running from. I used to think that was cowardice." She brushed dirt from her knee. "I don't anymore. It takes a particular courage to carry something alone so no one else has to hold it. Wrong, maybe. But brave."

The wren sang. The bad singer hit a note that made dogs bark two terraces over.

"You'd have hated all of it," Vesper said. "The war waking. The Court. Me breaking the oath." She smiled, small and crooked. "You'd have hated it, and then you'd have done exactly what I did, because we were always the same woman separated by sixty years and different lies."

She stayed until the light went long and gold, and then she walked home through grass wet with evening, and no god watched her, and she found that she did not mind.

* * *

They came in spring, the students.

Not many, at first. A letter arrived via the new post-road—the Hollow's first, its riders still so proud of themselves they polished their buttons daily—from a swordmaster in the river country who had heard rumors of a woman who spoke to blades. He wanted to learn. He wanted, his letter admitted in a postscript, to know if the stories were true that she could teach it.

She wrote back three drafts. In the first she said no. In the second she said the swordspeech was dead, buried with the covenant, dissolved into ten thousand mortal memories on the day she drove the sigil into the heartstone. Both of those were true, or nearly true.

But the third draft was truest: *Come at midsummer. Bring your own blade and no expectations. What remains of the speech is craft now, not power. It will not answer prayers. It will only answer work.*

He came. And after him came others, trickling in along roads that had been sealed for generations—roads that now stood open, the boundary stones just stones, the valley breathing in and out with the rest of the world for the first time since its founders had held it shut like a fist around a secret.

Maren watched them arrive from her chair in the sun outside the meeting house, ancient and unimpressed. "You'll ruin them," she said. "Teaching young people to talk to metal."

"The speech doesn't talk to metal anymore," Vesper said. "It talks to the body. Balance. Breath. Where the edge wants to go." She settled onto the bench beside the old woman. "It's just fencing, mostly. Fencing with good manners."

"Mm." Maren chewed on this, and on the stem of grass she'd been worrying all afternoon. "And when one of them is clever enough to make it more than fencing? When some bright-eyed child finds the old words still sleeping in the shape of a cut?"

"Then I'll teach her what I learned too late." Vesper watched the students in the yard below—six of them now, drilling footwork in the dust, laughing when they stumbled. "That power is a door, and doors don't care what walks through them. That oaths are how gods eat. That the most dangerous prayer is the one you make without knowing." She paused. "And that refusing a crown isn't the same as refusing responsibility. I got those backwards for a long time."

Maren snorted. "Your grandmother would have said it plainer."

"My grandmother said everything plainer. It's why she was terrible at letters and magnificent at leaving."

The old woman laughed—a dry rustle like wind through seed heads—and patted Vesper's knee with a hand like a bundle of twigs. "Stay, then," she said, as if Vesper had asked permission, as if anyone in the Hollow had ever had the authority to grant it. "Stay and ruin them properly."

* * *

Tomas visited at harvest, as he did every year now, arriving with his sister and too much cheese and the particular restlessness of a young man who had helped kill a god and couldn't decide if it should feel bigger than it did.

"It doesn't feel bigger," he confessed that night, sitting on the roof of Vesper's cottage where they'd taken to talking when the house felt too small for their histories. Below them, the valley lay under a fat moon, every field and terrace visible, every window lit. No darkness anywhere that wasn't just night doing its ordinary work. "Killing it. Ending it. Some days I forget it happened. Is that wrong? Ilse remembers every minute of it and I can't even—" He turned the pale crescent on his wrist toward the moon, though there was nothing left to see. "I gave up my happiest memory to buy us out of that bramble pit, and now I can't remember what it was. I just know there's a hole shaped like something wonderful."

Vesper passed him the jug of cider. "I know the hole."

"You do?"

"There's a day I can't find. My ninth summer, maybe my tenth. I know it existed because Brey mentioned it once—I was laughing at something, she said, laughing so hard she had to sit down. I've tried to rebuild it a hundred times. I can't." She drank. "When the covenant broke, everything it had ever taken came loose. Most of it scattered into strangers' dreams. But some pieces—" She shrugged. "Some pieces are still looking for their owners. Serevain says the dreams trade them, hand to hand, person to person, like coins. You can't buy them back. But sometimes they find their way home."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe Serevain believes it, and Serevain has been alive long enough to know what wishing looks like." She nudged his shoulder with hers. "Keep your hole, Tomas. Holes prove we were robbed. Robbed means it mattered."

He was quiet a while. Down in the village, someone was playing a fiddle, badly, joyfully.

"I'm staying through winter this time," he said finally. "Ilse wants to learn the footwork. The real kind, not the farmyard kind." A pause, and then, carefully, the way he asked all his real questions: "Will you stay? After? Everyone asks where you'll go next. The capital wants you. The river country wants you. Half the world wants the woman who wouldn't be a goddess to come tell them how she managed it."

Vesper looked out at the valley—at the lit windows, the singing fences, the ridge where thorn roses bloomed wild and harmless, the graves she tended, the training yard she'd built with her own hands from timber her own valley grew.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said, and heard, in her own voice, no grief at all.

* * *

Winter came, and the students stayed, and the cottage filled with the smell of woodsmoke and linseed oil and the particular sweat of people learning to fail gracefully.

She taught them the way she wished someone had taught her: slowly, honestly, with the whole ugly history laid out on the table beside the whetstones. On the first day of every cohort, she told them everything. The executed god. The jailer line. The seal bought with Ashgrove blood. The oath that fed on devotion. The war she had unleashed trying to end all wars. She showed them the scar on her palm where the sigil had burned, and the place on the ridge where the heartstone's shards had fallen like gray snow, and she watched their faces move through wonder and horror and the dangerous beginning of hunger—and then she taught them footwork until they were too tired to be hungry.

"The speech is gone," she told them, every cohort, every first day. "What's left is a way of moving and a way of paying attention. If you came for power, walk back down the road. If you came for craft, welcome. We start at dawn."

Every year, a few walked back down the road. Every year, more stayed.

Ilse turned out to have a natural gift—not for the old words, which slept on, but for reading a blade's balance the way her brother read weather. By midwinter she could disarm Vesper one bout in five, and her laugh when she did it was so exactly Tomas's laugh that Vesper had to turn away and tighten a strap that didn't need tightening.

One evening in the deep cold, after the others had gone, Ilse lingered in the yard, breath steaming, sword resting point-down in the snow.

"Can I ask you something no one asks?"

"People ask me plenty."

"They ask what it was like. Fighting the Queen. Breaking the oath." The girl's crescent mark caught the lantern light, faint as breath on glass. "Nobody asks what it's like now. After. Being ordinary again."

Vesper considered the question honestly, because the girl had earned honesty. "Quiet," she said at last. "It's quiet in a way I spent my whole life mistaking for safety. Only now the quiet doesn't press. It just... holds." She looked up. The stars over the Hollow were thick and indifferent and beautiful, and no constellation among them was watching her. "I spent thirty years in a prison I thought was a sanctuary, and two years running from a throne I was meant to die on, and now I sharpen swords and plant roses and argue with Maren about fence posts." She smiled. "It's the best thing I've ever done, being nobody's heir."

Ilse nodded slowly, the way young people nod when an answer is larger than they expected. Then she grinned, sudden and bright. "Good. Because Tomas says you're teaching me the seventh form in spring, and I intend to be insufferable about it."

* * *

On the first morning of thaw, Vesper walked out to her grandmother's grave with fresh-cut roses and found the thorn bushes she'd planted six years ago in full riot—grown together, grown wild, grown into a hedge that half-encircled the stone like a green embrace with teeth.

She stood before it a long time.

Then she took out her knife—the plain one, the working knife, not the sword, which hung at her hip these days less as weapon than as habit, as heirloom, as tool—and she cut away the dead wood and the crossing branches, opening the bush to sun and air the way a gardener does, the way a teacher does, the way you tend anything you mean to keep.

"You'd have hated this too," she told the stone conversationally. "Pruning. Letting things grow wild and then shaping them anyway. Choosing, every single season, instead of just obeying." She worked as she talked, thorns catching her sleeves, leaving small bright scratches on her forearms that she'd wear like medals for a week. "But that's the whole inheritance now, isn't it? Not the blood. Not the oath. Just this: choosing. Badly, sometimes. Blindly, often. But choosing."

From the training yard below came the sound of wooden blades and laughter and Ilse shouting corrections at a student two years her senior, and beyond that the whole waking valley—cart wheels, churchless bells rung just for the pleasure of the sound, Maren's great-nephew still singing badly, still undeterred.

Vesper wiped her knife, shouldered her sword, and walked down toward the noise and the light and the work.

The road ahead was just a road. It went wherever she pleased.
