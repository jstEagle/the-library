## 1. The Courier's Last Mile

There are three rules to guiding smugglers through Thornfell, and I've broken all of them at least once.

Rule one: never travel at night. Rule two: never trust a client who pays in advance. Rule three: never stop for anyone on the mountain.

That last one is the reason I'm standing over a dying man with blood on my boots and a war I didn't ask for folded against my ribs.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

The run had started badly, which for Thornfell passes for auspicious. My clients were the Vetch brothers—twin smugglers with the combined intelligence of one moderately clever goat—and their cargo was salt. Actual salt. The border villages had been paying triple for it since autumn, ever since the trade roads through the lowlands closed for reasons nobody would name aloud. Salt isn't glamorous work. It doesn't make you rich or legendary. It keeps you fed, and after six years of exile, fed is the summit of my ambitions.

I'd led them up the Shepherd's Cut by moonrise, because rule one exists to be broken when the alternative is crossing the Cut at noon where court patrols can see you from three ridges away. The night was clear and cold enough to make your teeth file formal complaints. Above us, Thornfell's peaks wore their snow like old men wear grudges—permanently, and with visible satisfaction.

"Vane," said the elder Vetch—I think it was Corren; honestly, telling them apart requires either love or a great deal of drink—"you're sure this way's safe?"

"No," I said.

"But you're leading us down it."

"I'm leading you down it because the other way has soldiers on it, and this way only has weather, wolves, and ghosts. Pick your poison."

He shut up. That's most of what people pay me for, if I'm honest. Not the route knowledge—though I know Thornfell better than the tax rolls know the king's debts—but the permission to be quiet and afraid at the same time without having to pretend otherwise.

We made the high shelf above Hollowmere around midnight, and that's where I heard it: a sound like wet cloth tearing, coming from the scree field below the path.

The Vetches wanted to leave him. I want to be clear about that, because later, when they tell the story in whatever den they drink in, they'll claim they wanted to help. They wanted to leave him. "Could be bait," Corren hissed. "Could be bandits. Could be plague."

"Could be," I agreed, and went down anyway.

Why? Six years of exile teaches you a lot of things, and one of them is exactly what it sounds like when someone dies alone on a mountainside calling for help that isn't coming. I'd heard it once before, from a village I refused to burn, and I'd sworn I wouldn't hear it again. Not because I'm good. Because I'm tired.

* * *

He was lying in a hollow between two boulders, and he'd done something clever before collapsing—he'd dragged himself into the shadow line where moonlight couldn't find him. A courier, by his gear. Light leathers, no armor, a satchel cut open by a blade rather than a fall. Three arrows stood out of him like fence posts in bad soil.

"Help's here," I said, because you say that first, even when it's a lie. Especially then.

His eyes found me. Gray eyes, sharp even now, doing arithmetic on my face—the scar, the exile's cropped hair, the guide's rope coiled at my shoulder. Deciding whether I was worth spending words on.

"Thornfell guide?" His voice came out in pieces.

"Retired. Semi-retired." I knelt and did what I could, which was nothing. The arrows had gone deep, and whoever had loosed them had known anatomy the way priests know scripture. "Who shot you?"

"Court riders. Kell's witch-blades." He laughed, and it cost him. Blood came up with the laugh. "They got the copies. All of them. You're the last copy now."

"I'm not anything of yours, friend. Wrong man. I don't carry messages, I don't carry cargo I can't eat, and I especially don't carry other people's wars."

"You do now." His hand came up—shaking, black-nailed with cold—and pressed something against my chest. Leather. Stiff with old handling and new blood. "The Seams are closing."

I knew the word. Everyone raised near the border knows it, the way children know about the thing under the bed: the Seams, the old passages between our world and the thorn-wilds, the places where the Fae covenant still breathed. My grandmother had sung about them. Songs are where such things belong.

"They're not closing," I said. "They've been quiet for a hundred years. Quiet isn't closed."

"This year they started closing." He gripped my wrist with the last of his strength, and there was a terrible clarity in him, the kind dying men get when they finally have nothing left to protect except the truth. "One by one. Deliberately. Someone at court is cutting them out like—like stones from a ring. And keeping the magic." His breath whistled. "Salt roads closing. Crops failing. Villages forgetting. It's all one wound, Vane. It's all the same knife."

I hadn't told him my name.

I looked down at him, and he looked back with the ghost of a smile. "You're Aldric Vane's son. You have his hands. And his jaw when you're being stubborn." A bubble of blood popped at the corner of his mouth. "He'd have taken the map."

"My father's been dead eleven years, and everything he touched turned to disgrace. Whatever he'd have done is no recommendation."

"He'd have taken it," the courier repeated, as if I hadn't spoken. Maybe to himself. Maybe to some third person standing just behind my shoulder, in whatever country the dying go to early. "Take it to the First Seam. Before the frost moon. There's a covenant to reforge, and the ones who broke it won't fix it. Only—" His hand spasmed. "Only a storm-caller can wake the binding. Only a Vane."

"I can't call storms anymore. That's rather the point of my exile."

"Then you'll die ordinary," he whispered, "in a country going dark. Like the rest of us."

Above us, on the ridge, a horn sounded.

* * *

Court horns have a particular voice. Low, sweet, almost mournful—the kind of sound that makes civilians think of processions and weddings. Smugglers hear it and start calculating distances to cover.

The Vetch brothers were already moving. I could see their lantern bobbing up the switchbacks, fast and cowardly, and I didn't blame them. Rule three existed for exactly this moment.

The courier grabbed my sleeve. "Riders. How many?"

I listened. Hooves on frozen scree, more than one set, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who own the mountain. "Four. Maybe five. They'll reach the shelf in minutes."

"Then they'll find me." He smiled again, and this time it was almost peaceful. "Good. Let them count one body and think it's finished. You—" He pushed the map hard against my sternum, hard enough to hurt. "You take the long way down. Hollowmere pass. The one nobody knows but you."

"How do you know about Hollowmere pass?"

"Aldric drew it. On the map." His eyes were glazing now, going somewhere past me. "Margins. Everything important is in the margins. He always said... he always said the margins were where the truth lived..."

"Hold on," I said, uselessly, the way everyone says it. "Hold on, I'll—"

"There's no holding on," he said gently, as though correcting a child. "There's just the next mile. Take it for me. I ran mine."

The horn sounded again, closer, and beneath it I heard voices—sharp, disciplined, a woman's voice giving orders in the clipped cadence of the court's blade-witches. Serah Kell's people, if the courier was right. Kell herself, maybe. I'd seen her once, years ago, across a courtyard, and I remembered thinking she moved like winter deciding where to fall.

I am not a brave man. I want that on the record. Bravery implies a choice made freely, and everything I did in the next thirty seconds was done because the alternative was worse.

I took the map.

It was lighter than I expected—a tube of oiled leather, stained dark along one seam where blood had soaked through. Warm, still, from his body. I shoved it inside my coat, against my skin, where it sat like a second heartbeat.

"Thank you," I told him.

"Don't thank me," he said. His eyes had fixed on the sky above the ridge, on the stars wheeling over Thornfell, and something in his face loosened into wonder. "Warn me. If it works. If you fix it. Find some way to warn me, so I know it wasn't for nothing."

"I don't even know your name."

"Wren's father," he said, and laughed softly at his own joke, at some private meaning in it. "Just Wren's father. She'll understand."

Then the hooves crested the ridge, and there was no more time for names.

* * *

Hollowmere pass is a crack in the mountain that shouldn't exist. My father found it—or made it; with him it was always unclear—and taught it to me when I was twelve, swearing me to secrecy with the solemnity of a man handing down a kingdom. It drops four hundred feet through the mountain's bones, through chambers where the rock has grown thorn-shaped crystals the color of old wine, and comes out in a ravine the maps all agree is impassable.

The maps, I thought, squeezing into the first throat of stone as torchlight bloomed on the shelf above, might be worth reading tonight.

Behind me, muffled by rock, I heard the riders find the courier. Heard a woman's voice, cold and clear, say: "Search him. Where's the map?" And then, after a pause, sharper: "Then where is it? Nobody climbs out of this scree in the dark. Find me tracks."

They'd find the Vetches' tracks. The Vetches, gods keep them, had left plenty.

I climbed down into the dark with a dead man's errand against my ribs and his blood drying stiff on my gloves, and I was angry—not at the riders, not really, but at the courier, at the sheer presumption of him. Dying on my mountain. Handing me his war like a beggar handing you a baby. *You're the last copy now.* What gave him the right? I had a life. A small one, a shabby one, but it was mine: salt runs and quiet dens and the particular freedom of a man nobody expects anything from.

Exile is a wound, but it's a wound that closes over. He'd torn it open with both hands.

By the time I reached the crystal chamber, my anger had burned down to something quieter and more dangerous, and I stopped, in the wine-dark glow of those impossible stones, and unrolled the map a hand's width, no more, just enough to see.

Roads. Trade roads, marked in faded ink, running to places I'd never charted—running *through* things, actually, through blank spaces on every honest map. And threaded among them, drawn in silver that seemed to hold its own faint light, lines like stitches. Like seams.

And in the margin, in a cramped, slanting hand I would have known anywhere—in the handwriting of the man whose disgrace had named me, whose treason had been the first story I ever learned about myself—

In my father's handwriting, someone had written:

*If the stitches fail, wake the storm.*

I rolled the map closed. I stood in the dark of the mountain my father had shown me, carrying the map my father had written on, bound for a covenant my father had—what? Broken? Kept? Died for?

The crystals threw my shadow long and thin against the wall, and for a moment it looked less like a smuggler and more like a soldier. Like the man I'd been before the border village. Before the refusal. Before the exile.

"The Seams are closing," I said aloud, just to hear how it sounded in my own voice.

It sounded like a war. And I was already carrying it.

I shouldered deeper into the pass, toward the long way down, and behind me the mountain sealed its silence like a held breath. Somewhere ahead lay Hollowmere, and beyond it the First Seam, and beyond that a frost moon that was running out of patience.

Somewhere behind me, a daughter was about to learn her father was dead.

I didn't know yet that our roads would cross. But the map did. It always had.
