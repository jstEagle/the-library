## 2. What the Map Refuses to Show

The den at Hollowmere was the kind of place that smelled like it had already survived one fire and was hedging its bets about the next. Wet wool, tallow smoke, and underneath it all the sour tang of cheap spirits cut with something that was probably not poison but only because poison cost more.

I'd been coming to Hollowmere for six years, back when exile still had edges I could feel. The smugglers who ran the den—a fat, cheerful pair of brothers called Ostler and Fen—had never asked what I carried or for whom, which in my experience is the highest form of hospitality a man can offer. They fed me stew of indeterminate meat, gave me a corner near the hearth, and charged me nothing but discretion.

I sat up most of the night with the dead courier's map spread across a table scarred by knives and candle-wax, and I did not touch the stew at all.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about maps before that night: they lie. That's their whole profession. A good map is a beautiful liar—it flattens mountains, straightens rivers, promises you that the world is knowable if only you'll trust the surveyor. I had spent six years learning where Thornfell's maps lied so thoroughly that I could walk the mountains blindfolded and be better guided than any cartographer's darling.

This map lied differently. It didn't pretend the world was simple. It pretended the world was *whole*.

At first glance it looked like an old military survey of the Thornfell range, ink faded to the brown of dried blood—which, given the state of the man who'd carried it, seemed thematically appropriate. Ridges, passes, watercourses, all rendered in a fine, patient hand. But threaded through the mountains were lines no military survey would bother with: thin silver traceries, like veins under skin, running from the peaks down toward the borderlands. Eleven of them. Each marked with a small symbol—a circle bisected by a line—and each annotated in a cramped, slanting script.

Six of the symbols had been struck through.

I sat with my chin on my fists and looked at those struck-through circles for a long time. The courier's words kept replaying in my skull with the stubbornness of a bad song. *The Seams are closing.* He'd said it like a man reporting weather. Like it was already too late to matter, and he was telling me anyway, because someone had to carry the news even if no one would act on it.

And then there were the margins.

Old maps accumulate marginalia the way old men accumulate grievances—one layer at a time, each generation adding to the pile. This one had notes in three different hands, maybe four. Measurements. Corrections. Little arguments between long-dead surveyors, one of whom had written *not a pass, a WOUND* beside the largest of the silver lines, underlined twice, in ink that had gone sharp and black with age.

But the newest annotations—the ones layered over everything, the ones that explained the struck-through circles, the ones that noted dates going back twenty years—those were in a hand I knew.

You have to understand what that did to me. There are certain things you expect never to see again. Your childhood home. Your mother's voice. And your father's handwriting, which is worse than either, because handwriting doesn't die, it just goes on living without him, patient as a spider in the corner of a page.

*The third seam fails from the anchor side,* my father had written, in the same slanted script that had once corrected my schoolwork, that had signed the letter informing me of my banishment, that had filled the ledgers of Vane House back when Vane House meant something. *Reinforcement possible but costly. Recommend we do not tell the court how costly.*

Underneath, in different ink, someone else had replied: *Costly to whom?*

And beneath that, my father again, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly:

*To whoever loves them.*

I read that last line perhaps forty times. I want to say I felt something grand—grief, revelation, the earth tilting. What I actually felt was cold, and then angry, and then a very specific kind of tired that lives in the bones and doesn't respond to sleep. My father had known. Twenty years ago, when I was a boy chasing gulls off the seawall at Vane Harbor, my father had been sitting in lamplight documenting the slow murder of passages between worlds, and he had written *to whoever loves them* and then folded the map away and said nothing to anyone, including his son, including especially his son, who went on to lose everything rather than burn a village that was already dying of exactly this.

Every choice I'd made since the trial—the smuggling, the aliases, the years of being nobody in particular—suddenly looked less like exile and more like inheritance. I hadn't escaped my family's story. I'd just been walking through it slowly enough not to notice the walls.

"Trouble sleeping?" Ostler asked, shuffling past with a lamp. He glanced at the map, and I watched his eyes do the arithmetic of a man deciding whether curiosity was worth the interest rate. He decided it wasn't. "Fen found more wine, if you want it."

"I want to know who else has seen this," I said.

"No one. You came in with it rolled in your shirt and you haven't let it out of arm's reach since." He paused. "That answer worry you?"

It did. Because the courier hadn't come to me by accident. Dying men don't choose their heirs at random—they choose whoever's nearest, or whoever's desperate, or whoever they've been *told* to find. And if someone had told a courier to find Gideon Vane in the Thornfell passes, then somebody knew I existed, knew what I carried in my blood, and wanted the map in my hands specifically.

Which meant the people hunting it weren't hunting paper. They were hunting bloodlines.

* * *

I got maybe two hours of sleep, wedged into the corner with the map folded against my chest like a second heartbeat. I woke to the smell of smoke that wasn't coming from the hearth.

Here is a thing about smugglers' dens: they have more exits than a dishonest man has excuses, and the brothers had drilled me on all of them during my first winter, back when I was green enough to think caution was a personality flaw. So when Fen came through the common room at a dead run, shouting the word *riders* in the tone other men reserve for *fire*, I was moving before my mind finished the sentence.

Not out the front. Not down the cellar tunnel, which every agent of the crown in three counties knows about because some talkative fool sold it five years ago. Out through the smokehouse, along the goat track, up.

Behind me, Hollowmere began to burn. I heard Ostler bellowing, heard the crash of casks, heard a voice—cold, carrying, unhurried—give orders in the clipped cadence of the court. Not soldiers. Soldiers shout. These people *instructed*. I crouched in the bracken above the treeline and watched orange light bloom through the den's windows, and I thought about twenty years of stew and unasked questions, and I added the den to the ledger.

The fire was thorough. Court fires always are. By the time the eastern sky went grey, there was nothing left of Hollowmere but a black toothmark on the hillside and two brothers standing in the road watching their livelihood curl into the air. I couldn't go to them. Going to them would kill them faster than the fire had ruined them. That's the arithmetic of exile—you learn to count the people you can't afford to love, and the list only ever grows.

So I climbed.

There's a pass above Hollowmere that isn't on any map, including mine, because I found it eleven years ago while fleeing something I don't care to remember, and I've told no one, because a secret passage you've shared is just a corridor. It isn't a pass, really. It's a crack in the mountain's face, hidden behind a waterfall that freezes half the year, wide enough for a man sideways, climbing nearly two hundred feet through the dark with the roar of falling water eating every other sound. Local shepherds call the whole ridge the Widow's Teeth. I call it the way out.

Halfway up, wedged in the freezing dark with the map pressed between my body and wet stone, I stopped—not from exhaustion, though there was plenty of that, but because of what the rock felt like under my palm.

Warm. Faintly, impossibly warm, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies. And beneath the warmth, a vibration, low and rhythmic, like something enormous breathing in its sleep.

I knew what it was. God help me, I knew, because the map had shown me: one of the silver lines ran directly beneath this ridge. A Seam. And it was doing what dying things do—flickering. Surging. The mountain's pulse stuttering like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

I put both palms flat against the stone, which was stupid, sentimental, the kind of thing my father would have frowned at, and I felt the Seam flutter against my hands like a bird caught in a barn. Six years of refusing to use the storm in my blood, six years of pretending the gift was a debt I'd stop paying, and here in the dark I reached for it without deciding to—just a thread of it, barely a whisper, the way you'd cup your hands around a match in wind.

The Seam steadied. Just for a moment. Just enough that the warmth under my palms firmed into something almost like gratitude.

Then it guttered again, weaker, and I understood with terrible clarity that whatever I'd done was a man bailing the sea with a cup. The Seams were closing, and they were closing fast, and my father had known twenty years ago, and someone at court was striking the circles off the map one by one like a child killing flies.

I climbed the rest of the way in silence.

Dawn found me on the ridge above the Widow's Teeth, looking south toward the lowlands, where the road to the capital ran flat and exposed and full of people who wanted me dead. The map's remaining open Seams clustered in the far north, around a peak the old annotations called by a name I'd only ever heard in my grandmother's stories: the First Seam. Where it all began. Where, if the oldest note in my father's hand was to be believed, the covenant had been signed.

*We broke it,* the note said. *God forgive us, we broke it first.*

*We.*

My family. My blood. The name I'd been stripped of was stitched through this whole rotten business like thread through cloth, and there was no walking around it anymore, no staying neutral, no being nobody. The war I'd refused to fight had come looking for me twice now—once at a border village, once at a smuggler's den—and it had burned down everything soft in my life both times.

I turned north.

I'd made it perhaps three miles along the high trail, congratulating myself on my solitude, when I heard the sound no lone traveler wants to hear on a mountain path: footsteps behind me, deliberate, unhurried, keeping pace.

I turned with my knife already out.

A girl stood ten paces back on the narrow trail, hood back, dark hair whipping in the wind, a short sword held low and easy in a grip that said she'd used it recently and intended to again. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely dry, which is the worst condition eyes can be in. She looked at me the way you look at a door you intend to break down.

"You're Vane," she said. "The courier was my father."
