## 3. Wren of the Ash Road

My father taught me three things before he died: how to read a trail, how to throw a knife, and how to lie to a magistrate with a straight face. He did not teach me how to grieve, which was careless of him, because it turned out to be the one skill I needed most and the one I had no talent for.

I'd been bad at it for six days. Bad at it on the Ash Road, where he'd bled out in the wheel ruts. Bad at it in the wayfarer's chapel where I'd washed his body and found the thorn-scratch across his palm that no honest courier should carry. And worst of all at it now, crouched in the bracken above the road with a knife in my teeth and murder in my chest, watching Gideon Vane walk into my ambush like a man who had never once in his life looked over his shoulder.

That was the thing about Vane. Everyone said he was the best guide in Thornfell — that he could smell weather three days off and find water under stone. But he walked like a man alone in his own country, unhurried, hands loose at his sides, gray cloak the color of the road itself. Either he hadn't spotted me or he didn't care. Both options offended me.

I gave him another fifty paces. The bracken tickled my chin. Somewhere below, a crow laughed at me, and I decided to take it as encouragement.

Then I stepped out onto the road with my father's second-best knife in my hand and said, "Turn around slowly."

He turned around not slowly at all, which was rude, but he stopped when the point of the blade found the space between his ribs. Up close he was younger than the stories made him — mid-thirties maybe, with storm-gray eyes and a face like a man who'd been arguing with the sky for years and losing politely. He looked at the knife. Then at me. Then back at the knife.

"You're doing this wrong," he said.

"Excuse me?"

"The stance. You're weighted on your back foot. If I step in, you'll be off balance before you finish the thrust." He said it the way a farrier points out a cracked hoof. Not cruel. Just tired, and certain.

"I've killed men from this stance."

"Men who were drunk, or slow, or both." His eyes flicked down the road, then up to the ridge line, cataloguing exits the way other men counted coins. "You're the courier's girl."

The world went very narrow and very red. "You knew him."

"I met him dying on a mountain pass. He put something in my hands and told me the Seams are closing, and then he died while court riders crested the ridge behind us. That's the whole of what I know about your father, and I'd thank you not to stick me while I'm explaining it."

"Liar." My voice cracked on it, which undermined the effect considerably. "He was headed for Lowmark. The only reason anyone goes through Thornfell is if they're running with Vane. You led him somewhere. Something followed. And you walked away."

"Yes," Vane said simply. "I walked away. It's the thing I'm best at."

I lunged.

To his credit — and I hated him for the credit — he didn't draw steel. He caught my wrist, turned with the lunge instead of against it, and suddenly I was facing the way I'd come and his forearm was a bar across my collarbone, not squeezing, just *there*, immovable as a gate. My knife-hand was pinned. My back foot, curse him, was exactly where he'd said it shouldn't be.

"Told you," he murmured.

I stamped his instep, drove my elbow back into whatever part of him was softest, and tore free. We ended up ten feet apart, both breathing hard, both holding knives, and neither of us bleeding yet, which by the arithmetic of the last six days counted as a truce.

"You fight like someone with nothing left to lose," he said. There was something in his face when he said it — not pity. Pity I could have spat at. It was more like recognition, and that was worse.

"My father is dead," I said. "Six days dead. Face-down in the ruts of the road he ran for nineteen years without so much as a sprained ankle. Somebody cut him down and took the satchel off his back and left him for the crows, and every trail leads through Thornfell, and every trail through Thornfell leads to *you*."

"And I'm telling you—"

"Don't tell me. Show me." I advanced a step. "You're carrying something. I watched you favor your left side coming down the switchback. Nobody favors their left side for a knife; they do it for a satchel strap. Give it here, or we find out which of us bleeds first."

Something shifted in his expression — the weary patience draining out of it, leaving something older and rawer underneath. For a long moment I thought he'd refuse, and I honestly don't know what I'd have done. Grief makes a poor tactician. It had already spent six days making terrible decisions on my behalf.

Then Gideon Vane reached inside his coat and drew out a leather roll, and held it out flat on his palm like an offering at a shrine.

"Come and take it," he said. "Since you don't trust my arm's reach."

I didn't trust his arm's reach. I took it anyway, because wanting a thing badly enough has always outranked good sense where I'm concerned.

* * *

I unrolled it on a flat stone at the roadside, weighting the corners with pebbles, and the first thing I felt was relief so violent it nearly buckled my knees — because it was his. I'd know my father's cartography anywhere: the cramped, slanted lettering, the little hatched marks he used for scree, the way he drew rivers like they were nervous. Nineteen years of carrying his maps home folded into squares soft as cloth. This one was different, though. This one was drawn on vellum gone amber with age, and the territory it charted wasn't any territory I knew.

It showed the mountains, but beneath them, in ink that shifted color when the light moved wrong, it showed something else. Passages. Threads running under the world like veins under skin, stitched between our realm and a border rendered in thorns. Someone had labeled them in a hand older than my grandfather: *Seam*. Dozens of them. And most of them had been crossed out.

Not erased. Crossed out. Deliberately, methodically, in fresh red ink, the way a merchant strikes debts from a ledger.

"He was carrying this?" I asked. My voice came out strange.

"He was carrying it and bleeding on it. Most of these stains are his." Vane crouched opposite me across the stone, keeping his distance, keeping his hands visible. A man who knows how he looks to a grieving woman with a knife. "There's writing in the margins. I can't make half of it."

I could.

Because the marginal hand — the annotations, the corrections, the small fierce notes in the corners, *this seam lies, measure again* and *the third passage floods at thaw* — that hand I'd grown up watching draft letters at our kitchen table. I'd learned my letters copying it. I'd know it blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of a well.

"That's his seal," I said, touching the wax stub still affixed to the corner, pressed with the signet of a thorn split by a road — the mark of the Wardens of the Ways, the order everyone said had died out two generations ago. My father had kept a ring in a box under the floorboards and told me never to ask about it. "And this—" my finger hovered over the margin notes, and I couldn't say it, so I said it anyway, "—this is his handwriting. Older. Younger. But his."

Vane went very still. It was the stillness of a man watching a door he'd nailed shut begin to open on its own.

"Your father was a Warden," he said quietly.

"My father was a *courier*."

"Couriers carry things. Wardens keep them." He sat back on his heels. "Whoever wrote those margins understood the Seams. Understood them well enough to correct the map. That's not a courier's knowledge, girl. That's covenant-keeping knowledge."

I wanted to throw the accusation back at him. I wanted to say *you're lying, you're twisting it, you killed him.* But the handwriting sat there on the vellum, patient as a confession, and grief — which had spent six days lying to me about everything else — could not make itself lie about that.

"So he knew," I said. The words tasted like ash. "He knew the Seams were failing. He knew someone was closing them. And instead of telling me, instead of telling *anyone*, he strapped a death-warrant to his back and walked into the mountains alone."

"Maybe he was going to tell someone. Maybe the someone he chose was me." Vane's jaw tightened. "Or maybe he was going to tell the person he trusted least, because that person was the only one who'd believe him."

"And that's you."

"That's me." He almost smiled, and there was no humor in it at all. "Exiled storm-caller. Traitor to the crown. The man who wouldn't burn a village, so they burned his name instead. If my own king told me the sun rose in the west, I'd check twice. Your father may have reasoned the same way."

I looked at him for a long time. The crow had come down to sit on a fence post, watching us with one bright eye, and the wind moved through the bracken with a sound like whispering, and somewhere south of us the court riders were presumably still hunting the map that lay open between a murderer-that-wasn't and a daughter who'd meant to kill him an hour ago.

Here is what nobody tells you about hatred: it's heavy. Six days of it had worn grooves in me like a cart in mud, and every hour it got deeper, and I was so tired, so bone-deep tired, of pulling against it. And here was a man offering me something better than vengeance. Offering me the truth, or at least the road to it, marked in my father's own hand.

"What's your route?" I asked.

He blinked. "What?"

"From here. Where does the map go? What's the next Seam?"

"Why?"

"Because you're going to walk it," I said, "and I'm coming with you, and I need to know how many knives to pack."

"No." He said it fast, reflexive, the way a man says no to a dog that wants to follow him into winter. "This isn't a road for—"

"For what? Say it. Finish the sentence and lose the fingers." I let him see my teeth. "You don't get to decide that. My father died carrying this. Whatever debt he owed and whoever he owed it to, I'm the collateral now. You want to be rid of me, you'll have to do what the court couldn't."

"I exiled myself precisely to avoid dragging people into wars," Vane said.

"Then you're bad at it. Look around. You've got one following you whether you like it or not."

We stared at each other across the map, across the crossed-out seams, across six days of dead men. And I saw the moment he lost — saw it arrive in his eyes like weather, resignation rolling in off some private sea. He rubbed his face with both hands and muttered something that might have been a prayer to a god who'd stopped taking his calls.

"There's a village," he said finally. "East of here, past the ford. People who remember me kindly, which makes them the last three people in the realm who do. We shelter there tonight. Tomorrow we start east." He stood, and rolled the map with more care than he'd shown anything else all day, and held it out to me.

I stared at it. "You're giving it to me?"

"It's yours by blood. And if the court takes me, they'll search me first and ask questions never." His mouth twisted. "Besides. You watch a road better than I expected. My father would have liked you."

"You knew his father too, then."

For just a heartbeat, Gideon Vane looked like a man standing at the edge of a long fall. "Everyone in Thornfell knew Aldous Vane," he said. "That's rather the problem."

He shouldered his pack and started east, and after a moment I followed, because the map was warm against my ribs and my father's handwriting was in it, and because the man walking ahead of me had just lied to me about something, and I intended to find out what.

Behind us, unnoticed by either of us, the crow lifted from its fence post and flew south — toward the lowlands, toward the court, toward whoever paid birds to watch roads.

But that, of course, I didn't know yet.
