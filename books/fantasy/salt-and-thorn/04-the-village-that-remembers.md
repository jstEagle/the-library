## 4. The Village That Remembers

The village was called Hollowmere, and it had every right to hate me.

I'd stood on its ridge six years ago with a company of the king's storm-callers at my back and an order in my pocket that read, in the Chancellor's own tidy hand: *Reduce the settlement. No survivors required.* I had refused. The court had stripped my name, my rank, and my right to sleep indoors within a week. Hollowmere had gotten to keep its roofs, its well, and its grudge against me for being the reason soldiers ever looked at it twice.

So naturally, when Wren and I needed shelter from a sleet that could flay paint off a fence, Hollowmere was where my feet took us.

"They'll spit on you," Wren said as we came down the hill.

"They'll be too busy spitting on the weather to aim properly."

She was quieter than she'd been on the Ash Road. Three days of walking beside me had done something to her grief—not softened it, nothing softens grief, but bent it into something she could carry without it cutting her legs out from under her. She still checked the map's hiding place against her ribs every hour like a woman counting coins she didn't trust the bank to hold. Her father's seal rode there too, wrapped in oilcloth. She hadn't asked me again whether I'd killed him. I think not asking was costing her more than asking would have.

The first person to see us was a boy of maybe ten, standing in a turnip field that should have been sleeping under winter mulch but instead lay bare and gray, the soil cracked like old pottery. He stared at us, then turned and ran toward the village shouting a name.

Not *strangers*. Not *riders*. A name—*Maren! Maren!*—as if we were someone he knew.

"He thinks he recognizes us," Wren said.

"Or he's forgotten who he doesn't recognize." I didn't understand my own words yet. Give it an hour.

* * *

Maren turned out to be the headwoman, a broad-shouldered woman in her fifties with a face like a door that had been slammed a few times but never broken. She met us in the lane outside the meetinghouse with a hay fork held low and ready, and behind her stood perhaps thirty villagers, and here was the thing that stopped my breath: they were all holding things that belonged to people.

A man clutched a child's wooden horse. A woman wore a man's coat two sizes too big. An old fellow cradled a pair of spectacles like a dead bird.

"Vane," Maren said. My name, spat like a pit. "Storm-caller. The one who wouldn't."

"The same," I said. "We need a barn and a fire for one night. We'll pay."

"With what?"

"News worth more than coin." I glanced at the wooden horse, the coat, the spectacles. "Though it looks like you've got troubles money wouldn't touch either."

Her jaw worked. "You don't know anything about our troubles."

"No," I admitted. "But I know about Seams closing, and if your crops died in a single season and your soil's gone to chalk, that's why."

That landed. I watched it land—the little telltale flicker in thirty faces at once, the way a crowd looks when a stranger names the sickness they've been pretending isn't in the house.

Maren lowered the fork an inch. "Inside," she said. "And Vane—if this is some court trick, some way to finish what you wouldn't do yourself—"

"Maren." I kept my voice level. "If I wanted this village dead, I'd have let them do it six years ago and slept soundly after. Ask anyone who remembers me."

Nobody laughed. One or two of them flinched. Fair enough.

* * *

They gave us the meetinghouse because, Maren said, nobody used it anymore. Nobody used it because half the village couldn't remember what it was for. They remembered *that* it mattered—they'd fight you over it—but the word *meetinghouse* sat in their mouths like a stone they'd found rather than a thing they owned.

We sat by the stove while Maren talked, and what she told me rearranged everything I thought I understood about the map pressed against Wren's ribs.

"It started small," she said. "Two winters back. Old Tam forgot his wife's name—called her 'woman' for a day until it came back. We laughed. Then Tam forgot his wife entirely. Not her name. *Her.* Looked at Widow Sorrel across the table and asked when his family was coming home. His family had been dead nine years, but Sorrel—he'd never once forgotten Sorrel before."

"And then?" Wren asked softly.

"Then it spread like rot through a sack of apples. Children forgetting their mothers' faces overnight. Men walking to fields they'd farmed forty years and standing there like lost cattle. And the crops—" Maren's hands closed around each other hard enough to whiten. "Three seasons running, the yield's dropped to nothing. Not blight. Blight you can name, treat, burn out. This is the ground itself going *thin*. Like the world's a cloth and somebody's pulling threads out of it."

Seams. The passages between the human realm and the Fae wilds, the old covenant-roads where magic bled through in a slow, lawful trickle—enough to keep soil fat and memory rooted. I'd grown up on stories of them. Every border family had. And I'd spent six years telling myself the court's hunger for the wilds' magic was somebody else's war.

Somebody was pulling the threads out. Somebody at court, hoarding what leaked through before it could reach the ground.

"How many have you lost?" I asked. "To the forgetting. People who wandered off?"

"Fourteen." Maren said it flat, which is how you say a number that big. "We tie string to their wrists now, write their own names on their palms in ink every morning. It helps. Sometimes." She looked at me, and her eyes were dry in the way that's worse than crying. "You said news worth more than coin, Vane. Is it good news?"

"No," I said. "But it's true, and true is the only kind that can be fixed."

* * *

Wren slept. I didn't. I sat by the stove with the map unrolled across my knees, reading my father's marginal handwriting by tallow-light, and tried to feel what I was supposed to feel.

He'd been dead four years. Disgraced before that, like me—though his disgrace had been quieter, a slow exile of silence rather than a public stripping. I'd assumed he'd simply been weak. Compromised. Now his hand crawled along the map's edges in notes about Seam-stations and covenant-terms, and I couldn't decide if I was looking at a traitor's confession or a prisoner's ledger. Maybe both. Maybe those were always the same document.

Outside, the wind changed, and I heard the thing I'd been dreading since the ridge: hoofbeats. Many. Moving in formation.

I woke Wren with two fingers on her shoulder and had the map folded and hidden before her eyes opened. "Riders," I breathed. "Back door. Go."

She went. I stayed one heartbeat longer than I should have, because through the meetinghouse window I saw the riders dismounting in the square, and at their head a figure in slate-gray leathers with a sword at her hip that drank the lantern-light rather than reflecting it.

Serah Kell. The court's blade-witch. The woman they sent when they wanted something done quietly and completely.

I should have run. Instead—and I want it noted that I am not a brave man, merely a stubborn one—I walked out the front door into the cold, because Serah Kell doesn't chase. If she'd come herself, she wanted to talk, and talking meant the map was worth more than my corpse.

"Vane." She said my name the way Maren had, but colder, like reading an inventory line. "Six years, and you look terrible. Exile agrees with no one."

"You've come a long way to insult my grooming, Kell."

"I've come to make you an offer." Behind her, her riders fanned out with the bored precision of men who'd done this a hundred times. In the doorways around the square, Hollowmere's people watched, string-tied wrists and inked palms, and I hated her a little extra for making them audience to it. "Full pardon. Name restored, lands restored, your father's records sealed clean. You hand over the courier's map and everything you know about the Seams, and you walk back into your old life by spring."

The words did exactly what she'd designed them to do. They slid under my ribs and found the soft places. My name. My father's good name, posthumously laundered. A bed. Walls. A life where nobody looked at me like a loaded weapon left lying in a ditch.

"That's a generous offer," I said, and my voice almost held steady. "What happens to Hollowmere?"

"Nothing."

"Liar."

Her expression didn't change. That was the frightening thing about Serah Kell—she never lied with her face, only with her mouth. "The village is a rounding error, Vane. A few hundred forgetful farmers. The Seams are strategic assets, and assets require management. You of all people should appreciate order over sentiment. You refused to burn this place. Fine. Noble. It's dying anyway—slowly, painlessly, forgettably. You can't save it. But you can save yourself, and your father's memory, and go home."

*Home.* She'd chosen the word deliberately. I felt it work on me like a hook working on a fish, and I was ashamed of how long I let it sit there.

Then I looked past her shoulder. The boy from the turnip field was standing in a doorway, and he was staring at me, and his lips were moving—shaping a name, my name, *Gideon*, which someone must have written on his palm this morning, because I'd never seen him before in my life. He was holding onto it the way you hold onto a rope over a drop.

Six years ago I'd refused to burn this village, and it had cost me everything, and I'd told myself ever since that the price was paid and the account closed. Standing in the square with pardon in reach, I finally understood the account was never closed. Refusal isn't a debt you settle once. It's one you pay every morning you wake up and mean it again.

"No," I said.

Kell tilted her head. "No?"

"No. Keep the pardon. Frame it." I stepped back toward the meetinghouse door. "Your Chancellor is draining these Seams into his own veins, Kell, and you're carrying his bucket. When the last Seam closes, whatever's keeping that sword of yours fed goes with it. Think about whose rounding error you are."

For one instant—just one—something moved behind her eyes. Something that looked less like anger than recognition, the look of a woman hearing her own secret said aloud by a stranger in a market square. Then it sealed over, smooth as ice.

"Kill him," she said, almost gently.

I was already moving. Back through the meetinghouse, out the rear, into the sleet-dark where Wren waited with a horse she'd absolutely stolen and zero remorse about it. Behind us the square erupted—shouts, the ring of steel, a witch-light flaring blue-white against the clouds.

"Hollowmere!" I shouted as we pounded past the first houses. "Cellars! Get everyone underground and stay there!"

Whether they heard, I never learned. The road swallowed us, and the mountain rose ahead black and patient, and somewhere on its flank a Seam was dying that I now had to reach before Kell's riders—or before whatever my father had helped break came apart in my hands entirely.

Wren glanced back once at the burning witch-light fading behind us. "You could have gone home," she said. Not accusing. Almost wondering.

"Yeah," I said. "Turns out I already had one. It just needed saving."

Ahead, faint as breath on glass, the thornline of the Fae wilds shimmered along the ridgeline—and for the first time in six years, it shimmered *wrong*, guttering like a lamp running out of oil.
