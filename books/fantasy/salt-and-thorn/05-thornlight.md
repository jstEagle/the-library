## 5. Thornlight

The Seam looked like a wound that had given up on healing.

I'd imagined it wrong, of course. I'd imagined doors, gates, something with hinges and intention. What I found instead was a place where the air simply stopped agreeing with itself—a shimmer in the hollow between two granite shoulders of the Thornfells, like heat off a forge in midwinter. The trees near it grew at angles that hurt to look at too long, and the moss was the color of old bruises.

"That's it?" I asked.

Gideon stood a few paces back, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man watching someone he disliked walk toward a cliff edge. "That's it."

"It looks sick."

"It is sick." He said it flatly, but something moved behind his eyes—something that wasn't flat at all. "They're all sick. That's rather the point of this whole suicidal expedition you insisted on joining."

I adjusted the strap of my pack and did not look at him. Three days on the Ash Road together had taught me his rhythms: when he was being cruel because he was frightened, when he was being cruel because he enjoyed it (rarely), and when he was being cruel because cruelty was cheaper than honesty. This was the third kind.

"My father crossed here," I said. "This Seam or one like it. He carried messages for people who thought the passages mattered. So they mattered."

"People die for things that don't matter every day, Wren. It's practically the national pastime."

"And yet here you are," I said, "leading me to it."

He didn't have an answer for that, which I'd learned was my favorite way to leave him.

The crossing itself was worse than I'd prepared for. Passing through the Seam felt like walking through a memory of cold—like the idea of winter rather than winter itself—and halfway through, everything flickered. For one heartbeat I stood in the pass under grey mountain light. The next, I was somewhere else entirely, and the somewhere else was *looking* at me.

The Fae wilds were not what the fireside stories promised. No glittering courts, no dancing lights. Just forest—dense, thorned, and *aware*, the way a sleeping cat is aware, all lazy attention and coiled potential. Brambles wove overhead into archways no human hand had trained. The light came down green-gold and thick as honey. And everywhere, everywhere, the thorns.

"Stay on the path," Gideon said quietly, and for once there was no mockery in it. "And whatever speaks to you, don't answer with your name."

"What happens if I do?"

"Then you've given it away. And things out here collect names the way magpies collect silver."

We walked. Or rather, we were *permitted* to walk—I understood that distinction within the first mile, when the path curved gently around us twice and I realized the forest was rearranging itself ahead of us like a host setting a table. Gideon moved through it with a terrible familiarity. He didn't hesitate at forks. He ducked under branches before they lowered. Once, a thorn-vine reached for his sleeve and he flicked it away without looking, the way you'd shoo a fly you'd been annoyed by your whole life.

"You've been here before," I said.

"A long time ago."

"With your father."

His jaw tightened. "With my father."

* * *

The thorn-lord's hall was a hill that had decided to be a castle, or possibly the other way around. Briars climbed its flanks in deliberate spirals, and the entrance was a mouth of woven blackthorn that opened as we approached—not welcomingly. More the way a trap opens.

Inside, the light was green and underwater-slow. The thorn-lord sat—or grew—at the far end of the chamber, and I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe him properly. He was made of the same material as the wilds themselves: bark and bramble and shadow, with eyes like frost on a windowpane, patterns that never quite repeated. When he spoke, the words arrived a half-second after his lips moved, as if language were a courtesy he extended reluctantly.

"Gideon Vane." The name came out tasting like something spoiled. "The storm-caller's whelp returns. Rootless. Giftless. Exiled by his own kind." A sound moved through the chamber that might have been laughter, if laughter had thorns. "How the mighty saplings fall."

"I need passage to the First Seam," Gideon said. No preamble, no bow. I'd have counseled politeness; then again, I'd never negotiated with a walking hedge.

"You need nothing. You *want*. Wanting is cheap here." The frost-eyes slid to me, and I felt them the way you feel a blade laid flat against your skin—not cutting, just reminding. "But the daughter of Aldous Renn comes asking. Now that is interesting. The courier's blood walks into my hall carrying his seal." Something in the pattern of his gaze sharpened. "Tell me, little ash-girl. Do you know what your father carried?"

"Messages," I said. My voice held. I was proud of that.

"Messages." Another rustle of thorn-laughter. "Yes. And do you know who wrote them?"

Gideon shifted beside me. It was a small movement—half a step, weight settling—but in that green slow light I saw it clearly, and I understood suddenly that he had known this conversation was coming. Had perhaps been dreading it since the courier died in the snow with our map pressed bloody into his hands.

"The covenant," the thorn-lord said, drawing the word out like wire, "was not broken by accident. Not worn through by time, not frayed by neglect, whatever your human songs claim. It was *severed*. Clause by clause. Signature by signature. By hands holding human pens." His head tilted, bark creaking. "By hands named Vane."

The silence afterward had texture. I could feel it against my face.

"No," Gideon said.

It wasn't denial. That was the thing I noticed, the thing I would turn over in my mind for days afterward. It wasn't the word you say when a lie strikes you blind. It was quiet. Wounded. The word of a man confirming something he'd already half-known, the way you confirm a diagnosis you've felt growing under your ribs for years.

"My father defended the covenant," Gideon said. "He spent his life—"

"Your father *signed* its death," the thorn-lord said, "and I watched him do it. In this hall. With ink from your world and a steady hand. He was not coerced, storm-whelp. He was not deceived. He bargained. He counted costs, as your kind does, and he chose." The frost-patterns swirled, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Ask him why, if you can find anyone left who remembers. We remember. Memory is the last crop we grow, and it grows thin now that the Seams starve."

I looked at Gideon.

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that moment, because honesty is the only inheritance my father left me. I had built three days of alliance on a simple architecture: Gideon Vane, exiled for conscience, wrongly cast out, a good man in a bad court. The man whose father had been destroyed by the same conspiracy killing mine. Fellow travelers. Shared grief.

And now the ground under that architecture had turned out to be thornland all along.

"You knew," I said. My voice didn't hold this time. "You've known since the den, since you saw the handwriting in the margins. You knew your family was in this and you let me walk in here blind."

"Wren—"

"*Did you know?*"

"Yes." He said it the way men say things that cost them. "Not this. Not exactly this. But I knew the Vane name was written somewhere in the covenant's ending. I found a letter when I was sixteen. I burned it unread because I couldn't—" He stopped. Started again. "Because some truths you can't unburn, and I wasn't ready to be my father's son and know what that meant."

"So you made me ready instead."

"Yes," he said. "I'm sorry."

The thorn-lord watched us with the patience of something that measured time in seasons. "Touching," he said. "Human grief is so wonderfully flammable. But before you burn each other down, little ash-girl, consider: the courier's daughter comes seeking her father's killers, and stands in a hall where she has just learned the killer's son led her here. And still she has not drawn steel." The frost-eyes narrowed. "Why not?"

Because I wanted to. That was the truth of it. My hand had actually moved toward my knife, and I'd had to tell it *wait*, the way you'd restrain a dog. Because if I cut him now, I cut the only road I had to the truth, and my father hadn't died so I could have vengeance. He'd died so someone would finish the delivery.

"Passage," I said to the thorn-lord, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "To the First Seam. You'll give it because my father carried your messages for twenty years, and because whoever is closing the Seams is starving you same as us, and because I'm going to stop them whether or not the man beside me is a traitor's son."

A long stillness. Then the thorn-lord did something I hadn't seen him do: he laughed, truly, and the whole hall shivered with it, leaves cascading from the briar vaults like green snow.

"Oh, I see it now," he said. "Ash and iron. Your father's fire in you, girl, and none of his softness." The frost-patterns fixed on Gideon with something older than hatred. "Very well. Passage granted—for both of you. Not mercy, storm-whelp. Debt. Your father owed my hall a debt he never paid, and I'll collect it from his son's feet walking the right road." He leaned forward, and the shadows leaned with him. "But hear me, both of you. The one who severs the Seams sits close to your human throne, and he does not work alone. There are others who signed. Others whose names would curdle your blood, girl, and yours, boy—names you trust more than each other."

"Names," Gideon said. "You'll give us names?"

"I'll give you a beginning." One branch-hand rose, and from its tip a single thorn detached, black and gleaming, and floated across the chamber to hang before me. "Take the thornlight. It burns only near broken oaths. Where it flares, a signer has passed—or lingers. Follow the burning, and it will lead you to the First Seam and to the hand that holds the knife." The thorn drifted into my palm, cool as a dead man's ring. "And girl—it will flare beside you, too, before the end. All debts do."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

But the hall was already dimming around us, the audience over, the host withdrawing, and the path out unwove itself through the briars ahead of us like a sentence finishing itself.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow the forest seemed to tolerate rather than permit, under stars that were slightly the wrong colors. Gideon sat on the far side of the small fire, which felt right, and necessary, and also unbearable.

"He loved the wilds," Gideon said finally, to the flames. "My father. He used to bring me through when I was small. He'd stand at a Seam and just... listen to it. Like music." His hands were open on his knees, empty. "I keep trying to make it fit. Coercion. Duress. Some court knife at his throat. Because the alternative is that he looked at that music and sold it."

"Maybe there's a reason you haven't found yet," I said, and hated how much it sounded like forgiveness.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Give me credit I haven't earned. You should hate me right now. It would be cleaner."

I turned the thorn over in my fingers. In the firelight it gleamed like a splinter of midnight. "The thorn-lord said it burns near broken oaths. Near signers." I looked up at him across the fire. "So here's my question, Gideon Vane, and think carefully, because I'm tired and armed and grieving. If it flares when you touch it—what am I supposed to do then?"

He met my eyes across the flames. And slowly—so slowly—with two fingers, he reached out and took the thorn from my hand.

It didn't flare.

It didn't even warm.

But as he set it down between us on the flat stone, in the space where both our shadows pooled and mingled in the firelight, the thornlight kindled—soft, unmistakable, burning low and steady at the exact point where his shadow touched mine.

Neither of us said anything for a long time.

Then, far off in the dark of the aware forest, something began to scream—not in fear, but in triumph—and every thorn in sight of our fire turned, all at once, toward the direction of the First Seam.
