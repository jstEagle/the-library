## 6. The Traitor's Ledger

They took me at the ford, which I consider a personal insult. I have crossed that ford forty times with contraband brandy, unlicensed iron, and on one memorable occasion a fugitive tax assessor, and never once been caught. But Serah Kell does not set ambushes the way ordinary people do. Ordinary people hide in ditches. Serah simply stands in the middle of the road, alone, hands folded, as though she has been waiting for you since before you were born and finds your lateness mildly disappointing.

Wren melted into the treeline the moment she saw the white cloak — good instincts, that girl; they'd keep her alive if her temper didn't kill her first — and I was left standing in the shallows like a man caught stealing his own horse.

"Gideon Vane." She said my name the way a physician says the name of a disease. "You've been difficult to find."

"I've been easy to find," I said. "I've just been expensive to catch."

Something moved at the edge of my vision — her shadows, or whatever you'd call them. Serah Kell's blade-witchery isn't the flashy kind. No fire, no lightning. Just darkness that arrives slightly ahead of where it should be, and a sword that seems to already be unsheathed no matter how many times you watch her sheathe it. Three of her riders came out of the trees behind me, which meant there had probably been nine.

"Give me the map," she said, "and I give you your pardon."

I laughed. It surprised us both, I think. "The last pardon I was offered came attached to orders to burn a village. Forgive me if I've developed trust issues regarding court stationery."

"This one's signed by the Chancellor himself."

"Even better. Then it comes pre-corrupted."

Her expression didn't change, but the temperature did. The river mist around her boots went still, the way air goes still around a drawn blade. "You mistake my patience for mercy, Vane. They are different animals."

"Oh, I know exactly what animal your patience is. I've been running from it for two weeks." I held up my hands, palms out, the universal gesture of a man about to do something stupid. "But here's my problem, Serah. If I give you the map, you burn it, the Seams finish closing, and everything green between here and the sea dies of thirst inside five years. And you'll have been the one holding the torch. That's a heavy thing to carry, even for someone who carries shadows for a living."

"The Seams are dying naturally. Decay. Old magic failing—"

"Old magic doesn't fail in a straight line." I'd had two weeks of Wren's furious scholarship and my father's margins to teach me that much. "It fails in tatters, ragged, from the edges in. These are being cut. Clean cuts, Serah. Someone is severing them with intent, and someone is *harvesting* what bleeds out."

For the first time, something flickered across her face. Not doubt. Recognition. Which told me she'd seen the cuts herself, and had been ordered not to look too closely.

* * *

They bound my wrists with cold-iron thread — standard procedure for storm-callers, and deeply unpleasant, like wearing a headache as jewelry — and marched me east through the Thornfell foothills to a waystation I recognized from my smuggling days: an old toll-house built into the bones of some earlier, politer civilization. They put me in the cellar, which had once held wine and now held ledgers, because apparently the court had requisitioned it as a field archive. The irony of imprisoning me in a room full of paperwork was not lost on me. Paperwork has always been the crown's preferred method of execution.

Serah came down alone after dark. No guards. She carried a lamp and a folder of documents, and she sat down across from me on a barrel of what I sincerely hoped was still brandy.

"You're going to talk," she said.

"I'm going to talk. The question is whether you're going to listen, and whether listening is survivable for you."

She opened the folder. "Convince me."

So I did what any sensible traitor does when chained in a cellar: I taught a history lesson.

"Your Chancellor — Lord Aldous Veyra, keeper of the seals, master of the treasury, the man whose portrait hasn't aged in thirty years, which the court calls 'good breeding'—" I watched her face carefully here, and saw the flicker again, faster this time. "He's been sending survey teams into the Thornfell for decades. Officially: mapping mineral rights. Unofficially: finding the Seams. And he found something in the old covenant records that nobody else bothered to read closely. The Seams don't just connect realms. They *generate*. Magic pools along them like water along a millrace. Enough power, flowing past, to keep a man young forever — if you dam the millrace and drink from the reservoir."

"That's superstition."

"It's arithmetic. Ask yourself why the border villages' crops fail while the Chancellor's estates have never seen a blight. Ask yourself why the wilds are starving and the capital's sorcerers have never been stronger. Ask yourself—" and here I leaned forward as far as the iron thread allowed, "—why *you*, Serah Kell, haven't been sick a day in six years, despite taking wounds that should have killed you twice."

The lamp flame bent toward her, as if leaning in to hear the answer itself.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, in the voice of a woman who knew precisely what I meant and had known for some time and had made a series of small, reasonable compromises, each one defensible, each one a step down a staircase.

"Your oath-bond. Blade-witches swear their lives to the crown, and the crown sustains them. Where do you imagine that sustaining power comes from? It comes from somewhere, Serah. Power always comes from somewhere. And lately it's been coming from dying Seams and forgotten villages and a courier named Aldric Wren who figured it out and got a knife in the dark for his trouble."

That landed. I hadn't meant it to land quite so hard — I'd planned to use Wren's father as evidence, not as a weapon — but her hand went to her sword hilt anyway, an old reflex, gripping the one honest thing she owned.

"Aldric Wren was a smuggler."

"He was a *surveyor* first. Twenty years in the Chancellor's service, charting the Seams, until he worked out what the charts were for and ran. His daughter has his seal. She's traveling with me. She thinks I killed him, incidentally, so if you want to arrest someone with genuine motive, I'm afraid you're a day late and emotionally unavailable."

"Why tell me this?" Her voice had gone quiet. Dangerous quiet, or wounded quiet; with blade-witches the two sound identical. "What do you imagine I'll do with it?"

"What you've already done. Nothing. That's the bargain, Serah. You let me walk out of this cellar, and you go on not-knowing what you already know, and in exchange I don't make you *say* it. Because the moment you say it — the moment you write a report, or open your mouth in front of the wrong clerk — you become a problem. And the Chancellor solves problems the way he solved Aldric Wren. You're not my hunter anymore. You're just another prisoner who hasn't noticed the bars. The only difference between us is that I got mine early and learned to live outdoors."

She stood. For a long moment the only sound was the lamp burning and the river muttering to itself through the floorboards above.

Then she drew her sword — and cut the iron thread off my wrists. One stroke, clean, precise. Everything Serah Kell did was clean and precise; it was the mess inside her that had no edges.

"If I find you again," she said, "I will kill you."

"I'd expect nothing less. It's the most honest offer anyone at court has made me in years."

She gathered her folder, paused at the stair, and without turning around said: "The eastern Seam. The one near the salt marshes. It closed three days ago. Before that, it was the healthiest passage left." A beat. "Clean cut. Ragged decay doesn't leave tool marks."

And then she was gone, up the stairs, and I sat alone in a cellar full of dead men's accounts, rubbing my wrists, feeling like a man who had won a duel by convincing his opponent to stab herself instead.

* * *

Wren found me an hour later, dropping through the cellar's coal chute with all the grace of a cat and none of the forgiveness.

"You're alive," she said, disappointed.

"Your faith moves mountains."

She'd picked my locks off the door bolt while I talked — of course she had; the girl could pick a lock with her teeth and a grudge — and now she crouched in front of me, eyes bright and hard in the lamplight. "Kell let you go. Why?"

"Because the truth is heavier than her sword, and she finally felt the weight of it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." I got to my feet, joints complaining. "But it comes with a direction attached. Kell confirmed the eastern Seam was cut deliberately, recently, and professionally. Which means whoever's doing the cutting is working fast now, finishing the job. Which means we're out of time for sneaking and grieving and pointing knives at each other."

"We were never done with the knives."

"We're pausing the knives. There's a difference." I pulled my father's map from my coat and spread it on the barrel — the First Seam marked in the high fells, ringed in handwriting I'd spent two weeks learning to hate and had begun, horribly, to understand. "We move tonight. North, through the pass, to the First Seam. And Wren—"

"What."

"When we get there, you're going to learn what your father died trying to stop. And you're also going to learn what my family started. Both of those things are true at once, and neither one cancels the other out. Can you carry that?"

She looked at the map a long time. At the seal pressed into its corner — her father's mark, beside my father's name.

"I can carry anything," she said, "as long as I get to put it down on someone's head eventually."

"Spoken like a true daughter of the Ash Road." I snuffed the lamp. "Come on, then. We have a covenant to break back open, and I hear the Chancellor throws lovely parties at the end of the world."
