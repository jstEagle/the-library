## 7. Where Loyalty Breaks

I had rehearsed the confrontation eleven times on the road from the thorn-lord's court, and in ten of those rehearsals Gideon Vane died.

In the eleventh he begged, which I found distasteful, so I discarded it.

The problem with rehearsing a murder is that the victim insists on being helpful. We'd made camp in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, out of the wind that came down off Thornfell smelling of iron and old snow, and Gideon was doing what he always did when he thought I wasn't watching: studying the map by firelight, tracing the Seams with one finger like a man reading letters from someone he missed. His father's handwriting crawled along the margins—*the anchor holds, the anchor holds, God forgive me*—and every time his eyes passed over those words, something in his face went carefully, deliberately blank.

That blankness had been eating at me for two days. Ever since the thorn-lord Brambleharrow—or whatever name he wore on Tuesdays—had leaned down out of his crown of blackthorn and told me, in a voice like roots splitting stone:

*"The covenant was not broken by accident, little ash-girl. It was broken by ink and signature. By human hands."*

And then, because thorn-lords are cruel the way weather is cruel, without malice but without mercy either: *"Ask your storm-caller whose hands."*

So I asked. Or rather, I didn't ask, which is its own kind of asking, and Gideon felt it. He'd been feeling it since we left the wilds. Men always know when the arithmetic around them has changed.

"Say it," he said, without looking up from the map.

"There it is," I said. "The famous Vane instinct. Pity you didn't have it when my father was bleeding out on the Ash Road."

He flinched. Good. I wanted the flinch. I wanted all of it—the guilt, the wince, the whole ledger of everything his family had cost mine—laid out where I could count it.

"Wren."

"The thorn-lord knew your father." I stood, because sitting felt too much like trust. "Knew him well enough to grieve him, or to hate him—I couldn't tell which, and I've been trying to decide for two days whether it matters. He said the covenant was broken deliberately. Signed. In ink." My hand went to my sword hilt, and I let it stay there, because honesty demanded it. "Your father's handwriting is all over that map, Gideon. Margins, annotations, corrections. A man doesn't annotate a thing he stumbled into."

Gideon closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were steady, and I hated him for that too, briefly, before I remembered I wasn't supposed to hate him anymore, and then hated myself instead, which was at least familiar ground.

"You read the ledger," he said quietly. "Serah Kell's ledger. The one she let us take."

"I read it." Every page. The Chancellor's beautiful, meticulous accounting of stolen years—Seam-magic siphoned into his veins decade by decade, the court's most faithful servant aging backward while the border villages forgot their children's names. "And do you know what page forty-one says? *Agreement executed per the original instrument.* Original instrument, Gideon. Singular. As in, there was a first betrayal, and everything since has just been housekeeping."

"He signed under duress."

"That's what sons say."

"My father," Gideon said, rising slowly, hands open and empty the way men hold them when they don't want to die, "has been dead for nine years, and I have spent every one of them being punished for his name. So believe me when I tell you I know exactly what sons say. I've said all of it."

"Then show me." I drew the sword. I want to be honest about this, because everyone else in this story gets to be noble, and somebody has to be true: I drew the sword because I needed to. Because if I didn't put steel between us, I would have to put my hands there instead, and I did not yet know what I would do with them. "Show me the original instrument. Show me proof your father broke it first, or prove he didn't. One or the other. Tonight."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, a nightbird made its idiot complaint.

Gideon looked at the blade for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat—not fast, telegraphing every inch—and drew out a folded packet of oilcloth I hadn't known he carried. Of course he carried it. He'd probably carried it since the den burned. Since before the den burned. Since the courier died in his arms, maybe, whispering about Seams and closing doors.

"This is the last page of the covenant," he said. "The original. My father kept it hidden inside a psalter for thirty years, and I found it the week before my trial, and I have never once shown it to another living soul, because showing it means admitting my family's name is worth defending. And I decided it wasn't." His mouth twisted. "Turns out exile makes cowards of us all. Here."

He tossed it to me.

Not handed it—tossed it, an arc of oilcloth over the firelight, trusting me to catch it rather than let it fall into the flames. That trust sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone as I caught the packet and unfolded it.

The paper was old. Old the way things get when they've been loved badly and hidden well. The script across it was formal, ceremonial, the kind of hand taught to clerks and lords' sons both—but I recognized the slant of certain letters, because I'd spent two days staring at margins.

Aldous Vane's signature sat at the bottom.

Above it, in the same hand, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places, ran a paragraph. I read it once. Then again. Then a third time, because grief does funny things to literacy.

*I, Aldous Vane, do set my seal to this severance knowing it to be murder of the world's own making. Let the record show why: the Chancellor holds three hundred souls of Saltmere village hostage behind his riders, and will burn them at dawn should I refuse. I am told my refusal would be noted as treason and my family taken besides. I choose the village. I choose it knowing I damn the border, the wilds, and every soul who ever trusted a Vane. Let my son know, if any son survives me: I did not break the covenant gladly. I broke it the way a man breaks his own arm to slip a rope.*

Saltmere.

I knew the name. Everyone past the Thornfell passes knew the name—it was the village Gideon Vane had refused to burn. The reason for his exile. The court had ordered him to call a storm down on it during the border rebellion, and he had stood on that ridge with lightning coiled in his fists like yarn, and he had said no, and they had stripped his name and cast him out, and the story got told in smugglers' dens and shepherds' huts as the one decent thing a Vane ever did.

His father had signed away the world to save that same village. A generation earlier. Under the same Chancellor's knife.

"It's the same village," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like sound traveling through water. "It's the same *village*."

"Now you understand," Gideon said, "why I couldn't burn it."

I looked at him across the fire. He stood very still, the way people stand when they've decided not to defend themselves, and I realized—with the particular horror of a woman holding a sword she no longer wants—that he had known. He had known since the den, since the map, since long before me, that his father's treachery and his own defiance were the same wound, opening twice. That the court's hatred of the Vanes wasn't spite. It was memory. They remembered what Aldous had done, and they'd been waiting thirty years for the son to prove the blood ran true.

And the son had proven it ran otherwise. And they exiled him anyway, because courts cannot forgive a debt that large; it embarrasses everyone.

"Put the sword down, Wren."

"No." But I lowered it. There's a difference, and we both pretended not to notice. "You could have shown me this days ago. Before the thorn-lord. Before—" I gestured vaguely at the last two days, which contained an ambush, a negotiation with a creature wearing a crown of thorns, and one deeply undignified incident involving a river crossing. "Before I spent two days deciding whether to kill you."

"If I showed you while you were still grieving," he said, "you'd have thought I was buying my life with my father's shame. Maybe you'd have been right to think it." He crouched, fed a stick to the fire, and for a moment the light moved across his face and made him look younger and more tired than any storm-caller had a right to be. "I wanted you to arrive at it yourself. To see the shape of it without me holding it up."

"That's either honesty or manipulation."

"It's usually both. Most honest things are."

I laughed. It escaped before I could stop it—one short, cracked sound—and it did something irreparable to the architecture of my anger. Anger needs a sealed room. It had just gotten a window.

I sat down across the fire from him, sword across my knees, because I'm not a saint, I'm a courier's daughter, and couriers keep their cargo close even after delivery.

"My father carried messages for the court," I said. "Did you know that? Little ones. Sealed ones. He used to joke that if he ever read one, it'd kill him." My throat tightened. "Something he carried must have led him to that map. Something he saw made him run instead of deliver. He died getting the truth to someone who'd use it." I looked up. "I used to think that someone should have been me. Now I think—" I stopped. Started again. "I think vengeance was easier when the enemy was a stranger with your surname."

"And now?"

"Now the enemy is a man who's been hoarding centuries at the expense of every village on the border, and who held a knife to your father's throat a generation before he held one to mine." I turned the sword, watched firelight run along the edge. "Duty says I finish what I came here for. Vengeance says the same thing, actually—they're rarely this agreeable. But duty also says the living matter more than the dead, and there are three hundred souls in Saltmere who only exist because your father chose them over the world, and a daughter in Ashford who exists because mine chose truth over a sealed letter." I met his eyes. "So here's my terms, Vane. I keep the sword. I keep the doubt. And when this is done—if we live, if the Seams open, if any of it matters—you stand in front of whatever's left of my father's grave and tell him why his death bought something. Not before. Not as payment. After. As a promise kept."

Gideon was quiet a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod when they've been given a sentence they intend to serve.

"After," he said. "As a promise kept."

We sat with that. The fire settled. Above the granite shelf, stars came out in the gaps between clouds, sharp and cold and indifferent, and I felt the strangest sensation—not peace, nothing so clean, but a loosening, like a knot untied by someone else's patient fingers.

Then Gideon went rigid.

I knew that rigidity. Every smuggler knows it. It's the body's way of saying *listen* before the mind catches up, and I listened, and heard it: hoofbeats. Many. Coming up the switchback road below us at a pace no patrol uses and no merchant can afford.

"Lanterns?" I whispered, already rolling the covenant page back into its oilcloth.

"No lanterns." Gideon's face had gone grey. "That's the point. Only one rider travels the Thornfell roads dark, because only one rider doesn't need light to find her blade's work."

Serah Kell. Alone, unannounced, at midnight—which meant she wasn't hunting us. Serah Kell hunted with a company and a warrant. This was something else.

She crested the ridge twenty minutes later, leading a horse that staggered, and dismounted before the animal finished falling. In the firelight I saw why she rode dark: her armor was scored nearly through, her left arm hung wrong, and there was blood on her that had dried in layers, hours old.

She looked at Gideon Vane—at the man she'd chased across half a kingdom—and said, in a voice scraped down to wire:

"He knows you have the ledger. And he knows about the First Seam. The ritual moves to dawn tomorrow." She swayed, caught herself on her saddle. "I need to tell you what's actually growing in that garden. And you're going to want to sit down."
