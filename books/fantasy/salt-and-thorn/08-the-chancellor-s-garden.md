## 8. The Chancellor's Garden

There are two ways into a great man's estate: the way he guards, and the way he's ashamed of. Lord Chancellor Aldous Merrivane had a hundred soldiers watching his gates, a dozen blade-witches walking his walls, and one drainage culvert running beneath the east terrace that his surveyors had marked on no map worth stealing.

I know this because my father helped design the drainage. Vane hands built half of Thornfell's waterworks before the crown decided we were more useful broken than building. There's a particular irony in sneaking home through your family's own plumbing, and I made sure to appreciate it fully, up to my knees in cold runoff, with Wren's elbow in my ribs every time I laughed at it.

"Something funny?" she whispered.

"I was just thinking that if my father could see me now, he'd say the masonry was shoddy."

"He'd be right. It's leaking."

"That's what makes it ours."

She didn't laugh, but she didn't stab me either, which by our standards was practically affection. Three days since the ledger, three days since she'd put her knife away instead of between my ribs, and we'd settled into something like trust—the fragile, untested kind, like ice you cross because the long way around is longer.

We came up through the culvert into a hedge maze gone feral, which should have been impossible. Merrivane's groundskeepers were legendary; the man kept roses that bloomed in winter. But these hedges were wrong in a way I felt before I understood—too green, too eager, branches curling toward us as we passed like beggars toward a rich man's coat.

"This is thornwork," Wren breathed. "Fae-grown. On *this* side of the border?"

"It shouldn't be." I touched a leaf and snatched my hand back. It was warm. Growing things on the human side of the Seams were supposed to be quiet things—wheat, oak, honest grass. This was hungry. "The Seams are closing everywhere else. So where is all that magic going?"

I already knew. I'd known since the ledger. I just hadn't let myself picture it.

We followed the hunger upstream.

* * *

The wall around the garden was white marble, veined with something red that pulsed if you looked too long. We went over it at the shadowed corner where a statue of some forgotten ancestor gave cover, and dropped into the most beautiful place I have ever wanted to burn down.

Roses the size of cabbages. Lilies that glowed faintly, like coals under ash. A lawn so deep and soft it swallowed our footsteps whole, and above it all an apple tree heavy with fruit that caught the moonlight and held it, refusing to let go. The air tasted sweet and thick, like honey cut with iron.

And threaded through everything—through the soil, up the stems, along the marble paths—ran veins of light. Pale gold threads, glowing softly, converging somewhere past the tree.

Wren knelt and pressed her palm to the grass. When she lifted it, her hand was trembling.

"Gideon. The ground is *breathing*."

It was. Slow, labored, the rise and fall of something enormous sleeping badly. I'd felt the land breathe once before—at a dying Seam in the Thornfell high country, when Wren and I had crossed into the wilds and I'd felt the wound of it, the slow suffocation of a passage being pinched shut. This was the same breath. The same rhythm.

Just smaller. Contained. *Potted.*

"There's a Seam under here," I said. "A living one. He didn't close this one—he planted it."

We followed the golden veins to their heart, past the apple tree, to a ring of standing stones no human mason had cut—thorn-black, spiraled, older than the estate that had been built around them like a jeweler's setting around a stolen gem. In their center, the light pooled ankle-deep, and where it pooled, the grass grew in patterns no seed had ever made: spirals, whorls, letters in a script that predated the kingdom.

A captive Seam. The last healthy one this side of the mountains, dug up like a tree with its roots intact and transplanted into a lord's garden, so that its magic could feed his roses while every other passage between the worlds withered and died.

And there were people tending it. Gardeners, or what passed for gardeners here—men and women in gray livery moving between the beds with shears, trimming back the light itself where it grew too thick. Their movements were smooth. Too smooth. And their faces, when one turned toward us—

"They're not asleep," Wren whispered. "They're *empty*."

She was right. The eyes were open and nothing lived behind them. Whatever drained lifeforce out of a Seam to make apples glow apparently didn't stop at apples. I thought of Serah Kell, ageless and ash-eyed, sustained by power she never asked where it came from. I thought of the Chancellor's famous vigor—a man of seventy who climbed his own stairs two at a time.

Everything blooms here, I thought. Everything except the people.

"We should go around," Wren said. "Find the house, find his study—"

"No need."

The voice came from the apple tree. From *beneath* it, where a bench sat in the tree's broad shadow, and on the bench sat an old man in plain wool, peeling an apple with a small silver knife. He hadn't been there a moment ago. Or he had, and the garden simply hadn't bothered showing him to us until he wished it.

Lord Chancellor Aldous Merrivane looked up at us with mild, grandfatherly blue eyes, and smiled.

"You're trespassing," he said pleasantly, "but then, so am I. Sit. The apples really are extraordinary. Everything here is."

Behind us, the empty-eyed gardeners set down their shears and turned to face us. They did not approach. They only waited, the way furniture waits.

Wren's knife was out. My storm was rising in my chest, that old familiar pressure behind the breastbone I'd sworn off for seven years—and Merrivane watched it gather with an expression of genuine delight, the way a connoisseur watches a rare vintage being poured.

"Oh, *yes*," he said softly. "That's the Vane gift. I've read about it for decades. Do call it, Master Vane. I promise you, I've grown very good at catching lightning."

* * *

We sat. It seemed wiser than the alternative, and I'll admit to curiosity—I've always been weak to the temptation of hearing a villain explain himself, mostly because they so rarely get the chance.

Merrivane peeled his apple in one long unbroken spiral and offered slices to each of us. Wren refused hers. I took mine, because I'm a smuggler before I'm anything noble, and because it was, infuriatingly, the best thing I've ever eaten. It tasted like summer. It tasted like someone else's life being spent so mine could taste like summer.

"You've seen the ledger," Merrivane said. "Serah tells me you escaped with it, though she was oddly reluctant to discuss the details. Women who let prisoners go are usually wrestling with something. You'll forgive her; she's been in my service since before you were born, and loyalty calcifies." He took a bite, chewed, swallowed. "So you know what I've done. Let me tell you *why*, and then let me make you an offer, and then—you may do whatever you like. I find that arrangements entered freely hold better than arrangements enforced. Ask any gardener."

"The gardeners can't answer," Wren said flatly.

"No," he agreed, without a flicker of shame. "They gave what they had. Willingly, I might add—there are worse retirements than becoming part of something beautiful. The point, Master Vane, is this: the Seams were dying anyway. Slowly, over centuries—the covenant your ancestors signed was always a stay of execution, never a cure. I merely... redirected the flow. Took the water from a hundred failing streams and poured it into one well. One well, kept open, kept *mine*. The wilds lose their passages gradually. Humanity loses its magic gradually. But Aldous Merrivane keeps his garden blooming, keeps his mind sharp, keeps his *years*—" he spread his hands, the silver knife glinting, "—and keeps the door open. One door. Guarded. Rationed. Is that so very different from what kings do with grain?"

"It's different," I said, "because grain doesn't scream."

His smile didn't move, but something behind his eyes did—a flicker, quick as a fish under ice. "You've heard it, then. The Seam. Yes, they sing when they're harvested. Your father heard it too."

The words landed like a hook behind my sternum. "*My father.*"

"He came to me, twenty-two years ago. Brilliant man. Guilty man. He'd helped write the covenant, helped build the mechanisms that closed the first Seams—did you know that? No, of course not; he let you carry the shame of his exile without ever telling you whose design it all was. He came here and begged me to undo it. And I told him what I'll tell you: the door cannot be reopened, only *kept*. He wept. Good man. Wept like a child. Then he went home and wrote you that letter you keep against your ribs, and hanged himself from the beam in his own study, and left you the exile and the guilt and the gift you refuse to use." Merrivane's voice was gentle, horribly gentle. "You've hated the crown for banishing him. Consider hating him instead, for what he built. Or consider," and here he leaned forward, and the garden leaned with him, every glowing vein brightening, the whole breathing landscape attentive, "considering my offer."

"Which is?"

"Give me the map. The courier's map—the last true chart of every Seam, including the First. With it, I can manage the closing cleanly. Humanely. I can spare certain villages, certain bloodlines. Yours, for instance." He said it lightly, offering bread. "Full pardon. Restoration of name. Vane lands restored, Vane honor restored, your father's grave moved from the criminals' row to consecrated ground. You could go *home*, Gideon. Storm-caller, exile, smuggler—none of it need have happened. One map. That's the price. The map, and your silence, and your signature on the new accord, lending your family's name to the closing—as your father lent it to the opening. Symmetry. History loves symmetry."

Home. The word did exactly what he intended. I felt it move through me like liquor—warmth first, then the burn. The farmhouse with the crooked chimney. The study with the beam. The village downriver that still spoke my family's name without spitting. Seven years I'd slept in smugglers' dens and told myself I needed nothing, and here was a soft-voiced old man handing it all back, asking only that I help him strangle the world quietly, in its sleep, village by village, memory by memory, while his roses drank.

Beside me, Wren had gone very still. She wasn't looking at Merrivane. She was looking at me, and her eyes said: *whatever you're weighing, weigh it fast.*

I finished my slice of apple. Set the core carefully on the bench between us, where the golden light in the ground rose up and began, gently, hungrily, to drink it.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "You give me the First Seam's location, release your gardeners' souls, and turn yourself in to whatever court remains to try you. In exchange, I don't call the storm."

Merrivane sighed, genuinely regretful, and stood. Around us the garden exhaled—a long, shuddering breath, and every vein of light flared gold, and the empty-eyed gardeners began to walk toward us with their shears held easy and low.

"I was afraid you'd be sentimental," he said. "Your father was sentimental too. It's the family flaw. Fortunately—" and the apple tree above us bent, its branches reaching down like arms, its glowing fruit splitting open to show rows of small, sharp, thorn-white teeth, "—the garden does the fighting. And it has been *so* hungry lately."

The ground under my boots drew its next breath—and held it.

I called the storm.
