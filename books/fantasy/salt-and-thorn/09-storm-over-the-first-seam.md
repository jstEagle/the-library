## 9. Storm Over the First Seam

### Gideon

The First Seam did not look like a wound. It looked like a garden.

That was the obscenity of it. Seven days of hard riding through country that had forgotten how to be itself—rivers running backward in their beds, birches growing thorns, a shepherd who greeted us twice and meant it both times—and at the end of all that wrongness stood a place of terrible beauty: terraces of black roses climbing a hillside that should have been bare granite, fountains that ran with light instead of water, and at the summit, split open like a seam in a well-tailored coat, the oldest door between worlds.

The Chancellor had made it lovely. That's what men like him do. They take a living thing, drain it pale, and hang jewels on the corpse.

"He's already begun," Wren said beside me. She'd gone very quiet on the last mile, the way she did before violence, which I had learned to read better than any map. "Listen."

I listened. Under the perfume of the roses there was a sound like a loom working—thread by thread by thread—the sound of the ritual pulling the Seam apart strand by strand, winding its magic onto the Chancellor's spindle.

"Any last words?" I asked her. "Now's the time. Once we start, I find people stop listening to me entirely."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference. Stalling is when you're afraid. Savoring is when you're afraid and honest about it."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she pressed her father's seal into my palm—she'd carried it since the Ash Road, and I understood the weight of handing it over—and said, "Bring it back to me. Both of you." Meaning the seal, meaning me, meaning herself. Wren never wasted words, which is why the ones she spent landed like coins thrown from a bridge.

Then we went down into the garden together, and the storm came up out of me like something that had been holding its breath for ten years.

* * *

You should understand what calling a storm costs an exiled man. It isn't like whistling up weather. The gift lives in the blood, and mine had gone fallow so long it had grown weeds. When I reached for it, it reached back, and for one long moment we regarded each other across the ruined years—me, the coward who had refused to burn a village; it, the power that would have burned everything else instead.

*I'm not asking you to kill anyone,* I told it. *Just to take back what's stolen.*

Thunder answered somewhere above the hill, low and skeptical, like a creditor being shown a promissory note.

The garden's guards were court soldiers in the Chancellor's livery, but they moved wrong—too smooth, too synchronized, puppets with their strings run through the drained Seam itself. Wren took the first three without breaking stride, ash-road knife-work, economical as bookkeeping. I took the rest with wind, because I could feel now—God help me, I could *feel*—that every drop of power in this place was stolen goods, and I have always been a smuggler at heart. I knew stolen goods when I touched them. And I took them back.

The roses screamed when the wind hit them. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone with a steadier hand than mine: they *screamed*, because they were not roses at all but the Seam's own flesh, grafted and bent into ornament, and every petal that tore away was a thread of covenant coming home.

At the summit, the Chancellor stood inside a ring of standing stones with his arms raised and his face turned up toward the half-open Seam, and he looked—this is the part I will never tell anyone who asks—*radiant*. Younger than his portraits. Unlined. A man drinking from a well that belonged to everyone, and glowing with it.

"Gideon Vane," he said, without turning. Of course he knew. Men like him always know; it's cheaper than vigilance. "I offered you your name back. Your house. Your father's honor restored posthumously, even. You come instead with weather."

"I come with an audit," I said, and called the lightning down.

* * *

### Wren

Here is what nobody tells you about holding a binding circle against Serah Kell: it is like arguing with winter.

Gideon had drawn the circle in salt and storm-light around the standing stones, and my whole task—my entire contribution to history, apparently—was to stand at its northern point and keep it whole while the world's most elegant murderess tried to unmake it. She came at me through the rain with her blades singing, and I planted my feet on my father's seal and held.

"You could walk away," she said between strikes. She wasn't even breathing hard. I hated her a little for that. "The Chancellor offered me the same thing he offered Vane. Pardon. Restoration. We are both women the crown used up and shelved, Wren. Why die for his redemption?"

"Because it isn't his," I said, and parried. "It's my father's. And yours. And every name those villagers forgot. You've seen the ledgers, Serah—I know you have, because you let him go, and you only ever let a man go when the evidence has teeth."

Her blade stopped an inch from my throat. Her eyes, gray as court slate, searched my face for the lie and found, to her visible disgust, none.

"The ledger says a Vane broke the covenant," she said quietly.

"The ledger says a Vane *signed* it. My father taught me the difference between a signature and a sin. Come see the last page sometime."

And then—because the storm above us chose that moment to tear a hole in the sky wide enough to drive a funeral through, and because the Chancellor's stolen youth began visibly to *drain*, running off him like dye in rain—Serah Kell did the thing I will be telling my grandchildren about, if I live to have any and if they prove worthy of the story.

She turned her blade on the Chancellor.

Not toward Gideon. Not toward me. Toward the radiant, unaging, magnificent thief at the center of everything, who had finally begun to look his true age, which was ancient, and his true nature, which was hollow.

"You promised me forever," he snarled at her, clutching at the light pouring out of the Seam like a man grabbing at water.

"You promised me *purpose*," she answered. "I find I prefer it."

What happened next happened fast, and I was holding a circle, not watching it, so my account is partial. I saw Gideon with his arms flung up and rain falling upward around him, giving the storm everything—not directing it anymore, just opening himself as a channel, which is how storm-callers die, and I think he knew it. I saw the garden's black roses come apart into threads of silver that streamed home into the Seam like rivers remembering their course. I saw Serah's blade take the Chancellor through the heart, and I saw him come apart with the garden, unraveling into the same silver, because a man made entirely of stolen magic is mostly magic, and magic goes home.

The Seam opened. All the way. Light like a sunrise happening sideways.

And Gideon fell.

* * *

### Gideon

Dying, it turns out, is mostly paperwork. You review the accounts. I lay in the mud at the top of the hill with the rain coming down gentle now, almost apologetic, and I reviewed mine: one village unburned, one map inherited, one father半 forgiven, one woman—

One woman kneeling over me, pressing her hands against my chest where the storm had gone out of me and taken too much else with it.

"Don't," Wren said. Just that. "Don't you dare do the noble fading thing, Gideon Vane, I will dig you up and kill you again."

"That's—" Blood. Talking around blood is underrated as a discipline; it teaches brevity. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's said to me."

"Shut up. *Stay.*"

Somewhere behind her, Serah Kell stood with her sword still drawn and the Chancellor's ashes cooling on the stones, looking older than she had an hour ago and somehow more herself. She looked down at me with an expression I couldn't name and didn't try to.

"The court will call this treason," she said.

"The court can get in line," I managed.

But it was the Seam that had the last word—or rather, what came through it. Because the Seams don't just open, I learned, lying there bleeding into the hillside while the covenant rewove itself thread by silver thread. They open *both ways*. And out of the light, slow as a queen descending into a room she owns, came the thorn-lord who had known my father, wearing an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of a creditor arriving at last to collect.

He looked at me—at the spent storm, the empty hands, the blood—and he smiled with too many teeth.

"So," he said. "The son pays the father's debt after all. But debts of this size, little storm-caller, are never paid in coin."

The light behind him shifted, and I understood, dimly and far too late, that the covenant reforged was also a contract—and contracts, in my long experience, always name their terms.
