## 10. Salt and Thorn

The morning after the end of the world smelled like rain and burnt sugar.

I stood at the edge of what had been the Chancellor's garden and tried to decide whether to laugh or be sick. The thorn-hedges that had drunk a captive Seam for thirty years were collapsing in on themselves, going grey and soft as old ash, and underneath them the ground was breathing—actually breathing, a slow swell and fall of earth like something waking from a long sleep. The First Seam lay open behind me, a wound of light stitched with wildflowers, humming a note I felt in my teeth more than heard.

And Gideon Vane was sitting in the mud.

Not slumped. Not dying, though he'd come close enough last night that I'd screamed his name into the storm until my throat tore. Just sitting, elbows on knees, watching his own hands like they belonged to a stranger. When I reached him he looked up, and there was something in his face I'd only seen once before—in my father's eyes, the day he came home from the border and told me he was done carrying messages for people who thought paper made things true.

"It's quiet," Gideon said. "In here." He tapped his temple. "Thirty years of weather living behind my eyes, and now it's just—" He searched for the word. "Weather."

"You gave it away," I said. "To the Seam."

"I gave it *through* myself. There's a difference." A ghost of his usual dryness, then gone. "The covenant needed a conduit, not a reservoir. Apparently I qualified." He flexed his fingers. "Do you know what's strange? I keep reaching for it. Like a tongue probing a missing tooth."

I sat down next to him in the mud because standing felt like a decision and I wasn't ready to make any decisions. My arms were striped with thorn-scratches from holding the binding circle while Serah Kell's blade came down and then—miracle upon miracle—turned. My hands were still shaking. They had been shaking since the Chancellor's ritual collapsed, since the stolen magic ripped free of its garden and poured back into the Seams like water finding its old channels, and I suspected they would go on shaking for some time, and that this was fine.

"Your father held this circle once," I said. "Didn't he."

"Under duress. To save a village." Gideon picked up a clod of grey hedge-ash and let the wind take it. "He signed the ledger because the alternative was Kell's predecessor burning Ashford Hollow off the map. And then he spent the rest of his life trying to undo what his signature set loose, and died disgraced for it, and passed the whole rotten inheritance to me." He glanced sideways. "You wanted vengeance on his son for most of our acquaintance. Any regrets?"

"I wanted vengeance on whoever killed my father," I said. "It turned out to be a Chancellor's blade-witch and a lie about smugglers. You were just the nearest grief-shaped thing to hit." I paused. "Also you dislocated my thumb on the Ash Road, so we're not exactly even, but I've decided to be gracious."

"Noted," he said. "Graciously."

The Fae came at noon.

They didn't arrive so much as become apparent—the way you notice frost has formed on a window without ever seeing it happen. One moment the ruined garden was empty; the next, the thorn-lord Vaelith stood among the dying hedges, tall and wrong and beautiful the way a winter branch is beautiful, his bark-dark face tilted as if listening to music under the ground.

"The Seams breathe," he said. His voice was wind through hollow reeds. "Small ones first. Then greater. In a season, perhaps two, the ways between will run full again."

"And the court?" I asked. "The new regents are already circling. They'll want the magic back under lock and key. Under *ledger*."

Vaelith turned those too-many-jointed eyes on me, and I felt the weight of being considered by something very old. "The covenant requires a keeper. A mortal hand at the border, sworn to neither crown nor wild, who holds the door open and judges who passes." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "The old covenant named a Vane. The Vane line is spent."

"Gideon's alive," I said, sharper than I meant to.

"He is *empty*," Vaelith said, not unkindly. "A warden must have something to give. Your father's daughter—" and here I understood, with a lurch, that he had known who I was since the moment I bargained passage from him in the thorn-wilds, that every question I'd asked about the broken covenant had been a test I hadn't known I was taking. "You crossed into the wilds grieving and armed. You learned the truth about your father's murder and did not turn your steel on the man beside you when it would have been easier. You held a binding circle against a blade-witch with nothing but stubbornness and salt." He inclined his head, an inch. "Stubbornness and salt are the two oldest wardens' tools. We would have you, Wren of the Ash Road."

The word *warden* landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I thought of my father, who had carried messages between worlds and died for one of them. I thought of the ledger, and the Chancellor's ageless smiling face, and Serah Kell riding east with her stolen years draining out of her, choosing at last which master to serve. I thought of every road I'd ever walked alone.

"I'm not swearing to the Fae," I said. "Or to the crown. You said it yourself—neither."

"Neither," Vaelith agreed. "To the door itself. It is a harder oath and a lonelier one."

"Good," I said. "I've had enough of easy masters."

Beside me, Gideon made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You realize," he said quietly, "that wardens traditionally live at the border. Permanently. That's rather the point of them."

"I'm aware."

"And that the nearest habitable structure is a smuggler's waystation with a roof that leaks in three places and a hearth that smokes unless you sweet-talk it."

"I'm *aware*, Gideon."

He looked at me then—really looked, the way he had on the Ash Road after our fight went to stalemate, when the fury drained out of both of us and left something rawer underneath. "Wren. I'm nobody now. No name, no gift, no family but a dead man's handwriting in a map's margins. You could have anyone. You could have someone whose hands don't shake when it rains."

"They shake when it rains because it *rains*, you idiot. That's what hands do." I took one of them anyway. It was warm, and calloused, and steady enough. "I spent a year wanting to kill you. Do you understand how much work that is? Planning it, rehearsing it, carrying it everywhere like a second knife? I'm not starting over with someone new. I'm keeping you."

Vaelith made a sound like wind moving through a house with all its doors open, which I chose to interpret as approval.

*

We buried my father's seal at the threshold of the First Seam.

It seemed right. He had carried the truth across the mountains at the cost of his life, and the truth had done its work—slowly, badly, through the hands of a smuggler and a furious girl with a grudge, but done. Gideon spoke the few words of the old covenant rite that his father's marginal notes preserved, and where his voice failed mine carried on, and the Seam's light bent over the small grave like a bowing head.

Then we walked down out of the high passes to the waystation, and I began the business of learning my post.

It was less grand than the stories make wardenship sound. Mostly it was mending the roof—which leaked in four places, Gideon having underestimated—and coaxing the hearth, and planting a kitchen garden in soil that remembered being fed on stolen life and needed reminding how to grow honest things. Salt went in a line along the eastern wall; thorn-cuttings from Vaelith's own boughs went along the western, and between them the house sat like a held breath, a hinge between worlds.

Travelers came, eventually. Fae who wished to walk in the mortal autumn and swear the passing-oath on salt. Humans—pilgrims first, then merchants, then, inevitably, spies—who came to see the famous open Seams and were judged at my threshold. Some I turned away. Some I let pass with a warning. Once, in the deep of winter, Serah Kell herself stood at my door, grey-faced and diminished, her stolen years nearly burned through, asking nothing except to look at the Seam she had helped close and helped reopen.

"Does it hurt?" she asked, watching the light breathe.

"Everything worth keeping does," I said, and gave her tea, because I am a warden and not a saint, and saints don't keep good tea.

Gideon took to the border life with the particular contentment of a man who has stopped running. He taught himself the Fae passing-tongues from a book Vaelith left us. He argued theology with thorn-imps. On stormy nights he would stand outside with his face turned up and his empty hands open, letting the rain hit him like an old friend's greeting, and I never asked what he felt, and he never said, but he always came in soaked and smiling.

One evening in spring—a year to the day since a dying courier pressed a bloodstained map into a smuggler's hands—I found him studying that same map by firelight, tracing his father's cramped marginal script with one finger.

"Thinking about him?" I asked.

"Thinking that he'd hate this," Gideon said. "All of it. The leaking roof, the imps, the warden who threatens travelers with her soup spoon." He smiled. "And thinking he'd have done it anyway. That's the difference between us and the Chancellor, I think. Doing the right thing even when it costs everything, and complaining the entire time."

"That's practically the family motto."

"It's on the coat of arms. Beneath the storm-cloud." He set the map aside and pulled me down onto the bench beside him, and outside, far up the mountain, the First Seam sang its low bright note against the dark, and the salt held, and the thorns kept their watch.

"Wren," he said, "the eastern Seam flickered today. Vaelith says the deep passages are waking faster than expected. There'll be traffic. Complications. Possibly another Chancellor someday, because there's always another Chancellor."

"I know."

"There's always another war."

"I know that too." I leaned my head against his shoulder, feeling the tremor in his hands that was just weather, just rain, just a man. "But tonight the door holds, and you're here, and the soup isn't burned. Tomorrow can look after itself."

Above the border, thunder rolled—harmless, ordinary, ours—and the Seams breathed open, and held.
