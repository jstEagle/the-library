## 2. Prayers That Get Answers

The rumor arrived in Saltmere the way weather arrives off the strait — first as a change in the light, then as a pressure behind the ears, then all at once, everywhere, undeniable.

Mirabel heard it first from the fishwives in the market below the counting house, their voices carrying up through the shutters of her workroom like smoke through a chimney flue. *The shrine at Candlewick Row gave bread to a beggar woman. Real bread, warm as a hearthstone. She'd only asked for it. Only knelt and asked.* By midmorning the story had grown a second loaf and a third witness; by evening it had grown a storm that turned aside from the fishing fleet at Marrowgate because an old man on the seawall had prayed aloud with his hat in his hands.

Mirabel did not go down to hear the fishwives tell it twice. She stayed at her bench, because the dead were already talking about it, and they talked louder than the living ever could.

She was carving a last breath into whalebone — the breath of a dyer named Anselm Petch, sixty-one years old, lungs ruined by thirty years over the vats. His widow had brought the breath to the Exchange three days ago in a stoppered clay vial, the way one brings anything: with a receipt, a fee, and eyes that kept darting toward the door as if she might yet change her mind. Last breaths were delicate inventory. They could not be sold whole — guild law forbade it, though Mirabel had long suspected the law existed less out of mercy than because a whole breath was worth more as forty small carvings than as one — but they could be worked into reliquary beads, mourning tokens, small bones of remembrance that let the bereaved hold a piece of the departed's final moment against their palm and feel, faintly, the shape of what had been.

Anselm's breath tasted of indigo and iron and, beneath both, a stubborn thread of satisfaction. He had been proud of his colors. He had died looking at a bolt of blue he'd finished that morning, and the blue had been good, and he had known it.

*Good cloth,* the breath murmured through her fingertips as her burin traced the spiral that would hold it. *Better than the merchants deserved. Tell her the third bolt was for the girl in Lantern Street. She'll know.*

"I can't tell anyone," Mirabel reminded him gently. "I'm not a messenger. I'm a bookkeeper."

The breath said nothing more. Breath never argued. It simply settled deeper into the bone, the way silt settles in still water, and waited to be read by whoever came after.

That was when the ledger page turned itself.

Not the ledger on her desk — the great ledger, the Ledger of Last Breaths itself, chained open on its lectern across the room because guild regulation required the master copy to remain visible during any carving session, so that every entry made could be checked against every entry held. It was a heavy thing, bound in salt-cured hide, its pages the color of old teeth. And one of those pages lifted, curled, and turned over with a sound like a dry tongue clicking against the roof of a mouth.

No draft. The windows were shuttered against the harbor damp. The brazier burned steady. Mirabel set down her burin and crossed the room, and the unease that walked beside her was quiet and cold and utterly unfamiliar, because in eleven years at the Exchange she had never once seen the ledgers do anything she had not told them to do.

The page that had turned was blank.

Not empty — blank. There is a difference, and every bone-carver knows it. An empty page is a page awaiting entry, white and expectant as fresh snow. A blank page is a page where something used to be. She could see the ghost-grooves of penstrokes pressed into the surface, the faint ridges where ink had once sat heavy enough to emboss the hide, and she could see, along the left margin, the stubs of letters that had been trimmed away as cleanly as if a razor had passed down each line. Something had eaten the entry. Not blotted it, not scraped it — *eaten* it, the way a moth eats the middle of a wool coat, leaving the seams standing.

She checked the folio number against the index. Entry 4,417. She found 4,416 without trouble — a ferryman's borrowed year, renewed twice, collateral a wedding ring. She found 4,418 — a name that had never been spoken, held in trust, holder deceased, disposition pending. Between them, nothing. The index itself had a gap where 4,417 should have been listed, a gap so smooth it looked deliberate, looked *designed*, looked like the work of something that understood bookkeeping well enough to falsify it.

Mirabel stood in the lamplight for a long time, listening.

The dead who lived in her carvings were always murmuring somewhere at the edge of her hearing — that was the gift, or the affliction, or whatever it was that had come down through her mother's mother's mother like a crooked finger inherited through four generations of hands. Usually it was no more than weather, a distant surf of voices she had learned to sleep inside. But now, as she stood before the mutilated page, one voice rose out of that surf with terrible clarity, close as a mouth at her ear, and it belonged to a man she had known alive.

*Beware the season of answered prayers,* said the voice of Oskar Vellum, senior broker, dead nine years, drowned in the strait under circumstances the guild had paid very good money not to investigate. *Every miracle is an invoice, girl. Every wonder comes due. I priced such things once, before you were born, back when the shrines still spoke. Ask me what happened the last time the gods remembered we exist.*

"Oskar," Mirabel whispered. "You're supposed to be in the reliquary drawer."

*I am,* he said, and there was something in his tone she had never heard from the dead before — urgency, yes, but underneath the urgency something worse. Fear. The dead were not supposed to be afraid. What could frighten someone who had already finished? *And I am speaking through my own death-token to do it, which costs me, which should tell you how much this matters. Listen to me. The bread at Candlewick Row. The storm at Marrowgate. Count them, Mirabel. Not bless them. Count them. Then find the entries they correspond to, and see what was owed, and to whom, and since when.*

"The ledgers don't record prayers."

*No,* Oskar agreed, and his voice began to fray at the edges, thinning like smoke in a draft. *They record debts. Prayers are just the collection notices arriving at last. Nine years I've been quiet in that drawer. Nine years I've watched your family keep the books and never once said a word, because there was nothing to say. But the pages have started turning, girl. The pages have started turning, and things older than ink are doing the erasing, and when the old gods begin calling in what they're owed—*

He was gone. Not faded — gone, cut off mid-word as cleanly as entry 4,417 had been cut away, and Mirabel was left standing alone in the counting house with the lamp hissing and her own heart going hard against her ribs.

* * *

By the second day, the brokers had stopped pretending it wasn't happening.

Mirabel knew this because the counting house filled with them — a tide of gray robes and ink-stained fingers, all of them talking at once, all of them asking variations of the same impossible question: *what is a miracle worth?*

Senior Broker Hasken Dree presided from the assessment dais, a narrow man with a narrow face and the particular pallor of someone who had spent forty years indoors pricing other people's losses. Before him, spread across three trestle tables, lay the morning's evidence, catalogued with the desperate thoroughness of men trying to build a fence around a flood.

A loaf of barley bread from Candlewick Row, purchased from the beggar woman for an outrageous sum and carried to the Exchange wrapped in linen like a relic, which, Mirabel thought, watching the brokers lean over it, was precisely what it was. It had not gone stale. That was the detail that had broken everyone's composure. Three days old and it was still warm. Warm bread does not stay warm. Warm bread is a fire that has not been told it should go out.

A jar of water from the shrine at Marrowgate, drawn after the storm turned. Fishermen were bottling it and selling splashes of it at the docks for more than the price of the fish it had saved.

A child's drawing from Tallow Street, depicting the local shrine of the Shepherd-of-Small-Things — a god so minor that most theologians had filed him under folklore — with the shrine drawn glowing, and beneath it, in a child's careful letters, the words MAMA'S COUGH WENT AWAY.

"And the valuation?" Dree demanded of the room at large, for perhaps the sixth time that hour. "I need a valuation. The Exchange cannot respond to events it has not priced. If we do not price these occurrences, some other concern will, and then we will spend a decade buying back the market we gave away for free."

"Price them as what?" asked Broker Immet, a young woman with quick eyes and the unguarded tongue of someone not yet sufficiently afraid of her superiors. "As commodities? As services rendered? We don't know who rendered them. We don't know what was rendered *to*. You want me to write an appraisal for a god, Master Dree? Shall I note the asset class as 'divine intervention, provenance unknown, resale value speculative'?"

A few brokers laughed. Most did not. Dree's expression suggested that Immet had just described exactly the sort of entry he wanted, and wanted by nightfall.

Mirabel stood at the edge of the crowd with her ledger keys at her belt and said nothing, because saying nothing was the discipline of her position, and because she was listening past the brokers to the dead.

The dead were loud today. Louder than she had ever known them, and getting louder, and the strange thing — the thing that raised the hair on her arms even as she stood in a warm room among living colleagues — was that they were not speaking to her. They were speaking to each other. All across the city, all across, she suspected, the free cities entire, the dead were turning toward the shrines the way birds turn before a storm, and the murmur that came off them sounded like the murmur of a crowd before bad news is announced.

*It's starting again,* they said to one another. *Do you feel it? Do you remember what it was like, the last time?*

*Remember,* said others, older voices, voices whose carvings predated the Exchange itself, fragments held in museum bones and grave-goods and the teeth of the long-buried. *We remember. We remember what was asked. We remember what was taken.*

Mirabel's hand drifted, without quite meaning to, to the whalebone pendant at her throat — her grandmother's carving, the first relic she had ever worn, holding a breath so old it had worn smooth as river stone. Her grandmother's own breath, given freely into her granddaughter's keeping, and even now, eleven years after the old woman died, it sometimes rose to meet Mirabel's ear in moments of trouble, patient and dry-humored, the steadiest voice in her life.

Today it was silent. Today her grandmother's breath felt, through the bone, like a hand gripping the edge of a table.

* * *

On the third day, the miracles came to Saltmere itself.

There was no avoiding them after that. A shrine on Rope Walk that had been a storage shed for thirty years began, overnight, to smell of incense no one burned. A fevered child in the Tanner's Quarter slept peacefully after her grandmother prayed at a cracked wayside stone, and woke hungry. And at low tide, in full view of half the harbor, the water off Gull Point went glass-still in a perfect circle two hundred feet wide, and stayed that way for an hour, while fishermen knelt on the quay and wept and did not, any of them, dare to put out their boats.

The Exchange declared a general assembly. Brokers came in from every district, and the counting house was so crowded that Mirabel had to stand on the stairs to see the dais, and the air smelled of wet wool and fear dressed up as procedure.

Dree read the reports. There were forty-one verified occurrences in six days. He had them listed by district, by deity invoked, by apparent effect. He had cross-referenced them against historical records going back two centuries, and here his voice changed, took on the flat carefulness of a man reading numbers he does not like.

"The last period of comparable shrine activity," he said, "ended one hundred and ninety-three years ago. The archives contain a complete valuation series from that era." He paused. "The series ends abruptly. The final entry in the miracle-valuation ledger of the old Exchange is dated the ninth of Frostmonth, and reads, in its entirety—" he squinted at the page, and Mirabel saw his hand tremble, just slightly, just enough — "*'All accounts suspended. All accounts called. May whatever gods remain forgive us the arithmetic.'*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water.

"After which," Dree continued, "the Exchange reorganized under new management, the valuation series was sealed, and the shrines went quiet for six generations. Until Tuesday."

"What was called?" someone asked from the back. "What did they owe?"

"That," said Dree, "is what I intend to find out. Effective immediately, all miracle-related inquiries route through the senior assessment desk. Nothing gets priced until I've seen the old series. Nothing gets traded until I understand what we're trading *with*." He gathered his papers. "And someone wake up the archivists. I want the sealed ledgers opened by nightfall."

Mirabel descended the stairs slowly, and in the press of bodies in the corridor, a dead man's voice found her again — Oskar Vellum, thinner now, more distant, as if he were shouting up from the bottom of a well.

*You heard the arithmetic line.*

"I heard it."

*Your grandmother heard it too, once. Did she ever tell you why the Jouberts keep the breath-ledgers? Why it's always been a Joubert?*

"She said it was our gift. Our charge."

*A gift,* Oskar repeated, and the word came out of the dead man's mouth with a bitterness that had survived nine years underwater. *Yes. That's one word for it. Girl, when you go home tonight, ask her breath what the veil is. Not what it does. What it* is. *Ask her what stands on the other side of it, and what it eats, and why your family has always been the ones who could hear through the wall. And then look at the crown the usurper wears, and ask yourself why a king who never sleeps chose to bind himself to a thing that hungers for memory — and whether the hunger started with the crown, or whether the crown was built to feed something that was already eating.*

"Oskar—"

*Count the miracles,* he said, already fraying, already going. *Count them, and mark the dates, and lay them against the debt-calendar in the sealed series if you can get your hands on it. Because I priced wonders once, Mirabel Joubert, and I will tell you the only rule I ever learned: the gods do not give. They lend. And the interest on a prayer is always, always paid in what the borrower loves.*

The voice cut out. Around her, the corridor of living brokers flowed on, arguing valuations, drafting memoranda, already rebuilding the comfortable fiction that everything — even this, even the gods waking like giants under the floorboards of the world — could be entered in a column and balanced by quarter's end.

Mirabel walked home through streets where candles had begun to appear in windows, little flames set before little shrines, the whole city quietly, hopefully, ignorantly placing its orders with powers it had forgotten how to read. She climbed to her rooms above the counting house, lit her lamp, and took her grandmother's pendant in both hands.

"Grandmother," she whispered to the smooth old bone. "Tell me what the veil is."

For a long time, nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the harbor lapped at its pilings, and beyond the harbor the sea lay dark and glassy all the way to Gull Point, unnaturally calm, like a held breath.

Then her grandmother's voice rose through the whalebone — and it was shaking.

*Child,* said the dead woman, *put down the ledger keys. Whatever you do next, whatever they ask of you — do not open the sealed series alone. And Mirabel. My darling girl. Before you go looking for what wears the king — you must first learn what your family has been wearing all along.*

Outside, far off across the water, every bell in the Temple District began to ring at once, though it was midnight, and no service had been called.
