## 3. Corvin of the Broken Oath

The Salt-Lantern smelled of tar, brine gone sour in the sun, and the particular despair of men who drank before noon and called it a schedule. Corvin Ashe had been sitting at the same table for eleven days now — the one nearest the door, back to the wall, sightline to the street — because habits outlive reputations, and his reputation had died a full season before he had.

He turned his cup slowly on the scarred wood. Half a measure of something the tavernkeep called rum and Corvin called regret. He was nursing it the way you nurse a wound you refuse to let close: not because healing would hurt, but because the pain was the last honest thing he owned.

Eleven days. He'd counted them the way he'd once counted contract clauses — precisely, obsessively, because precision was all that remained of the man he used to be.

Corvin Ashe, Enforcer Third Rank of the Veiled Exchange. It had a ring to it once, when he was twenty-two and still believed the guild's ledgers were a form of justice with better arithmetic. He'd worn the grey-and-silver sash like it meant something. He'd learned the repossession rites until he could recite them in his sleep: *By clause and by consideration, by seal and by signature, what was borrowed returns to the lender.* He'd taken back borrowed years from gamblers who'd drunk away their children's childhoods. He'd repossessed a name from a bigamist so thoroughly that both wives woke unable to remember ever loving him. He'd carried a stolen shadow through the rain-washed streets of Cindral Port in an iron box, and told himself every night that the ledger balanced, and balance was enough.

Then came Marta Voss.

He hadn't known her name at first. To the Exchange she'd been Account 4471-B, widow, sixty-one years of age, holder of a single borrowed year contracted seventeen years prior against her husband's deathbed debts — a year she'd purchased to see her son married, or so the broker's note read. Standard terms. Standard interest. The year had compounded, as years did, into something the clerks now valued at three years, four months, and eleven days of her remaining life, payable immediately upon audit.

Corvin had stood in her kitchen on a Tuesday morning with the writ in his hand and watched her knead bread. That was the detail that undid him, later, when he tried to explain it to the tribunal. Not weeping. Not begging. She just kept kneading the bread, because her hands had been making bread every Tuesday for forty years and they didn't know how to stop, even while a guild enforcer explained that she owed the mathematics of her own death.

"Will it hurt?" she'd asked him. Just that. Flour to the wrists. Eyes like a woman asking about weather.

And Corvin Ashe, who had never once failed to execute a writ, who had a commendation for flawless compliance framed above the desk he no longer possessed, heard himself say: "No, madam. Because I'm not going to do it."

* * *

They'd broken his oath-ring in front of the assembled brokers. That was the rite — a thin band of guild-steel worn on the smallest finger, cut away with shears while your name was struck from the rolls. The master of ceremonies had asked him, per procedure, if he wished to recant. Eleven days ago, standing in that cold marble hall with his finger bleeding where the shears bit, Corvin had looked at the row of faces above him — soft, well-fed faces, faces that signed papers and never visited kitchens — and said the only true thing left in him:

"You can take the ring. You can't take the arithmetic. I've done the sums, masters. Every year this guild collects from people like Marta Voss goes somewhere. I'd like to know where."

They hadn't answered. They never answered questions like that. They'd simply struck his name, and the silence where his name had been followed him out of the building like a smell.

Now he lifted the cup, found it empty, and signaled the keep for another half-measure he couldn't afford. His coin purse held four coppers and a guild token someone had pressed into his hand at the door of the tribunal hall — a kindness, maybe, or a bribe, or a test. He'd been turning it over ever since. One side bore the scales of the Exchange. The other was blank, which Corvin had come to think of as the most honest face the guild ever showed anyone.

The tavern door opened, letting in a knife of gray daylight and the cry of gulls off the Mirenth Harbor flats.

Corvin's eyes went to it the way they always did — old training, useless now but loyal as a dog. A woman stood in the doorway, small and dark-haired, dressed in traveling wool gone salt-stiff at the hems, carrying a leather case the size of a hymnal. She scanned the room with the unhurried thoroughness of someone reading a page, and Corvin understood, with a sinking feeling he was too tired to disguise, that she was looking for him.

She found him. Of course she found him. There were only six patrons in the Salt-Lantern at midday, and five of them were asleep in their cups.

She crossed the floor and stopped at his table. Up close she was younger than her bearing suggested — thirty, perhaps, with ink stains on her fingers and the kind of stillness he'd only ever seen in people who spent their working hours among the dead.

"Corvin Ashe," she said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking." He didn't get up. Getting up was for men with somewhere to be. "If you're here about the debt, I'm afraid my assets consist of four coppers, a blank token, and a hangover of considerable quality."

"I'm not here about a debt." She set the leather case on the table between them. "I'm Mirabel Joubert. Bone-carver, Second Ledger, Veiled Exchange."

"A carver." He raised an eyebrow. "Come to collect my bones, then? I hear they're worth more than my coin."

Something flickered across her face — not amusement, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of the particular flavor of bitterness she was hearing. "I need to know how the guild enforces its contracts," she said. "All of it. The rites, the loopholes, the ways they break. And I'm told there's exactly one man in this city who knows both how they're enforced and why they fail."

"There's no man in this city who knows why they fail," Corvin said. "That's rather the point of me. I'm the cautionary tale. They tell new recruits about me over supper. *Don't be like Ashe, children; he thought the ledger should answer to something besides itself.*"

"The dead talk to me," Mirabel said.

Corvin blinked. It was not the sentence he'd expected, though in fairness he hadn't expected any sentence. He gestured vaguely at the room. "Do they often? Talk? Because these gentlemen have been trying for hours and getting nothing but snoring."

"I carve bone." Her voice stayed level, patient, the voice of a woman explaining tides to a child. "Relics. When I carve a relic from bone that belonged to someone, I hear what that bone remembers. Last breaths. Old griefs. Things people said when they thought no one was listening. It's how I keep the ledgers honest — the Exchange trades in impossible things, Master Ashe, and someone has to verify the goods."

"And the dead sent you to me."

"The dead warned me." For the first time, something moved behind her composure — a hairline crack, quickly sealed. "A broker named Halloway. Dead eight years. He worked the miracle accounts before the miracles started coming back, back when answered prayers were just rumors priests told to fill the collection plates. His voice came through a relic I carved three nights ago, and he said—" She paused, and Corvin saw her choose the words carefully, the way you handle something with edges. "He said the old gods are paying their debts. And that when gods pay their debts, mortals learn what they owe."

"That's very poetic," Corvin said. "What does it have to do with enforcement?"

"Because something is erasing the ledgers." She opened the case. Inside, nested in green felt, lay a book bound in gray hide, its clasps tarnished silver. Even from across the table Corvin felt his skin prickle — he knew guild bindings when he saw them, knew the weight of a ledger that had been warded since before either of them was born. "Three nights ago, an entry turned blank in front of me. Not smudged. Not torn out. *Erased*, as if the ink had been unwritten. The entry was a last breath, sold to the Exchange forty years ago by a dying fisherman. And whatever took it was older than the ink, older than the paper, older than the guild." She closed the case. "I went to the masters. They told me to log the anomaly and continue balancing. So I am continuing. Balancing. Which is why I need you."

Corvin leaned back and studied her. She didn't fidget under the look. Most people fidgeted.

"Let me make sure I understand," he said. "You're a guild officer. Second Ledger. Keys and everything. And you're sitting in the worst tavern in Mirenth Harbor asking a nameless oath-breaker to help you investigate the guild's own books."

"Yes."

"Why not go to the enforcers? Oh — wait." He smiled without any humor in it. "Because you went to the enforcers already, and they told you the same thing the masters did. Log it and move along."

"They told me anomalies above a certain threshold are referred directly to the First Ledger." Her jaw tightened. "Which is closed to everyone below master rank. Including me."

"So you're going around them."

"I'm going *through* them," Mirabel said. "There's a difference. Around means sneaking. Through means finding the load-bearing wall and learning where it cracks." She met his eyes. "You know where it cracks, Corvin. You cracked it yourself, in front of the whole tribunal, and they cut your ring off rather than answer you. Where does the money go? Where do the collected years *go*?"

The question landed in the quiet place inside him where Marta Voss's flour-white hands still kneaded bread. He looked down at his cup, at the ring of moisture it had left on the wood, and thought: eleven days of this. Eleven days of watching the tide come in and go out, in and out, taking nothing and leaving nothing, and calling it peace.

It wasn't peace. It was just arithmetic with the sum hidden.

"Suppose I tell you," he said slowly. "Suppose I tell you everything — the repossession rites, the escrow vaults under the counting house, the way a borrowed year gets transferred, the seals that hold and the ones that don't. What then? You'll write a report. The masters will file it. The file will go into a vault, and the vault will go into the ground, and in forty years some other carver will open it and find the pages blank."

"Maybe," Mirabel said. "Or maybe I'll find out what's eating the pages, and stop it."

"And what's in it for me?" The words came out harsher than he intended, and he heard beneath them the thing he actually meant: *what could possibly be in it for me, a man with nothing, whose one act of decency cost him everything.* But Mirabel Joubert heard it too. He watched her hear it. She didn't flinch and she didn't pity him, and somehow that was worse than either.

"Nothing," she said. "Not coin. Not reinstatement — I couldn't give that if I wanted to, and frankly, if you wanted it, you'd have recanted at the tribunal." She paused. "But I'll tell you what I heard in the relic from the palace last week. A voice under the king's voice. Hungry. Patient. Reciting genealogies backward. And I'll tell you what Halloway said about answered prayers being called-in debts. And then you can decide whether the arithmetic you did at the tribunal — the sums nobody would check — might finally have an answer waiting at the bottom of them."

The tavern noise seemed to recede. Corvin became aware of his own heartbeat, steady and stubborn, the one organ that had never signed anything.

"You really believe it," he said. "The godling. The crown. All of it."

"I believe my ledgers," Mirabel said. "Everything else is interpretation."

He laughed — a short, rusty sound, startled out of him like a bird from cover. It hurt his face. He hadn't laughed in eleven days.

"All right," he said. "Carver Joubert. Here's your first lesson in enforcement, free of charge." He tapped the table twice, an old habit, marking clauses. "One: nothing in the guild's books is ever destroyed. Erased, misfiled, redacted, yes — but the Exchange keeps originals. There's a deep archive under the founding shrine, older than the counting house, and the masters will tell you it's flooded and inaccessible. It isn't. There's a maintenance stair behind the salt-cellar on Tidewatch Lane, and the ward-seal on it answers to enforcer's blood — which mine still does, ring or no ring. They cut the steel off my finger. Nobody thought to cut the oath out of it."

Mirabel had gone very still. "Two?" she said.

"Two: contracts bind both ways. Everyone forgets that. Every repossession writ, every sale of breath or year or name — there's a counter-clause, a reciprocal obligation written small, because the founders were careful men and their heirs are lazy ones. If something is eating the entries in your ledgers, carver, then something has a *contractual relationship* with those entries. And if there's a contract, there's a signature." He leaned forward, and eleven days of rum and ruin fell away from his voice like a coat set down. "Find the signature, and you find the terms. Find the terms, and you find what it wants. Nothing eats for free. Not even gods."

For a long moment Mirabel Joubert just looked at him, and Corvin had the strange sensation of being read — not judged, *read*, the way she presumably read bone, line by line, finding what the living surface remembered.

"They were right about you," she said quietly.

"At the tribunal?"

"In the guild halls. They say you're the most dangerous man the Exchange ever employed." She closed the case and drew it back toward her. "Because you actually believe the contracts mean what they say."

Corvin looked at his empty cup, at the four coppers, at the blank-faced token, at the gray light on the water beyond the window where the tide was turning.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When this is done — however it ends — somebody reads Marta Voss's account aloud. Full entry. Into the record, wherever the record ends up being kept. She knelt on a cold floor and lost everything she had, and the least the world owes her is to remember her name." He swallowed. "*Marta Voss*. Write it down. You carvers are good at that."

Mirabel reached across the table and, before he could react, turned his right hand over and examined the pale scar where the oath-ring had been sheared away. Her fingers were cool and ink-stained and very precise.

"It's healing crooked," she said. "Whoever cut it rushed." She released his hand and stood, tucking the case under her arm. "Tidewatch Lane. Tomorrow, an hour after dawn. Bring your blood and your arithmetic, Master Ashe. I have a feeling we're going to need both."

She walked out the way she'd come in, unhurried, and the door swung shut behind her, and the Salt-Lantern settled back into its fog of smoke and snoring as if nothing had happened.

Corvin sat for a long time with his crooked-healing finger curled against his palm.

Then he got up — getting up was for men with somewhere to be — left his four coppers on the table, and walked out into the gray morning to find out, at long last, where the money went.

Behind him, unnoticed, the blank-faced guild token lay on the scarred wood where he'd left it. And if anyone had been watching closely — no one was — they might have seen the blank side darken, faintly, like a page remembering ink, before fading again to nothing at all.
