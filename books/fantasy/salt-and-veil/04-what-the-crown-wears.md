## 4. What the Crown Wears

The priest arrived at the Veiled Exchange on the fourth night of the rains, and Mirabel knew before anyone told her that he carried something in his mouth that was not bread.

She was at her bench in the counting house, finishing a commission that had taken eleven days — a widow's grief carved into a knuckle of walrus ivory, the shape of it a door left open on a dark stair. The dead woman whose breath had been pressed into the bone years ago still murmured there, faint as tide through a shell, and Mirabel had learned to work with the murmur rather than against it, letting the voice guide the curve of her burin the way a blind man lets a wall guide his hand.

Then the murmur stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, the way a clock stops — mid-tick, mid-breath — and every other relic in the counting house went quiet with it. Forty years of last words, of borrowed confessions, of names spoken once into whalebone so they would not be lost: all of them silent at once, like birds going down before a storm.

Mirabel set down her burin.

The counting house of the Veiled Exchange did not look like a place where impossible things were traded. It looked like what it had been before the guild: a salt merchant's warehouse, all black beams and whitewashed stone, with ledgers stacked to the rafters and clerks scratching away at desks worn smooth by three generations of elbows. The impossible things were kept below, in vaults that smelled of brine and cold iron, but their echoes lived upstairs, in the bones and ivories and river-stones that passed across the counters daily. Mirabel had spent nine years learning to hear those echoes the way other people heard music. She had never once heard silence.

"Brother Anselm," said the door-warden's voice from the entry hall, strained in a way she had never heard it. "Brother Anselm, you can't — the Exchange is closed to petitioners after dusk, guild law is guild law—"

"I am not here to petition." The voice that answered was young and cracked and had been screaming recently or praying loudly; both left the same rasp. "I am here to testify. There is a difference. Ask for the bone-carver. They will know why."

*They will know why.* Mirabel was already moving, wiping her hands on her apron, and behind her the relics stayed silent as held breath.

* * *

He looked worse than his voice. He was perhaps twenty-five, gaunt in the way of men who have eaten little and run far, and his priest's grey robe had been dyed brown in patches — mud, she thought at first, until she saw the pattern of it and understood it was blood, old blood, soaked and scrubbed and soaked again. He stood in the entry hall dripping rainwater onto the mosaic floor, clutching a satchel to his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood, and when he saw her his eyes went first to her hands.

"You're her," he said. "The Joubert woman. The one who hears."

"I hear," Mirabel said carefully. "What I hear depends on what's brought to me. Brother Anselm, was it? You've come a long way to be turned away by a door-warden."

"From Cael Amarth." The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well. The capital. The usurper's city, six hundred miles east, past the border marches, past Vaelmoor and its watchtowers. No one came from Cael Amarth anymore except trade factors with sealed papers and men who no longer asked questions. "I was chaplain in the palace household. Third rank. I kept the king's private chapel accounts." A wet, broken laugh. "Do you know what I accounted for, Mistress Joubert? Candle wax. Incense. I counted candle wax for three years while—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "May I sit down? I have been standing for four days and I would like very much to sit down before I say the rest, because I think I may need to say it slowly."

They gave him a chair in the small ledger-room off the main floor, and tea, which he held without drinking, warming his hands on the cup as though it were the only honest thing left in the world. Mirabel sat across from him. Old Master Corvane hovered in the doorway pretending to inspect a shelf of contract copies, which meant half the senior clerks were listening from the corridor beyond, and Mirabel found she did not mind. Let them listen. Something in her chest had been tightening for two weeks — since the blank page, since the erased entry, since the dead broker's voice had whispered *debts being called in* — and she wanted witnesses for whatever came next.

"The king does not sleep," Anselm said.

It was such a plain sentence that for a moment no one moved. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, a vault door groaned on its hinges.

"Not badly. Not poorly. Not at all," the priest went on, staring into his cup. "I began keeping the chapel log in my second year, and I noticed the entries — His Majesty attended dawn prayers, His Majesty attended midnight offices — every night, every night, and I thought, what devotion, what piety, the court loves him for it. Then one night I stayed past the office to reset the candles, and I saw him. He kneels. He folds his hands. And nothing happens. Nothing *happens*, do you understand me? Sleep passes over him like weather over stone. Three years. The physicians give him tonics. The astrologers blame the conjunctions. The queen mother died believing her son was merely an early riser." His voice frayed. "And his shadow—"

He looked up then, directly at Mirabel, and she saw that his hands were shaking so hard the tea trembled in rings against the cup's rim.

"His shadow moves late. Half a heartbeat, perhaps less. You would never see it unless you watched for it, and who watches for a man's shadow? But I kept the midnight office alone with him for three years, and I watched, God forgive me, I watched because there was nothing else to look at, and the shadow comes down after. He raises his hand in blessing and the shadow follows, late, always late, like a servant who resents his master. Or—" Anselm swallowed. "Or like a hand on a leash, testing how much slack it has been given."

Master Corvane made a small sound in the doorway, dismissive, the sound of a man preparing to price a miracle or discount a ghost. Mirabel raised one finger without looking at him, and the sound stopped.

"Why come to us?" she asked. "Why not the temples? The free cities' councils? Why walk four days through rain to a counting house?"

Anselm set down the cup. He opened the satchel with fingers that fumbled twice at the buckle, and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table between them.

"Because of this," he said. "And because the temples are either bought or burning. And because I have heard — everyone in the capital whispers it now, in the kitchens, in the barracks — that the Exchange trades in things that cannot otherwise be sold. That you keep records of things the crown wants forgotten." His eyes were fever-bright. "I need this to be somewhere the king cannot reach. I need it to exist somewhere outside his arithmetic. Do you understand? Everything in Cael Amarth is inside his arithmetic now. Even the prayers. *Especially* the prayers."

On the oilcloth lay a reliquary pendant — silver, tarnished, the kind given to household chaplains so they could carry a fragment of some blessed bone close to their hearts. Its glass face had been shattered. Through the broken glass, Mirabel could see the relic itself: a curl of bone, yellowed, no longer than her smallest finger.

Whose bone, she could not tell. But she could feel it from across the table, the way you feel a fire from a doorway — a pressure against the skin of the world, a wrongness in the air above it, like heat-shimmer in winter.

"It was in the king's private chapel," Anselm said. "In a locked case, under triple seal, in a case marked with the royal cipher. Not displayed. Not venerated. *Hidden*. I broke the case the night I ran." He pushed it toward her an inch, and his hand jerked back as if the thing had bitten him. "I have carried it for four days and I will tell you honestly, Mistress Joubert, that I have not slept either. Whatever is in that case, it does not want to leave the palace. And it knows my name now. It uses it."

The relics in the counting house were still silent. All of them. Waiting.

Mirabel looked at the broken reliquary for a long moment. Nine years of guild discipline said: assess, appraise, record, file. Nine years of her grandmother's training said: never touch an unprovenanced relic barehanded, never touch a relic that frightens its bearer, never—

But the dead broker's voice had said *debts being called in*, and the ledger page had gone blank, and something older than ink was moving through the world's accounts, and Mirabel Joubert had spent her whole life listening to the dead because someone in her line had agreed, long ago, to be the ears for something she did not yet understand.

She pulled off her right glove.

"Mistress Joubert—" Corvane began.

"Record the time and date," she said. "Witnessed examination, unprovenanced relic, voluntary surrender by bearer. If I scream, note that too."

She reached out and touched the bone.

* * *

The voices of the dead came to her the way water comes to a well: seeping up through strata, filtered, cooled, made gentle by distance. That was the gift as she had always known it. A last breath in whalebone was a whisper. A name in river-stone was a sigh. Even the loudest death she had ever touched — a drowned pilot's terror sealed in a ship's figurehead — had come to her muffled, wrapped in cotton and years.

This was not muffled.

This was a mouth against her ear.

"—Aldous, son of Bertrand, son of Aldous the elder, son of Marguerite who was daughter of—"

The genealogy poured through her like black water, and it was running *backward*. Names flowing upstream, each generation younger than the last, the lineage reciting itself from root toward seed, and Mirabel understood with a lurching, sickening clarity that this was not remembrance. Remembrance flows forward, parent to child, the way rivers flow to sea. This was consumption. This was a thing drinking a family tree from the leaves down, swallowing generation after generation toward the living tip of the branch, and at the living tip —

At the living tip was a man kneeling in a chapel at midnight, hands folded, eyes open, three years unsleeping, while his shadow lagged behind him like a debt unpaid.

Beneath the genealogy, under it, around it, was the voice itself. Not the king's. Deeper than the king's. It did not boom; that was the horror of it. It was reasonable. Patient. Almost tender, the way a farmer is tender with his orchard, the way a lender is patient with a debtor he knows cannot escape the interest. It recited names the way Mirabel balanced columns — methodically, lovingly, each name a figure entered, each figure a claim established, each claim a hook set in the meat of the world.

And threaded through it all, so faint she might have imagined it, another voice. Thin. Far away. A man's voice, saying over and over, in the tone of someone who has said it ten thousand times and expects to say it ten thousand more:

*I am still here. I am still here. I am still—*

Mirabel wrenched her hand back.

She was on the floor. She did not remember falling. Anselm was crouched beside her, white-faced, and Corvane was shouting for water, and the ledger-room swam around her like something seen through tears. Her right hand burned — not the skin, the bones of it, as though the relic had read her hand the way she read relics, and had found something there it recognized. Found a name. Found a *lineage*.

Found an account already opened.

"Get it out of the room," she rasped. "Vault seven. Iron, salt, and no one touches it without me present. Seal the intake record under my personal key." She got a knee under herself, then a foot, and Corvane hauled her upright with surprising gentleness for a man who had voted to repossess a ferryman's borrowed decade just last spring. "Brother Anselm. Did it speak to you? In four days. Did it use your name?"

"Twice," the priest whispered. "Both times at dusk. Both times it said—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, as if the walls might be listening, and perhaps they were. "It said, *Brother Anselm, you may go home now.* Just that. Kindly. Like an employer dismissing a servant." His face crumpled. "That is the worst of it, Mistress Joubert. It was kind. It offered me my bed and my supper and my old life, and all I had to do was stop asking questions about candle wax and shadows and sleep. It would have let me go home. It only keeps what it eats, and I am too thin a meal to bother chasing."

Mirabel stood with her burning hand pressed flat against the table, and let the counting house settle around her — the clerks' faces, the rain, the terrible attentive silence of forty relics waiting for her to speak first.

Predation, she thought. She knew predation. Every bone-carver did; the guild's oldest contracts were written in the grammar of predators — the lure, the patience, the interest that compounds while the debtor sleeps. But she had always imagined monsters as hunger wearing darkness. She had never imagined hunger wearing a crown, sitting a throne, signing decrees with a stolen hand while the true king wept silently somewhere underneath, saying *I am still here* to no one who could hear.

No one who could hear.

Her grandmother's voice rose in memory, from a lesson thirty years old, delivered over a half-carved femur in a kitchen that smelled of tallow: *We are the ears, little Mira. Ours is the listening side of every bargain. Remember that, and remember it costs more than the speaking ever will.*

She had thought it a metaphor. Guild families loved metaphors; they dressed up their grim inheritances in poetry so the children could carry them.

She looked at her right hand, at the fine old scars across the knuckles from nine years of burins and bone-dust, and for the first time in her life she wondered what exactly had been handed down to her, and by whom, and under what terms.

"Brother Anselm," she said. "You'll stay tonight. We'll take your testimony formally, every word, and I'll carve it into bone myself so it can't be edited, lost, or priced." She flexed her aching fingers. "And tomorrow I'm going to ask you everything you know about the king's chapel accounts. Because whatever sits behind that crown has been keeping books, Master Corvane — real ones, in flesh and blood and generations — and I intend to audit them."

Corvane stared at her. "Joubert, that is the royal house of Aldous you're talking about. The Exchange doesn't audit kings. The Exchange *trades with* kings. There is a difference, and the difference is our continued existence."

"Yes," Mirabel said quietly. "I've begun to suspect there's a third thing we haven't priced yet. What happens to the traders, when the thing they trade with finishes eating."

Outside, the rain thickened against the shutters, and in vault seven, wrapped in iron and salt, the broken reliquary waited with the patience of a creditor — and beneath the capital, beneath the throne, beneath three years of unsleeping nights, a buried man went on whispering *I am still here* into a dark that had almost finished listening.

Almost.

Someone, somewhere, had finally begun to hear.
