## 5. The Usurper's Arithmetic

The provisioning office of Karst Hollow smelled of tallow, wet wool, and something sweeter underneath — the smell Corvin had come to think of as *paid*, though he couldn't have named it before tonight. He'd smelled it in the barracks outside the city, drifting from soldiers' bedrolls like a perfume no one wore on purpose. He smelled it now, stronger, rising through the floorboards from the counting room below.

He crouched in the rafters above the clerks' hall and waited for the last lamp to go out.

Infiltration was not what it had been. Ten years ago — before the oath-breaking, before the taverns, before his name went into the Exchange's ledger under a heading that meant *do not trust with a knife* — he could have walked through the front door wearing a forged collar and a bored expression. The usurper's new bureaucracy had no such easy doors. Every clerk in King Aldous's service carried a token stamped with the crown-and-thorn sigil, and every token was checked against a roster at dawn and dusk, and every roster was kept in a strongroom Corvin had spent three days watching without finding a way into.

So: the rafters. His knees ached. He was forty-one years old and had spent the better part of six of them drinking, and his body filed its complaints like a creditor who knew the debt would never be honored but enjoyed the ritual.

Below him, two clerks still worked by lamplight among the shelves. One was old, gray-braided, methodical; the other young enough that her hands shook whenever she opened a certain drawer. Corvin had watched them for three nights. The old one, Master Ellery by the name stitched on his coat, handled the grain accounts. The young one handled something else.

"Last batch," the girl said, so quietly that Corvin only caught it because the rafters carried sound down as well as up. "Forty-one units from the Third March Regiment."

"They'll want them tallied before the courier comes," Ellery said. "Bring the drawer."

She brought the drawer. Even from above, even in the lamplight's spill, Corvin saw that it was lined in lead — dull gray metal, the kind the guild used to line boxes meant to hold things that shouldn't touch the world more than necessary. He had packed such boxes himself, once. He had been very good at packing them.

Inside the drawer were coins.

No — not coins. They *looked* like coins at first glance, round and pale, stacked in paper sleeves like any mint issue. But they were the wrong color for any currency Corvin knew: a warm off-white, faintly translucent at the edges, and when the lamp flame guttered they seemed to gutter too, a half-beat behind.

Bone. Carved bone, thin as a wafer, pressed and polished into coin-shape.

Corvin's mouth went dry.

* * *

He should leave. That was the professional instinct, the one the guild had beaten into him across a decade of repossession work: *see enough to confirm, never enough to be seen.* He knew where the line between evidence and suicide ran, and he was crouched directly over it.

But the girl opened one of the paper sleeves, and Corvin did not leave.

"Read out," Ellery said. "I'll enter."

She held the first bone-coin up to the lamp, and Corvin saw that its face was engraved — fine lines, dense as scripture, a carver's hand unmistakable even upside down. The girl read from a separate slip, a manifest.

"Unit forty-one. Private Dannel Voss, Third March. Surrendered: the summer of his ninth year." Her voice flattened itself as she read, the way clerks learn to flatten their voices around numbers that ought to hurt. "Contents: a river, an older brother, a dog named Bristle. Assessed quality: high. Emotional density noted as exceptional. Assigned value: eight months' campaign pay."

"Eight months," Ellery murmured, writing. "The boy must have loved that dog."

"He won't remember loving it." The girl set the coin down with exaggerated care, as if it might bruise. "That's the point, isn't it? That's why they're worth more whole."

Corvin's hands had closed into fists without his permission. He made himself open them. Made himself breathe. The rafter dust tickled his throat and he dared not cough.

Ellery turned a page in a ledger so thick it needed two hands, and Corvin, angling his head, caught the column headings in the lamplight. Not *grain*. Not *steel*. The columns read: **RECEIVED** / **ASSESSED** / **DISBURSED** / **RESIDUE**.

And beneath the headings, row after row after row, regiment numbers. The Third March. The Ninth Foot. The Queen's Own Lancers — Aldous's own lancers, the ones whose banners flew over Vaelmoor's surrendered gates. Beside each regiment, figures in the hundreds. Beside some, in the thousands.

"You said the courier comes at dawn?" the girl asked.

"The paymaster does. Courier brought these in yesterday from the front — you slept through the wagon, lucky thing. It was full." Ellery rubbed his eyes. "Fuller than last month. They're advancing faster now, so they're harvesting faster. A company takes a village, the villagers surrender their memories rather than their lives — most of them, anyway, once they understand the choice — and the army pays the soldiers out of the take. Keeps morale up. Keeps them marching."

"On what?"

"Hm?"

"The soldiers, master. What do they march on? Bread costs bread. You can't feed a man on—" she gestured at the drawer "—on summers."

Ellery looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face aged ten years in the space of a breath.

"Girl," he said, "have you ever wondered why the hollow regiments don't desert? Why they don't eat much, don't complain, don't ask where the supply trains are? I asked that question my first year. I was told the crown's logistics were a marvel of modern administration." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "They are. We are. But we're not the marvel. The marvel is whatever sits on the king's head, because it doesn't need the men fed the way you and I are fed. It needs them *emptied* the way we are fed. A man who has given up his ninth summer doesn't miss hot meals the way you'd miss one. He barely misses anything. He marches until the coin runs out, and then—"

He stopped. The girl waited.

"And then?" she whispered.

"Then he becomes residue." Ellery tapped the fourth column. "Look at the arithmetic sometime. Really look. Every unit disbursed is entered here eventually, weeks later, at zero value. Do you know what the crown does with its residue soldiers, when the borrowed childhood is spent and there's nothing left to collect?"

"I don't want to know."

"No," Ellery agreed gently. "Neither did I. And yet we both keep working here, don't we. Because the alternative is the road, and the road belongs to them."

The lamp burned. Somewhere below, in the counting room under the floor, that sweet paid-smell seeped up through the boards, and Corvin understood at last what it was: it was the smell of memory going stale. Of summers sitting in lead-lined drawers, waiting to be spent by men who no longer remembered having been children.

* * *

He stayed until dawn because he had no choice. The office had a night watchman who made rounds at the turn of each hour, and the windows were shuttered, and the only exit that didn't pass the strongroom door was the coal chute, which emptied into a yard with its own guard. So Corvin folded himself smaller in the dark between the beams and did the one thing he was still, despite everything, genuinely good at.

He memorized.

Not the ledgers — he couldn't read them from here, not the entries themselves. But he memorized shapes. The rhythm of Ellery's pen. The exact weight of the lead-lined drawer when the girl lifted it — he could tell by how her shoulders dropped. The schedule of the paymaster's arrival. The sigil stamped on each sleeve of bone-coins: not the crown-and-thorn of Aldous's public arms, but something older, something that made Corvin's skin crawl when he finally resolved it in the pre-dawn gray.

A circle. And inside the circle, a spiral coiling inward, tighter and tighter, toward a point that the engraver had rendered as a single unblinking eye.

He had seen that mark once before, years ago, on a contract he'd been ordered to enforce and had refused. He hadn't known what it meant then. He'd thought it was just another guild house-mark, one of dozens, the kind of thing brokers inked onto papers about borrowed years and last breaths — impossible commerce, but commerce nonetheless, bounded by guild law, bounded by *rules*.

There were no rules here. He understood that now with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down the back of his neck.

Guild law said a repossessed year was returned to the pool and reassigned fairly. Guild law said last breaths belonged to the dying until freely sold. Guild law said names, even unspoken ones, even bought ones, could never be used to compel. The Veiled Exchange was rotten in a hundred small ways — he'd been one of the rot, he knew every worm in the wood — but it was rotten the way a great tree rots: slowly, with structure, with *limits*.

This was different. This was a machine for turning people into currency and currency into armies and armies into more people. The war wasn't conquest. Conquest wanted cities, thrones, tribute — things you could stop wanting once you had enough. This wanted the taking itself. This wanted every ninth summer in the world, every mother's face, every dog named Bristle, fed into the coil, spiraling inward forever toward that patient eye.

*Harvest,* Corvin thought, and the word sat in him like a swallowed stone.

* * *

He got out through the coal chute at the changing of the morning watch, when the guards traded jokes and looked at the gate instead of the yard. He came up gray with coal dust in a hedgerow behind a chandler's shop, and he lay there in the wet grass for a long time, shaking.

Not from fear. He catalogued the feeling carefully, the way you catalogue a wound to know if it needs binding. Fear lived in the belly and made the hands stupid. This lived higher, behind the eyes, and made the hands *steady*. He recognized it from the worst night of his life — the night he'd stood in a widow's doorway with a repossession writ and put the writ down on her table, unenforced, and let his career burn behind him like a bridge. It was fury. Cold, specific, and — this was the dangerous part — *useful*.

Because he hadn't just memorized shapes. He'd done something else, something he'd told himself he'd never do again, and he'd done it without deciding to.

While the girl read the manifests, Corvin had listened past her voice, the way the guild trains its enforcers to listen — because contracts carry echoes, because every transaction leaves a residue in the room where it's counted. He'd sworn off that trick along with everything else. But the room had been so full of taken things that the echoes came through whether he listened or not, like noise through a wall.

He had heard a river. An older brother laughing. A dog barking at gulls. Small, bright, ordinary things — a child's whole world in miniature — sounding faintly, terribly, from inside a lead-lined drawer, from inside a coin already stamped and sleeved and priced at eight months' pay.

Private Dannel Voss, Third March Regiment, had sold his ninth summer to the crown of King Aldous, and it had cried all night in a provisioning office in Karst Hollow, and no one had heard it but a disgraced enforcer lying in the rafters with his fists clenched so hard his nails left crescents in his palms.

* * *

By full light Corvin was three streets away, washed to the elbows in a public fountain, sitting on the steps of a shuttered shrine with a cup of bad coffee and a plan assembling itself in his head with terrible speed.

The free cities needed to know. That was the first thing, the obvious thing — but the obvious thing was also the useless thing, because he'd heard what happened to messengers. A fishwife in Vaelmoor's market had shouted the truth about the harvest processions and been collected by noon, smiling too widely, forgetting her own stall by suppertime. Truth without proof was just noise, and the usurper's machinery ate noise for breakfast.

Proof, then. Proof meant the ledger. The actual ledger, with Ellery's handwriting in it, and a sleeve of bone-coins, and a manifest in a frightened girl's voice describing a river, a brother, a dog named Bristle. Proof meant going back into Karst Hollow, which meant the watchman's schedule, which meant the coal chute, which meant doing it again and again until he had enough — and every return doubled the chance that the eye in the spiral would turn and look at him.

Unless someone else carried it. Unless the right people carried it.

Corvin stared into his coffee and admitted the thought he'd been circling since the rafters, the one shaped like a woman with ink-stained fingers and a bone-carver's patience and a way of looking at him across a harbor tavern as if he were a contract she intended to renegotiate.

Mirabel Joubert heard the dead. She carved their voices into whalebone and kept the Exchange's impossible accounts. If anyone in the free cities could take a stolen summer out of a lead-lined box and make it testify — make Dannel Voss's ninth year stand up in front of the guildmasters and the city councils and say *this is what they took from me* — it was her.

And if the Exchange refused to hear it, if the masters voted their usual neutrality while children's summers were minted into marching money — well. Corvin had been cast out of that institution once for a single act of conscience. He found, testing the feeling like a sore tooth, that he was almost looking forward to burning the whole rotten house down with him.

He drained the coffee, stood, and rolled his shoulders until they cracked.

Three days to reach the city where Mirabel worked, if the roads behaved. Less if they didn't and he cut cross-country through the marsh, which they wouldn't, and he would.

Behind him, the shuttered shrine's little bell stirred in the wind — a dead god's bell, silent for a generation, ringing once as if clearing its throat.

Corvin glanced back at it, frowning, and told himself it was only the wind.

It rang again.
