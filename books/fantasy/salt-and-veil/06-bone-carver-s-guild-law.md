## 6. Bone-Carver's Guild Law

The counting house smelled of tallow and old bone and the faint mineral sweetness of whale-ivory shavings, and on the morning of the Conclave it smelled, for the first time in Mirabel Joubert's memory, of fear.

She noticed it as she climbed the stairs from her workshop, her ledger satchel heavy against her hip. Fear had a scent here — she had learned that much in eleven years of keeping accounts for the Veiled Exchange. It was the smell of clerks who had stopped humming. Of ink pots left uncapped because hands were shaking too badly to bother with tidiness. Of the junior scriveners whispering over their desks instead of singing the tally-songs that made arithmetic bearable.

Three days ago, a broker named Halloway had walked into the Vaelmoor branch office and asked to withdraw his own mother's name from the guild's holdings — a name he had sold twenty years ago to pay a gambling debt, a name that had sat in a locked drawer ever since, doing nothing, harming no one. The clerk on duty had opened the drawer and found the deed blank. Not torn out. Not crossed through. *Blank*, as though the vellum itself had forgotten what it once held. And when they checked the master registry, every name the guild held in trust for the city of Vaelmoor had gone the same way — not stolen, not burned, simply *unwritten*, the ink lifted off the page like frost melting off a window.

Something was eating the guild's inventory. Something older than ink.

Mirabel felt it in her bones, too — literally in her bones, in the half-carved reliquary of a drowned fisherman that sat on her bench downstairs. For two days the relic had been silent. She would press her thumb into the groove where his last breath should have been and feel nothing but cold material, dead as any ordinary carving. The voices did not go quiet all at once. They went quiet the way birds go quiet before a storm: one at a time, then all together, then a silence so complete it rang.

Now the masters had called a Conclave, and every keeper, broker, and carver of rank in the free cities was summoned to the Round Chamber beneath the counting house, where the guild's founding contracts hung in glass cases like saints' bones.

Mirabel took her place along the lower arc of benches, among the keepers. Above her, tiered in a crescent, sat the nine masters in their grey-and-bone robes, each wearing the small carved token of their office at the throat — a scale, a quill, a key, a closed hand. Master Ondrell of the High Tally presided, an old man whose voice had the dry authority of a document read aloud in court. His token was the closed hand.

"Bring the record," Ondrell said, and the day began.

* * *

They heard reports first, because the Exchange always heard reports first; feeling came later, if there was time, and there never was.

A broker from Saltreach reported that prayers offered at the old shrine of the Tide-Mother were being answered — bread multiplying in poorhouses, storms turning aside from fishing fleets — and that the faithful were leaving offerings the guild could not price, because the offerings were gratitude, and gratitude had no exchange rate. A factor from the inland roads reported that King Aldous's provisioning offices were buying something new at the border markets: not grain, not iron, but *remembering* — childhoods, first loves, the faces of the dead — purchased from desperate soldiers at rates that should have been insulting and yet found sellers anyway. A keeper from the capital reported, in a voice that shook only slightly, that the palace had begun requesting guild relics by catalog number. Requesting them *by name*. As though someone behind the throne knew exactly what the Exchange kept, and wanted it counted.

Through all of it, Mirabel sat with her hands folded over her ledger satchel and listened to the thing beneath the words.

Her gift — her inheritance, her family's strange and terrible ear — let her hear the dead through the relics she carved. But it had always given her more than that. It gave her the undertone of every room, the residue of strong feeling soaked into wood and stone and bone. And the Round Chamber this morning was saturated. Underneath the procedural calm, under the rustle of pages and the measured cadences of the masters, she could hear what the chamber itself remembered of every Conclave held here in three hundred years — and it had never sounded like this. Conclaves smelled of ambition usually, or greed, occasionally of grief. This one smelled of men and women trying very hard not to think about a word none of them would say aloud.

The word was *complicity*.

When the reports ended, Ondrell set down his reading-glass and said, "We come now to the matter before us. The crown of Aldous. The disturbances in our holdings. The — irregularities." He said the last word carefully, the way a man steps over a corpse in the road without looking down. "The question put to this Conclave is the oldest question the Exchange knows. When powers contend above us, what does the Exchange do?"

He paused. He did not need to answer himself. Every person in the chamber knew the answer, because the answer was carved into the lintel above the counting house door in letters a foot high:

*THE VEIL TRADES WITH ALL. THE VEIL SERVES NONE.*

"Neutrality," Ondrell said, "is not cowardice. Neutrality is the load-bearing wall of everything we have built. Kings rise, kings fall, gods wake and sleep again, and through it all the ledgers balance, because we do not take sides. I propose the Conclave affirm standing policy: trade continues with the crown of Aldous, with the free cities, with whatever power emerges from the present troubles. Our brokers will honor existing contracts. Our carvers will fill existing orders. We are the riverbed, my colleagues, not the flood."

There was a murmur of assent around the upper tiers. Mirabel heard relief in it — real, human, shameful relief, the sound of people who had been afraid and were now being told they were allowed to stop being afraid.

She stood up.

It took her a moment to realize she had done it, because her heart was pounding so hard she could barely hear anything else. But she was on her feet, and forty faces were turning toward her, and Master Ondrell's eyebrows had risen into two pale arcs of mild surprise.

"Keeper Joubert," he said. "You have the floor, briefly."

What she had prepared, walking up the stairs that morning, had been careful. Measured. A keeper's argument, built of precedent and clause. But standing there under the glass cases with the founding contracts hanging in them — the actual signatures of the first carvers, people who had bound themselves to hold the line between gods and mortals so that ordinary people could live unhaunted lives — what came out of her mouth was not careful at all.

"I can't vote for neutrality," she said. "I won't."

A stillness went through the chamber, the particular stillness of an institution hearing something it has decided in advance not to hear.

"The godling in the king's crown eats inherited memory," Mirabel said. "That is not rumor anymore. I have held a relic from the palace. I have heard it. It recites genealogies backward, master — it is *drinking* them. Whole lines of descent, going down its throat generation by generation. And where does it feed?" She turned slowly, taking in the tiers. "Look at the reports we just heard. Soldiers selling their childhoods at the border markets — through *our* brokers' rate sheets. Names going blank in our drawers. Prayers being answered and debts being called in, and every transaction in between passing through our ledgers, taking our commission. We are not the riverbed." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "We are the *gullet*. We are the pipe it feeds through, and neutrality is just the polite word we use so we don't have to watch what goes down us."

"Keeper Joubert—" Ondrell began.

"You want precedent? Here is your precedent." She pulled the ledger satchel open and drew out the oldest book she carried, a contract-copy three centuries old, its margins annotated in her grandmother's hand. "The Founders didn't write *the veil trades with all*. That came later, when trading got comfortable. The Founders wrote that the veil *holds*. That someone must stand between the hungry things and the living, and that the Exchange exists to be that someone — to keep account of what crosses, so that nothing crosses unseen. Neutrality between a wolf and a flock isn't virtue. It's just deciding which side of the fence you'd rather stand on while you count the sheep going missing."

Silence. In the undertone of the room, Mirabel heard the old chamber itself seem to lean in — three hundred years of Conclaves holding their breath.

Master Vessica of the Bone Registry spoke next, an austere woman whose token was the key. "Your sentiment does you credit, Keeper. Your arithmetic does not. If we refuse the crown's business, the crown takes its business elsewhere — or takes our offices, our archives, our *carvers*. You propose to make the Exchange a combatant in a war between a godling and whoever opposes it. Do you truly believe we would survive that?"

"I believe we won't survive *this*," Mirabel said. "Not as what we're supposed to be. Master, I carve the dead. I have spent my whole life listening to what people leave behind. And I am telling you that what is happening in Vaelmoor right now — the names going blank, the deeds unwritten — that is not a market fluctuation. That is people being *erased*. And every one of those erased people passed through a contract we hold. When the accounting comes — and it always comes, that is the one law older than the guild — we will be on the wrong side of the ledger."

"Then let the accounting come," said Master Corvane of the High Tally's second chair, a fat, comfortable man with a broker's smile. "And let it find us having kept the ledgers balanced. Keeper, you are a bone-carver of rare gift, and everyone in this room knows it. But gift is not judgment. You hear the dead. You do not speak for the living, and the living — the living need the trade to continue. Widows eat because we buy borrowed years. Debtors walk free because we hold their names in trust rather than letting creditors take their flesh. Pull the wall down and see how long the roof shelters anyone."

"And if the roof is built on top of a mouth?"

The words came out quieter than she intended, and somehow that was worse. The silence afterward had texture to it, the silence of a wound.

Ondrell let it sit for a long moment. Then he said, gently, in the tone of a man closing a ledger, "The motion is called. All in favor of affirming standing policy — trade continues with all parties, without exception, without inquiry."

Hands rose along the upper tiers. One after another after another. Grey sleeves, bone tokens, faces Mirabel had known for years — had drunk wine with, had mourned with, had carved funeral pieces for their parents. Eight hands. Nine.

"Noted," Ondrell said. "The policy stands. And Keeper Joubert—" he looked down at her, and there was no cruelty in his face, which somehow made it unbearable, "—the Conclave notes your objection for the record. It also notes that a keeper who publicly impugns the Exchange's character cannot in conscience continue to speak for it in matters of external brokerage. Your commission as senior keeper is suspended. You retain your workshop, your carving rights, and your keys to the archive, per guild law. You are — reassigned. To internal records."

Internal records. The basement. The place where keepers went to be forgotten politely.

"Thank you, Master," Mirabel heard herself say, because eleven years of institutional habit dies hard, and then she gathered her satchel and sat back down and watched the rest of the Conclave proceed through agenda items twelve through nineteen as though nothing had happened at all.

* * *

Afterward, the chamber emptied the way water empties from a lock — quickly, in order, everyone careful not to look at anyone. Mirabel stayed until the last robe had swished up the stairs, and then she sat alone in the Round Chamber with the glass cases and the founding contracts and the great carved lintel motto that had been moved indoors a century ago because the weather was wearing it away.

*The veil trades with all.*

She wondered, looking at it, whether the Founders had ever actually said that, or whether some comfortable century had chiseled it and let everyone assume. She thought about opening the cases. She had keys. Guild law had seen fit to leave her keys even as it took her title — perhaps as a courtesy, perhaps as an insult, perhaps because some part of the institution understood, in the dumb loyal way institutions understand things, that the keys mattered more than the title.

She did not open them. Not yet. Opening them was a step you took once, knowing you could never take it back, and she wanted to walk out of this building first and feel the ordinary air and decide, in the open world, whether she was ready to become the kind of person who broke into her own guild's archive.

In the undertone of the empty chamber, the old stones murmured their three hundred years of Conclaves at her. She listened the way she listened to everything now — half attention, half dread — and caught, threaded through the sediment of old arguments and old votes, something she had not heard before. Faint. Recent. A voice pressed close against the stone the way a man presses his ear to a door.

Not a dead voice. A *hidden* one. Someone else had stayed behind in these Conclades before, alone, listening to the room. Someone who had objected once, long ago, and been demoted, and kept their keys, and waited.

Mirabel stood, slung the satchel across her body, and climbed the stairs into the grey afternoon light of the counting house floor. The clerks had gone home. The ink pots sat capped and tidy. On the message desk by the door, amid the routine correspondence, lay a single slip of guild paper with no sender's mark, addressed in a cramped, hurried hand:

*Keeper Joubert — You are not the first to say it in that room. Come to the drowned archive under the first shrine when you are ready to learn why the Founders really bound the veil. Bring your keys. Do not bring your ledger. Some accounts are kept off the books.*
