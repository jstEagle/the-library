## 7. The First Free City to Fall

The news reached Corvin in a tavern called the Drowned Bell, three days after Vaelmoor had already fallen, which was — he would think later — the whole of the story in miniature. He was always three days late. He had been three days late for the widow Marta Henn when the enforcers came to repossess her borrowed year, and he had been three days late for his own honor, which had died somewhere between the guildhall door and the harbor stones where he'd vomited up his oath along with the cheap wine.

"Vaelmoor opened its gates," said the man who told him, a salt-merchant with hands like knotted rope and the particular excitement of a man delivering news that is not his to grieve. "No siege. No battle. The magistrates just — handed over the keys. Said it was better this way. Said the king's terms were generous."

Corvin turned his cup on the scarred wood. Generous. He knew what generous meant in the language of contracts. Generous meant the other party hadn't read the fine print, or couldn't, or had read it and understood that refusal was priced higher than surrender. He had spent eleven years enforcing the Veiled Exchange's terms. He knew every euphemism the guild used for taking things from people who could not afford to keep them: *reclamation*, *recourse*, *equitable adjustment*. And now there was a new word drifting up from the capital, soft as ashfall: *contribution*.

"What were the terms?" he asked.

The merchant shrugged, pleased to have an audience. "No burning. No looting. No executions. The army walks in, the army feeds them through winter, and everyone signs a little paper. A census paper, they're calling it. Just names and histories. Where you were born, who raised you, what you remember of your mother's kitchen." He laughed. "Can you imagine? Asking grown men and women what they remember of their mothers' kitchens. Like a schoolmaster's game."

Corvin did not laugh. Something cold had begun walking up his spine, one vertebra at a time, the way dread always walked when it came to him — patiently, like a creditor who knows the debt is good.

"And the people signed?"

"Everyone signed." The merchant drained his cup and signaled for another. "Why wouldn't they? War's expensive. Peace is cheap. That's what my father always said." He wiped his mouth. "Cheap peace," he repeated, savoring it, and Corvin looked at the man's open, guileless face and thought: *you would sign too. You'd sign anything they put in front of you if the ink smelled like bread.*

He left before the second cup arrived. Outside, the harbor fog had come in thick off the water, beading on his beard, muffling the bell-buoys until they sounded like something drowning very far away. He stood in it a long moment, listening, though he couldn't have said for what. Then he went to find a horse, because Vaelmoor was four days' ride north and he was already three days late, and some animal part of him — older than his disgrace, older than his drinking — insisted that late was not the same as never.

* * *

He crested the ridge above Vaelmoor at dusk on the fourth day, and the first thing he noticed was that the city was lit like a festival.

That was wrong. Cities that surrendered did not light themselves like festivals; they shuttered their windows and banked their fires and waited in the dark to learn what surrender cost. But Vaelmoor blazed. Lanterns hung from every balcony along the riverfront. Music drifted up the hill to meet him — fiddles, actually, and singing, many voices, cheerful and slightly too loud, the way crowds sing when they are trying to convince themselves. Torches moved in the streets below in orderly processions, and above it all rose the walls, unbroken, the gates standing open like a held breath let out.

Corvin sat his horse in the shadow of a wayshrine — an old one, pre-Imperial, its stone god worn faceless by centuries of rain — and watched, and felt the cold thing on his spine grow teeth.

There were soldiers in the streets. Usurper's regulars, in the king's gray-and-gold, but they weren't patrolling. They weren't even armed, most of them. They stood at intervals along the procession routes holding lanterns, and their faces —

He spurred down the hill before he could finish the thought, because finishing it would mean turning around, and some stubborn drowned thing in him refused.

At the gate, no one stopped him. That was wrong too. A border city under occupation should have choked on checkpoints. Instead a single bored sergeant glanced at his travel-stained coat, saw nothing worth seeing, and waved him through with a smile. It was the smile that undid Corvin a little, right at the start. Too wide. Too long. It stayed on the sergeant's face after the reason for it had passed, the way a lamp stays lit a moment after the wick is pinched, and then it didn't so much fade as get put away, deliberately, like a tool returned to a box.

Inside, the festival was real enough. Tables in the square, actual food — he smelled roast meat, real meat, the first he'd smelled in weeks — and people eating and laughing and dancing with their children on their shoulders. An old woman pressed a heel of bread into his hand as he passed, beaming. "From the king's own stores!" she said. "They're feeding us all winter! Can you believe it? After all the fright?" She squeezed his arm with surprising strength. "You look half-starved, love. Eat. Eat. There's plenty."

Plenty. In a border city that had lived one bad harvest from famine for as long as Corvin had been alive. Plenty, out of the usurper's granaries, distributed free, three days after surrender.

He ate the bread. He hated himself for it. It was good bread.

He worked the crowd the way he'd once worked taverns hunting debtors, quiet, unhurried, asking questions that sounded like small talk. Where were the magistrates? Retired comfortably to their country estates, someone said. Where were the garrison? Disbanded with full pay. And the papers — the census papers — what happened at the signing?

Here the answers got strange.

"Oh, it was lovely," said a cooper's wife, her eyes shining. "They set up tables in the temple square, white cloths, inkwells, clerks with such kind voices. They asked you about your life. Your whole life. Your mother's kitchen, your first kiss, the day your daughter was born —" she laughed, a bright glassy sound — "and you told them everything, and they wrote it all down, and then they gave you a token. A little bone counter, see?" She held one up: a disc of pale carved bone on a cord, stamped with the king's seal. "You wear it and the soldiers know you've contributed. You're *counted*. My husband cried, he was so happy. We'd never been counted before. Nobody ever asked us about our lives before."

"And afterward," Corvin said carefully, "you gave them the paper, and they gave you the token, and —"

"And that was that." She smiled her too-wide smile. "That was that," she said again, softer, as if reassuring herself, and behind her eyes, for half a heartbeat, Corvin saw something flicker that was not happiness at all. It looked like a woman standing in a house where all the furniture had been removed, remembering that there had been furniture, unable to say what kind.

"The harvest moon's early this year," he said, testing.

She blinked. "Is it? I don't — I never much followed the moons." Her brow furrowed, a tiny crease, gone instantly. "My mother used to know all the moons' names," she added suddenly, wistfully, staring past him. "I can't — isn't that funny. I can't remember her face, but I remember she knew the moons." She touched her bone token, and the furrow smoothed away, and the wide smile came back like a tide. "Anyway. Eat! There's plenty!"

Corvin walked away with the bread going stale in his fist and his heart hammering a slow sick rhythm. *Nobody ever asked us about our lives before.* They'd asked. Oh, they'd asked. And the people had answered, gladly, gratefully, pouring their childhoods into clerks' ledgers like wine into a cup — and the ledgers had kept the vintage, and the people had been left with the empty vessel and a bone token and the vague sense of having been blessed.

He thought of Mirabel Joubert, the bone-carver with the haunted eyes who had come to find him in the Drowned Bell, talking about blank pages in impossible ledgers, about debts being called in. He had drunk too much that night and told her the guild's contracts were just paper, that paper couldn't hurt anyone who didn't sign. He remembered her looking at him with something like pity.

*Paper can't hurt anyone,* he'd said. He wanted to find the man he'd been five days ago and break his nose.

* * *

He found the procession on his second night in the city, and by then he understood what he was looking at, and understanding made it no more survivable.

It came down the Processional Way at moonrise — of course at moonrise, everything here happened at hours chosen for beauty, for ceremony, for the softening of horror into spectacle. Drums first, deep and slow, not martial but almost lullaby-slow. Then ranks of the gray-and-gold, unarmed, carrying lanterns. Then the wagons.

There were twelve of them. Long low carts draped in white cloth, each drawn by six horses, and on each cart stood a kind of booth or cabinet, tall as two men, built of dark wood and — Corvin's stomach dropped — bone. Carved bone panels, thousands of them, fitted together in patterns that hurt to look at directly, whorls and spirals that seemed to turn slowly when he wasn't watching. The cabinets glowed faintly from within, a warm amber light, like hearthlight, like candlelight in a childhood window, and the crowd along the street pressed forward toward that glow with expressions of naked longing.

And walking beside the wagons, hand in hand with the soldiers, laughing and waving to their neighbors, came the contributors.

They climbed the little steps into the cabinets willingly. That was the part Corvin's mind kept snagging on, the splinter he couldn't work loose: they *climbed in willingly*. A baker, flour still ghosting his sleeves, kissed his wife and stepped up and ducked inside the glowing cabinet, and the crowd cheered. A girl of maybe sixteen went in next, blowing kisses to her friends. An old man needed help with the steps and a soldier gave it, gently, courteously, and the old man patted the soldier's cheek like a grandson's.

The doors closed. The glow brightened. And Corvin, standing in the shadow of a shuttered chandler's shop with his hood up and his hand white-knuckled on a knife he had no intention of using against lantern-bearers, heard — or thought he heard, or perhaps the bone in those cabinets simply carried sound the way Mirabel's relics carried voices — a sound like a long slow sigh. Like a city exhaling. Like ten thousand small things being poured out all at once, and somewhere beneath the sigh, so faint he might have invented it, a voice reciting names. Backward. Genealogies unwinding in reverse, child to parent to grandparent, generation by generation, drinking itself down the family trees of Vaelmoor.

When the doors opened, the people who stepped out were smiling.

They were clean, calm, beautifully at ease. They descended the steps to applause and embraced their families, and their families embraced them back, and only slowly did Corvin begin to see the wrongness spreading through the reunions like oil through water. The baker's wife searched her husband's face and her smile faltered — she was looking at a stranger wearing her husband's body, a pleasant stranger who returned her embrace with perfect warmth and no recognition whatsoever. The old man who'd needed help with the steps stood in the street turning in a slow circle, looking at the houses, the faces, the festival lights, with the mild polite interest of a traveler in a foreign town. Someone's mother. Someone's mother was calling to him from a doorway, and he inclined his head to her graciously, the way one does to a friendly innkeeper whose name one has forgotten.

A boy of eight or nine tugged the girl's sleeve — the sixteen-year-old, the one who'd blown kisses. "Sera. Sera, it's me. Tam. We pick apples together, we —"

Sera looked down at him with her wide bright smile. "Hello," she said kindly. "Aren't you sweet." And she stepped over his outstretched hand as if it were a root in her path, and joined the others being led away toward the barracks quarter, where the gray-and-gold had quarters ready for them, comfortable ones, the merchant had said. Resettlement housing. For the counted.

Tam stood in the street. He did not cry. That frightened Corvin more than crying would have. The boy just stood there among the cheering crowd, watching his sister walk away, and his face was doing arithmetic — the terrible arithmetic of a child realizing that the adults have all agreed on something insane and he is the only one who remembers the world as it was.

Corvin moved without deciding to move. Later he would try to reconstruct the decision and fail; there was no decision, only the sight of that boy's face and the sudden absolute knowledge that he was not going to stand in another doorway three days late. He crossed the street, took the boy's shoulder — the child flinched, then clung, fingers digging in hard enough to bruise — and murmured, "Walk with me. Don't look at her. Walk."

"I'm not supposed to talk to strangers," the boy whispered. Automatic. Rote. And then, with a crack in it: "She doesn't know me. Nobody knows me. Mama looked at me yesterday like — like I was a lodger. Papa signed last week and now he calls me 'little sir.'" His breath hitched. "Everyone got counted except me. I'm too young to count. I'm the only one who —"

"Who remembers." Corvin's voice came out gravel. "Yes. I know."

"They think I'm lying." The boy's grip tightened. "They think I make things up for attention. Grandma's face is *gone*, mister, from my mama's head, and when I say her name Mama gets this look like I've said something dirty, and then she smiles —" he mimicked it, horribly, the too-wide smile — "and stops talking about it."

The procession rolled on behind them, wagon after wagon, cabinet after cabinet, the sighing sound continuing, the backward genealogies drinking and drinking. Corvin walked the boy out of the festival district with his heart in his throat and his mind working cold and fast, the old enforcer's machinery grinding back to life after two years of rust and wine. Curfew was midnight. The gates — he'd checked, obsessively, the way a man checks exits — the gates were open till dawn for festival traffic, lightly guarded, because who would flee a city that was feeding them? Who would run from plenty?

Someone who understood what plenty cost. Someone who still remembered his mother's kitchen.

Getting a child out was harder than getting himself out; he'd planned for one silhouette, not two. But the guards at the River Gate were counting wagons, not children, and Corvin had spent eleven years learning how enforcement worked from the inside, which meant he knew exactly how it failed. He walked the boy out through the fish-market lane with a borrowed basket on the child's hip and a story ready about a father waiting beyond the walls, and the guard glanced at them, smiled his too-wide smile, and waved them through into the dark.

Only when the walls were a mile behind them and the road bent north into the hills did Corvin let himself stop shaking.

The boy — Tam, his name was Tam, Tam of Vaelmoor, nine years old, and Corvin made himself repeat it like a ledger entry, because names were the one thing those cabinets could not take if the living refused to surrender them — Tam sat on a milestone in the starlight and finally, quietly, began to cry. Not loudly. Almost politely. As if he'd learned already that grief in Vaelmoor was considered bad manners.

Corvin crouched in front of him. "Listen to me. Everything in your head — your sister, your grandmother, your mother's face before it changed, all of it — that's the last copy in the world. Do you understand? Everyone else's was taken. Yours wasn't. That makes you the most valuable thing in this whole war, and there are people who will want to finish the job the cabinets started, and other people who will want to use you, and I am going to get you to both of them before either happens, do you hear me?"

The boy sniffed, wiped his nose on his sleeve, and looked at Corvin with red-rimmed eyes that were, underneath everything, appallingly steady. "The bone lady," he said.

Corvin went still. "What?"

"There's stories. Before. About a bone-carver in the free cities who keeps the dead safe in her carvings, so nobody can take them or change them." Tam's chin lifted. "If she carved my sister's name in bone, would it stay? Even if Sera forgets? Would the bone remember for her, until she comes back?"

Corvin thought of Mirabel Joubert in the Drowned Bell, her ink-stained fingers, her voice like a blade wrapped in cloth: *the ledgers are opening, Corvin. Something is erasing entries. Something older than ink.* He had told her she was mad. He had told her paper couldn't hurt anyone.

"Yes," he said. "I think it would. I think that's exactly what she does." He stood, knees cracking, and looked north, where the road ran toward the free cities and the counting house and the woman he'd dismissed as a frightened clerk. Behind him, far off, Vaelmoor glowed against the sky, still celebrating, still singing, still giving itself away wagon-load by wagon-load to the hungry thing in the king's crown. Somewhere in that glow was a girl named Sera who no longer knew her brother's name, and a city full of mothers whose faces had been filed away in someone else's archive.

"We walk tonight and every night," he said. "You'll tell me everything you remember — all of it, every detail, your sister's laugh, your grandmother's hands, the smell of your mother's kitchen. You'll tell it until you're sick of telling it. Because words are the first carving, boy, and I'm done arriving three days late."

Tam stood and took his hand without being asked.

Far to the north, past the hills, past the border, a ledger was waiting to be written in — and for the first time in two years, Corvin of the Broken Oath knew precisely what he was carrying, and to whom, and why.
