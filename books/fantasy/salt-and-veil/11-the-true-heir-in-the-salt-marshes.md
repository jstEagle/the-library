## 11. The True Heir in the Salt Marshes

The ledger did not want to be read.

Mirabel had felt resistance in documents before — a contract stiffened with guilt, a deed that seemed to shrink from her fingers as though the paper itself were ashamed. But the founding folio of the Veiled Exchange's southern annex resisted her the way a drowning man resists the hand that would pull him under: not with shame but with desperation.

She sat in the counting house long after the clerks had gone, one lamp trimmed low, her grandmother's magnifying lens balanced on her nose. The page before her was a transfer of claim, dated thirty-one years past, written in the careful copperplate of a broker named Hessel Vane — dead now, eleven years dead, and she had carved his last breath herself, a small sour thing like vinegar held too long on the tongue.

*Item: one (1) claim of succession to the throne of Aldermere and its dependencies, being the legitimate inheritance of Isolde, daughter of King Roderic the Second, surrendered voluntarily by the bearer's lawful regent.*

*Surrendered voluntarily.* Mirabel ran her thumb along the words until the ink smudged faintly grey beneath her skin. She had spent three nights tracing this entry backward through the ledgers — through the annex records, through the escrow rolls, through a chain of shell contracts so convoluted it could only have been designed by someone paid by the clause. And at every turn, the same signature anchored the whole rotten architecture: *Regent Maurel Dossin, acting for the Crown-in-minority.*

Dossin. She knew the name from history books, from the polite biographies that called him *the Steadying Hand*, the regent who had governed through the plague years and then gracefully retired to his estates when young Aldous came of age. Gracefully retired. Paid off, more likely. Bought and filed away like any other commodity that passed across the Exchange's counters.

The price of the claim was listed at the bottom of the page, and Mirabel had read it a dozen times hoping she had misread it.

*Consideration received: two hundred crowns, one annuity of forty crowns per annum payable to the said Regent for life, and forgiveness of certain debts outstanding against the estate of the late King Roderic.*

Two hundred crowns. A kingdom for less than the cost of a harbor warehouse. She had seen widows' borrowed years sell for more. She had seen a fisherman's name — his true name, the one his mother whispered over his cradle — go for three hundred and a pardon.

The lamp guttered. In the bone box at her elbow, the relics she carried shifted with a sound like dry leaves, and one of them — the broker Vane's breath, which she had brought along because dead men who wrote such contracts sometimes remembered things worth hearing — pressed warm against her palm.

She unstoppered it and set it on the desk. The little spiral of whalebone caught the lamplight, and the voice came up out of it thin and reedy, the way Vane had sounded at the end, when the accounts of his own life were being tallied.

"You'll want the annex," the dead broker murmured. "You'll want the annex, you'll want the annex—"

"I have the annex," Mirabel told him patiently. It was an old habit, talking to her carvings. Sefa thought it was funny; Corvin thought it was unsettling, which she privately enjoyed. "I need what's *under* the annex."

A pause, the way there is always a pause with the dead, as though they must cross a room to reach the answer. Then, slyly: "Under everything is the marsh ledger. Under everything is where we put the things we didn't want priced twice."

The marsh ledger. Of course. Every guildhouse kept its public books and its private ones, but the southern annex — the one that served the salt provinces, the drowned lands, the places where people went to stop being who they were — kept a third book besides. The *omissions* ledger. The record of what had been deliberately left out of the other two. Mirabel had heard of it only once, years ago, from a dying clerk who had gripped her wrist with surprising strength and said, *If they ever ask you about the marsh, girl, tell them the tide table lies.*

She closed her hand around Vane's breath and thanked him, and the relic went cool and quiet, satisfied, the way the dead were when their unfinished business finally found ears.

Outside the counting house window, the free city slept under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Somewhere east, beyond the mountains, Vaelmoor's empty streets stood open to the wind, and hollow men drilled on its walls with vacant eyes. Somewhere south, in the salt marshes, a woman who did not know she was a princess hauled nets or raked salt pans or did whatever salt-farmers did, while the crown that should have been hers ate memories in the capital and grew fat.

Mirabel blew out the lamp. She had a tide table to consult, and a clerk's warning to test, and a long ride ahead of her.

* * *

The tide table lied, exactly as the dead clerk had promised.

It showed the causeway to Tarnhallow — the mudflat village at the end of the salt roads — as passable at mid-morning on the third day after the new moon. What it did not show was the spring tide running a hand's breadth higher than any table accounted for, water sliding brown and muscular over the flats, erasing the marked channel, swallowing the guide-stakes one by one. Mirabel stood hip-deep in the flooding shallows with her horse snorting beside her and understood, with the particular clarity of a woman up to her ribs in cold seawater, that someone had wanted this place hard to reach, and had arranged it through the most respectable instrument available: a falsified document, filed in good faith, in a guild office.

Even the sea kept guild books here.

She got herself and the horse onto the shingle bank above the flats, soaked to the sternum, and sat wringing out her skirts while gulls screamed their opinions overhead. Ahead, half a mile across the remaining marsh, Tarnhallow crouched among the saltings like a handful of grey pebbles: low houses roofed with turf, drying-racks hung with kelp, salt pans glinting in the afternoon light like spilled coins. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, straight and unhurried. No watchtower. No banner. No sign anywhere that the woman who lived here had once been second in line to a throne that now devoured its own people.

Mirabel walked the rest of the way, leading the horse, her boots making sucking sounds in the mud, and had ample time to think about what she would say.

That was the problem with justice, she reflected. It arrived loudly. It announced itself. *I know who you are. I know what was taken from you. Come and claim it* — and behind those words, always, the fine print: *and be hunted, and be used, and be traded again by whoever holds your claim next.* She had watched brokers do it for years. A person's misfortune entered into evidence, and evidence became leverage, and leverage became coin. She refused — she *refused* — to walk into Tarnhallow sounding like a broker.

So she decided, wading the last channel with her bundle of relics held high above the water, that she would not announce anything at all. She would buy salt. She would listen. She was, whatever else she was, a bone-carver, and bone-carvers knew how to wait for a shape to show itself inside the material before ever setting blade to it.

The first house at the edge of the village had a rack of fish skeletons hung beside the door — cod spines, skate wings, the delicate ladders of ribs — and Mirabel stopped and looked at them the way another traveler might stop at a shrine. Someone here worked bone. Someone here kept the old craft, the humble end of it, the needles and awls and net-gauges that bone-carvers made when no one needed their ghosts.

An old man mending a net in the doorway followed her gaze and grinned through a beard like salt-crusted rope. "Looking for Marren? She's down at the pans. Tell her Tam says if she wants the good driftwood she'd best come before the tide takes it again." He squinted at Mirabel's soaked state. "You swum here?"

"Your tide table lied to me."

Tam laughed until he coughed. "Table's been wrong since before my father's day. Marren says it keeps the tax assessors honest — they won't come somewhere the maps say drowns." He jerked his chin toward the pans. "Down past the white posts. Mind you don't step in the brine-pits. Takes a week to get the smell out."

The salt pans stretched along the shore in shallow terraces, each one a mirror of pewter sky, and the women working them moved between the mirrors with their trousers rolled to the knee and their sleeves bound back, raking crystals into long pale windrows. They sang as they worked — a work-song, low and repetitive, the kind of song that exists to keep time and keep thoughts out. Mirabel caught fragments. Something about the moon owing wages. Something about a bride who married the sea and drowned rich.

And one voice, lower than the others, steadier, carrying the melody the way a keel carries cargo.

Mirabel found her without difficulty, because there was nowhere else to look. The woman at the head of the line was perhaps forty, broad-shouldered and sun-brown, with grey coming in early at her temples and hands that looked like they had been carved by someone in a hurry — strong, scarred, economical. She raked salt with the unhurried authority of a person who has done one necessary thing ten thousand times. When she saw Mirabel standing at the pan's edge with a horse's lead in one hand and salt water still dripping from her hem, she stopped raking and simply looked, and her eyes were the color of the deep channel at dusk, and something in Mirabel's chest turned over and went cold.

Because she had seen those eyes before. Not on a living face. On a coin. On the old mintings, the ones from before the usurpation, the ones the Exchange's clerks used as weights because they were struck true. King Roderic's face, worn soft by a century of thumbs — and the same level, unimpressed, faintly sorrowful gaze looking out of a salt-farmer's sunburnt features.

"Tam sent you?" the woman asked. Her voice matched the song. Low. Steady. Cargo-bearing.

"He did. I'm told you're Marren."

"I'm told that too." The rake rested across her shoulders. Around them, the other women had gone quiet, watching with the frank assessment of people who lived where strangers were weather — occasionally interesting, usually trouble. "You're guild."

It wasn't a question. Mirabel glanced down at herself: the ink stains, the calluses, the bone box slung at her hip. "Bone-carver," she said. "Joubert. Out of the free city."

Something moved behind those dusk-colored eyes — quick, submerged, gone. "Joubert," Marren repeated, tasting the name. "Carvers. You're the ones hear the dead."

"Some of us."

"And you came all this way through a lying tide table to buy salt from some of us."

"I came," Mirabel said carefully, "because I've been reading old contracts. Thirty-one years back. Southern annex. There's an omission ledger, and there's an entry in it that shouldn't exist, and the trail ends—" she gestured, taking in the pans, the racks, the low grey village, the woman holding the rake like a tool and not a weapon, though Mirabel suspected it could be either, "—here."

The silence that followed had texture. The wind moved the brine-smell across the pans. Somewhere a curlew cried its two notes, over and over, like a question nobody would answer.

"Girls," Marren said at last, without turning her head. "Finish the west rows. Take your time about it."

The women gathered their rakes and withdrew along the terraces, not hurrying, but not lingering either, and several of them looked back. When they were out of easy earshot, Marren drove the rake tines-down into the salt crust and folded her arms.

"Now," she said. "Say the rest of it plain, bone-carver, because I've had thirty-one years of people almost saying things, and I've buried better liars than the guild ever bred. Say it plain or walk back across your lying flats."

Mirabel had rehearsed gentleness all the way down the salt road. Standing in front of those eyes, she found she no longer had the heart for it. Gentleness would be another kind of filing-away, another way of deciding what this woman could bear to know.

"Your name is Isolde," Mirabel said. "Daughter of Roderic the Second. Your regent sold your succession to the Veiled Exchange for two hundred crowns and a life annuity when you were nine years old. The contract was never voided. It's been sitting in an omission ledger for three decades, and right now it is the single most dangerous piece of paper in the world, because it means the woman wearing the crown in Aldermere is wearing stolen goods — and the proof of the theft has been in our vaults the entire time, filed, indexed, and gathering dust while a god eats the country."

The words came out harder than she intended, and she heard why as they landed: not cruelty, but fury. Fury at the neatness of it. Two hundred crowns. A signature. A shelf. Justice hadn't even been murdered here; it had been *archived*.

Marren — Isolde — did not move for a long moment. Then she looked down at her own hands, the scarred, salt-cured hands, and turned them over as though seeing them newly.

"Nine years old," she said quietly. "I remember being nine. I remember thinking the regent was kind because he let me keep my dog when they moved us out of the palace." A short breath that was almost a laugh. "He let me keep the dog. He sold the throne and let me keep the dog."

"There's more," Mirabel said. "There's a memory in the contract — brokers recorded the signing. I can show you. Through a carving. If you want it."

"Do I want to watch my own childhood being sold?" Isolde asked the sky. The curlew answered. She rubbed her face with both hands, and when they came away she looked older and very tired and not defeated at all. "Aye. I want it. But not here, and not today." She picked up the rake again, and pointed it eastward, toward a house apart from the others, set on a low rise with a windbreak of stunted pines. "Come tomorrow. Bring your box of voices. And bone-carver—" the dusk-colored eyes fixed on her, and Mirabel felt weighed, measured, found worth a second look, "—you said the proof sits in your guild's vaults. Who else knows?"

"The masters voted neutrality last month. Officially, no one is looking for you. Unofficially—" Mirabel hesitated. "Unofficially, the thing wearing the king has started speaking to me through my carvings. It knows I'm tracing the binding. It offered me a bargain. It may already know what I know."

Isolde absorbed this the way she seemed to absorb everything: completely, and without visible disturbance, like a seawall taking a wave.

"Then we'd best not be slow," she said. "Tomorrow, then. And Joubert—" she paused, rake across her shoulders, silhouetted against the burning silver of the pans, "—when you show me that contract, when I see my own name signed away — remind me to thank you for saying it plain. Everyone else in thirty-one years was too polite to tell me what I lost. Politeness is just theft with good manners."

Mirabel walked back toward the village in the long gold light, her heart hammering with something that was not fear, or not only fear. Behind her, the women took up the work-song again, and this time she caught the verse clearly, and it stopped her mid-stride:

*The moon owes wages none can claim,*
*the crown lies drowned beneath the brine,*
*but salt remembers, salt remains,*
*and what was bought will be repined—*

They had been singing it for thirty-one years. Perhaps longer. Songs outlive contracts. Songs are the one ledger no guild can burn.

That night, lodging in Tam's loft with the horse stabled below and the relics quiet in their box, Mirabel took out the one carving she had not yet dared to finish: a blank disc of whalebone she had begun the day she left the city, intending — she had told herself — to record Isolde's testimony when she found her.

Now she understood what the blankness was for. Some shapes you cannot carve until the material consents. Tomorrow, a salt-farmer would sit across from her and decide whether to become a princess again, and whether becoming one meant being sold a second time — this time by the righteous, this time by Mirabel herself, who needed her claim the way the free cities needed a banner.

She turned the disc in her fingers. Tomorrow, then. Say it plain. And pray the plain truth cut cleaner than the pretty lie had.

Far to the east, unseen over the dark hills, the towers of the capital burned their endless lamps — and in the highest of them, something patient recited genealogies backward, and had begun, Mirabel was sure, to say her name.
