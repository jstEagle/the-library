## 13. The Godling Speaks

The trouble with a gift, Mirabel had learned, was that it never asked permission before it grew.

She had been carving since dawn — a small commission, nothing dangerous: a locket of antler bone for a widow in the Saltrow district who wanted to keep her husband's last words close to her throat. Ordinary work. The kind of work that used to feel like tending a garden, each stroke of the burin a small act of listening. She had done ten thousand of these lockets over the years, and the dead man's breath had come into the bone easily enough, a few words about rain and the price of mackerel and *tell her the blue dress*, the ordinary treasures that made up a life once it stopped being lived.

But tonight, as she set down her tools and held the finished piece up to the lamplight, the voice inside it did not stop.

It should have faded. That was how the craft worked: the dead spoke through the carving until they had said what they had carried, and then they went quiet, settling into the bone like sediment into still water. This voice kept going. It spoke about the weather in the capital. It spoke about the taste of pears. It recited, softly, almost tenderly, the names of every Joubert who had ever held a burin — back past her grandmother Odile, past the ones whose graves no one tended anymore, all the way to a woman named Aveline Joubert whose hands had first touched whalebone in a century so old that the calendar itself had been rewritten twice since.

Mirabel set the locket down on the bench very carefully, the way one sets down a cup when one suspects there is something alive in the tea.

"That's not him," she said aloud, to the empty workshop. "The widow's husband didn't know any of this."

*No,* the voice agreed, and it came not from the locket now but from everywhere at once — from the shelf of finished carvings along the north wall, from the bin of raw bone, from the half-worked blanks in their oiled cloth. From the little comb she had carved for Sefa last week, lying on the windowsill where the girl had left it. *He is sleeping. They always sleep, after. I am only borrowing the shape his breath left behind. You understand borrowing. It is your family's oldest trade.*

Every lamp in the workshop seemed to burn a shade lower. Mirabel's hands were steady — years of fine work had taught them steadiness under worse pressures than this — but she felt her pulse in her fingertips, quick and shallow.

"You're the thing in the crown."

*I am the thing the crown wears,* it corrected gently. *There is a difference. The crown is a door. I am what was put behind it. And you are the ear at the keyhole, Mirabel Joubert. You have been the ear since before you were born.*

It knew her name. Of course it knew her name; it had just recited four centuries of her family like a lullaby. But hearing it say *Mirabel*, in a voice assembled from borrowed breaths — part dead broker, part drowned sailor, part, horribly, her grandmother's cadence — made the room tilt.

"I'm not talking to you," she said. She began gathering her tools, an absurd gesture of housekeeping, putting the world back in order. "Whatever you are, you've eaten a king. You're eating a country. I have nothing to trade with you."

*Nothing?* The voice sounded genuinely puzzled, and that was the worst part — it sounded *reasonable*. It had none of the grinding horror she had heard beneath Aldous's voice in the palace relic, none of the backward-recited genealogies, the patient hunger. Or rather, it had those things, but folded underneath, the way kindness can be folded over appetite. *You have spent three weeks digging through drowned archives and sealed contracts looking for me. Do you think I haven't noticed? I notice everything my ears hear. You found the founding contract. You know what the Jouberts are to me.*

"Leashes," she said. "You're bound. We were the binding's ears. That's all."

*Is that what the archive told you?* A pause, and then something almost like laughter moved through the bones on her shelves — a dry rustle, a hundred small voices exhaling at once. *Let me tell you what a leash is, carver. A leash has two ends. Your family held mine for four hundred years. And in exchange, you heard the dead. Every grief given a voice. Every last word delivered. Every mother's message to every child. Do you imagine the guild would have let a bone-carver keep its ledgers if her only talent were craft? You were useful because you were mine, and you were mine because you were useful, and neither of us chose it — but we both profited. That is not a leash. That is a marriage neither party remembers consenting to.*

Mirabel stood very still. The lamplight guttered. Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour — nine of them, she thought, though she could not afterward have sworn to the number — and their sound seemed to pass through the workshop without quite arriving.

"You killed Vaelmoor," she said quietly. "A whole city. Mothers forgot their children's faces. There's a girl asleep upstairs who watched it happen and will carry it her whole life. Explain to me how that's a marriage."

*Explain to me how it isn't arithmetic.* The voice did not harden. That was the terrible thing; it stayed warm, reasonable, the tone of a guild master walking a junior clerk through a difficult entry. *Your King Aldous wanted a war. I gave him the means to pay for it. Would you rather he'd paid in grain and blood? Memories fade, Mirabel. Grief fades. I take what was already leaving. I am the most merciful creditor your species has ever invented, and you would call me a devourer because you have never read the ledgers of famine, plague, and ordinary kings. I have. I wrote some of them.*

"You're in my head," she realized suddenly, coldly. "This conversation — I'm not speaking aloud anymore, am I?"

*You haven't been for some time.* Gentle. Almost apologetic. *Forgive me. Distance is difficult for both of us. You are the closest thing I have had to a conversation in three hundred years. The king does not converse. The king endures.*

She wanted to throw every carving in the workshop into the sea. She understood, distantly, that it would accomplish nothing — that the voice lived wherever her gift lived, which was to say in her own inheritance, her own blood, the thing she had spent her whole life believing was a blessing. She sat down instead, slowly, on her stool, because her knees had opinions of their own.

"What do you want?"

*Ah.* A long silence, and in it she felt something vast turn its attention fully upon her, the way a lighthouse turns, the way winter turns. *I want what any prisoner wants. The door opened. Here is what I know, Mirabel, and weigh it well: my binding can be undone by consent freely given by my host. You discovered this in the drowned archive — yes, I know, I was listening; I am always listening; it is the loneliest thing about me. But consent requires a host who can choose. Aldous cannot choose. He has not truly been present in his own skull for three years. Even if you reached him — even if you carved his childhood from his own bone and held it up to him like a mirror — what remains of him is too worn to want anything. You would be asking a coat to decide whether to leave its hook.*

"But Isolde," Mirabel said. Her mouth had gone dry. "You want Isolde."

*The true heir carries royal blood — the same line that bound me, the same blood that wrote the terms. Blood can stand proxy for blood. That is in the contract too; your ancestors were thorough. One freely given consent from Isolde's lips, spoken at the binding-stone, and the door opens lawfully. No tearing. No war. The hollow regiments wake or they don't, but the harvest stops. Aldous dies — he is mostly dead already, poor man — and Isolde takes a throne that is hers by every measure except the one your guild filed away in a drawer.* The voice softened further, and now it was unmistakably her grandmother's warmth, her grandmother's humor, and Mirabel had to grip the edge of the bench. *And here is my offer, freely made, no trick in the wording — check it against the founding contract if you doubt me, I insist you check: deliver Isolde's consent, and the Joubert line is released. Fully. The leash cut at your end. Your children would carve pretty things and hear nothing. You yourself would keep the gift while you live — I am not cruel — but it ends with you. No more dead crowding your nights. No more ledger of last breaths. No more carrying every grief in the free cities in your two tired hands. You would finally be only a woman, Mirabel. Don't tell me you haven't dreamed of it. I have heard you dream.*

That was true, and she hated that it was true. She had dreamed of silence. In her worst hours — after the third funeral in a month, after the night a drowned child's voice had followed her from the workshop to her bed and whispered *it's dark, it's dark, it's dark* until dawn — she had dreamed of being an ordinary carver of ordinary trinkets, deaf to the dead, light as a woman with empty pockets.

And the godling offered it the way a good broker offers anything: truthfully. She sensed no lie in it. That was the trap's genius — there was no trap in the offer at all. The trap was that everything it said was fair.

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "If I warn her instead? If I tell Isolde exactly what you're asking and why?"

*Then you will have done your duty by her, and I will respect it.* A pause. *And then I will simply wait. Consent can also be manufactured, given enough time and enough despair. Isolde is a salt-farmer with a false name and a buried claim. Despair finds such women eventually. Kings fall on their own schedules. I am very old, Mirabel. I have outlasted every hand that bound me. I can outlast her hope, and yours, and I will grieve doing it — genuinely grieve; I have come to enjoy our conversations — but I will do it.*

The lamps burned low. Somewhere upstairs, Sefa turned over in her sleep, and the floorboard creaked, and the sound was so human, so ordinary, that Mirabel nearly wept.

"Why tell me at all?" she said. "Why show me the honest version? You could have lied. You could have posed as a dead relative, a saint, a friendly ghost. Why give me the choice honestly?"

*Because you are my ears,* the voice said, and for the first time it sounded tired — ancient, oceanic, sad in a way that had nothing to do with performance. *Because everyone who binds a thing forgets that the thing can hear everything the binders say, and no one has spoken to me honestly in four centuries. Because you looked for me in the archives not to destroy me — I felt it — but to understand me, and no one has tried to understand me since Aveline Joubert died with her burin in her hand. And because I think, Mirabel Joubert, that you may be the first keeper worth having. Not a jailer. Not a servant. A keeper. Think on what that word means. Think on who holds the veil, and what it costs, and who else is volunteering.*

Then it was gone. Not dramatically — the way a tide goes out, so gradually that she could not name the moment the bones went silent again. The workshop was only a workshop: shavings, lamplight, the smell of oil and whalebone, the widow's locket gleaming with a dead man's small last words about rain and the blue dress.

Mirabel sat for a long time. Then she took a fresh blank of bone, and her burin, and she did the only thing she knew how to do with unbearable things: she carved. Not testimony, not a ledger entry — a letter. To Isolde, in the salt marshes. Every word the godling had spoken, faithfully rendered, including the offer, including the threat, including the terrible reasonableness of it all. If the veil between gods and mortals was going to run through her, then it would run honest.

She carved until the candle drowned in its own wax, and when the letter was finished she wrapped it in oilcloth and woke Sefa, because the tide turned before dawn and Corvin's boat waited at the pier, and some messages could not wait for morning.

Sefa blinked at her in the lamplight, hair wild, eyes instantly sharp the way they always were — the eyes of a girl who had seen a city forget itself.

"It talked to you," Sefa said. Not a question. "Through the bones."

"Yes."

"Did it lie?"

"No," Mirabel said, and found the truth of it sitting in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. "That's what frightens me."

She took the girl's hand, and together they went down the stairs toward the harbor, the letter between them, and behind them in the dark workshop every carving on every shelf stood silent — listening, waiting, keeping.
