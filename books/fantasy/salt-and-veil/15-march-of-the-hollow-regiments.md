## 15. March of the Hollow Regiments

The first scout came in at dusk, and he would not stop shaking.

Corvin found him in the militia tent at Greywater Ford, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's sweat, a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands. He was young — nineteen, maybe twenty, with the soft cheeks of a boy who had never been hungry — and his eyes kept sliding to the door as though the thing he'd seen might follow him inside.

"Say it again," Corvin said. "Slowly. From the beginning."

The boy's name was Tam. Corvin had learned it an hour ago, along with the names of eleven other scouts, because a man fights better for a commander who knows what to call him when they drag what's left of him off the field. It was one of the few lessons from his enforcer days that translated cleanly to war.

"They were coming down the Salt Road," Tam said. "Maybe four hundred. We counted from the ridge, like you said, and we were going to ride back with the numbers, and then—" His throat worked. "Then one of them looked up. Right at me. Four hundred paces, maybe more, and he looked right at where I was hiding, and he *smiled*, ser. And then he stopped smiling, all at once, like a lamp going out, and kept marching."

"Were you spotted? Did they change course?"

"No. That's the thing." Tam's voice dropped, and Corvin had to lean in to hear him over the rain drumming the canvas. "He saw me. I know he saw me. And then he just... didn't care. Like I was weather. Like I was nothing at all." The boy looked up, and his eyes were wet and wide. "Ser, they weren't carrying food. I checked their column twice. No wagons but the banner-cart. No cookfires. They marched forty miles today through the pass and not one of them stumbled. What kind of men march forty miles on nothing?"

*Men who aren't men anymore,* Corvin thought, and did not say.

What he said was: "You did well, Tam. Get something to eat. Sleep if you can." He knew the boy wouldn't manage either, but the instruction was a kindness, and kindness was rationed these days like everything else.

Outside the tent, the free cities' camp sprawled across the ford in a chaos of canvas and argument. Three weeks ago it had been a mob; two weeks ago, a slightly larger mob; now, under Corvin's grinding daily discipline, it had become something that could generously be called an army — six thousand militia from Vaelmoor's surviving neighbors, farmers and dockhands and caravan guards, armed with whatever the quartermasters could scavenge before the armories burned. They had spears. They had crossbows. They had, in Corvin's estimation, perhaps four days of genuine cohesion in them before fear finished its work.

And fear was working fast. He could hear it in the clusters around the cookfires — voices pitched too high, the same rumors passed hand to hand like contraband: *they don't eat, they don't sleep, they don't bleed right, they took Vaelmoor without a single sword drawn.* Six thousand men and women waiting for the dark to confirm every story.

Corvin pulled his cloak tighter and went to find the captains.

* * *

The council convened in the millhouse, because it was the only building at the ford big enough, and by the time Corvin arrived the argument was already at full boil.

"We should fall back to Merrowgate!" Captain Ossen of the Caravaners was saying, jabbing a thick finger at the map spread across the millstone. "Let them take the ford. Let them stretch their lines. Every league they advance is supply we deny them—"

"They don't *use* supply, you fool," snapped Captain Breka, who commanded the river levies and had lost a brother at Vaelmoor. "That's the entire point. You can't starve them out. You can't outlast them. Every hour we retreat is an hour they gain for free."

"Then what do you propose? Stand here and die?"

"I propose we *fight*, since no one else in this room seems to remember how—"

"Gentlemen." Corvin set both palms flat on the map table. The room quieted — not out of respect, exactly, but because six thousand lives made his word heavier than it had any right to be, and everyone present knew it. "Ossen. When was the last time you saw a hollow soldier eat?"

Ossen blinked. "I—never. That's my point."

"It's mine too." Corvin drew a slow circle over the Salt Road with one finger. "They don't eat. They don't sleep — our scouts watched a full night and not one man sat down. They don't break rank, they don't loot, they don't desert, they don't even talk. Now ask yourselves: what does an army lose when it loses all of that?"

Silence. Rain hammered the millhouse roof.

"Everything," Breka said slowly. "It loses everything."

"It loses *judgment*." Corvin tapped the map. "A living soldier watches the ridgeline because he's afraid of ambush. A living sentry hears a noise in the reeds because some part of him knows the dark is full of knives. Fear is the finest officer any army ever had, and these men have been harvested clean of it. They will walk into a killbox as cheerfully as into a parade ground. They will hold a position until their bodies come apart, because nothing in them says *run*. They are, I promise you, the most obedient soldiers ever fielded." He looked around the millhouse. "And obedience without thought is just a machine. And machines have blind spots."

Captain Vess, the youngest of them, leaned forward. "Such as?"

"Everything that requires initiative. They won't flank you unless ordered. They won't pursue beyond their orders. They won't react to anything their commanders haven't anticipated — and their commanders, thank every god still listening, are mortal men riding well behind the lines." Corvin straightened. "So we don't fight them the way we'd fight men. We fight them the way you'd fight a flood, or a fire. You don't beat it head-on. You shape where it goes."

He spent the next two hours drawing it for them: the marshy ground south of the ford where four hundred hollow soldiers would sink to their knees in mud and keep marching anyway, drowning in eighteen inches of water because nothing in them suggested turning aside. The narrow defile at Miller's Bend where a single rockslide — pre-cut, pre-rigged, held back by ropes — could seal three regiments into a pocket with no exit. The simple, ugly arithmetic of the crossbow volleys: aim for the legs, always the legs, because a hollow soldier who cannot march is not a wounded soldier to be pitied or finished, he is simply cargo, and his own army will not spare one man to carry him.

"They'll leave their own fallen in the road," Corvin told the assembled captains, and watched several faces go grey. "Don't expect otherwise. Don't waste arrows on mercy. I know what that costs you to hear. Cost it to me too."

Breka stared at the map a long moment. "You've fought them before."

"No." Corvin's jaw tightened. "I've read their contracts."

* * *

Training began before dawn the next morning, and it was the ugliest work Corvin had ever done.

Not the drilling — drilling was drilling, and he'd spent fifteen years teaching guild enforcers how to move through crowds and breach doors and put down riots without killing half the bystanders. The ugliness was in what he had to teach them to *become*.

"You want to know the trick?" he told the first hundred irregulars, standing in the grey drizzle of the ford's eastern meadow. "The trick is that they will not hate you. A living enemy looks at you and sees a person, and something in him hesitates — a half-second, maybe less, where his mind asks *should I?* You're going to live inside that half-second. You're going to make your home there." He drew his knife and held it up, catching the flat light. "When one of them turns toward you, you do not stand and trade blows like a storybook hero. You move *sideways*. Their eyes track forward — always forward, always toward the objective, because nothing in them wanders. Step off their line and you step out of their world. Then you cut the hamstring and keep moving. Never stop moving. Never be where you were."

He drilled them until their thighs screamed. Sidestep, strike, roll away. Sidestep, strike, roll away. By midday the meadow was churned to slop and a third of the recruits had thrown up, and Corvin walked the lines correcting grips and stances with the same flat patience he'd once used on new enforcers, and hated himself a little more with each correction, because he knew what he was making them. Not soldiers. *Butchers of the helpless.* Men and women trained to slaughter things that could not hate them back, could not fear them, could not even understand they were dying.

"You're teaching us to murder," said a caravan guard named Doss, spitting blood from a split lip after a drill went wrong. There was no accusation in it — only exhaustion, and a question underneath.

"I'm teaching you to survive," Corvin said. "Murder is what the crown did to them first. We're just cleaning up the ledger."

Doss looked at him for a long moment, then nodded once and went back to the line.

In the afternoons, Corvin ran the archers. This was simpler and crueler: ranges marked with whitewashed stakes, orders repeated until they were liturgy. *Loose on my whistle. Loose again. Then fall back ten paces and loose again.* The whole art of it was refusal — refusal to close, refusal to engage, refusal to give the hollow columns anything solid to walk toward. Give ground, always give ground, and make every yard cost a hundred legs.

"The temptation," he told the archer-captains, "will be to stand. To plant your feet when they come up the slope, because running feels like cowardice and your fathers didn't raise cowards. I'm telling you plainly: your fathers raised you wrong for this war. The moment they reach your line, you have already lost. There is no melee with these things that ends well for tired people. Loose, fall back, loose, fall back, and let the mud do the rest."

"And if the mud doesn't do the rest?" asked a grizzled woman named Hetta, who had hunted marsh-fowl for thirty years and shot better than any three men in her company. "If they come through anyway?"

Corvin met her eyes. "Then the spear-walls at the ford do the rest, and a lot of you die buying the rest of us an hour. That's the contract. I won't dress it prettier than that."

Hetta snorted. "At least you don't lie. My last captain lied beautifully. Got forty of us killed admiring the architecture of it." She shouldered her bow. "Whistle-loud, boy. My ears are old."

* * *

On the fourth night, the fires of the usurper's vanguard became visible on the northern horizon — except they weren't fires, that was the thing that turned Corvin's stomach as he stood on the ridge with Breka and studied the darkness through a spyglass. An army of eight thousand should have blazed like a fallen constellation. Instead there was only a thin scatter of command-tents far behind the lines, and ahead of them, mile upon mile, a darkness so complete and so orderly that it looked like a hole cut out of the world.

"They're resting," Breka whispered. "No — they're *standing*. All of them. In the dark. In formation."

"Waiting for dawn," Corvin said. "They'll advance at first light. Straight down the Salt Road, exactly as ordered, no scouts out, no flanking parties, no feints." He lowered the spyglass. His hands were steady, which surprised him; somewhere in the last four days the fear had burned down to something colder and more useful. "They're going to walk into everything we built. God help me, Breka, it's almost not fair."

"Almost," Breka said. She was quiet a moment. "My brother's in there somewhere. Or what's left of what they took from him." Her knuckles whitened on the ridge-stone. "Tell me again about the blind spots, Corvin. Tell me it works."

Corvin thought of Mirabel, somewhere south with her bones and her dead and her impossible ledgers, carving testimony into whalebone while he taught farmers how to butcher the walking memoryless. He thought of the widow whose borrowed year he'd refused to repossess — the small, stupid, magnificent act of disobedience that had cost him everything and delivered him, by roads he couldn't have mapped, to this ridge, this night, this war.

"It works," he said. "Not cleanly. Not without cost. But it works."

Below them, six thousand irregulars slept badly in the rain, and eight thousand hollow men stood motionless in the dark, and between the two armies lay the flooded meadows, the rigged defile, the mud that swallowed knees, the whitewashed ranges, the whistle Hetta had demanded he blow loud.

At midnight, a rider came up the ridge at a lathered gallop — a courier from the south, mud to the eyebrows, thrusting a sealed packet at Corvin with a salute that was mostly a stumble. Corvin cracked the wax by feel. Inside, in a clerk's careful hand, were copied pages: founding contracts of the Veiled Exchange, sealed and smuggled out against the masters' orders. And beneath them, in a script he knew better than his own heartbeat — cramped, slanted, ink-blotted where the writer had pressed too hard — a note from Mirabel:

*We have the name. The true name, and the terms of the binding. It can only end with the king's own consent — and Isolde must be the one to ask him. We ride for the capital within the week. Hold the ford, Corvin. Hold it long enough for justice to arrive.*

Corvin read it twice, folded it into his breast pocket over his heart, and turned back to face the hole in the world where the usurper's army stood waiting for morning.

"Hold the ford," he murmured. "Aye. That part, at least, I know how to do."

Far to the north, the first grey light touched the hollow regiments, and eight thousand vacant eyes opened all at once, like a field of lamps — and began to march.
