## 17. The Palace of Borrowed Years

The credentials sat in Corvin's palm like a confession. Two thin leaves of vellum, guild-sealed in green wax, bearing names that had never lived: Master Ferrant Odo, Provisioning Auditor; his clerk, one Serl of the Marches. The forger — a one-eyed woman named Hesk who owed Corvin from the Vaelmoor days before Vaelmoor fell — had outdone herself. The seals were right down to the tooth-marks on the wax, an old Exchange habit meant to prove authenticity to any broker who knew to bite.

"You're biting them," Isolde said. It was not a question. She stood at the window of the rented room above a chandler's shop, watching the capital assemble itself for its feast. From here they could see the processional road climbing toward the palace, already lined with banners the color of old wine.

"Every broker bites." He handed her the smaller leaf. "If a gate-clerk doesn't bite yours, that's when you worry."

She bit the wax, made a face at the taste, and looked at him over the credential. Three days she had been Isolde-in-name only, still wearing the salt-farmer's name like a coat that didn't fit. Out here, away from the marshes, away from Mirabel's patient carving and the ledger-traces that had found her, she kept reaching for the false name the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth. *Maren*, she'd been called for eleven years. Maren who raked salt. Maren who buried a husband and two sons under the marsh grass and never once thought about thrones.

"Say it again," she said now. "What we're doing."

"We walk in as auditors. The Exchange audits every crown function on coronation-anniversary feasts — provisioning, gift-tally, memory-levy receipts. Nobody questions auditors. Auditors are furniture." He said it flatly, because saying it flatly was how he kept his hands steady. "We get inside the feast hall. You see him. That's all tonight is. You see him, and you decide whether you can do what Mirabel needs you to do."

"And if I see him and I decide I can't?"

Corvin had asked himself the same question every night since the salt marshes, and every night the answer came back the same, unwelcome as a bill at closing. "Then we've lost nothing but a feast's worth of bad wine." He buckled the auditor's satchel across his chest. Its false bottom held Mirabel's relic — a sliver of palace bone, carved fine as frost — wrapped in wool. "But you'll decide. I've watched you rake salt in a gale. You don't quit things."

"That was salt," Isolde said. "This is a king who eats children's summers."

He had no answer for that, so he lit the lamp low and opened the door.

* * *

The capital had spent three years learning to smile wrong, and tonight it smiled wrong at scale.

Corvin saw it first at the palace gates: the queue of petitioners and guests shuffling past the honor guard, each face arranged into festival gladness, each pair of eyes a half-beat late behind the mouth. Not vacant — not yet, not like the hollow regiments the scouts described — but *managed*. A city of people who had sold something small and necessary and were performing the absence of it. A woman ahead of them in line hummed a festival tune and got the words wrong in a way that suggested she'd forgotten the song rather than the words. A man clutched a gift-wrapped box and, when his companion asked what was in it, answered brightly: "Something he'll love," with the particular confidence of someone who no longer remembered what it was.

"Levy receipts," Corvin murmured to Isolde, nodding at the crowd. "Small trades. A first kiss. A mother's lullaby. The crown takes the cheap memories by the thousand so nobody notices the shape of the hole."

Isolde said nothing, but her hand found his sleeve — not for comfort, he thought, but to keep herself from turning around.

The gate-clerk was a young man with ink-stained fingers and the harried look of someone auditing joy. He took their credentials, examined the seals, and — mercifully, predictably — bit the wax. "Provisioning audit," he read. "You're late. Feast starts within the hour."

"Furniture arrives early or late," Corvin said. "Never on time. On time draws the eye."

The clerk almost smiled. Then his gaze flicked up, caught something — Corvin never learned what; perhaps the scar through his brow, perhaps the way Isolde stood too straight for a clerk — and hung there a moment too long. Corvin felt the whole night balance on that look.

"Third colonnade," the clerk said finally, stamping the leaf. "Auditors' gallery, left side. Don't touch the gift-tally tables."

They walked through.

Inside, the palace was a machine built to convert wealth into forgetting. Corvin had grown up poor in the harbor districts of a free city; he knew luxury as a rumor. This was worse than luxury. Every surface had been paid for twice — once in coin, once in whatever the previous owner had surrendered along with it. The tapestries were too beautiful; standing near them, he kept losing the thread of thoughts he'd been having. The great hall's chandeliers burned candles scented with something that made him think of his childhood kitchen, and then made him unable to remember why the thought hurt.

"The air," Isolde whispered as they took their places in the auditors' gallery, a stone balcony overlooking the feast floor. "It's thick. Like walking through water that remembers things."

"It's the crown." Corvin set the satchel between his feet. Below, the hall filled: lords in wine-colored silk, foreign envoys, guild masters — he recognized three faces from the Exchange, men who had voted neutrality with full bellies — and soldiers, always soldiers, stationed at every pillar with the perfect stillness of the hollowed. They didn't shift their weight. They didn't glance at the food. One of them had a fly walking across his cheekbone and did not blink it away.

Isolde followed his stare and went pale. "Those are the ones from Vaelmoor's border reports."

"Some of them. The rest are new harvests." He kept his voice level, auditor-flat. "Look at their boots. Provisioning marks. The army pays them in what they used to be."

Below, trumpets. The hall turned as one throat toward the far doors, and King Aldous entered.

Corvin had seen portraits: a broad man, black-bearded, famous once for laughing with his jaw full of apple. What descended the steps now wore that body the way a glove wears a hand. Aldous moved precisely, graciously, greeting each lord by name — and Corvin understood with a lurch that the precision was the horror. No king alive could greet four hundred courtiers by name without error. The thing beneath the crown could, because it had eaten the remembering of everyone in this room, and it was reciting them back.

"He hasn't slept in three years," Isolde breathed. "Mirabel's priest said it. Look at his eyes. There's nothing tired in them. Tiredness is a kind of honesty, isn't it? And there's none left."

Aldous reached the high table. He lifted the ceremonial cup. The hall hushed for the anniversary toast — three years since the coronation, three years since the binding had closed over him like ice over a drowning man.

And then the king's hand shook.

It was a small thing. A tremor, quickly mastered. But Corvin saw the cup tilt, saw a bead of wine run down the gold, and saw the king's face change — not the managed festival face, not the recited graciousness, but something underneath, surfacing like a swimmer breaking water after too long. The eyes went wide. Wet. Human. They swept the hall as if seeing it for the first time in years, and they filled with tears that the king himself seemed astonished by.

"I—" Aldous said aloud, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word, split between two speakers. "I had a daughter. Where—"

The hall froze. Somewhere a fork rang against porcelain and was stilled.

For three heartbeats — Corvin counted them, would count them for the rest of his life — the King of the Usurped Throne stood in his own body and wept, openly, terribly, a big man crying in front of four hundred people, saying in a raw and ordinary voice: "I had a daughter. Her name was— her name was—"

Then the crown drank him.

Corvin saw it happen and knew no one else could. There was no light, no thunder. The king simply *closed*, the way a ledger closes, the way a door closes on a room where something has been put away. The tears stopped mid-track. The trembling ceased. The face reassembled itself into graciousness so smoothly that the hall exhaled as one, deciding together, in the way crowds decide, that nothing had happened — because deciding otherwise was unthinkable, and unthinkable things were exactly what this crown ate.

Aldous raised the cup again, steady as marble. "To three years of plenty," the godling said through his mouth, warm and resonant, "and to many more. To memory rightly kept, and burdens rightly shared."

"To the king!" the hall roared, and the sound of it broke something in Corvin that he suspected would never knit quite straight.

Beside him, Isolde gripped the balcony rail hard enough that the stone bit her fingers. She was crying without noise, the salt-farmer's discipline holding her face together while everything behind it came apart.

"That was him," she whispered. "That was *him*. Underneath. He's still in there, Corvin. He's been in there for three years, screaming, and everyone toasts him."

"Yes."

"He said *daughter*." Her voice splintered. "He couldn't remember her name. They took his own child's name out of his mouth and he stands there and thanks them for it."

Corvin looked down at the feast — the laughter resuming, the music striking up, the hollow soldiers motionless among the living — and felt the pity arrive like a tide, unwelcome and undeniable. He had come hating Aldous. Hatred was clean; hatred fit in a soldier's kit beside the knives. But you could not hate a drowned man for drowning. You could only hate the water, and the water was wearing a crown, and the water had taught four hundred people to cheer.

"He surfaced," Corvin said quietly. "Three years under, and he surfaced. Do you know what that means?"

Isolde wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a smear of salt dust — marsh salt, he realized, she still carried it in the creases of her skin. "Tell me."

"It means the binding isn't total. It means there's a seam. Mirabel said the unbinding needs his consent, freely given — and everyone assumed consent was impossible, that there was nothing left in there to give it." He nodded at the high table, where the king laughed at some lord's joke with perfect, terrible grace. "There's something left. Something strong enough to fight up through three years of that. We don't need to break the crown open. We need to reach him at the moment he surfaces, and help him stay up long enough to say yes."

"And if the next time he surfaces, it's in front of the wrong person? If the godling learns to hold him down tighter?" She turned to him, and beneath the grief her eyes had gone hard and bright, the eyes of a woman who had raked salt through storms because the family ate or didn't. "How many more daughters does he lose the name of while we find our moment?"

Corvin had no good answer. He gave her the true one instead. "Fewer than if we rush and fail. More than if we'd started years ago. That's the arithmetic of being late, and I've been late my whole life — late to Vaelmoor, late to the harvest procession, late to every friend I ever tried to save." His hand found the satchel, the relic sleeping inside it. "I'm done being late. But done being late means doing it *right*, not doing it fast."

Below, the feast rolled on. The king rose to lead the first dance, and the hall parted for him like water around a ship, and Corvin watched the godling wear a weeping man's body through the measures of a festival reel, flawless, patient, hungry — and for one strange moment the king's eyes crossed the distance, crossed the crowd, crossed the balcony rail, and rested on the auditors' gallery.

On Isolde.

Nothing happened. The godling's gaze slid past her as it slid past everything, cataloguing, consuming, indifferent. But Corvin saw Isolde go rigid, saw her lips move around a word she didn't say, and when the dance carried the king away she whispered it anyway, so quietly it barely existed:

"*Father.*"

Not hers — she'd never met Aldous, had no kinship to him but the abstract kinship of crowns. It was the word the king himself had been reaching for. *Daughter.* She had given him back the shape of it, silently, across a feast hall, a thing returned that no ledger could hold.

Corvin touched her shoulder. "Time to be furniture. We audit the gift-tally, we note the memory-levy receipts, we walk out the third colonnade, and we get Mirabel what she needs."

"Which is?"

"A carving of tonight." He patted the satchel. "But there's more. While you were watching the king, I was watching the clerks. The gift-tally master keeps a private ledger — locked, chained, iron-bound. And on its spine, in guild cipher, it's marked with a word I haven't seen since the founding contracts Mirabel showed us."

Isolde waited.

"*Hostline*," Corvin said. "The genealogy of every vessel the crown has ever worn. And Isolde — the last entry isn't Aldous. There's an entry after him. Blank. Waiting. The godling is already choosing its next king."

Above the feast, the chandeliers burned on, sweet and thick with other people's childhoods, and somewhere below the music, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the palace began — very softly, very patiently — to laugh.
