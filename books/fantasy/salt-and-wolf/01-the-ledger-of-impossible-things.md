## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to surviving as a storm-caller in Greyharbor, and I have broken all of them at least once.

Rule one: never touch the weather. Rule two: never let anyone see you want to. Rule three — and this is the one the guild taught me without meaning to — keep everything in writing. Ink doesn't lie, ink doesn't panic, and ink has never once, in seven years of faithful service, called down lightning on a royal procession.

I was thinking about rule three when the thunder arrived in a bottle.

It came up from the docks in a crate packed with salt-hay, carried by a courier who sweated through his coat in the way men do when they've been paid too well for something they don't understand. The Saltglass Guild's receiving hall smelled of tallow and brine and old paper, and I sat behind my desk with my audit quill sharpened to a point that could take out an eye, doing what I do best: being boring.

Boring is a discipline. I want you to understand that. Anyone can be interesting; it takes real work to be forgettable. Seven years ago I was apparently very interesting indeed, though I'll take the historians' word for it, since I remember none of it. What I remember is ledgers. Columns. The satisfying scratch of a well-cut nib. I am Vesper Nakamura, senior auditor of impossible trades, and if you asked anyone at the Guild to describe me they would say *thorough* and then struggle to say anything else, which is exactly how I like it.

"Manifest," I said to the courier, and held out my hand.

He gave me a paper so damp it had begun to compost. I read it anyway. *One (1) bottled thunderstorm, coastal variety, captured off the Teeth during the spring squalls. Seller claims provenance from the storm-caller's own hand.* My quill paused above the page for precisely one heartbeat, which is the longest pause I allow myself before lunch.

Storm-callers don't exist. That's official Guild position, crown position, and church position, all three of which agree on very little else, so you know it's load-bearing. Storm-callers are a story grandmothers tell to get children out of the surf. There has never been a storm-caller in Greyharbor, there is not one now, and the woman who allegedly stood in the shallows at Saltmarch seven years ago calling lightning down on the king's own procession was, per every record I have been able to find, a rumor with wet feet.

I know this because I've read every record. Twice. It's amazing what a person can learn about herself by reading about someone else.

"Condition of the seal?" I asked.

"Intact," said the courier. "Mostly."

"*Mostly* is a condition I can't enter into the ledger."

"It's intact enough." He shifted his weight. Under the salt-hay, something in the bottle turned over — a slow, sullen roll, like a cat deciding whether to be offended. The hair on my forearms stood up in a neat little row, each one reporting for duty, and I pressed my palm flat against the desk until the wood took the feeling away.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about hiding a gift: it isn't like hiding a knife or a lover or counterfeit coin. A knife stays hidden if you don't reach for it. A gift is more like a dog. It hears things. It gets excited. And on grey afternoons in a room full of bottled weather, it presses its nose against the bars of its cage and *whines*, and you have to sit there with your clerical face on pretending you don't hear it too.

"Bottled thunder is class-three volatile," I said. "Storage surcharge applies. Who's the buyer?"

"No buyer yet. Consignment." He named a name I wrote down and immediately distrusted: Master Corvane, of the Corvane counting-house, whose family had made money in shipping and lost it in piracy and remade it in ways the Guild's ledgers were politely discouraged from examining. "Payment on sale. Seller wanted half up front, but—"

"But Master Corvane doesn't pay up front for goods he intends to never pay for at all," I finished. "Yes. Standard entry. Set it on the scale."

The scale is brass and older than the Guild, and it has weighed stranger things than weather: sins, mostly, though we log them as *miscellaneous bulk*. The courier heaved the crate up. The bottle inside was green glass, taller than my forearm, corked with wax the color of dried blood, and the cloud inside it was the particular bruised purple that means the storm hasn't finished being angry yet, only patient.

I have seen that color from the inside. I didn't know that until I saw it, and then I knew it the way you know a scar in a mirror.

"Provenance claim is fraudulent," I said, because saying it out loud made it true, or truer. "No storm-caller exists. Log it as *atmospheric salvage, origin unverified*. Value assessed at forty crowns, contingent on seal integrity."

The courier looked at me the way men look at women who correct their grammar. "You're not even going to open it? Check it's real?"

"If I open it, it's real *and* we're dead. Forty crowns says it's real. That's how assessment works." I signed the entry. *V. Nakamura, auditor.* My handwriting is small and upright and gives away nothing, which took years of practice; my natural hand slants like it's running somewhere.

That should have been the end of it. Entry closed, crate shelved, courier dismissed with the particular courtesy that means *leave*. But the client came in before the echo of the door had settled, and clients, in my experience, are where careful lives go to die.

* * *

He did not look like a man who bought storms. He looked like a man who bought *everything*, eventually — soft-bellied, ringed, with the damp handshake of a fishmonger playing at nobility. Master Corvane. He set a leather case on my desk and opened it with the flourish of a man who believes money is a personality.

"Auditor Nakamura." He said my name correctly, which put him ahead of most, and warmly, which put him behind them again. "I'm told you're the only honest clerk in this building."

"I'm told that too. It's why I'm allowed near the money."

He laughed. I logged the laugh under *liabilities*. "Then you'll appreciate that I intend to make this the cleanest transaction of your season. The bottle. I'll take it now, and I'll pay—" he drew a single coin from his waistcoat and placed it on the ledger with a click, "—that."

I looked at the coin.

Minting coins is a royal monopoly, and kings like to put their faces on them, which makes coins a kind of history book you can lose in a couch. This one bore the profile of King Aldous the Second, who had been dead eleven years, drowned off the Teeth in a squall the ballads still blamed on witchcraft. His face was sharp-struck, unworn, uncirculated. And the coin was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not pocket-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm after lightning finds it — warm from the middle out, warm with somewhere else's weather. I did not touch it. I want that recorded: I did not touch it. I looked at it sitting there on my honest ledger, steaming faintly in the cold air of the receiving hall, and every hair on my arms stood up again, and beneath my ribs something old and leashed lifted its head.

"That's not legal tender," I said. My voice came out level. I was proud of it then; I'm less proud of it now, knowing what it cost to keep level.

"It's better than tender. It's *rare*." Corvane smiled with all his teeth, which were excellent. "Aldous issue. Uncirculated. Collectors weep over these."

"Collectors weep over cold ones." I slid the ledger sideways, out from under the coin, a small motion, deniable. "Where did you get it?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. Coins minted by a dead king come from three places: collections, graves, and mints that shouldn't be running. Two of those are crimes. The third is worse." I said it dryly, auditor's humor, and watched him not laugh. "Master Corvane, I audit trades in bottled thunder. Do you understand what that sentence means? The Guild sells weather to people who want to flood a rival's fields or clear a road of soldiers, and my job is to write down who and how much. I am not paid to ask *why*, but I am absolutely paid to ask *where*, because if the crown ever audits us back, 'the seller declined to say' is the only sentence that keeps everyone's head attached."

Something moved behind his face — not guilt; calculation wearing guilt's coat. "The seller," he said carefully, "is a collector of antiquities. He has several such pieces. He may have more."

"He may."

"And he pays commissions to the house that asks no questions."

"We're back to the head-attachment problem." I pulled a fresh sheet toward me. "I'll need the seller's mark, the date of acquisition, and the point of origin. You can give them to me here, or you can give them to the Guildmaster, who will charge you a questioning fee and enjoy himself thoroughly."

For a moment — one moment, no longer — Corvane looked past me, out the high window at the grey sky over Greyharbor, and his expression went somewhere young and frightened. Then it came back, upholstered. "Of course," he said. "Forgive me. A misunderstanding between friends." He took the coin back. It left a faint warm ghost on the page, and I watched the ink of my last entry seem to lean away from it, which is not possible, and which I entered nowhere.

He bought the bottle at asking price, cold silver, clean weights, and had it carried out under canvas like a coffin. At the door he paused. "They say the old king's death wasn't natural," he remarked, to the room in general, to the weather in general. "They say somebody called that squall down on him. They say she's still out there."

"They say," I agreed, "a great many things. None of them are in my ledger."

"No," he said softly. "But things get *into* ledgers, Auditor Nakamura. That's rather the point of them." And he bowed, and was gone, and the hall smelled faintly of ozone, like the air after a strike, though the sky outside had not cracked all day.

* * *

I stayed late. I always stay late; late is quiet, and quiet is where I keep myself. I re-inked entries, squared the day's pages, and told myself the crawling feeling along my spine was the draft from the window.

Then I turned back to the morning's entry to check a figure, and found my own handwriting waiting for me — the small upright hand that gives away nothing — and beneath the line *atmospheric salvage, origin unverified*, in the margin, in the same hand, in ink I had no memory of dipping:

*Ask him about the procession.*

My quill was dry. My inkwell was sealed. Outside, far out past the harbor lights, over the black water where no ship would be fool enough to sail, thunder rolled once — a long, low, familiar sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and saying my name.

I sat very still in my careful, recordable life, and for the first time in seven years, I was afraid of my own margins.
