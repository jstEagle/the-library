## 2. Proclamation

The edict arrived on a Tuesday, which I have always considered an insult. Bad news should have the decency to come at night, or in winter, or at least with some theatrical flourish of thunder. Instead it came at midmorning, by courier, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with the crown's sigil pressed so deep into the wax that the lion's face came out looking like it was screaming.

I was in my office above the Greyharbor watchhouse, doing what captains of the watch do best: avoiding paperwork by reading other paperwork. The courier was a boy of maybe fifteen with mud to his knees and the particular blankness of someone who has been paid not to answer questions.

"From the capital," he said, unnecessarily. The seal said that well enough.

I broke it. I wish, in the way a man wishes he had not drunk the last cup before remembering tomorrow exists, that I had left it sitting on my desk for another hour. Another day. Another year.

*By order of His Majesty Aldric III, Sovereign of the Realm, Protector of the Coasts, Keeper of the Salt Throne:* — the titles went on for a while; kings collect them the way harbors collect wrecks — *all persons gifted beyond natural law are hereby declared subjects of examination. They shall be seized without warrant, held without charge, and conveyed to the capital for assessment of their condition. Resistance shall be construed as confession.*

I read that last line three times. *Resistance shall be construed as confession.* Somewhere in the chancellery there was a man paid a respectable salary to write that sentence, and somewhere in that man's childhood a tutor had told him words were tools, and no one had ever since asked him what he was building.

There was more. There is always more. The edict established a new office — the Office of Examination, reporting directly to the crown, bypassing every court, magistrate, and guild charter between Greyharbor and the capital. It granted hunters authority over local watch. It suspended, in language so polite you could serve tea with it, the right of the accused to know their accuser, their crime, or their fate.

And then, folded separately inside the proclamation, sealed with black wax instead of red, there was a list.

I knew what it was before I opened it. You spend twenty years as a lawman and you develop a nose for documents the way old sailors develop a nose for weather. This one smelled like a storm coming off the water — pressure behind the eyes, a taste of metal.

The list held forty-one names. Greyharbor's share. Each name had notes beside it: trade, address, observed behaviors. *Bottled thunder, purchased twice from the Saltglass Guild.* *Heats her own forge without coal.* *Speaks to gulls; gulls answer.* That last one made me want to laugh, and then made me want to be sick, because whoever wrote those notes had been watching this city for months, and I was the captain of the watch, and nobody had watched them watching.

I ran my finger down the names. Fishwives. A smith. Two midwives — midwives, gods help us, whose crime was presumably delivering children too successfully. And then, halfway down, in the same clerk's hand, the same careful ink:

*Maren Renner. Weaver, Lantern Row. Observed behavior: mends cloth by touch. Subject is sister to Capt. Ivo Renner, Greyharbor Watch.*

They hadn't even bothered to hide it. That was the part that sat in my chest like swallowed stone. Some clerk in the capital had written down that Maren was my sister, and then written down her name anyway, and sealed it, and sent it to me. Either they trusted me completely or they didn't trust me at all, and both possibilities meant the same thing: the law no longer cared what I thought. The law had stopped being a thing I served and become a thing that was served, and I had just been handed the bill.

I put the list down. I picked it up again. I read my sister's name until the letters stopped meaning anything, which took longer than it should have, because I kept getting stuck on *mends cloth by touch.* Maren had been doing that since we were children. She'd mend my torn sleeves when I came home bleeding from fights I'd started losing, and she'd press her palm flat against the rip and frown at it like it was being difficult, and the tear would close, and she'd say *don't tell Mother,* and I never did, and apparently all those years of silence had been for nothing, because somebody else had been telling.

* * *

I walked down to Lantern Row that evening without deciding to. My feet made the decision somewhere around the second cup of wine, and I let them, because feet are honest in a way that orders are not.

Maren's shop still had its lamp lit. Through the window I could see her at the worktable, hunched over a bolt of grey wool, one hand moving across it slow as a blind woman reading. The other hand held her needle, but she wasn't using it. She never really needed to anymore. I'd known that for thirty years and filed it under *things a brother doesn't see,* and now the crown had seen it, and written it down, and numbered it among crimes.

She looked up when I came in. Sisters always know.

"You've got your reading face," she said. "The one where you're reading something and hating it."

"The edict came today."

Her hands went still on the wool. Not dramatically. Just still, the way water goes still before it freezes. "And?"

"And there's a list."

She nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed something she'd already priced out. "How many names?"

"Forty-one."

"Am I on it?"

"Maren—"

"Am I *on it,* Ivo?"

"Yes."

She turned back to the wool. For a long moment the only sound was the harbor breathing outside, the slap of water against pilings, a gull arguing with the dark about something. When she spoke again her voice was level, which frightened me more than screaming would have. Screaming means a person thinks there's still time.

"Do you remember when Father was taken by the press-gang?" she said. "You were nine. You stood in the doorway and told the sergeant the king's law said they couldn't take a man with a family, and you'd read it yourself. And he laughed at you. Do you remember what he said?"

"I don't want to remember what he said."

"He said, *the law is whatever the man holding the paper says it is.* And you cried, and I mended your sleeve, and we both decided he was wrong." She smoothed the wool flat. "He wasn't wrong, Ivo. He was just early."

"They'll take you to the capital. For examination. That's all it says — examination. People come back from examinations." I heard myself saying it and hated the sound, the wet desperate optimism of it, a lawman quoting his own edict back at the dark like a prayer to a god who'd moved.

"People come back," Maren agreed. "Have you seen what comes back?"

I had. Three weeks ago, a cooper from the east quarter, seized under some earlier quiet version of the hunt, returned to his shop with a smile fixed on his face like paint. He worked. He ate. He answered questions pleasantly. But his wife told me privately that he no longer remembered their wedding day, or their son's name, or the taste of anything. He would hold salt in his palm and say *I'm told this is important to people here.* The guild physicians called it recovery. His wife called it a burial with the body still walking.

"He smiles," I said finally. "That's what comes back. Smiles."

Maren set down the wool. She crossed the room and did the thing she'd done since we were children — took my face in her hands, palms warm from the work, and looked at me the way she used to look at torn sleeves. Assessing. Deciding whether I was worth the thread.

"Then listen to me," she said. "Whatever that paper tells you to do, when they come for me — and they will come, Ivo, lists always get used — you decide which law you serve. The one with the seal on it, or the one that made you cry in a doorway when you were nine years old. Because only one of them is going to survive this year, and I need to know, before they take me, which one my brother chose."

I didn't answer. I kissed her forehead and walked home through streets that suddenly looked different to me — every window a place where someone might be heating a forge with no coal, every rooftop a perch for boys who talked to gulls. Forty-one names. In a city of sixty thousand, forty-one names, and I understood with total clarity that the number meant nothing. Lists grow. Lists are how predators eat politely.

* * *

Back in my office, I built a fire, because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking, and I held the black-sealed list over the flames.

I held it there for a long time. Long enough for the wax to soften. Long enough to imagine how it would go: the list gone, the names safe, forty-one people waking tomorrow unaware they'd been saved by a piece of arson. Long enough to understand, too, exactly how it would end — with me hanged for destroying crown property, the list recompiled from the original register within the week, and Maren taken anyway, minus one brother.

The fire licked up and I pulled the list back. Folded it. Put it inside my coat, against my chest, where it lay like a coal that wouldn't burn through.

Because here is the thing I realized somewhere between the wine and the firelight: a list in a captain's pocket is still evidence. Evidence of what the crown intends. Evidence of what the hunt actually is — not justice, not even cruelty, but appetite. And if there was going to be any accounting, someday, in some court or some corner of history, someone would need to produce the paper. Someone would need to stand up with the thing in hand and say *here, look, this is what they wrote, this is what they meant to do.*

I am not a brave man. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps such entries. I am a procedure man, a form-and-warrant man, a man who believes in doors and locks and the slow machinery of due process. But the machinery has been seized by the thing it was built to restrain, and a man in my position has exactly two choices: feed it, or keep one hand free.

I kept one hand free.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with my sister's name against my heart, and that was when I heard the bells — not the watch bells, not the harbor bells, but the deep ones from the Saltglass Guild district, rung backward, the old signal for sorcery. And beneath the ringing, faint but unmistakable, rolling in off the water like weather:

Thunder.

Out of a clear night sky. Out of a season that hadn't seen a proper storm in seven years.

I was moving before I finished thinking, coat on, list against my chest, boots on the stairs three at a time, and the last coherent thought I had before the street took me was this: *the storm started at the Guild, and the Guild keeps ledgers, and ledgers are just lists that tell the truth.*

Gods help me, I was about to go hunting a list of my own.
