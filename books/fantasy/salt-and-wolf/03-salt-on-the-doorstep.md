## 3. Salt on the Doorstep

I have always believed that a well-kept ledger is a form of prayer. You write down what was, you balance it against what is, and if your columns are honest, the world stays put. For seven years I had kept my columns immaculate. Bottled thunder, entry four hundred and eleven. A widow's luck, sold by weight. Three jars of last summer's sunlight, provenance disputed. I recorded everything and touched nothing, and in exchange the storm inside me slept like a dog that has been fed on schedule.

Then the hunters came to Greyharbor, and my schedule went to hell.

It began, as catastrophes do at the Saltglass Guild, with paperwork. I was at my desk in the audit room correcting a clerk who had valued a mermaid's tooth at eleven crowns when any fool could see it was worth no more than six — grief inflates prices; I made a note to speak to him about sentiment — when the bells started.

Not the harbor bells. Those ring in threes for tide and fours for fire. These rang wrong. Fast, arrhythmic, the sound of men hauling rope with panic in their arms. Alarm.

The junior clerks looked up from their desks like rabbits deciding which way to bolt. Old Maren, who kept the vault keys and had survived three guild wars and two marriages, simply closed her ledger, squared its corners, and said, "That'll be the crown, then."

"The crown audits nobody," I said. "The crown doesn't know we exist."

"Girl," said Maren, "the crown knows everything now. That's rather the point of crowns."

I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere — that my first instinct was bureaucratic. I gathered the day's ledgers because they were mine, because their accuracy was my responsibility, because seven years of careful nothing had taught me that the only safety in this world is a record no one can argue with. I did not think: *run*. I thought: *file*.

The shouting started on the stairs.

They came up in grey cloaks with the king's sun stamped on their shoulders, six of them, and behind them a man in half-plate with a captain's knot at his throat — dark-haired, tired-eyed, wearing authority the way some men wear wet wool, as though he'd been told it was his and couldn't figure out how to get it off. I would learn his name later. At the time I only noted, with the detached professionalism that is my single talent, that he looked ill.

"By edict of the crown," announced the lead hunter, a broad man whose voice had the rehearsed flatness of someone reading scripture he didn't believe, "all persons gifted with unnatural power are to surrender themselves for examination."

Maren snorted. Actually snorted, at an armed man. "Examination. Is that what we're calling it this season?"

"Madam—"

"I'm seventy years old, boy. My gift is remembering where I put my teeth." She held up her ledger. "Shall I come quietly?"

He raised his hand — not toward her, past her, toward me, standing frozen behind my desk with an armful of accounting books like the world's most cowardly archivist. "Her," he said. "The Nakamura woman. She's on the list."

And here is the thing I still cannot explain, the thing I have written and rewritten in every margin of every book I own: *how did I get on the list?*

I was careful. I was so careful. Seven years of never calling so much as a breeze, of flinching from open water, of sleeping with iron filings under my pillow because a hedge-witch once told me they grounded a storm-caller's dreams. Nobody knew. The Guild thought I was a clerk with excellent penmanship and no family. My name should have been nowhere near a hunter's tongue.

But he said it. *Nakamura.* And something in my chest heard it the way a struck bell hears its own note.

"No," I said, out loud, to him, to myself, to whatever was waking up behind my ribs. "No, you've made a mistake."

The world went white at the edges.

I remember it in pieces, the way you remember lightning. The smell first — ozone, sharp as a needle through the sinuses. Then the taste of copper. Then the sound, which was not thunder but its opposite: a vast intake of breath, the whole street inhaling.

The windows blew outward. Not shattered — *outward*, all of them, down the entire face of the guildhall, glass leaving in a hurry like sailors deserting a sinking ship. The hunters were thrown against the far wall. The captain caught a doorframe and held, boots skidding, staring at me with an expression I'd catalog later as recognition, though I didn't know yet what he recognized.

The building groaned. Somewhere below us, timber cracked like knuckles.

I did not do it. I need that understood. Whatever lived in me did it, the thing I'd starved for seven years, and it stood up inside my skin and stretched, and the stretch took the roof off the counting house across the lane. Slate rained into the street. A chimney folded with a sound like a sigh. The air itself bent toward me, every loose leaf and scrap of paper in the room rising in a slow spiral around my shoulders, and I understood with perfect clarity that I was going to kill everyone in this room, and that part of me — the part that had been starving — wouldn't mind.

So I ran.

That is the whole of my heroism: I ran. Down the back stair as the front of the guildhall shed its face, out through the records room, clutching my ledgers to my chest like a mother with children, over the garden wall while the sky above Greyharbor turned the color of a bruise and rain came down sideways. Behind me I heard the captain shouting — not orders, oddly; his voice sounded more like a man arguing with his own side — and then I heard nothing but the storm, my storm, the one I hadn't called, roaring through streets I'd walked calmly for seven years, tearing awnings loose and setting boats grinding against their moorings, and I ran through the wreckage of my own careful life without looking back, because looking back felt like an invitation.

* * *

I stopped running when the streets ran out — the low lanes east of the tanneries, where the houses lean together like conspirators and the cobbles haven't been level since the last king's grandfather drowned. Rain followed me the whole way, thinning as I went, as though even weather could be embarrassed into moderation by distance. By the time I ducked under the sagging eave of a house with salt-crusted shutters, I was soaked, shaking, and holding three ledgers of impossible trades against my heart like scripture.

The door opened before I knocked.

An old woman stood in the gap, small and round as a teapot, with white hair cut short and eyes the pale grey of a sea just before it decides to eat you. She looked at me the way Maren looked at mispriced inventory: assessing, unimpressed, already decided.

"You're dripping on my step," she said. "Come in or move along."

Every sensible nerve I owned said *keep moving*. But sensible nerves had gotten me a leveled street and a name on a list, so I stepped inside.

The house smelled of dried kelp and lamp oil and, underneath it, faintly, of lightning — that same needle-sharp ozone I'd been breathing in the guildhall, faded to almost nothing, like a room where a storm once lived and had since taken up quieter work. Shelves lined every wall, and the shelves held jars. Not bottled thunder. Seawater, mostly, dozens of jars of seawater, each labeled in a spidery hand with dates going back decades.

"Sit," she said, pointing at a chair by the stove. "And give me those books before you soak them."

I handed them over like a child surrendering contraband sweets. She set them on the table, weighed one in her hand, and read the spine. "*Ledger of Impossible Things,*" she read aloud. "Volume... nine?" Her eyebrows went up. "You've been busy."

"They're just accounts," I said. "Guild property. Trades. Nothing—"

"Vesper."

My name. My name, spoken plainly, correctly, by a stranger in a stranger's house, and there was nothing strange about it except that she pronounced it with a particular softness on the second syllable, a little downward turn, the way you say a word you chose yourself long ago and have said to yourself many times since.

Except I had never chosen it. Had I? It was simply my name. It had always been my name. Vesper Nakamura, exile, clerk, careful woman. I reached for the memory of choosing it — people choose names, exiles especially, we take new ones like coats — and found a hole. Not an absence. A hole, with edges, the way a burned page has edges. I could feel the shape of what wasn't there.

"How do you know my name?" I asked, and my voice came out smaller than I intended.

The old woman set the ledger down and looked at me for a long moment, and in her pale eyes I saw something that frightened me worse than the hunters had: pity. Specific, informed pity, the kind you can only offer someone whose losses you can name.

"You gave it to me," she said. "Seven years ago, on the beach at Saltmere, with the royal procession burning behind you and the lightning coming down like judgment. You gave it to me to keep, along with a message, and you made me promise to tell you neither until you asked." She folded her hands. "You're asking now. Or you're close to it."

"That's not—" I stood up. Sat down. The stove ticked. Outside, the rain had stopped entirely, and the silence was worse. "I've never met you. I've never been to Saltmere. I don't—"

"Remember. No." She said it gently, horribly gently. "You don't. There's a great deal you don't, girl. You came out of that night with your life and your hands and precious little else. I watched you stand in the surf afterward, dry as bone in all that spray, trying to recall your own mother's face, and then you walked inland and forgot me too, exactly as you'd arranged." She pushed a cup of something hot across the table — I hadn't seen her pour it. "Drink. And then ask me the question you actually came here to ask."

I wrapped my hands around the cup because they needed occupation. "Which question is that?"

She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it was terrible.

"The one you wrote on the outside of volume nine, in your own hand, and then sealed under a spell so strong it took me three years to crack it." She turned the ledger toward me, to the back cover, to a line of writing I had no memory of making in handwriting that was unquestionably mine:

*If she comes asking: the crown is not empty. Tell her what she traded to learn it.*

The stove ticked. The jars of seawater gleamed on their shelves like a congregation waiting for a sermon.

"What did I trade?" I whispered.

The old woman leaned forward, and beneath the kelp-and-lamp-oil smell of her kitchen, the scent of lightning sharpened, close now, familiar, like something returning home.

"Not what, dear," she said. "*Who.* Sit down properly. This will take all night, and you'll wish it hadn't."
