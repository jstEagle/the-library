## 5. The Storm She Cannot Remember

The ledger said the village of Brackwater had sold forty-one jars of preserved herring to the Saltglass Guild in the spring of the year I was exiled. It also said, in my own cramped hand in the margins, that the herring had been paid for with a favor owed by a woman who could walk on water.

I had written *walk on water* and then underlined it twice, which is the clerical equivalent of screaming.

That was the thing about seven years of meticulous bookkeeping: you couldn't lie to yourself in ink. Somewhere between the bottled thunder and the coin minted by a dead king, still warm, still humming against my palm like a struck bell — somewhere between all of that, I had left myself messages. Little lighthouses. *Look here. Look here.* And every one of them pointed south along the coast, to a village that no longer appeared on any chart the Guild kept, in a bay where the tide came in wrong.

So I went.

*

Brackwater wasn't drowned so much as half-drowned, which is somehow worse. The sea had taken the lower street whole — you could still see chimneys standing out of the shallows at low tide like the fingers of drowning men — and the survivors who remained had simply moved uphill and stopped talking about it. They mended nets. They salted fish. They looked at strangers the way people look at weather that has already ruined them once.

I arrived at dusk with a false name, a merchant's papers, and the particular posture of a woman who wants nothing but a bed and a fair price for her horse. The posture is a lie I've practiced until it doesn't feel like one, which I suspect is true of most of my life.

The inn — if you could call three rooms above a smokehouse an inn — was run by a widow named Maren Ostrow, who took my coin without counting it, which told me she either trusted everyone or no one, and her eyes said it was the second one.

"Salt trade," I said. "Auditing accounts down the coast."

"No accounts down this coast worth auditing." She set down bread, smoked fish, something dark in a cup that might have been beer or might have been punishment. "Unless you're auditing why the herring left."

"The herring left?"

"Seven years back. All of them. One night." She wiped the table with a cloth that had given up on cleanliness years ago. "Whole shoals beached themselves rather than come into this bay. Fishermen know things. That's why they drink."

I ate and asked careful questions and got careful non-answers, and then an old man by the fire — the kind of old that comes from being broken and reassembled slightly wrong — began to laugh. Not cruelly. The way people laugh when a memory has finally caught up with them after years of running.

"You're the auditor," he said. "Come to audit us."

"I'm just passing through—"

"Stand up," he said.

Maren said, "Pell, leave the girl alone—"

"Stand up and turn around. Slowly."

And because there was a note under the fear in his voice, a note I recognized from the mirror, I stood up and turned around slowly, feeling like an idiot, feeling like a specimen, and behind me Pell made a sound like a man being forgiven for something.

"The lightning girl," he whispered. "You came back."

*

They put me in a chair by the fire, and they told me the story the way people tell stories they've told each other a hundred times, filling in each other's gaps, arguing over details, agreeing on the shape of it. Seven years ago. The royal procession coming down the coast road — the king's banner, the king's carriage, the king himself behind smoked glass because even then, apparently, someone knew he shouldn't be looked at directly.

And standing in the surf, ankle-deep, arms raised, a young woman calling lightning out of a clear sky.

"That was you," Maren said. She'd brought out a bottle of something older and worse than the first bottle, and we were all drinking it, which is what coastal people do instead of crying. "We thought you were one of ours at first. You had our look. Grey eyes, salt-pale. But you spoke like a city clerk. You apologized to the waves before you stepped into them. Polite as a debt collector."

"What did I say? Before the lightning?"

Pell's laugh again, thinner now. "Nothing. That's the thing nobody believes. No storm-words, no chanting. You just — asked. Stood there asking the sky for help like it was a neighbor. And the sky *came*. Came down the beach like it had been waiting seven hundred years for somebody to finally ask properly."

The lightning hit the procession road ahead of the carriage. Split a mile of cliff into the sea. Killed four guardsmen, I learned — four names, recited solemnly, because this village remembered its dead even when they were strangers' dead. The carriage turned back. The king lived.

"And then?" I asked.

"And then nothing," Maren said. "You walked out of the surf and your face was — wrong. Empty. Like a house with the windows blown out but the walls still standing. You asked us where you were. Asked us who you were. We told you your name was Vesper because that's the only word you kept saying, over and over, like a prayer or a bill of sale. Then the king's riders came through two days later with proclamations, and a storm was blamed, and you were gone before dawn. Took the coast road north. Never looked back."

"Because she couldn't," Pell said. "You don't look back at a door you don't remember walking through."

I sat with my cup going cold in my hands and felt grief arrive in the strangest costume: not sorrow for something lost, but vertigo — the specific horror of discovering the floor of your life has been missing the entire time and you've been walking on air with excellent posture.

I had tried to kill a king. Or stop him. Or warn him — the story wouldn't hold still long enough to tell me which. I had paid four strangers' lives and my own entire past for whatever I'd seen in that carriage, and I had done it so thoroughly that the woman sitting here now, taking notes in a ledger margin, didn't even have the decency to remember being brave.

"Why?" My voice cracked on it. "Why would I do that and then forget?"

Maren reached across the table and turned my hand palm-up, the way you check a worker's calluses, and looked at the faint pale scar across it that I've always assumed came from a paper knife.

"Because you paid," she said simply. "That's how it works. Everyone knows that. Power doesn't take coin. It takes *keeping*. Whatever you saw that made you stand in that water — you bought it, and memory was the price, and you signed the receipt yourself. Probably in that book you keep hugging like a child."

I looked down at my ledgers. Seven years of impossible trades, recorded in my own hand, in margins full of messages I'd left like breadcrumbs for a woman I'd known would come looking.

She hadn't forgotten everything. She'd just filed it.

*

I didn't sleep. I sat up with the ledgers open by candlelight while the tide did its wrong slow breathing outside, and I read the margins the way you read a letter from someone you loved who is now dead. There it was, in the spring entries of seven years ago — the last pages written in the old hand, looser, faster, a hand that hadn't yet learned to hide:

*A favor owed by a woman who can walk on water.*
*The crown pays its debts in salt.*
*He is not the king. He wears him.*

And beneath that, pressed hard enough to tear the page, three words repeated over and over like a man scratching at the inside of a coffin lid:

*THE HUNT REMEMBERS. THE HUNT REMEMBERS. THE HUNT REMEMBERS.*

I closed the book.

Outside, far down the coast road toward the capital, the sky flickered — not lightning, not quite. A light with patience in it. And Maren appeared in my doorway with a lantern held high, her face gone grey, and behind her I could hear Pell shouting from the bluff in a voice stripped down to its oldest animal layer:

"Riders! Riders with the king's colors — and they're not slowing for the tide!"

Maren looked at me, and I watched seventy years of caution lose to one moment of certainty.

"They've come for you," she said. "Haven't they. The hunt remembers."

And God help me, so did I — suddenly, partially, horribly — because the light on the horizon had a rhythm to it, and my body knew that rhythm the way a debt knows its collector, and the last thing my erased self had ever written was burning cold against my palms:

*Run north. Find the captain whose name is on the list. He hasn't been fed to it yet.*

*His name is Renner.*
