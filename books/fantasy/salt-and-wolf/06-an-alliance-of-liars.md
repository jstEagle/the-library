## 6. An Alliance of Liars

### Ivo

I had been tracking Vesper Nakamura for three days when I realized I was doing it badly on purpose.

That's the thing about a man who has spent twenty years learning to read tracks: you can't lie to yourself about what your feet are choosing. Her trail was easy — she'd fled Greyharbor with a satchel of ledgers and no provisions, heading south along the coast road toward the drowned places, and a woman carrying that much paper leaves a signature in every muddy ditch. Twice I found her camps cold and moved faster. The third morning, near the ruins of Sallowmere where the sea had eaten half the village and left the other half to rot politely, I smelled woodsmoke and knew my feet had finally caught up with my conscience, which had been lagging somewhere around the city gates with the sealed list of names still folded against my ribs.

My sister's name was third from the top. Maren Renner, hedge-healer, widow, terrible cook. The edict said *examination*. I had seen what examination meant. I had carried four people to the capital myself before I understood, and the understanding had arrived all at once, like stepping off a dock you thought was longer.

So here is what I told myself as I walked into the trees above Sallowmere with my hand nowhere near my sword: *I am making an arrest.* And here is what I actually intended, which I will write down because this account is the only ledger that will ever hold it: I wanted her to prove me wrong. Or right. Either one. I just wanted someone else to have seen it too, so that the shape of it wouldn't live only in my head, where it was starting to make rooms.

She heard me coming, of course. A storm-caller who hides for seven years hears everything. When I stepped into the clearing she was sitting by a small fire with her boots off — deliberately, I'd note later, so I'd know she wasn't running — and a ledger open across her knees like a shield made of arithmetic.

"Captain Renner," she said. "You walk loudly for a hunter."

"I walk honestly," I said. "It's the only way to track anyone."

"And yet you took three days to cover two."

I sat down across the fire without being invited, which is either surrender or siege, depending on how the evening goes. "You counted."

"You're a lawman. You count everything. It's why you're going to be useful." She closed the ledger. "Or why we're both going to die. Hard to say until we talk."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind us the sea did its patient work on the village bones.

"Tell me about the king," I said.

Her eyebrows went up — genuine surprise, the first honest expression I'd seen on her. "Straight to it. No 'come quietly, witch.'"

"The last woman I brought in quietly is in the palace cells. They don't come out. So no, I'm not going to pretend this is an arrest, because we'd both know I was lying, and I've had enough of lying for one career." I pulled the name-list from my coat and held it up. Sealed wax, crown sigil. "There are forty-one names under this seal. Mine is thirty-ninth."

"Yours?"

"They listed me as gifted." I let that sit a moment. "I've never called a storm or healed a wart in my life. But somebody upstairs needed the list full, and they put down names of anyone who'd ever looked at them sideways." I tossed the list onto her side of the fire. "Break the seal. Read it. Third name is my sister."

Vesper didn't touch it. She looked at it the way you look at a snake that might be dead. "Why give it to me?"

"Because if I'm going to commit treason, I want it done properly, with documentation. You strike me as a woman who keeps records."

For the first time in three days of pursuit, Vesper Nakamura laughed. It was a short, ugly sound, and I liked it immediately, because it meant she was frightened and covering it, which is the only kind of laughter worth trusting.

* * *

### Vesper

He broke his own seal for me, which told me more than anything he'd said. A man who guards a document for years and then hands it over within an hour of meeting you isn't reckless; he's been waiting for an excuse, and he wants you to be the one who gives it, so the sin lands on both accounts.

We traded truths the way smugglers trade cargo — carefully, one item at a time, each party checking the weight.

He gave me his sister first. Maren, hedge-healer, sets bones for fishermen's children and charges in soup. He talked about her the way men talk about things they've already decided to burn their lives down over, flat and fast, like reciting coordinates. Then he gave me the cells. What the examinations actually were. How the gifted came out hollow and smiling, how they thanked the examiners on the way to whatever room they went to afterward and were never seen again.

In exchange I gave him the drowned village. The survivors of Sallowmere — the eleven who still lived in the dry half — remembered me standing in the surf seven years ago with lightning climbing my arms like ivy, screaming words none of them could recall but all of them described the same way: *like a door opening in the sky.* The royal procession had been crossing the coast road below. The storm came down on the king's banners specifically, on the king himself, and then —

Nothing. In every memory, the moment after the lightning struck was gone. Not blurred, not faded. Cut. Eleven witnesses, eleven identical holes, and me at the center of them with seven blank years where my life should have been.

"They didn't cut just your memory," Ivo said slowly. "They cut everyone who saw you do it. That's not hiding evidence. That's surgery."

"That's appetite," I said. "Hiding is what you do with something you fear. This is what you do with something you eat."

He stared at the fire for a long time. I watched him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic I'd been doing since the old woman called me by a name I didn't remember choosing, since I found my own handwriting in the margins of ledgers I bought secondhand, writing instructions for a rite I had no memory of learning. *Unbinding,* my past self had written. *The vessel must consent. The debt must be named.*

"What was your storm trying to kill?" Ivo asked.

"A thing wearing the king," I said. "And it failed. And it took payment."

Here is where I should tell you what I felt, sitting across that fire from the man sent to arrest me: relief. Ugly, shameful relief. Seven years I had built my careful clerical life on the belief that I was a monster with amnesia, that I had called down death on a royal procession for reasons my own erased heart kept from me, that exile was mercy. One conversation, and the monster turned out to be a victim twice over — once by the thing in the crown, once by whoever I'd been before it got to me. Relief, and beneath the relief, rage with a long slow fuse.

"So here's my proposition," I said. "You get me inside the palace. As your prisoner — that part's true enough, you can even bind my hands if it helps your sleep. I find proof the king is bound to something older than the crown. Proof that would convince a council, a general, a mob, anyone."

"And if there's no proof?"

"There's proof. My own handwriting says so, and I trust my handwriting more than I trust my memory, which currently consists of seven years of bookkeeping."

"And once you have your proof?"

I fed a stick to the fire. "Then we decide what to do with it. Together. That's the whole bargain — I'm not offering you a plan, Captain, because plans made between strangers are lies with a schedule. I'm offering you a shared crime scene."

He was quiet for a while. A man trained to betray weighs betrayal differently than the rest of us; he doesn't ask *can I trust her*, he asks *what does she gain by deceiving me, and does the math favor it*. I could see him running it.

"You could turn me in," he said finally. "Walk into any garrison, show them my face next to that list, claim I tried to extort you. You'd be rewarded. Freed."

"Yes."

"Why wouldn't I?"

"Because you pocketed the list instead of executing it," I said, "which means you already committed treason, alone, in the dark, where nobody would ever know. Men who betray in secret for no reward aren't traitors, Captain. They're just honest men who ran out of lawful options. I've spent seven years reading ledgers. I know the difference between a falsified entry and a desperate one."

Something in his face settled, like a ship taking ballast.

"We go in as prisoner and captor," he said. "You follow my lead on protocol. If I say run, you run."

"If you say run, I'll assess whether running serves me and act accordingly," I said. "I'm a liar too, Captain. That's rather the title of our arrangement."

Despite himself — I watched it happen — he almost smiled.

"One condition," he said, standing, brushing off his coat. "When this is done, whichever of us survives writes down what happened. All of it. Names, debts, truth."

I looked at him for a moment longer than the joke required, because he'd just quoted, almost word for word, a line written in my own lost hand in the margin of a ledger he had never seen.

"Agreed," I said. "It's what I'm best at."

We broke camp before dawn. He walked ahead of me with my wrists bound loosely in front of me — loosely enough to slip, tight enough to satisfy any checkpoint — and if either of us noticed that the storm clouds gathering over the capital looked less like weather than like a held breath, neither of us said so aloud.

But I counted them. Lawmen count everything.

So do storms.
