## 7. The Examination Halls

The trick to being a prisoner, I had learned in the first hour of the wagon ride south, was to be exactly as frightened as they expected and not one ounce more. Too calm and they suspected a witch's composure. Too wild and they bound your hands twice and watched you all night. So I sat in the straw with my wrists chafed raw by honest rope — Ivo had insisted on honest rope; "the guards check knots," he'd said, which was either tradecraft or an apology — and I let my face do the work while my mind did the arithmetic.

Three days to the capital. Two days of corridors. One night in the cells before Ivo presented me to whoever wore the king these days.

I had rehearsed my name until it stopped feeling like mine, which was convenient, since it wasn't. *Maren Ostwick*, hedge-witch, caught brewing charms in Greyharbor's fishmarket. The ledgers were hidden in a false bottom of my trunk, which was now in a confiscation room somewhere, which meant my whole life was sitting in a government warehouse waiting for someone literate and curious. I tried not to think about that. I thought about it constantly.

"You're doing it again," Ivo murmured from the bench across the cell. He was playing the loyal captain, come to gloat over his prize. He was very good at it. It unsettled me how good he was at it.

"Doing what?"

"Counting exits."

"I'm auditing," I said. "There's a difference. Counting exits is hope. Auditing is planning."

He almost smiled. In the lamplight, with his captain's face on, it looked like a wolf deciding whether something was food or family.

* * *

They took me down on the fourth morning, past the kitchens, past the barracks, down stairs that were older than the palace built around them. That was the first wrong thing. Palaces grow upward — kings build towers so the world knows where they stand. Nobody builds *down* unless there's something they want kept close and quiet.

The stones changed underfoot. Greyharbor granite gave way to something darker, veined with pale threads like frost in wood. My skin prickled. Not fear — recognition. I knew this stone. I didn't know how I knew this stone, but some part of me that predated seven years of ledgers put its hand flat against the memory and said: *you have stood here.*

I filed that away with all the other holes in my exile and kept my chin down like a properly terrified hedge-witch.

The corridor opened into what the edicts called the Examination Halls, and I will give the crown this much: the name was honest, if you examined the word *examination* closely enough. A long vaulted chamber, cells along both walls — not barred, but walled in glass thick as my forearm, each one lit from above by a shaft of white light that didn't come from any window I could find. And in each cell, a person.

That was the second wrong thing. They weren't chained. They weren't struggling. They sat or stood or lay down according to their own mild preference, and every one of them was smiling.

A woman near the front — young, hair cropped short, a burn scar across her jaw that spoke of fire-gift gone wrong — pressed her palm to the glass as I passed. She looked straight through me. Her lips moved. No sound came out, but the light above her dimmed, just slightly, the way a candle gutters when something drinks the air around it.

"Fresh stock," said the examiner.

He was a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the cheerful efficiency of a customs clerk, which should have comforted me and instead made everything worse. I know clerks. I am a clerk. Clerks who smile like that are clerks whose ledgers would make you weep.

"Maren Ostwick," he read off a slate. "Charm-craft, minor divination, no formal training. Greyharbor." He looked up and smiled at me with genuine warmth. "You're lucky, actually. We've been thin on weather-adjacent gifts since the storm-talker."

Something in my chest went very still, the way a bird goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"The storm-talker," I said. Careful. Curious, not hungry.

"Seven years back." He made a note on his slate. "Before my time, but they still talk about it. Pulled enough power out of her to run the halls for a year." He tapped the glass wall beside him fondly, the way a man taps a good barrel of wine. "You can tell the old cells by the frost. They remember being full."

The pale veins in the dark stone. The light that guttered. The people who smiled because whatever was inside them had been poured out so gently, so *thoroughly*, that nothing remained to grieve with.

These weren't examinations. They were harvests. And I was standing in a cellar built to feed something vast and patient, and the vintage was human souls.

I let my knees shake. It wasn't entirely performance.

* * *

They put me in a holding cell — iron bars, thank the salt gods, honest ugly iron — to await processing. Processing took two days. I spent them doing what I do best: keeping accounts.

The examiner came twice daily with his slate. Guards rotated on a schedule I could set a clock by. The light-shafts brightened at dawn and dusk, and when they did, the harvested ones turned their faces up like sunflowers, and the frost-veins in the stone pulsed faintly, and somewhere far below — below even this place — something swallowed.

I counted the cells. Forty-one occupied. I counted the empty ones, the frosted-old ones. Sixty-three. Over a hundred gifted people, taken in seven years, and the hunt had only just begun in earnest. The edict hadn't created the harvest. It had industrialized it.

On the second evening, Ivo came down in his captain's coat, and the guard let him into the corridor without question, because Captain Renner was famous now, the man who'd delivered the storm-witch of Greyharbor.

"You look well," he said quietly, "for a woman about to be processed."

"I've seen the menu. I'm not hungry." I gripped the bars. "Ivo. The cells. Did you know?"

"No." His jaw worked. "I knew the examination rates were low. One in ten ever released. I told myself the rest were executed." He stared at the smiling woman across the corridor. "I told myself a lot of things. It turns out I'm a very good liar after all. You'll appreciate that."

"What happens in processing?"

"They bring you before the light and ask you questions. Childhood. First time your gift showed. Your mother's voice. Your worst grief." His voice flattened. "It sounds like mercy. Gently asked, gently answered. Then you walk into the cell and sit down and smile, and the light drinks everything you told them, and everything you didn't."

Memory. It drank *memory*. Power and memory together, the way wine carries the flavor of the soil.

And seven years ago, something had drunk *me*. Whole. Not a cell, not a gentle questioning — a single catastrophic draught, a storm called down on a royal procession and then erased from the drinker's own cup. I had walked out of the surf with sixty years of my life missing and a hole shaped like lightning where the rest should be.

The entity hadn't just survived my storm. It had eaten the evidence.

"Ivo," I said. "The old woman. From Greyharbor. Sela, she called herself — the one who sheltered me after the raid. She was taken the next morning. Is she here?"

He hesitated. That hesitation was its own answer.

"She's here," he said. "Third gallery. I saw the intake list."

"Take me to her."

"That's not — Vesper, if anyone sees—"

"You owe me a prison break anyway. Consider this a partial payment."

* * *

Getting to the third gallery required Ivo to be magnificent and me to be invisible, which between us we managed with only one incident involving a startled page, a dropped tray, and my sincere promise to haunt him. The third gallery was older than the others. The glass here was clouded, the light-shafts dimmer, the cells half-empty — the vintage of earlier years, weaker stuff, mostly forgotten.

She was in the last cell.

Sela sat on a stool with her hands folded in her lap, and she was smiling, and the smile was the worst thing I had ever seen, worse than the raid, worse than the rope, because it was *peaceful*. Whatever had lived behind those sharp old eyes — the woman who had known my true name on sight, who had fed me stew and called me by a name I didn't remember choosing — was gone. Poured out. Drunk.

"Sela." I put my hand against the glass. "Sela, it's me. It's—" I almost said Maren, then hated myself. "It's the girl from the storm. The one you sheltered."

Her head turned. For one heartbeat something moved behind her eyes, deep down, like a fish under winter ice. Her mouth opened.

"*Vesper,*" she said.

My own name, in her ruined voice, went through me like a hook.

Then the ice closed over. The smile returned, serene and vacant, and she turned her face up toward the dimming light, and she began to hum — an old salt-song, a blessing for the drowned, the kind fishermen sing when there's no body to bury. She hummed it perfectly. She hummed it the way you hum something when the feeling has been taken out and only the shape remains.

I stood there with my hand on the glass until my palm went numb, and the cold fury that had been building since the wagon ride finally finished hardening into something with edges. Not rage. Rage is hot and spends itself. This was colder and more patient. This was the fury of a ledger-keeper looking at columns that don't balance and understanding, at last, exactly what has been stolen, and from whom, and by what right — *no right at all.*

Forty-one people smiling in the dark. An old woman humming a funeral song for herself. A kingdom drinking its own protectors to keep a thing alive in a crown.

I was going to unmake it. Whatever it cost — my memories, my name, the seven years I'd already lost — I was going to stand in that throne room and open every account this thing had ever kept, and I was going to call the debt due.

"Vesper." Ivo's hand was on my shoulder. "We have to go. The king's steward sent for me. Tomorrow morning." His face in the gloom was grey. "He wants to meet the woman who caught the storm-witch. Which means tomorrow morning, you get your audience."

Below our feet, faint as a heartbeat, something vast and patient swallowed, and waited to be introduced.
