## 8. Audience

The palace at Carrowmere smelled the way I imagined a wound would smell if it could afford incense.

I'd been in great houses before — manor courts, garrison halls, the governor's residence back when Greyharbor still pretended to have a governor. They all had the same architecture of power: high ceilings to make you feel small, long walks to make you feel late, guards positioned to remind you that feeling small and late was a privilege. The royal palace had all of that, and underneath it, something else. A hum. Not sound, exactly. More like the pressure before a storm, except it came from below, from the foundations, from whatever the palace had been built on top of and never told anyone about.

Vesper felt it too. I knew because she went very quiet, and Vesper Nakamura quiet was a different animal than Vesper Nakamura silent. Silent meant she was choosing not to speak. Quiet meant she was listening with parts of herself she didn't have names for.

"Stop doing that," I murmured. We were walking the Long Gallery, me two paces ahead of my prisoner, her wrists bound in front of her with rope I'd tied myself — loose enough to slip, tight enough to look sincere. "Whatever it is you're doing."

"Listening."

"Don't."

"You don't get to give me orders anymore, Captain. You gave up the law when you pocketed the list." She said it without heat, which was worse. "Besides, it's already listening back. Can't you feel it? It's *interested* in us."

I could feel it. That was the problem. Seven years hunting gifted folk had given me a nose for the wrongness in them, a sense I'd once described to my sister as smelling lightning before it struck. Lira had laughed and said that made me a dog. Lira, whose name sat third on a sealed list folded against my heart like a knife I kept forgetting to use.

"Keep your eyes down," I said instead. "You're a prisoner. Prisoners don't listen. Prisoners count the steps to their cell and pray."

"Which are you?"

"Neither. I'm a man about to commit treason in front of witnesses who can dissolve him." I glanced back. Under the hood they'd given her, her eyes caught the gallery's candlelight and held it a beat too long, like the light was asking permission. "Any regrets?"

"About the plan? Several. About you?" She considered. "Ask me again if we survive the throne room."

* * *

They took her from me at the doors.

That hadn't been the plan. The plan — Vesper's plan, refined over three nights of firelight and mutual distrust into something almost elegant — was that I present her as my prize capture, the hunt's first genuine storm-caller in a generation, and that my service earned me the audience I'd been requesting for a month through channels that kept swallowing my letters. Then, in the king's presence, I would lay out what I'd found: the examinations, the harvests, the pattern of emptied villages radiating outward from the capital like ripples from a stone dropped in black water.

Simple. Suicidal, but simple.

Instead, four men in white-and-gold took her arms at the threshold of the Audience Chamber, and a chamberlain with a voice like dry paper said, "His Majesty will receive the Captain alone. The prisoner will be held in readiness."

"In readiness," I repeated.

"For examination," said the chamberlain, and smiled with a mouth that did all the work while his eyes stayed closed to the business, like a shopkeeper's shutters.

Every instinct I owned screamed at once. Handing Vesper to them even for an hour was handing a lit lamp to a man standing in spilled oil. But refusing was its own confession, and I had come too far and burned too many bridges to be arrested in a hallway on suspicion of *hesitation*. So I watched them take her — she looked back once, and her expression said, plainly, *don't do anything stupid without me* — and I walked alone into the presence of my king.

* * *

He was smaller than his coinage.

You grow up with a face on every stamp and seal and gallows-poster, and you forget it belongs to a body. King Aldric IV, by the grace of the Nine and the blood of the First Charter, was a narrow-shouldered man in his fifties with ink stains he never quite scrubbed from his right hand and a habit of rubbing his thumb across his knuckles when he thought no one watched. He'd been a decent king, once. Roads mended. Granaries filled. There had been years when the word *Aldric* in a market square got nods instead of spit.

Now he sat the throne like a man sitting in a river, letting the current move him, and the crown on his head was old iron worked with patterns that hurt to look at directly — not ugly, just *wrong*, the way a word repeated too many times becomes noise.

"Captain Renner." His voice was his own. Tired. Human. "Your letters were... insistent."

"Forgive the insistence, Majesty. The matter wouldn't wait."

"Matters rarely do. That's how they get to be matters." He gestured, and the chamber emptied — chamberlain, guards, the scribes who recorded everything, all of it, always. Which meant this conversation would exist nowhere but here. Someone had arranged that. I doubted it was the king.

When the doors closed, Aldric looked at me for a long moment, and then he did something no king does. He slumped. Shoulders dropping, head bowing until the crown's weight seemed less ornament than yoke. And when he spoke again, his voice had changed — not louder, not softer, just *nearer*, the way a voice is near when it comes from a few feet away instead of a throne's ceremonial distance.

"Ivo." My name. Not *Captain*. Not *Renner*. "Ivo Renner. You have your sister's chin. Did you know that? Lira. She used to leave offerings at the boundary stones — bread and honey, for the field-wights. Your mother scolded her. Your father pretended not to see."

Cold walked up my spine one vertebra at a time. "Majesty—"

"She's alive. On your list. Third name." He raised his head, and his eyes were wet and furious and *his*, entirely his, a man looking out of a window from a house that wasn't his anymore. "Listen to me because I don't know how much longer I have this hour, and the hours are getting shorter. The thing you're hunting for — the thing your edict serves — it isn't in the kingdom. It's *on* the kingdom. It's on *me*. It has been for eleven years, and everything since — the examinations, the lists, the hunt — is it feeding. Every gifted soul your hunters drag in, it drinks them down and grows a little more patient and a little more certain that no one will ever stop it."

"The entity." Vesper's word. I'd thought it a scholar's abstraction. Hearing it confirmed by the crown itself rearranged my whole skeleton.

"It came as a bargain. They always do. Drought year, failing harvests, a young king desperate enough to listen to something ancient that spoke kindly." He laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh. "It saved the harvest. It asked so little. A crown to wear. A voice to borrow. Just a little, it said. Just sometimes. Eleven years of *just sometimes*, Ivo, and now I surface like a drowning man between waves, and each wave lasts longer than the last."

"Then let me end it. Give me the terms of the binding. There must be terms—"

"There are. Written where it can't reach them, in a hand it erased from the world." His gaze sharpened. "There was a woman. Seven years ago. She saw it — the only one who ever saw it whole — and she called a storm down on this palace that should have torn the binding out by the roots. It stopped her. It couldn't kill her — the storm was hers, rooted too deep — so it did worse. It took the night away from her. Took her memory of it, and buried her so deep in exile she'd never dig her way back."

My mouth had gone dry. "There's a woman in your cells downstairs wearing a hood."

Something moved across the king's face — hope, terrible and fragile, like frost forming on a pond. "Nakamura," he breathed. "Vesper Nakamura. Then the ledgers survived. Then she—" He stopped. His hand flew to his throat, clawing at nothing, and for one heartbeat his whole body seized like a puppet checking its strings.

And when he looked up, he was smiling.

It was still Aldric's face. It was not Aldric's smile. Nothing that lived behind a human face should smile like that — wide, unhurried, delighted, the smile of something that had been waiting a very long time and had just heard footsteps it recognized.

"Captain," it said, and the voice was layered now, his voice with a vast patience underneath it, the way bedrock sits under soil. "How good of you to bring her. We had wondered. Seven years, and she finally comes home."

I did not draw my sword. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger of my own: there was a moment when drawing steel on my king felt like the only honest act left, and I did not do it, because my hands remembered Vesper's voice saying *it wants you to. Fear is a door. Don't open it.*

"What do you want," I said.

"Want? Such a small word for such large appetites." The king rose from the throne, and the movement was wrong — too smooth, joints moving like water finding its level. He descended the dais step by step, iron crown gleaming, and stopped close enough that I could smell him: cold stone and ozone and, beneath it, faintly, the sea after a storm. "Here is what I offer, Captain, since you've been so diligent. The woman downstairs is missing a night. One night. The most important night of her life, carved out of her like the pit of a fruit. I have it. All of it. Every thunderclap. Every choice. Every reason."

"Vesper doesn't trade with you."

"Vesper hasn't heard the price yet." The smile widened a fraction. "Her memories for the ledgers. The ones she carries. The margins, especially — the little rite written in a hand she doesn't recognize as her own. Bring them to me, and she walks out of this palace whole. Complete. Seven years returned to her, every grief and every glory. She'll know why she exiled herself. She'll know *who she was*. Isn't that worth more than paper?"

"And if she refuses?"

"Then she stays incomplete forever, and you stay a man who handed a storm-caller to the thing she tried to kill." It tilted the king's head, studying me with Aldric's tired eyes. "But you'll say yes, Captain. You'll persuade her. Because you carry a list with your sister's name on it, and I can make that list very short indeed — or very, very long. Family is such useful leverage. You people build your whole hearts out of it."

The wave broke. I watched it happen — the smile collapsing, the body sagging, Aldric surfacing gasping into his own face, tears cutting down through the powder of kingship. "Renner," he whispered, gripping my sleeve with ink-stained fingers. "Don't let it have her. Whatever it offers. Don't let it have her. End it. Please. *End it—*"

Guards opened the doors. The chamberlain's dry voice announced the return of the prisoner. And I stood in the middle of the Audience Chamber holding the worst knowledge of my life: that my king was a hostage, that my hunt was a feeding trough, and that in a few minutes I would have to look Vesper Nakamura in the eye and tell her the thing she wanted most in the world was being sold by the thing she hated most — and that part of me, the part with Lira's name folded against his heart, wanted her to say yes.

They brought her in flanked by white and gold. She pulled free of the guards' hands the moment they loosened — they let her, I noticed; everything here was allowed, everything was arranged — and crossed the floor to stand beside me, hood back, eyes going from the king's empty face to mine.

"Well?" she said quietly. "What did I miss?"

I opened my mouth to tell her, and found I already knew, with sick certainty, exactly which words she would refuse to hear.
