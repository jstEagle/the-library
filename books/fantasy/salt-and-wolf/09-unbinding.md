## 9. Unbinding

The throne room smelled of salt and old incense, which struck me as a small cosmic joke. Salt was mine. It had been mine since before I could write my own name — the ledger said so, in handwriting I still couldn't quite believe belonged to me: *V. Nakamura, apprentice auditor, notes on binding rites, do not attempt alone.* Whoever I had been seven years ago had known exactly what she was doing and had left me instructions like a woman leaving a note for a burglar.

*Do not attempt alone.*

Well. I'd brought Ivo Renner. That counted as not-alone, if you were generous.

The king sat on his throne beneath banners that hadn't been dusted since the last good reign, and the thing wearing him watched me with eyes that were patient the way tide is patient. Around us stood twelve guards in royal black, and behind them, filling the great doors, the sound of a city holding its breath.

"You refuse," the entity said through the king's mouth. Both voices at once now — the thin human one underneath, cracked and tired, and the vast one over it, layered like sediment. "I offer you everything you have spent seven years clawing toward. Your storm. Your night. The name you gave yourself standing in the surf. And you refuse."

"I'm an auditor," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "We're trained to recognize bad terms. You're offering to sell me my own property back at a markup. That's not generosity, that's usury."

Something moved behind the king's face — amusement, maybe, or hunger; with entities of that vintage the two are difficult to distinguish. "And what will you do instead, little clerk?"

"An audit." I set the ledgers down on the flagstones between us, all nine of them, their spines cracked, their margins crowded with my lost self's cramped, slanting hand. "Full reconciliation of accounts. You've been drinking this kingdom for centuries — its gifted, its memories, its names. Every harvest went into your ledger, and every ledger balances." I knelt and opened the first volume. "Yours is about to."

"Don't," said the king.

Not the entity. *Him.* The human voice, alone for the first time, small and ragged as a man shouting up from the bottom of a well. His eyes — his actual eyes, brown and human and drowning — found mine across the length of the throne room. "Girl. Whatever it costs. Don't let it bargain. It bargains the way rot bargains."

"Your Majesty," I said, and bowed, because some habits survive anything, "with respect, I'm not here to bargain. I'm here to close the books."

Then I began to read aloud.

* * *

The rite was written in the margins of a trade record dated eleven years ago — bottled thunder, three vials, buyer unknown — and threaded through it like veins through marble were annotations in my own hand. *Anchor words. Speak them against the current of it. Do not stop when it shows you what you paid.* That last line was underlined twice, and whoever I had been had pressed hard enough to nearly tear the page.

So I did not stop.

The first anchor word left my mouth and the candles guttered sideways, all of them, as though the room had tilted. The guards shifted. Ivo moved — I heard him, boots on stone, placing himself between me and the throne with the unhurried confidence of a man who has decided exactly how much trouble he is willing to be.

"Captain Renner," the entity said pleasantly, in the king's voice, "arrest her."

"No," Ivo said.

"No?" Twelve guards looked at each other. It is remarkable how much chaos a single honest word can cause in a room full of men who have spent their careers pretending not to notice things.

"The edict says seize the gifted for examination," Ivo continued, drawing his sword — not pointing it at anyone, just holding it, the way you hold a fact you can't put down. "I've seen the examinations. I've carried the orders. And I am telling you, whatever you are, that I'm done being the hand that feeds you." He planted himself. "Anyone who touches her goes through me, and I want to be clear that I'm very tired and extremely motivated."

The entity considered him the way a scholar considers a footnote.

Then it turned back to me, and stopped pretending to be a king.

The change was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. The man on the throne simply became *less* — less present, less lit, a shape worn hollow by something vast occupying it — and the air of the throne room thickened until breathing felt like a negotiation. The pressure of it settled on my shoulders like wet wool.

"You read my accounts," it said, and the voice no longer bothered with the king's throat at all; it came from everywhere, from the stones, from the salt in the air. "Very well, auditor. Let us reconcile. But understand the currency."

"I know the currency." I kept reading. Second anchor word. Third. "You take memory. You've been taking it since before Greyharbor had a name. So pay up."

"It will not be the memories you would choose to lose."

"I know that too."

"It will be *all* of them."

My hand paused on the page. Just for a heartbeat. Long enough for me to see, laid out plain and terrible, what I was agreeing to: the drowned village, the surf, the old woman's shelter, Ivo's fire, the taste of guild coffee at dawn, the smell of ink, the small daily architecture of a life I had built plank by plank out of the wreckage of the one they took from me. All of it. Everything. Again.

Seven years ago, someone with my face had stood in the sea and paid this same price to slow this same thing down, and had thought it worth it, and had written *do not attempt alone* because she knew someday I'd be standing where she stood.

"Vesper." Ivo's voice, low, from behind me. Not arguing. Just saying my name, the way you check that a lamp is still burning.

"That's the idea," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let it crack, because I was done performing calm for anyone. "Keep the name handy, Captain. Someone should remember it existed."

Fourth anchor word.

* * *

It tore.

There is no gentler word for it. The entity did not reach into my head like a thief; it reached in like a creditor, like a flood, like hands made of cold water, and it began to pull. The first thing it took was recent — I felt the fire go, Ivo's face across the flames, our bargain, gone as cleanly as a page torn out — and I understood with awful clarity that it was eating backward, newest first, making me watch.

I kept reading.

Fifth anchor word. The candles died entirely. Something in the walls groaned, deep, structural, the sound a ship makes deciding whether to break. The guards had backed away to the far doors; Ivo had not moved from his patch of floor, though he'd gone white as gull-bone, and he was talking — steady, low, reciting something. Names. He was reciting names off the hunt's list, his sister's among them, anchoring himself the only way he knew how.

Sixth anchor word. The entity took the Guild then — the smell of the counting room, Master Corvin's cough, the particular squeak of the third-floor stair — and I grieved it even as I understood it was the least of what I owed. Then the old woman's kitchen. Her hands around a cup. Her voice saying a name I didn't recognize: *storm-caller.* Gone.

Seventh. The drowned village. The survivors' faces. The surf.

Eighth —

And there I was, suddenly, seven years younger, standing in cold water under a sky the color of a bruise, watching a royal procession crawl along the cliff road, and I remembered — I *remembered* — why. Not the storm. The reason. The king's carriage passing, and inside it something looking out through the king's eyes at the crowd of gifted villagers gathered to greet him, and the thing smiling, and me understanding in one lightning-flash of clarity what that smile meant, what the "examinations" would mean, what had been feeding on this coast since before my grandmother's grandmother. I remembered raising my hands. I remembered calling the sky down.

I remembered choosing it.

*Ninth anchor word,* said my own handwriting, somewhere very far away, on a page I could no longer see. *This is the one that costs. This is the one that buys.*

The entity screamed — a soundless scream, a pressure-scream, the whole throne room flexing around it — and I felt it understand, too late, what the rite actually was. Not a banishment. Banishment it could wait out; it had waited out centuries. No. The ninth word was a transfer. A closing of accounts. Everything it had taken, everything it was, funneled into a single vessel strong enough — mad enough — to hold it.

Me.

"Vesper—" Ivo again, closer now, shouting over a wind that had come from nowhere, from *me*, the suppressed gift finally off its leash and howling through the hall, tearing banners, scattering guards. "Vesper, what happens to you?"

I tried to answer. What came out was the ninth word.

* * *

The world went white, then quiet, then white again, and when it settled I was kneeling on the flagstones with nine open ledgers around me and a crown lying on the steps of the empty throne — just a crown now, gold and cold and ordinary, and a king collapsed beside it, breathing, weeping, human, seventy years of stolen life flooding back into him all at once like blood into a numb limb.

The wind was dying. Through the high windows I could see the sky over Greyharbor splitting open — not into violence but into rain, clean rain, the first honest weather this coast had seen in seven years.

I knew what I was carrying. I could feel it coiled in me, vast and furious and patient, hating me with a hatred that would outlast kingdoms. I knew what the margins said about that, too — the last note, the one I'd saved for this exact moment, in handwriting going blurry even as I read it:

*One vessel can hold it. One vessel can carry it where nothing living follows. The sea takes what the land cannot keep. Write everything down first. Debts, names, truths. Ink remembers what flesh forgives.*

Ink remembers.

I picked up the pen.

Somewhere behind me, Ivo Renner was saying my name, and I was already forgetting why it mattered — no. Not forgetting. *Spending.* There was a difference, and the difference was that I chose it, and the choice was the last thing I wrote down, in letters that shook, in a margin, in the dark:

*Entry final. All accounts closed. Let the record show that someone paid.*

I stood. The throne room door was open. Beyond it, down through the waking city, the sea was waiting — and it had always been waiting, and now, at last, I remembered the way.
