## 10. Ledger Closed

*Entry the last. Saltglass Guild, ledger nine, page —*

*Page. Page. What page.*

There is a thing in my chest that is not mine.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the whole of my life for seven years. Precision kept me alive. A number entered correctly is a small prayer against chaos; a column that balances is a wall between you and the lightning. I built my exile out of neat figures and dry ink, and I told myself it was a life.

So: precisely. There is a thing in my chest. It is old, and it is hungry, and it has been drinking kings from the inside out for longer than Greyharbor has had a name. And as of an hour ago — or a year, time has gone soft at the edges like wet paper — it lives in me instead of him.

It does not scream. That's the worst of it. I expected screaming. It *settles*, the way silt settles in a harbor after a storm, patient, certain that all water comes back to it eventually. When I close my eyes I can feel it reading me the way I used to read ledgers. Line by line. Looking for errors.

*I am not an error,* I tell it.

It laughs without sound, which is somehow worse than sound.

* * *

Fragments now. Forgive me. The margins are where I keep what I cannot say plainly.

— Ivo's face when the binding took. Not relief. Grief wearing relief's coat, because he understood before I said it aloud what taking the crown's burden meant. He always understands too fast. It's his most annoying quality and I have decided to treasure it.

— The king, on his knees on the throne room floor, weeping like a man who has set down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. Seven years he has been a door with something standing behind it. He looked at me and said, "Your name, girl. Tell me your name so I can pray for you properly." I told him. He didn't know it. Nobody knows it. That's rather the point.

— My own handwriting, looping across the margins of ledger after ledger, seven years of entries I don't remember making: *the rite is written backwards so they won't find it. the price is memory. the price is memory. the price is memory.* Apparently past-me was fond of repetition under pressure. Apparently past-me knew exactly what she was doing and did it anyway.

— The entity, as I pulled it out of the king and into myself, whispering its offer one final time: *your memories, whole, returned, if you only let me stay where I am warm.* And me answering with the words I wrote in a margin seven years ago by candlelight in a drowned village: *No trade. No bargain. Ledger closed.*

The memories did not come back anyway. I checked. I have been checking all morning. There is a hole where the storm should be, where the surf and the lightning and the girl standing in it should be, and the hole is shaped exactly like me.

* * *

Greyharbor smells like rain coming. Real rain, clean rain, the first honest weather this coast has seen since I called the other kind down onto a royal procession and paid for it in everything I was.

I am sitting in the Saltglass Guild's back room, which is rubble now except for this table, because some things are sturdier than buildings. The ledgers are stacked in front of me. Nine volumes of impossible trades — bottled thunder, bottled grief, a widow's second heart sold to a man who wanted to love twice as hard — and every margin annotated in a hand I know better than my own, because it is my own, just a version of me who remembered why any of this mattered.

Ivo stands in the doorway holding the hunt's list. He has been holding it for an hour. It's a long list. Sealed in the king's own wax, names of gifted folk from every port and village on the coast, each one destined for the examination halls, each one destined to come out smiling and empty like Marta did, like Marta will forever, sitting in her ruined kitchen smiling at nothing while whatever made her kind and sharp and stubborn drains into a crown.

"Captain," I say, "you're going to crease that."

"It's evidence," he says. His voice is steady. His hands are not. "Evidence of crimes. It should go to — " He stops. We both look at the same empty space where a functioning justice system would theoretically stand. "There's no one to give it to."

"There's fire," I say.

He looks at the list. Then at me. Then, briefly, at the window, where the sky over Greyharbor is the color of a bruise healing — purple going gold at the edges, which is not a color storms usually make, unless the storm itself is being unmade.

"My sister's name is on it," he says quietly. "Third page. In case there was ever any doubt about what I was carrying."

"I know."

"If I burn it, those people stay free. Also hunted, also frightened, but free, and their names belong to themselves again." He turns the pages slowly, like a man saying goodbye to each face individually. "And if I keep it —"

"You'll keep telling yourself it's evidence."

"Yes."

"Captain Renner." I put my hand flat on the top ledger. "You once told me the law was the only thing between this coast and the wolves. You were wrong, but you were wrong honorably, which is rarer than being right. Burn it. Let the law catch up to justice later. Justice shouldn't have to wait for permission."

He struck the flint on the third try. I counted. Some habits survive anything, even possession by an ancient hunger.

The list caught light along the seal first — the king's wax burning green, then black, then gone — and Ivo held it until the flames licked his fingers, because he is dramatic, and dropped it into the cold hearth, and we watched seven years of predation curl up into ash and smoke that smelled, faintly, stupidly, like a hearth fire in winter.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet. I'm about to do something unforgivable."

* * *

Here is what binding means, for the record, since record is all that will remain:

An entity like the one in the crown cannot be killed. It can only be moved. And it can only be held by something that consents to hold it — that is the flaw in it, the crack, the reason it needed a king bound and drugged and hollowed rather than simply taken. Consent. Willing hands. A vessel that says yes and keeps saying yes, forever, because the moment the vessel says no, the thing spills out and goes looking for a warmer door.

A kingdom could not consent. A king stopped consenting decades ago and has been a prisoner ever since.

But a woman who has already lost everything she was? A woman whose past is a hole and whose future is a tide?

She can consent just fine.

* * *

The final entry. I write it slowly, because my hands shake now in a way they never did before, and because I want it legible. Legible for whom, I couldn't say. Perhaps for the sea. Perhaps for whoever finds these books in a hundred years and thinks them fables.

*Saltglass Guild, ledger nine, final entry.*

*Debts owed: To Captain Ivo Renner, the truth, freely given: the hunt was never law, it was feeding, and he ended it tonight with fire and a locked door and the best lie of his career. To King Aldric, my apology for the seven years I could have acted and did not remember why. To the village of Lowmarsh, drowned and half-forgotten: you were right about me. To Marta, who sheltered a stranger and called her by her true name and paid for it: your name is here. It stays here. Nothing drinks it now.*

*Debts collected: One kingdom, saved. One king, returned to himself. One storm, finally clean.*

*Assets surrendered: Everything else.*

*Filed by: Vesper Nakamura. Storm-caller. Exile. Clerk. Vessel.*

*Ledger closed.*

I blow on the ink until it dries. Outside, the first drops hit the cobbles, fat and warm and ordinary, and somewhere down the street a fishwife swears cheerfully at the weather, and I think: that. That is what I bought. Fishwives swearing at rain. Children running through puddles. A coast that gets to be boringly, blessedly mortal.

The thing in my chest shifts, testing me, tasting the edges of my will.

*You will tire,* it says, in the voice of deep water. *Everyone tires. And when you sleep, when you grieve, when you grow old and your consent goes soft —*

"Then you'll still be stuck with me," I say aloud, and Ivo glances over, and I wave him off. "I'm a bookkeeper, monster. I balance accounts for a living. You think I don't know how to carry a debt?"

It subsides. Sulkily, I think. Ancient horrors, it turns out, can sulk.

* * *

Ivo walks me to the water. Of course he does. He argues the entire way, quietly, furiously, the way he argued in the throne room and on the stairs and in the doorway while the binding took — that there must be another way, that we'll find one, that he'll chain the thing to a rock himself, that he is not, absolutely not, going to stand on this beach and watch.

"You are," I say. "Because someone has to remember, and you're the only one left who can."

"The ledgers remember."

"The ledgers are paper, Captain. Paper burns. You've proven that twice tonight." I stop at the tideline. The sea is black glass under the clearing sky, and the moon is out, whole and indifferent, and the waves come in the way they always have, patient, enormous, keeping their own accounts. "Besides. You'll have work. Someone has to tell the coast what happened. Someone has to make sure the examinations end, the halls open, the emptied ones get tended. Marta deserves someone stubborn."

"She deserves you."

"Marta deserves someone who remembers her name." I take off my boots. Old habit — I never did learn to walk into water shod. "Ivo. Look at me."

He does. Grey eyes, red-rimmed, furious, loyal past all sense. If things had been different — if I had been a woman with a past instead of a woman with a hole where one should be —

"Don't," he says, reading my face the way he reads everything, too fast.

"I wasn't going to say it."

"You were going to write it in a margin."

"And you'd have found it eventually, because you're insufferable." I hold out my hand. He takes it. His grip is careful, like I'm something breakable, which is funny, because I am currently the most dangerous object on this coastline. "Goodbye, Captain."

"That's not goodbye. That's 'until you find another way.' I've read your margins, Nakamura. You always leave yourself a way."

I smile at him. It costs more than the binding did.

"Not this time," I say. "This entry's final."

Then I turn, and I walk into the Greyharbor tide, and the water takes me up to the knees, the waist, the shoulders, cold and salt and honest, and the thing inside me goes very quiet, very still, because even ancient hungers know what the sea is. The keeper of debts. The oldest ledger there is.

At the last, when the water closes over my mouth, I do not scream. I recite. Names, debts, truths, all of it, every line of every margin, filing it away into the dark and the salt where nothing can drink it and nothing can burn it.

Above me, through the surface, the sky breaks open — not storm, not lightning, just rain, clean and endless, falling on Greyharbor, on the guild ruins, on a captain standing alone at the tideline with ash on his fingers and a stack of impossible books under his arm.

The waves keep their accounts.

Somewhere in them, so do I.
