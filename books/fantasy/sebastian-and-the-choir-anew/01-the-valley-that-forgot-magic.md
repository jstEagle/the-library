## 1. The Valley That Forgot Magic

The fox was late again.

Astrid Iversen sat on the flat stone beside the millstream with her mending basket untouched at her feet, watching the alder shadows lengthen across the water and pretending — the way she had been pretending for nineteen years — that she was simply a girl enjoying the evening air, and not a girl waiting for an animal to tell her about his day.

He came at last out of the bracken on three legs and one opinion, favoring his bad hind paw with the exaggerated dignity of a creature who refused to be pitied. He was red as rust, thin as winter, with a torn ear and eyes the color of amber held up to lamplight. The valley children called him Old Lamefoot when they called him anything at all. Astrid called him Sebastian, though she had never told him so, because she suspected he would be insufferable about it.

*You're late,* she said — not aloud, never aloud, but in that other way, the way that lived behind her teeth and below her skin, the way no one in Haldensmark knew existed.

*I am punctual,* Sebastian said, arranging himself on the warm stone with his tail curled over his ruined paw like a feathered quilt. *You are simply early. There is a difference, and it flatters me.*

*There's no bread today.*

*There is never bread.*

*There's sometimes bread.* She dug into her pocket anyway and produced the heel of a loaf she'd smuggled out under her shawl, hard enough now to drive nails with, and he took it from her fingers with a gentleness that always undid her a little — this wild thing, all teeth by nature, handling her hand like something breakable. *Mrs. Osten will notice the loaf's short.*

*Then you must steal more convincingly.* He crunched. *A criminal talent, Astrid. Truly, your gift is wasted on beasts.*

She laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, quick and bright, and then immediately glanced over both shoulders as though the sound itself might be evidence.

No one was there. No one was ever there. That was rather the whole trouble.

* *

Here is what the people of Haldensmark knew about speakers: nothing true, and everything terrible.

They knew that generations ago — great-great-grandmother times, the era of burned cottages and midnight flights — the valley had driven out its beast-speakers, root and branch, and built the wards afterward to keep such folk out and, if the old hymns were honest (they were not), to keep any new ones from sprouting up inside. They knew the words *speaker* and *witch* belonged in the same breath. They knew that magic was what had gone wrong with the world beyond the passes, where crops failed and rivers ran strange, and they knew that here, behind the wards, things grew the way things were meant to grow, and that this was not luck but virtue.

*A people who remember,* Pastor Eide called them. Astrid thought: *a people who forgot, very loudly, and called the forgetting faith.*

What Astrid knew — what she had known since she was six years old and told a limping goat exactly which stone in the creek bed was hurting its hoof, and understood from the goat's astonished stare that she had done something unspeakable — was that the wards had missed a spot. Or perhaps the wards didn't work the way anyone believed. Or perhaps, and this was the thought she kept in the deepest drawer of herself, only opening it at night when even she could pretend she hadn't: perhaps the gift wasn't a blight at all. Perhaps it was just a sense, like hearing or sight, and the world outside the valley had gone deaf and blind and blamed the ones who could still see.

She'd tested it, over the years, the way you test a loose tooth. Chickens, mostly, at first — chickens are gossips, and forgiving, and their standards are low. Then the dairy cows, who had opinions about everything and gratitude for anyone who listened. Then the ravens along the ridge, who taught her that some creatures lie recreationally. And then, four winters ago, a young fox caught in a snare above the mill, feverish and furious, who snapped at every hand that came near until Astrid knelt in the snow and said, *I'm going to help you, but you have to hold still, and I know you understand me* — and the fox went still.

That was Sebastian. The snare had taken the use of his hind leg; the fever had nearly taken the rest. She'd fed him through that winter in the hollow tree past the north fence, and in spring he'd followed her at a distance, and by summer he'd decided — in the manner of foxes and grandmothers — that she could not be trusted to feed herself properly.

No one else knew. Not the Ostens next door, who thought her a shy, dutiful girl. Not Pastor Eide, who thought her quietly devout. Not even her mother, who thought —

Well. Her mother thought whatever her mother thought, which was often difficult to say, and lately harder than ever.

* *

*Marta Brenna's hens are plotting,* Sebastian announced, licking crumbs from his whiskers. *The speckled one has organized the others against the rooster. It is a coup. Quite literally.*

*Hens are always plotting. What did you actually come to tell me?*

He looked at her with those lamp-amber eyes, and for a moment the humor in him went quiet, the way it did maybe twice a year, and Astrid felt the familiar drop in her stomach, because Sebastian only got serious when something was worth being serious about.

*I came up through the high meadow today,* he said. *Past the shepherd stones.*

*You hate the high meadow. You said it was "windy, exposed, and morally offensive."*

*I said that. And yet.* His ears turned, slow, toward the northern wall of the valley — toward the pass, and the ridgeline above it where the ward-stones stood in their ancient ring, grey and lichened and older than the church, older than the mill, older than memory. *The birds are leaving, Astrid. Not migrating. Leaving. The choughs off the scree, the nutcrackers from the pine stands — gone in a night, all together, no calling, no flocking. Just gone. And the hares won't cross the treeline anymore. They sit at the edge of it facing uphill, all of them, all facing the same direction, like an audience waiting for a curtain.*

*Waiting for what?*

*That's the thing.* He shifted his weight off the bad leg. *I don't think they're waiting. I think they're listening. Everything up there is listening. Even the stones feel — attentive. Like a room after someone stops speaking.*

Astrid looked north. The peaks stood pink in the last light, serene as painted scenery. Somewhere up there, invisible, the wards hummed their silent note that kept Haldensmark safe and green and asleep. She'd grown up inside that hum the way fish grow up inside water. You didn't hear it. You were it.

Except lately — and she'd told no one this either — she thought she could hear it. Faintly. The way you hear the floorboards of an old house settling. A strain in it. A tiredness.

*The wards are fine,* she said, and heard how much she sounded like someone repeating a lesson. *Pastor Eide blessed them at midsummer.*

*Yes, well.* Sebastian yawned enormously, showing every tooth, a performance of unconcern that fooled neither of them. *Pastor Eide also believes the earth is carried on the back of a penitent giant, so we may wish to diversify our sources.*

*Sebastian.*

*Eat something. You've gone thin again. Your mother notices these things, and unlike you, she asks questions.*

At the mention of her mother, something closed in Astrid's chest, soft and heavy as snowfall. *Speaking of questions — I should get back.*

*You should.* He rose, shook himself, favored the bad paw. But before he slipped into the bracken he paused, half-shadow already, and said, in a voice with none of the wit left in it:

*Astrid. Whatever comes down out of those hills — and my nose says something is coming, though my nose declines to say what — do not be standing between it and your family pretending to be an ordinary girl. Ordinary girls survive. You would not. You'd run straight at the danger trying to explain yourself to it.*

*Explain myself to what? There's nothing coming. The wards—*

*Are humming a tired note. Yes. Goodnight, Astrid.*

And he was gone, and the stream talked on to itself in the dark, and Astrid sat alone on the stone with her empty basket and thought: *this is the shape of my life.* A stolen loaf, a lame fox, a secret folded small enough to hide in a smile. Belonging nowhere — not among the people, who would burn her if they knew her; not among the beasts, who were hers but could never be her peers. A hinge between two doors, swinging, attached to neither frame.

She was so tired of swinging.

* *

Her mother was waiting up, of course. Signe Iversen waited up the way the mountains stood — inevitably, weatherlessly.

She sat in the kitchen by the banked fire with her mending in her lap, though the needle hadn't moved, and she watched Astrid come in with a gaze that made the back of Astrid's neck prickle. Her mother had gray coming in at her temples and her father's stubborn jaw and hands that had worked every day of forty years, and she loved Astrid with a love so fierce and so frightened that the two had long since become indistinguishable.

"You're late," Signe said. "Again."

"The light was nice."

"Mm." Her mother set down the mending. "Astrid." Just the name, nothing after it. Which meant the sentence had been weighed and found too dangerous to speak.

"Mother?"

"I found the flour tin moved. And there was bread missing, and you've stopped meeting my eyes at supper, and you come home smelling of river stones and—" her mother's voice did something complicated, "-and fox."

The word landed in the kitchen like a dropped glass.

Astrid kept her face still. Nineteen years of practice. "Foxes live near rivers, Mother."

"Yes," Signe said softly. "They do."

For a moment — one suspended, terrible moment — Astrid thought her mother might say more. Might ask the question underneath the question, the one with wards and fires and exile coiled inside it. The clock ticked. The fire settled. Outside, far away in the dark, some bird cried once and stopped, abruptly, mid-note, as though hushed.

Signe picked her mending back up. "Wash your hands. Supper's cold, but it's food."

Astrid washed her hands. At the window over the basin, she looked north one more time, out of habit, out of that restless hinge-swinging longing that never let her alone.

The stars over the pass were very bright tonight. Brighter than she'd ever seen them — so bright they seemed less like lights than like eyes, countless and patient, all of them fixed on the little hidden valley, all of them watching.

All of them, she noticed, with a cold creeping startle she couldn't name,

beginning to go out.
