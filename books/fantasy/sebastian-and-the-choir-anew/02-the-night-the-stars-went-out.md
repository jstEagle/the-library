## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with living in a valley that had forgotten magic, Astrid Iversen thought, was that you had to do your remembering in secret.

She crouched at the edge of the turnip field with her skirts full of burrs and her heart full of nonsense, listening to a fox complain about the weather.

"It's the damp," Sebastian said, in the dry, precise voice he used for matters of great importance. "It gets into my hip. The lame one. You'd think after eleven years a body would make peace with a bad joint, but no. It nags. Like a mother-in-law made of ice."

"You don't have a mother-in-law."

"That's how I know it's a bad comparison. If I did have one, and she were made of ice, she would nag exactly this much." He flopped onto his side in the stubble, red fur gone copper in the last light, and gnawed thoughtfully at his own shoulder as though the damp might be hiding there. "You could rub it. The hip. Your hands are warm."

Astrid glanced over her shoulder at the village below — forty rooftops, one bell tower with a cracked bell nobody rang anymore, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless dusk. Her father was in the barn. Her mother was at the stove. Nobody was watching the turnip field.

Nobody ever watched the turnip field. That was why it was theirs.

She knelt and worked her fingers into the coarse fur along his ruined hip, feeling the old wrongness of the bone beneath, and Sebastian sighed the long, theatrical sigh of a creature who intended to enjoy every second of being pitied.

"Harder," he instructed. "You have the hands of a blacksmith's daughter and the spine of a wet leaf."

"I have the hands of a *farmer's* daughter, thank you, and if you'd rather I stop—"

"I said nothing of the kind." He tipped his head back so his ears flopped, an expression of shameless bliss. "There. Yes. Astrid Iversen, you are wasted on turnips."

"So everyone keeps telling me." She smiled despite herself. This was the joke they never said aloud: that she was wasted on turnips because she could hear him at all. That somewhere under her skin was a thing the valley had spent four generations pretending didn't exist. Her grandmother's grandmother had been driven out for less than this — for less than a fox's complaints about the weather. The story was told every winter at the long-table feast, solemn as scripture: *the speaker who spoke with beasts, and the sickness she brought down on the herds.* The wards around the valley had been raised the same year, old magic laid down by frightened people, and the whole point of them, Astrid had understood since childhood, was not to keep danger out.

The point was to keep things like her quiet.

"Your face is doing the thing," Sebastian observed.

"What thing?"

"The folding-up thing. Like a letter someone's decided not to send." He rolled upright and looked at her with his amber eyes, which were, infuriatingly, wiser than most people's. "It's nearly dark. Go home. Eat too much bread. Argue with your brother about whose turn it is to muck the pens. Be ordinary. You're better at it than you think."

"I'm terrible at it."

"Yes," he agreed comfortably, "but you're *committed*."

She laughed, threw a burr at him, missed on purpose, and started up the slope toward the lights of home. Behind her she heard him say something soft — she caught only the shape of it, *goodnight, girl* — and then the small quick sound of him trotting off toward the treeline, three-legged grace and all.

That was the last ordinary hour of her life. She would remember it afterward the way you remember the last flat stone before a cliff edge: the smell of turned earth, the smoke rising straight, the fox's parting words, the first star coming out over the eastern ridge like a lamp lit in a window.

She counted stars, sometimes. It was a habit from childhood, when she'd believed — before the winter stories taught her better — that counting them kept them lit. That night there were perhaps two hundred visible above the valley walls, more than usual, sharp and close, as though the sky had leaned in to listen.

Astrid stood in the farmyard with a pail of water in each hand and counted ninety-one before her mother called her in to supper.

* * *

Supper was barley stew, brown bread, and an argument about goats, which was the Iversen family's version of grace. Her brother Torvald maintained that the new billy was possessed. Their father maintained that Torvald was possessed, by laziness. Their mother maintained that both of them would be possessed by hunger if they didn't stop talking and eat, and Astrid maintained a pleasant, practiced silence, nodding in the right places while inside her head a fox complained about the damp and a hundred unspoken sentences crowded her throat like birds at a window.

It was a good silence, mostly. She had learned to make it comfortable. But some nights — and this was becoming most nights — it felt less like a coat she wore and more like a room she'd been locked in, where the door was locked from the outside by people who loved her.

Her mother caught her eye across the table. Marta Iversen had a way of looking at Astrid that Astrid had never quite been able to read — something between worry and watchfulness, as though Astrid were a fire her mother kept meaning to bank and never quite dared approach. Tonight the look lasted a moment too long.

"Eat," her mother said, and pushed the bread toward her, and that was all.

Afterward there were dishes, and Torvald's snoring through the wall, and their father checking the barn lanterns out of habit though nothing in the valley had needed guarding in living memory. Astrid lay in her narrow bed under the eaves with the window cracked to let in the cold, doing what she always did last thing: counting stars.

Ninety-one earlier. She found eighty-eight now, working east to west. She frowned. Clouds, probably. Thin ones, high ones, the kind you couldn't see but that ate light anyway.

Seventy-four.

She sat up.

Sixty-one. Fifty-five. They weren't dimming, that was the horrible thing — they were going *out*, each one snuffed clean, the way a candle is pinched, leaving nothing behind. Not clouds. Not haze. One by one, in no order she could find, beginning in the east and moving — she pressed her cold fingers to the sill, counting faster — moving *toward her.*

"Fifty-two," she whispered. "Fifty. Forty-nine—"

Below her window, across the whole sleeping valley, dogs began to howl.

Not the ragged neighborhood chorus that meant a stray cat or a wedding. This was one voice at first, then ten, then every dog in Vardal Valley at once, a single high keening note of pure animal terror. The cattle came next — a lowing that rolled up from the pastures like floodwater. Somewhere the cracked bell in the tower began to ring, though no hand touched it; it swung in the dark and groaned out its broken tone again and again, and Astrid realized the rope wasn't moving, the *tower itself* was trembling.

Then the sound came.

Later she would try to describe it to herself and fail. It was glass — it was every pane of glass in the world breaking at once, high and bright and thin — but underneath the brightness was something older, a deep groan like a glacier calving, like a mountain deciding to sit down. It passed through the valley floor and up through the soles of her feet and into her teeth. The water in the basin on her dresser shivered into rings.

And above the eastern ridge, where the wards had stood unseen for four generations, the night sky *cracked.* A seam of pale light split the darkness from peak to peak, hung there shimmering like heat-haze, and then — folded. Closed. Gone. Astrid felt its absence the way you feel a tooth pulled: not pain exactly, but a shocking, whistling *hollowness*, a place in the world where something used to be.

The stars stopped going out.

Forty-one remained, hanging over the western peaks, and they burned suddenly fierce and desperate, like eyes wide open.

"Astrid!" Her mother's voice, sharp with a fear Astrid had never heard in it before. Boots on stairs. The door burst open and her mother stood silhouetted against the lamplight of the hall, hair loose, still in her apron, holding the iron poker as though the dark itself might be driven back with it. "Get your boots. Get your brother. Downstairs *now.*"

"Mama, what—"

"*Now.*"

Downstairs the house was chaos and candlelight. Her father stood in the doorway with his old spear — a wall decoration her whole life, a relic from some grandfather's grandfather's war — and his knuckles were white around the haft. Torvald stumbled down half-dressed, asking questions nobody answered. Through the windows the valley glowed with scattered lantern-light as neighbors poured from their houses, and over everything rose the animals' crying, and under everything, faint and getting fainter, a sound Astrid alone seemed to hear: a vast, slow *unraveling*, like a song losing its notes one at a time.

"The wards are down," her father said. He said it quietly, to himself, the way a man says *the roof is gone* in a storm. "Four generations. They're just — down."

"Pa, what does that mean?" Torvald's voice cracked in the middle. "What comes in when the wards come down?"

Their father didn't answer. Their mother did, pulling both her children against her apron, one arm around each, and her voice was steady in the way of a woman spending her entire strength on a single word:

"Nothing tonight. Nothing gets in tonight. Now hold the lanterns high and let's see to the stock."

* * *

They saw to the stock. It was what farming families did when the sky broke: they milked, they fed, they checked fences, because the work was the only prayer they knew. Astrid carried feed through the dark barn with her heart hammering and her ears straining upward, and the animals pressed against her — the cows lowing low and miserable, the goats huddled in their corner, the old mare sweating and wild-eyed even after Torvald calmed her twice. Everything alive in that barn was begging without words, and Astrid walked the aisle murmuring to them, *easy, easy, I'm here,* and felt their terror ease fractionally where her voice touched it, and hated herself for noticing, and noticed anyway.

It was near midnight when the sky tore open over the western peaks.

Not cracked — torn, a ragged wound of darkness deeper than the night around it, and through it came a shape. Astrid saw it first as a falling star, except it was falling *wrong*, sideways, trailing sparks, screaming — actually screaming, a cry like an eagle's and a lion's braided into one voice of agony. It struck the far pasture hard enough to shake the ground, plowed a furrow through the frost, and fetched up against the fence line in a tangle of broken rails and white feathers.

A griffin.

Astrid knew the word from books she wasn't supposed to want. Eagle forequarters, lion hindquarters, wings that even crumpled and bloodied spanned wider than the barn door. It lay in the ruined fence with one wing bent at an angle wings should never bend, gold-brown feathers dark with blood, sides heaving.

And behind it, on the road over the western pass, came torchlight.

Riders. Six of them, maybe eight, descending the switchbacks at a pace no sane traveler used at midnight. They wore no colors she recognized — just dark cloaks with deep hoods, and the torches they carried burned a color torches shouldn't burn, a cold silver-white that cast no warmth. Even at this distance Astrid heard them singing. It was the most beautiful sound she had ever heard and it made her skin crawl: many voices woven into one, perfect, seamless, and utterly empty, like a hymn sung by a machine.

The horses in the barn went mad. The mare screamed and kicked her stall; a rail splintered; Torvald shouted. In the yard her father's voice rose, calling to the neighbors, and lanterns began converging on the pasture where the great creature lay bleeding.

The griffin lifted its head.

Its eyes found Astrid across forty yards of frozen pasture — found *her*, specifically, unmistakably, the way a compass finds north — and it opened its beak, and what came out was not a scream.

It was a word. A word in a voice like gravel and gold, pitched precisely, impossibly, for her alone, beneath the singing, beneath the chaos, beneath the whole unraveling night:

"*Speaker.*"

Every hair on Astrid's body stood up. The riders crested the last rise. The hooded song swelled, sweet and hollow, and the nearest torch tilted downward toward the fallen griffin like a head bowing over prey.

"*Run,*" said the griffin, and its voice broke on the word, and its eyes never left hers. "*Please.*"

Behind her, in the doorway of the barn, her mother stood very still with the poker in her hand, staring at the pasture, at the griffin, at Astrid's face — and Astrid saw, in one lightning flash of understanding, that her mother was not looking at the griffin at all.

Her mother was looking at her daughter, and whispering, almost too softly to hear:

"Oh. Oh, *no.* It's begun."
