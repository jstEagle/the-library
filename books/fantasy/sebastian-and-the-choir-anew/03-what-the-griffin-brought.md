## 3. What the Griffin Brought

Astrid had imagined, in the secret theater of her daydreams, what it would be like if an animal finally spoke to her. She'd pictured a badger, mostly — badgers seemed like they'd have opinions worth hearing — or perhaps one of the high-ridge hawks, all sharp grammar and sharper eyes. She had never once imagined it would happen like this: at dawn, in the wreckage of the barn door, with blood soaking through her nightdress and something enormous and impossible breathing ragged breaths into the straw.

The griffin lay on its side where it had fallen, wings half-spread like a shipwreck's sails. Its forequarters were eagle — golden-feathered, hooked beak, eyes the color of storm-lit amber — but its hindquarters were lion, tawny and powerful even in ruin, and the whole of it was streaked with dark blood from three wounds that wept across its flank. One wing bent at an angle wings were not meant to bend.

And it was looking at her.

Not the way animals looked at people. Not with wariness, or hunger, or that patient tolerance foxes wore like a coat. It was looking at her the way a person looks at a person.

"Speaker." The voice arrived inside her skull rather than her ears — a dry, wind-scraped voice, like autumn leaves deciding to fall. "You hear me."

Astrid's knees gave out. She sat down hard in the straw, which was probably for the best, because standing suddenly felt ambitious.

"You can talk," she said. Her own voice sounded very small. "You're talking. Foxes don't talk. Badgers definitely don't talk. I've *asked*."

"I am not a fox," said the griffin, with the faintest edge of offense, as though she'd compared it to a turnip. "I am Ashveil. Last warden of the Northern Reach. And you are the first speaker I have found in nine years who is still breathing." A pause; the great chest hitched. "That number is smaller than it should be. It was much larger once."

Outside, the light was going strange. Astrid noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song — the dawn should have been gold by now, but it came up gray and sourceless, the sky a blank ceiling with no sun she could find and no stars, no stars, no stars. She kept not looking at it. Looking at it made her stomach do things.

"The stars," she said instead. "Last night they went out. All of them. Was that—"

"Yes," said Ashveil.

"Was it us? The valley's wards?"

"No, child." The amber eyes half-closed. "It was them. The Choir. They have finished their long theft. What remains of the sky is what they haven't yet swallowed." The beak clicked softly, a sound like a key turning in a stubborn lock. "And now they hunt what's left. Speakers. Beasts. Anything wild enough to remember how the world used to sing."

Astrid's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "Why did you come *here*?"

"Because your valley has hidden itself for two hundred years, and because I am dying, and because dying creatures go home if they cannot go somewhere safe." A wet, rattling breath. "But I did not come only to hide, speaker. I came to beg."

"Beg?" Astrid blinked. "Beg me? I'm — I'm nobody. I'm a farm girl who talks to a lame fox behind her father's fence line. I've never done anything. I've never even left the valley."

"That is precisely why I crawled here over three hundred miles with my wing broken and my blood half-spent." The griffin shifted, wincing, dragging itself an inch closer to her, close enough that she could feel the heat rising off its body and smell blood and feathers and something underneath both — a smell like high cold air, like the tops of mountains. "Because nobody is exactly what this moment requires."

"I don't understand."

"You will." Ashveil's head dipped until the beak nearly touched the straw between them. When it spoke again, the wind-scrape had gone out of its voice, and what remained was older, and heavier, and terribly tired. "Listen to me carefully, Astrid Iversen, because I will not have the strength to say this twice. There is a place beneath the world called the First Hollow, where everything the Choir ever stole was poured away and buried. Every voice they silenced. Every speaker they consumed. The stars themselves — do you understand me? The stars are not fires. They are voices. They are *singers*. And they are drowning down there in the dark, and they have been drowning for two hundred years."

Astrid's mouth had gone very dry. "Voices. The stars are — people?"

"Speakers, mostly. The ones who fought. Some who merely refused to kneel." The griffin's tail lashed once, weakly, stirring the straw. "The Choir bound them into their Song, and the Song ate them, and what was left over they locked beneath the earth so no one would ever hear them crying. But the lock is failing. The Hollow is waking. And when it opens fully — soon, child, within a season, perhaps within weeks — either the stolen voices rise back into the sky and live again, or the Choir comes to drink the last of them and finishes the job."

"And you want me to—" Astrid laughed, a thin, disbelieving sound. "Open it? Me? I can't even get the neighbor's goat to stop eating my mother's roses, and I speak *fluent goat*."

Something happened then that Astrid would carry for the rest of her life, though it took her years to name it: the great wounded creature looked at her, and made a sound that was unmistakably, impossibly, a laugh. Rattling, pained, abbreviated by the wounds in its flank — but a laugh.

"Oh," said Ashveil, "you are going to be *trouble*. Good. The obedient ones are always the first to die." Then the humor drained out of it, and the amber eyes fixed on hers, and the weight of the world settled into the space between them like snowfall. "No, speaker. I ask something harder than opening the Hollow. I ask you to run. Tonight, tomorrow, however long it takes you to gather your courage. Out of the valley. Beyond the pass. Because they know about this place now — the wards fell, and falling wards leave scars, and scars can be read. They are coming. Riders in gray hoods, with bells on their bridles and silence in their throats. And when they come, they will find you."

"How could they possibly—"

"Because you are the last unbound speaker in the North," Ashveil said simply, "and because last night, when the sky went dark, every wild thing within fifty miles heard you screaming at it in the old tongue. Including the fox. Including, unfortunately, the hunters' beasts, who are compelled to report such things." A pause. "Your goat also heard you, incidentally. She is deeply offended. You might address that before you leave."

Despite everything — the blood, the dying sky, the impossible talking wreck of a legend bleeding out in her father's barn — Astrid felt her mouth twitch. "She started it."

"They always say that," said Ashveil gravely. "Goats. Empires. Same thing."

Then the griffin shuddered, head drooping, and the laughter was gone as fast as it had come. "I cannot fly. Perhaps never again. But I can teach you, on the road, what speakers used to know — the deep speech, the binding words, the songs the stars sang before they were stars. If you carry me. If you save me. That is my begging, Astrid Iversen: not for mercy. For *purpose*. Let me die useful, or let me live long enough to see the sky remember its own voice. Either way — take me with you when you run."

Astrid stared at the vast broken body, at the blood pooling black in the straw, at the amber eye watching her without judgment and without hope either, which somehow was worse.

"I'm sixteen," she whispered. "I've never been past the mill. My mother doesn't know about the fox. My mother doesn't know about *any* of it. How am I supposed to just—"

The kitchen door banged open.

Astrid flinched so hard she nearly fell backward into the water trough. And there stood her mother, Marta Iversen, in her work apron, hair unbound, a lantern in one hand and her face lit from below in a way that made it hard to read — except that Astrid had spent sixteen years learning to read that face, and what she read there stopped her heart.

Her mother wasn't looking at the griffin.

Her mother was looking at *her*, and her expression was not surprise.

"Mama," Astrid said stupidly. "Mama, the barn door—"

"I saw the door." Her mother's voice was flat and careful, the way it got when she was holding something enormous underwater. "I saw the sky. I saw the beast come down over our roof like the end of the world, and I ran out here certain I'd find my daughter dead." She set the lantern down on an overturned bucket with exaggerated gentleness, the way she did when she was angriest. "Instead I find her sitting in the straw having a conversation with it."

"Mama—"

"*Can you.*" The words cracked out of her like a whip. Then, immediately, softer — her mother's anger always collapsed inward, turned on herself, the way a wave breaks against a seawall. "Can you, Astrid. Talk to it. To animals. Tell me plainly, because I need to hear it, and I need to hear it now."

The lie rose in Astrid's throat — old, practiced, worn smooth from use. *It's nothing, Mama, it followed the wounded bird in, I was just looking.*

She looked at the griffin. The griffin looked back, amber and patient, saying nothing at all, which was somehow the loudest thing it could have done.

"Yes," Astrid said. "Yes, Mama. Since I was six."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats and contained an entire lifetime.

Her mother crossed the barn in five strides, and Astrid braced for a slap, for shouting, for the cold shoulder that could last a week — and instead Marta Iversen grabbed her by the shoulders and pulled her in so hard the breath went out of her, and held on, and shook, silently, her chin resting on top of Astrid's head like she'd done when Astrid was small enough to fit under it properly.

"You knew," Astrid managed, muffled into her mother's apron. "You *knew*. All this time. You knew and you never—"

"I suspected at six. I knew by eight." Her mother's voice came out rough. "You think I didn't notice my daughter standing in the rain talking to a fence post? You think I didn't see the drawings?"

*Oh,* thought Astrid, and went cold.

There had been drawings. Dozens of them. Astrid had filled sketchbooks before she could properly write — a fox mid-laugh with a speech bubble of squiggles, a hawk dictating weather reports, a bear complaining about its feet. She'd kept them in a box under her bed for years, and then one spring when she was eleven the box was gone, and she'd assumed her mother had cleared it out with the rest of the clutter, and she had grieved those drawings quietly and privately for five years without ever asking why.

Her mother went into the house. Astrid stood in the barn, torn between the griffin and the doorway, until Marta returned carrying a small tin box — dented, rust-freckled at the corners — and Astrid knew it instantly, knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

"You didn't throw them away," she breathed.

"No." Her mother knelt beside the lantern, opened the box, and drew out a handful of papers — soft with handling, edges browned. On top was the fox. Sebastian, mid-wink, his speech bubble full of six-year-old scrawl that meant, as near as anyone could translate it, *your fence is insulting.* "I couldn't. Gods forgive me, I told myself I was clearing clutter, and then I sat in this barn with the box in my lap and I couldn't make myself burn a single one, because—" Her voice snagged. She cleared it. "Because burning them felt like admitting what they were."

"What were they, Mama?" Astrid asked quietly. "What did you think they were?"

Marta was quiet a long moment. In the corner, Ashveil breathed — slow, labored, listening.

"When I was a girl," her mother said at last, "my grandmother told me stories about the speakers. How the valley drove them out before the wards went up, generations back. How the wards keep magic *out*, but they also keep it *quiet* — smother it, like ash over coals. She said the gift skipped and hid and came back sometimes, in families that didn't know to look for it. She said—" Marta stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, lower. "She said, *if a child of yours ever draws an animal with words coming out of it, you'll have a choice to make, Marta, and there's no right answer, only the one you can live with.*"

"She knew too," Astrid whispered.

"Everyone old knows. Nobody says." Her mother looked down at the drawings, fanned across her knees like fallen leaves. "So I made my choice. I let you believe you were hiding it. I let the whole valley believe it, because I watched you, Astrid — I watched you learn to be quiet, to be careful, to fold yourself small enough to fit through the world's eye of a needle — and I hated it, and I *let it happen*, because the alternative was telling you the truth, and the truth was that this valley would turn on you. Would have turned on you since before either of us was born. So yes. I burned nothing and I said nothing and I let my daughter grow up lonely in her own home, because I was afraid." Her jaw tightened. "I was afraid for you. Which is not the same as being right."

"Mama—"

"Let me finish. I've had sixteen years of not finishing." She gathered the drawings up, squared their edges with unnecessary precision. "Last night the stars went out, and this morning a griffin fell out of the empty sky onto our property, and my daughter talked to it, and every fear I ever buried just dug itself up and stood in our yard wearing armor. So here is what happens now. You are going to help that creature. Don't argue — I can see you arguing in your head, you get your stubbornness from me and I recognize the posture."

Astrid, who had indeed been arguing in her head, closed her mouth.

"You'll need food, and the good travel cloak, and the money tin under the floorboard in my room — yes, I know about the money tin, I put it there — and you'll need to be gone before your father wakes properly, because your father loves you more than he can say and less wisely than he thinks, and he would try to stand between you and whatever's coming, and he'd die doing it." Her hands trembled around the box. "And I will hate every second of letting you go. I will hate it until the day I die. And I am going to do it anyway, because keeping you here safe and small is no longer safe at all, and because—" the crack finally came, wide and unavoidable, "—because I have watched you fold yourself small for ten years and I cannot bear to watch you do it one more day. Even if unfolding kills you. At least you'll have been *whole* for a little while."

Astrid didn't remember crossing the distance. One moment her mother was kneeling with the drawings, the next Astrid had her arms around her again, and they were both crying, badly and without dignity, while a dying griffin observed them with the tactful silence of the terminally embarrassed.

"I thought you'd be angry," Astrid sobbed.

"I *am* angry. I'm furious. I'm furious at the Choir and the sky and the valley and your grandmother and myself most of all." Her mother pulled back, wiped her face with her apron in two fierce swipes, and became abruptly, aggressively practical — which was how Marta Iversen loved things: at volume, with lists. "Now. The cloak is in the cedar chest. Take the left-hand saddlebags, not the right, the right one smells of onions. And take these." She pushed the tin box into Astrid's hands. "All of them. Especially the fox."

"Mama, they'll get ruined on the road—"

"They've survived ten years in a tin. They'll survive anything." Marta stood, brushed off her knees, and paused. Something moved across her face — grief and love and terror braided tight as rope. "Astrid. Whatever that creature told you about what you are — whatever you can do — I need you to promise me one thing."

"Anything."

"Don't apologize for it. Ever. Not to riders, not to strangers, not to gods." Her mother's grip on her shoulders was bruising and perfect. "This family made you ashamed of the truest thing about you for sixteen years. We don't get to add one more sorry to the pile. Promise me."

"I promise," Astrid whispered.

Her mother nodded once, briskly, the nod of a woman rationing her breakdowns. Then she picked up the lantern, turned toward the house — and stopped.

Across the yard, at the tree line beyond the pasture, the lame fox sat in the gray false dawn, watching the barn. Just sitting. Just watching. As if it had been waiting all along for exactly this moment to witness.

And far to the north, past the pass, past the ridgeline, carried on air that had no business carrying anything — Astrid heard it. Faint. Metallic. Rhythmic.

Bells.

Ashveil lifted its bloody head, and its voice in her mind had gone iron-hard.

"They're early," said the griffin. "We leave now."
