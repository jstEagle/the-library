## 4. Riders in the Pass

The fox had told her first.

Astrid woke in the grey hour before dawn with Sebastian's voice still ringing in her head — *they come, they come, boots and iron and a smell like burnt hymns* — and lay very still beneath her quilt, listening past the creak of the house, past her father's snore, to the valley itself.

The valley was holding its breath.

She'd lived all seventeen years of her life inside that held breath and never noticed it until now. The stream below the pasture wasn't babbling; it was whispering. The pines on the ridge weren't sighing; they were pressing close together like children at a sickbed. Even the wards — the old woven protections her great-great-grandparents had laid into every stone wall and hedgerow — were guttering. She could feel them the way you feel a candle flame about to go out: a warmth thinning toward nothing.

*They come,* Sebastian had said, three nights ago, from his hollow under the willow where he slept off his lame hip. *Riders. Hooded. They sing when they ride, little speaker, and everything that hears them forgets its own name.*

At the time she'd thought it was one of his stories. He told stories the way old soldiers tell war tales — half memory, half appetite for an audience. But he hadn't been telling a story. His ears had been flat to his skull, and his one good eye had been fixed on the northern pass with an expression Astrid had never seen on a fox before.

Fear.

She dressed in the dark. Wool stockings, two skirts, the coat her mother had re-lined last winter because Astrid wore through pockets faster than anyone in the family — always filling them with acorns, with feathers, with things the birds brought her. Her hands shook so badly on the laces that she had to start twice.

In the barn, Ashveil lay where they'd dragged him three days ago, behind the hay wall her father had built overnight without asking questions, which was its own kind of terrifying. The griffin's bronze feathers had gone dull as old pennies. The wound along his flank — a long wealing burn, as though someone had drawn a line of fire across him — had stopped bleeding but started smelling wrong, sweet and rotten together.

His golden eye opened when her boot scuffed the straw.

*Little speaker.* His voice in her skull was weaker than it had been, a bell rung through wool. *You heard them too.*

"I don't know what I heard," Astrid whispered. "Tell me what's coming."

*The Choir.* The word came out of him like a tooth being pulled. *What is left of it. They have hunted me since the sky went dark. I carried messages between the last free eyries, and now there are no eyries, and the messages are ash, and I am the only one who knows where the Hollow sleeps.* His talons flexed once against the floorboards, leaving four fresh grooves. *They cannot let me live. And they cannot let you live either, because you heard me, and hearing is the door, and through the door is everything they buried.*

"Then we run," Astrid said. It came out steadier than she felt. "There are passes south of the valley. My grandfather used to take goats over them. If we—"

The dogs started barking.

Not the lazy, conversational barking that meant a deer at the fence line. This was the flat, continuous, hackle-raised clamor of animals announcing the end of the world, and it rose from every farmstead down the valley at once, a wave of sound rolling uphill toward the house.

Ashveil hauled himself upright. It cost him visibly — his wings shuddered open half-mast, and he made a sound like a hinge that had never once been oiled — but he got up, and up, until his crested head brushed the rafters.

*They are here,* he said. *Now, little speaker. Now or never, and never is winning.*

* * *

Her mother was already in the yard.

That was the thing Astrid would remember afterward, in the years when remembering was all she had: not the riders, not the burning wards, but her mother standing in the dooryard in her nightgown and boots, a lantern in one hand and a hay knife in the other, hair loose down her back like a girl's, planted between the barn and the lane as if a woman with a hay knife could stop what was coming down that lane.

"Mama." Astrid's voice cracked. "Mama, you have to wake Papa, you have to—"

"Your father is waking your brothers." Her mother didn't turn around. "They're going out the cellar way, down to the Anderssons'. You—" and here her voice did something complicated, folded itself around something enormous, and came out flat and practical, "—you're going over the south pass with the griffin."

"How did you—"

"Astrid." Now her mother turned, and the lantern light caught her face, and Astrid saw that her mother had been crying already, earlier, alone, and had finished, and had packed whatever grief she had into a small tight parcel to be opened later. "You think I didn't know? You think I burned those drawings because I hated them?" She thrust a bundle at Astrid — bread, cheese, a flask, a spare knife wrapped in oilcloth, all of it prepared, all of it ready, which meant her mother had packed this bag days ago, while Astrid slept, while Astrid sat in the willow hollow talking to a lame fox. "I knew what you were since you were four years old and told me the cow was lying about her hoof. I kept you alive by keeping you secret. That work is done. The rest is yours."

Down the lane, torchlight. Not many torches — six, maybe seven — but the dark between them seemed to lean away from them, as though even the night didn't want to be touched by what carried them.

And they were singing.

It shouldn't have been singing. It was more like a machine made of voices, all of them locked to a single line, no harmony, no breath, no human wobble anywhere in it. Astrid felt it hit the wards and heard the wards answer — a sound like a wineglass singing itself to destruction, higher and thinner and thinner —

The hedge along the Anderssons' field burst into white fire and was gone.

"Mama," Astrid said, and couldn't say anything else.

Her mother grabbed her by both shoulders. Up close her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "South pass. Don't stop at the tree line, don't stop at the scree, don't stop until the sun finds you. The griffin can't carry you far in his state, so walk when he needs you to walk and hold on when he doesn't. And Astrid—" her mother's grip tightened, hard enough to bruise, hard enough to mean it, "—whatever they offer you, whatever they promise, whatever song they put in your ears: do not sing back. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Liar," said her mother, almost gently. "You'll want to. That's why I'm telling you now." She kissed Astrid's forehead once, brief and fierce, like stamping a seal. Then she turned to face the lane, raised the lantern, and walked toward the torchlight with the hay knife low at her side, calling out in a clear carrying voice: "This is the Iversen farm! Who's trespassing on Iversen land?"

Astrid stood frozen for one heartbeat, two, watching her mother's back grow smaller.

*Little speaker.* Ashveil's beak closed gently — as gently as anything that size could manage — on the strap of her pack, and pulled. *Your mother has bought us a hundred heartbeats. Do not spend them standing here.*

* * *

They went up through the south pasture with the world coming apart behind them.

Astrid heard it in fragments as she climbed: her mother's voice, high and furious and unafraid, demanding names; the Choir's answering Song, smooth as poured wax; then a sound she had no name for, a sound like a chord being ripped out of the air, and her mother's voice stopped, and something in Astrid's chest tore along the same seam.

She didn't stop. That was the horror of it — she didn't stop, couldn't stop, her legs carrying her upward on animal instinct while her mind screamed at them to turn around. Ashveil flew short, agonized bursts above her, landing ahead, waiting, hissing *faster*, and she ran, and the guilt ran with her, matching her stride for stride.

Escape purchased with someone else's sacrifice. She understood, climbing, that this was the coin her whole life would be spent from now on. Every step she took north-and-south-and-away was paid for by a woman in a nightgown with a hay knife, by a father she hadn't even been able to say goodbye to, by brothers whose names she was too winded to think.

The tree line swallowed her. Behind and below, the valley glowed in patches — the Anderssons' barn, someone's haystack, the ward-fires dying one by one like lamps blown out down a long table.

"They'll be alive," she said aloud, to nobody, to herself, to the dark. "They have to be alive. She talked to them. People who talk don't—"

*The Choir does not need to kill to conquer,* Ashveil said quietly. *Silence is their preferred harvest. Your mother may yet live, little speaker. But she will not remember why she stood in that yard.*

Astrid put her head down and climbed.

* * *

The south pass was a liar.

From the valley it looked like a gentle notch between two shoulders of mountain, the kind of gap a child could skip across. From inside, it revealed itself as a throat of broken stone, switchbacking up through scree fields and deadfall, narrowing in places to a ledge barely wide enough for a goat — and Astrid was not a goat, and Ashveil, with his ruined flank and dragging wing, was considerably less goat than she was.

They climbed through the last of the night. Astrid fell twice, skinning her palms raw, and learned to fall better. Ashveil fell once — a horrible sliding scrabble of talons on wet rock, forty feet of skid ending against a boulder with a crack of bending feathers — and after that he refused to speak of it, and Astrid pretended not to have heard, and they were bonded forever by the shared treaty of pretending.

Dawn found them at the top of the world, on a saddle of bare stone with the clouds below them and the sun coming up the color of weak tea, because there was no star left bright enough to make it gold.

Astrid looked back.

The valley was hidden. All of it — the farm, the willow, Sebastian's hollow, the church, the graves of her grandmothers — folded away behind the shoulder of the mountain as if it had never existed. Somewhere down there, her mother was either dead or emptied. Somewhere down there, a fox with a bad hip was hiding from men who sang.

"We should have taken him," she said suddenly. "Sebastian. We should have—"

*A fox cannot climb a mountain pass at speed, and you know it.* Ashveil settled onto the stone with a groan of exhausted muscle. *He bought you time in the nights before I ever reached your barn. He watched the roads. He counted their fires. He is not a pet you left behind, little speaker; he is a soldier who stayed at his post.* A pause, and then, gentler than she'd ever heard him: *Guilt will lie to you. It will tell you that you abandoned everyone. Look at your feet instead. You are standing where they cannot reach you today. That is not abandonment. That is survival, and survival is the first duty of the hunted.*

Astrid wiped her face with her bleeding palm, which stung, which helped. "Where are we going? You said something about a Hollow. About messages. About—"

*I said I know where the Hollow sleeps.* Ashveil's crest flattened. *I did not say the road to it is kind. South, past the drowned orchards, past the cities where the bells never stop. Weeks, if the weather holds. Months, if it doesn't. And they will follow us the whole way, because I am the last creature alive who knows the door, and you are the last creature alive who can hear it knock.*

Something moved in the pass below them.

Astrid saw it before she understood it: a flicker of torchlight, far down the switchbacks, impossibly high already — impossibly fast. Then another. Then the sound reached them, threading up through the thin cold air, and her whole body went rigid with recognition.

The Song. They were singing as they climbed. Singing as if the mountain were a stairway built for them.

*How—* Astrid's voice failed. She tried again. "We climbed all night. How are they already—"

*Because they do not tire,* Ashveil said. *The Song carries them. It eats the weariness out of their bodies the way it ate the stars out of the sky. Everything the Choir takes, it spends. Everything it spends, it must take again.* He heaved himself upright, and this time when his wings opened, they opened fully, and the pain in his eyes was a choice he was making rather than a thing happening to him. *Which means, little speaker, that we cannot outrun them. We can only become somewhere they do not expect us to be.*

"There's nowhere they don't expect us. They're mages. They're—"

*They are singers,* Ashveil said. *And every singer is deaf to one thing: silence. Come. There is a place, half a day east, where the mountain broke long ago and never healed. The locals call it the Grieving Stair. No beast goes there. No bird nests there. The Song cannot hear what happens in a place that has already given up making noise.* His golden eye caught the tea-colored sunrise and held it. *It is a terrible place, and we are going to hide in it, and then we are going to go south through country so wild that even the Choir fears to sing in it.*

"And what's in that country?"

Ashveil looked at her for a long moment.

*Everything that used to have a voice,* he said. *And none of it is tame anymore.*

Far below, the torchlight gained another switchback. The Song drifted up to them, beautiful and empty, a melody with nothing living inside it.

Astrid shouldered her mother's pack, checked the spare knife, and turned east — away from home, away from everything she'd ever been allowed to love — and began, for the second time that day, to run.
