## 5. A World Without Stars

The valley had taught Astrid that the world was small: a ring of mountains, a river with opinions, a village of two hundred souls who mostly agreed on bread and mostly disagreed on everything else. She had known, in the abstract way one knows about weather in far-off countries, that there was more beyond the passes.

She had not known it was *dying*.

Three days out from the valley, following Ashveil's slow, labored flight from ridge to ridge — the griffin could no longer carry her; his left wing dragged like a wet banner — she crested a hill and stopped so suddenly that the mule she'd bought in a border town walked into the back of her knees.

"Oh," she said.

Below her, the land rolled away in waves of brown and gray to a horizon that had no end and no stars above it. Fields stretched in every direction, but they were the wrong color for late summer. Wheat should have been gold by now. This wheat was the color of old bone, stalks collapsed into each other like tired dancers. An orchard stood in neat rows, every tree bare, though it was weeks too early for even the first leaf to fall.

And the sky — she kept looking up despite herself, hoping against hope that past the mountains the stars had survived. They hadn't. The darkness overhead was total, a black so complete it felt less like night than like a lid. She had grown up under those vanished stars; she knew their names the way farm children know the shapes of clouds. The Shepherd. The Loom. The Seven Sisters who were really nine if you knew where to look. Their absence was a wound she could feel behind her eyes.

"You're staring," Ashveil said. He had landed beside her with a gracelessness that would have broken her heart a week ago and now merely broke it a little more each day. His feathers, once the silver of birch bark, had gone dull as ash.

"I'm allowed," Astrid said. "I've never seen the world before. I'd hoped it would be better at hiding its problems."

"The world has never been good at that. Only people are." He settled onto his good side, talons flexing in the dry dirt. "Come. There's a town ahead. You need food you didn't pay a bandit's price for, and I need to stop pretending my wing doesn't hurt."

"It doesn't hurt?"

"Of course it hurts. But a griffin who admits pain is a griffin who gets pitied, and I would rather be eaten."

Astrid almost smiled. It was the first thing in three days that had come close.

* * *

The town was called Marrow's Ford, which struck Astrid as an unfortunate name until she saw the river running brown and thin through its center, barely deep enough to wet a boot. The ford itself was a memory — a line of stones crossing nothing much.

She tied the mule at the edge of town and went in alone, hood up, Ashveil hidden in a ravine outside with strict instructions to remain invisible and *not* eat anyone's livestock, however provokingly slow they might be.

Marrow's Ford was dying politely. That was the only way she could describe it. The buildings stood. The well still worked — she heard its creak and splash. People moved through the streets doing the things people do, buying and selling and nodding to neighbors. But everything was quieter than a town should be. No dogs barked. No birds argued in the eaves. And when she passed the tavern, she heard no laughter inside, only voices pitched low, the way voices go when everyone is tired all the time.

She bought bread and hard cheese with the last of her mother's coins, and because she was hungry and lonely and the tavern was the only lit place, she sat on its bench outside to eat.

An old man joined her without asking. He had a face like a walnut and hands that had clearly done decades of work and were now reduced to holding a pipe he couldn't afford to fill.

"Not from here," he said. Not quite a question.

"No. Traveling through."

"Traveling where? Everything worth going to is closed." He nodded at the fields beyond the rooftops. "Third failed harvest. River's down to its bones. Beasts went strange in spring — our ox wouldn't plow in April, just stood there looking at the sky like it owed him money. Had to sell him for meat. Worst beef I ever ate, and I've eaten some beef."

"I'm sorry," Astrid said. "That's — did something happen? To cause it?"

The old man gave her a look she would come to know very well over the following weeks. It was the look of a man about to tell a stranger whose fault everything was.

"The speakers," he said, spitting the word like a seed. "Last of the wild ones. They did this. My grandfather told me — when the Choir silenced them, the land got quiet but it stayed *alive*. Now somebody's been meddling again, waking things up, and look what comes of it. Stars snuffed out like candles. Crops gone. Beasts mad." He tapped his cold pipe against the bench. "Choir's hunting them down, last I heard. Good, I say. Should've finished the job years back."

Astrid's mouth went dry around her bread. She made herself chew. Swallow. Keep her face smooth and stupid and safe.

"Do you know that?" she asked carefully. "That it's the speakers? For certain?"

"For certain as anything is. Who else could it be? Magic's theirs. All this—" he waved the pipe at the dead orchards, the starless dark, "—is magic gone wrong. Choir says so, and the Choir would know."

*The Choir would know,* Astrid thought, and something cold slid down her spine, because that was exactly the sort of sentence that sounded like wisdom right up until the moment it wasn't.

"They're saying there's one left," the old man went on, warming to it now, enjoying having an audience. "A girl. Beast-speaker, they call her. From some hidden valley up north. They say she can talk to animals, and that's how the beasts went mad — she called them wrong, or woke them bad, or some such. Choir's offering a reward." He squinted at her. "Big one. Enough to plant five springs' worth of grain."

"Is that so," Astrid said. Her voice came out steady, which she counted as the finest piece of magic she had ever performed.

"Mm. Girl can't be more than sixteen, they say. Imagine. Sixteen and ruining the world." He shook his head at the wickedness of youth, then seemed to remember his manners. "You want water? Well's free."

"That's kind of you," said the girl ruining the world, aged seventeen, and accepted a cup of water from a dead-dry river town.

* * *

She did not run. Running was a thing guilty people did, and besides, her legs had their own opinion about hills lately. She walked, at a pace she hoped read as unhurried, out past the last house, past the mule, up into the ravine where a griffin lay watching the road with one furious golden eye.

"They know," she said, before he could ask. "There's a reward. They think I did this, Ashveil. They think I *unmade the stars*."

Ashveil was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its dryness.

"What else did they say?"

"That the Choir silenced the speakers long ago and the land stayed alive. That someone must have woken the wild magic again, and that's why everything's failing." She sat down heavily on a rock. "It's a story, isn't it? A tidy one. Bad thing happens, find the witch, burn the witch, sleep soundly."

"It is a very old story," Ashveil said quietly. "Older than your valley. Older than me." He shifted, winced, hid the wince badly. "Astrid. There is something you should understand, and I should have said it sooner, but I was busy bleeding. The Song — the Choir's Song — it does not just silence wild magic. It *eats*. Every note of it is built from something taken. And the first things it took—"

"Were the stars," Astrid said. "You told me. Voices in the sky."

"I told you what I felt when the wards broke. I didn't understand it myself, not fully. But these people—" he tipped his beak toward the town, "—they aren't lying, exactly. Things ARE failing. The beasts ARE going mad. They simply have the villain backwards. The world isn't dying because wild magic woke up. It's dying because what was left of the wild magic is being devoured down to the roots, and the roots are coming up everywhere at once."

Astrid looked at her hands. Ordinary hands, flour-scarred, good at kneading bread and scratching foxes behind the ears. Somewhere under her skin was the thing the whole world was hunting, the thing an old man with an empty pipe would sell her for enough grain to plant five springs.

"When I talked to Sebastian," she said, "back home. The fox. It never hurt anyone. Talking never hurt anything. So how can they all believe—"

"Because believing is easier than grieving," Ashveil said. "Grief makes you helpless. Blame gives you a job to do." He rose, slowly, favoring the bad wing. "We should move. Towns talk, and you have a memorable face."

"I have a completely forgettable face."

"You have the face of someone who argues with griffins in ravines. Move."

* * *

They traveled east along the failing river for two days, because Ashveil remembered a forest where the old magic ran deep enough that the Song's reach thinned — a place called the Thornwood, where a speaker might breathe. Astrid learned the shape of the dying land the way you learn a language: by immersion.

The farms came first. She counted them without meaning to — eleven abandoned homesteads in two days, doors swinging, wells capped, a child's wooden horse left on a porch step with its paint peeling. At the twelfth, someone had nailed a sign to the door. *GONE TO MY SISTER'S IN THE SOUTH. THE LAND IS CURSED.* Underneath, in different handwriting, someone else had added: *THE SPEAKERS DID IT.*

Then the villages stopped altogether, and the beasts began.

The first was a deer, standing in a fallow field at dusk. It should have fled at the sight of them. Instead it turned its head and watched them pass with eyes like smoky glass, unblinking, and Astrid felt something brush against her gift — not words, not anymore, but a static hum, like a voice trying to speak through a wall of wool. *Wrong wrong wrong,* it seemed to say, or maybe that was her own fear translating. She reached out gently, the way she'd reached for Sebastian a thousand evenings, and the deer shuddered all over and bolted, crashing into a fence it should have cleared easily.

"It didn't understand me," she whispered.

"It understood you fine," Ashveil said grimly. "It just couldn't answer. The starlight carried the deep speech, little speaker. The old current that runs under all voices. Without it, the beasts hear each other the way you'd hear music through a stone wall. Some go quiet. Some go mad from the loneliness." He paused. "I am one of the lucky ones. I am old enough to remember the stars myself. The young ones were born into the silence. They don't know what they're missing. They only know something is."

The second beast was not lucky. They found it at a crossroads shrine — a wolf, or what had been a wolf, worrying at the base of a stone marker as if trying to dig through the world itself. Its flanks were bloody. Its claws were worn to the quick. When Astrid approached, singing low in her throat the way she soothed frightened animals, the wolf spun on her with eyes full of white and screamed — there was no other word for it — a sound with no wolf in it at all, and fled into the dark.

Astrid stood shaking for a long time afterward. Ashveil pressed his good wing around her shoulders, which was as close as a griffin came to an embrace.

"That could be me," she said. "If I lose the gift. If it goes quiet in me. Is that what waiting looks like?"

"No," Ashveil said. Then, honestly, because he had never lied to her and she suspected he never would: "I don't know. Your gift is not like theirs. Yours is a door, not a window. But Astrid — hear me — whatever happens, you will not end up digging at a crossroads. I will sit on you first."

Despite everything, she laughed. It came out cracked, but it was real.

* * *

On the fourth day they reached the edge of the Thornwood, and Astrid understood why the Choir's reach thinned here.

The forest was alive in a way the farmland was not. Not healthy — nothing was healthy anymore — but stubborn. Trees leaned together like conspirators. Moss grew thick and green in the shadows. And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat, she felt the old current running, the deep speech, muted and wounded but present. When she laid her palm against a birch trunk, she didn't hear words, but she felt attention. The forest noticed her.

"It knows what I am," she murmured.

"Everything wild does," Ashveil said. "That's rather the problem."

They made camp in a hollow ringed by hawthorn, and Astrid cooked the last of the Marrow's Ford cheese over a small fire while Ashveil dozed upright, one eye on the sky out of habit, though there was nothing up there to see. Ever. Again.

She found herself thinking of home. Of her mother's hands in bread dough. Of the lamplit kitchen and the way her mother had burned her childhood drawings — the talking fox, the weeping bear — without a word, and how the firelight had caught something in her mother's face that wasn't shame. She was still deciding what it *was*, that expression. She thought about it the way you probe a bruise.

The world was bigger than the valley. That was today's lesson, written in bone-colored wheat and screaming wolves and a kindly old man who would have sold her for seed grain. The world was big, and it was hurting, and it had chosen a story to explain the hurt, and she was the monster in the story.

And yet.

She looked at her hands again, over the fire. The same ordinary hands. But tonight, resting on her knee, they tingled faintly, the way they always did near deep magic — and the tingle was stronger here than it had been in the valley. Stronger than it had been yesterday. As if the darker the sky grew, the louder the wild things called, and the more of their calling pooled in her.

*A door, not a window,* Ashveil had said.

She was still turning that over when the hawthorn around their hollow rustled — not wind. Something moving with intent, several somethings, circling. Ashveil's head snapped up, crest rising, and a growl built in his chest like distant thunder.

From the darkness beyond the firelight, a voice spoke. Human. Calm. Almost gentle.

"Don't run," it said. "Please. I've been following you for two days, and I'm very tired, and I promise you — I'm not the one you should be afraid of."

Astrid's hand found the fire iron. Ashveil rose to his full height, wings half-spread, broken wing and all.

The voice sighed, and footsteps crunched closer, and a figure stepped into the light wearing a dead man's name and a Choir cantor's boots.
