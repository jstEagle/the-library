## 6. The Defector

Corin Hale had been dead for eleven months, and he was finding it agreeable work.

The dead, he had learned, were permitted a great many things the living were not. The dead could walk out of Vesperhold without a bell tolling their departure. The dead could let their beards grow gray at the chin and stop humming the Song on the hour, every hour, until their jaws ached from the habit of it. The dead could sit in a ditch above a mountain pass in the dark, eating cold goat cheese and watching a girl sleep beside a wounded griffin, and no one would think to ask them what they intended.

He intended nothing. That was the honest truth of it, and honesty had become a kind of luxury since he'd died — or rather, since he'd arranged his death, which was a different thing entirely and required considerably more paperwork.

*Corin Hale, Cantor Third Voice, lost to river ice during the winter crossing at Marrow Ford.* He had written the report himself. It had been strangely moving, composing his own eulogy. *A faithful servant of the Song. His voice will be missed in the lower registers.* The Maestro had signed the mourning order with her left hand, which meant she hadn't believed a word of it, but she'd signed it all the same, because Solenne Vael did not waste energy chasing ghosts when there were living speakers to hunt.

Or so he had thought, eleven months ago.

Now he watched through the dark as hooded riders picked their way up the pass below him — six of them, moving wrong, moving like men who had been told to be quiet by someone they feared — and he understood that his ghosthood had an expiration date after all.

Because those riders were hunting the girl in the hollow below. And Corin Hale, dead man, traitor, coward, debtor, could not make himself stand up and walk away.

* * *

Three days earlier, he had first seen her from a ridge above the valley, and he had nearly turned back.

That was the thing about Astrid Iversen that no one had warned him about: she looked ordinary. He had spent years among the Choir's archives, reading about speakers as though they were weather phenomena — forces, threats, wild notes that needed muting before they unraveled the Song. He had imagined them glowing. Marked. Something you could see coming.

Instead she was a thin young woman with straw in her hair, kneeling in a frozen turnip field, talking to a lame fox.

Corin had gone very still against the granite, the way he'd learned to go still in the Choir's dormitories when the night-cantors made their rounds. Below him, the girl cupped her hands around her mouth and said something too quiet to carry up the slope, and the fox — the *lame* fox, favoring its bad leg even as it trotted — sat down in the snow in front of her like a dog called to heel, and answered.

Corin couldn't hear the answer. He never could. That was the whole cruelty of his position: he had spent nine years inside the machine that devoured voices, and he himself had no gift worth devouring. He was a cantor the way a tuning fork is music — precise, trained, empty. The Choir valued him exactly as much as a tuning fork is valued, which is to say, they kept him in a velvet case and used him to check whether other things were true.

But he didn't need to hear the fox. He could see the shape of the conversation in the girl's shoulders. She laughed — a small, guilty laugh, the laugh of someone who has just remembered she isn't supposed to exist — and the fox flopped over dramatically in the snow, and she scolded it, and it ignored her, and something in Corin's chest cracked along a seam he had thought was scar tissue.

He knew then why he had come. Not the reason he told himself — not the archive, not the evidence, not the ledger of stolen stars he'd memorized and smuggled out in fragments. Those were reasons. This was the reason:

Nine years ago, they had brought a speaker into the Choir's binding hall. A boy, younger than Corin had been. And the boy had looked up at the circle of cantors, at Corin specifically, at the one face in the ring that wasn't singing yet, and he had said — in a voice that made the candles lean toward him — *"You don't have to do this. You know you don't."*

And Corin had sung anyway.

So. There it was. Eleven months dead, three hundred miles walked, one valley found, and the entire grand architecture of his defection reduced to a single outstanding debt with compound interest.

He watched the girl say goodnight to the fox. He watched her look up at the sky — at the few stubborn stars still burning above the valley's wards, the last ones left anywhere — with an expression he recognized from mirrors. Longing with nowhere to put itself.

Then the wards failed, and everything got much worse very quickly.

* * *

Corin had felt wards break before. They usually went quietly, a sigh of released pressure, like a door opening in a house you thought was empty.

These shattered. The sound rolled up the mountainside like a wineglass the size of the sky being snapped in half, and above the valley the remaining stars went out — not fading, not dimming, but *going*, one by one, snuffed in sequence like candles pinched by a careful hand. Left to right. Deliberate.

"No," Corin whispered, because there are some words that must be said aloud even when alone on a freezing ridge with no one to hear them. "No, no, we're not due here for another—"

He stopped. Did the math again. Went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude.

They weren't due here. Which meant this wasn't the planned harvest. Which meant either the Choir had moved faster than the intelligence he'd risked his life to send south — or someone else had broken the wards, someone the Choir hadn't sent, and the riders now climbing the pass behind him were not cantors at all.

He got his answer within the hour, when the griffin fell out of the sky.

It came down through the starless dark like a thrown spear, wings tattered, trailing blood that steamed in the cold, and it hit the barn roof of the nearest farmstead hard enough to crack a beam. Corin heard the family shouting. Heard the girl — Astrid, he was nearly certain it was Astrid, the age fit, the valley fit — run out into the yard. Heard her stop.

And then he heard nothing, which frightened him more than the crash had, because a farmyard where a griffin has just fallen should be full of screaming dogs and panicked livestock, and instead there was only silence, a held-breath silence, the silence of every animal in earshot turning to listen to something.

To someone.

Corin pressed his forehead against the cold rock and breathed once, slowly, the way the Choir taught initiates to breathe before a binding. *In for the count of four. Hold for the count of four. Out for the count of four. Your voice is not your own; your voice belongs to the Song.*

He had obeyed that instruction for nine years. He was done with counts of four.

"Right," he said to no one. "Hale. You're going to get yourself killed properly this time."

* * *

The trouble with following a speaker across open country, Corin discovered, was that the animals kept announcing her.

Not loudly. Nothing so useful as barking. But twice on the second day out of the valley he watched crows lift off a fence line in perfect unison and wheel once in the air — a movement with no purpose a crow would recognize — before scattering. Once, a herd of deer stood at the forest edge facing precisely the direction she'd passed, motionless, attentive, like an audience between acts.

She couldn't help it. That was the horror of the gift, and the beauty of it, and the reason the Choir feared it. Wild magic didn't wait to be invited. It leaked. Every beast within a mile of Astrid Iversen became, briefly, a little more awake than beasts were meant to be.

Which also meant she was the easiest quarry the hunters had ever tracked. They didn't need hounds. They needed only to follow the wake of attention she left through the world.

Corin followed the same wake, two hours behind, and hated himself for the efficiency of it.

He caught up to her on the third night, at the place where the old shepherd's road narrowed between a cliff face and a drop into black pines. He had meant to simply watch. Observe the hunters' pattern, count their numbers, find the flaw in their formation — that was the plan, the sensible plan, the plan of a man who had survived the Choir by never volunteering.

Then he saw the crossbow.

It was set up on the ledge above the road, and it was not aimed at the girl. It was aimed at the space *ahead* of the girl, at the point on the path she would reach in forty seconds, and Corin understood the design of the trap in one sick instant: they didn't want her dead. They wanted her *frightened*. Frightened straight down the side trail, into the ravine, where four men waited with a binding net and a suppression collar, and where the procedure would begin before she ever saw a face.

Standard harvest formation. He had drilled it. He had, on two occasions, held the collar.

Forty seconds.

Later — in the long sleepless nights afterward, and there would be many — Corin would try to reconstruct what happened next and fail. He would remember standing up. He would remember the cold air going into his lungs like a blade. He would remember that somewhere between the ledge and the crossbowman's position he began to sing, which was insane, which was suicide, because his voice carried the Choir's training in every note and any cantor within a mile would know it instantly —

— but that was rather the point.

He came over the rocks singing the *Requiem for the Unbound*, which was not a requiem at all but a Choir composition, a hunting song, designed to freeze a speaker's gift in their chest like a hand closing around a bird. He sang it wrong. He sang it a quarter-tone flat and dragging, deliberately, the way you'd play a beloved piece badly to ruin it forever, and the effect on the crossbowman was immediate and beautiful: the man flinched as though struck, whirled toward the sound, and made the worst possible assumption — that the Choir had arrived, that the trap was compromised, that his superiors were watching.

He fired wide. Then he ran.

Corin dropped off the ledge onto the road directly in front of Astrid Iversen, who had stopped walking and was staring at him with an expression composed of equal parts terror and fury, and who had, he noticed distantly, a griffin limping behind her like an enormous wounded shadow.

"Down the road," Corin gasped. "Not the side trail. The side trail is—"

The net took him mid-sentence.

* * *

*Astrid*

The man in the net fought like someone who had read about fighting in books.

That was Astrid's first coherent thought, crouched behind a boulder with Ashveil's hot breath stirring her hair, watching four hooded figures close on the stranger they'd snagged. Her second thought was less charitable: *he led them right to us.* Because of course he had. A stranger appearing out of nowhere on a mountain road, singing — *singing*, of all the cursed things — days after the stars went out and the riders came? She wasn't stupid. She'd grown up in a valley that survived by assuming the worst of everyone.

Except.

Ashveil rumbled low in her chest, and Astrid felt the words arrive the way she always felt them — not sound, exactly, but meaning pressing against the inside of her skull, feathered and strange.

*He smells of bells,* the griffin said. *Old bells. And under the bells — rot. And under the rot—* the griffin's talons flexed against stone *— grief. Old grief. The kind that's been carried so far it's worn smooth.*

"That could mean anything," Astrid whispered.

*It means he is telling the truth,* Ashveil said, *about being sorry. Whether he's telling the truth about anything else, little speaker, is your problem, not mine. Decide quickly. They're going to cut his throat.*

Which they did appear to be preparing to do, and Astrid discovered that her body had already decided, because she was already standing up, already reaching for the only weapon she had, the forbidden one, the one that had gotten her whole lineage exiled from decent company a hundred years before she was born.

She opened herself to the dark hillside and *asked*.

What answered was not gentle. Three days without starlight had done something to the beasts of these mountains — she'd seen it in the foxes, in the cattle, a rawness behind the eyes, a hunger with no floor to it. But rawness could be spoken to. Rawness was, if anything, easier to speak to than calm.

The ravine erupted. Goats — a dozen feral goats with eyes like coals — came off the scree in a clattering avalanche, and behind them something larger, a mountain cat gone gaunt and half-mad, moving with the terrible economy of a starving thing. The hooded men shouted, broke, scattered. The net's owner abandoned it. And in the sudden ringing quiet, the stranger lay tangled in rope in the middle of the road, bleeding from a split lip, looking up at Astrid with an expression she would remember for a long time afterward.

Not fear. Worse than fear. Recognition — the look of a man seeing a debt walk toward him wearing a face.

"You," he said hoarsely, "are supposed to be running the other direction."

"And you," Astrid said, "have thirty seconds to explain why I shouldn't let the cat come back."

* * *

His name was Corin Hale, and he was a liar, and he admitted it almost immediately, which Astrid found more disorienting than any smooth story would have been.

"I was Choir," he said, sitting against a boulder with his wrists still bound — he'd refused to let her cut the net until she'd heard him out, which was either integrity or theater, and she genuinely could not tell. "Cantor, third voice. Nine years. I faked my death last winter. I've been following you since the valley."

"Why?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at his hands.

"I can't tell you that," he said finally. "Not — I won't lie to you, that's the one thing I'm asking you to believe. I can't explain it yet. If I explain it, you'll do the brave thing instead of the smart thing, and the smart thing is that you keep moving north and pretend you never saw me."

"The brave thing being?"

"Helping me." He said it flatly, without self-pity. "And you shouldn't. Whatever you've guessed about the Choir, it's worse. And whatever you think I can give you—" he gestured vaguely at the starless sky, and Astrid's stomach clenched, because how did he know, how did he *know* what she looked at when she couldn't sleep— "it won't be worth what it costs. Ask me anything except why, and I'll answer true."

Ashveil shifted behind her, a landslide of feathers and muscle, and spoke into the quiet place behind Astrid's eyes.

*Ask him what he held,* the griffin said. *Everyone carries something. Watch the hands when he answers.*

So Astrid asked. "In the Choir. What did you do?"

And Corin Hale looked at his hands, exactly as the griffin had predicted, and turned them over slowly, palms up, as though checking them for stains that wouldn't wash.

"Held collars," he said. "Twice. On children." His voice didn't waver, which somehow made it worse. "I sang the calming verse while they were fitted. It's designed to feel like being rocked to sleep. That's the cruelty of it — we were taught to make it *kind*. And both times, the speaker looked at me the same way. Like they were trying to figure out how the person rocking them to sleep was also the person locking the cage." He closed his hands. "I never figured out how either. So I died, and I started walking, and I walked until I found you."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere upslope, the feral cat screamed once, a sound like a hinge in a haunted house, and went silent.

"You could still be lying," Astrid said.

"Yes," Corin agreed. "I could. That's the trouble with me. Everything I say, you'll have to weigh yourself, and I'll never be able to tip the scale for you. All I can offer is this:" he finally held out his bound wrists toward her knife. "Cut me loose or leave me tied for the hunters. Either way, I'm going north, because north is where the answer is, and I've spent nine years being a note in someone else's song and I am *done* with it."

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. Then at Ashveil, who regarded the man with one ancient golden eye and offered no opinion at all, which from Ashveil was practically a shrug.

She cut the ropes.

"North," she said. "But you walk downwind, where I can smell you, and if the animals start acting strange around you, I'll know."

"They'll act strange around me regardless," Corin said, rubbing his wrists. "Everything does, lately. It's one of the perks of being dead."

Behind them, far down the dark road, a bell rang — a single clear note, impossible this deep in wild country, where no church stood for fifty miles. Both of them went still.

Corin's face, in the starless dark, went the color of ash.

"That's a calling note," he whispered. "That's the Maestro. She knows I'm alive."
