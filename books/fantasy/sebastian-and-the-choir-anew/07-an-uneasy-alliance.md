## 7. An Uneasy Alliance

The fox had been right about the man, which was the most irritating thing about the whole situation.

*He smells of bells and burnt paper,* Sebastian had said, watching Corin Hale from the shelter of a gorse bush with his one good eye narrowed. *But underneath that, he smells of a grave. His own.*

Astrid had not known what to do with that information, so she had done what she always did with things she didn't know how to hold: she'd put it down carefully and walked around it.

Now she walked beside it instead, because the road south gave her no choice. Three days since the ambush at the ford — three days since six hooded riders had come out of the alder trees with nets woven from something that hummed, and Corin Hale had stepped between her and them with a cantor's staff in his hands and something in his voice that made the humming nets *flinch*. Three days since she'd watched him break a fellow Choirman's wrist with an apology so quiet she'd almost missed it.

Three days of not trusting him, and counting.

"You're staring at me again," Corin said. He didn't look up from the fire he was building. Small, smokeless, efficient — everything about him was small and efficient, as though he'd practiced taking up less room than he actually did.

"I'm looking at the road behind you," Astrid said.

"Ah." He struck flint to steel. "The road behind me. Of course."

"The road behind you is where more of your friends will come from."

"They are not my friends." The fire caught. He fed it a twig like he was paying it. "And they are not coming from behind us. They'll swing wide to the east now. They think I'm leading you toward the coast."

"And why would they think that?"

"Because I told them I would." He glanced up then, and his eyes were gray and tired and entirely too honest for a liar. "Before I faked my death. Before any of this. I told my superiors I would hunt you to the sea if you ever surfaced, and they believed me, because until eight months ago I was exactly the sort of man who would have."

Astrid crouched across the fire from him, arms on her knees. Ashveil lay behind her in the shadow of a rock overhang, the griffin's wound dressed with moss and the last of her poultice, her golden eyes half-lidded but never fully closed. The griffin slept the way soldiers sleep — ready to stop.

"Prove it," Astrid said.

Corin's hands went still. "I beg your pardon?"

"The ambushers. You say they weren't yours. You say you didn't call them, didn't lead them, didn't sell me to them for whatever the Choir pays traitors who aren't actually traitors." She kept her voice flat. It was easier to be hard when you were flat. "Prove it."

"How?"

"That," said Astrid, "is your problem."

* * *

His problem turned out to be a matter of walking north for half a day along the riverbank, with Ashveil limping sulkily above them on the ridge line — she refused to be left behind, and communicated this by landing in front of them twice and blocking the path until they adjusted course to include her — until they reached the burned-out shepherd's hut where the ambush had been planned.

Astrid knew it was the place before Corin said anything. She knew it because the animals knew it. A pair of crows sat on the blackened roof beam, and when she looked at them the way she was not supposed to look at anything, they showed her: men in gray hoods, a map held flat against a knee, a voice saying *the defector will bring her through the ford, the Maestro wants her breathing*, and under all of it a smell like cold iron and candle wax.

She came out of the crow-sight with her heart going hard and found Corin watching her with an expression she couldn't read.

"You saw them," he said. Not a question.

"Six men. Gray hoods. They had orders about the ford." She swallowed. "They had orders about *you*, too. If you appeared, I was to watch what happened next."

Something moved behind his face — quick, like a fish under ice. "And what happens next, according to their plan?"

"They weren't told. That's the part that frightened them." She hugged her arms around herself. "Cantors don't frighten easily, Corin. These men were frightened of you."

He nodded slowly, and then he did something she hadn't expected. He sat down on a fallen roof beam, in the middle of the evidence, and said, "Ask them anything else. Ask the birds, the mice, whatever it is you do. Ask them whether I was here."

"They wouldn't know you."

"They would know a cantor's staff. They would know the Song-mark." He pushed back his sleeve, and there it was on his inner forearm: a scar, old and white, in the shape of a stylized bell. Every Choir mage bore one, branded at initiation. Ashveil had nearly taken his arm off the first time she'd seen it. "If I scouted this ground for them, there'd be traces. Cantors leave marks. We can't help it — the Song runs through everything we touch. Ask the crows if they smelled bell-wax here before the ambush. Ask them."

Astrid asked.

It took an hour. Crows were gossips but poor witnesses; she had to triangulate through a nervous wren, a very opinionated magpie, and a field mouse who mostly wanted to discuss the ethics of owls. But the picture assembled itself, piece by piece, and every piece pointed the same direction: six men, arriving two nights before the ambush, smelling of iron and wax and nothing else. No bell-scarred hand had touched this ruin in years.

"No," she said at last, quietly. "You weren't here."

"No," Corin agreed. "I wasn't."

"So someone else sold me. Someone who knew your routes. Knew your signals." She looked at him. "Someone close enough to you to impersonate you."

"Yes." He said it steadily, but his hands, she noticed, were folded very tightly in his lap, knuckles pale. "That would be Cantor Veyra. My former mentor. She has my cipher-ring. She's been using it since the day I died."

* * *

They walked back south in the long gold light of afternoon, and Astrid spent most of it thinking about trust the way a farmer thinks about weather: watching it, measuring it, refusing to bet the harvest on it.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it.

The crows had shown her the ambushers' faces. And in showing her, they had also shown her something else, something she hadn't gone looking for: the memory of the moment those six men had first caught scent of *Corin Hale* on the trail — dead man, defector, ghost — and every single one of them had taken an involuntary step backward.

Ghosts, apparently, still frightened the Choir. And the Choir did not frighten.

Which meant either Corin Hale was a very good actor, or he was genuinely the kind of man whose own people feared what he might do. Neither option made her feel safe. One of them, possibly, made her feel *less alone*.

That evening they camped in a hollow of birches, and Ashveil allowed Corin — *allowed*, there was no other word for it, the griffin extended one wing like a drawbridge lowering and permitted him to re-dress the wound along her flank — and Astrid decided it was time for the second test.

The first test had been the crows. The second test was dinner.

"We need supplies," she said, while the rabbit Corin had snared turned on its spit. "There's a farmstead half a mile east. Goats, a dog, chickens. I'll trade healing for flour and salt."

"I'll come with—"

"No." She stood, brushing off her skirt. "You'll stay here. With Ashveil. And when I come back, I'm going to ask the dog what happened while I was gone."

Corin blinked. Then, slowly, the corner of his mouth moved. "You're going to interrogate a dog."

"Dogs are terrible liars. Dogs think everything is interesting and they tell you everything twice." She shouldered her basket. "If you moved wrong, touched my pack, read my notes, or so much as breathed on Ashveil's saddlebags, I'll know. The dog will tell me. In detail. Whether I want it or not."

"That is," Corin said gravely, "the most terrifying intelligence network I have ever heard of."

"Welcome to my life."

* * *

The farmstead belonged to a widow named Marta whose goat had a twisted gut and whose dog, a brindled lurcher named Bristle, appointed himself Astrid's escort the moment she scratched behind his ears. The goat was easy. The widow wept with gratitude and sent Astrid off with more flour than the healing was worth, plus a jar of honey "for the road, for whoever's waiting for you, you shouldn't travel alone, girl, it's not safe."

*If only you knew,* Astrid thought, and smiled, and left.

Bristle escorted her back to the birch hollow, and it was Bristle who told her — in the rambling, joyful, everything-is-news way dogs tell things — what had happened in her absence.

*The sad man sat very still the whole time,* Bristle reported, trotting ahead with his tail going. *The big bird slept and the sad man watched the trees. Once the bird whimpered in her sleep and the sad man went to her and put his hand very soft on her neck and sang — not loud, tiny singing, inside-singing — and the bad smell in her hurt got quieter and she stopped shaking. Then he cried a little bit. Just a little. Then he went back and sat still. Also a squirrel fell out of a tree.*

Astrid stopped walking.

*What?* said Bristle, circling back, delighted by the delay. *Do you want the squirrel part again?*

"No," Astrid said faintly. "No, I — thank you, Bristle. Go home."

She stood at the edge of the hollow for a long moment, basket heavy on her arm, watching the firelight through the birches. Inside-singing. Healing-song, worked into a wounded griffin without being asked, without being seen, expecting nothing. Cantors didn't do that. Cantors *took*. The whole architecture of the Choir was built on taking — that was what Ashveil had gasped at her in the barn, that was what the beasts' madness beyond the valley whispered, that was the shape of the horror growing clearer every mile south.

And yet.

She walked into camp. Corin looked up from the fire. Ashveil's breathing was deep and even, deeper than it had been in days, and the griffin opened one eye, regarded Astrid with unmistakable smugness, and closed it again.

"He sang to your wound," Astrid said.

Corin went very still. It was remarkable, really, how completely still a man could go — like a held breath, like a note sustained past all reason. "I don't know what you mean."

"Bristle does. Bristle tells me everything twice." She set down the basket. "You healed her. Quietly. While I was gone. Why?"

For a long moment he said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere overhead, in the starless dark, the silence pressed down the way it always did now — that awful felt absence where the sky used to sing.

"Because she's in pain," he said finally, "and I could hear it. All the time, Astrid. Every hour of every day since the ford, I can *hear* her hurting, the way you'd hear a violin played badly in the next room. I've been hearing it for three days and I thought I was going to climb out of my skin." His voice frayed at the edges. "So yes. I sang to her. Because the alternative was listening to her suffer and doing nothing, and I spent eleven years doing that. Doing nothing. Listening." He looked into the fire. "I'm finished listening."

Astrid sat down across from him. She unwrapped the honey jar and set it on the stone between them, deliberately, like a flag planted.

"Marta says honey makes the road kinder," she said.

He stared at the jar. Then at her. "Is this—"

"It's honey, Corin. Don't make it a treaty." But her mouth was doing something complicated, and she suspected it was losing the fight to stay stern. "One condition."

"Name it."

"You teach me that inside-singing. Not the Choir's version. Yours. If I'm going to be hunted across half the world, I want to know how to quiet a wound." She paused. "And Ashveil gets to decide if your lessons are honest. She has excellent taste in lies. She's smelled yours."

"She has," Corin agreed solemnly. "How do I rate?"

Astrid considered. Above them the empty sky wheeled, vast and hungry and waiting. Below them, a dying griffin slept soundly for the first time in days, and a jar of honey sat on a stone between two people who had every reason not to trust each other, sharing a fire anyway.

"Lies and grief," she said. "In equal measure. But the grief's winning."

Corin laughed — short, surprised, rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut. And it was that sound, more than the crows' testimony, more than the healed wing, that tipped something in Astrid's chest a half-inch toward belief.

They ate. They divided the watches. And in the smallest hours, when it was her turn to keep the fire and his turn to sleep, Astrid heard him murmur in his dreams — fragments, half-words, the shapes of names — and one phrase repeated, over and over, soft and broken:

*I didn't know. I didn't know. I didn't know.*

She was still wondering what he hadn't known when Ashveil's head came up off the ground with a snap, nostrils flared, golden eyes fixed on the southern dark — where, far off but closing, Astrid heard it too.

Bells. Many bells, ringing in a pattern that was not a pattern, that was a *summons*.

And beneath the bells, thin as wire, a voice on the wind that made Ashveil scream and Corin bolt upright, white-faced, whispering a name:

"Veyra."
