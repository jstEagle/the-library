## 8. The Choir's Song

Corin Hale had learned to sleep in the saddle the way other men learned to pray — out of necessity, and with a persistent suspicion that he was doing it wrong.

He woke on the third night after the ambush with his cheek against his mare's warm neck and Astrid's fire burning low across the clearing. She sat up watching him. She had been watching him when he closed his eyes, too. He was beginning to suspect she never slept at all, but simply went somewhere inside herself where the animals kept watch for her.

"You snore," she said.

"Horses snore," Corin said. "I emit a dignified rumble."

"The mare says otherwise."

"The mare is a gossip."

The corner of her mouth twitched — the first almost-smile he'd coaxed from her in three days of hard riding — and then it was gone again, folded away like a letter she couldn't afford to be caught reading. He understood. Trusting him cost her something every hour, and she paid it anyway, and he had not yet earned a single coin of it.

He should tell her, he thought. Not everything. But some of it. The part that mattered: why a Choir cantor would fake his own death and spend two years eating roots and lying to wardens just to end up here, shivering beside a girl who spoke to foxes.

But the truth was a long road, and the night was long too, and so for once he did what the old cantors taught novices to do when they could not bear the present: he went back into memory, and let the Song carry him.

* * *

He had been seven years old the first time he heard the Choir sing.

Not the way everyone heard it — the hum that lived under Vesperhold's cobblestones, the lullaby mothers breathed over cradles without knowing they did it. He meant *heard* it, the true hearing, the way only the chosen few ever could. His mother had taken him to the Grand Amphitheater on the Feast of Concord, and when the nine hundred voices of the Choir rose together, Corin had felt the sound go through his chest like a hand through water.

Every voice separate. Every voice one. That was the miracle they taught you first, before they taught you anything else.

"They ended the war," his mother whispered, tears standing in her eyes as the harmonies climbed toward the vaulted ceiling. "Remember that, little bird. Whatever else anyone tells you — they *ended* it."

The war had been older than any kingdom. The histories called it the Discordance, though the name was scrubbed soft by centuries of retelling. Two great powers — the star-crowned mages of the old courts and the wild speakers who commanded beast and root and storm — had ground the world between them for three hundred years. Rivers ran backward. Forests walked. Children were born already knowing how to scream in wolf.

And then, the story went, twelve mages climbed the mountain of Vel Sarran and made a choice no one had made before: instead of fighting the wild magic, they would *out-sing* it. They bound their twelve voices into a single harmony so vast, so perfectly consonant, that chaos itself had to fall quiet and listen. Where the Song passed, beasts lay down. Where the Song passed, rivers remembered their banks. The war ended not with a battle but with a chord, held for nine days and nights, until the whole world forgot how to be anything but calm.

That was the story. It was even mostly true.

What the story left out was what happened on the tenth day — when the twelve tried to let the chord go, and found they couldn't.

Corin learned that part at nineteen, in his third year as a novice cantor, from a dying archivist named Brother Ellery who had decided, for reasons he never explained, to tell one novice the truth before the truth died with him.

"Come here, boy," Ellery had rasped, from his cot in the infirmary of the Lower Conservatory, where they sent cantors whose voices had begun to fray. "And close the door. And if anyone asks, you came to fetch my slippers."

"I came to fetch your slippers," Corin said solemnly.

"Liar. Good. You'll need that." Ellery's laugh turned into a cough. When it passed, he gripped Corin's wrist with fingers thin as winter branches. "They told you the origin story. Twelve mages, one chord, war ends. All true. What they don't tell you — what maybe only four people alive still know — is what the chord was *made of*."

"Harmony," Corin said, because that was the catechism answer.

"Harmony, yes. And what holds a harmony together?" Ellery's eyes were bright and terrible. "Tension, boy. A chord is a held breath. Twelve voices pulling against each other and refusing to let go. And a held breath needs *fuel*. Do you know what fuels the Song? What has always fueled it?"

Corin shook his head.

"Gifts." The word dropped like a stone down a well. "Wild speaker-gifts. The thing the Song was built to silence is the thing it eats. The twelve didn't defeat the wild magic, boy. They built a furnace, and the wild magic is the coal."

The infirmary was very quiet. Somewhere below, the evening bells rang the Hour of Concord, and Corin felt the familiar hum rise through the floor stones, through his boot soles, into his teeth. He had loved that hum his whole life. In that moment he felt it against his bones like the breath of something enormous sleeping underneath the city — something that stirred, and dreamed, and was hungry.

"The stars," Corin said slowly. He didn't know why he said it. The thought arrived whole, the way truths do when they've been waiting a long time for someone foolish enough to speak them aloud. "The stars went out during the Discordance. Everyone says the war burned them away."

Ellery smiled, and it was the saddest smile Corin had ever seen on a human face.

"Did you ever wonder," the old man said, "why the sky went dark all at once? Not gradually. Not like fires dying. All at once, in a single night, hundreds of lights — gone. Like someone snuffed them." His grip tightened. "Stars aren't fire, boy. The oldest records say the stars are *voices*. The first voices. The world's own song, hung in the sky where nothing could silence it. And when the twelve needed fuel for their great chord —"

"No," Corin said.

"— they reached up," Ellery finished gently, "and they took it."

* * *

After that night, Corin did what clever cowards have always done: he kept walking, kept singing, kept his face arranged into devotion, and told himself he'd find proof before he judged anyone.

Proof took him six more years, and it was worse than the rumor.

It was in the Archive Beneath — the sealed levels under the Grand Amphitheater where the Choir kept its true history, guarded by oaths and wards and the simple fact that no cantor ever wanted to look. Corin got down there on a forged requisition, heart hammering, carrying a lantern he was technically not permitted to light.

The first thing he found was ledgers. Beautiful ones, gold-tooled, lovingly maintained. *Acquisitions*, they were titled. Centuries of them. And Corin, who had grown up on hymns about mercy, read entry after entry in a fair hand that never trembled:

*Speaker, female, approx. 30 years. Gift: weather-sense. Yield: exceptional. Taken at harvest, per schedule.*

*Speaker, male child, approx. 9 years. Gift: beast-speech. Yield: modest but sustained. Note: subject sang beautifully for the first week.*

Beast-speech. A nine-year-old child who talked to animals, brought in like a sack of grain, and *sang beautifully for the first week* — until the Song drank what was singable in him and left the rest.

Corin sat down on the archive floor among four hundred years of immaculate bookkeeping and was sick into a waste bin meant for candle stubs.

Because here was the arithmetic no one said aloud: the Song needed feeding. Always had. The twelve founders had discovered it on the tenth day, when their chord began to gutter, and they had faced the choice every addict faces — let the music die, or find something to burn. They chose the sky first, because the stars were far away and beautiful and no one owned them. And when the stars ran low, they turned to the speakers. And when the speakers grew rare — hunted, hidden, driven into valleys behind failing wards — the Choir's power grew thin, and the hunger grew loud, and the Maestros of recent years had begun to whisper about *final solutions*, about finding the last gifts and ending hunger forever, one way or another.

Corin had been raised on the story of heroes who ended a war. He had given his voice — literally, ritually, in a ceremony at twenty-one where they unspooled something golden from his chest and wound it into the greater harmony — to that story. And the whole time, he had been a finger on the hand that fed the furnace.

He remembered the exact moment complicity stopped being an abstraction. It was a routine assignment, his fourth year vested: accompany the Wardens to a village report. A woman there, a hedge-witch the neighbors suspected. Nothing dramatic. He stood in her kitchen while the Wardens questioned her, and she had flour on her hands, and a cat sat in her window glaring at him with an expression Corin now recognized everywhere — the look of an animal watching a cage being carried toward it.

She looked at Corin — at his vestments, his kind young face — and asked, politely, hopefully: "You're a man of the Choir. Will you vouch for me?"

And Corin, whose orders were to observe and confirm, had looked at the flour on her hands and said, "I'm sure it's all a misunderstanding, madam."

They took her that night. Her yield was recorded in the spring ledger. *Modest but sustained.*

Her name had been Maren. Corin had looked it up afterward, which was the worst thing he ever did, because a number can stay a number, but Maren had a name, and a kitchen, and flour on her hands, and a cat that had watched him lie.

* * *

He faked his death fourteen months later. Poorly, as it happened — a staged drowning in the Vesper canal that fooled the parish records but apparently not the Maestro's spies, though he wouldn't learn that until much later, at a price. He shed his vestments in a farmer's pond, buried his ring under a cairn, and walked north with nothing but a stolen coat and a plan so vague it was less a plan than a direction: *away from the furnace, toward whatever was left.*

Two years of that. Two years of learning how much of him the Choir had taken without asking. His voice still worked, but it worked *wrong* now — there was a thread of the greater Song woven through it, and when he sang alone, he could feel the harmonic tugging at him like a leash, reporting back, he suspected, to whoever still held the spool. So he mostly didn't sing. A cantor who doesn't sing is a fish that has decided against water; he had gotten very good at silence, and silence, he discovered, was where all the things he'd outrun were waiting.

Including this: the knowledge that knowing the truth was not the same as paying for it. He had read the ledgers. He had said *I'm sure it's all a misunderstanding.* Knowing changed nothing for Maren. Knowing was free.

Paying would cost something real. He just hadn't figured out what yet.

And then, three weeks ago, in a market town at the edge of the wastes, he had seen a lame fox sit down in front of a girl in a patched cloak and *talk to her* — not in words, exactly, but in that leaning-in way, that conversation of tilt and ear and eye that Corin had only ever seen described in confiscated books. And he had seen the girl talk back, shyly, secretly, like someone greeting an old friend in a country where the friend's language was forbidden.

A speaker. A living, breathing, unrecorded speaker, walking around loose in the world with a gift the ledgers would call *exceptional*.

His first thought — and he was honest enough with himself, most nights, to admit it — had been fear. For her, yes, but also a smaller, uglier flicker of *not me, please not my problem*. He had spent two years being no one's problem.

His second thought, arriving a heartbeat later and staying forever, was Maren's kitchen. Flour on her hands. *Will you vouch for me?*

So he followed the girl. Badly, at first — she caught him twice and glared like the cat in the window. Then the riders came, hooded and patient, and Corin understood that the Choir had found her too, and following became saving, and saving became — this. A cold fire. A girl who claimed he snored. A griffin dying slowly in the trees above them, breathing a smell like hot iron and snow.

* * *

"You're doing the thing again," Astrid said.

Corin blinked back into the present. The fire had burned lower. Ashveil's silhouette shifted painfully in the branches above, feathers rustling like dry paper.

"What thing?"

"The remembering thing. Your face goes somewhere and comes back bruised." She poked the fire with a stick. "The horse wants to know if you're going to betray us. I told her probably not, but she says your scent changes when you think about the past. Goes bitter. Like yarrow."

"The horse has strong opinions about my inner life."

"All the animals do. It's exhausting." She looked at him across the flames, and her eyes were the gray-green of deep water, and they missed nothing. "You were going to tell me something. Three days ago, when the riders nearly boxed us in the pass. You started to say *the reason I really left the Choir* and then the horn sounded and you never finished."

Corin turned a stick in his fingers. Above them, through a tear in the clouds, one patch of sky showed black and empty where men had once navigated by stars.

"If I tell you," he said, "you'll want to know why it took me so long. And the answer to that is shameful, and you'll look at me differently, and I've gotten fond of you looking at me the current way. Suspicious, but not disgusted. It's a comfortable arrangement."

"Try me," Astrid said. "I grew up hiding what I am from everyone I love. Disgust is a language I'm fluent in."

Something in the plainness of it undid him. He looked at the fire and told her — not all of it, not the ledgers, not yet, those were for a braver hour — but the shape of it. The twelve mages and the chord that wouldn't release. The hunger underneath the harmony. The stars, and what the stars had actually been, and who had put out the lights.

When he finished, the clearing was silent except for Ashveil's labored breathing and the small industrious sounds of the fire.

"The stars were voices," Astrid repeated. Her voice was very quiet. "Someone *ate the sky*."

"For fuel. To keep the peace running." Corin laughed, and it came out cracked. "That's the joke of it, if you want the joke. They built a machine to end violence, and the machine runs on devouring, and they've been calling the devouring 'harmony' for four hundred years. Nine hundred cantors in Vesperhold, singing their hearts out every feast day, and every note is a little bit of somebody's stolen gift. Mine included. I gave mine willingly. There's a ceremony. There's *cake*."

Astrid was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "The fox I knew — Sebastian — he told me once that the stars used to talk to the old speakers. Before his grandmother's time. He said they went quiet all in one night, and the elders wept like the sky was a graveyard." She looked up at the torn clouds. "He was right. It is one."

"I'm sorry," Corin said. "For all of it. For my part in it, which is larger than you know and worse than I've said."

"I believe you," Astrid said, and then, before he could decide whether to be relieved: "The mare still thinks you might betray us, though. She says bitter isn't the same as sorry."

"The mare," Corin said, "is a hard audience."

"She's the only honest one here." Astrid lay back, arms behind her head, and stared at the starless dark. "Tell me the rest tomorrow. The ledgers, or whatever you're not saying. I want all of it before we get wherever we're going."

"Wherever we're going," Corin said, and did not say: *Vesperhold. The city of bells. The throat of the furnace itself,* because Ashveil's wound was worsening and the only healer who could mend a griffin lived behind the Choir's own walls, and because he had not yet found the courage to suggest it.

Instead he banked the fire, and settled against his mare's warm side, and let himself drift — and in the drifting, half-dream, he heard it: faint, far off, threaded through the wind from the south.

Bells.

Vesperhold's bells, ringing the Hour of Concord across forty miles of empty country, and beneath them — so faint only a trained ear would catch it, and Corin's ear was trained whether he wanted it to be or not — the hum of the Song itself. And tonight, for the first time in two years, the hum sounded different.

Thinner. Searching. Like a great blind thing turning its head, tasting the air.

Like something that had caught a scent it had been starving for.

Corin opened his eyes in the dark, heart hammering, and found Astrid already sitting up, listening too — though she couldn't possibly have heard it, not with human ears.

"It's humming," she whispered. "The whole sky. Why is the sky humming?"

Corin looked south, toward the bells, toward the city he had sworn never to see again, and understood that the choice he had been putting off had just been made for him.

"Pack up," he said. "We need to reach Vesperhold before it does."
