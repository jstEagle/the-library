## 9. City of Bells

The first thing Astrid noticed about Vesperhold was that it had no birds.

She had been noticing the absence for a full day before she understood what she was missing. There were crows in the lowlands, jackdaws on the ruined watchtowers, even a fat and insolent magpie who had once tried to steal Ashveil's breakfast feathers and lived to regret it. But as the road climbed toward the capital — toward the great bowl of the valley where the Choir had its seat — the sky grew emptier and emptier, until at last there was nothing in it at all but cloud and the pale, starless grey of noon.

"No birds," she said quietly, riding close beside Corin's mule.

"Bells," Corin said, as if that explained everything. To his credit, he seemed to realize it didn't, because after a moment he added: "The Choir doesn't like anything singing that they haven't taught to sing."

Ashveil, trussed beneath a canvas tarpaulin in the cart behind them as an injured hunting-bird — which was not entirely a lie, since he was injured, and he was, taxonomically speaking, half bird — made a sound from under the canvas that Astrid chose to interpret as agreement rather than indigestion.

*They will hear me before they see me,* he had warned her, back when they'd planned this in the shelter of a shepherd's bothy three days ago. *A griffin's heart is loud. Even wounded, even wrapped in their stinking cloth, I will smell like the wild to anyone with ears for it.*

*Then you'll be a very quiet wild,* she'd told him, and he'd grumbled, and she'd pretended she wasn't terrified.

Now the road bent around a shoulder of hill, and Vesperhold opened up below them all at once, and Astrid forgot to be terrified because she was too busy being astonished.

The city sat in a cup of green hills like an egg in a nest — white walls, slate roofs, terraced gardens climbing the slopes in neat green steps. And rising from its heart, a cluster of towers, slender and pale as candles, each one crowned with a bell. Dozens of towers. Hundreds of bells. Even from this distance she could see them swinging gently in the wind, though no wind reached them down in the streets, and no sound came up the hill either. The bells moved in silence, like fish swimming behind glass.

"They don't ring?" she asked.

"They always ring," Corin said. "You just can't hear them yet. You'll hear them soon enough." He said it lightly, but his knuckles were white on the mule's reins, and Astrid had learned enough about Corin Hale in the past weeks to know that lightness was his armor, worn thinnest exactly where the blows landed hardest.

He was going home. She could see it in the way his eyes moved over the city — cataloguing, remembering, flinching. A man returning to the house where something terrible had happened to him, except the house was the size of a city and the terrible thing was still living in it.

"You could wait outside the walls," she offered. "Ashveil and I could—"

"And get arrested by the second gate guard, yes, I know. You have the subtlety of a landslide, Astrid Iversen." He glanced at her sidelong, and some of the tension cracked into a crooked half-smile. "No. They know my face here, which is a problem, but they also know my gait, my posture, the way I hold my hands. If I skulk at the edges looking like a man skulking at the edges, I might as well hang a sign around my neck. Inside, among crowds, doing honest work with my head down — that I can manage. We go in together or not at all."

*Or we go in together and are all arrested together,* Ashveil observed from under the tarpaulin, *which would at least save the Choir the trouble of a search.*

"Thank you, Ashveil," Astrid murmured, "for your unflagging optimism."

"I am told it is one of my finest qualities," said the griffin, "along with modesty."

Corin watched her lips move as she spoke to empty air, and shook his head, but there was no fear in it anymore. It had taken weeks, but he had stopped looking at her gift like a wound and started looking at it like weather — something that simply was, sometimes foul, occasionally magnificent, best endured with good boots and dry humor.

"Remember," he said, as the walls rose up before them, "you're Marta. Marta is from the eastern farms, she lost her voice to fever when she was small, she is shy, hardworking, and completely unremarkable. Can you be unremarkable?"

Astrid thought about it seriously. "I've never tried."

"Wonderful. Now is the time."

* * *

The gate into Vesperhold was not guarded so much as *listened to*.

That was how it felt, anyway. Two guards stood at the archway, but between them hung a bell no bigger than a fist, silent as all its sisters, and every traveler passing beneath it paused — a heartbeat, two — as if waiting for something. Most passed without incident. One ragged peddler was stopped, questioned, waved through. A young woman with a lute strapped to her back was turned away entirely, her protests swallowed by the crowd.

"What's the bell do?" Astrid whispered.

"Hears intent," Corin said out of the corner of his mouth. "Old magic, pre-Choir, repurposed. It listens for the Song's absence. Everyone in Vesperhold carries the Song inside them — hummed so long, so deep, they don't even know they're doing it. The bell hears the gap where the Song should be." His jaw tightened. "You won't have it. Neither will Ashveil. So keep your mouth closed, your eyes down, and walk like you're tired and bored and thinking about supper."

They joined the queue. Astrid fixed her gaze on the middle of Corin's back and practiced being Marta: shoulders rounded, hands rough-looking, mouth soft and slightly open the way people's mouths were when they had nothing to say and knew it. Under the cart, Ashveil's heart hammered against the boards, and Astrid sent what calm she could down the invisible thread between them — *quiet, quiet, sleepy bird, nothing here but a tired servant girl and her master's cargo* — and felt him slow his pulse to match hers, grudgingly, the way he did everything.

Then it was their turn, and the little bell swung once above them, tasting the air.

Nothing happened.

Except — Astrid felt it. A brush against the inside of her skull, delicate as a moth landing. Something listening. Not hostile, exactly. Curious. Hungry, in a distant, patient way, the way a spider is hungry without hurrying.

*Marta,* she thought at it, stubbornly. *Tired. Bored. Supper.*

The moth-touch withdrew. The guard waved them through, already yawning.

Only when they were three streets deep did Astrid allow herself to breathe. "It touched me," she breathed. "Inside. Like a finger tapping on a window."

"It does that to everyone. It's how the Choir counts its sheep." Corin turned the cart down a narrower street, all shadow and laundry-lines. "You'll feel it more the longer you stay. Try not to tap back."

"I would never."

"You absolutely would. I've seen your face when someone tells you not to do something."

She couldn't argue with that, so she didn't.

* * *

Vesperhold, up close, was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful: perfectly composed and perfectly still.

The streets were clean. The houses were handsome. The people were well-fed, well-dressed, and moved through their days with a smoothness that Astrid slowly realized was the problem. Nobody hurried. Nobody dawdled. Nobody laughed too loudly or argued in doorways or stumbled home drunk singing. The whole city flowed like water finding its level, and everywhere, underneath everything, ran the hum.

She heard it properly for the first time in the market square. Not from any one throat — from all of them at once. A low, wordless melody threading through the clink of coins and the calls of vendors, so constant that the vendors' calls seemed laid over it like decoration on a cake. A woman haggling over apples hummed it. A child chasing a hoop hummed it. An old man asleep on a bench, mouth open in the sun, hummed it in his sleep — and that was the detail that crawled up Astrid's spine and settled there.

Even unconscious, they sang.

"It's not compulsion," Corin said quietly, watching her watch them. "Not mostly. That's the thing nobody outside understands. The Song feels *good*. It feels like belonging. Like being held. People come to Vesperhold from all over and within a month they're humming in their sleep and they'll tell you, honestly, that they've never been happier." He picked up an apple, weighed it, paid. "I believed that too, once. Maybe it was even true. The happiness isn't a lie. It's just... bought with something."

"With what?"

He didn't answer. He never answered that question directly; he only got a certain look, the look of a man standing at the edge of a hole too deep to see the bottom of, and changed the subject.

They found lodging above a chandler's shop in the tanners' quarter — chosen by Corin because, he said, nobody with a sensitive nose asked questions in the tanners' quarter, and the smell gave Ashveil's wild scent excellent cover. The chandler's widow, a broad comfortable woman named Grete, took one look at Corin's coin and one look at "Marta" and asked no questions at all beyond whether the girl could scrub a floor.

"She can't speak," Corin said smoothly. "Fever, when she was small. But she works hard and she's quick."

"Poor lamb," said Grete, and patted Astrid's cheek with a hand that smelled of tallow, and that was that. Astrid was employed.

That night, in the narrow room under the eaves, she unwrapped Ashveil and fed him dried fish while he complained about the smell, the ceiling, the quality of dried fish, and civilization generally.

"The worst part," he said, gnawing thoughtfully, "is that they are not unhappy. I could forgive screaming. Screaming means the animal knows it is in a cage. These people purr."

"I know."

"When we burn this place down—"

"*When we don't.*"

"—metaphorically," Ashveil said with dignity, "when we metaphorically burn it down, I want it noted that I suggested it first."

Astrid laughed despite herself, then pressed her forehead briefly against his feathered neck, feeling the slow furnace-warmth of him, the only familiar thing in a city of strangers. Outside the window, the bells swung in their silent circles, and somewhere below, Grete hummed herself to sleep.

* * *

Her work took her through the city, and the city taught her its lessons one by one.

Lesson the first: the bells were never silent. On her third morning, carrying tallow down the high street, Astrid finally *heard* them — a sound below sound, felt in the teeth and breastbone rather than the ear, a chord so vast and steady it stopped being music and became climate. The whole city rang with it, constantly, and the people moved inside the ringing like fish inside a current, and none of them appeared to notice.

Lesson the second: the animals were gone, but not entirely. In a weed-choked corner behind the chandler's shop she found a cat — thin, grey, wild-eyed — and reached out to it the way she'd reached out to every creature since childhood, without thinking, the way other people breathed.

The cat looked at her.

And Astrid felt, with a shock like cold water, that the cat had *nothing to say*. Not silence the way Sebastian was silent, a comfortable animal silence full of meaning. This was emptiness. The cat's mind was a room with the furniture removed. It stared at her hand, flinched from nothing, and fled, and Astrid stood in the weeds with her heart pounding, understanding for the first time what the Choir had actually done to the world. They hadn't killed the beasts' voices. They had starved them. A voice needed listening to grow; a whole city of people who could not hear had raised generations of animals born mute and dying mute, and called it peace.

She went back inside and scrubbed floors until her arms ached and she could think about something else.

Lesson the third: everyone watched everyone. Not maliciously — worse, *kindly*. Neighbors smiled at neighbors. Strangers helped strangers. And every smile carried a small question in it: *are you singing? are you with us? are you well?* When Astrid dropped a basket in the square, four people rushed to help her, laughing warmly, humming, and she thanked them with gestures and a shy smile and felt their eyes follow her all the way across the square, gentle and total, like sunlight through a lens.

"Marta is doing well," Corin observed that evening, watching her mime exhaustion with theatrical commitment. "Very convincing."

"I'm not acting," Astrid muttered. "I'm exhausted. Your city is terrifying in a way I don't have words for, which is fortunate, since I'm not allowed any."

He almost smiled. Then his face did the thing it did whenever he looked at the towers — the flinch, quickly buried. "Wait until you see the Maestro's tower," he said. "Then you'll have plenty you can't say."

* * *

She saw it on her fifth day, and she never managed to stop seeing it afterward.

Grete sent her with a delivery to the Bellwrights' Row, the street of workshops at the base of the central spires, and Astrid was hauling her basket past the row's end when the crowd around her simply stopped. Not scattered, not panicked — stopped, the way a congregation stops, and turned, all together, toward the tallest tower, and hummed a little louder.

Astrid turned too.

A procession was descending the tower stair: a dozen cantors in dove-grey robes, and among them, at the center, a woman in white.

Maestro Solenne.

Astrid had imagined her a hundred times in the weeks since Corin first spoke her name, and every imagining had been wrong. She had pictured a tyrant — vast, armored, thunderous. What descended the stairs was a small woman, silver-haired, fine-boned, with the kind of face that made you want to confess things to her. She walked slowly, leaning on no cane, and as she passed, people reached out — not to touch her, never to touch her, but toward her, fingers curling in the air like plants toward sun — and their humming swelled, sweetened, harmonized, and Solenne smiled at each of them with such warmth that Astrid felt the warmth herself, three rows back, like standing near an open oven.

And underneath the warmth, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, Astrid heard — *felt* — the other thing. The hunger. It poured off the woman like heat off a forge, vast and patient and terribly controlled, and it was aimed at the crowd the way a farmer's attention is aimed at wheat. Not hatred. Not cruelty. *Appetite.* Solenne loved these people the way a singer loves a held note: completely, and with the absolute intention of using every last bit of them.

Beside her, a baker's apprentice sighed, "Isn't she wonderful," and meant it with his whole heart.

Astrid kept her eyes down. Kept her mouth soft and empty. Kept being Marta, tired Marta, boring Marta, while the most dangerous person in the world passed within ten feet of her, and the little listening bell above the gate a mile away suddenly seemed like a kitten compared to whatever Solenne carried behind those warm grey eyes.

Then it happened.

Solenne paused. Mid-stride, mid-smile, she stopped — and turned her head, unhurried, graceful, and looked directly into the crowd. Not at the crowd. Into it. Her gaze drifted across faces the way a lighthouse beam drifts across water, and Astrid felt it coming and did the only thing she could think of: she thought of floors. Scrubbing floors. Lye and blisters and Grete's tallow smell and the ache in her lower back, wrapping herself in the dullest thoughts she owned like pulling a blanket over a candle.

The gaze passed over her.

Paused.

Came back.

For three heartbeats — Astrid counted them, they were the longest three heartbeats of her life — the Maestro of the Choir looked at a mute servant girl holding a basket of tallow candles, and something moved behind those warm grey eyes, some vast slow attention turning over like a leviathan in deep water, considering.

Then a cantor said something to her, and Solenne smiled, and moved on, and the crowd exhaled and resumed, and Astrid stood in the flow of bodies with her basket digging into her fingers and sweat running cold between her shoulder blades.

*She heard something,* Ashveil said that night, and it was not a question. He had smelled it on her when she came in — fear has a scent, he claimed, like copper and vinegar.

"Maybe. Maybe nothing. Maybe I imagined it."

"You did not imagine it. You are many things, little speaker, but imaginative about danger is not one of them. You have the imagination of a turnip when it matters, which is why you are still alive." He shifted his wings, wincing; the wound was healing, but slowly, starved of the wild air. "The question is what she heard. A wrong note. A gap. Or—"

"Or she recognized what a gap means," Astrid finished quietly. "Corin said the bell at the gate listens for the absence of the Song. What if she does too? What if she spends her whole life listening for gaps, and I walked past her smelling like the biggest gap in the city?"

Under the tarpaulin, the griffin was silent for a long moment.

"Then," he said at last, "we are on a clock. And you should tell the defector that his plan to poke around in archives beneath the Maestro's own tower had better happen quickly, or not at all."

Which was, of course, precisely what Corin proposed the next morning — standing in the chandler's back room with a stolen set of keys spread on the table before him, wearing the expression of a man about to suggest something so foolish that only its necessity could excuse it.

"There's a way down," he said. "Beneath the central tower. And Astrid — there's something down there I need you to see."
