## 10. The Archive Beneath

There were three ways into the Archive Beneath, and Corin Hale had spent eleven years making sure he could name all of them.

The first was the Grand Stair, reserved for the Maestro and her Circle — a sweep of white marble that descended beneath the Cathedral of Bells like a tongue sliding down a throat. The second was the Cantors' Door, warded with a hymn only full members of the Choir were permitted to know, a melody so beautiful it made your teeth ache if you sang it wrong.

The third was the coal chute behind the tannery on Sable Lane, which smelled exactly as one would expect a coal chute behind a tannery to smell, and which Corin had personally widened by two inches during his third year in the Choir, using a stolen chisel and an amount of stubbornness that would have gotten him commended if it hadn't been for treason.

"We're going *down there*," Astrid said, looking at the chute the way most people looked at open graves.

"Down there is where the truth lives," Corin said. "Truth has never once been found anywhere pleasant. It's a rule."

Ashveil, folded into the shadow of the tannery wall with her injured wing pressed tight, made a sound low in her chest that Corin had learned to translate. It meant: *I do not like this plan, but I have decided to allow it, mostly because I cannot fit through the chute myself.*

"She'll wait here," Corin said. "Griffins don't do well underground. Neither do I, honestly, but I've had practice."

Astrid pulled her hood lower. In Vesperhold she wore the gray of a mute servant, and she had not spoken above a whisper in three days — not because she couldn't, but because the city listened. That was the thing about Vesperhold that no visitor ever quite believed until they felt it: the whole city hummed. The Song ran through the bells and the gutters and the throats of everyone who lived there, so constant and so soft that citizens stopped hearing it the way fish stop noticing water. And when someone walked through those streets carrying a voice that did *not* hum — a wild voice, an unbound voice — the Song noticed. The way a sleeping dog notices a mouse.

Corin felt it too, now. He'd felt it since the valley, a faint dissonance walking beside him, like a note held against its neighbor. Eleven years ago he would have called it corruption. Now he thought it might be the truest sound he'd ever heard.

"After you," he said, and gestured at the chute with the gallantry of a man offering a ballroom door.

"You first," said Astrid. "If there's a trap, I want to watch it eat *you*."

"That," Corin said, "is the healthiest attitude toward me anyone's shown in years."

* * *

The chute dropped them into the old coal cellar, and the coal cellar opened onto corridors that predated the Choir itself.

That was the secret nobody in Vesperhold remembered: the city had not been built by mages. The mages had come later, after the war, after the Song, and they had built their white cathedral on top of bones much older than their music. The Archive Beneath lived in those bones — vaults cut from living rock before anyone thought to write down why, lit now by lamps that burned without flame, fed on something Corin had never been brave enough to ask about.

He led Astrid past shelves of bound canticles, past jars of preserved throat-tissue that he steered her quickly away from ("What was—", "Don't ask," "Was that a—" "*Don't ask*"), down a stair worn concave by centuries of feet, until the air changed. Colder. Quieter. Even the hum of the Song thinned here, as though the rock itself were holding its breath.

"The restricted vaults," he murmured. "Cantors aren't allowed past this point. Only the Circle. Which is why," he added, producing a ring of keys from inside his coat, "I spent four years volunteering to organize the Circle's correspondence. Nobody suspects the man who fetches the ink."

"You stole the keys off a desk?"

"I *earned* the keys off a desk. There's a difference, morally. Ask any thief."

The last lock was old iron, older than the rest, and it turned with a sound like a sigh. Beyond it lay a round chamber, small, unremarkable — except for the walls.

The walls were covered in names.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Cut into the stone in columns that climbed from floor to ceiling and vanished into shadow, each name in a different hand, some careful, some hurried, some scratched by fingers that must have been shaking. Corin watched Astrid step inside and stop breathing.

"What is this place?" she whispered.

"This," Corin said, "is what I faked my death for."

* * *

He had found the chamber in his ninth year, on a night he still could not describe without his hands going cold. He'd been sent below to retrieve a genealogy — the Choir loved genealogies, loved tracing speaker-bloodlines the way farmers traced breeding stock — and he'd taken a wrong turn in the dark, and the wrong turn had ended at a door that should not have been unlocked.

Inside, he'd found a ledger. One ledger among hundreds, but this one different: not a record of consumption, as the operational archives kept, all numbers and yields and dates. This was personal. This was a cantor's private confession, written two hundred years dead, and it began with a single line that Corin had never managed to forget:

*We told ourselves we were silencing weapons. We were silencing people.*

"They call it the Ledger of Voices," he said now, lifting the book from its niche and setting it in Astrid's hands. "There are others like it. Confessions. Records the Circle keeps locked away because they tell the truth about where the stars went."

Astrid didn't answer. She was reading. Her lips moved slightly, the way they did when she read aloud to animals who couldn't read — a habit she probably didn't know she had, and which Corin had privately decided was the most endearing thing he'd witnessed in eleven years of treachery.

"It's a list," she said finally. Her voice came out strange. Flat. "It's just... names. Dates. Places." She turned a page. "'Maren Ostwick, beast-speaker, taken winter of the Long Frost. Her gift yielded a star of the second magnitude.'" She looked up. "Second magnitude. They measured them. Like — like grain harvests."

"The Song needed feeding," Corin said. "Every gift they took became a note, and every note became light, and they hung the light in the sky and called it order." He gestured at the walls of names. "These are the ones somebody bothered to mourn. The ledgers in the operational archive are worse. Columns of figures. 'Yield per speaker.' Some of the Circle can recite them."

Astrid set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down something when the alternative is throwing it.

"The stars," she said. "All of them? Every star in the sky is a — a person?"

"A speaker. Their gifts, anyway. The bodies stayed behind." He heard how it sounded and winced. "I'm sorry. There's no kind way to say it. The Choir spent three hundred years eating magic and hanging the leftovers in the sky, and then they lost the recipe, and now the Song is starving, and that's why they're hunting you. You're not the last wild speaker because you're special, Astrid. You're the last wild speaker because they ate everyone else."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, a bell rang the hour, and even that sound arrived already bent into the shape of the Song.

Astrid walked slowly along the wall of names, trailing her fingers an inch from the stone without touching it. Corin recognized the gesture. It was how she touched nervous animals — close enough to offer warmth, far enough to give them room to bolt.

"There are children's names here," she said.

"Yes."

"Some of these are marked with little symbols. Stars, next to the names."

"The brightest ones." He hated himself for knowing that. "The Circle marks the yield."

She stopped walking. When she turned around, her face in the lampless dark was pale and absolutely steady, and Corin thought, with a lurch, *oh no*, because he had seen that steadiness before, in the eyes of beasts backed into corners, and it never meant surrender. It meant arithmetic.

"Then my gift," she said. "If the Choir takes me. My voice becomes a star."

"One star. Small. The Song is so hungry now it would barely notice you." He said it gently, and immediately wished he hadn't, because her chin came up.

"And if I'm not taken. If I get to choose." She turned back to the wall. "You said the stars are imprisoned voices. Imprisoned things can be freed."

Corin had known this conversation was coming. He had rehearsed it, actually, on the road, composing careful sentences about uncertainty and theory and the limits of what the old records claimed. But standing in the chamber, watching her look up at ten thousand names the way other people looked up at constellations — which, he supposed, was exactly what she was doing — every rehearsed sentence died in his throat.

"The old texts disagree," he said instead. Honestly. "Some say the voices dissolved when they were consumed. That the stars are just... residue. Light without anyone home. Others—" He stopped.

"Others say what?"

"Others say the voices are still singing." His own voice had gone quiet. "That the stars shine because the consumed speakers are still trying to be heard. That the Song didn't silence them. It just made them sing *together*. Forever. Whether they wanted to or not."

Astrid stood very still. Then, softly, in the speech-voice she used for creatures rather than people — low, unhurried, leaving space for answers — she said, to the chamber, to the walls, to the names:

"Is anyone there?"

Nothing. Of course nothing. Rock and dust and two hundred years of locked doors.

But Corin had spent eleven years learning to hear things other cantors couldn't — guilt does that, it sharpens the ears — and in the silence after her question, he would have sworn, he would have staked his remaining hand on it, that the faint hum of the Song threading down through the stone *wavered*. Just slightly. The way a choir wavers when one singer, deep in the ranks, forgets the words and reaches for help.

Astrid heard it too. He saw it hit her — saw her hand press flat against the wall at last, palm to stone, the way she'd laid her hand on Ashveil's wound in the barn.

"They're in there," she breathed. "They're all in there. They've been in there the whole time, singing, and nobody—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "Three hundred years. Nobody asked them a single question."

"No," Corin said. "They asked them for *yield*."

* * *

They sat on the floor after that, because the floor seemed appropriate. Some discoveries demand chairs; others demand that you sit down in the dust among the names and stay there a while.

Astrid had the ledger open across her knees. "If the voices are still alive in the stars," she said slowly, working it through the way she worked through everything, out loud, patiently, "then my gift isn't just — hearing animals. Talking to foxes. It's the same thing, isn't it? The same root. Speakers don't talk *at* things. We listen, and things listen back. The stars are listeners who got forced to perform instead." She looked up. "Could I reach them?"

"I don't know."

"Could I free them?"

"I don't know," Corin said again, and then, because she deserved better than the honest coward's answer twice in a row: "Maybe. The binding that holds them is the same binding that holds the Song together. Pull one thread and — I genuinely don't know whether the whole weave comes apart, or whether it just tightens around whatever's pulling. The texts argue about it. The texts argue about everything. Scholars are the same in every century."

"So it could free them." Astrid closed the ledger. "Or it could bind them tighter. Me, included."

"Yes."

"And the Choir wants me either way. If they take me, I'm a star. If I try to free them and fail, I'm a tighter knot. If I run and hide—" she laughed, short and without humor, "—the sky stays dark, the land keeps dying, and I live long enough to watch it."

"That's roughly the shape of it, yes."

"You're very comforting, Corin Hale."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them are anxiety."

To his enormous relief, she smiled — small, tired, real. Then she looked back at the wall of names, and the smile faded into something else, something he recognized from mirrors: the expression of a person realizing that the weight they've been carrying is about to get heavier, and deciding, quietly, to pick it up anyway.

"When I was little," she said, "I used to think my gift was a burden. All I wanted was to put it down. To be normal. To belong somewhere without hiding half of myself in a barn." Her fingers traced one name near the bottom of a column — a woman's name, Maren Ostwick, taken in the Long Frost. "And now I'm standing in a hole under the people who would have burned me, reading a list of everyone like me who ever lived, and every one of them would have given anything to have what I have. To have a *choice*." She swallowed. "They didn't get a choice. They got yield."

"Astrid—"

"Don't." But gently. "I'm not angry at you. I'm angry at three hundred years, and you're just the nearest person who speaks its language." She rose, brushing dust from her gray servant's dress, and squared her shoulders the way she did before entering a room full of strangers. "We need to see the original binding. Whatever holds the stars. Not confessions — mechanics. Do the archives have it?"

Corin stared at her. "You understand that answering that question means going deeper. Past the vaults. Into the Circle's private stacks, where the wards don't just alarm, they *report*. Where a defector and a mute servant girl will be identified within minutes."

"Yes."

"And you want to go anyway."

"I want to know what I'm choosing," Astrid said. "Before it chooses me."

Somewhere above them, distant and terrible, the great bell of the Cathedral tolled a pattern Corin knew by heart and had hoped never to hear again: the summons for the Circle. An emergency convocation. At midnight.

Eleven years of organizing correspondence had taught him what midnight convocations meant. They meant the Song had felt something. A wavering. A question asked in a voice it did not recognize, a hundred feet below its own heart.

He got to his feet and pocketed the ledger.

"Astrid," he said, "we need to leave. Right now. Because I think we just knocked on the sky — and I think it knocked back."

Far overhead, faint as memory, the bells of Vesperhold began, one by one, to change their tune.
