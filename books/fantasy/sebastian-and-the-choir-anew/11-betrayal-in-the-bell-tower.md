## 11. Betrayal in the Bell Tower

The bells of Vesperhold never truly stopped.

Astrid had learned this over three days of posing as a mute scullery girl in the household of a Choir archivist, her hands raw from scrubbing pots that were already clean, her ears full of bronze. The bells rang the hour, the half-hour, the quarter. They rang for births, for deaths, for the changing of the watch, and — most unnervingly — they rang for no reason at all, long rolling peals that seemed less like announcements than like breathing. The city breathed in bronze. And beneath the bronze, always, ran the Song: that vast, wordless hum the citizens carried in their throats like second heartbeats, humming while they kneaded bread, while they swept steps, while they slept.

*It gets into your bones,* Corin had warned her on the road. *By the third day you'll catch yourself humming it. When you notice, stop. That's how it finds people.*

She had caught herself twice. Both times she'd bitten the inside of her cheek until she tasted copper, and both times Ashveil had given her a look from the shadows of the archive cellar — a griffin's look, which is to say an expression of profound feline judgment delivered through the medium of a golden eye the size of a soup bowl.

*I don't judge,* Ashveil had said, which was a lie, because griffins judged everything, constantly, as a matter of spiritual principle. *But if you begin to hum, I will sit on you.*

"You can't sit on me," Astrid had whispered back. "You weigh as much as a hay wagon and my ribs are still bruised."

*Then I will lie near you in a menacing fashion.*

That had been two nights ago, and she'd laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the stars went out — and then felt guilty about laughing, because laughing in Vesperhold felt like giggling during a funeral. But Ashveil's wing had curled around her shoulders afterward, warm as a hearthstone, and she'd slept better than she had in weeks.

Now it was the fourth night, and everything was about to go wrong, though of course she didn't know that yet. What she knew was this: Corin had come to the cellar at moonrise, dressed in the grey-and-gold vestments he'd sworn he'd never wear again, his face pale as tallow, and told her the plan had changed.

"The Maestro keeps a private archive in the Bell Tower," he said. "Everything we found below — the ledgers, the lists of speakers, the star-charts going back four hundred years — there's more. The original binding. The *first* Song, written out note by note. If we take it, we don't just have proof. We have the thing itself."

"And you know this how?" Astrid asked. "Three days ago you didn't know which corridor led to the kitchens."

"I knew someone. Before." He wouldn't meet her eyes. "A cantor named Lys. She kept Solenne's private papers. She owed me — or I owed her, depending on how you count, and I've stopped counting. She sent word tonight. One hour. The tower's east stair, during the midnight peal, when the bells are loudest and every guard in the building is deaf."

Ashveil, folded into the dark behind the coal bins, made a sound low in her chest — not quite a growl, not quite a purr. Astrid had learned her vocabulary by now. That one meant *I do not like this.*

"Griffin says no," Astrid translated.

"Griffins don't get a vote."

"Griffins have survived four hundred years of your Choir hunting them. I'd say they've earned a vote." She looked at Corin properly — at the sweat at his hairline, the way his fingers kept moving against his thigh like a man playing an invisible lute. He was frightened. That much was honest; the beasts had taught her what honest fear smelled like, and Corin reeked of it. But there was something else under it, something eager, and eagerness in a man who had spent years learning to lie with his whole body was not a thing she trusted.

"Why now?" she asked. "We have the proof from the lower archive. We could be over the walls by dawn."

"Because the Maestro moves the first archive in six days. Because every night we wait, another speaker dies somewhere out there feeding the Song." His voice cracked on the last words, and she believed the crack. "And because I need to see it, Astrid. The first page. I need to see what I sang into for eleven years. I need to *know*."

There it was. Not strategy. Guilt. Guilt wearing armor, charging at the thing that hurt it.

Astrid thought of her mother, kneeling at the cookfire with an armful of childhood drawings — foxes with speech-scrolls curling from their mouths, a bear wearing a crown of bees — feeding them to the flames one by one while her face stayed perfectly, terribly calm. *I always knew,* her mother had said. *Knowing was how I kept you alive.* People did reckless things to pay down old debts. She understood that better than she wanted to.

"One hour," she said. "If anything smells wrong — anything — we run. Ashveil carries me, you keep up or you don't."

*Or I carry him too,* Ashveil offered. *Briefly. Then drop him.*

"She says she'll carry you."

"She's lying."

"They're all liars," Astrid said, and was surprised at how much fondness got into it. "Welcome to the alliance."

* * *

The midnight peal began as they crossed the archive courtyard, and it was like walking into the inside of a drum.

Twelve great bells overhead, each tall as a chapel, swinging in staggered waves so the sound came not as ringing but as weather — pressure, weight, a tide of bronze that Astrid felt in her teeth, her sternum, the fluid of her eyes. Guards along the colonnade stood with wax in their ears and their eyes half-closed, swaying slightly, men made stupid by the very instruments meant to keep them sharp. Above them the Bell Tower climbed into darkness, its belfry open to the sky like a mouth mid-song.

And the sky above it was black. Not night-black — *empty*-black. Astrid still hadn't gotten used to it, three weeks on. In the valley, the stars had been furniture, wallpaper, the ceiling of her whole life, and she'd never once looked up and thought *thank you.* Now every cloudless night was a wound overhead, and she found herself doing what she'd mocked her grandmother for: counting the few dim survivors, five of them tonight, huddled in the south like sheep after a wolf has been through the flock.

Corin moved ahead of her through the courtyard, sure-footed, grey robes blending with stone. Twice he paused at corners and made small hand-signs she didn't know, listening. Once he pressed himself flat against a wall and pulled her after him as two cantors passed, humming their rounds, and Astrid held her breath and felt the Song press back against her eardrums like water against a plugged hole, patient, curious, wanting in.

*They're looking for something,* Ashveil murmured. She was somewhere above — rooftops, probably, moving in the blind spots between watch-fires, a shadow with opinions. *Not us specifically. A shape. A wrongness in the Song. Keep your gift folded down small, little speaker. Small as a mouse.*

Astrid did what she'd practiced: gathered the bright animal-sense inside her and wrapped it tight, the way you'd wrap a mirror in wool. It hurt, holding it in. It had always hurt. All those years in the valley, swallowing every word the foxes and crows and half-wild goats said to her, she'd thought the ache was shame. Now she suspected it was simply pressure — a voice built for open country living its whole life indoors.

The east stair of the Bell Tower was a spiral of worn granite, lit every ninth step by a lamp burning oil that smelled of incense and something else, something faintly sweet and wrong, like flowers arranged over a grave. Corin took the stairs two at a time. Astrid followed, counting turns. Four. Seven. Nine.

At the eleventh turn, Ashveil's voice arrived in her head, and it was flat as slate.

*Little speaker. Stop climbing.*

"What?" she whispered.

*There are cantors in the belfry. Six. No — eight. They are not hiding. They are waiting, the way spiders wait, and the web is already spun.* A pause, and when the griffin spoke again there was something in her voice Astrid had never heard before: fear. *And the Maestro is here. I can smell her. She smells like the space where a forest used to be.*

Astrid grabbed Corin's sleeve. He turned, and in the lamplight she watched his face do something complicated — watched hope and horror arrive at the same door together, jostling.

"That's impossible," he breathed. "Lys swore—"

"Lys," said a voice from above, gentle and clear as a struck glass, "swore nothing. Lys doesn't exist. Lys is a name I invented eleven years ago to test whether you would run to her the moment you grew a conscience. Congratulations, Cantor Hale. You took rather longer than I estimated, but you did exactly as predicted."

Footsteps on the stair above. A woman descended into the lamplight slowly, unhurried, the way someone walks through their own home. She was small — smaller than Astrid — silver-haired, neat as a pin, wearing dove-grey robes with a single gold thread at the collar. Her eyes were the color of the sky these days: dark, and missing things.

Maestro Solenne.

Behind her, filling the stairwell like water filling a cup, came eight cantors in grey and gold, and every one of them was humming, and the hum was not the city's comfortable round. It was aimed. It pressed down the stairwell like a hand.

"Run," Corin said — to her, only to her, shoving her back toward the way they'd come. "Astrid, *run*, she wants me, that's all this ever was, she let me go because a defector leads you home to whatever he loves—"

"Oh, he does love you, by the way," Solenne said pleasantly, descending another step. "He hasn't said it. Cantors are terrible at saying things. We say everything in unison or nothing at all." She tilted her head at Astrid with genuine, awful curiosity. "So you're the valley speaker. The fox-girl. Do you know, I've read every report on you since you were nine years old? Your village reported a child who calmed a stallion that had kicked two grown men senseless. They called it gentleness. I called it what it was." She smiled, and it was almost kind. "I've been waiting for you a very long time."

The world went narrow and loud. Astrid heard her own heartbeat, heard the bells overhead still rolling out their midnight weather, heard — beneath everything, everywhere, in the stones themselves — the Song, vast and hungry, turning its attention toward her like a head turning in a crowd.

*Folded small,* Ashveil reminded her, from somewhere far above. *Stay folded small. And little speaker — the window on the tenth landing. Behind the lamp. It opens onto the buttress. I am on the buttress.*

"Ashveil—" Astrid started aloud, and Solenne's eyes sharpened.

"There it is," the Maestro said softly. "Say it again. Speak to your beast where I can hear the shape of it. Do you know what a gift sounds like, from the outside? Like light through a keyhole. Like—" and her voice changed, deepened, became suddenly enormous, suddenly the size of the stairwell, suddenly the size of the *Song* — "*like dinner.*"

The hum surged.

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do, each piece sensible in isolation and monstrous in sum. Corin threw himself up the stairs at Solenne — not to fight her, he couldn't fight her, he had to know he couldn't fight her, but to stand between, the way you throw yourself between a child and a falling beam knowing the beam wins. Two cantors caught him. He fought anyway, badly, bravely, shouting words Astrid barely heard over the bells: *"Go! The window! GO!"*

Astrid ran. Up, not down — the window was above, Ashveil was above, and some animal part of her brain that had been making survival decisions since the night the wards broke simply took her legs over. She took the stairs three at a time. Behind her, Corin's voice cracked on something that might have been her name, and she hated herself for not turning around, and she turned around anyway, one half-second, one look —

— and saw him on his knees between two cantors, blood at his hairline, and saw that he was *smiling*. Smiling at her. Go, the smile said. You're free. This was the plan all along, the oldest plan, the one where the liar finally tells the truth with his whole stupid body.

Then a cantor's hand closed over his mouth, and the Song poured into the silence where his voice had been, and he was gone behind grey bodies, and Astrid ran.

Tenth landing. Lamp. Window — painted shut, of course, because everything in Vesperhold was shut and humming and lying. She hit it with her shoulder. Once. Twice. On the third blow the frame cracked and cold night air poured in and there was the buttress, a spine of wet stone arcing away into darkness, and standing on it with her wings half-spread and her tail lashing like a metronome set to fury, Ashveil.

*Jump,* said the griffin.

"It's a hundred-foot drop—"

*On the far side of the jump, yes. Between here and there is six feet of air and my back. Jump, or stay and become dinner, and I remind you that I called this. I called this on the FIRST NIGHT—*

Astrid jumped.

For one endless moment she was in the air, in the dark, over the city of bells, and the bells chose that exact instant to begin the quarter-hour peal, twelve towers' worth of bronze detonating around her, and then Ashveil's back hit her like a feathered floor and they were falling together, diving off the buttress into the canyon between rooftops, wind screaming, the city wheeling up past them — bell towers, watch-fires, the black socket of sky — and Astrid pressed her face into feathers that smelled of smoke and storm and held on.

* * *

They flew until the bells were a rumor and the rumor faded, until Vesperhold was a smudge of orange light on the southern horizon like the last coal of a burned-down house. Then Ashveil found a crag in the hills — bare rock, wind-scoured, nothing living on it but lichen and one furious griffin — and landed hard enough to knock the breath out of Astrid, and stood there shaking.

Griffins, Astrid had learned, did not cry. What Ashveil did instead was worse to watch: she preened. Frantically, mechanically, dragging her beak through her own chest feathers over and over, smoothing what needed no smoothing, the way Astrid's mother had scrubbed a spotless pot the morning after the wards fell.

"He knew," Astrid said. Her voice came out scraped. "He knew it was a trap and he walked into it anyway, because he thought — he thought trading himself was the only way to make sure I got out clean. That idiot. That *lying, singing idiot*—"

She discovered she was on her knees. She discovered her fists were full of gravel. She discovered she was angry in a way she had never been angry in her life — not hot anger, not the flash of temper, but a cold, structural rage, load-bearing, the kind you could build a house on. At Solenne, who had played a lonely, guilt-ridden man like an instrument for eleven years. At Lys, who had never existed. At the bells, at the Song, at the empty sky, at the whole grey machine of a city that hummed while it ate people.

And underneath the rage, quiet as a mouse under floorboards, sat the other thing. The thing she'd have to look at eventually.

*You left him,* said the thing.

*I had to leave him. There were eight of them. Staying would have just been two prisoners instead of one.*

*He smiled at you.*

*That doesn't make it right.*

*No,* the thing agreed. *But it makes it heavier.*

Ashveil stopped preening. She came over the rocks sideways, low, the way she approached wounded animals, and folded herself down beside Astrid, and put one wing around her, careful of the bruises.

*We will get him back,* the griffin said.

"How? She has him. She has the tower, the city, the Song, eight cantors, and four hundred years of practice eating people like us. I have—" Astrid gestured at the barren crag, the wind, the five surviving stars huddled in the south. "This."

*You have more than that.* Ashveil's golden eye reflected the starless dark, and in it Astrid saw something move — not reflection. Memory. *You have every beast between here and the mountains, and they are done being afraid. You have a defector who bought your escape with his voice, which means the Choir thinks they've won, which means they are careless for exactly as long as it takes them to discover what he's really done. And you have—* the griffin hesitated, and for the first time since Astrid had known her, Ashveil sounded young. *You have me. And I have not told you everything about why the Choir hunts griffins, little speaker. About what we were before the Song. Where we come from.*

The wind moved over the crag. Far to the north, past the hills, past the dead orchards and the silent farms, something answered the wind — a sound Astrid felt in her folded-down, wrapped-in-wool gift before she heard it with her ears. Low. Many-throated. Patient.

Beasts. Hundreds of them. Moving toward her through the dark like a tide coming home.

"They followed us," she whispered.

*No,* said Ashveil, rising, spreading wings that blotted out the stars-that-weren't. *They followed the Song. It wounded everything wild, and everything wild remembers who bled. They've come to find the one creature in the world whose voice doesn't hurt them.* The griffin looked down at her, and there was iron under the gold. *Get up, Astrid Iversen. Grieve on the move. We have a war to build.*
