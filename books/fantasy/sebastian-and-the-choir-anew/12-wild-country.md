## 12. Wild Country

For three days after leaving Corin bleeding in the bell tower's shadow, Astrid did not speak to anyone.

This was not difficult, since there was no one to speak to. There was Ashveil, but the griffin had gone quiet in the way of creatures carrying wounds deeper than flesh, flying above her in long, silent arcs and landing only when she stopped, as if he were afraid that if she walked out of his sight she might simply cease to exist. And there were the wild things of the starless country — but they did not want conversation. They wanted blood, mostly hers.

She learned this on the first night, when a pack of feral dogs came out of a dead orchard with their lips peeled back and their eyes reflecting nothing at all. Under a proper sky, dog eyes caught starlight like coins at the bottom of a well. Under this empty black, they were just holes.

"Easy," she said, out of habit, and then laughed — a cracked, ugly sound — because easy was a word for another world. Easy belonged to a girl who had hidden in a valley her whole life, who had apologized to a lame fox for existing wrong.

The dogs did not go easy. She ran, and Ashveil dropped out of the dark like a falling anvil with opinions, and the dogs scattered, and Astrid sat down in the frost-killed grass and put her face in her hands and let herself shake for exactly as long as it took to remember that shaking was a luxury.

*You left him,* said the part of her mind that sounded unpleasantly like her mother.

*I left him or we both died,* said the part that sounded like her, only harder.

*Those are not different things, little one.*

"Shut up," she told her own head, and got up, and kept walking north, because north was away, and away was the only direction left.

* * *

The country beyond the pass had been dying slowly, she'd seen that much on her flight from the Choir's hunters — brown fields, silent woods, villages where people watched the sky with the particular desperation of the drowning watching for a rope. But she hadn't understood until now how strange the beasts had become.

They were everywhere, and they were wrong.

Deer stood in open meadows at midday, motionless, facing the empty sky as though waiting for something to be switched back on. A bear crossed her path without seeing her, its gait lurching, its head swinging in slow, unhappy arcs. Birds — the few that remained — flew in tight, frantic circles, as if the ground had become unbearable and the sky offered no answer either. Everything was restless. Everything was grieving in a language just past the edge of her hearing.

And everything, when it noticed her, went still.

It happened first with the deer. She came around a granite shoulder of hill and there were six of them, heads raised, and instead of bolting they turned toward her — all six, in eerie unison — and stared. Not at her face. At her chest. At whatever it was inside her that had always been able to hear the fox say *cold today, stay, den*, and had spent nineteen years pretending it was nothing.

"I'm not going to hurt you," she said, and then, absurdly, "sorry," because apologizing was the oldest habit she owned.

The lead doe took one step closer. Then another. Then it lowered its head and breathed her breath, whiskers brushing her knuckles, and made a sound Astrid had never heard a deer make — low, questioning, almost conversational.

And Astrid heard it. Not words, exactly. Something older than words: *you are loud. You are very loud. Why are you so sad?*

"I'm not sad," she lied, to a deer, in front of God and everyone.

The doe looked at her with the flat patience of all herbivores everywhere, and Astrid felt something crack loose in her chest — not painfully, more like ice going out of a river. She sat down in the meadow. The deer settled around her like gray stones. And for the first time since the bell tower, since Corin's face going away behind her, since the sound of her mother feeding her childhood into the fire — for the first time, she slept without dreaming of running.

* * *

By the fifth day she had stopped apologizing to animals, which was, she reflected, probably progress.

By the seventh, she had noticed the other thing.

Her gift was growing.

There was no other word for it. In the valley, speaking to Sebastian had been like listening through a wall — muffled, effortful, a trickle. Out here, under the murdered sky, it was a flood. She could feel every creature within a mile as faint warm presences, the way you feel sunlight through a window without looking for it. When she reached toward them, they reached back. The feral dogs from the orchard — she found them again on the sixth day, and instead of running, she opened herself the way she'd once opened herself to Ashveil, and the pack circled her twice and then lay down around her campfire like it was theirs, which, she supposed, it now was.

"It doesn't make sense," she told Ashveil, who was picking at a haunch of something unfortunate with surgical disdain. "The stars go out, the world gets worse, and I get *stronger*. That's backwards."

Ashveil swallowed, considered, and spoke in that voice like wind through a canyon that only she could hear. *"Is it? A lamp burns brighter in a dark room."*

"That's not comforting. That's ominous."

*"Most true things are both."*

"You're insufferable."

*"I am a griffin. Insufferability is load-bearing."*

But he wasn't laughing, really, and neither was she, because underneath the joke they both knew what he'd actually said. The sky was going dark. Her gift was getting brighter. Those two facts were holding hands, and Astrid did not want to look at what they were holding.

She thought about the archive beneath Vesperhold — Corin's voice, low and ashamed: *the stars aren't dead, Astrid. They're imprisoned. Every speaker the Choir ever consumed. They've been singing them into the sky for three hundred years.* She thought about the way the Song had hummed through the city's bones, through the citizens' sleep, through her own teeth. She thought about the Maestro's hands, white and precise on the bell ropes.

If the stolen voices were locked away, and the land was starving for them —

Then maybe the wild magic wasn't fading out here. Maybe it was *pooling*. Maybe every unclaimed drop of it, every beast-gift and hedge-charm and speaker's whisper the Choir hadn't managed to harvest, was sinking into the soil and the blood and the bone of the world, waiting. And maybe she was standing in the deepest water of it, a lightning rod in a country full of storm.

"Great," she muttered. "I'm a lightning rod. That's fine. Nothing bad ever happens to those."

The nearest dog thumped its tail without opening its eyes.

* * *

On the ninth day, the wolves came, and Astrid discovered what she had become.

She heard them before dawn — not with her ears, which were merely adequate, but with the new sense that had been unfurling in her like fern fronds: a cold, coordinated pressure moving down the valley, eleven minds moving as one mind, and that one mind was *wrong*. Star-mad. It had been too long since anything sang to wolves in the dark, and the silence had gotten into them and started rearranging the furniture.

They hit the camp at first light. The dogs scattered — even her dogs, her loyal orchard thugs, took one look at eleven winter-thin wolves and remembered urgent appointments elsewhere. Ashveil screamed and rose, but a wing is a poor shield against eleven angles of attack, and Astrid saw him bank wide, screaming, unable to come down without giving the wolves the opening they wanted.

Which left her. Alone. In her nightshirt. Holding a stick.

The lead wolf was enormous, gray gone nearly white at the muzzle, and its eyes were the worst thing she had ever seen — not cruel, just *empty*, the eyes of a creature whose inner voice had gone quiet and taken the whole world's voice with it. It flowed toward her, and the others fanned out with terrible patience, and some ancient, sensible corner of Astrid's brain began composing her obituary (*she was eaten by wolves; the stick did not help*).

And then she stopped being sensible.

She didn't decide to do it. It happened the way breathing happens. She reached out — not with words, not with the polite, pleading whisper she'd used on Sebastian all those years, *please don't, please be calm, I'm sorry, I'm sorry* — but with everything. With the grief of the bell tower. With the burn-smell of her drawings. With nineteen years of swallowing her own voice, and the raw, furious joy of the last nine days of finally, *finally* using it. She reached out and she did not ask.

She *told*.

***Stop.***

The word went through the clearing like a struck bell. Eleven wolves froze mid-stride, snow spraying, and the lead wolf's empty eyes filled — not with fear, but with something worse and better: recognition. Its ears came forward. Its head tilted. And into the silence between them it poured a sound like a broken hymn, and Astrid understood it, all of it, all at once:

*We forgot. We forgot the song. Where did it go? Who took it? We have been so hungry and we did not know for what.*

Astrid sank to her knees in the snow, shaking, and the lead wolf came to her — came right to her, pressed its broad scarred forehead against her sternum, right against the place where the loudness lived — and held there, breathing, while ten other wolves arranged themselves in a ring around them both like a congregation.

"I know," she whispered, into the fur, and her voice broke. "I know who took it. I'm going to get it back."

She had no idea if that was true. But saying it felt like setting a bone.

* * *

After that, the wild country stopped trying to kill her and started, in its battered, half-mad way, taking care of her.

The wolves hunted for her — or near her, which amounted to the same thing, since they were constitutionally incapable of sharing and simply left her the parts they found embarrassing. Crows brought her shining things: a bent buckle, a coin stamped with the Choir's triple-bell sigil (which she threw into a ravine with great satisfaction), a button that had clearly come off a Choir cantor's coat, which she also threw into the ravine, harder. A blind old badger adopted her camp and complained about everything with the tireless devotion of the elderly everywhere, and Astrid found herself talking to it for hours, telling it things she'd never told anyone — about Sebastian, about her mother's face in the firelight, about Corin Hale turning to look at her as the trap closed, and whether the look had meant *run* or *why*.

"He knew it was a trap," she said. "He had to have known. He walked into it anyway, and I let him."

The badger chewed a beetle and radiated the opinion that men were overrated but guilt was worse company than either.

"And the worst part—" Astrid pulled her knees up. "The worst part is I'd do it again. If it was Ashveil, or the dogs, or — I'd leave him again. That's who I am now. Someone who does arithmetic with people."

The badger, who had survived twenty years in a dying forest by doing precisely that arithmetic daily, was unmoved.

Ashveil, for his part, watched her with an expression she was coming to dread, the one that meant he was about to say something true.

*"You are changing,"* he said one evening, as she calmed a trembling elk-mare with a touch and a hum — a hum, an actual note, the first sound she'd made in days that wasn't speech or tears.

"I'm adapting."

*"You are becoming."* He mantled his wings, uncomfortable. *"In the old stories, when the sky went dark the first time — before the Choir, before the Song — there were speakers who grew great in the darkness. The beasts called them lanterns. The mages called them fuel."*

Astrid's hand stilled on the elk's neck. "Which am I?"

*"That,"* said Ashveil, *"is the question, isn't it."*

She looked at her hands. Ordinary hands, chapped and dirty, a healing scratch across one knuckle from a wolf who'd gotten enthusiastic. They didn't look like a lantern. They didn't look like fuel. They looked like a farm girl's hands, and that, she realized — sitting in a circle of wolves and crows and one profoundly judgmental badger, under a sky with no stars in it — that was the whole point.

She had spent her whole life apologizing for what she was. To the valley. To her mother. To a fox, for heaven's sake. *Sorry I can hear you. Sorry I'm strange. Sorry I exist wrong.* And the world had agreed with her apology, had exiled speakers and burned drawings and sung its great suffocating Song, all built on the same polite premise: that wildness was a mistake to be corrected.

Well. The world could take its correction and stuff it.

"I'm not sorry," she said aloud, testing the shape of it.

The wolves lifted their heads. The crows went quiet.

"I'm not sorry," she said again, louder, and something in the air around her *shifted*, the way air shifts before weather, and every beast in the clearing turned to face her, and far off across the dark hills she felt — actually felt, a mile away, two miles, more — other minds stir and turn toward her like flowers toward a sun that wasn't in the sky anymore.

It was in her, apparently. She was the sun now. Wonderful. No pressure.

* * *

It was the wolves who found the road, on the twelfth day, and the travelers on it, and the travelers who brought the news that changed everything.

Astrid felt them first — human minds, which she was learning to read the way you learn to read weather, roughly and with frequent errors. Fear, exhaustion, and underneath it a thin bright thread of hope, which was rare enough these days to be remarkable. She sent the lead wolf ahead to look, and the wolf came back with its hackles smooth, which meant: *people, but not hunters.*

They were pilgrims, four of them, trudging the old mountain road west with bundles and blisters. They flinched badly when a wolf the size of a pony stepped out of the trees, and flinched worse when a young woman followed it out — but hope won, the way it does with the desperate. They had news, and they gave it freely, the way people give news when the news has been eating them alive.

"The Hollow," gasped the eldest, a stooped woman with a pilgrim's shell at her throat. "You've heard? Everyone's heard. The beasts are all moving west — every flock, every herd, everything with fur or feathers, they're all walking the same direction, like — like iron filings to a lodestone. Toward the First Hollow. The old place, under the mountains. My grandmother said the stars were born there." She laughed, high and unhinged. "Born there! As if stars are *born* anywhere. But the beasts believe it. And the Choir—"

She stopped. Her companions exchanged glances.

"The Choir's marching too," said the youngest, a boy with a split lip. "Whole companies. Bells on wagons. They're going to the Hollow, mistress. Whatever's waking up out here—" he gestured at the treeline, at the wolves, at Astrid, at the whole restless breathing dark, "—they mean to be there when it does."

Astrid stood very still. Around her, the wolves had gone silent and attentive, ears pricked west. Above her, Ashveil circled once, low, and she felt his thought brush hers, grave and clear:

*The pool of buried light. The First Hollow. It's real, and everything in the world is converging on it — the starving, the stolen, and the thieves.*

The old pilgrim was squinting at her now, at the wolves sitting at her heels like house cats, at the crow on her shoulder, at the impossible arrangement of her entirely.

"Mistress," the woman said slowly, "what *are* you?"

Astrid opened her mouth to say *nobody*, because that was the old reflex, the valley reflex, the burned-drawings reflex.

She closed it again.

"I'm the last speaker," she said. "And I'm going to the Hollow."

Far to the west, under the starless sky, something vast and buried turned over in its sleep — and Astrid felt it call her name.
