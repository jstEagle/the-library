## 13. The Maestro's Offer

Solenne Vael had not slept in eleven years.

This was not a boast, though the younger cantors sometimes treated it as one, whispering it in the cloisters as if it were a miracle rather than an affliction. It was simply the truth of the Song. A Maestro did not sleep; she *held*. Every hour of every day and night, she held the great chord that kept the Choir's power flowing through the bells of Vesperhold, through the veins of every cantor who had ever bound their voice to it, through the wards that kept the wild things at bay and the hungry dark from pouring in through the seams of the world.

She held it the way a woman holds a drowning child above water: with both arms, forever, smiling so no one would think to ask how tired she was.

"Maestro." The under-cantor in the doorway was young, round-faced, terrified of her in the way of all new cantors, which she supposed was better than the alternative. "The prisoner is refusing his meal again."

"Is he?" She set down her pen. The ledger before her was open to the page she had been annotating for three days — the page where the numbers refused to behave. "Has he spoken?"

"He said—" The boy swallowed. "He said to tell you the porridge tastes like penance."

Solenne allowed herself a small smile. "Corin Hale always did have a gift for phrasing. Bring the tray to my study. I'll take it to him myself."

The boy's eyes went wide. "You'll— yourself?"

"I am old, Cantor Pellin, not made of glass. And I have been singing to him for six days without result. Perhaps it's time someone tried talking."

* * *

The cells beneath the Bell Tower were not cruel places, whatever the rumors said. Solenne had designed them herself, decades ago, when she was still young enough to believe cruelty was inefficient rather than wrong. Each cell was warm, dry, lit by soft lamps. What made them terrible was only what they lacked: silence. The Song ran through the stone like blood through flesh, and a prisoner could not close his ears against it any more than he could close his heart.

Corin Hale sat on the edge of his cot with his back to the door, which was either defiance or exhaustion, and with Corin it had always been impossible to tell the difference.

"You look dreadful," she said, setting the tray on the floor beside him.

"You should see the other guy," he said. His voice was rough. Six days of singing into his mind left marks, even when the singing was gentle. Especially when it was gentle. "Oh wait. There wasn't one. Your people just found me asleep in a ditch."

"You were faking your death at the time. One does one's best."

That earned her half a laugh, quickly smothered. Good. Anger was useful; despair was not; but laughter meant there was still a man in there, and men could be reasoned with.

She lowered herself onto the stool across from him, joints protesting. Eleven years without sleep wrote themselves into the body eventually, whatever the Song did for the spirit. "You've been difficult, Corin."

"I've been *honest*."

"You've been silent, which in this building is the same thing as lying." She folded her hands. "I'm going to tell you something I haven't told anyone in thirty years. Not because you've earned it. Because you're the only person alive who might understand it, and I am very tired of carrying it alone."

His shoulders shifted. Curiosity — the oldest hook there was. She had caught many fish with it.

"The Song is dying," she said.

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He picked up the bread from the tray instead, turned it over in his fingers, tore off a piece, ate it. Deliberately. Making her wait, making himself seem unafraid. She let him have the performance. She remembered being twenty-two and performing fearlessness for the Maestro of that age, and how much it had cost her, and how little good it had done.

"The Song has fed us for four hundred years," she went on. "It ended the Sundering War. It silenced the wild magic that was tearing the world apart — do you know what the world was like before? I've read the archives. Whole provinces eaten by beasts that dreamed themselves real. Rivers that changed their minds. Children born speaking languages that hadn't existed yet. The speakers called it a gift. It was a wound, Corin. In the skin of everything. And we closed it."

"With a song," he said quietly.

"With a song. With our voices, braided together, one will, one chord. And it worked. For a hundred years it worked beautifully, and we were heroes, and then—" She stopped. Even now, after thirty years of knowing, the next part came hard. "And then we learned what we were feeding it."

"The stars," Corin said.

Her head came up sharply. "Who told you that?"

"No one told me. I *read*, Maestro. Archive level nine, the sealed stacks, third shelf behind the false panel with the bad warding. You really ought to fire whoever warded that. Or promote them, since apparently they wanted it found."

For a moment — a long, dangerous moment — Solenne considered having him killed. It passed, as such moments always did, leaving only its sediment. "Go on."

"The stars aren't lights. They're voices. Every speaker your predecessors ever consumed — their gifts didn't vanish. They were pulled up, out of the world, and hung in the sky where they couldn't cause trouble. The stars are a prison. And the Song has been eating the prisoners to keep itself alive." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were the worst thing in the room: not hateful, just *knowing*. "That's why they started going out. That's why the sky's been emptying for a century. We didn't lose the stars, Maestro. We digested them."

"Yes," she said.

"And you knew."

"For thirty years."

He stood up. The cot creaked. "Then why are you still *singing*?"

"Because if I stop, we all die." She said it flatly, without drama, because drama would have cheapened it. "Sit down, Corin. This is the part no archive contains."

He sat. Slowly. Like a man sitting down to hear a diagnosis.

"The Song is not merely sustained by the gifts we feed it. It has become *hungry*. It wants more, always more, and the supply of speakers has run out — the last true ones died or hid, generation after generation, until now there is one girl in a valley that forgot magic existed, and a griffin, and a defector who thought he could walk away." She smiled thinly. "Do you know what happens when the Song runs out of food? It starts eating its singers. From the inside. I have watched it happen. Cantor Maren, who taught you counterpoint — you remember her voice? She didn't lose it to age. The Song took it, and then it took the rest of her, over eleven months, while we sang louder and louder trying to drown out the sound of her screaming. The Song doesn't hate us, Corin. It's worse than that. It needs us. The way a fire needs a forest."

"So let it burn out," he said. "Let it starve. End it."

"And the moment it ends, every seam it sealed for four hundred years opens at once. The wild magic comes back — all of it, at once, unfiltered, ungoverned — into a world that has forgotten how to survive it. The beasts return. The wounds reopen. And the Choir, every man and woman who gave their voice to the binding, dies screaming as the thing inside them tears loose." She leaned forward. "I have run the arithmetic, Corin. I have spent thirty years running the arithmetic. There are exactly two endings. Either the Song devours the last speaker and completes itself, becoming self-sustaining, eternal, and the hunger goes quiet forever — or we all die, and take the world's skin off with us."

"That's not two endings," Corin said. "That's one ending and a lie you tell yourself at night."

"There are no nights for me anymore. I told you. I hold the chord."

"Then put it down."

"I *can't*."

The words cracked something in the air between them. She heard it — heard herself — and for one unguarded breath she was not the Maestro of Vesperhold, terror of two continents, keeper of the great chord. She was an old woman in a cellar, holding up a weight no one else could see, asking a boy she had once loved like a son to help her set it down.

Corin saw it too. She watched him see it. And she watched him, being Corin, refuse to be moved by it.

"There's a third way," he said. "You know there is. The girl. Astrid. Her gift isn't like the others — Ashveil says—"

"Ashveil," Solenne repeated, and laughed, short and bitter. "A griffin. A dying, half-mad griffin is your authority on metaphysics now."

"She's more honest than any archive."

"Perhaps. But here is what I know." She rose, smoothing her robes, gathering the Maestro back around herself like a garment. "The girl's gift is the largest we have ever measured. When the hunters first felt her across the wards, the Song *shivered*, Corin. Three hundred cantors woke in the night hearing a note none of them had ever sung. If I feed her to the Song — if I bring her here willingly, gently, and let the binding take her slowly, with her consent if I can get it — the chord completes. The hunger ends. The stars stay where they are, dim but stable, and no more voices are ever consumed, because there will be nothing left to consume *with*. Magic ends. All of it. Forever. And the world gets to be ordinary, and safe, and whole."

"And the girl?"

"Will live. As a woman, not a speaker. Loved, honored, given anything she wants for the rest of her natural life." She met his eyes. "I am not the monster in your archives, Corin. I am the woman doing surgery on the world with no anesthetic and a very dull knife. I would spare her if I could. I have looked for another blade for thirty years. There isn't one."

* * *

She left him then, because the argument was won — she could feel it, the small give in him, the place where his certainty had softened into doubt — and because winning arguments with Corin Hale had never once meant winning Corin Hale.

In the corridor outside, Cantor Pellin was waiting with his round face full of questions he was too frightened to ask.

"Double his rations," she said. "And send word to the hunting choirs. Where is the girl now?"

"The trackers believe... the wild country east of the Grayfell range, Maestro. The beasts there have gone strange. Some of our riders were—" He hesitated. "Unhorsed."

"By beasts."

"By *weathered* beasts, Maestro. Half-starved, star-mad. They say the animals are... organizing."

Solenne stopped walking.

Organizing. In her long life she had seen many alarming things, but organized wildlife ranked surprisingly high among them. And there was only one force in the world that could organize a starving wilderness, and she was currently eating prison porridge and insulting her cooking.

"She's growing stronger," Solenne murmured. "As the sky empties, the gift pools in the last vessel. Of course. Of *course* it works that way. Everything about this has been a lesson in conservation of misery."

"Maestro?"

"Nothing, Cantor Pellin." She resumed walking, faster now, her mind already composing the orders, the deployments, the careful net of bells and riders that would close around the eastern wild country. "Prepare the Great Hall. All three hundred voices, in rotation, day and night. If the Song must be sung at full strength soon, I want the Choir ready to receive."

"Receive what, Maestro?"

She paused at the foot of the stairs, and for a moment the lamplight caught her face, and the young cantor saw — though he would spend years failing to describe it — something that was not cruelty and not madness, but a weariness so vast it had worn smooth into something resembling peace.

"A guest," said the Maestro of Vesperhold. "Tell the kitchens we may soon be feeding a hero."

Above her, faint through forty feet of stone, the bells began the evening change, and the Song rolled out over the city like a tide — and somewhere far below the harbor of the world, in the dark where the stolen voices pooled, something ancient stirred in its sleep and listened.
