## 14. What Corin Chose

The cell had no windows, which Corin decided on the first day was a mercy, and by the third day understood was the whole point.

A man who cannot see the sky cannot count the days. A man who cannot count the days cannot hold on to himself. The Choir had refined this over centuries — they were, after all, an order built on patience and harmony, and torture was only harmony turned inside out, every voice pressed until it gave up its own note and joined the chorus whether it wished to or not.

They had taken his boots first. That had seemed petty at the time. He understood now: bare feet on cold stone kept a body too miserable to think clearly, and thinking clearly was the one weapon they could not confiscate.

"Again," said Cantor Vess, who had been his instructor once, long ago, back when Corin still believed the Song was a kindness.

Corin sat in the center of the room with his hands loose in his lap, because they had learned that strapping him down only gave him something to strain against, and strain was a kind of rhythm, and rhythm was a kind of song, and he would take any song he could get. His ribs ached in a way he had catalogued rather than feared — two cracked, probably, from the second session. His left ear rang constantly since the bell-hammer, a thin silver tone like a struck wineglass that refused to finish dying.

He was, all things considered, in excellent spirits.

This was not stoicism. This was arithmetic.

Every hour they spent pulling Astrid Iversen's location out of him was an hour she spent farther away. Every note of pain they fed into the network through his body was a note he could bend, if he was careful, if he was patient, if he was willing to pay for each falsehood with a fresh stripe of agony. He had been doing the sums for three days now, and the ledger read: girl safe, ledger growing, Corin Hale increasingly expensive to maintain.

He could live with that. Possibly literally.

"The valley," Vess said. She was a small woman with a voice like warm honey poured over a blade, and she genuinely believed she was gentle. That was the horror of the Choir, Corin thought — not cruelty but sincerity. "The girl fled east along the river. You watched her go. Say it."

"She fled east along the river," Corin repeated.

Vess smiled. "Into the network, Cantor Hale. *Sing* it."

And here was the trick, the small treasonous miracle he had discovered on the first night, half-delirious, when they'd chained him to the resonance frame and forced his voice into the great web of the Song so that everything he confessed would be carried instantly to every listening cantor in Vesperhold:

The Song did not check its sources.

It couldn't. It was too vast, too hungry, too old. It swallowed voices the way the sea swallows rivers — without tasting them. A confession sung with true grief read as truth. And grief, thank the vanished stars, was the one thing Corin Hale had in inexhaustible supply.

So he sang it. He sang *she fled east along the river* with the full weight of his ruined heart behind it, and he let the memory underneath be real — the memory of watching her go west, up into the wild country, Ashveil's broken wing beating against the dawn — and the Song drank the sorrow and never noticed the direction was a lie.

East along the river. There was no river east. There was a gorge full of dead orchards and feral goats, and somewhere in it, right now, a squad of the Maestro's hunters was wasting its strength terrifying livestock.

Corin finished the phrase and sagged, breathing hard. Sweat stung the cuts on his back. The silver tone in his ear sang on.

"You're very cooperative," Vess observed, suspiciously.

"I'm very broken," Corin corrected her, and gave her a smile with most of his teeth. "There's a difference. Ask your frame-masters; they'll show you the readings."

She studied him a moment longer, then made a small gesture, and the resonance frame went quiet, and two acolytes hauled him back to his corner of the cell and left him with bread and water, because even the Choir understood that a voice must be maintained.

He ate. He counted the cracks in the ceiling — forty-one, same as yesterday, same as always — and he thought about Astrid Iversen, whom he had known for perhaps nine days and betrayed in perhaps four different ways, and about how she had looked at him when the trap sprang in the bell tower. Not surprised. That was the worst of it. She hadn't been surprised. She had simply added him to the long list of people who had ever lied to her, and walked away, and taken the griffin, and left him to the Maestro's hands, and she had been *right* to.

He deserved the bell-hammer. He deserved the ringing ear. What he didn't deserve — what he intended to steal anyway, with both hands — was another chance.

*Hold on,* he told her silently, the way you can talk to someone who isn't there. *I'm making them look east. Keep going west. Keep being impossible.*

Somewhere above him, muffled by stone, the bells of Vesperhold rang the hour, and beneath the bells, beneath the city, the Song hummed on, vast and blind and slowly, slowly beginning to taste wrong.

* * *

On the fifth day they changed tactics, which meant Corin was winning.

He knew this the way a sailor knows weather: by the feel of the air. When an interrogation stops escalating, the interrogator has stopped believing the prisoner is hiding something simple. They had sent riders east along the river — he'd felt the orders move through the network like ripples through his own chest, little cold pulses of *confirmed, confirmed, confirmed*, each one a lie he had planted now bearing fruit — and the riders had found nothing but goats, and the goats had found nothing but trouble, and the Maestro's patience, which was legendary, had begun to wear audibly.

So they brought in the Resonant.

Corin had seen a Resonant exactly once before, years ago, at a purification in the southern provinces. They were not cantors. They were what happened when the Choir took a speaker-child too young to remember being anything else and hollowed them into instruments — living tuning forks, tuned to the Song itself, capable of hearing a false note the way a healthy ear hears a cracked bell.

The one they brought into his cell was maybe fourteen. She had white hair and eyes like frosted glass, and she did not look at him. She looked *through* him, at some middle distance where music lived, and when she spoke, her voice came out in unison with itself, doubled, as though two girls shared one throat.

"He sings lies," she said.

Corin's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

Vess, standing by the door, went very still. "Explain."

"When he confesses," the Resonant said, "the grief is true. The words are false. The Song does not taste the difference." Her glass eyes finally focused, and there was something in them — faint, buried, but unmistakable — that might once have been pity. "I do."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats, during which Corin ran every calculation he owned and came up empty. Then Vess said, softly, almost kindly: "Thank you, child. Wait outside."

The door closed. Vess turned to him, and for the first time in five days she looked tired.

"Do you know what happens now?" she asked.

"I imagine it involves the frame," Corin said. "It usually does."

"It involves the frame, yes." She crossed the cell and crouched in front of him, and this close he could see she was older than he remembered, older than the Song should have allowed anyone to become. "But listen to me, Corin. I taught you counterpoint when you were nineteen and arrogant. I have watched you throw away a brilliant career for — whatever this is. For a farm girl and a crippled griffin and a conscience you were supposed to have outgrown at initiation." She sighed. "The Maestro will ask me to open you. To take the Song apart inside you, thread by thread, until we find every lie you've planted and pull them out like splinters. It will take days. You will beg me to stop by the second hour, and I will not stop, and by the end there will be very little of you left to put back together."

"That's a compelling recruitment speech," Corin said. "Is there a version where I sign something?"

"Corin."

"Vess." He leaned forward, and his cracked ribs screamed, and he kept his voice level anyway, because this next part mattered more than the pain, more than anything. "Ask yourself one question. Just one, before you open me. The Song eats speakers. It ate the stars. It is eating *us* — you've felt it, don't pretend you haven't, the hunger under the harmonies, getting worse every year, the way the initiates come back thinner, grayer, emptier. Five hundred years of feeding, and the hunger only grows. So ask yourself: when it finishes eating everyone else—" he tapped his own chest, gently, "—who do you imagine is on the menu?"

Something flickered across Vess's face. Fear, old and well-practiced, the fear of a woman who had asked herself that question long ago and buried the answer deep enough to keep functioning.

"You always did ask the wrong questions beautifully," she said. Then she stood, and straightened her robes, and became again the instrument of the Maestro's will, and Corin understood that whatever she felt, she would do her duty, because that was the tragedy of Vess: she had never once in her life chosen anything.

"Begin the dissonance extraction at dawn," she told the door. "I want him rested. A broken voice gives broken readings."

The door opened. The acolytes came. And as they led him back to the frame, past the Resonant waiting in the corridor with her doubled voice and her frosted eyes, Corin did the bravest and stupidest thing available to him.

He hummed.

Just a fragment. Two notes, low, almost subvocal, pitched beneath the hearing of the acolytes — but not beneath hers. Not beneath a creature built to hear everything. Two notes of an old lullaby from the southern provinces, a song about a lark who outflies the hawk by flying at night, when hawks sleep.

The Resonant's head turned. Their eyes met for half a second.

Her face did not change. But as the acolytes dragged him past, she began, softly, to hum the answering phrase — the part of the lullaby where the lark reaches morning — and no one in the corridor marked it as anything but a hollowed child making idle noise, because that was all she had ever been permitted to make.

Inside the Song, invisible, a seed of night-flight took root.

* * *

Dawn came, and with it the frame, and with it Vess, and with it pain beyond the careful catalogue he had built, into country he had no maps for.

Dissonance extraction was not like the beatings. The beatings had been *theirs* — fists, hammers, the honest brutality of bodies. This was the Song turned surgical. They bound him into the resonance web and let the network flow through him, hunting the false notes he had seeded, and when it found one it gripped it — gripped *him*, because the lie and the liar were woven together now — and pulled.

The first lie came out of him like a tooth. He felt it leave, felt the memory of *east along the river* ripped free and held up to the light, and heard, distantly, through the web, the network shudder as five hundred years of confirmed reports curdled into doubt.

*False,* the Song whispered to itself, in ten thousand throats. *False. False.*

Good, Corin thought, from very far away, through a haze of white. *Now you know I lied. Now you know you can't trust anything I sang. Now every report I ever gave you is poison, and you'll spend weeks untangling which ones, and while you untangle—*

The second lie came out. Then the third. Each one cost more. By the sixth he had stopped forming sentences. By the ninth he had stopped forming the thought *hold on, Astrid* and reduced it to its essential syllable, a single silent pulse he offered to the dark like a coin tossed in a well: *west. West. West.*

At some point Vess's hands were on his face, holding his jaw steady, and her voice was saying something — *stay with me, boy, stay* — and there were tears on her cheeks, which was interesting, because he hadn't known she could.

At some point after that, the extraction stopped, either because they had pulled everything or because there was no longer enough of him left to pull from, and he lay in the wreckage of the frame like a gutted instrument, and the silver tone in his ear was joined by others, a whole ghost-choir of damaged frequencies, and he thought, with the last coherent corner of his mind:

*Worth it.*

Because here was what they had missed — what the Maestro, in all her terrible brilliance, had failed to account for. They had pulled his lies out of the network. But a lie, once tasted, teaches the mouth doubt. And doubt, in a system built entirely on unison, spreads like rot through timber. Already he could feel it through the web's fraying edge: cantors whisper-checking their own reports, harmonies hesitating half a beat before resolving, the great confident chord of the Choir developing, for the first time in centuries, a tremor.

And deeper still — so deep he might have imagined it, except the Resonant's answering lullaby said otherwise — something was moving in the foundations. Small. Night-flying. A handful of hollowed children who had heard, threaded through the Song they were made of, two notes of a lark, and begun — carefully, secretly, in the spaces between their duties — to pass the melody along.

You could not unmake the Song, Corin knew. It was too big, too old, too woven into the world's bones.

But you could teach it a new note. One singer at a time. Even singers who had been turned into forks and bells. Especially those.

* * *

He woke to footsteps, and the smell of blood that wasn't his, and shouting in the corridors above — distant, muffled, urgent, the particular cadence of an institution discovering bad news.

A key turned. His cell door opened.

The Resonant stood in it. Alone. Behind her, the corridor lamp swung wildly, throwing shadows that ran and scattered.

"They're calling the cantors to the Maestro," she said, in her doubled voice, and then — wonderingly, as if reporting a miracle — "all of them. Even the frames. Something has happened in the wild country. The beasts are moving. All of them, together, toward—" she frowned, reaching for a word the Choir had forbidden her, "—toward a *hollow*. And the Maestro is afraid."

Corin lay in the dark and began, painfully, joint by joint, rib by cracked rib, to laugh.

"Afraid," he repeated. "Solenne is afraid."

"The girl," the Resonant said. "The speaker. She's alive. She's *leading* them." Glass eyes found his in the dark. "West, Cantor Hale. Everyone says she went west."

He got his feet under him. The floor tilted; he ignored it. Somewhere far below the city, beneath stone and bell and five hundred years of stolen voices, the lullaby was spreading, note by hidden note, and above the city the starless sky waited, and somewhere west a lame fox and a dying griffin and a woman who had every right to let him hang were converging on the place where all of it would end.

"Well then," Corin Hale said, gripping the doorframe, standing up out of his ruin like a lark rising against the hawk's sleeping hours. "We shouldn't keep her waiting."

Behind them, the first bell of Vesperhold rang — and cracked.
