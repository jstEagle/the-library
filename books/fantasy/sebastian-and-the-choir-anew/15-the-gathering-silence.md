## 15. The Gathering Silence

The wolf had been following her for three days before Astrid finally lost her temper.

"Not another step," she said, turning on the trail with her hands on her hips. "I mean it. You've been breathing on my neck since the ford at Marrow Creek, and I have had *no* coffee, and my boots are wet, and if you sigh at me one more time—"

The wolf sighed.

It was an enormous gray thing, ribs showing through its winter coat like the slats of a barrel, and it had the particular expression of a creature that understood every word and intended to be unhelpful about all of them. Behind it, half-hidden in the pines, waited a dozen more shapes: a lynx with a torn ear, two elk who kept bumping their antlers together in what might have been conversation or might have been nerves, a family of badgers arranged in a grim little row like mourners at a funeral.

"They want you to follow," Ashveil said from above. The griffin was perched on a dead birch, wings folded tight against his body. He still favored his left side when he landed; the wound the Choir had given him months ago had healed to a pale seam of scarred feathers, but he guarded it like a secret. "They've been trying to tell you since the river."

"I know they've been trying to tell me. They've been *extremely* loud about it without saying anything." Astrid pushed a strand of hair out of her face. "That's the whole problem with beasts, in case anyone's wondering. All signal, no sentences."

The wolf made a sound that was unmistakably, insultingly, a laugh.

"Don't you start."

*You could simply ask them,* Ashveil said, and there was something underneath the words — not quite reproach, more like the careful gentleness of someone approaching a sleeping bear. *You've been avoiding asking anything since we left the high country.*

Astrid looked at him. Then at the wolf. Then at the trees beyond, where the light came down strange and gold-green through branches that had stopped dropping leaves weeks ago and simply refused to let go of them, as if the whole forest were holding its breath.

She knew what they wanted. She'd known for days, the way you know a storm is coming — not because anyone announced it, but because your knees ache and the birds go quiet and every animal within fifty miles starts walking in the same direction, and the direction is *down*, always down, toward the deep places.

The beasts were going somewhere. And they wanted her to come.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Lead on, then. But if this is another one of those things where you take me to a haunted ruin and stand around looking meaningful while I nearly die, I'm going to be very cross."

The wolf turned and trotted north, tail low, and the whole strange procession moved with it — elk and lynx and badgers and a fox that had appeared from nowhere and fell into step beside Astrid's boot as though it had always been there.

She glanced down at it. Red coat, white chest, one ear folded crooked.

"You're not Sebastian," she said quietly. "Don't pretend."

The fox looked up at her with amber eyes and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

* * *

They walked for two more days, and the world grew quieter the way water grows colder — gradually, then all at once.

It wasn't silence exactly. There were still sounds: wind, the creak of branches, the small business of hooves and paws on frost-hardened ground. But beneath it all ran a hush like the one inside a church, or inside a held breath. No birds sang past the second morning. No insects. Even the wind seemed to move carefully, as though it might wake something.

Astrid had grown used to the starless sky. That was the terrible part, she thought — how quickly a person got used to the end of things. Three months ago she would have wept to look up at that blank black velvet where the stars should have been. Now she just checked it the way she checked the weather, out of habit, and felt the old grief pass through her like water through a net.

But here, under these trees, the absence pressed closer. Because the beasts all felt it too, and unlike her, they couldn't look away.

The elk hadn't spoken — properly spoken, mind-to-mind — in over a week. None of them had. Their voices came to her thinner each day, like songs played through a wall. She'd told herself it was distance, exhaustion, her own frayed nerves. Walking now in that gathering hush, she admitted what she'd been refusing to admit: the wild voices weren't fading because the speakers were gone.

They were fading because the sky was empty. Whatever the stars had been — whatever the Choir's Song had swallowed, all those centuries ago — the beasts had been listening to it their whole lives, the way sailors listen to the sea. And now the sea had gone dry.

"The Hollow," Ashveil said at dusk on the third day.

He said it the way other people said *the grave*.

Ahead of them the land dropped away into a bowl of ancient forest — oaks so wide that six riders abreast couldn't have circled one, their trunks furred with moss older than any kingdom Astrid could name. The bowl's floor was dark even in daylight, and now, with the last light failing, it was darker than dark. Not shadow. Shadow has edges. This was a smooth, total absence, like a hole cut out of the world.

And at its center, far below, something glimmered.

Not brightly. Not the way fire glimmers or water glimmers. It was a faint, slow pulse of silver-gold, rising from underground like light remembered rather than light seen — and Astrid felt it in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes, in the place behind her sternum where her gift lived.

Every beast at the rim of that bowl went still.

"Oh," Astrid said. Her voice came out very small. "Oh, that's — that's what I think it is, isn't it?"

*Yes,* Ashveil said.

"It's *them*. The stars. They're not — they didn't go out. They were *put* somewhere." She gripped the strap of her pack until her knuckles whitened. "All this time. All this time everyone said the stars died, burned out, abandoned us — and they've been buried under a hill like treasure, like *prisoners*—"

*Like voices,* Ashveil said. *Stolen voices pool, Astrid. The Choir did not kill the stars. It silenced them. And everything it silences, it must keep. Somewhere. Forever.* His head turned, that cruel eagle-beak catching the last light. *This is where forever lives.*

The descent took hours. The beasts would not enter the bowl itself — Astrid watched the wolf stop at a certain line of stones, hackles raised, and refuse with its whole body, and she did not blame it. Only she and Ashveil went down, along paths that wound between roots vast as shipwrecks, and the closer they came to the center, the louder the silence became, which should not have been possible and yet was.

At the bottom there was a hollow in the earth — the First Hollow, though no sign named it, no marker, nothing but a depression in the ground perhaps thirty feet across, ringed by white mushrooms that glowed faintly with borrowed light. And beneath a skin of soil and root, visible only when the pulse came, lay the pool.

Astrid knelt at its edge and pressed her palm flat against the cold ground.

The gift rose in her like a tide answering the moon.

She heard them. Not words — not yet — but *voices*, hundreds of them, thousands, layered and braided and singing so softly that only a speaker could catch the thread of it. Voices that had once hung in the night sky over every valley and harbor and mountain in the world. Voices that children had wished on. Voices that sailors had steered by and lovers had sworn by and her own grandmother had sung lullabies beneath, back when there was still something up there to sing to.

They were singing in the dark. They had been singing in the dark for three hundred years, and no one had heard them, and they had never once stopped.

Astrid came back to herself with her face wet and her whole body shaking.

"Ashveil." Her voice cracked. "How do I open it? Tell me how to open it."

The griffin was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his mind-voice had gone very gentle, the way it did when he talked about the nest he'd lost, and she understood, with a cold sliding certainty, that she was not going to like what came next.

*There is a lock on this place. The Choir built it when they buried the sky — a binding woven from the same stuff as their Song. It cannot be broken by force. It cannot be picked. It can only be opened by a key of the same nature as the voices themselves.* A pause. *Wild magic. Speaker-gift.*

"Then mine will do. I'm right here."

*Astrid.*

"What? I pour myself into it, the lock opens, the stars go home. I've been rehearsing this speech since Vesperhold, Ashveil, don't make that face at me, you don't even have a face that does expressions—"

*It is not a door you unlock,* he said. *It is a door you *become*. The binding drinks what opens it. Every drop. Your gift would not be spent — gifts are spent, and recover, like breath. It would be given. Entirely. Permanently. You would speak to beasts tonight and never again hear them tomorrow. You would be, for the rest of your life, what your valley always pretended you were.* His voice softened further. *Ordinary.*

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Astrid sat back on her heels. Around her the mushrooms pulsed gently, and beneath her the buried stars sang on, patient as mountains, and somewhere above them the empty sky waited with three hundred years of patience of its own.

"No," she said.

Ashveil's crest lifted.

"No, I mean — not *no*, I won't do it. I mean no, you don't get to hand me that like it's simple." She was crying again, furiously, and wiped her face with her sleeve. "Do you know what my mother did? She burned my drawings. Every picture I ever made of a talking animal, she fed to the stove while I watched, and she said, 'Now you can be safe.' And I hated her for it. For *years* I hated her. And then I found out she'd known all along — she'd always known what I was — and she burned them anyway because she thought love meant making me small enough to survive."

Her voice steadied. Or hardened. She wasn't sure which.

"And I swore, walking away from that valley with you bleeding in my arms, that I would never again let anyone decide how much of me was allowed to exist. Not the Choir. Not my mother. Not *me*, most of all. So don't — don't stand there being kind about it. Don't tell me the price like it's fair. Nothing about this is fair. The stars didn't deserve burying and I didn't deserve exile and Corin didn't deserve—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Corin didn't deserve what I left him to."

*No,* Ashveil agreed quietly. *None of it was deserved. That is rather the shape of the world lately.*

"But I'll pay it." She looked down at the ground, at the faint golden heartbeat beneath the soil. "I'll pay it, because those voices have been screaming in the dark for three hundred years and I'm the only one who can hear them, and because some things are worth more than being whole. I just — I need a day. Maybe two. I need to say goodbye to it properly. To *them*." She gestured at the waiting beasts at the rim of the bowl, invisible now in the dark, but present; she could feel them the way you feel a hand resting on your shoulder. "Is that cowardly?"

*It is human,* Ashveil said. *I am told the two are frequently confused.*

Despite everything, Astrid laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but real. "When did you get wise?"

*I have always been wise. You were too busy feeding me rabbits to notice.*

She settled against his warm feathered side as the night deepened, and one by one the beasts crept down into the bowl after all, drawn past their fear by something older than fear, until she was surrounded — wolf and lynx and elk and badgers and the crooked-eared fox curled against her knee — a congregation gathered in the last church of the wild world.

She fell asleep listening to the stars sing.

* * *

She woke to Ashveil's talons closing on her shoulder and his mind-voice sharp as a blade:

*Astrid. Wake up. They've found us.*

Down the far slope of the valley, torchlight was blooming among the black trees — dozens of them, moving in formation, too orderly for anything wild. And riding the wind ahead of the torches came a sound that turned her blood to ice water, because she knew it, she would know it anywhere, she had heard it humming from the lips of servants in Vesperhold and from the throats of hunters in the passes:

The Choir was singing.

And this time, they were singing the binding-song — the one meant to seal a Hollow forever.

"They can't," she breathed, scrambling upright. "If they close it while I'm—"

*Then the stars are buried till the end of the world,* Ashveil said, spreading his ruined wings, *and your choice is already made for you. Decide fast, speaker. We have minutes, not days.*

Below her feet, the buried stars began, very softly, to sing louder.
