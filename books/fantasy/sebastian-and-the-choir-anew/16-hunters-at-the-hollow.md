## 16. Hunters at the Hollow

The wolves woke her before dawn, and they were singing.

Not howling — Astrid had heard plenty of howling in her life, mournful and long and mostly about territory. This was different. This was a thread of sound passed from throat to throat down the mountainside, a relay of warning pitched so high that ordinary ears would have caught nothing but wind. *They come. They come. They come.*

She sat up in the bracken at the mouth of the First Hollow, and every beast around her was already awake. The great elk stag stood with his head high, breath steaming. The bear brothers had stopped their endless bickering over a fish carcass and were facing downhill together, which she had never once seen them do. Even the mad ones — the foxes that had chewed their own tails to bloody stubs in the starless years, the owl that flew by day because night meant nothing to it anymore — even they had gone still, as if the whole slope were holding its breath.

Ashveil limped to her side. The griffin's wounds had closed to silver scars over the weeks of travel, but he moved like an old soldier now, favoring his left foreleg, his eagle eyes fixed on the pass below.

"How many?" Astrid asked.

The answer came not in words but in weight — a pressure against her gift, like a hand pressed flat against a door. She had learned to read the beasts' feelings as shapes, and this shape was enormous. A column of riders. Bells. So many bells.

"The whole Choir," she said aloud, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

*The whole Song,* Ashveil corrected grimly. *They bring the singers too. Not just hunters. Cantors. They mean to sing the Hollow shut forever — or open it and drink.*

Astrid looked down into the valley bowl where the stolen voices pooled underground, where the grass grew silver and the air hummed like a struck glass. Somewhere beneath her feet lay every speaker the Choir had ever consumed, every star they'd pulled out of the sky, waiting. And somewhere behind her, at the bottom of the ravine where the beasts had hidden her these three days, Corin Hale was either dead or singing lies into the dark.

She had abandoned him in the bell tower. She had run. She had told herself it was survival, and it was, and she hated herself for it anyway, and now the bill for her escape had arrived with bells on.

"Then we hold," she said.

The elk stag turned his great antlered head toward her, and there was no fear in him. There was something worse and better: certainty. He had been mad for six years, since the stars went out, tearing bark off trees and goring anything that startled him. Three days ago she had put her hands on his neck and given back to him whatever the sky had taken, and now he would die for her without being asked.

*We hold,* the herd-mind agreed, rippling outward through the slope like a stone dropped in a pond. *We hold, we hold, we hold.*

Astrid Iversen, who had spent twenty-two years hiding her voice in a valley that exiled speakers, stood up in front of two hundred beasts and gave the only order of her life.

"Then let them hear us coming."

* * *

Corin Hale had counted four hundred and eleven hours in the dark, or he had tried to, before they took his boots and then his belt and finally the little bone button off his sleeve, leaving him nothing to count with but his heartbeat, which was unreliable under torture.

He hung in the binding-circles beneath Vesperhold's great bell tower, arms above his head, and he sang.

That was the cruelty of it, and Solenne understood cruelty the way a jeweler understands facets. They could not simply beat a location out of him, because the Choir did not work in questions and answers. They worked in song. Every cantor was bound into the Great Song, one voice among thousands, and when they needed something from you they didn't ask — they *harmonized* at you until your own voice betrayed you, until your mind opened like a door in a gale.

So Corin sang. That was expected. That was required. A cantor who refused to sing was severed from the Song, and a severed cantor felt the absence the way a man feels the absence of blood.

What was not expected — what only Solenne suspected, and could not prove, and could not stop — was what he sang.

He had spent thirty years inside the Choir. He knew the Great Song the way a carpenter knows a house: every joist, every load-bearing wall, every place where a clever man might knock out a brick and bring the ceiling down. And for four hundred and eleven hours, while the binding-harmonies pried at him looking for Astrid Iversen's location, Corin Hale had been feeding false notes into the network. Tiny ones. A third where a fourth belonged. A rest held a half-beat too long. Nothing any single listener would catch. But errors propagate. Errors compound. Four hundred hours of compounded error, sung faithfully onward by ten thousand trusting throats, and the Great Song had developed a limp.

It cost him. Every false note cost him; the Song punished discord in its own body, and he felt each correction come back up through the binding-circles like a whipcrack along his spine. His voice was wrecked. His ribs had been broken twice and healed wrong by indifferent magic. His hands, hanging in the circles, had gone from white to blue to a color he preferred not to name.

And still, when the alarm came — when the tower shuddered and the bells above began to swing unbidden, all of them, the whole carillon crying out at once — Corin laughed until his broken ribs screamed.

Because he knew what that sound meant. The bells rang true alarms only for one thing.

*They've found the Hollow.*

* * *

The Choir came down into the valley bowl at first light, and it was not a raid. It was a procession.

Astrid watched from the ridge with her heart hammering against her ribs. Two hundred riders in grey, ranks closing like water. Behind them, walking, came the cantors — hundreds of them, robed in white and gold, and they were already singing. The Great Song rolled ahead of them up the slope, and where it rolled, things died. Not violently. Worse. The grass lay down flat. A hawk mid-flight folded its wings and dropped like a stone, caught by a cantor who didn't look up. The wildness went out of the world in a widening circle, the way color drains from a photograph.

"That's what they do," Astrid whispered. "That's what they *are.*"

*Yes,* said Ashveil. *Now you understand why I hate them in my bones. Literally in my bones, child. My bones remember when the sky had lights in it.*

Below, the vanguard reached the silver grass at the lip of the Hollow, and the ground itself seemed to flinch.

"Stag," Astrid said. "Now."

The elk threw back his head and bellowed, and two hundred beasts answered.

It was not a battle in any way the songs describe battles. Beasts do not form lines. What came down the mountainside was an avalanche with teeth — the bear brothers leading a wedge of boar, the wolf packs fanning wide through the bracken, the mad owl diving out of a pale sun with its talons aimed at the nearest cantor's eyes. The riders' lances took the first boars. The boars' tusks took the first horses. And through it all, under it all, Astrid stood on the ridge with her arms spread and her gift blazing out of her like heat from a forge, pouring courage into animals whose courage should have failed, pouring calm into the maddened ones at the exact moment the Song tried to strip it away.

It was like arm-wrestling a glacier. The Great Song pushed *silence* — obedience, emptiness, the tamed and bell-rung world — and she pushed back everything else. Fear became fury. Fury became purpose. A wounded wolf, gut-open and dying, dragged itself the last ten feet to hamstring a rider rather than crawl away, because Astrid's voice in its blood said *not yet, brother, not yet, one more step, one more—*

She was crying and she did not stop fighting. Both things were true at once.

"They're falling back to regroup," she gasped to Ashveil. "The left flank—"

"No." The griffin's head snapped around, beak parted. "Look."

At the center of the Choir's formation, the cantors had stopped singing.

All of them. At once. The silence that fell was so total Astrid's ears rang, and in that ringing silence she heard — faintly, horribly — the Great Song continuing without them. Ten thousand distant throats in Vesperhold, miles away, carrying the melody onward, exactly as composed.

Exactly as composed. Including the errors. Including four hundred hours of Corin Hale's beautiful, patient sabotage.

The cantors stood frozen in the valley, mouths open, faces slack with horror, as the Song they had served all their lives began — note by note, voice by voice — to sing *wrong*. A chord curdled. A harmony inverted. Somewhere in the vast machine of the Choir's music, a gear stripped, and every cantor within earshot staggered as though the ground had tilted.

"What did he do?" Astrid breathed. "Oh, Corin. What did you *do?*"

What he had done, though she couldn't know it yet, was give them their opening. The riders wheeled in confusion, their mage-support collapsing into disarray, and the beasts hit the gap in the line like a flood hitting a broken dam.

But the Choir was not finished. Far from it. From the rear of the column, where the palanquin had waited untouched all morning, a figure rose — small, silver-haired, wrapped in white — and Maestro Solenne Vane opened her mouth and sang alone.

One voice. One old woman's voice, thin as wire.

And the whole battle stopped.

Every beast within a mile froze mid-stride. The bear brothers collapsed panting. The elk stag's legs buckled. Astrid felt the Song seize her gift like a fist closing around a candle flame, and she went to her knees on the ridge, gasping, as Solenne's solo wound upward through the morning air — searching, patient, *hungry* — a net cast across the entire valley for one specific voice.

For hers.

"There you are," the Maestro's voice said, everywhere at once, gentle as a lullaby. "There you are, little speaker. Come down. Come down, and I'll let the animals live. You have my word, and my word is the only honest thing left in this world."

Astrid knelt in the crushed grass, pinned, drowning, and the beasts around her were screaming silently, and the Hollow beneath her feet hummed with buried light, and she thought: *this is how it ends, then, caught like a rabbit—*

Which was when the mountain above Vesperhold's bell tower, forty miles away, began to ring every bell it owned — wildly, discordantly, gloriously wrong — and Solenne Vane's perfect solo stumbled for the first time in sixty years.

In that stumble, the fist around Astrid's flame loosened.

And out of the smoke and chaos below, limping, bleeding, half-deaf, wearing the rags of a white cantor's robe, came a man running uphill toward her as fast as his ruined body would allow.

"ASTRID!" Corin Hale's voice was destroyed, barely a croak, but she heard it anyway — heard it with her gift, with her bones, with the part of her that had abandoned him in a bell tower and never forgiven herself. "THE ARCHIVES! THE BINDING RUNS THROUGH THE HOLLOW FLOOR! THERE'S A SEAM — SOLenne KNOWS I KNOW — THAT'S WHY SHE LET ME LIVE—"

He fell. Got up. Kept running. Behind him, riders wheeled to chase, and the wolf pack hit them sideways in a grey wave, buying him seconds at the cost of blood.

Astrid got to her feet.

Ashveil landed beside her, sides heaving, one wing bent at an angle wings should not bend. "Child," the griffin said, and for the first time since she'd known him, the old soldier sounded afraid. "If what he says is true — if the seam opens — the price stands. You know the price. A speaker gives up everything, or the Hollow stays shut, and everything we bleed for today buys nothing."

"I know the price."

"And you'll pay it?"

Astrid watched Corin fall again, watched him crawl, watched a young rider raise a lance over him and watched the mad owl take the man's helmet off instead, shrieking defiance at a sunless sky.

She thought of her mother feeding drawings of talking animals into the fire, one by one, tears streaming, saying *I did it to keep you safe* — and how safety had turned out to be a thing you couldn't keep, only spend.

"Ashveil," she said, "get me to the bottom of the Hollow."

The griffin spread his broken wing, and grinned — griffins can grin, though it's unsettling — and said:

"Hold on to something that loves you."

And dove.
