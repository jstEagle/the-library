## 17. The Price of Survival

The Hollow had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Astrid crouched at the lip of the great stone basin, her palms flat against ground that hummed like a plucked string that never quite finished its note. Below her, through a seam in the rock no wider than a hand, light leaked upward — not firelight, not moonlight, but something older and warmer, the color of a voice you remembered from childhood. The stolen stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Pooled beneath the earth like buried treasure, except treasure was the wrong word entirely.

Voices, Ashveil had said. They are voices, Astrid. Every one.

And every one of them was singing, faintly, in a key only she could hear.

"You're doing the face again," said Sebastian.

The fox sat beside her with his lame leg stretched out before him like a gentleman at a fireside, tail curled over his paws, ears swiveled toward the seam of light. He had found her three days ago — or perhaps she had found him; neither of them was willing to concede the point — trotting out of the treeline with the particular offended dignity of an animal who has walked a very long way to deliver a scolding.

"What face?" Astrid asked.

"The one where your mouth says *I'm fine* and your eyes say *I am about to do something catastrophically stupid.*" He licked a paw. "Your mother had the same face. I imagine that's where you got it."

"Don't talk about my mother."

"I wasn't going to say anything unkind. She fed me half a chicken once, under the table, when she thought nobody was looking." Sebastian's whiskers twitched. "People are rarely what they pretend to be, little speaker. That cuts both ways."

Astrid didn't answer. She couldn't, really, because the thing Sebastian had just done — casually, carelessly, the way he did everything — was remind her that he had known her mother. Had sat beneath her family's table. Had been part of a life Astrid thought she'd understood, back when her biggest secret was a girl whispering to animals in the woods and pretending, afterward, to be nothing at all.

Below them, down in the dark, the pooled starlight pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat waiting for permission.

*They know we're here,* Ashveil had said this morning, before flying north to watch the passes. The griffin's wounds had closed to angry pink seams, but her voice still carried the rasp of a bird who had screamed too long. *The Song bends toward power like a tongue to a broken tooth. Solenne will come. It is only a question of how many she brings.*

It had not been a long question. The answer had arrived at dusk: a line of torches snaking down the far ridge, orderly as a hymn, accompanied by that low, grinding hum that was the Choir's Song made almost audible. Not hundreds of cantors. Dozens. Which was worse, because it meant they were confident.

"They're close," Astrid said now. "You can feel it too, can't you?"

"I can smell them," Sebastian said. "Ozone and self-regard. Very distinctive."

Despite everything — despite the torches and the humming dark and the fact that her hands were shaking — Astrid laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out. "You are the worst comfort in the entire valley."

"I'm not from the valley. I'm from *beyond* the valley, which makes me exotic." He stood, shook himself, and looked up at her with those amber eyes that saw entirely too much. "Talk to me, Astrid. What are you actually deciding?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the other questions, the one she had been circling for two days like a moth around a lamp she knew would burn her.

Because Ashveil had told her the price, and the price was simple, and simple things were always the cruelest.

*Opening the Hollow requires a speaker,* the griffin had said. *Not a ritual. Not a sacrifice of blood or gold. A gift, given wholly. You must pour what you are into the lock until there is nothing left of it. The stars rise. And you become —* a pause, a ruffle of feathers, the griffin equivalent of wincing — *ordinary.*

Ordinary. The word should have been a relief. For twenty years Astrid had wanted nothing more than to be ordinary, to walk through her village without flinching at every crow, to stop swallowing words whenever a horse looked at her with questions in its big liquid eyes. Ordinary was safety. Ordinary was her mother's whole life, built brick by careful brick around a daughter's dangerous secret.

But ordinary, she understood now, standing above a pool of imprisoned voices while an army sang itself closer — ordinary was also silence. Forever. No fox's dry commentary. No griffin's fierce counsel. No feeling the grief of a dying ewe or the joy of a foal's first gallop. Just a woman in a world of animals who could no longer tell her anything, ever again, about anything.

Or she could run.

That was the other door, and it stood open, and it was tempting in the way that rotting fruit is sweet. Flee south, past the Choir's reach, into wild country where the beasts already loved her. Live voiceless-but-free — no, not voiceless, that was the trick of it, she'd keep her gift, keep Sebastian's voice and Ashveil's, keep everything except any hope of ever seeing the stars again, of freeing whatever was down there in the dark, singing. The Choir would hunt her for the rest of her life, but she was quick, and the beasts would warn her, and maybe — *maybe* — she'd die old, in some warm den, having saved exactly no one but herself.

Her mother would want her to run. Her mother had spent Astrid's entire childhood teaching her to run — to hide the gift, to hunch her shoulders, to be small. To burn the evidence.

Astrid closed her eyes, and she was nine years old again, kneeling on the cold hearthstones, watching her mother feed page after page into the fire. Drawings. Forty of them, fifty, every talking animal Astrid had ever sketched — the fox with the crooked smile, the bear who complained about bees, the owl who quoted things nobody had said. Her mother's face had been terrible and calm, lit orange by the flames, and she had said, in a voice like a door closing:

*I knew. I always knew. And I let you believe you were hiding it because a child who thinks she's hidden is a child who's safe. But the world has changed, my love, and the hiding is over. From tonight, you're just a girl. Say it. Say you're just a girl.*

And Astrid had said it. Nine years old, watching five years of her own heart curl into ash, she had said *I'm just a girl*, because her mother's hand was trembling and her mother's eyes were wet and love, she understood even then, can demand terrible things of us in the shape of protection.

She had hated her mother for one full year afterward. Then she had simply grown used to the smaller life, the way a foot grows used to a shoe that doesn't fit. You stop noticing the pinch. You call the pinch *safety*.

"Astrid." Sebastian's voice, gentler than she'd ever heard it. "Where did you go?"

"To the hearth," she said. "To the night my mother burned my drawings."

"Ah." The fox settled against her knee, a warm weight. "I wondered when you'd get there."

"She wasn't wrong," Astrid said. "That's the worst part. She was trying to save me. Everything she did — the burning, the rules, the *be small, be nothing* — it worked. It kept me alive for twenty years in a valley that would have exiled me, or worse. If I'd been loud about what I was, I'd have died at twelve."

"And yet," Sebastian said.

"And yet." Astrid opened her eyes. Down in the seam of light, the pooled stars pulsed again, faster now, as if they could sense the torches on the ridge. As if they could hope. "She kept me alive, Sebastian. She kept me alive so well that I forgot I was ever meant to *live*. There's a difference. I think she knew the difference too, near the end. When she pushed me out the back gate with Ashveil bleeding in the cart — she wasn't telling me to hide anymore. She was telling me to go. I just... kept running anyway. Because running was the only thing she'd ever taught me."

The fox said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing when nothing was the answer.

Far off, across the basin, the Choir's hum deepened. Astrid felt it in her teeth, in the bones of her feet, a pressure like the whole sky leaning on her shoulders. The torches had reached the tree line. Somewhere among them, Maestro Solenne was walking — Astrid could feel her the way you feel a storm front, a vast organized hunger wrapped in silk and genuine, heartbreaking conviction. *I will end magic to end the hunger. I will devour my children to save them from devouring each other.* Even now, knowing what Solenne was, Astrid could not make herself hate her cleanly. That frightened her more than the army did.

Corin was down there somewhere. Or he wasn't. She had left him in the bell tower with the Maestro's hands closing around him, and she had told herself it was survival, cold arithmetic, one life weighed against the mission — and then she had spent eleven days not thinking about him, which is how people think about things most thoroughly. If he lived, he was a prisoner. If he'd escaped, he'd have come to her. Wouldn't he? Unless he'd decided, sensibly, that a woman who abandoned him owed him nothing, least of all her trust.

The beasts had told her otherwise, in their wordless way. The ravens that morning had brought her the smell of iron and blood and a scrap of Choir gray, torn, and the memory of a voice singing *false notes, deliberately false, poison poured into the Song from inside* — and the beasts had looked at her with that patient animal certainty that said: *he is coming. He is paying for it. Are you worth what he's paying?*

She hadn't answered them. She hadn't been able to.

Now, crouched above the buried stars with an army descending, Astrid found that the choice had quietly rearranged itself while she wasn't looking. It wasn't flee versus fight. It had never been flee versus fight.

It was: *who are you going to be?*

The girl who said *I'm just a girl*? The woman who ran because running was the only lesson she'd ever been given? Or —

"Sebastian," she said. "If I open the Hollow. If I give it up. Will you still—"

"Talk to you?" The fox flicked his ear. "No. Obviously not. Magic's magic, little speaker; it doesn't survive being spent." He paused, and his tail swept once across the stone. "But I'll stay anyway. I stayed before you could hear me, if you recall. Twenty years of sitting under tables and stealing chickens and being looked at by a small girl who thought nobody understood her. I understood her fine. I just couldn't say so." His amber eyes caught the light seeping up from below. "You gave me a voice, Astrid. Voices aren't the only way of being heard. You taught me that. Now it seems I get to teach it back."

Astrid's throat closed. She put her hand on his head, between the ears, and he allowed it with the weary tolerance of the very old and the very fond.

"If I run," she said slowly, working it through aloud, "I keep you. I keep Ashveil. I keep everything except the sky."

"Yes."

"And everyone down there—" she nodded toward the seam, toward the thousands of voices pressed thin beneath the rock, "—stays buried. And Solenne hunts me forever, and the world keeps dying by inches, and someday the Choir finds me anyway, because they always find speakers, and I die tired in a hole having saved no one."

"That is a fair summary," Sebastian said. "I'd add that the weather in the southern wilds is dreadful, but you'd only accuse me of editorializing."

"And if I stay—"

"You lose everything," the fox said simply. "The gift. My voice. Ashveil's. Every conversation you've ever treasured. And in exchange, the stars come home, the Song dies, the world gets a chance — and you get to find out whether you were ever just a girl." He tilted his head. "Terrible bargain, honestly. I'd drive a much harder deal if I were you."

Astrid laughed again, wetter this time, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

Her mother's face rose in her memory — not the terrible calm face at the hearth, but the last one, at the back gate, ash still on her sleeves, pushing the cart into the dark. *Go,* she'd mouthed. Not *hide*. Not *run*. *Go.* And Astrid had spent weeks understanding it as permission to flee before realizing, with a lurch, that it might have been something else entirely. Permission to stop hiding. Permission to be, at last, at whatever cost, the thing she actually was.

Her mother had burned the drawings to keep her small. And then, at the end, she had opened the gate to let her grow. Both things were true. Both things were love. And Astrid was done letting the first one choose for her.

She stood up.

The motion felt enormous, though it was only a girl rising from a crouch on a stone rim above a buried sky. Below her, the seam of light brightened, sensing her, reaching for her the way flowers reach for sun. The hum of the Choir crossed the basin, closer now, and threaded through it — faint, ragged, unmistakable — came another voice. Off-key. Deliberately, gloriously off-key. Poisoning the Song note by note, unraveling it from within, at a price Astrid didn't dare imagine.

Corin. Still fighting. Still paying.

"Well," she said to the fox, to the griffin wheeling down from the northern ridge with a shriek that scattered crows, to the gathered beasts emerging silent from the treeline — wolves and elk and one enormous, improbable bear, all of them looking at her with the patience of creatures who had already decided. "I suppose I should say something dramatic."

"Please don't," said Sebastian.

"I'm going to open the Hollow," Astrid said anyway, because some things deserved to be said aloud, even to an audience of one sarcastic fox. "I'm going to give them back their voices. And then I'm going to stand here, with nothing, and watch the sky fill up." She flexed her fingers. They had stopped shaking. When had they stopped shaking? "Solenne wants to consume a speaker to complete her Song. Fine. She can have one. Right up until the moment the speaker comes apart in her hands and becomes a sky instead."

Ashveil landed beside her in a rush of wind and copper scent, and the griffin's golden eye regarded her for a long moment. *You are certain,* the griffin said. Not a question, quite. A doorway held open, one last time.

"No," Astrid said honestly. "I'm terrified. I've been terrified since the night the stars went out. But my mother taught me to run, and I've finally learned the rest of the lesson, and I am *done*—" her voice cracked, steadied, "—done apologizing for what I am. Whatever's left after tonight, it won't be someone who hid."

Down in the dark, the buried stars were singing now, all of them, a sound like dawn breaking underground, and the seam of light widened at her feet as if the earth itself had heard her and agreed.

On the far ridge, the torches began to descend.

And through the Choir's great grinding hum, clear as a bell through fog, came a single voice raised in song — Corin's voice, ragged and defiant and utterly, beautifully wrong — singing the first true note the Choir had heard in a hundred years.

Astrid knelt at the edge of the Hollow, spread her hands over the light, and began to sing back.

* * *

The Maestro felt it before her cantors did — a thread of wild music rising from the basin floor, young and furious and unmistakably alive. Solenne Vael stopped mid-stride on the slope, torchlight gilding the silver in her hair, and closed her eyes as if in prayer.

"There she is," the Maestro murmured, and smiled, and gestured her cantors forward into the dark.
