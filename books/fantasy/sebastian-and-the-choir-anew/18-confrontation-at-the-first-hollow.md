## 18. Confrontation at the First Hollow

The Hollow opened beneath them like a throat full of dawn.

Astrid stood at its rim, boots planted in soil that hummed against her soles, and looked down into the pooled light of every voice the Choir had ever stolen. It was beautiful the way a fever is beautiful — too bright, too much, light that pressed against her eyes and begged to be let out. Thousands upon thousands of voices, folded small and buried deep, waiting in the dark like seeds that had forgotten which way was up.

Ashveil's wing brushed her shoulder. *They can feel you,* the griffin said. Her voice was thinner now, worn down to wire. *All of them. They know a speaker stands above them.*

"Then they know I'm not leaving," Astrid said.

She meant it to sound brave. It came out sounding tired, and she supposed that was more honest anyway. Three days of running, two nights among beasts gone half-mad with starlessness, one long descent into the earth following fox-tracks and badger-roads that no human map had ever charted — she was done being brave. She was just here now, the way a stone is here at the bottom of a river.

Below, in the bowl of the Hollow, the Choir was assembling.

They came in ranks, robed in grey that drank the light, and at their center walked Maestro Solenne like a woman descending stairs only she could see. Even at this distance Astrid could hear it — not with her ears but with the place behind her ribs where her gift lived — the Song, vast and hungry, turning over in its sleep as its mistress drew near.

And beside Solenne, walking with his hands bound in silver cord and his face bruised the color of a stormcloud, was Corin.

Her heart did something complicated and stupid. Ashveil made a low sound in her chest.

*He came back,* the griffin said. *Or he was brought. Those are not the same thing.*

"I know," Astrid said. "I know."

*Do you?*

No. She didn't. She had left him in a bell tower with the Maestro's hands closing around him, and she had run, and she had told herself it was survival, and the telling hadn't helped even a little. Now here he was, alive, captured, and she had no idea whether he was bait or traitor or something stranger — a man trying to reach her through the only road his enemies would give him.

The beasts had gathered on the ridgelines. Wolves with frost in their coats, elk with mad white-rimmed eyes gone calm under her touch, a bear the size of a haywagon who had decided, somewhere in the last day, that he belonged to her. They ringed the Hollow in silence, hundreds of them, because she had asked them to come and they had come. That was the thing about her gift that the Choir had never understood, she thought. You couldn't take it by force. It had to be given.

Solenne stopped at the center of the bowl and raised her head.

"Astrid Iversen," she called, and her voice carried up the walls of the Hollow without effort, warm as bread, reasonable as rain. "You've brought an army of animals. I've brought forty cantors and the end of hunger. Shall we discuss terms?"

* * *

Solenne had imagined this moment for thirty years, and in all her imaginings the girl had been afraid.

It was disappointing, then, to find her looking mostly exhausted, perched on the ridge with a lame-winged griffin beside her and the expression of a farmwife regarding a salesman at her door. Solenne adjusted. Thirty years of conducting the Song taught you to adjust.

"The valley wards fell," she called up, "because we needed them to fall. The stars went out because we took them. None of this was accident, child, and none of it was malice. Come down and I will show you what we are actually dying of."

The girl said nothing. But Solenne felt her listening — truly listening, the way no Choir-trained ear had listened in generations — and that, at least, was promising.

"You have seen the land beyond your valley," Solenne continued. "The blight. The beasts going silent. The crops failing. Your gift grows stronger as the world dims — did you wonder why? Because wild magic is a tide, girl, and when the sea retreats from one shore it piles up on another. The Song has been eating the tide for three hundred years. And now the Song is nearly full." She let that sit. "Nearly full, do you understand? A vessel that fills must either stop pouring or break. We cannot stop. We are the vessel. Every cantor in my choir carries a piece of the binding, and if the Song breaks, every piece tears free, and everything we swallowed comes back at once — through us. Do you know what it is to have ten thousand dead voices pour through your chest? Neither do I. I intend never to find out."

She spread her hands.

"So. One last speaker remains. One gift, freely given — or taken, though I confess I hoped for better from you — and the Song completes itself. Magic ends. Quietly. Everywhere. No more tide, no more hunger, no more beasts going mad in the dark. The world becomes ordinary, and ordinary things survive." Her voice gentled. "I am not offering you death, Astrid Iversen. I am offering you the chance to be the last wild thing, and then to be a woman, safe, in a quiet world. There are worse fates. I have read about most of them."

The girl on the ridge finally spoke, and her voice was smaller than Solenne expected, and harder.

"You keep saying 'freely given.' You chained a man to get me here."

"Necessity wears ugly clothes," Solenne said. "It always has. I won't apologize for the coat."

* * *

From below, from between the ranks of grey-robed cantors, Corin watched Astrid on the ridge and did the only useful thing left to him: he listened.

Not to Solenne. He knew Solenne's music by heart; he'd sung in her choir for eleven years. He listened instead to the ground beneath his knees, where the silver binding-cord bit into his wrists, where the Hollow's buried light pressed upward like a heartbeat through a floor.

*Here,* the light said. Or something like it. Voices didn't have words anymore, exactly, but they had shapes, and the shape pressing up through the soil was the shape of a door.

Corin Hale had spent eleven years singing the Song, and six months faking his own death, and one terrible night in a bell tower learning precisely how much of a fool he'd been. He knew the binding's architecture the way a mason knows a wall he built — including the flaw he'd never reported, the half-note in the seventh measure that he'd smoothed over rather than admit his error to the Maestro, back when he was nineteen and terrified and the error had seemed so small.

Small errors in a song three hundred years old are not small. They are doors.

The cantor nearest him shifted, humming the opening phrase under her breath, the way they all did now, the way the whole city of Vesperhold hummed it in its sleep. Corin let his lips move with hers. Let them think he was singing along, broken and compliant.

And underneath, so quietly that only the ground could hear it, he began to sing the true note.

* * *

Astrid heard it before she understood it.

It came up through her boots, through the soles of her feet, thin as a hairline crack in glass — one note, wrong, deliberate, threaded into the vast fabric of the Song like a single grey thread pulled crooked in a tapestry. And around that thread, the whole weave began very slowly to pucker.

*Corin,* she thought. *Oh, you impossible, stubborn—*

"He's poisoning it," she breathed to Ashveil. "Right in front of all of them. He's—"

*I feel it,* Ashveil said. Her feathers were lifting, one by one, like hackles. *The Song stumbles. The Maestro feels it too.*

Below, Solenne's head turned — a slow, precise rotation, a conductor locating the sour note in her orchestra. Her gaze found Corin the way a hawk finds a vole.

"There you are," she said softly. "Six months dead, and still making mistakes. I should have let Veyra finish you in the tower."

"Solenne." Corin's voice was wrecked and steady. "The seventh measure. Check the seventh measure. I got it wrong when I was nineteen, and nobody caught it, and I have been *so sorry* ever since."

For one heartbeat — one — the Maestro laughed. It was a real laugh, startled out of her, and Astrid saw in that instant the woman she might have been, some clever tired musician in another life who ran a tavern and argued about harmony.

Then the laugh closed like a door, and Solenne sang.

* * *

She sang the counter-binding, the great flat chord that had silenced the world once already, and Astrid felt it come for her like weather — pressure, weight, the taste of iron. The beasts on the ridgeline flinched as one. The bear roared and the wolves dropped their bellies to the stone, and Astrid stood in the middle of it with her gift rising in her throat like a scream, and she did the only thing she knew how to do.

She answered in silence.

Not absence-of-sound. Silence-with-shape — the hush of a den at dusk, the held breath of a doe, the pause between a mother-bird and her fledgling when no words are needed because love doesn't use any. She poured it down the slope of the Hollow, and where it met the Song the two forces ground against each other like millstones, and the air between them began to shine.

Solenne advanced, singing. Astrid retreated, silencing. Step by step they moved across the bowl of the Hollow, and beneath their feet the pooled light of ten thousand stolen voices churned like a sea in a storm, rising, pressing, wanting out.

"You could stop this," Solenne said between phrases, and her voice was almost kind, and that was the worst part. "Look at yourself. You're stronger than any speaker we ever took. Stronger than the ones who built the first binding. Do you know what that means? It means you're not a footnote, girl. You're a *chapter*. Give me your hand instead of your grief and we will rule the quiet together — you and I, the two last musicians of the old world. Keep your gift. Keep it whole. Share mine. The Song needs two voices at its crown, and I am so *tired*, Astrid. Three hundred years of conductors, and I am the last, and I am so tired of holding this alone."

And there it was. The offer, naked at last, gleaming under the ruined sky.

Power shared. Power kept. Everything she was, preserved — crowned, even. All she had to do was help finish the cage.

Astrid thought of her mother feeding the drawings to the fire, page by page, and the look on her face — terror wearing love's clothing. She thought of Sebastian's lame foot tapping against her palm. She thought of Ashveil falling out of a starless sky into her family's barn, bleeding, asking for help in words only she could hear.

Every good thing in her life had come from listening. Every terrible thing had come from someone deciding that some voices were worth less than others.

"No," Astrid said.

Solenne's eyes narrowed. "Think carefully—"

"I did. For twenty-two years, actually, mostly in the dark, talking to animals nobody believed existed." Her gift rose in her, huge and calm, bigger than fear. "You want to complete the Song. You want the world quiet and safe and *yours*. But those aren't stars up there you put out, Maestro. They're people. They're *voices*. And I'm not going to help you bury them deeper, and I'm not going to take your hand, and I'm not going to rule anything." She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, but it was a true one. "I'm going to open the door."

Beneath them, the ground split with light.

* * *

Corin felt the binding fail before he heard it — felt the seventh measure buckle, the half-note he'd hidden for fifteen years finally doing its work, the great chord tearing along a seam no one had ever inspected because no one had ever believed a nineteen-year-old's mistake mattered.

The silver cords on his wrists went slack. The cantors staggered, clutching their throats, as the Song they had carried since childhood suddenly weighed what it actually weighed.

And Astrid stepped to the edge of the opening Hollow, arms spread, gift blazing, and began — not to fight, not to flee, but to *give*.

Solenne lunged for her, singing, desperate, magnificent, and the last duel of the old world rang out over the First Hollow: one woman offering power, one woman refusing it, and between them the buried light of ten thousand stolen voices rising at last toward a sky that had been dark far too long.

The first star came back over the eastern ridge.

Ashveil spread her broken wings and leapt to meet it.

* * *

The light took Ashveil the way spring takes a river — all at once, and gently, and forever.

Astrid saw it happen and would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it. The griffin rose into the column of returning voices, and the voices recognized her — the first of them, the oldest prisoner, the one who had carried word of the Hollow through three hundred years of darkness — and they gathered around her like a family around a table. Her wounds closed in light. Her lame wing straightened. And then she simply came apart, feather by golden feather, each one kindling into a fixed and burning point, until where a dying griffin had been there hung instead a great sweeping curve of new-made stars, wings outstretched across the northern sky.

The Griffin. Children would someday trace her shape with cold fingers and make up stories about her. Astrid would tell them the true one, eventually. Not yet. Not tonight.

Because the rest of the voices were still rising, thousands of them, streaming past her like migrating birds, and the Choir's Song was unraveling overhead into — of all things — music. Just music. Ordinary chords, unbound, harmless, drifting down over the stunned grey ranks of cantors who were hearing their own art for the first time without teeth in it. Some of them knelt. Some of them wept. One began, hesitantly, to harmonize.

And Maestro Solenne stood alone in the center of the emptying Hollow, her great work dissolving around her, her offered hand still half-extended toward a girl who had refused it.

"It didn't have to end this way," Solenne said quietly.

"No," Astrid agreed. "It ended the way it began. With somebody choosing."

The last of the stolen voices poured past them both and up, and up, and the sky filled like a cup, and Astrid felt her gift go with them — streaming out through her open hands, spending itself coin by coin, voice by voice, until the silence inside her was not emptiness but room.

The last star found its place. The sky was whole.

And somewhere in the crowd of kneeling cantors, a half-deaf man with ruined wrists started, unsteadily, to walk toward her.
