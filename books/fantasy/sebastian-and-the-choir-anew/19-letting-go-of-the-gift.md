## 19. Letting Go of the Gift

The First Hollow did not look like the end of the world. It looked like a bowl of grass and grey stone, ringed by hills that had once been green and were now the color of old ash, with a pool at its center that glowed faintly through the earth itself — as if someone had buried a lantern and forgotten to blow it out.

Astrid stood at the rim of that glow and felt her gift lean toward it like a dog toward an open door.

*There,* said every beast she had ever spoken to, all their voices braided together in her chest. *There. There. There.*

"Yes," she said aloud. "I heard you the first eleven times."

Ashveil laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like wind through a broken shutter. The griffin lay on the grass nearby, wings spread in the dirt, golden feathers gone dull as tarnished coins. She had not flown since the battle below the pass, and they both knew she would not fly again.

"You're stalling," Ashveil said.

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference. Stalling is when you're afraid. Savoring is when you're afraid but dignified about it."

"Ah. My mistake."

Below them, at the pool's edge, Corin knelt in the churned mud with his hands pressed flat to the ground. He was singing — or trying to. His voice had been ruined in the Choir's keeping; what came out of him now was a threadbare scrap of melody, cracked and wavering, and yet where it touched the earth, the buried light stirred. The binding he had spent his whole life weaving was coming apart under his fingers, note by poisoned note, and it was costing him everything he had left to give.

He had crawled out of the burning wreckage of the Choir's line half an hour ago, half-deaf, bleeding from one ear, and the first thing he'd done was look at Astrid — really look, the way you look at something you're certain will be taken from you — and say, "You don't have to do this. There's another way. I'll find another way."

There wasn't another way. They both knew that too. But she had appreciated the lie. It had been a very kind lie, and kindness was in short supply.

She crouched beside him now. "How long?"

"As long as I can." He didn't look up. "The Song's still fighting me. It knows what I'm doing. It's — " he winced — "it's singing back."

"What's it saying?"

"Nothing I want to repeat." A ghost of his old dry humor flickered across his face. "Mostly it's calling me names. It's very creative. Centuries of practice."

Above them, on the ridgeline, the last of the beast-allies held their ground against the scattered remnants of the Choir's hunters — wolves and bears and one enormous, improbable elk who had taken personal offense to the entire concept of mages. The fighting was thinning. Not ending. Thinning, like cream in water. Somewhere up there, Solenne was regrouping, and everyone in the Hollow could feel it: the pause before the final verse.

Astrid walked down to the pool.

Up close, the buried light was not light at all. It was sound. She could see it moving under the soil like fish under ice — thousands upon thousands of small bright voices, circling, circling, waiting. Every speaker the Choir had ever consumed. Every wild song they had swallowed to feed their own. Generations of them, pressed down here into the dark, still singing to each other because there was nothing else left to do.

Her eyes burned. "Hello," she whispered.

The voices went still. Then, tentatively, one rose toward the surface — a small voice, high and young, the voice of someone who had died centuries before Astrid's grandmother was born — and brushed against her gift like a child touching a stranger's hand.

*Are you real?*

"I'm real," Astrid said, and her voice broke on it. "I'm so sorry. I'm so late."

*Is it over?*

"It's about to be."

She sat down at the edge of the glow, cross-legged, the way she used to sit in the valley with Sebastian the fox, back when her biggest problem was hiding her gift from neighbors who would have burned her for it. Back when she'd thought belonging was something other people got to have.

"You don't have to watch," she said to Ashveil, without turning around.

"Don't be absurd." The griffin dragged herself closer, feathers whispering over the grass. "I've carried you through collapsing passes and feral country and one truly humiliating incident involving a river and a moose. I'm not missing the ending."

"The moose started it."

"The moose absolutely did not start it."

Astrid smiled. Then she closed her eyes and reached for her gift.

It came eagerly. That was the terrible part — how eager it was. All her life it had been a thing she hid, smothered, apologized for, and now, when she finally opened her hands to it, it poured toward her like it had only ever been waiting for permission. Every conversation she'd ever had with a beast, every secret shared with foxes and ravens and one memorable badger, every moment she'd spent being the strange girl who talked to animals — it was all there, warm and vast and *hers*, and she was about to give it away.

*You can stop,* the gift seemed to say, in no voice at all. *This is your choice.*

"I know," she said.

She thought of her mother, feeding drawings of talking animals into the fire, tears streaming down a face that never once asked forgiveness. She thought of the valley, and the wards, and the neighbors who had exiled speakers before Astrid was born and would have exiled her too, if they'd known. She thought of Solenne's offer, three days ago, delivered in that beautiful reasonable voice: *Share it with me. Keep your gift. Rule beside me. Why should sacrifice be the only door?*

Because power kept was power corrupted. Because the Choir had started exactly this way — good people refusing to let go.

And because somewhere under this ash-grey sky were thousands of stolen voices who had been waiting in the dark for longer than kingdoms had existed, and somebody owed them the sky.

"Corin," she called. "Whenever you're ready."

His cracked voice lifted. And Astrid opened herself completely, and poured.

It did not hurt. That surprised her most. She had braced for pain, for tearing, for the ripping-out feeling the beasts had described when the Choir took speakers by force — but this was nothing like force. This was like exhaling after holding your breath for twenty-five years. Her gift flowed out of her and down into the buried light, and the buried light *recognized* it, the way one flame recognizes another, and —

The Hollow sang.

Not the Choir's Song — nothing like it. This was ragged and plural and gloriously unharmonized, ten thousand voices rising at once, each one its own tune, colliding and tangling and laughing. The ground shook. The pool of buried light surged upward through the soil like spring floodwater, and Astrid felt her gift go with it, dissolving into the crowd of voices until she could no longer tell which threads had been hers.

For one vertiginous moment she was everywhere. She was the small voice asking *are you real*. She was a shepherd-speaker from the founding wars. She was a woman who had sung whales out of storms, a boy who had argued politics with crows, a hundred generations of people who had loved the wild things enough to be destroyed for it. She held all of them and all of them held her, and then —

They let go of her, gently, the way you set down something precious, and rose.

The first star ignited directly overhead. It bloomed out of nothing — a white point of fire punching through the starless dark — and the sound it made was a single clear note, and Astrid wept without noticing she was doing it.

Then a second star. A third. A constellation unfolding like a hand opening, and another, and another, voices pouring up out of the Hollow and into the sky, each one taking its ancient place, and the land below began to *breathe*. Astrid felt it through her bare feet even after her gift was gone — or maybe that was just the ground shaking, maybe it was just physics, but the hills shuddered and the dead grass trembled and somewhere far off, impossibly, a wolf began to howl, and it was not a mad howl. It was a *joyful* one.

In the sky above Vesperhold, far to the south, the Choir's great Song must have been screaming. Astrid couldn't hear it. What she heard instead, rolling across the hills like weather, was the Song collapsing — unraveling into its component parts, the stolen notes going home, until what remained was just music. Ordinary music. Human music, mage-made, humble, imperfect, sung by frightened people in a city of bells who would wake tomorrow and find the bells were only bells.

She hoped some of them would dance anyway.

"Astrid." Ashveil's voice. Very quiet.

Astrid turned. The griffin had risen to her feet — risen, somehow, though her legs shook and her wings dragged — and she was looking up at the new stars with her fierce gold eyes full of something Astrid had never seen in them before.

"Ashveil?"

"There's a place," Ashveil said. "At the front of the rising. The first voice led them out, you understand. Someone has to lead them home." She looked at Astrid, and her gaze was steady and unbearably fond. "I'm the oldest voice here. Older than the Choir. Older than the war. I've been dying since the pass, little speaker, and I would rather spend my last breath flying than lying in the dirt watching other people's stars."

"No," Astrid said. It came out very small. "No, wait —"

But Ashveil was already gathering herself, and the rising voices were gathering around her, thousands of them swirling like snow in wind, and the griffin spread her ruined wings and — impossibly, beautifully — they held. Light poured along the dull feathers and made them gold again, brighter than she had ever been in life, and Ashveil leapt.

She flew straight up into the falling dark, trailing voices behind her like a comet's tail, and Astrid ran beneath her — ran stumbling and sobbing across the Hollow, shouting words she couldn't have repeated if you paid her — and Ashveil banked once, low, close enough that Astrid saw her eye, saw the wink in it, heard the last thing she ever said:

"*Look up, little speaker.*"

Then she climbed, and climbed, and at the top of the sky she stopped, and spread her wings wide, and became light.

The first new constellation burned above the northern hills: a griffin in flight, wings unfurled, forever ascending. Around her, the other stars settled into their places, and the sky filled, and filled, and filled, until the darkness that had eaten the world for a generation was stitched through with more stars than any living person remembered existing.

Astrid stood alone in the middle of the Hollow, empty-handed, empty-hearted, empty-gifted, and looked up at her friend.

The silence where her gift had been was enormous. All her life there had been a second language running underneath her words, a constant low murmur of beast-thoughts, and now there was nothing. Just her own voice, and the wind, and the distant confused bleating of sheep who had woken up in a starred world and found it very strange indeed.

She laughed. It came out wet and cracked and slightly unhinged, and she decided she didn't care.

"He'd have hated it," she said to the griffin-star. "Sebastian. All those years telling me stars were just holes where the night leaked. Now he'll have to live under a sky full of evidence."

The star didn't answer. Stars, it turned out, were terrible conversationalists. She'd have to get used to that.

Footsteps behind her — uneven, exhausted. Corin came down the slope with one hand pressed to his bleeding ear, and stopped a few paces away, and looked up at the sky for a long time without saying anything. When he finally spoke, his voice was hoarse as an old rope.

"It's quiet," he said. "In my head. The Song's gone. It's just — quiet." He sounded like a man describing the ocean to someone who'd lived his whole life underwater. "I'd forgotten what quiet was."

"How's the ear?"

"Gone, probably. The other one's fine." He hesitated. "Astrid. Your gift —"

"Is in the sky." She gestured upward, at the several thousand stars. "All of it. I checked. There's nothing left down here but me." She flexed her fingers experimentally, half-hoping, half-dreading. Nothing stirred. A crow perched on a stone nearby regarded her with perfect, ordinary bird indifference. "Do you know, I think that crow is thinking about worms. Actually thinking about worms. Not plotting anything. Not judging me. Just — worms."

"That sounds restful."

"It sounds *lonely*," she said, and then, surprising herself, she laughed again. "It also sounds like everyone else's whole life. Imagine that. Everyone else has been living like this the entire time."

Corin looked at her — at the tear-streaks and the mud and the empty hands — and whatever he saw made him look away quickly, jaw tight. "You gave up everything," he said. "And I gave up an ear. I'm aware of the accounting."

"Stop it."

"You should hate me. Half of what happened to you traces back to —"

"*Stop it*, Corin." She said it tiredly, without heat. "We're alive. They're free. The sky works again. Tomorrow there will be plenty of time for guilt — I've been told it keeps. Tonight I intend to sit on this hill, look at my friend, and refuse to be tragic."

From the ridge above, the elk bellowed something triumphant and possibly insulting. Down in the Hollow, the last of the buried light sank peacefully into the earth, its work done, leaving only ordinary soil and extraordinary grass that was already, visibly, turning green at the roots.

And far to the south, over the city of Vesperhold, the great bells began to ring — not the Song, not the binding, just bells, clanging wildly, joyfully, out of tune, as the people of the capital woke beneath a starred sky for the first time in their lives and discovered they had absolutely no idea what to do about it.

Corin tilted his head, listening with his one working ear. "Some of them are dancing," he reported. "I can hear it from here. Badly."

"Good," said Astrid. She settled into the grass, pulled her cloak around her shoulders, and looked up at the griffin-constellation until her eyes grew heavy. "Let them practice."

She slept that night under a thousand voices, dreamless and light, and woke at dawn to find Corin standing at the edge of camp, packed and saddled and clearly, awkwardly, preparing to leave without saying goodbye.

"Oh no you don't," she said, sitting up. "First rule of surviving an apocalypse: nobody sneaks off before breakfast."
