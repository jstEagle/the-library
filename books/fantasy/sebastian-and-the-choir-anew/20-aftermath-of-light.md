## 20. Aftermath of Light

The first thing Corin Hale understood about silence was that it had a texture.

For thirty years he had lived inside the Song — wrapped in it, sustained by it, suffocated by it. The Choir's great binding-voice had been the constant weather of his life, a pressure behind the ears and beneath the breastbone, so familiar that he had stopped hearing it the way a fish stops noticing water. Now the water was gone, and he stood gasping in air that rang.

Because it did ring. That was the joke of it, the cruel, beautiful joke: when the Song died, it did not go quietly. It came apart all at once above the First Hollow, every stolen voice tearing free of the binding at the same instant, and the sound of thirty years of theft unspooling into freedom had gone through him like a bell struck by God. He had been standing forty paces from Astrid Iversen when she poured herself out into the sky. He had felt the last note pass through his skull and take something with it on the way out.

Something small, as it turned out. Something he could live without. But not nothing.

He could no longer hear the upper registers. Birdsong arrived muffled, as if the birds were singing through wool. Solenne's voice — when she had still been speaking, before the end — had sounded to him like someone calling up from the bottom of a well. The healers among the beast-folk who picked their way through the battlefield afterward, tending broken griffins and broken men with equal indifference, said the damage might mend. They said it in the tone people used for things they did not believe.

Corin found he didn't much mind. Half-deafness seemed a fair tariff for what he'd done, and cheap besides.

* * *

The Choir dissolved the way sugar dissolves in rain.

There was no battle after the stars returned. There could not be. Every cantor in Vesperhold had felt the Song collapse through the marks burned into their throats — Corin knew exactly what that moment had been like, because he had engineered a smaller version of it in himself weeks before, singing false notes into the network until the binding frayed around him like rotten rope. For the cantors, the death of the Song was the death of the thing that fed them. He had watched men and women who could level mountains stand in the mud of the Hollow and simply... stop.

Some knelt. That surprised him more than anything else that terrible night. He had expected rage, or flight, or the feral desperation of starving animals — and there was some of that, certainly; three cantors went mad outright and had to be restrained by beasts twice their size, which Corin privately thought served them right. But a dozen others, maybe more, had looked up at the sky filling with returning stars — voices rising out of the earth like lanterns let go of — and dropped to their knees in the churned mud and wept.

He recognized the weeping. It was the same shame he carried. The stars overhead were not new stars. They were old ones, restored — and if you had any part in the Choir, you knew what that restoration meant. Every point of light climbing back into heaven was a speaker the Choir had eaten. A child taken from a valley. A grandmother dragged from her garden. Generations of them, sung down and swallowed and hung in the dark as fuel.

Now they were free, and they were *looking down*, and there was nowhere in the world to hide from that particular gaze.

One of the kneeling cantors was Maestra Odile Ferran, who had taught Corin counterpoint when he was nine years old and had once, gently, corrected his hand position on the bell-ropes with a touch light as moth wings. She knelt in the mud with her silver-grey braids coming loose and her hands pressed flat to the earth, and when Corin passed near her she looked up at him with an expression he would carry longer than the deafness.

"You knew," she said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — wondering. "You knew what we were feeding on, and you stayed."

"I stayed because leaving meant dying," Corin said. "I left eventually. Neither fact redeems me."

"No," she agreed, and bowed her head, and began, very softly, to sing — not the Song, never the Song again, but something small and plain, a lullaby from the northern provinces that predated the Choir by a thousand years. A song with no power in it at all except the power of being sung. Other kneeling cantors took it up, haltingly, like people remembering how to walk.

Corin walked away before it undid him entirely.

* * *

He found Astrid at dawn, sitting on the rim of the Hollow with a fox in her lap.

This required some unpacking, even now. The fox — Sebastian, though Corin had learned the name only recently and still found it faintly absurd — was lame in one leg and opinionated in all of them, and had apparently been Astrid's first friend and last secret back in the valley where she'd grown up. During the battle the creature had fought like something out of an old soldier's nightmare, darting between the legs of Choir hunters and hamstringing them with a efficiency that suggested either long practice or deep grievance. Corin suspected both.

Astrid sat with her arms around the animal and her face tipped up toward the sky, which was doing something no living person had ever seen it do. The stars were still arriving. All night they had come, in ones and twos and then in rivers, climbing out of the Hollow's pooled light and taking their places in the dark like guests finding seats at a wedding. And woven among them, larger than the rest and burning a soft ash-grey, was a shape Corin had watched form over the course of four hours with a grief in his chest that had no bottom: a griffin, wings spread, head high, flying forever across the northern sky.

Ashveil. Who had carried Astrid out of a burning valley and named her *speaker* with his last strength, and who had chosen, at the end, to become the thing he had always flown beneath.

"He'd have hated this," Astrid said, without turning around. Her voice was different now. Flat in one way and rich in another — the gift was gone from it, spent entirely, and what remained was just a young woman's voice, tired and cracked and human. "Being looked at. He was very dignified."

"Most constellations are," Corin said. "It's practically a job requirement."

That earned him half a smile. He counted it, privately, as one of the significant achievements of his life.

He sat down beside her, at a careful distance — close enough for conversation, far enough that she could pretend he wasn't there if she wanted. He had gotten good at that distance over the months of their uneasy alliance. It was, he suspected, the only kind of closeness he'd earned from her, and he intended to respect its borders the way other men respected property lines.

Below them, the beast-folk and the freed speakers' descendants and even a few surrendered cantors were working to seal the Hollow — not to imprison the light again, but to give it a proper door, a place where pilgrims could come and sit with the rising stars and remember. It was slow, muddy, unglamorous work. Someone was arguing cheerfully about drainage. The world, Corin thought, was already forgetting how to end itself, which was the most hopeful thing he'd witnessed in decades.

"I owe you an accounting," he said.

Astrid's shoulders tensed. "If this is going to be a speech—"

"It is absolutely going to be a speech. I've been composing it since the battle. You're obligated by the laws of hospitality to hear at least the first draft."

The fox made a sound that was unmistakably a snort. Corin chose to ignore it.

"Here is the accounting," he said. "I joined the Choir at six years old because they found me in a famine winter and fed me, and I told myself for twenty-five years that gratitude covered it. I helped bind speakers. I trained hunters. I wrote two of the pursuit-liturgies they used to track you across half the country — you were hunted, in part, by music I composed, which I think deserves its own special circle somewhere." He kept his eyes on the horizon, on the grey line where the stars were fading into morning. "And then I faked my death and followed you, and everyone in the Choir assumed I wanted your gift for myself, and the truth is I don't fully know why I did it. I've had a great deal of time in various cells to think about it, and the best I can construct is this: I heard you speak to a horse once, through a wall, in a market town, and the horse answered you like you were the first kind word it had ever received. And I realized I had spent my whole life inside an organization dedicated to making sure no such conversation ever happened again. And I could not bear it. That's the whole confession. Not principle. Not courage. I just couldn't bear it."

Silence, except for the distant argument about drainage and the wind moving through the grass.

"Say something cruel," Corin said. "It'll help. I've found cruelty very clarifying lately."

"You want me to absolve you by insulting you."

"I want you to do whatever costs you least. You've paid enough."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way people are careful around wounds — his, she seemed to understand, and not only hers.

"My mother burned my drawings," she said. "Every picture I ever made of a talking animal. She stood in the yard and fed them to the fire while I watched, and she told me it was to keep me safe, and she was right, and I have spent every day since trying to decide whether love that protects you by breaking something in you is still love." She turned and looked at him directly, and her eyes were the same steady grey as the new constellation. "So no. I'm not going to say something cruel. I know exactly what it costs to be loved badly by people who mean well. But I'm also not going to tell you it's fine, because it isn't fine, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of lie, and I've had my fill of those."

"That," Corin said, "is the most generous cruelty I've ever received."

"I'm told I have a talent."

* * *

The delegations started arriving within the week, and Corin discovered that the death of the Song had created a problem no one had anticipated: somebody had to decide what happened next, and nobody wanted the job.

Kings sent envoys. The beast-folk sent elders who communicated mostly in pointed silences. The kneeling cantors organized themselves, with the grim competence of people who had run an empire and didn't know how to stop, into something they insisted on calling a Reconciliation Chorus, which Corin thought was the single worst name he had ever heard and said so, repeatedly, until a young maestra threatened to have him removed from the tent.

Which raised the question of what, precisely, Corin Hale was to be removed *from*, because everyone kept assuming he would stay.

It was Odile Ferran who put it to him plainly, on the fourth day, catching him at the edge of camp where he went each evening to listen — as best he could — to the ordinary sounds of an ordinary evening. Cookfires. Horses. Someone playing a fiddle badly, which he could only hear as a warm blur, but a warm blur was more than he'd had in thirty years.

"The Reconciliation needs experienced hands," she said. "People who understand the binding-work well enough to undo what remains of it. There are sealed archives in Vesperhold, resonance-chains in six provinces, pockets of the old Song still caught in mountain stone. You know these systems better than anyone alive. You could have any title you asked for. First Cantor. Warden of Voices. They'd carve your name in the capital."

"And my name in the capital would sit under a sky full of the people we ate," Corin said.

Odile flinched. Good, he thought. Let it cost her too.

"I am going to tell you something I have never told anyone," he continued, "which is that I liked the power. I want that on the record, witnessed, before you start building monuments. I liked walking into a room and having the Song announce me. I liked that my voice could open doors and close them on people. If I stay — if I take a title, if I help rebuild — I will spend ten years being indispensable and twenty years being tempted, and somewhere around year fifteen I will find a reason why just a little binding, just a small Song, just this once, is necessary. I know the architecture of my own soul, Maestra. It has excellent acoustics and no load-bearing conscience."

"So you'll leave."

"So I'll leave." He smiled, and found it hurt less than expected. "Exile. Proper exile. Somewhere with bad roads and no bells. I'm told the southern coast has fish and no interest whatsoever in governance."

Odile studied him for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody.

"You know," she said, "you were the most talented student I ever had, and the most miserable. I used to tell the other teachers you sang like someone apologizing."

"I was."

"Yes," she said. "Well. Go apologize somewhere warm." And she touched his shoulder, once, lightly, the way she had corrected his hand on the bell-ropes thirty years ago, and walked back toward the tents and the terrible name of the Reconciliation Chorus.

* * *

He left on a grey morning, on a borrowed mule, with a pack containing one change of clothes, a book of northern lullabies he had rescued from the Archive fires, and nothing else worth mentioning.

Astrid met him at the crossroads south of the Hollow, which he suspected was not a coincidence, because the fox was with her, and the fox went where it pleased and brought her along as a courtesy.

"You're really going," she said. "Just like that. No farewell banquet?"

"I attended a Choir investiture ceremony once. Fourteen hours. There was a movement for solo throat-singing that lasted ninety minutes and represented, I can only assume, someone's revenge on humanity. I've sworn off banquets on religious grounds."

"What religion?"

"The Church of Never Again, newly founded, congregation of one."

Sebastian made a chuffing noise that Corin had learned to translate as *he thinks he's funny*, delivered with the weary tolerance of a creature who had heard considerably better material. Astrid scratched the fox's ears and almost smiled.

Then her face settled into seriousness, and Corin braced himself, because he had learned that Astrid Iversen's seriousness was not a weapon but a weight, and it always landed honestly.

"Come to the valley," she said. "When you find your place on the coast, or wherever you end up — come north first. My mother will want—" She stopped. Started again. "My mother owes you. We both do. And the valley's rebuilding the wards themselves now, all of us, neighbors who never touched magic in their lives, learning it from scratch because the last speaker gave hers away. It's clumsy. It's slow. Marta Iversen holds the eastern stones and swears at them constantly, and yesterday the whole western line collapsed into a hedge, and everyone laughed, and then they rebuilt it." She looked up at the pale morning sky, at the grey star still visible near the horizon, stubborn against the daylight. "You could see it. What comes after. What it looks like when people fix what they broke instead of hiding it."

Corin stood very still.

Every instinct he had — the old instincts, the Choir instincts, the ones that measured every kindness for hooks and every invitation for price — went looking for the trap in her words and found none. That was the thing about Astrid that had undone him from the beginning, back in a market town, through a wall, with a horse: she meant what she said. She had spent her whole life being unseen, and so she saw other people, completely, including their worst rooms, and offered them entrance anyway.

"I owe you everything," he said. "You know that. Everything I have, everything I am—"

"You owe me nothing," Astrid said, "and that's precisely why I'm asking. If you came to repay a debt, you'd spend the whole visit calculating. Come instead as a man with no debts, eating soup, swearing at hedges. It's very restorative. Marta swears beautifully. You'd learn things."

And Corin Hale — former cantor of the Choir, architect of pursuit-liturgies, author of his own damnation and his own narrow salvation — laughed. Really laughed, loud and helpless, for the first time since he was a boy, and did not care that he couldn't quite hear the sound of it, because he could feel it in his chest, and his chest had been silent long enough.

"All right," he said. "North first. Soup and hedges. But I warn you, I hold grudges against livestock, and your valley is full of animals who remember me from the pursuit."

"They remember," Astrid agreed gravely. "The goats especially. You'll want to make offerings."

"What sort of offerings?"

"Hay. Apologies. Hay *with* apologies."

The mule shifted. The fox yawned, displaying an alarming number of teeth, and set off down the south road at a deliberate limp, glancing back once with an expression of profound expectation, as if to say that the world had been remade overnight and everyone was dawdling.

Corin swung up onto the mule and fell in beside Astrid, and together they rode north — away from the capital, away from titles, away from the carved names and the resonant halls — toward a valley full of clumsy wards and swearing mothers and animals who held grudges, where a man with no debts and half his hearing might, at last, learn to sing like someone who had nothing left to apologize for.

Behind them, the stars faded gently into morning, still there, still watching, still free.
