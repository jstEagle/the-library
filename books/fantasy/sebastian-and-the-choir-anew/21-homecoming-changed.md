## 21. Homecoming, Changed

The valley looked smaller from the pass.

Astrid had known it would — everything did, once you'd carried it on your back across half a broken country — but knowing did nothing to soften the ache of it. The houses huddled along the river like sheep in bad weather. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, obedient lines. The terraced fields were green again, greener than she remembered them being even before the stars went out, and above it all the sky wheeled with stars — real stars, thousands of them, burning like a city that had finally turned its lamps back on.

She stood at the mouth of the pass for a long time, her pack straps biting into shoulders that had grown too thin, and let herself look.

There was no fox beside her. There was no griffin's shadow crossing the moon. There was no whisper of beasts at the edge of her hearing, no low murmur of the world talking to itself, which had been the background music of her entire life and was now simply — gone. She had poured it all into the Hollow. Every drop. She had done it kneeling in pooled starlight with the Maestro's song tearing at her ears and Corin bleeding below her, and she would do it again, and she had told herself on the long road home that this was not grief, because you could not grieve a thing you had given away freely.

She had told herself that for six hundred miles.

It was mostly true now. Mostly.

"Come on," she said aloud, to no one, because there was no one to hear her, and that was still strange enough to make her laugh — a small, cracked sound that echoed off the rocks and startled a marmot out of its burrow. The marmot chittered at her indignantly and did not say a single word, and Astrid wiped her eyes and said, "Yes, all right, I'm going," and walked down into the valley that had exiled her grandmother's grandmother and everyone like her since before anyone could remember why.

* * *

The wards stopped her at the tree line.

That was the first surprise. She felt them the way you feel a change in temperature — a faint pressure, a hum against her skin that was not magic, because she had none, but something older and simpler: the body remembering what it used to lean against. She put out one hand and touched air that pushed gently back, like a dog leaning into a stranger to decide about them.

Someone had rebuilt them. Not the old wards — those had been woven by speakers, generations ago, and had failed the night the stars went out with a sound like breaking glass that Astrid still heard sometimes when she was very tired. These were different. Cruder. She could see the knots of them now that she knew to look: bundles of rowan tied with red thread at intervals along the boundary stones, braided horsehair, little clay bells that held no enchantment whatsoever but swung solemnly in the evening breeze as if they believed in themselves.

Human wards. Neighbor-wards. The kind of thing people built when they had no magic left in the whole wide world and decided to build something anyway.

"They don't keep anything out," said a voice behind her. "We're not fools. But they mark where we begin."

Astrid turned. Old Marta Voss stood ten paces off with a lantern in one hand and a walking stick in the other, wrapped in so many shawls she resembled a respectable haystack. Marta had taught half the valley's children their letters, including Astrid, and had never once in Astrid's memory looked directly at her for longer than a sentence required.

She was looking at her now.

"You came back," Marta said.

"I came back."

"We heard things." The lantern light caught the deep creases around the old woman's mouth. "Riders in the passes. Then the sky catching fire — well. Lighting up. Halvard swears he saw a griffin made of stars fly over the western ridge, and Halvard also swears he once wrestled a bear, so." She paused. "But then the stars stayed. So perhaps."

"The griffin was real," Astrid said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Her name was Ashveil."

Marta nodded slowly, as though this confirmed some private ledger. Then she held out her free hand — not to shake, but palm up, open, the way you offer your hand to a bird or a frightened child — and waited.

Astrid took it. Her hand was shaking. Marta's was not.

"Your mother's been lighting a lamp in the west window every night since midwinter," Marta said. "Every single night, that stubborn woman. Half the valley thought she'd lost her wits. The other half lit theirs too, after a while, so that from the ridge it looked like—" She stopped, cleared her throat, and became briskly practical, which in Marta Voss was the closest thing to tenderness. "Well. Come down before you fall down. And give me that pack; you're carrying it like it owes you money."

* * *

The walk through the village took less time than Astrid dreaded and more time than she expected.

People came out of their houses. That was the thing she had braced herself for and still wasn't ready for — doors opening, lamplight spilling onto packed earth, faces she had known her whole life looking at her without the careful blankness she remembered. The blankness had been the valley's armor for three generations: the polite nothing that said *we do not see what you are*. Now there was no armor. There was just Tomas the miller standing in his doorway with flour to his elbows, and his wife behind him holding their youngest, and the boy wriggling free and running straight at Astrid with his arms out before anyone could stop him.

She caught him. He weighed almost nothing and smelled of woodsmoke and porridge.

"Did you really talk to animals?" he demanded, at close range, with the total fearlessness of the very young.

"I did," Astrid said. "Not anymore."

"Why not?"

"I gave it away. To fix the sky."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Was it worth it?"

Above them, unobstructed by any cloud, the stars burned in their thousands — and among them, low in the southwest where no constellation had ever hung before, a new figure spread its wings across the dark: a great four-pointed shape that the star-charts hadn't named yet but that every child in the valley already called the Griffin.

"Yeah," Astrid said. "It was worth it."

The boy nodded, satisfied, and ran back to his mother, and Astrid stood in the lane with her arms still curved around the ghost of his weight while more doors opened and more neighbors came out, and nobody said *speaker*, and nobody said *abomination*, and old Halvard — who had definitely never wrestled a bear — raised his mug to her from across the fence and shouted, "About time you came home, girl, your mother's garden is a disgrace without you."

It should have been enough. It was more than she had ever let herself want.

And yet her feet kept moving past all of them, up the last rise, toward the farmhouse with the lamp burning in the west window, because there was one door left and it was the only one that mattered.

* * *

Her mother didn't come out to meet her.

Astrid hadn't expected her to. Greta Iversen met avalanches and blizzards and the death of her husband the same way: by standing exactly where she was and refusing to be moved until the emergency agreed to come to her. So Astrid opened the gate — the hinge still needed oiling, some things were eternal — and walked up the path between the winter-bare rose bushes, and the door opened before she reached it, and her mother filled the doorway with the lamp behind her so that Astrid couldn't read her face.

For a moment neither of them moved.

Then Greta Iversen crossed her arms, looked her daughter up and down with the expression she reserved for livestock bought at a bad price, and said:

"You're too thin."

"I—"

"And your boots are ruined. Don't tell me how much a cobbler will charge, I don't want to know." She stepped aside. "Supper's cold. It'll take an hour to heat. Sit down and stop dripping snow on my floor."

It was so perfectly, so devastatingly her mother that Astrid laughed — really laughed, helplessly, standing in the doorway with snow melting off her ruined boots — and Greta's stern face cracked down the middle, and then Astrid was inside and her mother's arms were around her and the lamp was swinging wildly overhead, throwing their joined shadow huge against the wall, and Greta Iversen was crying into her daughter's hair without making a single sound, the way she did everything: thoroughly, silently, and as though it were a chore she intended to see through to the end.

They stood there a long time. The soup could wait. The soup had waited months; it could wait an hour.

When they finally separated, her mother held her at arm's length and studied her face with a ferocious attention, cataloguing damage. Astrid let her. This, too, was a language they had always spoken.

"I burned your drawings," Greta said abruptly.

"I know. I watched you do it."

"I knew what you were since you were four years old. You told the chickens when to lay and they listened, Astrid. Chickens don't listen to anyone." Her jaw worked. "I burned them because I was afraid that if I let you keep them, someone else would find them, and then it wouldn't be drawings, it would be evidence. And I never told you I knew, because telling you meant admitting the valley was right to be afraid, and I couldn't—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I chose your safety over your truth. Every day, for twenty years. I told myself that was love. Some nights I still tell myself that."

Astrid looked at her mother — at the gray that had taken over her temples, at the burn scar on her forearm from the night she'd fed the drawings to the stove rather than let them be found — and understood that this was the apology. There would be no finer words. There were no finer words. There was only this: a woman who had lied to her child for two decades in order to keep the child, standing in the wreckage of that choice and naming it out loud at last.

"I know," Astrid said. "I know, Mamma."

Greta nodded once, sharply, the way she did when a difficult calving was over and both animals had survived. Then she turned to the stove, because grief and joy were both best processed through work, and said, "Sit down. Tell me about the griffin. And eat. In that order or any order, but eat."

* * *

Later — much later, with the fire banked and the soup demolished and the whole story told twice, once for her mother and once for the trickle of neighbors who appeared at the door as word traveled, each of whom brought something (a jar of preserves, a bundle of candles, Halvard brought an entire ham and pretended it was a burden) — Astrid climbed the stairs to her childhood room.

Someone had cleaned it. That stopped her on the landing. The bed was made with fresh linens. The window was shuttered against the cold but the latch was oiled. On the desk, squared neatly, sat a stack of paper and a pot of ink and three new quills, and on top of the stack, weighted down with the smooth gray stone Astrid had kept on that desk since she was seven, was a drawing.

A fox. Ink and charcoal, unmistakably her own hand — the quick strokes, the crooked ear she'd never learned to fix. Sebastian, drawn the spring she was nine, sitting in the clover with his lame leg tucked under him and his head cocked as if listening to a joke only he found funny.

She had thought it ash. She had stood in this room at fourteen and watched the flames take the whole stack, and she had hated her mother for a full year afterward, and she had grieved these pictures like graves.

But here was Sebastian. Slightly smoke-curled at one corner. Rescued.

She sank onto the bed and held it and did not cry, or cried exactly as much as she wanted to, which amounted to the same thing.

Her mother had pulled one drawing from the fire. One. The one fox who had ever truly talked back. And had hidden it for seven years, and kept the lamp lit in the window for five months, and rebuilt nothing and apologized for everything, and left ink and paper on the desk of a daughter she had every reason to believe might never come home — because whatever else Greta Iversen was, she had never once doubted that stories wanted writing down.

Astrid set the stone back on top of the drawing, and looked at the blank pages beneath it, and felt something turn over in her chest that was not magic. It was lighter than magic. It was the thing magic had been standing in front of her whole life, blocking the view.

* * *

In the morning she walked out to the east pasture, because there was one more grave to visit and she had been putting it off since the pass.

The fox was waiting for her.

He was old now — she could see it even without her gift, in the silver threading his muzzle, in the way he rose stiffly from the clover favoring the bad leg. Seven years since she'd first heard his voice in her head, dry and sardonic and impossibly patient. Three years since she'd left the valley. A lifetime since a lonely girl had knelt in this exact grass and heard a lame fox say, *Well. You're the first human in a hundred years worth the trouble of talking to.*

He trotted to meet her, unhurried, and sat down in front of her, and looked up.

And said nothing.

Because there was nothing to say with. Whatever fragment of wild voice had lived in him — whatever stray ember of the old magic had let a girl and a fox speak across the gap between species — it had gone into the Hollow with everything else. He was just a fox now. Clever, and old, and hers.

Astrid knelt in the wet grass. "Hi, Sebastian."

His ears tilted. His tail moved once, slowly, sweeping the clover. He cocked his head at exactly the angle from the drawing, as if listening to a joke only he found funny, and Astrid laughed and buried her face in the fur of his neck, and he tolerated this with the weary dignity of someone who has been loved by humans for a very long time and has made his peace with it.

Around them the valley woke: smoke rising, bells ringing from the village — ordinary bells, ringing ordinary hours, singing nothing but themselves. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing in particular. Somewhere children were already arguing about which of them the Griffin constellation was watching over. The wards hummed their honest, powerless hum along the tree line, marking where the people began, and the people had begun to include her.

She had come home with empty hands. No gift, no griffin, no part in the great Song or the silencing of it. Just a name the valley finally spoke without flinching, a mother's lamp finally answered, and a stack of blank paper waiting upstairs.

Sebastian sneezed, licked her chin once — undignified, final, a verdict — and set off along the fence line at his old rolling trot, glancing back once to make sure she was following.

She was. She always had been.

And far to the north, in the schoolrooms of Vesperhold where the bells now rang plain and tuneless, children were beginning to tell a strange new story: that if you whispered your secrets to certain animals — a fox, a crow, an old blind mare — sometimes, just sometimes, they seemed to understand.

Astrid smiled, and followed her fox home.
