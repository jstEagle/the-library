## 22. Sebastian and the Choir Anew

The fox was old now.

Astrid could see it in the way he moved down the orchard rows at dusk — not the liquid glide of his youth, all muscle and mischief, but a slower, more deliberate walk, as though each step were a small negotiation with his hips. His muzzle had gone silver at the edges, which she privately considered an affectation. Foxes did not go gray at the chin like dignified old men. Sebastian had simply decided, somewhere along the way, that he would age with style, and so he had.

He stopped at the fence post where he always stopped, sat, and looked up at her with those amber eyes that had lost none of their insolence in twenty years.

"Don't give me that look," Astrid said. "I fed you this morning."

The look continued. It was a masterful look. It contained multitudes: hunger, betrayal, the long suffering of a creature who had once dined on the gratitude of a grateful valley and was now reduced to whatever the Iversen kitchen deigned to spare.

"You are a liar and a thief," she told him fondly, "and you have never once been hungry in your life."

His tail swept the grass, once, twice — the old signal, the one that had meant *you're lying, Astrid* back when lying between them had still been possible, back when words had passed between them without sound or effort. Now it meant only what it meant, a tail moving through evening air, and yet Astrid felt the old warmth of it anyway, the way you feel warmth from a fire that has gone out because you remember how it burned.

She went inside and came back with the heel of yesterday's bread and the rind of cheese she had absolutely been saving for him, and he took both with the delicacy of a creature accepting tribute rather than charity.

* * *

The children came on the fourth day of the harvest festival, as they always did.

They arrived in a noisy pack down the lane from the village — six of them this year, the eldest maybe ten, the youngest still fat-cheeked and lisping — and they made straight for Astrid's gate with the confidence of pilgrims who know their shrine will receive them. Their mothers watched from farther up the lane, arms crossed, smiling in that way adults smile when they think no magic is happening.

"Astrid! Astrid!" The littlest one, Tova, reached the fence first and hung off it like laundry. "We saw him! We saw Sebastian!"

"Did you." Astrid leaned on the gatepost. "Where?"

"By the mill!" This from Bren, the eldest, who considered himself the expedition's official chronicler. "He was sitting on the wall and we were quiet like you said, and he looked right at me. Right at me. And then—" Bren paused for effect, having learned effect from years of watching Astrid tell stories at the festival fires. "Then he walked along the wall, and every time we moved, he stopped. And when we stopped, he walked again. Like he was *leading* us."

"Leading you where?"

"To the blackberries," said Tova solemnly. "The ones past the nettles. Nobody knows about those ones."

"Ah," said Astrid gravely. "The secret blackberries. That does sound like him."

"He understood us," Bren insisted. "I asked him if the nettles stung and he shook his head. He *shook his head*, Astrid. Like this." Bren demonstrated, shaking his own head with such vigor that his hat fell off.

"Well," Astrid said. "Foxes shake their heads all the time. To get the flies off."

"Not *like that* they don't."

The other children murmured agreement. There was a whole theology here, Astrid reflected, built out of berry bushes and head-shakes and one old fox's talent for appearing exactly where curiosity was thickest. Every child in the valley grew up on it. They learned that Sebastian could be found if you were patient; that he tolerated being talked to but never being grabbed; that he seemed, somehow, to *follow* a conversation — tilting his head at questions, huffing at jokes, turning his back with magnificent offense when anyone said something unkind within his hearing.

They learned, above all, that he understood them. And because no magic remained in the world — because everyone knew it, the way everyone knew the shape of the mountains — they filed this understanding away under things-that-are-not-magic-but-are-strange, alongside first snow and the way a mother always seems to wake when her child cries.

"He's just an ordinary fox," said the middle girl, whose name was Signe and who was going through a phase of rigorous skepticism that Astrid found deeply promising. "Mama says so. He's just old and tame because you feed him."

"Do I?" Astrid said.

"You do. Everyone knows."

"Hm. And yet I've never once seen him eat where anyone could watch." She let that sit. Signe's brow furrowed with the beautiful agony of a mind encountering its first genuine contradiction. "Perhaps your mama is right. Perhaps he's ordinary. Or perhaps—" Astrid crouched to the child's level, and lowered her voice as though passing along contraband, "—perhaps some things don't need magic to be true."

Signe stared at her. Behind her, Tova whispered to Bren, with the reverent tones of scripture being transmitted: "*Some things don't need magic.*"

Astrid stood before the theology could grow any further out of hand, though privately she hoped it would. A valley needed its small mysteries. Heaven knew it had earned them.

* * *

That night there was dancing in the village square, and Astrid went, as she always went, though she danced as rarely as ever.

The festival had changed since her childhood, or perhaps she had changed and the festival had merely waited patiently for her to catch up. In the old days — the days before the stars went out, before the Choir, before everything — the harvest festival had been a careful thing, joy kept behind fences, music played quietly, everyone minding the wards and the rules and the long memory of exile. Speakers had been driven from this valley generations ago. Everyone had known that. Everyone had known to be afraid.

Now the fiddlers played loud enough to startle owls, and nobody minded. Now the song the whole valley sang at midnight was not the Choir's Song — that dead, devouring harmony that had eaten stars and speakers alike — but something ragged and human and occasionally off-key, taught by grandmother to granddaughter with errors faithfully preserved, until the tune itself had become a kind of family tree.

Corin had come for the festival, as he did most years. He sat near the cider barrel as always, half-deafened ear turned toward the fire, watching the dancers with an expression that on any other face might have been called contentment. He had grown older gracefully, in the way of men who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to them and found everything afterward to be, comparatively speaking, a pleasant surprise. His hands were scarred. When the fiddles played certain notes — low, sustained, the kind of note the Song had used to bind — his jaw tightened, and Astrid would see him press his thumb into the oldest of the scars, and breathe, and stay.

He never stayed past dawn. He never explained why, and she never asked. Some debts, she had learned, were paid in installments across a lifetime, and the paying was the point, not the finishing.

"You're staring," he said, without looking at her.

"I'm observing. It's different. Staring is rude; observing is scholarly."

"Mm." He handed her a cup of cider. "Your fox was in the square earlier. Sitting on the well. Watching the musicians."

"Sebastian appreciates music. Always has."

"Does he." Corin's mouth twitched. "The second fiddle went flat during the reel. Just for a moment. And the fox made a sound — I'd swear it was a scoff — and the fiddler corrected herself immediately."

"Fiddlers correct themselves constantly. It's called practice."

"It's called practice," Corin agreed solemnly, "when it happens to people who haven't got a fox critiquing them."

Astrid drank her cider and said nothing, and Corin, who had spent enough years beside her to read her silences the way sailors read weather, smiled into his cup and let it lie.

* * *

Later, when the fires had burned down to orange hearts and the last dancers had stumbled home, Astrid walked the orchard path with Sebastian trotting ahead of her, pausing at intervals to make sure she was following — leading her, though there was nowhere left to lead her except home, and they both knew the way.

The sky overhead was thick with stars. Twenty years on, she still caught herself stopping to count them, the way a woman who has recovered from a long illness catches herself breathing deep just to feel it work. The stars the Choir had stolen — the voices of every speaker they had ever consumed, pooled for centuries beneath the First Hollow — burned now in patterns no astronomer had names for, constellations born the night she gave her gift away. Children in Vesperhold learned them in school. There was one shaped like a griffin with spread wings, brightest of all, and the schoolmasters taught that it had no name, and the children taught each other otherwise.

Ashveil, they called it. Of course they did. Ashveil, who had crashed bleeding into a barn on the worst and best night of her life; who had named her *speaker* when she had been nothing but a frightened girl hiding in plain sight; who had carried her through collapsing passes and burning skies and died, at the last, pouring himself into the empty dark so that the light would have somewhere to begin. The constellation wheeled slowly overhead now, patient and golden, and Astrid looked at it the way she looked at her mother's grave — with grief that had long since ripened into something gentler, something closer to company than sorrow.

Sebastian stopped at the old stone wall and sat, and looked up too. He always did. He would sit for long minutes, ears forward, watching the Griffin turn, and Astrid had never once caught him doing anything so undignified as howling at it, though more than once she had seen his lips move slightly, silently, as though he were humming along to something only he remembered.

"No magic left in the world," she said to him conversationally. "That's what the scholars say. You heard the same lectures I did, presumably, from your position on various walls. The Hollow is closed. The Song is broken. Wild magic is gone — poured out, spent, finished. All that remains is ordinary foxes doing ordinary fox things."

Sebastian turned his head and gave her the look. The full look. The one with multitudes.

"Yes, well." She sat down beside him on the cold stone, and he shifted, with the weary tolerance of the elderly, to lean against her hip. "Scholars also said the valley's wards couldn't be rebuilt by farmers. And here we are."

It was true, and it was the valley's quiet pride. After the Choir fell — after the Song collapsed into mere music and the cantors scattered or knelt or fled — the neighbors had come up the mountain to the Iversen farm with lanterns and shovels and no apologies, because apologies were not how her people did things, and set about rebuilding the wards with their own hands. They had known, by then, what she was. What she had been. Her mother had made sure of that in the end — had stood in the square and told the whole valley the truth of the drawings she'd burned, the daughter she'd hidden, the fear that had made her complicit in her child's loneliness. The apology had needed no words after that. It had been a rebuilt ward-stone here, an extra place at table there, a hundred small permissions granted without discussion, until belonging had grown over the old wound like bark over a scar.

Her mother had lived four more years. Long enough to meet every child in the valley twice over. Long enough, one autumn evening, to watch Astrid talking to a fox at the fence line, and to say, quietly, to no one in particular: "There she is." As though she had been waiting all that time simply to see her daughter whole.

* * *

The frost came early that year, and with it a letter from Vesperhold, bearing the seal of the new Archive — the one Corin had helped found in the ruins of the old forbidden one, where the history of the speakers was finally written down honestly, names and gifts and fates, so that no one would ever again be erased into fuel.

*The scholars ask again,* Corin wrote, in his careful cantor's hand, *whether you will consent to be interviewed. They wish to record the account of the First Hollow from the only living witness. I have told them you will refuse, as usual, and that I will write it down myself instead, as usual. Correct me if I am wrong.*

*I have enclosed the draft. You will notice I have made you taller.*

Astrid laughed alone in her kitchen, which was the best way to laugh, and read the draft by lamplight while wind worried the eaves. It was a good account. Accurate in all the places that mattered and generous in all the places where the truth had been ugly — though not dishonestly generous; Corin had never once flinched from writing down what the Choir had done, including what he had done, including the bell tower where he had let himself be taken as bait knowing she would have to choose between saving him and saving everything. He had written that chapter of it plainly. *She chose rightly,* the draft said. *I would have chosen wrongly, and I was the one who taught her to suspect me.* Only a man who had made peace with himself could write a sentence like that. It had taken him most of twenty years. He was getting there.

At the bottom of the draft, below his signature, someone else had written a line in ink that was not ink-brown but amber-dark, in a spidery scrawl that no scholar would admit to:

*Tell the tall one the part about the blackberries. — S.*

Astrid sat very still for a moment, lamplight flickering, wind pressing at the shutters.

Then she took up her pen, wrote *approved, with corrections* beneath the final page, added three corrections (all of them making her shorter), folded the pages, and set them aside for the morning post. She did not mention the last line. She did not mention it to Corin, or to the Archive, or to anyone, ever, in all the years that followed.

* * *

Winter settled in, deep and clean. The valley slept under snow, wards humming their wordless hum, chimneys standing up their smoke like slow gray trees. And on clear nights, when the cold made the stars burn hard and close, the children of the village would sometimes see an old fox walking the ridge line above the farms, unhurried, silver-muzzled, pausing now and then to sit and look up at the Griffin constellation as though checking on an old friend.

They told each other he was immortal. They told each other he had been there since before the stars went out, since before they came back, since the beginning of everything. They told each other that if you were very quiet, and very kind, and asked him a question with your whole heart, he would answer — not in words, never in words, but in a tilt of the head, a flick of the tail, a leading walk to berries or shelter or home.

And their parents smiled the smile of adults watching no magic happen, and the scholars in far-off Vesperhold wrote their careful volumes proving that wild magic was gone from the world entirely, and the fox went on walking the ridgeline, aging or not aging according to his own private schedule, keeping his appointments with curious children and lonely ones, understanding them perfectly.

Astrid, gray now herself, met him on the winter path one last evening of that year. He was sitting on the stone wall where he had always sat, and she sat beside him, and together they watched the stars wheel — the Griffin, and the Fox that some cartographer had finally gotten around to naming, tucked small and sly near the Griffin's wing, as though the two of them had arranged it.

"Some gifts outlive their givers," she said softly. It was not a theory. It was not even, strictly speaking, a hope. It was simply what she knew, the way she knew the weight of snow coming, the way she had once known the minds of beasts: surely, quietly, without needing to explain it to anyone.

Beside her, the old fox's tail swept the snow once, twice — the old signal, the old joke, the old truth.

*You're lying, Astrid,* it said.

And she laughed, because of course she was. She had been lying for twenty years, to scholars and neighbors and wide-eyed children, telling them there was no magic left in the world.

Some secrets you keep by telling everyone the truth except the part that matters. And some gifts — the best ones, the stubborn ones, the ones poured out completely and given away forever — simply refuse, with magnificent insolence, to die.
