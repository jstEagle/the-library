## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had been burning since before dawn, and by the time the light through the high window turned from pewter to gold, Ulyana Thornbury had catalogued forty-one impossible things and drunk two cups of chicory gone cold in its cup.

This was how every day began in the Vesper Choir: not with bells, though the guild kept a bell, nor with prayer, though Odile Vesper spoke of devotion often enough. It began with ink. Ulyana sat at the long scarred table in the counting room with the Ledger of Small Debts open before them, a book bound in grey calfskin so old its corners had gone soft as bread, and they wrote down what the night's acquisitions were worth.

Not what they cost. What they *were*.

Item forty-two was a jar of last year's summer. It sat on the shelf behind Ulyana's left shoulder, between a birdcage that held one borrowed song and a shoebox of unspent apologies, and it glowed faintly even now, a honeyed amber light that made the dust motes drift like slow bees. Inside the glass, if you tilted it just so, you could see heat shimmer over a field of barley that no longer existed anywhere but in there. The man who'd sold it to the Choir had wept at the counter. He'd said he couldn't afford to keep a whole season alive on a clerk's wages, not when winter was coming and his daughter needed boots. Ulyana had written: *One (1) jar, summer, previous year, contents intact, slight fading near the lid.* They had written it in their small, precise hand, and the moment the ink dried, the fading stopped. The summer inside settled, sealed, perfect, as though it had been waiting all along for someone to describe it correctly.

Ulyana did not notice this. Ulyana never noticed this.

They dipped the pen, blotted it against the rim of the well, and moved on.

*Item forty-three: one key, iron, opens yesterday.* The key was warm to the touch, the way a stone is warm after an animal has been lying on it. It had come in three nights ago from a widow in the Saltmarket who claimed she'd used it once, on the worst morning of her life, and stood again in her kitchen while her husband was still alive and still whistling, and had chosen—this was the part she could not stop saying, over and over, her hands flat on the counter as if bracing against a wind—she had *chosen* to come back rather than stay. Ulyana wrote the entry carefully: provenance, condition, the single use already spent, the residual warmth noted in the margin. Their handwriting was very neat. It was always very neat. Mistress Vesper said once, in that voice like a door closing softly, that Ulyana's ledgers were the only ones in the guild that never required correction, and Ulyana had taken it for praise, because it was offered as praise, and because Ulyana Thornbury had been raised in this building and knew better than to look too closely at anything the Choir gave them.

Below stairs, the guild was waking. Ulyana heard the thump of Tobias's boots on the stair—he took stairs the way other people took opinions, loudly and all at once—and then he filled the doorway, carrying a crate against his chest, his brown hair stuck up on one side where he'd slept on it.

"Delivery from the Marshside contact," he said, setting it down with a grunt. "You'll want gloves for the top layer."

"What is it?"

"Bottled luck." He peeled back the sacking. Rows of little bottles, each no bigger than a thumb, each holding a color that didn't sit still—a green that slid toward gold when you weren't looking, a gold that shivered into something almost red. "The poor ones," Tobias said. "Small luck. Finding a coin. Catching a busker's eye. Not stepping in the thing you stepped around." He grinned at Ulyana, and there was flour or chalk dust or possibly breakfast still at the corner of his mouth. "Odile wants them counted before the brokers come Thursday."

"Then they'll be counted." Ulyana pulled the ledger closer. "Forty-three entries done. These will make ninety-one."

"Ninety-one." Tobias leaned on the table, watching the pen move. He did that sometimes—watched Ulyana write with an expression Ulyana had never learned to name, something between admiration and hunger, as though the neatness itself were a kind of magic. Which was absurd. Ulyana was a scribe. A good one, maybe, the best the Choir had, but a scribe. "You know what I love about your pages?" Tobias said. "Nothing in them ever lies."

"Everything in them is true," Ulyana agreed mildly, and wrote: *Bottled luck, minor, sixty bottles, one cracked.*

The cracked bottle was the seventh in the second row, hairline fracture running down the glass like a frozen river. As Ulyana recorded the flaw, the crack stopped spreading. It had been creeping since the crate came off the cart; Tobias had watched it go. He opened his mouth, closed it, and finally just said, "Huh," and went to fetch the tea.

---

The morning arranged itself into its usual shape. Ulyana catalogued a compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost most recently (item fifty-one), a letter that arrived angry no matter who wrote it calmly (fifty-four), a child's drawing of a house that made the room around it smell of baking whenever it was unrolled (sixty). The light climbed the wall. Somewhere below, the apprentices were practicing the Choir's scales—not music exactly, but the humming exercise Odile insisted taught discipline, a low sustained note that made the bottled echoes on the shelves ring faintly in sympathy until the whole counting room trembled like a struck glass.

Ulyana worked through it. Ulyana worked through everything. That was the gift the Choir had given them, or so they believed: a place at a warm table, steady work, a trade. Orphaned at six, found on the guild steps with nothing but a name pinned to their coat—*Thornbury*, in handwriting Ulyana had spent twenty years trying not to remember—and taken in by Mistress Vesper herself, who had looked down at the child and said, *Well. Every choir needs a scribe.*

By midday the fog off the canal had thickened against the windows, turning the street below into a smear of lanterns and dark coats. Ulyana was re-inking the entry for a mirror that showed the viewer at age nine (seventy-eight) when Tobias came back up the stairs two at a time, and stopped in the doorway, and did not come in.

"Toby?"

"You should see this." His voice had gone strange. Flat, careful, the voice of a man carrying something breakable. "On the door."

Ulyana set down the pen—set it down parallel to the ledger's edge, because some part of them always needed things ordered—and followed him down.

The proclamation was nailed to the guild's front door at eye height, four nails, driven deep. It bore the crown's seal in red wax, and beneath the seal, in print so crisp it might have been stamped by the king's own teeth:

*BY ORDER OF THE THRONE. Be it known that persons harboring the talent of UNMAKING—that is, the power to render null, void, or undone that which exists—are declared enemies of the realm. Any soul possessing knowledge of such a person shall report to the Crown Wardens within three days. Reward: fifty crowns silver. Concealment of the same shall be judged treason, and treason is answered at the gallows.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought: *The hunt begins at Advent. God keep the true.*

Tobias read it twice. So did Ulyana. Around them, the fog pressed close, and the street had gone quiet in that particular way streets go quiet when everyone is reading the same paper through their windows.

"Unmaking," Tobias said. "I've never even—" He stopped. Started again. "Do we have anyone? I mean. In the guild. Anyone who..."

"I don't know," Ulyana said.

It was true. It was entirely true, and it would remain true for some time yet, and neither of them knew it. Ulyana reached out and touched the edge of the proclamation, feeling the cheap pulp of it, the wet cold of the nails' heads. Fifty crowns. Enough to live on for a year. Enough, in the Saltmarket, to buy a neighbor.

Behind them, inside the warm and lamplit guild, the bell above the door began to ring—the low, disciplined note of the Choir's evening hour—but Ulyana heard, beneath it, fainter than memory, a sound like a page turning somewhere far away, as if some vast ledger had just been opened, and a name written in it.

Their own hand, they would think later, had already begun to shake.
