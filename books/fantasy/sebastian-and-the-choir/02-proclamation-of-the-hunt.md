## 2. Proclamation of the Hunt

The proclamation went up on a Tuesday, which Ulyana would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day the Choir took inventory and the crown, apparently, had decided to do the same.

They heard it before they saw it: the sound of the city changing its breathing. Vellichor's streets had always murmured—fishwives haggling over eels that still remembered being rivers, the bell of Saint Aldhelm tolling hours that arrived slightly out of order—but this morning the murmur had a new grain to it, a low collective hum like bees deciding whether to swarm. Ulyana came down Copperlace Street with a satchel of ledger paper under one arm and stopped at the mouth of the square, where a knot of people had gathered around the guild's own door as though it were an altar.

Someone had nailed a sheet of vellum to the oak, right through the Vesper Choir's brass moth. The nails were new. The hammer lay in the snow at the foot of the door, dropped by whoever had been too eager or too frightened to carry it home.

Ulyana read it twice, because their trade was reading, and reading twice was how you caught the lie inside a truth.

*By order of His Majesty's Grace,* it began, in the fat black hand of a court scrivener who pressed too hard on his downstrokes, *let it be known throughout the annexed territories that a bounty of two hundred crowns silver shall be paid for the discovery and surrender of any person bearing the gift of unmaking—that is, any person by whose touch or word that which is written may be made false, undone, or otherwise unmade.*

*Report to the nearest garrison. Informers will be rewarded. Concealers will be counted among the guilty.*

Two hundred crowns. Ulyana did the arithmetic without meaning to; it was a habit of clerks. Two hundred crowns was four years of a lamplighter's wages. It was a winter's coal for a whole tenement. It was, for half the hungry faces around them in the square, more money than God had ever shown them in one place.

An old woman near the fountain was already looking at her neighbor's hands, turning them over as if the gift might be written on the palms. A boy of perhaps nine stood on the fountain's rim, counting shadows. That was the game children were already inventing, Ulyana realized with a cold settling behind the ribs—the hunt hadn't even properly begun and the children were playing at it. *Count your shadow. If it moves wrong, tell your mother.* As if unmaking left some visible stain, some smudge a careful eye could catch.

The gift didn't work like that. The gift—if you had it—worked like weather. Like forgetting. You wouldn't see it coming. You'd only notice, afterward, that something written wasn't true anymore, and by then the person responsible had walked three streets away and was buying bread.

Ulyana pulled their collar up and slipped past the crowd into the guildhouse, and the door closed behind them with the soft, satisfied click of a thing that intended to keep secrets.

* * *

Inside, the Vesper Choir smelled of tallow and ink and, faintly, of the jarred summer on the third shelf—last year's July, bottled at its golden hour, exhaling warmth and cut grass into the cold corridor like a prisoner dreaming aloud. Ulyana paused beside it out of habit. The light inside the glass shifted when they passed, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar footstep.

"Ledger room," said Marta from the stairwell, not looking up from the basket of borrowed luck she was decanting. Marta was seventy and had hands like tree roots, and she had survived every purge, pogrom, and royal whim of the last fifty years by cultivating the facial expression of a woman who has never once been interested in anything. "Mistress wants everyone. Don't run. Running looks guilty."

"I never run."

"You walk quickly when you're frightened," Marta said. "It's worse."

Odile Vesper received them in the long hall beneath the moth-and-moon window, standing where the colored light fell across her like a verdict. She was not a tall woman, but she had the particular gravity of someone who had made herself load-bearing; rooms arranged themselves around her. Forty members of the Choir stood in uneven rows—scribes and decanters, the locksmith of impossible doors, the twins who kept the guild's stock of secondhand dreams—and none of them spoke, because Odile had not yet begun, and in the Vesper Choir silence was a currency you saved until asked to spend it.

"You have seen the proclamation," she said, without preamble. Her voice was dry and level, a page being turned. "I will not insult you by pretending this is the first hunt. I will not comfort you by promising it is the worst. What I will do is tell you how we survive it, because we have survived eleven of these, and the Choir is still standing, and so are you, which is more than can be said for the kings who ordered the other eleven."

A ripple went through the rows—not laughter, nothing so warm, but the ghost of it.

"The rules are these." She raised one finger. "You pass as ordinary. From this morning forward, no member carries, sells, or touches inventory outside these walls. No gifts are used. Not small ones. Not convenient ones. Nothing." A second finger. "Your papers are ordinary papers. Your trades are ordinary trades. You are clerks, chandlers, bookbinders, widows. If anyone asks what the Choir does, we are a charitable society for the preservation of old music. We have pamphlets. Learn them." A third finger, and here her eyes moved along the rows and settled, briefly, on Ulyana, with a weight that felt less like attention than like a hand placed flat on the back of the neck. "And you report to me anything unusual. Anything written strangely. Anything remembered wrongly. The gift of unmaking announces itself, eventually, by its accidents. Our task is to hear the announcement before the crown does."

She dismissed them with a nod, and the hall exhaled forty different kinds of fear, each member carrying theirs home in a different pocket.

Ulyana lingered, as clerks do, straightening a bench that did not need straightening.

"Mistress," they said carefully, "the proclamation says *unmaking*. Rewriting. Making false. My work is accuracy. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Odile looked at them for a long moment, and her face did the thing it did best, which was nothing at all. "Accuracy is a knife too, Thornbury. It depends what you cut with it. Go count the shelves."

* * *

Ulyana counted the shelves. Counting was prayer, counting was armor, counting was the shape their fear took when it wanted to be useful. They worked down the inventory row by row in the grey afternoon light: the jar of last year's summer (seal intact, contents restless); the key that opens yesterday (do not duplicate; do not ask it questions); seven bottles of borrowed luck, decanted by Marta, each stopper tied with red thread; a compass that pointed toward whatever you'd lost most recently, currently trembling between north and a dead man's wedding ring.

It was on the bottom shelf of the deeds cabinet, in the section marked *TERMINATED HOLDINGS*, that they found the ruin.

Or rather, the deed to the ruin—a property on Gullwing Lane, seized by the crown twenty years ago, roof fallen in, walls open to the sky, entered in the ledger in someone else's neat hand: *Uninhabitable. Condemned. Value: nil.*

Ulyana had copied that entry. Three years ago, during the great reorganization, they had recopied every terminated deed in their own fair clerk's script, because the old entries were fading and fading things get lost, and Ulyana's whole life had been organized around the principle that nothing should be lost.

They had copied it, and somewhere in the copying—in the pressure of the nib, in the care taken with the descenders, in the small unconscious tenderness of a hand that loved what it wrote—they had improved it.

The entry now read: *Habitable. Sound roof. Value: modest but honest.*

Ulyana sat down on the cellar step with the deed in both hands and read it four times, and the fourth time their vision blurred, because memory was arriving all at once, the way floodwater arrives through a door left ajar.

They remembered the reorganization. They remembered copying the deed. And underneath that, surfacing now like bodies after a drowning, they remembered other things. The time the rent ledger had said the widow Czerny owed six months and Ulyana had copied it clean and afterward it said she owed nothing, and no one had questioned it, because Ulyana's books were always right. The time the inventory had listed a bottle of grief and Ulyana had found it empty, and written *contents dispersed*, and the widow Czerny—who had carried that grief like a stone in her chest for a decade—had come in the next morning lighter, bewildered, saying she'd slept for the first time since the funeral. The time Ulyana had mended a torn map of the eastern passes and the passes had mended too, and the surveyors' guild had spent a season baffled by a road that now existed where a ravine used to be.

Their handwriting had never merely recorded the world. Their handwriting had been quietly editing it, entry by entry, year by year, the way water edits stone—with patience, without malice, without ever once announcing itself.

*I am the thing,* Ulyana thought, and the thought did not land so much as open beneath them, like a trapdoor, like a door. *I am what they're hunting.*

Not unmaking, exactly. Something stranger, something the crown's scriveners hadn't found a word for because it wore the mask of diligence. But the proclamation's net was wide, and nets do not check whether the fish meant to be caught. Two hundred crowns. The boy counting shadows. The old woman turning her neighbor's hands over in the square.

Anyone could inform. Everyone would be tempted. And Ulyana's evidence was sitting in their own ledgers, in plain sight, in their own unmistakable hand—for anyone who knew what to look for, which now, thanks to Odile's third rule, the entire Choir had been instructed to do.

The jarred summer breathed its warm grass smell across the cellar, absurd and gentle, and Ulyana held the deed against their chest like a shield that had just informed them it was also a confession.

Footsteps on the stairs. Measured ones. Odile's.

"Thornbury," said the mistress of the Vesper Choir from the darkness above, in a voice that gave away nothing except that she had known exactly where to find them, "bring that deed up with you. We need to talk about your penmanship."
