## 3. What Odile Keeps in the Cellar

The cellar of the Vesper Choir was not on any inventory Ulyana had ever catalogued.

They knew the guildhouse the way a blind woman knows her own hands—by touch, by habit, by the small betrayals of creaking boards. They knew that the third stair from the landing sang a low E when stepped upon at dawn and said nothing at midnight. They knew the smell of each room: beeswax and old paper in the scriptorium, tallow and iron in the counting room, the faint green breath of bottled summer leaking from its jar on the high shelf where Odile kept it out of reach of hopeful apprentices.

But the cellar—*the* cellar, below the wine cellar, below the cold room where perishable miracles were kept at a temperature just shy of forgetting—that one appeared in no ledger Ulyana had ever copied. It appeared in no ledger at all.

Which, Ulyana understood now, with the proclamation's ink still fresh under their fingernails, was itself an entry. Absence is a kind of writing. A blank page shouts.

The order had come that morning, delivered in Odile Vesper's voice like a coin dropped into a well: *Go down and fetch me the winter stock. The luck, the echoes, the small mercies. We will need to be generous soon, and generosity must be rationed.* She had said it while looking out the window at the snow beginning again, and she had not looked at Ulyana once, which was how Ulyana knew the errand was also a test, or a trap, or both—the two being, in Odile's household, frequently the same object viewed from different angles.

So Ulyana took the lamp and went down past the wine racks, past the cold room with its jars of last year's summer sweating condensation like nervous men, to the door at the very back that they had walked past four hundred times without seeing because it did not wish to be seen. It was made of the same grey stone as the wall around it, fitted so precisely that the seam was thinner than a hair. Only now, with the hunt loose in the city and their own name presumably already written on someone's list, did Ulyana's fingers find the seam and follow it down to a handle shaped like a closed eye.

It opened at their touch.

That should have been the first warning. Doors in the Choir opened for Odile, for the senior factors, for the ledger-keepers whose names were written in the front matter of the great books. Ulyana was a copyist, a clerk, a keeper of small debts. Doors should have ignored them the way cats ignore furniture.

The stairs went down farther than the guildhouse had foundations. Ulyana counted forty-one steps and stopped counting when the numbers began to feel like a dare. The air changed as they descended—cooler, then warmer, then neither, then old. It smelled the way a locked room smells after twenty years: dust, yes, but under the dust something sweeter and more terrible, like flowers pressed in a book by someone who meant to come back and never did.

The lamp found the shelves first.

Row upon row of them, receding into dark, and every shelf crowded with the same kind of inventory Ulyana spent their days cataloguing upstairs—except these were not acquisitions. These were confiscations. Ulyana could tell by the labels, because the labels were written in the Choir's own hand, in the neat clerical script they taught to every apprentice, and the entries were not purchases.

*Confiscated from Maren Ostrova, factor, third rank. Gift: the mending of broken promises. Taken: 11th of Frostmoon, Year of the Grey Bell.*

*Confiscated from Pell Harrow, doorwarden. Gift: walking through walls that remember him. Taken: spring, Year of the Drowned King.*

*Confiscated from —* and here the label was scratched out so thoroughly the wood beneath had been gouged, *—gift: the unmaking of what is written.*

Ulyana stood very still. The lamp flame leaned toward the scratched-out label as if reading over their shoulder.

There were hundreds of them. Jars of voices. Keys to hours that no longer existed. A coat that made the wearer beloved, folded with its sleeves crossed like arms. A birdcage containing, if you looked too long, a country. And on the lowest shelf, in a row of plain lead boxes with no labels at all, something that made the lamp gutter and made Ulyana's teeth ache the way a struck bell aches in the jaw—something that knew they were there.

Every gift had belonged to a person. Every person had belonged to the Choir. And every entry ended with the word *taken*, in handwriting Ulyana recognized, because it was the handwriting Ulyana had been imitating since they were nine years old and first allowed to hold a pen in the scriptorium.

Odile's hand. Odile's hand, going back decades, patient and beautiful and merciless as frost on a window.

The Choir had not survived the hunts by hiding. The Choir had survived by feeding.

* * *

When Ulyana was seven, before the Choir, there had been a kitchen in a house outside Vellichor City, and their mother standing at the table with flour to her elbows, teaching them letters in the spilled grain.

*Write your name,* she said. *But write it small, and crooked, and wrong. Write it the way a stranger would.*

*Why?*

*Because someday someone will read it who shouldn't, and I want them to read a stranger.* She had smiled, and flour had drifted from her cheek, and outside the window the annexation banners were going up along the road, red and gold, one for every village that had agreed to stop existing. *Names are doors, little wren. Whoever holds your true name can open you. So we keep a false one hanging by the entrance, like a coat.*

Their mother had written things that came true. Ulyana had known this the way children know the sun—not as information but as weather. When she wrote *the fever breaks tonight* on a scrap of paper and burned it in the stove, the fever broke. When she wrote *the tax collector loses our address* in the margin of her recipe book, he rode past their gate three times and three times saw only an empty house. She called it nothing. She called it *keeping the accounts balanced*. She hid it inside ordinary chores the way other mothers hid coins inside loaves.

And once—only once—she had sat Ulyana down at the kitchen table, taken their small hand in her floured one, and guided it through a sentence written in a language Ulyana did not know, letters like birds' footprints in snow. Their hand had cramped. Something in the room had leaned closer, the way the dark leans when a lamp is carried into it.

*What does it say?* Ulyana had asked.

*It says you were here before they erased this place,* their mother said, and her voice was steady but her eyes were wet. *It says the door remembers you even if the maps forget. One day, if everything burns, you go to the door and you say your name—the real one, the one we wrote tonight—and it will know you.*

Then the soldiers came, or the plague came, or the erasure came—memory had worn the edges off that night until only the flour and the letters remained—and Ulyana had gone to the Choir with nothing but a false name hanging by the entrance like a coat, and Odile Vesper had looked at them across her desk and said, *You have accurate hands, child. Accuracy is a rare virtue. Here, accuracy is survival.*

Ulyana had believed her for twenty years. They had thought their talent was diligence. They had thought that when they copied a deed and the ruin described in it became, some months later, mysteriously habitable, it was coincidence wearing the costume of clerical precision. They had thought the Choir sheltered the gifted.

The Choir collected them. That was all. The Choir collected them the way the cellar collected gifts: labeled, shelved, and taken.

* * *

"You shouldn't be able to open that door."

Odile Vesper stood at the top of the stairs, holding a candle that did not flicker, because candles near Odile had learned better. She wore her grey dressing gown and her silver hair unbound, and she looked, for the first time in Ulyana's memory, like an old woman—which lasted exactly as long as it took her to descend the first step, after which she was simply Odile again, inevitable as interest accruing.

"The door opened for me," Ulyana said. Their voice sounded far away, as if someone else were speaking through a jar.

"Did it." Odile came down the steps slowly, letting each one speak. "Forty-one steps, and a door sealed against everyone whose name isn't written in my private register. Opened for a copyist." She reached the bottom and looked at the shelves, at the lamp, at Ulyana's face, reading all three the way she read ledgers. "Or perhaps not a copyist."

Ulyana's hand drifted, without permission, toward the gouged label. *Gift: the unmaking of what is written.* "Whose was it?"

"A factor who asked questions in the wrong tone." Odile set her candle on a shelf between a jar of hush and a bottle of someone's borrowed courage. "You are asking questions in that tone right now."

"The Choir sold them." It was not a question, and saying it aloud made it heavier, made it land in the room like a body. "When the crown hunted, the Choir handed over its own and kept the gifts."

"The Choir *survived*," Odile said. "Vellichor no longer exists, Ulyana. You have seen the maps. There is no country here for us to be loyal to, no law above the crown's hunger except the law of continuing. I have kept two hundred people alive through three purges by making certain the purge always had somewhere else to look. Yes. Sometimes it looked at us. Sometimes I chose which of us it saw." Her eyes were pale and level and terribly clear. "I have never pretended otherwise. That is more honesty than any government in this city has managed in a century."

"And now the hunt is for people like me."

"Now the hunt is for people like you," Odile agreed softly. "And I am the only person in Vellichor with both the means to hide you and the knowledge of what you are. Which brings us to terms."

"I haven't agreed to terms."

"You climbed down forty-one steps into my cellar. That is a signature." She moved closer, and the candlelight made her shadow climb the shelves behind her, tall and many-fingered, touching every confiscated thing. "Here are the terms, and they are simple, and they are the same terms every soul on these shelves implicitly refused. You belong to the Choir entirely. Your gift works when I require it and sleeps when I do not. You write what I put in front of you and nothing else. In exchange, you live. Not merely survive—live. Warm rooms, full larder, a place in the family. I protect what is mine, Ulyana. Ask anyone I have ever protected."

Ulyana looked at the shelves. At Maren Ostrova's mended promises. At Pell Harrow's remembering walls. At the lead boxes that ached in the teeth. *Ask anyone I have ever protected*—and the cellar answered for them, hundreds of labels deep, in Odile's beautiful hand.

Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, a bell rang the hour, and beneath the bell, fainter, came the sound of the city: boots, many boots, moving through snow in the pattern of a search.

"You're afraid," Odile observed. "Good. Fear is honest. Sign, and fear becomes prudence instead of doom." From her dressing gown she produced a single sheet of paper and a pen, and held them out. "Your mark at the bottom. Obedience, total and written. That is the price of the door staying shut behind you."

Ulyana took the pen because their hand was shaking and holding something steadied it, and because refusing outright would close every path including the ones not yet visible, and because some traitorous part of them still loved the warm rooms, the full larder, the family-shaped cage. They looked at the page. The clause was short. The clause was, in its way, beautifully written.

And beneath the clause, in the margin, in ink so faint it might have been compression marks from a page pressed long ago, Ulyana's eye caught a ghost of older writing—a few letters in a language like birds' footprints in snow.

Their mother's language. On Odile's paper.

"What is this?" Ulyana whispered. "Where did this page come from?"

Odile's face did not change, which was itself a change. "Sign," she said, "and I will tell you about your mother."

Outside, the boots in the snow stopped directly above them, and the floorboards of the counting room groaned under a weight of men.
