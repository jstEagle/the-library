## 4. A Country That No Longer Exists

The ledgers of the dead were kept in the east alcove, where the candlelight went amber and the dust tasted faintly of cloves. Ulyana had been given the task without being told why—Odile Vesper simply appeared at their elbow one grey morning and said, *Reconcile the estate accounts. Every name, every debt, every entry since the founding.* Then she was gone, her black skirts whispering against the stone like a tide going out.

It was work made for hiding in. Ulyana took down volume after volume from the shelves that leaned like tired old men against the alcove wall: *Ledger of Bottled Weather, 1803–1811. Ledger of Borrowed Years. Ledger of Names Returned Unopened.* The Vesper Choir had been trading in impossible things for two centuries, and every impossible thing left a paper trail, because that was the one law Odile never broke: **if it is not written, it did not happen.**

Ulyana wrote that law into their bones long before they understood it. Ink was truth; ink was safety. A thing recorded could be defended. A thing recorded could be *proven*.

They were three days into the reconciliation when they found the handwriting.

It was in a ledger with no title on its spine, bound in green leather gone soft as moss at the corners. The entries inside were ordinary enough—a door knocker that answered questions, a mirror that showed the room as it would look if no one had ever loved anyone in it—and then, halfway down a page dated twenty-two years past:

*Item 44. One (1) door, standing, oak and iron, last egress from Vellichor. Condition: sealed. Key: none. Opens only to a name written upon it before the erasure.*

Ulyana's hand stopped moving.

They knew that slanting script. They knew the way the descenders curled like ferns unrolling, the way the crossbar of the *t* swept back as if reluctant to finish the word. They had spent their whole childhood watching that hand write shopping lists and lullabies and letters to a sister in a city whose name Ulyana had been told to forget.

Their mother's handwriting was in the Choir's oldest ledger, cataloguing a door out of a country that officially did not exist.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above, the guildhouse settled its timbers with a sound like an old man clearing his throat. Ulyana sat very still, the way one sits when a floor has begun, very quietly, to tilt.

Vellichor. Annexed by the crown twenty years ago, struck from every map, its cities renamed, its language declared a dialect of nothing. Children born after the erasure learned the word only as a warning—the way you learn *wolf*, or *plague*. And here it was, in faded iron-gall ink, in their mother's hand, entered into the inventory of the guild that had taken Ulyana in off the streets after she died.

*Item 44,* Ulyana read again. *Opens only to a name written upon it before the erasure.*

There was a smaller notation beneath, cramped into the margin as if the writer had feared being interrupted:

*Names on the door: eleven. Ten are dead or lost. Ask me about the eleventh someday, little wren, if you ever find this page. I hope you find it in better years than these.*

*Little wren.*

No one had called them that since the funeral. No one alive.

Ulyana's eyes burned. They let them. There was no one in the alcove but the dead and their debts, and the dead had seen people cry over ledgers before; it was practically a form of prayer.

But grief, Ulyana discovered as they sat there while the candle burned lower, is a solvent. It dissolves the varnish off things. Underneath the ache of seeing their mother's hand, something else was surfacing, cold and bright as a coin at the bottom of a well: *she left this for me.* Not accidentally. Not idly. She had written *little wren* into a ledger she must have known would outlive her, in a guild she must have known kept everything, because paper was the one country the crown could not annex.

Her inheritance was not money. It was a map drawn in instructions.

*Ask me about the eleventh someday.* But there was no one to ask. Unless—

Ulyana turned back through the ledger, faster now, hunting. Their mother's entries ran for pages: acquisitions, appraisals, small trades. And then, near the end, in a different ink, darker, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three leaves:

*Correction to Item 44. The door does not open to any of the eleven names. It opens to whoever's name was written on it LAST, before the seal was set. I have checked the registry twice. The last name is mine. I have scratched it out and rewritten it so the door will know my child instead. Forgive me the presumption, little wren. It is the only large thing I ever gave you.*

Ulyana read it four times. Then a fifth, aloud, barely breathing the words, because some sentences change shape when spoken and this one needed to hold still.

Their mother had rewritten the door. Had taken the one gift she apparently possessed—no grand talent, nothing the hunts would ever come for, just the small stubborn magic of a woman who fixed what was miswritten—and bent it toward the future, toward a child who might one day need a way out of a country that was busy forgetting itself.

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. Ulyana closed the ledger gently, the way you close a hand around something living, and carried it to their room beneath the eaves, wrapped in their spare shirt like contraband, which, under the new proclamation, it very nearly was.

* * *

Sleep did not come so much as ambush them in fragments, and in the gaps between fragments Ulyana dreamed of the door. Oak and iron, standing alone in a field of snow that had no edges, and carved into its planks, half-erased, a name that kept almost resolving into legibility and then sliding away, like a face glimpsed in a crowd. In the dream Ulyana reached out to touch the carving and their fingers came away stained with ink.

They woke before dawn with the answer sitting in their chest, fully formed, the way answers do when they have been waiting all night at the foot of the bed.

*A name written upon it before the erasure.* Their mother had rewritten the registry—but rewriting required reading. To make the door know Ulyana's name, their mother must have written Ulyana's true name somewhere, in the old script, in the old tongue, in the alphabet of a country that no longer existed. Which meant Ulyana had a name the crown's census had never recorded. A name that existed nowhere official. A name that was, in the most literal sense the Choir's law allowed, *unwritten*—and therefore free.

And therefore, if the hunt's definition of the gift of unmaking spread even one inch wider than it already had, dangerous beyond telling.

Ulyana lay under the eaves listening to the guildhouse wake below: Tobias's cough, the kettle's whistle, the creak of Odile's boots crossing the hall on her morning rounds. Two weeks ago this had all been shelter. Now every familiar sound had a second meaning underneath it, the way a palimpsest holds a ghost-text under the visible one. Odile's boots paused outside Ulyana's door—one heartbeat, two—and moved on.

She knew about the cellar. She knew about the hunts. Did she know about Item 44? The ledger had sat unopened in the east alcove for twenty-two years, but Odile Vesper forgot nothing that was written, and Ulyana was beginning to suspect she forgot very little that wasn't.

There was only one way to find out, and it was the same way forward regardless: Ulyana had to see the door.

The ledger gave a location, of course it did—*stored, per mistress's instruction, in the deep archive beneath the salt house, crate marked with a wren.* Their mother had hidden her own inheritance inside the guild's memory, trusting the institution even if she had perhaps not entirely trusted the woman. Twenty-two years of dust between Ulyana and a plank of oak that knew their name.

Getting to the salt house meant crossing the city, and the city had changed. The proclamation had been nailed up for a fortnight now and already the walls wore its consequences: new lanterns at the corners, pairs of soldiers at the bridges, chalk marks on certain doors that had not been there in autumn. Neighbors counted each other's shadows. The baker's boy had been taken away on Tuesday for whistling a tune that turned out to be from Vellichor, though he'd only learned it from his grandmother and could not have found the country on a map, since no such map existed anymore.

Ulyana wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, tucked it inside their coat, and went down to breakfast wearing their most ordinary face.

Tobias was already at the table, hunched over his porridge like a man defending it. He looked up, and for a moment his eyes were strange—guilty, almost, or frightened in a way that had nothing to do with porridge—but then he managed his usual crooked smile and said, "You were in the alcove until midnight. I heard the ladder."

"Reconciling estates." Ulyana ladled porridge, keeping their hands steady through sheer clerical discipline. "Odile wants everything accounted for."

"Everything always gets accounted for here," Tobias said, and there was something under the words too, some weight he set down carefully between them like a package neither wanted to claim. "That's the whole trouble with this place."

He said it lightly. He did not say it lightly. Ulyana filed both versions away, the way they filed everything now—because that was the other thing the ledger had taught them overnight: everyone in this house was writing something, and most of it was in invisible ink.

After breakfast, Ulyana waited for Odile's boots to pass the study, waited for the front door to admit a client wanting a jar of borrowed courage, waited for the house to fill with its daytime noise. Then they slipped out the kitchen door into the white morning, the ledger flat against their ribs like a second heartbeat, and turned toward the salt house and the door that had been waiting twenty-two years to be knocked on by the right name.

The snow squeaked underfoot. Somewhere behind them, a bell began to ring—not the guild's bell, not any church bell Ulyana knew. A new bell. The city was full of new bells lately, and every one of them sounded, to Ulyana's ear, like a page being torn out.

They walked faster, and did not look back.
