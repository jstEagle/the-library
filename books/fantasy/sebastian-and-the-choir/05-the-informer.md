## 5. The Informer

Tobias Quill had learned to measure hunger in ink.

A full belly was black ink, glossy and certain, the kind that dried fast and held its line. Hunger was the pale wash they used for rough drafts—thin, watery, a color that apologized even as it touched the page. He had been living in the wash for eleven days now, ever since the Choir's pay chests went dark and Odile Vesper announced, in her voice like a key turning, that all wages would be suspended *for the duration of the present difficulties*.

The present difficulties were nailed to every door in the city: the crown's proclamation, printed in red and gold, promising forty silver crowns to any loyal subject who delivered up a bearer of the gift of unmaking. Forty crowns. Tobias had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn grooves in him. Forty crowns was a year of bread. Forty crowns was his mother's cough seen by an actual physician instead of the herb-wife's guesses. Forty crowns was warmth, was light, was the difference between writing with numb fingers and writing with ones that could feel the pen.

He told himself he would never do it. He told himself this while counting the coins left in his tin, which numbered four, and then three, and then two, because bread did not care about his principles.

* * *

The stranger appeared at the Lamplighter's Rest on a Tuesday, in the hour when the tavern's smoke hung thickest and everyone's face looked like someone else's.

Tobias noticed him the way a scribe notices anything—by the details that didn't fit. The man sat alone but had set out four cups. His coat was good wool gone gray with road dust, and when he paid for his ale he counted the coins wrong, sliding one across the table twice as if he'd forgotten it had already left his hand. And when the lamplight caught his fingers, Tobias saw the marks: faint silver scarring across the knuckles, the kind that came from handling things that resisted being handled. Bottled things. Borrowed things.

Tobias knew those scars. He catalogued them weekly, in the Choir's inventory ledgers, under *acquisition artifacts, personal.*

The gift of unmaking wasn't only unmaking. That was what the proclamations got wrong, or chose to get wrong. The gift was a whole family of small impossibilities—the talent for making written things true, for rewriting what was, for closing doors that had been open since the world began. The crown wanted them all. The crown had learned, somewhere in its cold archives, that a person who could rewrite a deed could rewrite a border, and it had begun to hunt accordingly.

The stranger drank his ale slowly, watching the door. Waiting for someone, maybe. Or waiting out the checkpoints, which had multiplied through the city like frost-flowers on glass.

Tobias sat with his own thin ale and felt the two coins in his pocket like a stone in a shoe.

He thought about his mother's cough. He thought about the proclamation's phrase, *loyal subject*, and how easily loyalty could be spelled with silver. He thought about Ulyana Thornbury, his closest friend in the Choir, who had once told him over cold tea that the difference between a clerk and a coward was only which way you filed your fear.

Then he thought about nothing at all, which is what hunger does to a man, and he stood, and he walked to the garrison post on Candlemaker's Row, and he said the words.

*"There's a man at the Lamplighter's Rest. Silver-scars on both hands. I think he carries the gift."*

The sergeant wrote it down without looking up. She paid no bounty on suspicion; she explained this twice, patiently, the way one explains weather. But she took his name, and she took the description, and she said the watch would look into it, and Tobias walked home through streets that seemed suddenly made of ears.

Forty crowns, if it proved true. He lay awake all night rehearsing how he'd spend them, and hating himself in the pauses between purchases.

* * *

They took the stranger on Thursday.

Tobias saw it from the window of the scriptorium, where he was supposed to be fair-copying a contract for the sale of a bottled echo. Four soldiers, unhurried, the way men are unhurried when the outcome is not in question. They walked the stranger out of the Lamplighter's Rest with his arms folded behind him, and the man did not struggle. He looked almost relieved, Tobias thought. Like a debt being collected at last.

The crowd watched in silence. That was the new weather of the city: silence, everywhere, the sound of neighbors counting each other's shadows. Old Marta from the chandlery crossed the street to avoid walking past the arrest. A boy spat—not at the soldiers, but at the stranger, quickly, guiltily, the way you spit to prove you're on the right side before anyone asks.

Tobias turned back to his copying. The echo-contract swam in front of him. He had written the same clause three times, each version slightly different, and when he checked them against the original none of them matched, because his hands had stopped believing in what they wrote.

That evening, unable to bear his own room, he went to the garrison post to ask about the bounty. Verification first, the sergeant had said. Payment upon confirmation of the gift.

The sergeant found the record. Read it. Her face did something complicated and then went flat.

"Released this morning," she said. "No gift. The scars are old burns—a tallow-vat accident, per his own account, corroborated by his travel papers." She slid a slip of paper toward him, official, stamped. "He's been cleared. You can tell whoever sent you."

"Sent me?" Tobias said. "No one sent me. I—I saw—"

"Mm," said the sergeant, in the tone of a woman who processed a great many citizens who had seen things. "Cleared," she repeated, and returned to her ledger, and Tobias understood that he had just been entered into someone else's book, in some column he could not read, and that the entry would not be erased.

* * *

He walked home the long way, past the river, where the ice was beginning to build its white grammar along the banks. The stranger had been innocent. Not merely innocent—*empty*. No gift, no danger, nothing but bad luck and a tallow-vat and a tired traveler's habit of miscounting coins. And Tobias had fed him to the machine anyway, for the price of a rumor, for the *possibility* of forty crowns.

He tried to construct the usual excuses and found they had all been confiscated too. He hadn't known. He'd only suspected. Suspecting wasn't a crime. Everyone was suspecting everyone; it was practically civic duty now. But the excuses rang false in his skull, and worse—they sounded *written*, composed, drafted in the pale wash of self-pardon, and Tobias had spent twenty years training his ear to detect forgery.

Here was the thing he could not stop turning over, though, the splinter under the skin:

The Choir had known.

He hadn't told anyone at the guildhall what he'd done—he was sure of it, nearly sure—but the next morning, when he came in, Odile Vesper was waiting near the stair with that stillness she wore like a second cloak, and she said, "You've been eating again, Mr. Quill. Good. A scribe thinks better on a full stomach." And there were coins pressed into his palm before he could speak, more than his suspended wage, exactly the weight of a bounty, and her eyes held his for one long moment with something that was not quite kindness and not quite threat but was somehow both at once, the way a trap is both shelter and teeth.

She had known. She had let him do it. Perhaps she had arranged it—the stranger at the Lamplighter's Rest, the visible scars, the timing of it all—and let her starving scribe walk into the crown's arms carrying a report, so that the Choir would know exactly how the garrison verified claims, how long verification took, which sergeant kept which records. Or perhaps she had simply watched, curious, to see what hunger would make of him. To see whether he was the kind of asset who could be used again.

Either way, the coins in his hand were not wages. They were payment rendered. He had been entered into *her* ledger now, in a column marked with his name, and Odile Vesper never left a column blank.

* * *

That night Tobias did not sleep. Instead, by the light of a stub of candle he was saving for emergencies, he took a cheap quire of paper—the rough stock, the wash-colored kind—and he ruled it into columns with a steady hand that surprised him.

At the top he wrote: *What Was Done.* Then, beneath it, in his smallest, most careful script, the entry that had been burning in him for three days:

*Tuesday last. Reported a stranger at the Lamplighter's Rest to the garrison on Candlemaker's Row. Name unknown. Cleared Thursday, innocent of all charges. Bounty unpaid. Debt incurred: total.*

He looked at the words a long time. In the Choir they were taught that a ledger was a moral instrument—that every entry was a small act of truth-telling against the world's enormous drift toward lies. It was the first lesson, the founding lesson, the one Odile herself recited at every induction. *What is written remains.*

Very well. Let it remain.

He turned the page and began a second column, headed *What Was Witnessed,* and entered the arrest, the spitting boy, old Marta crossing the street, the sergeant's flat voice saying *cleared* like a door shutting. He entered the coins from Odile, their weight, their probable source. He entered the proclamation's promise and the city's silence. Page after page, night after night, while the snow came down over Vellichor like a country erasing itself, Tobias Quill kept his secret ledger—every report, every arrest, every neighbor's averted eyes, everything the crown's hunt was writing into the world.

Because here was the thought that had finally come to him, plain as a fair copy, undeniable as ink:

Someone was keeping accounts of the guilty. But no one was keeping accounts of the harmed. And a ledger with only one column was not a ledger at all. It was a weapon.

On the fifth night, near dawn, he heard footsteps in the corridor outside his cell of a room—soft, deliberate, stopping just briefly at his door before moving on. When he opened it, the hall was empty, but wedged under the far wall, where someone had clearly meant him to find it, lay a folded scrap of paper.

He recognized the handwriting before he recognized the words. He had spent years admiring that hand in the shared ledgers—precise, modest, always slightly warmer than clerical work required.

The note read: *"Tobias. If you have done something you cannot unsay, come find me. There is a door, and my mother knew its name. —U."*

Tobias stood in the freezing corridor with the note trembling in his hand, and somewhere below him, in the cellar of the Vesper Choir, he imagined the shelves of confiscated gifts standing silent in the dark, and above him the snow falling on a country that officially did not exist, and he understood that whatever came next would be written by people like him—or written over them.

He folded the note into his secret ledger, between the debts and the witnesses, and began to dress for the cold.
