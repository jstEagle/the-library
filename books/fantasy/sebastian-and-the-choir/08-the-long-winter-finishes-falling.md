## 8. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The snow came the way bad news comes—first a few flakes that no one took seriously, then all at once, everywhere, burying the roads and the gutters and the names of everyone who owed anything to anyone.

Ulyana watched it from the ledger room window, a candle guttering at their elbow, and understood with a clerk's cold clarity what it meant. The passes were closed. No one would leave Vellichor now by any road. Which meant the hunt could not leave either. It had come inside to winter with them.

They could hear it in the streets below: boots, and the particular rhythm of boots that were counting doors. Three knocks, a pause, three more. The soldiers had learned the city's architecture of fear quickly. They knocked on every door twice, because frightened people answered the second knock faster, and frightened people told you things.

Ulyana turned back to the ledgers and did what they always did when the world grew too loud: they worked. Ink, nib, the small civilization of columns. A jar of last year's summer needed re-cataloguing; its light had gone thin as broth. A key that opened yesterday had begun to rust, which meant yesterday was getting harder to reach. Ulyana wrote *condition: failing* beside it, and their handwriting sat on the page like a small animal, warm and alive, waiting to make itself true.

That was the thing about their gift. It never announced itself. It simply tidied. What Ulyana wrote became accurate, and accuracy was such a modest virtue that no one had ever thought to call it magic.

A sound at the door—not the soldiers' triple knock but a soft, arrhythmic scratching, like a bird trapped against glass.

Ulyana opened it on Tobias, snow in his hair, snow in his lashes, snow melting down his face in tracks that might have been something else entirely. He was holding a satchel against his chest the way one holds a wounded animal or an accusation.

"I need to show you something," he said. "And I need you to listen before you decide what I am."

* * *

Tobias had walked through the falling city for six hours deciding how to do this, and in those six hours he had composed forty openings and discarded all of them, because there was no honest way to begin except at the worst place.

So he began there.

"In autumn," he said, standing in the doorway of the ledger room while Ulyana lit a second candle, "I reported a stranger to the crown. A woman who sold mended shadows near the Tanner's Gate. I told them she had the gift. I told them where she slept."

Ulyana's face did not change. That was worse than anger. Tobias pressed on before the silence could finish him.

"They paid me eleven silver." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry broken thing. "Eleven. For a person. She had a laugh like a hinge, Ulyana. She used to give children little folded paper birds that cast two shadows, and both shadows were kind."

"You didn't know her," Ulyana said quietly. "You couldn't have known she was innocent."

"No." Tobias set the satchel on the table between them. "But here is what I learned after, and this is the part I need you to hear. I went back to the Tanner's Gate. I asked questions I should have been too afraid to ask. And I found out that Odile Vesper knew the woman. Had known her for years. Had bought from her, sold to her, kept her name in a ledger I have since seen with my own eyes." His hands were shaking; he let them. "Odile knew the woman had no gift at all. Knew it. And when I came to her with my report, trembling, asking if it was right to do such a thing—she thanked me. She praised my loyalty. She sent me home with coin and told me the Choir protects its own."

Ulyana looked at the satchel. "What's in it?"

"Proof," Tobias said. "Or what passes for proof, in a country that officially doesn't exist. Copies of correspondence. Names. Dates. Eleven years of it, Ulyana. Every time the hunt has come close to the Choir, someone has been fed to it. Someone small. Someone whose absence wouldn't trouble the ledgers. Odile calls it *pruning*. She has a word for it. That's the part I can't stop hearing—the word. Like we're a garden."

The candles burned. Somewhere below, the triple knock sounded and moved on, and a door opened, and a voice said something pleading, and the boots went away satisfied.

"Why are you showing me this?" Ulyana asked. "You could burn it. You could forget it. Most people would."

"Most people haven't helped kill anyone," Tobias said. He pulled out the first document—a list, in Odile's hand, of names surrendered to the crown across three winters—and laid it flat. "I've started keeping my own ledger. Of what I owe. And I can't pay it, Ulyana. There's no currency for it. But I can stop the account growing." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I heard what they're saying about you. In the scriptorium. That your handwriting does things. I didn't report it. I want you to know that. I stood in front of the proclamation board for an hour with the silver price in my head and I walked away, and walking away nearly killed me, because walking away felt like the first honest thing I'd done since autumn."

Ulyana touched the list of names. Their fingers stopped on one entry near the bottom, dated four years past: *M. Thornbury — surrendered, no gift confirmed, executed as precaution.*

Their mother.

The room went very far away and then very close again. When Ulyana spoke, their voice was almost normal, which is how you can tell when someone is holding themselves together with both hands.

"She wasn't pruned," Ulyana said. "She was harvested. She found the door, didn't she. She found it, and Odile sold her rather than let her use it."

Tobias said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and sometimes the truest response to grief is to stand witness without ornament.

"The cellar," Ulyana said at last. "I saw it. Confiscated gifts, row upon row. I thought it was hoarding. It's worse—it's a larder. She keeps us the way a farmer keeps seed grain, and eats whoever the season demands." They drew a slow breath. "Thank you, Tobias. For coming here instead of the barracks. I don't forgive you yet. I want to be honest about that—I'm a bookkeeper, and forgiveness is a debt, and debts take time. But I believe you intend to pay. That's worth more than most things in this building."

"That's more than I hoped for," Tobias whispered.

"Don't spend it all at once." Ulyana closed the satchel and held it against their chest, the way he had carried it. "There's something I have to do tonight. If I don't come back from it—" they hesitated, then chose truth, since truth was the evening's currency— "then take these papers to the debtors' hall and read every name aloud. All of them. Even hers. Especially hers."

"Where are you going?"

"To sign something," Ulyana said. "And to write something else beneath the signature."

* * *

Outside, the snow fell without pause or mercy or beauty, though it was beautiful anyway, the way terrible things sometimes are when they finally commit fully to arriving. It sealed the mountain passes like wax over a letter. It erased the roads out of Vellichor one white line at a time, so that the country that officially did not exist now barely existed even unofficially—just rooftops and chimneysmoke and the small stubborn lights of people who had not yet been counted.

In the ledger room, two liars sat together over a single candle and read the names of the dead aloud until dawn, keeping a new kind of inventory, and neither of them flinched from a single entry.

And in the cellar below, behind a wall of confiscated wonders, a door that had been waiting twenty years for a particular hand began, very softly, to warm.
