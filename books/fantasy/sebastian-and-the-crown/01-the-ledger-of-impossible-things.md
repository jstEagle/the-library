## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with trading in impossible things, Oscar had learned in his first month at Vell & Sons, was that the impossible still demanded receipts.

He sat at the long walnut desk in the counting room with a pot of ink going tacky beside him and three ledgers open in a fan: the daybook, the year-book, and — kept under a cloth because even the clerks weren't supposed to look at it too often — the oldest ledger, bound in grey leather gone soft as ear skin. Rain worked at the windows. Somewhere below, in the weighing room, a client was arguing about the weight of a memory, which was an argument that never went anywhere, because memories didn't weigh anything until you bottled them, and by then it was too late to argue.

Oscar dipped his pen. He was Sebastian today. He was Sebastian most days now, and the name sat on him like a coat cut slightly wrong in the shoulders — wearable, warm enough, but a reminder with every movement that he had not been born inside it.

*Item forty-one,* he wrote. *One (1) summer, aged nine, sold by T. Marlowe of Copper Lane. Condition: fair. Some water damage. Buyer satisfied.*

A whole summer. The man had come in on a Tuesday with rain still on him and said he couldn't afford to keep his ninth summer anymore, that it cost too much to carry, that every time he remembered his mother's kitchen he couldn't work for the rest of the day. Vell & Sons had paid him eleven crowns. Eleven crowns for a summer, and the buyer — some merchant's wife who collected childhoods the way other women collected lace — had been *satisfied*. Oscar reconciled the entry against the vault manifest, confirmed the bottle's location in Rack Nine, Drawer C, and moved on.

That was the job. That was the whole job. Things came into the guildhouse heavy with meaning and left light as inventory, and somewhere in between they passed through Oscar's pen, and his pen made them real. A stolen year became a line item. A borrowed face became a notation: *one (1) face, male, approx. thirty years, worn six months, returned with minor scarring, deposit forfeit.* He had written that one himself, three weeks ago, and had spent the evening after wondering what the scarring looked like, and whether the man who wore the face had been someone else's son once, too.

His old mistress would have had opinions about all this. Brannoc had opinions the way rivers had water. But Brannoc was two hundred miles and one entire former life behind him, and Oscar — *Sebastian* — did not think about her before noon if he could help it.

He turned to the oldest ledger.

This was the part of the day he both dreaded and loved, the way you might dread and love a cold swim. The oldest ledger predated the current guildmaster, predated the guildhouse itself, predated, according to the flyleaf, *the present troubles*, whatever those had been. Its entries were in hands long dead, and the ink had gone brown, and the things recorded there were stranger than summers and faces. *One (1) grief, communal, city-wide, purchased and distributed.* *One (1) name, royal, retired from circulation.* He'd read that last one a dozen times and never understood it. Names retired from circulation. Like coins clipped and melted down.

He ran his thumb along the page edges to find his place —

— and felt it before he saw it. A thinness. A whisper of paper where there should have been the confident thickness of a leaf.

He turned back. Turned again. There, between the entry for the communal grief and something recorded only as *the arrangement*, was a gap. Not torn roughly — no ragged edge, no violence. Cut. Sliced close to the spine with a razor or a very patient knife, so cleanly that if you weren't reconciling the book against its own stitching you would never know a page had ever lived there.

Someone had removed a leaf from the oldest ledger in the guildhouse of Vell & Sons.

Oscar sat very still. Rain ticked against the glass. Below, the argument about the weight of a memory had resolved into the particular silence of a client being overcharged.

Now, a sensible clerk — and Oscar was, on paper, a very sensible clerk — would have reported this. There were protocols. Irregularities in the historical ledgers went to the under-master, who went to Master Vell, who went wherever masters went when the books needed them. That was the machine working as designed. You fed it anomalies; it produced consequences.

But Oscar had been feeding machines all his life, first Brannoc's and now the guild's, and he had learned something in the feeding: the machine did not ask how you knew what you knew. It only asked what you knew. And if a page had been cut from the oldest ledger in the house — cut carefully, cut *privately* — then the person who cut it had access, and the people with access were few, and the people who asked questions of the few were fewer, and none of them were runaway apprentices whose entire existence depended on nobody looking too closely at their papers.

Because that was the thing. If the under-master began asking who had handled the oldest ledger, the answer would be: everyone, eventually, but most recently the new boy, the quiet one, the one who'd arrived eight months ago with references that were good but not *deep*, the one called Sebastian who wrote a beautiful hand and asked no questions. References could be checked. References were always checked eventually. It was a miracle they hadn't been already, and miracles, Oscar knew from hard experience, had short terms and high interest.

So he did what he had gotten frighteningly good at doing. He made the absence look intentional.

He took a slip of fresh paper and wrote, in his best clerical hand: *Leaf removed for restoration binding, per Under-Master Corvane's instruction, 14th of the Wet Month. See restoration docket.* Then he slipped it into the gap, sized to the missing page, and closed the ledger over it gently, the way you'd close a coffin over someone you hoped was sleeping.

There was no restoration docket. There was no such instruction. Under-Master Corvane had never in eight months given Oscar any instruction beyond *mind the ink* and *don't whistle in the vaults*. But the note would buy time. Anyone who opened the book would see a bureaucratic explanation, and bureaucracy was the one magic everyone in the guildhouse believed in without question.

He sat back. His heart was going harder than the work warranted, and he knew why, and he didn't want to know why. Because covering for the missing page hadn't felt like fear. It had felt like *choice*. For eight months he had done exactly as instructed, kept his head down, kept his name folded away like contraband, and it had been safe — safe the way a cellar is safe, safe the way small things are safe. And now, for the first time, he had acted instead of recorded, lied instead of transcribed, and some low animal part of him had *purred*.

That was the danger. Not the enforcers, not the audits, not the checking of references. The danger was that hiding was starting to feel less like survival and more like a vocation, and vocations, in Oscar's experience, always ended with someone asking you to give up everything else.

He finished the reconciliation. Item forty-two: half a lullaby, sold twice, which shouldn't have been possible and was noted as such with the guild's customary shrug. Item forty-three: twenty years of a marriage, amicably divided, each spouse keeping alternate decades. His pen moved. The rain moved. The daybook filled.

At dusk, Tam the junior clerk stuck his head in, cheeks red from the stairs. "Master wants the oldest ledger up for the quarter review. Tonight."

"Fine," Oscar said. "It's in order."

"You're sure? Corvane's been in a mood all week."

"It's in order," Oscar said again, and heard, in his own voice, a steadiness he hadn't earned.

Tam shrugged and went. Oscar wrapped the ledger in its cloth, and his fingers paused on the knot, and he thought: *I could look. Tonight, before it goes up. I could find what's written just before the gap, and just after, and read the shape of the hole.*

He thought about it the way you think about a door you've walked past a thousand times without opening.

Then he tied the knot, carried the ledger upstairs through the smelling-of-wax corridors, and laid it on Master Vell's table among the candlesticks and the quarter accounts, and went back down to his desk, and told himself the feeling in his chest was only hunger, because he'd missed supper.

But he didn't believe it. Because underneath the hunger was something else, something with a shape he recognized from the worst nights of his running — the shape of a thing approaching. He just couldn't hear it yet.

Three streets away and seven hours later, a courier with blood in his boots would begin looking for the night door of Vell & Sons. But that was tomorrow's entry, and the books, as yet, said nothing about it at all.
