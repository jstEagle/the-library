## 2. A Stranger at the Weighing Door

The night door of Vell & Sons opened inward, which was a design flaw Oscar had noted on his third day and never been permitted to fix. It meant the wind off the canal came straight through the receiving hall, and it meant that when the door groaned open past midnight, anyone standing at the weighing counter saw the whole street behind it — and the whole street saw in.

Oscar was alone because Oscar was always alone on reconciliation nights. The other clerks went home to wives and lodgings; he had neither. He told them he liked the quiet. What he actually liked was that a small, narrow life, kept carefully in order, drew no attention. He had learned that lesson under Brannoc's roof, where being noticed was the same as being caught.

So when the door swung wide at half past the twelfth bell, he saw first the fog, then the shape staggering through it, and his hand did what his hand had been trained to do: it slid the ledger shut and covered it with his forearm, as though he were merely resting.

The man made it four steps inside before his legs quit. He went down against the weighing door itself — the great brass-bounded scale set into the floor where incoming goods were assessed — and caught himself on its rim with one hand, leaving a smear that would take an hour to scrub out. Blood, Oscar thought, distantly. That's blood. A lot of it.

The courier was young. Younger than Oscar. He wore the oilskin of a river-runner, soaked through, and his left boot was dark and heavy in a way boots shouldn't be. His face had gone the color of tallow.

"Guildhouse," the man said. "This is — this is Vell's?"

"It's closed," Oscar heard himself say. "Come back—"

The courier laughed, or coughed, or something between the two, and dragged himself up onto one knee beside the scale. "No time. No time for closed." He fumbled at his chest, inside the oilskin, fingers clumsy with cold and wet. "You keep ledgers. You're the night clerk. I know the rotation. I've watched this door three nights."

That should have frightened Oscar more than the blood did. It didn't, quite. There was a strange relief in being known, even by a stranger — in mattering enough to be watched.

"I don't know what you think we trade here," Oscar said.

"I know exactly what you trade." The courier got the thing free at last: a roll of stiff vellum, bound in wax the deep red of arterial blood, stamped with a seal Oscar didn't recognize — not guild, not crown, not any house sigil from the heraldry primers he'd memorized to pass as educated. "Then hold it. Hold it and understand it. You're good at understanding. I can tell."

"How?"

"The way you stand. Clerks who are hiding stand like clerks who are hiding." The courier pressed the roll into Oscar's hand, folding his fingers over it with terrible strength for a dying man. His palm was ice. "They took my brother's face two years ago. Sold it. I've spent everything since finding out where they keep the things they steal."

"You have the wrong—" Oscar began, and stopped, because lying to a dying man sat differently in his throat than lying to a living one.

"The map," the courier said. "It charts the crown. The true crown. Not the tin hat the regent wears to funerals. *The* crown."

Every apprentice at Vell & Sons was taught the same first lesson, drilled by Maren herself in her dry ashen voice: *We do not deal in politics. Politics is a market with only two buyers, and both will cheat you.* Oscar recited it now, internally, like a prayer. We do not deal in politics.

"The crown remembers," the courier said.

"What does that mean? What remembers?"

But the man's eyes had gone somewhere else. He sagged against the scale's brass rim, and his breathing changed — the long, dragging pulls of someone whose body has begun closing accounts. "Don't give it back," he whispered. "Whatever they offer. Whatever they threaten. Don't let them put it back where they found it." His grip tightened one last time around Oscar's wrist. "There was a king. There *was* a king. They took him out of the world like a page torn from—"

He died between one word and the next. Oscar would think about that afterward — that there hadn't been a last breath so much as a sentence interrupted, mid-phrase, the way a ledger line simply stops when the ink runs out.

* * *

Oscar did not move for what felt like a long time. It cannot have been, because when the knocking started at the front entrance, the blood on the scale was still wet.

Three knocks. Paused. Three again. The rhythm of people who expected doors to open for them.

Through the shuttered window of the receiving hall he could see lantern-light on the canal stones — four men, maybe five, in the grey travel cloaks that every clerk in Vell & Sons knew on sight. Enforcers. The guild called them *reconcilers*, which had always struck Oscar as a joke told with a straight face.

His heart was doing something frantic and animal. But his hands — his hands were already moving, and that was the part that frightened him most, later. Because the decision was made below thought, in some cellar of himself that had apparently been waiting years for exactly this moment.

He couldn't leave the map in the receiving hall. They'd search it; enforcers searched everything, it was practically their motto. He couldn't carry it on his person — same problem. And he could not, absolutely could not, let it be found on the dead courier, because then the question would become *who else did he speak to*, and Oscar was the only answer available.

The dead man needed to look untouched.

He rolled the courier onto his back — the weight of it shocking, real, nothing like the neat entries he reconciled all day — and arranged the oilskin loosely, the way a man falls, not the way a man is laid. He wiped the scale's rim with his apron. Then, holding the map, he stood in the middle of the room and did the hardest thing he'd done since running from Brannoc: he waited, and let himself be afraid, and did nothing anyway.

The knocks came again. "Vell & Sons! Open in the name of the guild!"

"We're closed," Oscar called, and was surprised his voice held. "Receiving resumes at dawn."

A pause. Then, muffled through the shutters: "We seek a runner off the *Grey Tern*. He's carrying fever. Guild quarantine requires we recover anything he touched."

Fever. Quarantine. It was competent lying — specific, procedural, the kind that works on people who trust procedure. Which was, Oscar realized with a chill, precisely what he was.

"He came through the night door already," Oscar said. "He's dead."

Silence outside. Long enough that Oscar counted his own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve.

The bolts shot back, and Maren entered first.

She was smaller than her reputation, as she always was — a compact woman in undyed wool, silver-streaked hair pinned without ornament, eyes like a moneylender assessing collateral. Two reconcilers flanked her, and behind them, filling the doorway, a third with a crossbow held low but not carelessly.

Maren looked at the body. Looked at the scale. Looked at Oscar.

"Night clerk," she said. "Sebastian, isn't it."

"Yes, mistress."

"Tell me what happened."

And here it was. The first lie. He had rehearsed lies his entire adult life — the lie of his name, the lie of his history, the lie of his small harmless ambitions — but those were walls, not words. This was different. This required him to build something new, in front of its intended audience, while his pulse hammered in his ears loud enough that he was certain she could hear it.

"He forced the door," Oscar said. "I told him we were closed. He collapsed against the scale. He spoke — fever-talk, mostly nonsense — and then he died. I haven't touched anything else. I thought it best to wait rather than disturb... whatever needs disturbing."

*Fever-talk, mostly nonsense.* Four words. He had just described a dying man's testimony as delirium, and done it smoothly, and Maren was nodding.

"Nonsense," she repeated. "What sort of nonsense?"

"Kings," Oscar said. "Something about kings."

Something crossed Maren's face. It was gone too fast to name, but it was not surprise, and it was not confusion. It looked, horribly, like *recognition* — the expression of a woman hearing an echo she'd hoped had finished echoing.

"Search him," she said to the reconcilers. "Gently. He's a clerk, not a criminal."

They patted down his apron, his sleeves, his boots. The map was not on him. The map was, at that exact moment, inside the binding of the *Master Reconciliation Ledger, Year of the Regent 12* — because in the ninety seconds between the courier's death and the first knock, Oscar had slit the hollow spine-seam with his letter knife, rolled the vellum tight, and sewn it shut again with thread pulled from the binding itself. Nine years of bookbinding drills, hated at the time, performed nightly until his stitches were indistinguishable from the original. Brannoc had taught him that too. *Anything worth hiding,* she used to say, *is worth hiding well.*

The reconcilers found nothing. Of course they found nothing. Oscar Sebastian, runaway apprentice, keeper of small tidy lies, was exactly the kind of man nothing was ever found on.

"Take the body," Maren said. "Full processing. And the scale gets scoured before dawn — I won't have contamination near the incoming shipments." She turned to go, then paused at the threshold, and looked back at him with an expression he would spend the rest of the week trying to read.

"You stood your ground well, Sebastian," she said. "Most clerks run, or faint, or try to rob the corpse. You waited and protected the inventory." A thin smile. "That's good instinct. Where did you learn good instinct?"

"A hedge-witch," Oscar said. "Before this life."

"Mm," said Maren. "Hedge-witches. Yes." And there it was again, that flicker — not suspicion exactly, but attention, the way a weighmaster's thumb rests on the scale when something doesn't balance.

She left. The bolts shot home. The lanterns moved off down the canal, and Oscar stood alone in the receiving hall with a corpse-shaped absence on the floor and a heartbeat that refused to slow.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He told himself it was protocol — the body needed reporting, the entry needed writing, the morning shift needed briefing. All true. None of it the reason.

At his desk, by one candle, he opened the *Year 12* ledger to its spine and drew out the map with hands that shook only after the work was done, which he was learning was how fear worked: it arrived late, politely, once the emergency had concluded.

The vellum was old. Older than the courier, older than the seal. He weighted its corners flat and held the candle close.

It was a map of the city — his city — but wrong. Streets ran where canals now flowed; the river bent differently; and beneath it all, rendered in faded iron-gall ink, a network of vaults and galleries threaded under the water like roots under a lawn. At the center, marked with a symbol he almost recognized from the oldest ledgers' marginalia — a circle crowned with a broken line — sat a chamber labeled in a dead dialect he could only partly parse.

But one word he could read. Everyone in the trade could read it, because it appeared in the guild's founding documents and in the regent's oaths and nowhere else:

*Cathair.*

The old capital's name. The name that had been struck from every signpost and charter when the kingdom unified, the way you strike a debtor's name after the debt is forgiven — except nobody remembered the debt, either. Nobody remembered why the word felt like a bruise when spoken aloud. Nobody even knew it should feel like one.

Oscar sat back. His candle guttered. Somewhere above him, the guildhouse settled into its nighttime creaks, and the canal slapped at the pilings, indifferent.

*The crown remembers.*

He thought about the torn page in the oldest ledger — the gap he'd covered for in Chapter One of his own quiet life, the absence he'd smoothed over without asking why. He thought about Maren saying *hedge-witches, yes* in a voice that had recognized something. He thought about a dying man who had watched the night door for three days and chosen *him*, specifically, out of every clerk in Vell & Sons — chosen the one whose stitches were invisible, whose lies were structural, whose name was borrowed.

Had he been chosen? Or placed?

The question slid into him coldly, like a knife between binding boards, and he pushed it away, because it was too large and the hour was too small. Tomorrow he would decode the rest. Tomorrow he would find out what the guild had erased, and whether the courier's ghost had picked a fool or a weapon.

For tonight, one task remained. He re-rolled the map, returned it to the hollow spine, and sewed the seam shut — nine stitches, even tension, matching thread.

Then he opened a fresh ledger page, dipped his pen, and wrote the official record of the night in his best clerk's hand:

*Night of the 14th: one courier deceased at receiving door, cause apparent fever. Remains released to guild processing. No goods received. No irregularities.*

*No irregularities.*

Oscar looked at the words for a long time. Then he blotted them, closed the book, and blew out the candle — and in the dark, for the first time since he'd fled Brannoc's cottage, he smiled.

It was not a comfortable smile. It was the smile of a man who has just discovered that the locked room he thought was keeping the world out might, in fact, have been keeping him in.

Somewhere under the river, a crown was waiting to remember. And now, whether he wanted it or not, so was he.
