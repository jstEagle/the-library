## 3. What the Guild Erased

The map did not want to be read.

Oscar discovered this on his second night with it, hunched over the binding-slit of *Ledger IX* where he'd hidden the thing, working by candle stub in the counting room after Vell's clerks had gone home to their suppers. The parchment was wax-sealed and heavier than paper had any right to be, and when he finally peeled the seal back, the surface showed him nothing. Blank vellum. A faint water stain shaped like nothing at all.

He tried ink. He tried holding it over steam. He tried the clerk's trick of raking candlelight across it at a shallow angle, hunting for impressions left by a stylus that had never touched it.

Nothing. The map sat in his hands like a closed door.

It was Brannoc's voice that solved it, arriving uninvited from three years away: *Everything true is paid for. Nothing worth knowing shows itself for free.*

Blood, then. Or something like it.

His hand shook so badly the first drop missed entirely, landing on the ledger's spine. The second fell true — a single bead from a nicked thumb pressed to the corner of the vellum — and the map drank it. He watched the red spread outward like frost blooming in reverse, veins of it crawling across the blankness, and then the stain lifted, cleared, and lines remained where blood had been. Coastlines. A river. And at the center of it all, beneath a crossing marked with a king's crown drawn upside down, a chamber no bigger than a chapel.

*The hollow beneath the river.*

He knew that crossing. Everyone in Correnmark knew the Tollbridge, or thought they did. There were warehouses on the near bank and a drowned mill on the far one and children dared each other to swim under the arches. Nobody had ever suggested there was anything *under* it but mud and old pilings.

Around the edges of the map, in a cramped antique hand, someone had written marginalia. Not directions. *Arguments.* Oscar read them twice before he understood what he was looking at, and when he understood, he set the map down on the ledger and stepped away from his own desk as if it had bitten him.

> *The erasure holds because it is total. One mind remembering is a crack. Ten thousand is a breach.*
>
> *We did not win the war. We unmade its cause. Let no successor guild call this mercy lightly — we took the war out of every head alive, and what we took we buried with its reason.*

There was more, but he couldn't read any more that night. His eyes kept sliding off the letters the way they slid off fine print in bad light. He rolled the map, slotted it back into the binding of *Ledger IX*, pinched out the candle, and lay awake in his cot above the counting room listening to the guildhouse settle around him like an animal rearranging itself in sleep.

A runaway apprentice keeps ledgers for a guild that trades in impossible things. That had been the whole of his story for three years, and he had liked the shape of it. Small, safe, explicable.

Now the shape had a hole torn in the middle of it, exactly the size of a missing page.

* * *

In the morning he went looking for the oldest ledger.

Vell & Sons kept its deep archive behind two doors and a clerk who owed Oscar a favor involving a miscounted shipment of bottled Sundays. The favor got him through the first door. The archive's inner room held the guild's founding records, and there — third shelf, between a ledger of purchased deaths and one of returned faces — stood the gap where the oldest book should have been.

Not gone. *Replaced.* Someone had shelved a dummy in its place: same dimensions, same oxblood leather, spine lettered in faded gilt. But when he drew it out, it weighed almost nothing. Hollow. Inside, where six hundred years of entries should have been, there was nothing but a note in a hand he recognized from the map's margins.

> *Removed by order of M.V., Guildmaster. The record is not lost. The record is dangerous. Some things are only safe when no one has written them down.*

M.V. Maren Vell herself. The woman who signed his wages. Who had, three years ago, personally interviewed a hedge-witch's runaway boy and — he remembered now, with a coldness spreading up through his chest — asked strangely few questions. Had looked at him too long when he gave the name Sebastian. Had said, *Well then, Sebastian. Let's see how you keep books,* as though she were testing a lock she already owned the key to.

He put the hollow ledger back precisely where it had been, squared to the finger-width, and walked out past the sleeping clerk without breathing.

* * *

That night he didn't hide in his cot. He hid in the counting room, and he read the rest of the map.

The blood-ink had faded by morning and needed renewing; he cut his thumb again without ceremony this time, and the lines came up faster, as if the map were growing used to him. The marginalia resolved into a full argument, a case being made to some future reader — and Oscar understood, with a lurch, that he *was* the future reader. That the courier had died pressing this document specifically toward the guild's night door, toward whichever apprentice happened to open it. Toward him.

> *You will ask what right we had. Ask instead what the war was. Two crowns, one true. The pretender held the city; the true king held the people; and both held the same claim, which is that God and blood agree. Fourteen years of it. We counted the dead in ledgers because no one else would count them at all.*
>
> *So we bought every memory of the true king. Every song, every face, every name. We did not kill him. Killing makes martyrs, and martyrs make wars. We made him no one. We put him somewhere time pools and forgot to flow, and we erased him so thoroughly that even our own grandchildren could never restore him by accident.*
>
> *This is the guild's great sin and its great mercy, and I no longer know which word belongs to which act. If you hold this map, you hold the choice we made once already. Choose better than we did, or choose nothing. Choosing nothing is also allowed. It is what most of you will do.*

Below that, the final entry, in a shakier hand:

> *The erasure is fraying. The preacher's flock dreams of a face no painting shows. Our own enforcers report dreams. A thing erased leaves a shape, and shapes fill in. When the dreaming spreads to the streets, someone will come asking, and the answer must be ready or there will be no one left to give it.*

Oscar sat back. The candle had burned to a puddle. Somewhere below, the night door rattled in its frame — wind, probably — and he flinched hard enough to knock the inkwell over.

His apprenticeship. Three years of reconciling entries, of learning which memories aged well and which soured, of being praised for discretion and rewarded with small raises and a slightly better cot. He had built the whole edifice of himself on a simple story: *I ran from Brannoc, I hid here, I am safe because I am small.*

But runaways weren't interviewed by guildmasters. Runaways weren't placed in the counting room nearest the deep archive. Runaways didn't get the night-door shift, the one shift where a bleeding stranger could reach someone before the enforcers reached the stranger.

And the courier hadn't stumbled against that door. He'd aimed for it. Aimed for the one person inside who would open it, who would take the map, who would *read* it — and Oscar felt the vertigo rise through him like floodwater as he saw the other possibility lying underneath his whole life like the chamber under the river: that Brannoc had always known where he fled. That Maren had known he was coming. That the hedge-witch who taught him to pay for truth in blood had trained him *because* something about him rang strange, threw gaps, cast a wrongness that witches could feel —

Erased bloodlines throw off gaps.

He didn't know where he'd learned that phrase. It wasn't on the map. It surfaced from somewhere deeper, some shelf in himself he'd never inventoried, and it fit his life the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

He was not a runaway who had found a guild. He might be a piece the guild had placed. Or a piece someone else had moved onto the board first.

Oscar pressed his bleeding thumb flat against the map's edge and whispered, to no one, to the dead courier, to whoever had written arguments to a future reader: "Who am I supposed to be?"

The map, being a map, answered with geography. The upside-down crown beneath the Tollbridge. The hollow beneath the river. And in the newest margin, fainter than the rest, in ink still drying though the blood had dried hours ago, one fresh line:

> *She knows you went home. Go home.*

* * *

Across the city, in a room with no windows above the weighing floor, Maren Vell opened her private journal — a slim volume bound in the same oxblood as the hollow ledger, because symmetry comforted her — and wrote by lamplight with the unhurried hand of a woman who had been writing the same sentence, in variations, for forty years.

> *The courier is dead. The map is loose. I have known this for two days, and I have done nothing, and my doing-nothing is itself a decision I do not like examining.*
>
> *The boy — Sebastian, or whatever his true name is, if even Brannoc ever knew it — has the archive smell on him. I passed him on the stairs tonight and he smelled of dust and old vellum and fear. He covered well. He has always covered well. Too well, for a runaway.*
>
> *I could take the map from him tomorrow. I could erase him tonight and sleep soundly, the way my grandmother slept after the Great Forgetting. But an erasure is fraying, and you do not mend a fraying cloth by cutting another piece. Sooner or later someone must look at what we did with open eyes and decide it anew. I had hoped never to be that someone. I had hoped, frankly, to be dead first.*
>
> *If he goes to the river, he will need protecting from everyone including me. If he does not go, then perhaps the shape fills itself in and we begin again the slow work of forgetting.*
>
> *Note for whatever poor clerk inherits this journal: we told ourselves we ended the war. Read the oldest entries. We did not end it. We amputated it, and amputation is not healing, it is only a smaller wound that never closes.*

She blotted the page, closed the journal, and turned the lamp down to a coal. Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the boy moving in the counting room long past his hours — pacing, stopping, pacing again — a man walking the perimeter of his own life, checking for the place where the wall had quietly become a door.
