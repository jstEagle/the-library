## 4. The Zealot's Sermon

Oscar counted other people's sins for a living. That was the joke he never made aloud: a ledger was just a confession with columns.

He sat in the counting room above the weighing floor, lamplit, reconciling the week's entries while the city went about its evening below. Stolen years, left column. Borrowed faces, right. A widow in Copperlane had sold three days of her wedding to pay a debt; somewhere a man was living them now, kissing a wife who wasn't his, and Oscar had initialed the transfer in neat brown ink. The guild called it *reconciliation*. He had once called it theft. He'd stopped calling it anything, because naming it required deciding, and deciding was dangerous.

The map lay hidden in the binding of Ledger Nine, sewn into the spine beneath the false cover, where his fingers kept drifting back to it like a tongue to a broken tooth.

*The crown remembers.*

Three nights since the courier died against the night door, and Oscar still hadn't decoded more than fragments — a river, a depth in fathoms, a sigil that matched no house he knew. But enforcers had begun asking questions with their eyes instead of their mouths. Maren had summoned him twice for no reason at all, studied him across her desk like an entry that wouldn't balance, and dismissed him. Someone had torn a page from the oldest ledger before Oscar ever found the gap. Someone else wanted this story unread.

He dipped his pen. His hand was steady. That frightened him most of all — how quickly terror became routine, how fast a lie learned to sit comfortably in the mouth.

Below, the weighing bell rang once, late, wrong. Oscar set down his pen.

Then came the shouting.

* * *

Sister Ivet stood on the milk crates in the tannery yard because pulpits were for churches and churches were for men who needed walls to say what they believed.

"Brothers. Sisters." Her voice carried without effort; she'd preached in salt storms off the Gray Quay, and a hundred upturned faces in a tannery yard was nothing. "You have dreamed. Do not be ashamed of it. You have dreamed of a face you cannot name, a crown you cannot describe, a grief you were never given."

Murmurs. She saw them nodding — the dyer with his stained hands, the two sisters who'd come every week since the dreams started, the old soldier who wept during the litany and didn't know why. They all dreamed it now. Fragments. A hall of green stone. A voice saying *rise*. Hands reaching for something just out of frame, always just out of frame, as if God Himself had put a thumb over the center of the picture.

"You ask me: Sister, why do we mourn a king whose name no chronicle keeps?" She let the silence stretch. "Because they took him from you. Not from your books. From *you*. From the place inside where a people keep its heart. There was a war, and there was an ending, and I tell you the ending was not peace. It was surgery. Someone cut the true king out of every mind alive and stitched the wound shut and called the scar a kingdom."

She let them hate for a moment. It was a gift she gave them freely; hatred was easier than grief.

"The guild did this," she said. "Vell & Sons, who weigh years like flour. Who sell you your own pasts back by the ounce. Ask yourselves who profits when a nation forgets its crown. Ask who has been keeping the ledger of your amnesia for a hundred years — and charging interest."

The old soldier stood. "What do we do, Sister?"

Ivet climbed down from the crates. She took his weathered hands in hers.

"We find the map," she said gently. "There is a map, brothers. It exists. It is written down, because everything is written down — that is the guild's whole religion, ink and arithmetic. And somewhere in this city is a clerk, or a courier, or a keeper of bindings who knows where it sleeps." She raised her voice again, warm as a hearth. "We are not cruel people. We are grieving people. But grief that is refused becomes fire, and I would rather we burn with purpose than be burned as fools. Bring me the one who knows. Bring him to me kindly if you can."

She paused at the door of the tannery office, where two of her sturdiest faithful were already waiting, and added, quieter, only for them:

"If kindly fails, then not."

* * *

They took Pellam Wray from the guildhouse annex on Cordwainer Street at dusk.

Oscar heard about it the way bad news travels in a guild — sideways, in pieces, each messenger pretending to know less than the last. Pellam was a senior clerk, sixty, half-deaf, famous for watering his tea three times. He had access to the archive. He had access to everything.

By midnight, Oscar was standing in Maren's office watching her listen to a report he could see she already knew by heart.

"Royalists," said the enforcer, a flat-faced woman named Corvane. "A dozen or more. They held him in a tannery off Ropewalk. He's at Saint Ferren's now. He'll live, but—" Corvane hesitated, which enforcers did not do. "They asked him about a map. About the old vaults under the river. They used words like *restoration* and *the true line*. Words nobody's spoken in this building since before my time."

Maren sat very still behind her desk. In the lamplight she looked less like a guildmaster than like a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"And what did Pellam tell them?"

"Nothing he knew. Possibly nothing at all. He's confused. They may have thought he was lying." Corvane's jaw tightened. "He wasn't lying. He doesn't know anything. That's what frightens me. If they're torturing clerks who know nothing—"

"Then they'll keep going until they find one who does." Maren lifted her eyes to Oscar, standing by the door with a ledger under his arm he had no reason to be holding. "Sebastian. You reconcile the archive. Has anything gone missing? Any binding disturbed, any seal broken?"

Here it was. The question he'd been rehearsing answers to for three days, arriving dressed as a formality.

"No, Guildmaster," Oscar said. "Everything balances."

It was the second lie he'd told her, and it came out smooth as poured wax, and some small honest part of him noted with horror that he was getting good at this.

"Double-check," Maren said. "Quietly. If so much as a thread is loose in that archive, I want to know before anyone else does. Before *anyone* else." Something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion exactly. Weight. "The guild ended a war once, Sebastian. Whatever these zealots think they're digging toward, we buried it for reasons that would take longer than your life to explain. We will not dig it up again because a preacher had dreams."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

He bowed and left, and walked home through streets that had changed shape around him. Every doorway might hold a royalist. Every friendly face might be an enforcer's informant. And between the two of them — the guild that would erase again rather than confess, and the faithful who would torture a tea-watering old clerk to reach their phantom king — lay the city, flammable as a hayrick.

Oscar stood on the bridge over the river and looked down at the black water moving under the lamps.

*Under the river.* The map's fathoms. The sigil. He hadn't known what he was carrying until tonight, and now he understood it precisely: he was carrying the match into a room full of oil, and both sides could smell it on him.

Bookkeeping, he thought, was over. Neutrality had been a luxury item, and the invoice had just come due.

* * *

Sister Ivet visited Pellam Wray's sickroom at Saint Ferren's on the third morning, wearing a merchant's gray and carrying flowers, because even righteous work required craft.

Pellam was asleep, bandaged, breathing shallowly. She stood over him a long moment and felt no triumph. Only the old cold certainty, the one that had carried her through twenty years of preaching to congregations who didn't know what they were mourning.

"I'm sorry," she told the sleeping man sincerely. "You were a wall between the people and their king. Walls get broken. It isn't personal."

One of her faithful, waiting by the door, murmured: "He truly knew nothing, Sister. The healers say his confusion is real."

"Then someone else knew something and stayed silent while an old man paid for it." Ivet set the flowers down anyway — cruelty was not the same as faithlessness. "A clerk who knows nothing but lies about a map. Think about what that means. Someone below him in the archive is hiding it. Young enough to be afraid, senior enough to have access."

"The new one," the faithful said. "The quiet one. Sebastian."

Ivet turned the name over. It meant nothing. That was itself interesting — names meant things, in her experience. Names had histories, families, debts. A name that meant nothing was either very boring or very well made.

"Watch him," she said. "Don't touch him. A man carrying a secret walks differently — heavier on one side. Find out which side." She pulled her hood up and paused at the window, looking out at the city roofs, at the guildhall spire rising above them like a needle sewing the sky shut.

"They erased a king," she said softly, to the glass, to God, to whoever kept the true ledger. "But you cannot erase a dream forever. It leaks. It leaks through sleeping minds and cracked bells and old songs nobody remembers learning. And when it finishes leaking, sister-brothers, there will be a reckoning — and I intend to be standing at the front of it with the crown in my hands and mercy in my heart, in that order."

* * *

Oscar didn't go home that night.

He went to the archive, lit one lamp, and pulled Ledger Nine from its shelf with hands that finally, properly shook. He cut the stitches, drew out the map, and spread it under the light — river, vault, fathoms, sigil — and forced himself to look at it not as a clerk but as what he had become: a man in possession of the thing both armies would kill for.

Somewhere out there, Pellam Wray was learning what questions cost. Somewhere out there, Ivet's faithful were walking the streets heavy on one side, hunting a man who walked like guilt.

And somewhere under the river, in the dark the map promised, whatever the guild had buried was waiting — patient as an unpaid debt.

Oscar folded the map along its old creases, and understood, with terrible clarity, that he could not stay in Vell & Sons another day. The counting room was a country that no longer existed. He would need help to move unseen, help to read the rest of the cipher, help that didn't wear a guild badge or a preacher's gray.

He blew out the lamp and slipped into the night, toward the one quarter of the city where neither law nor faith kept order.

The salt markets opened at dawn.
