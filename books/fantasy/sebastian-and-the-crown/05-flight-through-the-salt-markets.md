## 5. Flight Through the Salt Markets

The salt markets woke before dawn, as they always did, in a haze of white dust and shouting. Oscar moved through them with his collar up and his ledger-hand tucked inside his coat, where the map's binding pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

He had told himself he was only running an errand. Vell & Sons kept a counting-house contact among the salt factors — a woman named Pell who bought memory-salt by the barrel and never asked what it had been scraped from — and a junior clerk fetching her quarterly tallies was the most invisible thing in Virelay. That was the story. He'd rehearsed it twice on the walk over.

But enforcers did not wait for stories.

He saw them at the same moment they saw him: three figures in undyed wool moving against the current of the crowd, unhurried, the way water is unhurried when it finds a crack in a hull. The tall one raised two fingers — not a shout, not yet. Guild manners. *Come quietly and we'll only ruin you.*

Oscar turned and walked the other way, fast, and the market closed around him like a fist.

* * *

The salt markets of Virelay were built for goods, not people. Rows of trestles stacked with barrels; awnings bleached grey; the air so thick with brine that every breath tasted of preservation, of things kept past their season. Porters shouldered past with sacks that left white handprints on everything they touched. A factor's boy cried the price of grey salt from Kessrin. Somewhere a knife was being sharpened against a strop with long, patient strokes.

Oscar ducked under a trestle, came up between two barrels, and heard the tall enforcer's voice behind him, mild as arithmetic:

"Sebastian. Master Maren would like a word about the night door."

*The night door.* They knew. Of course they knew — the courier had died at their threshold, and whatever Oscar had said to Maren in the grey morning after, she had weighed it and found it light. He wondered, with a strange floating calm, whether she had known since the hour it happened. Whether the errand to Pell's counting-house had been bait dressed as trust.

He ran.

Not well. He was a ledger-keeper; his legs were made for stairs and standing desks. But the market was crowded and the enforcers were guild-trained, which meant they moved like men who expected the world to make way politely, and the world declined. A porter's sack caught the tall one across the chest. A fishwife screamed at the second for knocking her tray. Oscar vaulted a barrel of pickled eels, slipped on brine, went down hard on one palm, tasted salt and blood, got up.

They were still coming. They would always still be coming. That was the thing about enforcers — they were not angry, they were *patient*, and patience in a narrow lane is worse than fury.

The lane narrowed. Stalls leaned together overhead, awning to awning, and ahead the crowd thickened around a salt-fire performer — a woman spinning flame off wet crystal, drawing oohs from a hundred bodies packed shoulder to shoulder. No gap. Behind him, boots. To the left, a wall. To the right—

To the right, a dead-end alley stacked with empty casks, and above it, a ledge, and above the ledge, the roofline of the tannery row.

There was no time to think, which was fortunate, because thinking would have told him what he already knew: there was only one way out of this, and it was the way he had sworn never to use again.

Brannoc's voice came back to him across four years and one burned bridge, dry as chalk: *"Magic isn't power, boy. It's attention. You pay attention to something until it agrees to be other than it is."*

The hedge-trick had no name she'd ever given him. She'd taught it on a winter evening when he was fourteen, bored, complaining that the fire wouldn't catch. She'd taken his hand, held it toward the hearth, and said: *Don't ask the fire to burn. Ask the cold to leave.* And the cold had left — visibly, a shimmer like heat-haze running backward out of the room, and the kindling had caught without a spark.

It worked on anything that could be persuaded to be elsewhere. Cold. Light. Attention.

Oscar stopped running. He turned in the mouth of the alley, put his back against the casks, and looked at the three enforcers closing down the lane — and he paid attention to their attention.

It felt like holding water in cupped hands while running. Their eyes were on him; he gathered their eyes the way Brannoc had gathered cold, felt the weight of six gazes as almost a physical thing, warm and heavy and *agreeable*, if you asked correctly. *Not here,* he asked. *Look elsewhere. There is nothing in this alley worth your notice.*

The shimmer ran backward through the lane. The tall enforcer's gaze slid off Oscar like rain off waxed canvas. The man frowned, scanned the crowd, scanned the rooftops, and said to the others: "Lost him. Fan out toward the fish gate."

They walked past him. One of them — the young one, the one with ink-stained fingers who reconciled seizure reports — glanced directly at Oscar for half a heartbeat, and something flickered in his face, doubt or mercy or simply a mind that resisted persuasion more than most. Then he too looked away, and followed his fellows into the crowd.

Oscar stood in the alley shaking, palm bleeding, salt dust settling on his shoulders like a judgment.

Four years of nothing. Four years of honest columns and false names, of keeping his hands clean of the one inheritance Brannoc had actually given him. And it had come back in ten seconds, easy as breathing, because it had never really left him — because you can run from a mistress but not from what she taught your bones.

"You're leaking," said a voice above him.

* * *

The smuggler sat on the tannery roof with his legs dangling over the ledge, eating an apple, watching Oscar with the frank appraisal of a man pricing livestock. He was maybe fifty, built like a mooring post, with a face that had been broken often enough to become interesting rather than merely ugly. A leather satchel lay beside him, and a coil of rope, and the unmistakable bulge of someone who had been waiting.

"Leaking," Oscar repeated.

"Attention." The man took another bite. "You pulled a veil over yourself in the middle of a market with three guild hounds in sniffing range. Sloppy work. Half the roof-rats felt it. I felt it from the canal." He nodded at the alley below. "They'll circle back in a quarter hour once the trick wears thin. Veils do. Everything does."

"Who are you?"

"Doss." As if that settled anything. He swung down onto the ledge, dropped into the alley without ceremony, and stood brushing salt from his knees. "And you're the Vell & Sons boy who's been carrying something hot in a ledger binding for nine days. Don't gape, it's unbecoming. I buy information the way Pell buys salt, and lately everyone's selling pieces of you."

Oscar's hand went to his coat before he could stop it. Doss smiled with genuine pleasure.

"There it is. The little flinch. Worth every copper." He jerked his chin down the alley. "Walk with me or don't, but decide before the quarter hour's up. I have a cellar under the chandlery where the walls are thick and nobody asks questions, and I'm the only man in this market offering it to you."

"Why?" Oscar didn't move. "What's your price?"

"The map."

The word landed between them like a dropped coin. Doss watched him with those appraising eyes, patient now, guild-patient, and Oscar understood that this man had been following him not for days but perhaps since the courier died — that the cellar, the rope, the rooftop seat were all part of a longer arithmetic than chance.

"No," Oscar said.

Doss raised an eyebrow. "You're in no position to haggle."

"I'm always in a position to haggle. It's the one thing I'm good at." His heart was hammering, but his voice came out level, ledger-level, and he clung to that. "If I give you the map, you sell it. Within a month it's in Maren's hands or Sister Ivet's, and either way I'm dead and the city burns. That's not payment, that's just spending me slowly."

"And the alternative?"

"Partnership." He said it before he could lose his nerve. "You keep the cellar. I keep the map. You want to know what's on it — fine, I'll tell you what I know, which is more than anyone else alive can say, and I'll tell it to you a piece at a time, as we go. What I won't do is hand a loaded weapon to a stranger and hope he aims it kindly."

Doss chewed the last of the apple thoughtfully, core and all. "You know what happens to partners of mine who lie?"

"They prosper, if the lies are well-kept. I've read enough ledgers to know that's how the trade actually runs." Oscar held his gaze. "I don't need you to trust me. I need you to find me useful. Those hold longer."

A long silence. Somewhere beyond the alley, the performer's crowd roared at some new flourish of flame. Doss spat an apple seed into the salt dust.

"Useful," he repeated. "Huh. Most of them beg. Most of them cry, or offer me everything, which is the same as having nothing." He picked up his satchel and slung it. "All right, ledger-boy. Partnership. Trial terms. You'll sleep in the cellar, you'll cook, and if the guild offers you amnesty you'll tell me before you take it, because I'll want to beat their price."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly how partnership works. Welcome to the salt trade." He set off down the alley at a stroll, then paused, not turning around. "One question first. Honest answer and I'll forget I asked. The courier at your door — dying man, blood everywhere, pressing paper into a clerk's hands. Why you? Out of all the doors in Virelay, why yours?"

Oscar opened his mouth to say *chance*, and found the word wouldn't come. Nine days of decoding had taught him things about the map's margins — marks in a cipher keyed to guild training, routes only a Vell & Sons clerk could read. The courier hadn't stumbled. He had chosen. Which meant someone had known what Oscar was before Oscar knew it himself.

"I don't know," he said, and it was the truest thing he'd said all week.

Doss grunted. "Best answer you could've given. Come on, then. And boy—" he glanced back, and for a moment the merchant's face fell away and something older looked out, something that had survived a great many quarters of an hour, "—that trick you pulled? Never do it twice in the same day. Things notice when you borrow their attention. And some things," he added, walking on, "come to collect."

Oscar followed him into the dark of the chandlery stair, and behind them, far off across the roofs, a bell began to ring — not the market bell, not any hour he knew. Three strokes, a pause, three again.

Doss stopped on the stairs. All the ease went out of his shoulders.

"That's the tide-bell," he said quietly. "It hasn't rung in twenty years. It rings when the river gate opens from the *other side*."

Below them, in the cellar that was supposed to be safe, something was already waiting.
