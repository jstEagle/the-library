## 6. The Mistress Who Kept His Name

The road to Brannoc's cottage had not gotten shorter in three years. If anything it had grown longer, the way roads do when you leave them carrying a lie.

Oscar walked it through the grey end of the night, past the hedgerows that still smelled of the salt markets on him — Doss's smuggler's soap and brine and fear-sweat — with the map riding against his ribs where he'd sewn it into his coat lining. Twice he stopped and told himself to turn around. Twice he kept walking. That was the thing about going home, even to a home you'd fled: your feet remembered the way before your courage caught up.

The cottage sat in its clearing like something grown rather than built, wattle and turf and a chimney that smoked sideways no matter the wind. Chickens muttered in their pen. The herb beds were weeded. Someone had mended the gate since he'd last seen it, and the sight of that small repair did more damage to him than any ambush could have.

She was waiting at the door. Of course she was.

"You've been walking past my wards since midnight," Brannoc said. She was smaller than he remembered, or he'd gotten taller; her hair had gone from iron-grey to white at the temples, and her hands — those quick, clever hands that could gut a fish or stitch a curse with equal indifference — were folded over her apron like she'd been standing there for hours. "I let you, because I wanted to see if you'd actually come up the path or stand in the trees all night feeling sorry for yourself."

"I nearly stood in the trees," Oscar admitted.

"I know. You always were honest at the worst moments." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the light does. And wipe your boots. Some things haven't changed."

* * *

Inside smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and the particular bitterness of her tinctures, a smell that hit him so hard with memory that he had to grip the back of a chair. Three years of ledgers and guildhouse corridors fell away, and he was fifteen again, grinding valerian wrong and getting his knuckles rapped with a spoon.

She put water on to boil without asking what he wanted. She'd never asked. She read what people needed off them like weather.

"You're thin," she said. "Guild feeding you, are they? Or do they just feed the books?"

"The books eat well." He sat because his legs told him to. "Mistress—"

"Brannoc. You didn't come three years' distance to call me mistress, and I'm too old for the ceremony."

"Brannoc, then." He took a breath. "How did you know I went to the guild?"

She didn't turn from the fire. "Because I watched you go there."

The words landed strangely, one at a time. "You *watched* me?"

"I followed you out of the village, saw which road you took, and turned around and came home and made myself tea." Now she looked at him, and her eyes were the same flat grey as ever, eyes that had never once lied to him and never once softened either. "What did you think, boy? That a hedge-witch who can feel a lie through a wall wouldn't notice her own apprentice packing his bag at midnight? That I couldn't find one runaway in a country I've walked my whole life?" She snorted. "I knew where you were within a week. I've known every day since."

Something in his chest cracked along an old seam. He'd spent three years believing he'd been clever — that his escape had been clean, that the smallness of his new life was a fortress he'd built himself. "Then why didn't you come for me?"

"Because you ran *from* me, and chasing a boy only teaches him to run faster." She poured the water over the leaves. "And because I wanted to know what you'd become when no one was shaping you. That's not a kindness, Oscar. It's a habit of mine. I watch things grow."

He flinched at his own name. It sounded enormous in this room, too big for him, a coat he'd outgrown. "I don't use that name anymore."

"I know what you use." She set the cup in front of him. "Sebastian. A dead man's name, by the look of it — some clerk whose records you swallowed. It sits on you like a borrowed coat two sizes wrong, and yet." She tilted her head, studying him the way she studied a plant deciding whether to flower. "Yet you wear it better every month. That's the interesting part."

"How can you possibly know all this?"

"Because of why I took you as an apprentice in the first place." She sat down across from him, and for the first time he saw that her hands weren't folded in calm. They were folded to keep steady. "You think I chose you because you had tidy handwriting and a head for herbs. You had neither. Your handwriting was a scandal. But when you were nine years old you walked past my hedge and every ward on it rang like a struck bell, and I thought: now there's a hole walking around in the shape of a boy."

"A hole."

"A gap." She said the word carefully, tasting it. "Magic is pattern, child. Names, bloodlines, debts, memories — everything knits together, and a trained eye sees the weave. But some threads have been pulled out. Not cut. *Pulled*, cleanly, from the whole cloth, everywhere at once. When someone from such a line walks past, the weave puckers around them. The world leans toward the empty space and doesn't know why." Her eyes held his. "Your family line has been erased, Oscar. Not forgotten — erased. There's a difference, and the difference is the size of a war."

The cup trembled in his hands. He set it down before she could see, knowing she already had. "Erased how?"

"That I couldn't tell, then. Gaps don't carry labels. But I trained you anyway, because a gap that learns to see itself is the most dangerous thing in any weave — dangerous to whoever did the pulling." She leaned forward. "And now you walk into my kitchen smelling of old wax and older magic, with something sewn into your coat that makes my teeth ache, and I'd wager my last hen that you've found the edge of the same hole I've been circling for forty years."

* * *

He showed her the map.

He hadn't meant to — he'd meant to ask careful questions, give nothing away — but she held out her hand and he heard himself say "the courier said the crown remembers" and then the coat lining was open and the wax-sealed vellum lay on her table between the teacup and the mortar, looking suddenly obscene, like a corpse laid out at dinner.

Brannoc didn't touch it. She bent over it, close but not touching, and Oscar watched forty years of craft move across her face — recognition, confirmation, and underneath both, a grief so old it had gone smooth.

"Yes," she said quietly. "This is guild work. Old guild work. The best I've ever seen and I hate it." Her finger traced the air above the lines without landing. "It's not drawn, it's *woven*. Every thread of it anchored in the erasure itself. The map exists because the crown was pulled out of the world — it lives in the gap. Which means whoever carries it carries a hole that wants filling." She straightened and looked at him. "Who gave it to you?"

"A dying man. Enforcers came for it minutes behind him. They've been hunting me since. So have zealots — royalists who dream about a king they can't name." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Half the city is dreaming fragments of a man who was never in anyone's memory to begin with. How does that even work?"

"Gaps leak," she said simply. "A pulled thread leaves a scar, and scars itch. Minds rub against the absence and invent shapes for it. Dreams. Sermons. Wars." She sat back down, slowly, like a woman conserving strength for what came next. "Now I'll tell you the part you actually came for, though you don't know it yet. That map can be unmade. Not stolen, not hidden — unmade. Unpicked, thread by thread, back into nothing. Then the crown stays buried, the enforcers lose their scent, the zealots lose their relic, and the gap closes quiet."

Hope rose in him so fast it frightened him. "Then do it. Tonight. What do you need?"

"Nothing. I need to *not* do something." She met his eyes and didn't blink. "Unpicking it means working inside the erasure, and the erasure takes payment in kind. The map is woven from removed memory, so it unravels through removed memory. Mine won't do — I have no thread in it. Yours barely. But you've lived beside this thing for weeks. It's soaked into your days, your fears, your choices." She paused, and he watched her choose the next words like a surgeon choosing where to cut. "And me. The cost will be every memory you carry of me. All of it. The training, the hedge, this kitchen, my name. You'll walk out of here knowing you came for a reason and unable to say what it was."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a chicken complained at the coming dawn.

"No," Oscar said.

"It's not a negotiation."

"Then find another way. You're the finest hedge-witch in—"

"There is no other way. Magic pays what it weighs, and this weighs exactly that." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had gone white-knuckled in her lap, and he understood, with a lurch, that she had known this price since the moment he'd shown her the map. Perhaps since the moment she'd seen him on the path. Perhaps — the thought arrived like cold water — perhaps she had offered to teach him years ago *because* someday she would have to pay this bill, and she'd wanted the payment to mean something.

"Why would you do that?" His voice cracked on it. "Why would you let me forget you?"

"Because I've spent forty years watching a hole in the world, and you're the first thing that ever grew out of it worth keeping." She said it flatly, factually, the way she announced a fever breaking or a storm coming. "And because whatever you decide to be — Oscar, Sebastian, some third thing nobody's named yet — you'll only get to choose it if the factions don't get the crown first. My memories of you are a fair price for a free choice. I've had worse trades. I once bought rain with ten years of my own singing."

He was crying. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else. "There has to be—"

"Oscar." She reached across the table and took his hand, and her grip was as strong and unsentimental as ever. "Listen to me. Forgetting isn't dying. I'll still be here, weeding, swearing at chickens, teaching some other gap-eyed child bad habits. You'll pass through this county someday and feel a strange ache and not know its name, and that ache will be me, and it will be enough." Her thumb pressed hard against his knuckles. "But hear the other half, because I'll only say this once. Whatever you choose after — whether you burn that map or spend it or kneel to some preacher's paper kingdom — make sure it's *yours*. Not the guild's arithmetic. Not Ivet's law. Not even mine. You were the only student I ever had whose greatest talent was wanting things. Don't waste it on being wanted."

They sat like that while the sky outside went from black to bruise to pale gold, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left that words could carry.

At last she stood, rolled up her sleeves, and nodded at the map.

"All right," Brannoc said. "Before I start unpicking — tell me where this thing leads. Not for the map's sake. Because once you forget me, boy, someone in this world ought to know what you're about to face down there under the river — and I intend to write it down where you'll find it, even if you won't know why your hands shake reading it."

She was already pulling thread and bone needles from the drawer when the first horn sounded, far off across the fields — the deep, patient note of a guild enforcer's call, and beneath it, thinner and hungrier, the answering cry of zealot bells.

They had found the road.
