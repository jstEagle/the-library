## 7. Ivet's Bargain

Sister Ivet had prayed over better men than Doss, and she had prayed over worse, and the difference had never mattered to her much. What mattered was that he was awake now, and awake men talked.

He hung from a beam in the undercroft of the Chapel of the Returned King by his wrists, toes barely brushing the floor. His coat was gone — she'd had it searched twice — but no map. That was fine. Maps lived in heads until they didn't.

"You're not zealot material," Doss said. His voice was rough but steady. She gave him credit for that. "You've got ink on your cuffs. Preachers don't have ink."

"I keep records," Ivet said. "A kingdom cannot be restored without records."

"A kingdom can't be *restored* at all. It has to be built. Ask anyone who's ever tried to unburn a house."

She let him hang in silence while she crossed to the table where her clerks worked by lamplight, copying fragments of dream-testimony onto fresh paper. Forty years of dreams. Fragments of a king with river-grey eyes, a crown like a ring of thorns made silver, a vault beneath the water. Her congregation dreamed him nightly and woke weeping without knowing why, and every one of them swore they'd never heard his name, because there was no name to hear. The guild had seen to that. The guild had reached into forty million minds and scooped out one man, and called it peace.

Ivet called it theft.

"Here is what will happen," she said, turning back to Doss. "The man who carries the map — Sebastian, he calls himself, a clerk of Vell & Sons — will come looking for you. Or he won't, and you'll die here, and I'll find him anyway, because everything in this city flows through ledgers eventually. But I would rather he came willingly. So when he comes, you will tell him what I offer. And you will tell him truthfully, because I am going to say it in front of witnesses, and my witnesses write things down."

Doss laughed, which cost him something; she watched the pain move through his shoulders like weather. "And what does the righteous sister offer?"

"A trial," she said. "A lawful one. Public charges, public defense, a verdict rendered by twelve sworn citizens. For him, and for you. Amnesty for any crime committed in possession of the map. And afterward—" she paused, letting the word do its work, the way she'd learned to let words work on congregations of thousands— "afterward, a kingdom where a man named Sebastian can exist. Papers. A name entered in the rolls. A legal self, signed and sealed, that no enforcer can tear out of a ledger."

She saw it land. Even upside down and bleeding, Doss went still in a way that told her he understood exactly what such a thing would mean to a man who had none.

"You've thought about him," Doss said quietly.

"I think about everyone," said Ivet. "It's the job."

* * *

They brought Oscar in at dusk, two of her sworn men walking him between them with a courtesy that was more frightening than force. He looked exactly as her informants had described: young, careful, a clerk's stoop already setting into his spine, eyes that counted exits before they counted people.

She received him alone in the chapel proper, beneath the unfinished mosaic of the king's face — the artisans had worked from dreamers' descriptions, and the face they'd made was wrong in a way that haunted her, a face assembled from forty thousand broken memories that none of them quite agreed on.

"Sebastian," she said.

"That's the name I'm told I have."

"Good. Keep it. It's about to become true." She gestured him to a chair. He sat the way bookkeepers sit, spine straight, hands folded, as if the chair might audit him. "Your friend is alive in my undercroft. He will remain so under conditions I'll state plainly. You give me the map. In exchange: amnesty, trial by sworn jury, and legal recognition of your name. Not a forged identity. A real one. Entered in the rolls of the restored kingdom. Sebastian of Vell & Sons, freeborn, unindicted, *existing*."

Oscar — Sebastian — did not answer at once. She watched him do arithmetic. She had spent enough years reading congregations to read the sum on his face: the small safe life against the sanctioned one, the false name against the true. Every runaway wanted papers. Every fugitive wanted the past to stop being a debt.

"And if I refuse?" he said.

"Then I take the map from your corpse or your effects, whichever proves easier, and your friend hangs for obstruction. I'd rather not. I want this done *lawfully*, clerk. That's the entire point. The guild ended a war with erasure and called it mercy. I intend to end it with law and call it justice. Law requires a beginning — a lawful restoration, a lawful king, and yes, lawful trials, even for smugglers and runaway apprentices." She leaned forward. "You've been living outside the law so long you've forgotten what it feels like to be inside it. I'm offering you the door."

He closed his eyes. She recognized surrender when she saw it; she'd seen it on ten thousand faces at the altar rail. When he opened them again, something had gone quiet in him.

"The map is hidden in the city," he said. "Not on me. If I bring it to you, my friend walks free first. Before I hand it over. Those are my terms."

"Reasonable terms," Ivet said, and meant it, and meant also that she would honor them precisely as written and not one inch further. "But before you decide, I want you to see something. You're a ledger man. You'll want to verify my good faith."

She led him down the corridor to the record room, where her clerks kept the trial rolls of the restored order — the cases already tried, the judgments already rendered, the precedent of the new kingdom being built case by case. She unlocked the cabinet herself. She had nothing to hide. That was what she believed, right up until the moment she saw his face change.

He wasn't reading the way clerks read, top to bottom. He was reading sideways, comparing columns, the way men catch frauds. And then he went very still, and she followed his finger to the page.

*Verdict: guilty.* Written in the clerk's neat hand, dated three days hence. Below it, blank lines waiting for the hearing. Below *that*, the sentence, already fixed: *restoration of property, term of service to the Crown.*

"They pre-date the hearings," he said. His voice was flat, the flatness of a man checking sums that refuse to balance. "Every one of them. The verdicts are written before the trials happen. Sister — these aren't trials. They're ceremonies. The law isn't deciding anything. It's just... narrating decisions already made."

"It's *efficiency*," Ivet began, and heard, even as she said it, how the word sounded in this room full of pre-written justice.

"It's a ledger with the entries reversed," he said. "You write the outcome first and invent the causes after. I've seen merchants hang for less." He turned to look at her, and there was no fear in his face anymore, which frightened her more than rage would have. "You offered me a lawful existence. But your law is theater with a docket. You'd crown a king with one hand and pre-write his subjects' fates with the other. That's not restoration. That's the guild's trick with better handwriting — deciding for everyone what the story is, and erasing anyone who disagrees."

"The alternative is chaos—"

"The alternative," he said, "is a page left blank until the facts are in. I know how hard that is. I keep books for a living."

He walked out of the record room ahead of her, unhurried, and she did not order her guards to stop him, because some part of her — the part that still prayed honestly, late at night, to a king whose face she couldn't assemble — wanted to hear what he would say next.

At the chapel door he stopped. "Keep my friend fed. I'll come back for him. But I'm not trading the map for a seat in your theater." He glanced back once. "For what it's worth, Sister — I believe you think you're right. That's the worst part."

Then he was gone into the dusk, and Ivet stood alone beneath the wrong-faced king, and for the first time in twenty years she did not know what to write next.

Behind her, in the undercroft, Doss began — softly, painfully, and with unmistakable relish — to laugh.
