## 8. The Hollow Beneath the River

The river kept its secrets the way the guild kept its ledgers: in a locked room, underwater, where no one honest would ever think to look.

Oscar stood at the edge of the old sluice gate with the map unrolled across his knees, watching moonlight fail to penetrate more than a hand's depth of black water. Behind him, Doss checked the knots on their rope for the third time. Beside her, Brannoc hummed something tuneless and old, the way she always did when she was working magic she didn't want anyone to notice.

"The map says the entrance floods at high tide," Oscar said. "We have until the moon turns."

"Then stop admiring the view," Doss said.

He rolled the map away before he could admire it again. He'd been admiring it too much lately — not the ink, but what it promised. A buried crown. A true king. An answer to the question that had been eating him since Sister Ivet's sermon, since the salt markets, since the night a dying man had pressed this thing into his hands and said *the crown remembers*.

He was tired of questions. That was the dangerous part. Tired men grabbed at answers without weighing them first, and Oscar had spent three years learning that everything in Vell & Sons had a weight, and every weight had a price.

Brannoc stopped humming. "The warding is guild-work," she said. "Older than anything I've felt. It won't keep us out. It will just... note us."

"Note us?"

"Write us down." She smiled, thin as paper. "Everything down here is written down somewhere, boy. That's how they built it. Memory is just a ledger you can't close."

* * *

The sluice gate opened onto a stair cut into living rock, descending below the waterline behind a skin of worked stone that held the river back the way glass holds back air. Impossible engineering. Guild engineering. Oscar felt the familiar vertigo of it — the sense that the world he'd been reconciling entries for was only the topmost page of a much deeper book.

They went down with lanterns hooded, single file, the rope between them. The air grew cold and mineral. Somewhere above them, the whole weight of the river pressed against the vault's shell, and Oscar tried not to think about it, and thought about nothing else.

Doss counted steps under her breath. At four hundred she said, "Anyone else feel that?"

Oscar did. A pressure behind the eyes, like a word sitting on the tip of the tongue. Like a name he almost knew.

"That's the erasure," Brannoc said quietly. "Residue. They scrubbed this place out of ten thousand minds, but scrubbing leaves the shape of what was removed. You're feeling the hole where a king used to be."

"And the crown?"

"Deeper. Keep walking."

At six hundred steps the stair ended in a chamber so vast their lantern light couldn't find its walls. Water lay across the floor in shallow sheets, mirror-still, and their reflections walked beneath them upside down like drowned twins. And in the center of the chamber, on a plinth of black stone, sat the crown.

It was smaller than Oscar expected. Iron and silver, plain-wrought, the kind of thing a frontier king might have worn — no jewels, no ostentation. Just metal, and around it an aura of attention, of *being looked at*, so strong that Oscar's skin crawled.

"There's no guard," Doss whispered. "No trap. Nothing."

"There doesn't need to be," Brannoc said. "Who could steal a thing no one remembers exists?"

But Oscar wasn't looking at the crown anymore. He was looking past it, at the far wall of the chamber, where the lantern light finally found something else.

A door. And in front of the door, a chair. And in the chair —

"Oh," Brannoc said. Softly. The way you speak at a deathbed. "Oh, they never told even themselves."

* * *

The man in the chair was alive.

That was the first horror — that he was alive, unmistakably, his chest rising and falling, his skin warm-looking in the lantern light, his beard grown long and white against robes that might once have been royal purple and were now the color of dust. Chains of gold linked his wrists to the arms of the chair, but they hung loose. He wasn't restrained. He simply hadn't moved in a very long time.

The second horror was his eyes. They were open. They were calm. They were looking directly at Oscar, and there was nothing behind them. No fear, no curiosity, no recognition. Looking at those eyes was like looking into a ledger page from which every entry had been scraped away — the vellum still good, the writing gone.

"He's breathing," Doss said. Her voice had gone flat, the smuggler's voice, the voice she used when feeling would get someone killed. "How is he breathing?"

"The working," Brannoc said. She had approached the chair slowly, hands open, the way you approach a frightened animal or a god. "The oldest one. The one they built the whole guild on. You don't erase a king and leave a body — bodies make martyrs, martyrs make wars. So they left the man. They took everything else. Every memory of him, from every mind alive — including, I think, his own."

Oscar stepped closer. Up close, the king — if it was the king; the map said so, and the crown's pull said so, and the hollow ache behind Oscar's eyes said so — up close he looked ordinary. A farmer's hands. A seaman's weathered face. Someone's father, once. Someone's son.

"Can you hear me?" Oscar asked.

Nothing. The calm eyes held him, patient as furniture.

"He can't," Brannoc said. "Or rather — there's no one left in there to hear. What's looking out at you is just... continuation. Breath without a breather." She touched the man's wrist, gently, and read something there that made her close her eyes. "The working sustains him. It has sustained him for — saints. For longer than the guild has existed under its current name. They didn't end the war, Oscar. They froze it. They put the whole argument in a chair and left it in the dark."

Oscar's legs didn't want to hold him. He sat down on the wet stone, right there, in the flooded vault beneath the river, because the alternative was falling down.

All those ledger entries. All those counterfeit years and bottled memories he'd reconciled with such careful neutrality, telling himself the guild traded in oddities, in comforts, in small mercies. And underneath all of it, funding all of it, justifying all of it: this. A man emptied like a cask. The guild's founding asset. Its oldest entry, still open.

"They'll want to renew it," he heard himself say. "Maren. When she finds out we're here. She'll come to renew the erasure, because if he wakes up—"

"If he wakes up, the war wakes up," Doss finished. "Ivet gets her relic. The zealots get their martyr. Half the city burns before winter." She crouched beside the chair, studying the king's empty face with something that surprised Oscar — tenderness, of a hard practical kind. "And if we leave him exactly as he is?"

"Then we're bookkeepers," Oscar said. "Keeping the entry open forever. Letting him sit in the dark so everyone else can sleep."

Nobody answered that. The water lapped at the stones. The crown sat on its plinth, radiating its terrible patience, and Oscar understood suddenly why the courier had died saying *the crown remembers*. The crown remembered everything. It was the only witness left. Every mind in the world had been scrubbed except this one object, this iron-and-silver ring of attention, holding the truth the way a ledger holds figures — faithfully, uselessly, waiting for someone to read it.

"We have to decide," Brannoc said. "Tonight, before the tide turns. Wake him, leave him, or—" she glanced at the crown, and didn't finish.

"Or break it," Oscar said. "Break the binding. Free him into an ordinary life. An ordinary death, someday, like anyone."

"He wouldn't thank you," Brannoc said. "There's nothing left in him to be thankful. You'd be freeing a man who isn't there."

"I'd be closing the account," Oscar said. His voice shook and he let it. "Three years I've balanced other people's books and told myself it was honest work. This is the first entry I've ever seen whole. And you know what it says? It says nobody down here is innocent. Not the guild. Not Ivet. Not us, if we walk away."

Doss stood. "For what it's worth, bookkeeper — I've hidden cargo in a hundred holes, and I've never once seen a hole this well kept. Somebody comes here. Regularly. Fresh oil on those hinges." She nodded at the door behind the chair. "Which means somebody's due."

As if the vault had been waiting for the words, the door behind the king began, very softly, to open.

Lantern light spilled through the widening gap. Voices — more than one. And cutting through the cold air of the hollow, clear and unhurried, a voice Oscar knew from a thousand quiet mornings over the ledgers of Vell & Sons:

"Well," said Maren. "You found him."
