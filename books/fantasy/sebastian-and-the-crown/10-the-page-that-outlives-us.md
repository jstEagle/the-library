## 10. The Page That Outlives Us

The salt markets smelled of brine and roasting chestnuts, and the man who had once been a king was arguing over the price of herring.

Sebastian watched him from the edge of the fishmongers' row, a ledger tucked under his arm out of habit more than need. The man — no name, because names had to be remembered before they could be given back, and nobody remembered his — had taken work hauling crates for Old Marta's stall. He was good at it. He laughed easily. He had a way of tipping his head when he listened that made people tell him things, which was either the residue of kingship or simply kindness. Sebastian had stopped trying to decide which.

"You're staring again," Doss said, materializing at his elbow with two cups of mulled wine and the air of a man who had never paid for either. "He doesn't remember you saving him. He doesn't remember anything. That was rather the point."

"I know what the point was."

"Then drink your wine and stop staring like a creditor."

Sebastian took the cup. The wine was too sweet and too hot and exactly right for a morning with frost still on the awnings. Below them, the king — *the man*, Sebastian corrected himself, *the man* — hefted a crate of grayling onto his shoulder and said something that made Marta cackle. A child chased a gull between the stalls. The river ran brown and patient under the pilings, keeping its flooded vault to itself, keeping its silence.

"He asked Marta my name last week," Sebastian said. "Not yours. Mine."

"And what did she tell him?"

"Sebastian." He turned the word over like a coin he was still checking for clipping. "And it fit. That's the strange part. It fit like it never quite did before."

Doss clinked his cup against Sebastian's. "That's because you stopped wearing it and started being it. There's a difference. I'd know. I've been six men, and only one of them stuck."

* * *

Ivet's congregation had fractured the way ice fractures on a thawing pond: all at once, and then in long groaning pieces.

Without the crown, without the king, her sermons about restoration had nothing to point at. She still preached — Sebastian had heard her twice from the back of the salt market square, her voice carrying that same terrible certainty — but the crowds were thinner now, and angrier, and some of them had begun asking questions she couldn't answer. Where is the relic? Why won't you show us? If the kingdom was stolen, sister, who stole it?

She had answered the last one, three Sundays past, by pointing directly at the guildhouse on the hill. And so the fragile thing Maren was building — the truth-telling, the slow unsealing of ledgers, the guild's confession offered in careful doses — had become not just dangerous but necessary. Because Ivet had seen enough fragments in her dreams to know the shape of the hole in the world, even if she couldn't prove it. And a preacher with a grievance and no proof would burn a city looking for it.

Maren had received Sebastian in the guildhouse's counting room, a month ago now, across the same table where Oscar Okafor had once reconciled entries for traded years.

"You understand what I'm offering them," Maren had said. Not asking. Maren never asked; Maren proposed terms. "Truth. In installments. Enough to keep Ivet's people from tearing up the cobblestones, not so much that they learn their grandmothers loved a king they can't name. The guild ends the way it lived — by managing memory. Only this time we manage it toward honesty."

"That's not honesty," Sebastian had said. "That's bookkeeping."

"Yes," Maren had agreed, without shame. "Bookkeeping is just honesty with discipline. Which is why I'm offering you a position. Reconciler. You were the best apprentice I ever misplaced."

It had been, Sebastian reflected, the strangest compliment of his life. He had declined. Maren had nodded as if she'd expected it, slid a single key across the table — access to the deep archive, where the oldest ledgers slept — and said, "Then take what you came for and go. But write it down somewhere, boy. Everything. Unmanaged truth is the only kind worth the ink."

* * *

The archive smelled of dust and tallow and the particular cold of stone that hadn't seen sun in a century. Sebastian went at night, when the guildhouse was down to its skeleton crew of clerks who owed him small favors and asked smaller questions.

He brought one blank page. Good rag paper, the heavy stock reserved for entries that mattered.

He sat at the reading desk beneath the lamp and thought about what an honest ledger entry looked like. Debits and credits. What was taken; what was given. The guild had taught him that every transaction balanced, eventually, if you were willing to look at the whole account. So he looked.

*Item:* one true king, removed from the memory of every living soul, sustained mindless beneath the river for a generation so that a war might end. *Cost:* everything he was. *Paid to:* peace, such as it was.

*Item:* one crown, buried with him, its binding broken by an unnamed clerk. *Cost:* Brannoc's memory of her student — the hedge-witch who had trained him precisely because erased bloodlines throw gaps she could feel, who had known where he fled all along, who had paid the price of the breaking without complaint and now sold charms in a village where a young man named Oscar was, to her, a stranger's name in a stranger's story. He had gone to see her in the autumn. She had been kind to him the way she was kind to everyone, and it had been the worst kindness of his life.

*Item:* one map, destroyed. One courier, dead. One apprentice named Oscar Okafor, fled, unfound, unwritten.

*Item:* one name, kept. Not granted. Not sanctioned. Kept.

His pen hovered. This was the part the ledger couldn't teach. The guild recorded transactions between parties. But this — the choosing of a self, the decision to answer to a name you made instead of one you were given — there was no column for that. No debit. No credit. Just a line, and a hand, and the terrifying freedom of a page that belonged to no master.

He wrote until the candle guttered. He wrote about the vault and the man who didn't remember wanting anything. He wrote about Ivet's trial records with verdicts written before hearings, and about Maren's managed truths, and about Sister Ivet herself, who dreamed of a king and deserved to know why even if the knowing broke her movement. He wrote about Brannoc. He wrote about Doss, who had demanded a map as payment and settled for a partnership and had never once, in all the months since, asked for more than he was owed.

He did not sign it. He did not date it. Signatures were claims, and dates were anchors, and he wanted neither. He wanted the page to wait — for some clerk a hundred years hence, some runaway hiding under some borrowed name, to pull it from the shelf and read it and know that someone had stood exactly where they stood and chosen, freely, badly, bravely, to be someone new.

He sealed it in plain wax — no sigil, no house mark, just the impression of his thumb — and carried it into the stacks.

The oldest ledger lay where it always had, its spine cracked, its torn page still missing from the night a dying courier changed everything. Sebastian slid his page into the gap. It fit the way a name fits when you finally grow into it.

He closed the cover. Somewhere above him, the guildhouse settled and sighed, and the river kept its secrets, and a man with no name laughed over herring prices in the cold morning light.

The page would outlive them all. That was enough.

That was, at last, everything.
