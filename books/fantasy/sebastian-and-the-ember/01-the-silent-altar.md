## 1. The Silent Altar

The rain came down like it had a grudge.

Wendell Ishikawa stood at the edge of the burning farmhouse and let it hammer the back of his neck while his company finished the work inside. Smoke curled out of the thatch in fat grey ropes, thinned by the downpour, and somewhere behind him a horse screamed once and went quiet. He didn't turn to look. He'd learned years ago that looking never helped anything—it just gave you more things to carry.

"Clear," called Doss from the doorway, wiping his blade on a rag that had stopped being white around midsummer. "Bandits are dead. The one with the scar tried to run for the treeline."

"And?"

"And I have excellent aim."

Wendell nodded. That was the whole contract. Three weeks ago a wool merchant in Thornfell's foothills had paid the Gray Company forty crowns to clear bandits off his trade road, and the Gray Company did not leave contracts half-finished, because the Gray Company's reputation was the only thing it owned outright. Wendell had been with them since he was nine years old—fed by them, trained by them, named by them, in the way that mattered—and he knew exactly what they were worth and exactly what they cost.

He walked the perimeter once, professional habit, counting bodies. Seven. The bandit camp had been sloppy, overconfident, feeding fat on a road nobody patrolled anymore. One of them was barely older than Wendell had been when Maren first put a spear in his hands. He crouched beside the boy for a moment, closed the boy's eyes with two fingers, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the rain wouldn't drown anyway.

"You going to do that thing where you get sad about it?" Doss asked, coming up beside him. Not cruel—Doss didn't have a cruel bone, just an honest one, which was sometimes worse.

"No," Wendell said.

"Good. Because we're moving before dark. Maren wants us at the crossroads shrine by nightfall, and you know how she gets about the pass."

"I know how she gets about everything."

"That's why she's captain." Doss clapped him on the shoulder and went back toward the horses, boots sucking in the mud.

Wendell lingered. This was the part of the job nobody paid for: the standing still afterward, the letting the adrenaline drain out through your boots into ground that had already drunk enough today. He looked at the farmhouse—the merchant's waystation, technically, though it had walls and a hearth and someone had planted marigolds under the window—and felt the familiar hollow arithmetic settle over him. Seven dead men for forty crowns and a road made safe for wool carts. Somewhere in the free cities there were clerks who could make that equation look clean on paper. Wendell had never met one who could make it feel clean.

He mounted up and rode north through the rain.

* * *

The crossroads shrine sat where three old roads met below the shoulder of Thornfell, and it was the kind of place most people rode past without seeing. A squat stone altar under a leaning wooden canopy, moss-eaten, its carved flame-grooves filled with black water. No priest. No offerings except what the weather left. On the map it would be marked as a waypoint, nothing more—a place to water the horses and stretch your legs.

Ten years ago it had been something else entirely.

Wendell remembered it the way you remember a wound: precisely, uselessly. He'd been nineteen, newly blooded, riding escort on a salt caravan when the storm caught them here. They'd sheltered under the canopy, six soldiers pressed together against the cold, and the caravan master—an old woman with ash-grey braids and a hearthstone pendant she wasn't ashamed of—had knelt at the altar and prayed aloud to Ember, god of the small fire, the banked coal, the light you keep alive when keeping it alive is all you can do. Nobody else prayed. The hearth-faith was dying even then; the altars had been going cold for a generation, and everyone agreed it was because people had simply stopped believing, the way everyone agrees the fish are smaller than they used to be.

But Wendell had been cold and tired and nineteen, and he'd knelt too, mostly to be polite.

And Ember had answered.

Not in words, exactly. In warmth. In a pressure against his chest like a hand laid flat over his sternum, patient and enormous, and a sense—not a thought, but a *knowing*—that he was seen. That the seven dead men he'd counted that autumn were counted too. That the small stubborn thing inside him, the thing that closed a dying stranger's eyes, was not weakness but fuel, and the god had been tending it like a coal all along.

It had lasted maybe ten heartbeats. It had rearranged his entire life.

He'd told no one. What was there to tell? *I touched the god at a roadside shrine and it noticed me.* The hearth-priests would have wept. His company would have laughed, or worse, believed him, and started looking at him sideways ever after. So he'd kept it, carried it like a coal cupped in both hands, gone back to that shrine every chance the contracts allowed, and for four years the warmth came back—fainter each time, like a voice calling from farther away, until—

Until it didn't.

Ten years now. Ten years of kneeling at cold altars across half the map, telling himself each silence meant nothing, that gods breathed slowly, that faith was patience. Ten years of watching the last ember-priests die off or go quiet or take up other trades, Sera-knows-where, until the hearth-faith was a story grandmothers told and the altars were waypoints on maps.

He dismounted at the crossroads and told Doss he'd catch up.

* * *

The rain had slackened to a fine grey drizzle by the time he stood alone under the canopy. His horse cropped the wet grass at the roadside, indifferent. Down the eastern road, the rest of the company was a smudge of movement already disappearing toward the pass.

Wendell knelt.

His knees found the same two worn depressions in the stone flagging that generations of pilgrims had left, and his hands found the altar's edge, and the carved flame-grooves ran cold water over his fingers. Up close the old carvings were still legible if you knew what you were looking at: the flame with seven tongues, one for each virtue the hearth-faith had taught—tending, sharing, guarding, mourning, mending, remembering, enduring. Half the tongues had been chiseled off by some pious vandal or practical stonemason decades ago. What remained looked less like a flame than a wound.

"Hello," he said quietly. It was absurd and he did it anyway, because he always did it anyway. "It's me."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. The wind moved in the canopy overhead and dripped through a split in the boards onto the back of his neck, cold as a fingertip.

"I finished a contract today," he went on, low, the words more breath than sound. "Seven bandits. One of them was a kid. I closed his eyes." He paused. "You used to care about that. I think you cared about that. I've decided you did, because the alternative is that I made you up, and I'm not ready to live in the world where I made you up."

The altar said nothing. Moss grew. Water dripped.

This was the ritual, and he knew it, and knowing didn't blunt it. Every few months he found an excuse—a waypoint on the route, a shrine near a contract, a detour he could justify as scouting—and he knelt and he spoke and he listened, and the listening was the worst part, because listening is hope wearing work clothes, and hope is the one enemy Wendell Ishikawa had never learned to fight properly. You can't kill it. You can't outrun it. It just kneels there next to you in the rain, year after year, refusing to die, which should have made it a god in its own right.

He pressed his palm flat against the stone. Cold. Stone-cold, ordinary-cold, the cold of a thing that had never held anything warmer than weather.

"Ten years," he said. "Most men would stop."

Most men hadn't heard it. Most men hadn't spent four years being quietly, secretly known by something vast and gentle, and then had it taken away without explanation, without even the courtesy of a final word. Grief for a person at least has a shape—you know what you lost, you can visit the grave. Grief for a god's attention was a hole shaped like nothing, and Wendell had fallen into it so many times he knew its dimensions better than his own quarters.

He thought, not for the first time, about his father. Their father had kept a hearth-shrine in the house too, a little clay stove with a real coal in it, fed daily, and he'd told them—Wendell and Liora, sitting cross-legged on the hearthstones—that this was the whole religion in miniature. *You feed the fire every day whether you feel like it or not,* he'd said. *Feeling is weather. Tending is faith.* And then their father had sold Liora to whatever waited beneath Thornfell, and Wendell had stopped thinking of him as a man who understood faith.

That was eleven years ago now. Eleven years since Liora vanished between one morning and the next, and their father's face went smooth and shut like a door, and Wendell joined the Gray Company full-time and stopped going home. He'd told himself for a decade that she'd died of fever, that grief was simpler than suspicion, that a man couldn't survive in this trade carrying two unanswered questions instead of one.

He was starting to suspect he'd only ever carried one question, wearing two faces.

"Wendell!" Doss's voice, far back down the road. "Maren says move or walk!"

He rose. His knees ached the way knees do, ordinary and mortal. He looked at the altar one last time—at the five surviving tongues of the carved flame, at the black water standing in the grooves like veins—and did what he always did, which was tend it anyway. He cleared the moss from the central channel with his thumbnail. He righted the fallen offering-stone. Small maintenance, invisible devotion, the kind of work that changes nothing and is everything.

Then he swung into the saddle and rode for the pass, and the rain closed behind him like a curtain, and the shrine stood empty again at the crossing of three dead roads, holding its cold water and its patience, waiting the way stones wait.

He was half a mile gone when he saw the rider.

The figure came up the western road at a stumbling canter, wrong for the pace, wrong for the terrain—a horse driven past its judgment by a rider who swayed in the saddle like a man held upright by momentum and nothing else. Even at distance, even through the drizzle, Wendell read the signs the way he read everything: blood-dark stain down the rider's left side, no pack, no escort, and behind him, far back along the road, a knot of movement that might have been riders in formation.

Company colors, maybe. Or something worse.

Wendell's hand found his sword hilt out of pure habit, and then, because the rider was alone and bleeding and swaying, he turned his horse to meet him—which was, though he had no way of knowing it, the last decision Wendell Ishikawa would ever make as a man with nothing to lose.

The stranger saw him and spurred forward, mouth opening around words the wind stole.
