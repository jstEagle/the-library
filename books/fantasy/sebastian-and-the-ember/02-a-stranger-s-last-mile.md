## 2. A Stranger's Last Mile

The rain came down the mountain in sheets, and Wendell Ishikawa let it.

He'd made camp in the crook of a granite shoulder where the smugglers' road bent away from the king's highway, a hollow deep enough to hold a fire if you built it low and fed it wet wood until the smoke learned to crawl instead of climb. He'd done that now, crouched over the flames with his cloak steaming, watching the smoke thread sideways through the pines like something trying to escape without being seen. It was a skill from another life—the low fire, the crawling smoke—and he found he still had it. That was the trouble with the things you learned young. They stayed in the hands long after the reasons for learning them were gone.

The contract was finished. Three days ago he'd put a knife through the throat of a bandit captain named Orliss who'd been taxing pilgrims on the Thornfell road, and the company's paymaster would have the confirmation by raven before week's end. Clean work. Professional work. The kind of work that let him tell himself, in the hour after, that what he did for a living was simply the shape the world had left for men like him. He was good at not thinking about the rest of it. He'd had ten years of practice.

Ten years since Ember went silent.

He didn't go to the dead altars anymore. He'd stopped around year four, when the last one—a wayside shrine outside Cinderhold, its hearthstone cold as riverbed stone—had given him nothing but the smell of old ash and the particular silence of a thing that has never existed at all. The priests said the god slept. The priests said the god walked beyond the mountains. The priests said a great many things, their voices getting thinner every year, and the free cities got colder every winter, and somewhere in all of it Wendell had stopped waiting for a voice that wasn't coming back.

But he still built his fires low. He still faced them toward the east, where the old temples stood. Habit, he told himself. Nothing more.

* * *

He heard the horses first.

Not on the road—off it, above him, coming down through the trees where no sensible rider went at night in this rain. Four sets of hooves, maybe five, moving fast and careless, the way men move when they think the law is theirs and the dark belongs to them. Wendell killed the fire with two sweeps of his boot and was into the rocks before the thought finished forming, knife already loose in his hand, back against wet stone. Twenty years a mercenary. His body knew what to do about riders in the night, and it did it while the rest of him was still deciding.

They came down through the pines in ones and twos, torches guttering, and even through the rain he could see the colors: black and bone-white, quartered on the shields, a white tower stitched at the shoulder. Vaelric's colors. He'd seen them once before, years ago, at a distance—Vaelric's outriders riding through a border market like they owned the ground under everyone's feet, and everyone letting them believe it.

They didn't stop. That was the strange part. They swept past his cold camp without so much as a glance, torches swinging over the flattened grass where his bedroll had been, and drove on down toward the road, toward the low country, calling to each other in short clipped phrases the wind tore apart before they reached him. Hunting something. Or someone. And whatever they hunted had passed through this camp recently enough to make them look.

Wendell waited until the last torchlight drowned in the trees below, then counted to three hundred, which was how long it took his heart to remember it wasn't at war.

That was when he heard the breathing.

It came from upstream, along the streambed where the water ran loud enough to cover a man's approach—or a man's collapse. Wendell moved toward it with the knife low and open, because twenty years of this work had taught him that a sound like that in the dark was either a wounded animal or bait, and both deserved the same caution. He found the stranger crumpled against a boulder with his legs in the shallows, one hand pressed to his side, and even in the dark Wendell could see the black shine of blood between the man's fingers. A lot of it. More than a man could argue with.

"Don't." The word came out wet and cracked. "Don't run. I can't—I followed your smoke."

"You followed my smoke," Wendell said, "and brought four riders behind you."

"No." A cough, terrible and long. "Behind me. Past me. They lost me at the ford." He laughed, or tried to, and it turned into something else. "Lost me. Like I'm worth losing."

Wendell crouched, keeping the knife where the man could see it, and looked at him properly. Fifties, maybe. Merchant's hands gone soft, then ruined—rope burns across the palms, fresh. A courier's satchel, empty, strap cut clean. Someone had taken everything off him except his life, and now his life was going too. "Who cut your strap?"

"Me. So they'd think—" Another cough. "So they'd think they got it. They'll figure it out. They always figure it out." His eyes found Wendell's in the dark, and there was something in them that Wendell recognized from mirrors, from the faces of men who'd run out of time and knew it exactly. "Water."

Wendell gave him water. He didn't know why. The man was dead; the rain and the rock and the arithmetic of the wound all agreed on that. But he held the skin to his lips anyway and watched him drink half of it, spill the rest, and come back to himself by degrees, the way a candle comes back after a draft.

"You're not Vaelric's," the man said.

"No."

"You're nobody's. Good. Nobody's is what this needs." His hand came up—slowly, trembling with the effort of it—and pressed something against Wendell's chest. A roll of oilskin, small, sealed with wax stamped by a signet ring whose face had been filed smooth. "Take it."

"I don't take things from dying men. Bad trade."

"Then take it from a frightened one." The stranger's grip tightened, and beneath the weakness of it there was a courier's strength, the trained stubbornness of a man whose whole life had been carrying things through. "Listen. There's a court under Thornfell. Under the mountain. You've heard the stories—all of you have heard the stories and none of you believe them. They're true. Every one. Worse than true." His breath hitched. "There's a map inside. Routes. The real routes, the ones the smugglers sold and the crown bought and buried. It ends at a door. The door is the point. Everything is the point."

"Why me? You fell into my camp by accident."

"Nothing," the man said, "is an accident anymore." He pulled a breath that whistled. "I carried this for eleven months. Eleven months, and do you know what I learned? Everyone who should have it won't take it. The lords are paid. The temples are afraid. The companies are bought—one of yours especially." Something moved across his face that might have been pity. "I need someone nobody owns. Someone with a reason to hate the mountain and no master to tell him not to."

"I have a master. I have a company."

"Not after tonight." The stranger smiled, and it was the worst smile Wendell had ever seen, because it was kind. "Riders in Vaelric's colors just rode past your camp looking for the man who robbed them. What do you suppose happens when they circle back?"

* * *

They talked for another hour, or the man talked and Wendell listened and the rain came down and the wound did its slow certain work. His name was Aldric Vane, though he said it like a coat he'd worn so long he'd forgotten what was underneath. He'd been a courier for interests he wouldn't name, carrying messages between people who paid to remain unnamed, and eleven months ago he'd been given the oilskin roll and a route and a sum of money that should have kept him honest, except the money ran out and the honesty hadn't mattered anyway, because the people who wanted the map stopped being willing to wait for it.

"They took the satchel," he said. "Let them. The map was never in the satchel. Eleven months I carried it sewn into my coat, next to the skin, where no thief looks because no thief wants to find it." His laugh again, that broken thing. "Paranoia. It keeps a courier alive right up until it doesn't."

"What's at the end of it?" Wendell asked. "The door. What's behind the door?"

Aldric was quiet for a long moment. Rain filled the pause, drumming on the boulder, running off the brim of Wendell's hood.

"I don't know," he said finally. "Eleven months, and I never opened it. Do you understand what that cost me? A man like me lives on knowing things. It's the only coin we're ever really paid in. And I sat in a hundred taverns with this against my ribs and I never broke the seal, because the man who gave it to me said—" his voice dropped, went careful, reverent almost, "—he said the seal is part of the lock. Break it wrong and the door knows. The door knows, and the door tells."

"The door tells," Wendell repeated. "Tells who?"

"Whoever's listening under the mountain." Aldric's eyes had begun to lose their focus, wandering past Wendell to the dark. "There are things under Thornfell that listen. That's the whole horror of it, if you want the truth. Not armies. Not usurpers. Listeners. Everything you say near the wrong stone, they keep. Forever. And they spend it." He shuddered, or the cold took him—it hardly mattered which. "You'll want to break the seal. Everyone wants to break the seal. Don't. Not until you're standing in front of someone who can read it whole. There's a woman—"

His voice failed. Not gradually. All at once, like a snuffed wick.

"Aldric." Wendell caught his shoulders. "Aldric. A woman what?"

The courier's eyes came back from wherever they'd gone, and fixed on Wendell's face, and something in them changed—the fog clearing, the dying man spending his last coin of clarity on one final act of precision. His hand rose. Found the collar of Wendell's coat. Pulled him close, close enough that Wendell could smell the blood and the rain and underneath them the faint sweet rot of a body finishing its accounts.

"There's a name," Aldric whispered. "On the map. At the end of the routes. One word, written in a hand I'd know anywhere, because I've carried her letters before." His grip tightened. "She signed it with a sigil. An old one. A house sigil."

"Whose house?"

And Aldric Vane, courier, eleven months faithful, spent his final breath on the answer:

"Ishikawa."

* * *

The world went very quiet and very far away.

Wendell knelt in the streambed holding a dead man's collar, and the rain fell on both of them equally, and somewhere downstream the water went on over the stones the way it had gone on for ten thousand years, indifferent, and none of it reached him. Because the name had gone through him like a spear through a shield—not stopping him, going through, coming out the other side into some country behind his own body where a boy stood in a doorway watching his sister pack a bag she'd never unpack.

Liora.

Sold. That was the word his father had used, wrapped in softer cloth—*placed*, *settled*, *given a future*—but Wendell had been fourteen, not stupid, and he'd seen the ledger lines in his father's study, the payments that arrived after she was gone, the way his father couldn't meet his eyes at supper for a season. He'd told himself a hundred stories since. She'd been married well. She'd been apprenticed to a distant guild. She'd gone to the coast. He'd built those stories brick by brick over ten years because the alternative was a house he'd burned down in his memory and refused to walk through even in dreams.

And now a dying stranger on a smugglers' road had pressed a sealed map into his hands and said *Ishikawa*, and every story he'd built came down at once, and under the rubble was the thing he'd always known: his father had sold her. Sold her south, sold her under, sold her to whatever paid, and the family had survived on the price, and Wendell had eaten that price, worn that price, trained with that price in his belly every day of his mercenary life.

The map lay against his chest where Aldric had pressed it. Warm from the dying man's body. Light as a sin.

He should burn it. That was the sane thought, arriving late and thin. Riders in Vaelric's colors were circling the hills looking for whoever had robbed their courier, and the company would learn by morning that Wendell Ishikawa had been camped on the smugglers' road the night a Vaelric agent died, and Maren—Maren would ask questions, and Wendell had never lied to Maren, not once in fifteen years, and the map in his coat was the beginning of a road that ended everywhere he'd sworn never to go.

Burn it. Walk away. Let the dead stay dead.

His hands, which had a life of their own and had always been wiser than the rest of him, tucked the oilskin roll inside his shirt, against his skin, where Aldric had carried it for eleven months.

* * *

He buried the courier at dawn, badly, because the ground was stone and the rain had turned the little soil there was to soup, and because he was digging with a knife and a helmet and grief he refused to name. He stacked stones over the shallow grave the way travelers do on the high roads, and he had no prayer to give because his god had been silent for ten years, so he gave the only words he had left, the ones every soldier knows, the ones for brothers-in-arms: *rest easy, the watch is ended.* It wasn't true—nothing about this watch was ended—but it was what he had.

Then he sat on a rock above the grave and did the arithmetic.

The riders would circle back. Aldric had said so, and Aldric had struck him as a man who died the way he lived, accurately. When they found the grave—if they found the grave—they'd read it for what it was: someone had been here, someone had taken something, someone was now ahead of them on roads that led, sooner or later, to Thornfell. To the mountain. To the door.

And the company. That was the worse arithmetic. Vaelric had bought loyalty the way he bought everything, Aldric had said—one of yours especially—and Wendell had laughed inwardly at that, then, because what courier wouldn't say a thing like that, dying, to make his burden someone else's? But it didn't feel funny now. It felt like a stone in his boot he'd been walking on for years without looking down.

He took out the map.

He didn't break the seal. His thumb rested on the wax, on the smooth blank face of the filed signet, and everything in him wanted to tear it open right there—to see the routes, to see the sigil, to see the word in his sister's hand or his father's or whoever's hand it was, to make it real or make it lie. But Aldric's voice was in his ear, careful and reverent: *the seal is part of the lock. Break it wrong and the door knows.*

Instead he turned it over. And there, pressed into the oilskin's underside where no one would look unless they were looking, was a mark burned small and neat: a single character, a waystation glyph, the kind smugglers chalked on doorposts to mark safe houses. Beneath it, in different ink, fresher, hurried: a place-name.

*Cinderhold.*

Two days' walk south. A border town he'd passed through twice and forgotten both times, full of tanneries and cheap rooms and the kind of people who asked no questions because questions were bad for business.

The rain was easing. The light was coming up gray and thin through the pines, and somewhere below, on the king's road, he could hear the first carts of the morning, ordinary people starting ordinary days that had nothing in them but weather and bread and worry. He envied them with a purity that surprised him.

Wendell stood, rolled his bedroll, kicked the last coals of his low fire apart, and looked once at the mound of stones that was all the world would get back of Aldric Vane.

"The watch is ended," he said again. "Mine isn't."

Then he turned south, toward Cinderhold, with a dead man's map warm against his chest and his sister's name ringing in his skull like a bell that would not stop—and behind him, far up the trail, faint through the thinning rain, he heard hoofbeats. Coming back around.
