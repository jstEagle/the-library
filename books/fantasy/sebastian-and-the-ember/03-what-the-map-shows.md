## 3. What the Map Shows

The waystation had been abandoned so long that the mountain had begun to take it back. Moss climbed the eastern wall in green fingers. The roof sagged over the stable like a tired shoulder. Someone had carved a hearth-god's face into the lintel stone decades ago, and rain had worn its mouth to a smear, so it looked less like a blessing now and more like a warning swallowed halfway.

Wendell shut the door behind him and set the bar.

Then he stood in the dark with his back against the wood and listened to his own breathing, which was too fast, and made himself slow it down the way you slow a horse after a hard ride—not all at once, or you break something.

The courier's blood was still under his fingernails.

He'd washed twice at the stream before climbing here, scrubbing until his hands went raw, but it didn't matter. It wasn't the kind of thing that came off with water. Ten years of contracts had taught him the difference between the blood you carry on your skin and the blood you carry somewhere else, somewhere the stream can't reach. This was the second kind. A stranger had died holding his wrist, and the last word out of the man's mouth hadn't been *help* or *mercy* or even *run*.

It had been *Ishikawa*.

Nobody said that name. Wendell had spent a decade making sure of it. He'd buried it under the company name, under the mercenary shorthand everyone used—*Reyes' man, the quiet one, Ishi*—until even he almost believed the person called Wendell Ishikawa had died somewhere between his father's house and his first contract. Almost. Apparently not far enough. Apparently the past could smell him anyway, across half a country, through the dark, dying men and all.

He crossed the room by feel. There was an old supply alcove behind the collapsed stall partition—a smuggler's cache, once, long emptied—and it gave him a corner where lamplight wouldn't show through the broken window. He lit the stub of candle he'd taken from the courier's pack, cupped it, and unrolled the map for the third time since the man died.

Third time. He'd looked three times and understood almost nothing, and every hour he waited made the not-understanding worse, because out there on the smugglers' road were riders in Vaelric's colors who had chased a dying man up a mountain for whatever this was, and they would not stop at one corpse.

The wax seal was already broken—he'd done that in the first minute, hands shaking more than he'd have liked—but the impression itself still held its shape in the fragments. He turned a piece of it toward the candle flame.

A mountain. Not Thornfell's peak as any cartographer drew it, but a cross-section, cut away like a dissected animal to show what lived inside. And beneath the roots of the mountain, drawn small and precise, a door.

Not a gate. Not a pass. A door, rendered in fine black lines, set into the rock like a tooth in a jaw.

Above the door, inked in a hand steadier than the rest of the map, was a sigil Wendell knew better than his own heartbeat. A mountain split by a single vertical line, with three horizontal strokes beneath—the ember-grate, his grandmother had called it, the fire that burns below the floor. The mark of House Ishikawa. His father had stamped it into the lead seals on the grain ledgers. Wendell had spent his childhood grinding it into the dirt with a stick, drawing it in the ash of the hearth, and then, later, after everything, trying to grind it out of himself entirely.

It was on this map the way a signature is on a contract. Not decoration. Ownership.

His mouth had gone dry. He made himself keep looking.

The routes were the strange part. Whoever had drawn this thing knew the mountains the way Sera's people—the smugglers, the shrine-keepers, the ones the company called *ash-rats* when they thought no one was listening—knew them: from underneath. The roads weren't roads. They were a lattice of tunnels and old mine-workings and dry riverbeds beneath the ice, threading through Thornfell's massif like veins through a hand. Some were marked with symbols he half-recognized from his years running border contracts—collapsed here, flooded there, watched. Others were marked with symbols he didn't recognize at all: small circles, a spiral, a repeated glyph like a tongue of flame lying on its side.

And every route, every vein, every line, converged. No matter where you started—at Cinderhold, at the salt passes, at the drowned stair near Greywater—all the lines bent inward, funneling down through the mountain's roots to that single door beneath.

Someone had drawn a map to one place, and disguised it as a map to everywhere.

Wendell sat back on his heels. The candle guttered in air that suddenly felt thin.

There was more. In the lower corner, in that same steady hand, a column of figures—dates? payments?—and beside them, abbreviated to near-nothing, a ledger-shorthand his father had used, the private cipher of a man who kept two sets of books and trusted no one with either. Wendell recognized the style before he recognized a single character, and recognition landed in his stomach like a dropped stone. He'd learned that shorthand copying letters at his father's desk, twelve years old, proud to be trusted.

Trusted. He almost laughed. The sound came out wrong and he strangled it.

At the bottom of the column, underlined twice, was a single word.

*Sera.*

Just that. No route number, no symbol, no explanation. One word in the same careful hand, sitting at the end of the ledger-lines like a signature at the end of a debt.

He stared at it until the letters stopped meaning anything. Then they started meaning too much.

Sera. Ash and salt, in the old hearth-tongue. His mother had burned a sprig of it at the winter altar every year of his childhood—*so the god remembers us*, she'd say, *even when we forget ourselves*. It was a word from the faith his father had abandoned and his sister had loved. Liora used to press dried seras-flowers between the pages of her books, saying the god lived in them, that Ember spoke softest through things that had learned to survive fire.

But this wasn't a flower pressed in a book. This was a word written on a map to a door beneath a mountain, in his father's cipher, sealed with wax that men had died carrying.

Which meant it was either a place, or a person, or a price.

Wendell rolled the map closed with hands that were not quite steady and sat in the dark of the dead waystation while the candle burned down, and let himself think the thought he'd been refusing since the courier died.

His father had sold something to buy the family's survival. Everyone in the valley had known the shape of it without knowing the details—the bad harvests, the debts, the year the tax-collectors came and then, miraculously, never came again; the year Liora vanished and their father told the village she'd gone to an aunt in the lowlands, and never once met anyone's eyes when he said it. Wendell had been fourteen. He'd believed the aunt story for exactly as long as believing it was survivable, and then he'd stopped believing it and started running—first to the company, then deeper into the company, until the work filled up all the space where the question used to live.

*What did Father sell?*

He'd always assumed he knew the answer. Land. Labor. Secrets. Something ordinary, dressed in shame.

But a map in his father's cipher, leading through the bones of Thornfell to a door marked with the Ishikawa sigil, carried by a man hunted to death by Vaelric's riders—

That wasn't land. That wasn't labor.

That was the kind of thing you sold only once, and only if you'd already decided your soul could bear the interest.

The candle died. Wendell didn't relight it. He sat in the black and listened to the rain start up again against the roof, and somewhere in the middle of the night he realized he'd drawn his knife without deciding to, and was holding it across his knees like a talisman, which was stupid, because knives didn't help against the past.

He put it away. Eventually.

* * *

Dawn came gray and mean through the broken window.

He'd slept maybe two hours, in snatches, boots on, pack strapped. Every noise the mountain made—a settling beam, a bird, the drip of water finding its way through old mortar—dragged him up out of sleep with his heart slamming. By the time the light was strong enough to move by, he'd catalogued every exit twice and decided the waystation had been a mistake. Too obvious. Smugglers' road, abandoned station, shelter from the rain—it was the first place anyone tracking the courier would look, and Vaelric's outriders tracked people for a living.

He should go. Higher, off the road, through the scree fields where horses couldn't follow. He should burn the map, even—no. He touched the oilcloth roll inside his coat and couldn't make his fingers want to let it go. Burning it meant staying ignorant, and ignorance, he was beginning to understand, was the luxury his father had actually purchased with whatever he'd sold. Wendell was done being bought peace with someone else's coin.

He was gathering his gear when he heard the horses.

Two of them, maybe three, coming up the smugglers' road at a walk—that particular unhurried pace of men who are searching, not fleeing. Wendell killed the last inch of candle with his thumb, moved to the gap in the wall where the moss had eaten the mortar, and looked down through the rain-haze.

Three riders. Company cloaks.

His own cloaks. The grey-and-ember of Maren's outfit, mud-spattered, practical, the sight of it hitting him with a homesickness so sudden and stupid it took a moment to see the wrongness underneath. Company scouts didn't ride the smugglers' road. Company scouts had no business this deep in the hills, nowhere near any contract Wendell knew of—and he'd finished the last one yesterday, signed and sealed, which meant officially he was between jobs, invisible, free.

Unofficially, someone had sent them looking.

They drew rein in the mud below the station. Wendell knew two of them by silhouette alone: Doss, broad and round-shouldered, and young Ferren, who still rode like riding was a favor the horse did him. The third was a lean figure in a hooded oilskin he didn't know, and it was the third one who swung down first and stood studying the waystation with his head tilted, the way you study a trap you're fairly sure has something in it.

"Wendell!" Doss called up, cheerful, carrying. "You in there? Saw smoke-sign on the ridge trail."

There had been no smoke. Wendell's mind went cold and quick, the way it did before a fight, cataloguing. They were fishing. Which meant whoever sent them didn't know where he was—which meant the courier hadn't talked before he died, or hadn't had time—and these three were sweeping blind along the only road through the hills.

Which meant he had time. A little.

He stepped back from the gap, rolled his shoulders, put on the face he wore for captains and clients—bored, competent, slightly put-upon—and lifted the bar.

* * *

"Thought you'd be long gone south," Doss said, stamping rain off his boots in the doorway. "Contract's done, isn't it?"

"Done." Wendell crouched by the cold hearth, feeding it kindling like a man with nothing better to do than get warm. "Road washed out at the ford. Waiting for the water to drop." True enough—the ford *was* high. He'd checked it himself yesterday, back when checking it was just a thing a careful man did.

"You alone?"

"Mm."

Ferren drifted toward the stable partition, all curiosity, nineteen years old and incapable of standing still, and Wendell watched him with the edge of his eye while keeping his voice easy for Doss. "Why're you on the smugglers' road? We've got nothing running out here."

"New orders." Doss shrugged, but something in the shrug was careful. "Company's taking a retainer. Big one. Vaelric's steward wants eyes on the hill roads—smugglers moving contraband, shrine-trash, that sort of filth. We're to stop and question anyone unusual."

"Anyone unusual," Wendell repeated.

"You know how it is." Doss grinned, apologetic. "Anyone who looks like trouble."

The lean one in the oilskin hadn't spoken. He'd come in last, positioned himself by the door—Wendell noted it, filed it—and now he walked a slow circuit of the room, touching nothing, looking at everything. At the cold hearth. At the single bedroll. At the empty candle-stub, and the alcove behind the fallen partition where a man might sit with a light hidden from the window.

Wendell fed another stick to the fire and made himself breathe.

"So I'm unusual now?" he said. "Ten years in the company and I'm contraband?"

"You're not the target." It was the lean one who answered, finally, and his voice was flat and educated and wrong for a scout, wrong for the whole mud-and-rain business of the company, and Wendell's neck prickled. "We're looking for a courier. Man in his forties, grey beard, lame in the left leg. Carried a satchel. He was seen on this road two nights ago, headed north, bleeding."

"Bleeding," Wendell said.

"Riders ran him down outside Cinderhold. Lost him in the fog." The man turned. Under the hood his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of someone paid to be patient. "If he reached you, he may have passed something. Papers. A parcel. Men like that often do—hand their burden to a stranger and die happy. You understand. It's nothing personal. It's the steward's property."

Here it was, then. The moment, arriving exactly as it had been waiting to arrive since the courier's weight went heavy against his chest. Wendell had spent ten years being the man companies could trust with money, with messages, with the ugly ends of contracts. Maren's word for him, half-affectionate, was *the vault*—you put something in Wendell Ishikawa and it stayed there. He had built his whole self on the principle that he did not lie. Not from virtue, exactly. From economy. Lies needed maintenance, and maintenance was risk, and risk was death, and so he'd simply arranged a life where the truth served him well enough.

The truth would not serve him now.

"He reached me," Wendell said. "Dying. I did what I could, which was nothing. He bled out before dawn." He kept his eyes on the fire, let a beat pass, added the part that cost: "He said a name. Didn't make sense. I figured it was fever."

The lean one had gone very still. "What name?"

"Ishikawa." Wendell spat into the hearth, casual, disgusted. "Some dead lordling out of the valley histories. My grandfather's generation. Fever-talk."

Silence. Rain on the roof. Ferren had stopped drifting and was watching the exchange with the open confusion of a boy who couldn't tell yet whether he was in a conversation or an interrogation.

"That's all?" the lean one asked.

"That's all. He grabbed my wrist, said the name, and was gone." Wendell looked up then, straight at the man, and gave him the full weight of ten years of professional boredom. "If he was carrying something, it died with him. I searched the body for identification—company habit—and found a purse and a saint's medal. You're welcome to both. I was going to turn them over at Cinderhold."

A lie. All of it, every word past the first sentence, smooth and warm as forged steel, and it came out of him without a stutter, and some detached part of Wendell stood to one side and watched himself do it and felt the floor of his life shift half an inch. So that's what it was like. So that's what his father had felt like, every day, smiling across the grain-ledgers.

The lean one studied him for a long moment. Then he smiled, thin as a blade's edge, and shook his head.

"No need. Keep the purse. Bury the poor devil if you haven't." He turned for the door, and paused, and said, without turning around: "The steward's paying a bounty, incidentally. For recovery of the property. Considerable. More than a season's contracts." Now he glanced back, pale eyes mild. "In case it turns up. Things do turn up. On roads like these."

"I'll keep it in mind," Wendell said.

They rode south, back down toward Cinderhold, and Wendell stood in the doorway until the rain folded them away, and then he barred the door again and leaned his forehead against the cold wood and laughed once, silently, at absolutely nothing.

* * *

He left within the hour, north, off the road, up through the scree where the horses couldn't follow, because the lean one's parting words had told him everything he needed to know about what happened next: they'd be back, or others would, and next time the questions would come with irons attached. The bounty speech hadn't been an offer. It had been a hook, left dangling to see what bit.

By midday he was high enough that Thornfell's massif filled half the sky, white-capped and indifferent, the door beneath it somewhere under all that stone, waiting. He stopped in the lee of a boulder, took out the map, and read the word at the bottom of the ledger-column one more time.

*Sera.*

Ash and salt. A flower that survives fire. A name, or a place, or a debt his father had underlined twice.

And beneath it, so faint he'd missed it in every previous reading—missed it because he'd been looking at the word instead of around it—one final mark in the margin. Not a symbol from the routes. Not a cipher-figure.

A tiny sketch, five strokes of the pen, of a sprig of dried seras pressed flat, the way a girl presses flowers between the pages of her books.

Liora's habit. His sister's small devotion, reproduced in his father's hand, at the end of a map men died carrying.

Wendell folded the map away, stood, and turned his face north toward Cinderhold—because the courier had come from there, because the riders had lost him in the fog outside its walls, because if the word *Sera* meant a person, then a person who mattered enough to write down was a person who could be found, and Cinderhold was where finding started.

Behind him, far below, faint on the wind, he thought he heard a horn. Once. Maybe just the mountain.

He walked faster.
