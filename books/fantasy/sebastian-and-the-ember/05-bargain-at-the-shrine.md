## 5. Bargain at the Shrine

The shrine was behind a tannery, which Wendell decided was either the best hiding place in Cinderhold or the worst, depending on how much you trusted the noses of the people hunting you.

Sera led him down an alley that smelled of lye and rot and old smoke, past drying racks hung with hides stretched like flayed men, to a door with no handle. She pressed her palm flat against the wood and said something under her breath—too quiet for him to catch—and the door swung inward on darkness that smelled of woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath. Incense, maybe. Or ash that remembered being incense.

"After you," she said.

"I don't like rooms I can't see out of."

"Nobody who does what you do likes rooms they can't see out of." She stepped past him, unbothered. "That's why you do it in the open, where everyone can watch you kill. In here, nobody's watching. That's the point."

He followed her anyway. He told himself it was because the street behind them had eyes—two men in undyed wool who'd been mending the same net for an hour without catching anything—but the truth was simpler and he didn't look at it directly: she had recognized the seal on the map, and he needed to know how.

The room inside was small and low-ceilinged, lit by a single brazier whose coals glowed a color Wendell hadn't seen in ten years. Not orange. Not red. A deep, banked gold, like the last light of a fire that refused to die. Above the brazier, set into the wall itself, was a slab of black stone no bigger than a paving tile, and carved into its face was a spiral—the mark of Ember, worn nearly smooth by touch.

An ember-shrine. Illegal in every city Vaelric's writ reached, which was all of them now, and yet here one sat behind a tannery in a border town, tended like a garden.

"You're a priestess," Wendell said.

"I'm a smuggler." Sera crouched at the brazier and fed it a twist of dried herb. "The priestess part is a hobby that gets me hanged."

"The god is dead. Everyone knows the god is dead."

"Everyone knows a lot of things." She looked up at him, and the coal-light caught her face from below, hollowing her cheeks, brightening her eyes until they seemed almost feverish. "Sit down, mercenary. You've been bleeding since the waystation and pretending you haven't."

He touched his side and his fingers came away red. The bolt-graze from the ambush on the smugglers' road had opened again during the walk into town. He sat, mostly because standing had become negotiable.

She cleaned and stitched him with the same brisk hands she'd used to haggle over the map, working by brazier-light, saying nothing for a long time. It was a comfortable nothing, or would have been if his ribs didn't ache and if the map weren't sitting in his head like a splinter. *A door beneath Thornfell. The Ishikawa sigil. One word: Sera.*

Not her name. He'd checked the lettering twice. The word on the map was older than her, older than any living tongue that used it—a title, maybe, or a function. *Sera* meant something else in the dead language of the under-court. But when he'd shown her the seal, her hands had gone still on the parchment, and stillness like that couldn't be faked.

"There," she said, tying off the last stitch. "Try not to get stabbed for a day or two."

"What did you recognize?" he asked. "On the map. When you went quiet."

Sera wiped her hands on a rag and didn't answer right away. She knelt back at the brazier and watched the coals breathe.

"When I was fourteen," she said, "I heard a voice come out of this stone. Just once. Three words. My mother heard it too, but she pretended she didn't, because pretending was safer, and a year later she was dead of a fever and I stopped pretending." She turned. "The voice hasn't spoken since. Except it has. Little pieces. Leaks. A word here, a feeling there, always through fire, always faint, like someone shouting up from the bottom of a well. Ten years of leaks, mercenary. And three nights ago, the leaks got louder. Like whatever's down there felt something move."

"The map," Wendell said quietly.

"The map." She held out her hand. "Show me the whole thing. Not the seal. All of it."

He hesitated. Every instinct he owned said don't show anyone, don't trust anyone, the company taught you better than that. But the company had also taught him that instinct without information was just fear wearing armor, and he was bleeding in a priestess's cellar with half of Vaelric's border riders looking for a courier's killer.

He drew the map out and unfolded it between them.

Sera read it the way he imagined scholars read scripture—slowly, lips moving, fingers hovering above the ink without touching. Her breath changed halfway down. By the time she reached the bottom, where the routes converged beneath Thornfell on a small black rectangle marked with the Ishikawa spiral and that single impossible word, she had gone very pale.

"This is real," she whispered. "Gods below, this is actually real."

"It's bait," Wendell said. "Or a trap. The courier died getting it to me. Someone wanted it in my hands badly enough to spend a man's life."

"Yes. Which means it matters." She looked up. "You know what's at the end of these lines?"

"A door."

"You know what's behind the door?"

"No."

"Neither do I." She laughed, short and sharp, a sound with no humor in it. "And I have spent eleven years listening to leaks from behind that door, and I am going to find out, and you are going to take me there."

"I'm not taking you anywhere. I work alone."

"You worked alone for exactly as long as it took you to bleed through your shirt in my shrine." She folded the map with careful, almost reverent hands and held it out to him. "Here's my offer, and I'll only make it once. You can't cross the mountains ahead of Vaelric's army—not wounded, not alone, not with riders sweeping every pass. The roads are closed. The passes are watched. But there are other ways. Old ways. Smugglers' roads under the skin of the world, tunnels my people have kept secret through three wars and two purges. I can put you beneath Thornfell before the first snow. Nobody else alive can say that."

"And the price?"

"You help me reach the source of the whispers." Her eyes were steady and burning. "Whatever's behind that door—I go in with you. That's the whole price. No coin. No blood-oath. Just that."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. He thought about the courier's blood cooling on the road, and the name the man had gasped—*Ishikawa*—and the sigil on the map that matched the one burned into his family's signet before his father sold it along with everything else. He thought about the fact that he had lied to company scouts yesterday, the first lie he'd told in six years, and how easy it had been, and how that frightened him more than the riders did.

Every rational part of him said walk away. Give the map to the coals, let the dead courier's errand die with him, go back to contracts and rain and the clean simplicity of being paid to point a blade.

But the map ended at a door sealed with his own bloodline. And somewhere behind that door, if the courier's dying urgency meant anything at all, was the reason his father had sold what he'd sold. Wendell had spent ten years telling himself the silence of the altars was grief he could carry. He was starting to suspect it was a debt instead.

"One condition," he said.

Sera raised an eyebrow.

"We go fast, we go quiet, and when we reach the door, we decide together what happens next. You don't get to run ahead into whatever's down there because a stone talked to you when you were fourteen."

"That's two conditions."

"Take both or leave me here."

For a moment he thought she'd argue. Instead she smiled—small, tired, and genuinely pleased, which unsettled him more than anger would have.

"Agreed," she said. "We leave tonight. There's a hatch behind the tannery's smoke-house that drops into the old salt-roads. We'll be under the hills before dawn."

He took the map back. His hand was steadier than it had any right to be.

"It's about the map," he told her, and himself, and the dark little shrine with its stubborn golden coals. "Nothing else."

"Of course it is," Sera said, and began banking the fire.

---

They came out of the alley onto Cinderhold's high street as the sun went down, and that was when Wendell smelled it.

Smoke. But wrong smoke—thick, resinous, greedy. Not hearth-smoke. Burning-oil smoke.

At the far end of the street, where the town gate stood open to the evening road, torches were coming through. A dozen riders in colors so dark they swallowed the torchlight, moving at a walk, unhurried, the way men move when they own everything they're looking at. Behind them, at the treeline, Wendell could see the rest—banners furled, camp-fires kindling in disciplined rows. Not a raid. An occupation. Vaelric's outriders, arriving to make an example of the last free market on the border.

"They're early," Sera breathed beside him. "They weren't due for—" She stopped. Turned to him. "They followed you. From the waystation. They let you reach town so they could see who took you in."

Wendell's stomach dropped. Every face on the street had turned toward the gate now, and he watched the town's mood shift in real time—the way fear moves through a crowd like water through cloth. Somewhere a child started crying. Somewhere a shopkeeper began hauling shutters closed with shaking hands.

An officer at the head of the column dismounted. He was tall and gray-templed and moved like a man who had done this many times, and he produced a scroll and began reading aloud in a clear, carrying voice. Wendell couldn't make out the words from this distance. He didn't need to. He knew the shape of them. *By order of the Lord Protector. Harborers of fugitives. Traitors' goods forfeit.* And then, inevitably, the punishment, delivered to a town for the crime of containing them.

"They'll burn it," Sera said. Her voice had gone flat and cold, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant. "Cinderhold. They burn one town a season, usually. To keep the rest compliant." She was staring at the shrine's direction, at the tannery, at the illegal little coal-glow she'd kept alive for eleven years. "Everything I built is behind that wall."

"Sera." He caught her arm. "We can't stop it. Twelve riders now, four hundred behind them. We can't stop it."

"I know we can't stop it." She pulled free of his grip, and for one raw moment he saw what was underneath the smuggler's calm—a girl kneeling at a black stone, hearing three words from a dead god, and building her whole life around the hope of hearing them again. "But I'm not watching it from the street."

They ran.

Through back lanes while the proclamation echoed off the walls, past families loading carts with the grim efficiency of people who had practiced this, past an old man standing in his doorway refusing to move, arms crossed, watching the riders come. Sera moved through it all like water finding cracks, and Wendell followed, and hated himself a little for how good at this he was—at running from burning things, at choosing survival, at the arithmetic of who can be saved and who cannot.

The tannery yard was already chaos. The tanner and his sons were dragging hides off the racks, and the tanner's wife was hurling jars of dye into a cart, and nobody asked Sera anything. She tore open the smoke-house, kicked aside a rack of hanging sausages, and wrenched up a floor-hatch that Wendell would have walked past a hundred times without seeing.

"Down," she said. "Now."

From beyond the yard wall, the first crash came—wood splintering, a door giving way—and then orange light bloomed against the low clouds, washing the smoke pink. They had started at the gate quarter. Wendell knew the method; he'd served companies that did this work. Start visible, finish thorough. The town had perhaps an hour.

He dropped through the hatch into darkness and cold, onto slick stone steps that sloped down into the earth. Sera came after him, hauling the hatch closed above them, and the light died to nothing, and the sounds of Cinderhold became muffled—distant, thick, like grief heard through a wall.

Her hands found his shoulder in the dark. Steady. Shaking only a little.

"The salt-roads run north-northwest," she said, and her voice was level, and he gave her credit for that. "Three days underground. There are caches. Water. Don't ask about the food."

"Sera—"

"Don't." Quiet. Final. "Whatever you were going to say. Don't. We made our bargain. Get me to the whispers, mercenary, and I'll get you to your door. Everything else is smoke."

Above them, muffled by stone and earth, Cinderhold began to scream.

Wendell stood in the dark and listened to it, because he owed the town that much—he had brought the wolves to its gate, even if the wolves had been coming anyway. Then he turned and followed the sound of Sera's footsteps down into the old roads, into the dark under the hills, carrying a dead man's map toward a door with his name on it.

Behind them, faint even through the stone, the glow of a burning town seeped down through the hatch-cracks and painted the stairs gold.

Like embers, he thought. Like something that refused to die.

* * *

They walked for hours before either spoke.

The salt-roads were older than any kingdom Wendell knew—smooth-walled tunnels cut by hands that predated history, wide enough for two abreast in places, narrowing elsewhere to crawls that Sera navigated by memory and a stub of ember-candle she'd taken from the shrine. The candle burned with that same strange banked gold, and its light didn't flicker. It just glowed, patient, like it had all the time in the world.

"Where does it come from?" Wendell asked finally. "That color."

"My grandfather made the first one. Said he caught a piece of the last altar-fire out of Thornfell before the purges." She glanced back at him, candlelight carving her face out of the dark. "Every ember-candle in the smugglers' roads is descended from that flame. Eleven years of keeping one fire alive, passed hand to hand. Why do you think I recognized your seal, mercenary? We've been waiting for something out of Thornfell my whole life. I just expected it to arrive less bloody."

They made camp in a widening where an ancient side-tunnel branched off, its mouth choked with rubble. Sera produced hard bread and smoked fish from a cache niche sealed with wax, and they ate in silence while the candle glowed between them.

It was Wendell who broke it, because the question had been sitting in him since the shrine, growing heavier with every mile.

"The word on the map," he said. "*Sera*. It's not a name."

She went still, fish halfway to her mouth.

"It's a title. Old-court language. My father used fragments of it sometimes, when he was drunk enough to talk about the war. *Sera* — the one who listens. The one who keeps the hearth and hears what speaks through it." He watched her carefully. "The map doesn't mark a person. It marks a function. Whoever holds that title when the door opens—"

"Is me," Sera finished softly. She set the fish down. "Eleven years of leaks. Three words when I was fourteen. A candle-flame carried hand to hand through three generations of smugglers." She laughed, and this time there was wonder in it, and terror too. "My grandmother wasn't hiding candles. She was keeping a post."

"Then you understand why I need you calm," Wendell said. "If Vaelric's people figure out what you are—"

"They won't. Because you won't tell them, and neither will I." She picked the fish back up, and some of the steadiness returned to her, armor sliding back into place. "Besides. If I'm the one who listens, then listen to this: the leaks got louder the night you took the map. Louder than they've ever been. Whatever's behind that door knows you're coming, Wendell Ishikawa. And it's not afraid."

He wanted to ask how she could possibly know that last part. He wanted to ask what the voice sounded like, whether it sounded like the voice he remembered from the altar at Thornfell's foothills, warm and vast and gone. He didn't. Some questions you save for when you're strong enough to survive the answers.

Instead he lay back against the cold stone, one hand near his blade out of habit, and stared up at darkness the candle couldn't reach.

"It's about the map," he said again, to no one.

Above them, miles of rock and earth away, Cinderhold burned through the night. And far ahead, deeper than the salt-roads, deeper than the roots of the mountains themselves, something behind a door sealed with his bloodline waited—and for the first time in ten years, Wendell let himself imagine that the silence on the other side of it might not be silence at all.

Sera snuffed the candle. In the dark, very faint, so faint he could have imagined it, the black stone in her pocket—she'd pried it from the shrine wall before they fled; he'd seen her do it, cradling it like a child—grew warm.

"Did you feel that?" she whispered.

Wendell had.
