## 6. Old Roads, New Ghosts

The tunnel mouth opened behind a collapsed shrine on the Cinderhold road, hidden under forty years of ivy and one very convincing rockfall. Sera had pried the first stone loose with her boot heel and said, "After you," and Wendell had looked at the black gap in the hillside like it owed him money.

"Smugglers' roads," she told him as they descended. "Older than Vaelric. Older than Thornfell's kings, probably. My grandmother ran salt through these when the border tolls got greedy. Her mother ran gunpowder. Before that, who knows—bones, relics, people."

"People," Wendell repeated.

"Sometimes people need moving quietly." She held up her lantern and watched his face do something complicated. "Not everyone who disappears is sold, mercenary. Some of us just leave."

He didn't answer that. He'd been not answering things for two days now, ever since the outriders' torches had lit up Cinderhold's skyline like a festival nobody wanted. She was starting to build a catalog of his silences: this one meant *I don't want to talk about my family*, that one meant *I'm counting exits*, and the long one at dusk yesterday, staring at nothing, meant *I heard something I didn't tell you about*.

She'd get to that last one eventually.

The tunnel swallowed them by degrees. First the light of the burning town behind them, then the sound of it—a low crackle, like the world clearing its throat—and then nothing but their boots and the drip of water somewhere ahead and the lantern's small stubborn circle of warmth.

Sera knew these roads the way other priests knew scripture. Not every passage—the network was older than any single memory—but enough. Left at the third junction where the ceiling dropped. Right where someone had carved a fish into the wall two hundred years ago, because smugglers marked their turns the way sailors marked coasts: with jokes, mostly, and prayers underneath.

She touched the fish as they passed. Habit.

"You've used these before," Wendell said. Not a question.

"I lived in them for three years." She ducked under a beam some forgotten crew had braced and never come back to check. "When the hearth-faith got outlawed in the borderlands, some of us decided outlawed wasn't the same as wrong. There's a whole congregation down here, if you know how to look. Waystations. Shrines. People who leave bread out for travelers they'll never meet."

"And Ember?" His voice came out careful, the way men handle a blade they're afraid of. "Your god lives in tunnels?"

"My god lives wherever someone keeps a flame alive." She glanced back at him. "Which is more places than you'd think. More than *he* wants anyone thinking, anyway."

"He?"

"The one who snuffed the altars." She let that sit. Ten years of silence across the whole faith, and every priest she'd ever known had a different theory—schism, plague, punishment, death—and none of them had ever sat right with her, because gods didn't die quiet. Gods died loud. Whatever had happened ten years ago hadn't been a death. It had been a *muffling*. A hand over a mouth.

She'd never said that out loud before. Saying it to a man carrying a map sealed in wax that led beneath a mountain felt, for reasons she couldn't name, safe.

They walked. The dark did what dark does: loosened tongues or sealed them, depending. With Wendell it did both, in alternation, which Sera found almost endearing. He asked questions in bursts—how far to the next waystation, whether the tunnels flooded, had she really killed a man in Cinderhold (yes; he'd been reaching for her, not the map)—and then went silent again for an hour at a stretch, and she learned to read the silences better than the words.

Somewhere past midnight by her count, she stopped at a junction where four passages met and said, "Time for your lesson."

"I didn't sign up for lessons."

"You signed up for passage. Passage includes curriculum." She set the lantern down on a flat stone and sat beside it, cross-legged, palms up on her knees. "Sit."

He stood there a moment, all six feet of professional reluctance, then sat, mirroring her badly, like a man pretending he'd done this before and hoping she wouldn't notice he hadn't.

"Close your eyes."

"Sera."

"Wendell." She let his name hang there, and something flickered across his face—surprise, maybe, that she'd never once said it until now. "You spent ten years going to a dead altar and listening for a god who didn't answer. That's devotion, even if you dress it up as superstition. But you were listening with your ears. Ears are the wrong instrument."

"What's the right one?"

"That's the lesson." She lowered her own voice until it was barely breath. "Ember isn't a voice in the dark. Ember is the dark noticing you're warm. Stop listening *for* something. Just be still, and notice what notices you back."

For a long time there was only the drip of water and the lantern's faint oil-smell and, distantly, the mountain settling its weight around them like an old man in a chair.

Sera watched him instead of doing the exercise herself. She watched the tension go out of his shoulders by degrees—not relaxing, exactly, but redistributing, the wariness sinking from muscle into marrow where it could rest without exhausting him. She watched his breathing slow. She watched his lips part slightly, the way they do when a person stops managing their face.

And then she watched him go white.

It happened fast. One breath he was still, and the next his eyes were open and he was on his feet with his hand on his knife, scanning the tunnel like the walls themselves had threatened him.

"What did you hear?" she asked quietly.

"Nothing." Too fast. "Nothing. It's nothing. We should keep moving."

"Wendell."

"*Keep moving.*"

She rose slowly, deliberately, letting him see her hands were empty. "You almost heard something. That's not nothing. That's the first thing in ten years that's been anything, and you flinched from it like it bit you."

His jaw worked. The lantern threw his shadow huge against the tunnel wall, and for a second she thought he was going to deny it, and then he said, in a voice stripped down to studs and rivets:

"If it answers me, then the silence was a choice. Mine or its. And either way—" He stopped. Started again. "A man can survive a dead god. You grieve, you bury it, you get on with the work. A god that's been *there* the whole time, listening while I—" He shook his head. "I don't know what I'm supposed to do with that."

Sera considered several answers. The kind ones. The theological ones. In the end she gave him the true one.

"You do what I did. You get angry, and then you keep walking, because anger is heavy and the road doesn't care." She picked up the lantern. "Come on. Waystation's an hour ahead, and there's bread waiting that some stranger left for us years ago."

He followed. After fifty paces, quietly, he said, "Thank you," and she pretended not to hear, because some thanks are too fragile to acknowledge directly, like new ice.

* * *

The waystation was everything smuggler architecture promised: dry, hidden, and ugly. A chamber cut into the living rock, low-ceilinged, with a stone hearth that hadn't held fire in years, shelves carved from the walls, and a sleeping alcove behind a curtain of rotted canvas. On the shelf, exactly as promised, sat a clay jar sealed with wax, and inside the wax seal someone had pressed a thumbprint and a tiny stamped sun.

Sera broke the seal and breathed in flour and salt. Hardtack, honey-cakes gone dense with age, a twist of dried plums. Someone had left this decades ago for travelers they would never meet, trusting strangers across time.

"Eat," she said. "It's still good. They baked for keeping."

Wendell turned a honey-cake over in his fingers, looking at the sun-stamp. "Who leaves food for ghosts?"

"Everyone leaves food for ghosts. Most people just call it offerings." She built a small fire in the hearth—wood was cached here too, stacked and tarped, another stranger's gift—and set water boiling. "This whole road is a religion, if you squint. Leave something for the one behind you. Take only what the one ahead left. Nobody meets. Everybody's carried."

He ate in silence, and it was a different silence than before. Warmer around the edges.

It was Wendell, later, feeding sticks to the fire, who found the bones.

She heard him stop moving. Heard the particular quality of stillness a soldier has when he's spotted something wrong. Then: "Sera. Light."

She brought the lantern over. Behind the wood cache, half-hidden by a fallen shelf, lay a man—or what the years had left of one. Leathers gone stiff and gray. Boots still good, which told her he hadn't starved. And pinned to his chest, waxed and cracked but intact, a folded paper.

Wendell crouched. Didn't touch. "How long?"

"Years. Five? Ten? Long enough the rats finished with him." She eased the paper free with two fingers. The wax on it bore no sigil—just a smear, deliberate, anonymous—but the paper itself was fine stock, and when she unfolded it by lantern light, her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

It was a map. Hand-drawn, annotated, corrected in three different inks. Routes through Thornfell's under-roads. Notes on tolls and wardens and doors. And at its heart, circled and recircled until the paper had nearly worn through, a door beneath the mountain—the same door, the same mark, that waited at the end of the map in Wendell's coat.

Around the final destination, in a cramped, desperate hand, someone had written the same phrase over and over, filling every margin:

*the door knows blood. the door knows blood. the door knows blood.*

"There were others," Sera said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Looking for the same thing. Before you. Maybe years before."

Wendell took the map from her gently, the way you take evidence from a widow. He studied it for a long time. Then he began, methodically, to search the body—not robbing it, she realized. Reading it. A soldier reading a corpse for its story.

He found the second document tucked inside the leathers at the small of the back, where a courier carries things meant to survive. A letter, unsealed, unfinished. The ink had faded but the words held:

*—if whoever finds this is also following the seal, know that I got within a day of the door before they closed the pass behind me. They let me get close on purpose. It's a sieve, not a secret. Everything flows toward it and nothing comes back. If you have blood that opens it, turn around. If you don't, you're already—*

The letter ended there. No signature. No last words. Just a line trailing off into blank paper, the way a life trails off into a mountain.

"They let him get close," Wendell said. "On purpose."

"It's what I'd do." Sera heard her own cynicism come back like a reflex, and hated how comfortable it felt. "Let them come. Learn who's seeking. Close the trap when they're close enough to catch but far enough from home that no one asks questions." She looked at the bones, at the unfinished letter, at the margins full of *the door knows blood.* "Or worse—they weren't catching him. They were *measuring* him. Seeing how close the bait would draw the fish."

Wendell sat back on his heels. Firelight moved on his face and made him look briefly, terribly young. "My father's seal was on my map. His design on the door, if the ledger's right. Which means whoever built the cage built a lock only our blood opens." He looked up at her. "So why is the road littered with dead men who tried?"

"Because a lock that only opens for Ishikawa blood needs Ishikawa blood to come to it." The words left her mouth and immediately felt true in the bad way, the way true things often do. "They can't force you, Wendell. Whoever *they* are. They have to make you walk in on your own feet, believing it's your idea."

The fire popped. Somewhere deep in the mountain, water dripped its patient count.

Wendell stood. He folded the dead man's letter and slid it inside his coat next to his own map, one ghost traveling with another. Then he did something Sera didn't expect: he went back to the shelf, took a honey-cake from the jar, and set it on the dead man's chest.

"For the one behind me," he said. "Take only what the one ahead left. Right?"

Sera's throat tightened. She turned away to tend the fire so he wouldn't see what that did to her face.

They slept in shifts. In her watch, near dawn by the drip-count, Sera sat with her palms open on her knees and listened the way she'd taught him to listen, and this time she didn't guard herself, and the whisper came through clear as a struck bell:

*hurry. tell him hurry. tell my warden's son the cage is thinning and the thief is fat and the door will not stay shut much longer—*

Her eyes flew open. The fire had burned to coals. Across the chamber, Wendell slept the way soldiers sleep—lightly, armed, ready—and above him, on the tunnel wall where the lantern light reached, the shadows of the old carved marks seemed to shift and settle, though the air was perfectly still.

The voice had said *warden's son*.

The voice had known his name before Sera had ever shown him the map.

She sat in the coal-light with her heart hammering and understood, finally, the shape of the thing she'd walked into: not a smuggling run, not a rescue, but a summons—one sent a decade ago, answered late, and already expected.

Behind the rotting curtain of the alcove, Wendell stirred and murmured something in his sleep. She leaned forward to catch it.

"—Liora," he said. "I'm coming. I'm sorry. I'm coming."

Sera fed the fire a fresh stick and watched the flame stand up straight, like something listening.

One day to the high pass. Two, if the snows had closed the switchbacks. And somewhere behind them, closing fast, riders wearing colors she'd started to hate.

She let him sleep another hour. He'd need it. Everyone walking toward that door would.
