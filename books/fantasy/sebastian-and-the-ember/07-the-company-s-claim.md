## 7. The Company's Claim

They came out of the tunnel mouth at dawn, into a valley that smelled of pine and cold stone, and Wendell knew within a hundred paces that they were being watched.

He didn't say anything at first. Sera had slept in fits, curled against the tunnel wall with her pack for a pillow, and she was moving stiffly, rubbing her hands together against the mountain chill. Let her have the morning. He'd spent enough years in the field to know how to carry a bad feeling without letting it show—shoulders loose, pace even, eyes doing all the work.

The work told him plenty. Fresh hoofprints in the soft ground by the stream. A snare line reset on the game trail, tied off in a knot style he recognized the way a man recognizes his own handwriting. And smoke—not campfire smoke, but the thin, disciplined thread of a cook fire banked low, the kind you build when you don't want to be found but expect to be fed anyway.

"Company knots," he said quietly.

Sera glanced up. "What?"

"The snares." He nodded toward the treeline. "That's a Ninth Knot tie-off. Only one company ties them that way."

She went still. "Your company."

"My company." The words tasted strange, like coin left too long in another man's mouth. "The Graywater Free Company. Two hundred spears, forty riders, and a quartermaster who watered the wine and still made everyone love her." He kept walking. "Maren Voss."

"You trust her?"

"I trusted her with my life for eleven years." He adjusted the strap of his pack. "That's not the same as trusting her now."

* * *

They met at midday, because of course Maren would choose midday—no shadows to hide in, no advantage for an ambush, everything laid open and honest under a flat white sky. That was Maren. She believed fairness was a weapon you could sharpen like any other.

She sat her horse in the middle of the valley meadow with four riders behind her, and she'd dressed down: no captain's braid, no company colors beyond the gray cloak every Graywater swore on. Her hair was shorter than he remembered. There were new lines around her eyes, carved deep, the kind that came from making decisions she hated.

Wendell stopped twenty paces out and put Sera slightly behind him, which was pointless and which they both knew was pointless, and which Maren absolutely noticed.

"Wendell Ishikawa," she said. Her voice hadn't changed. Low, unhurried, the voice that had talked him through his first battle and read him the rites over three dead friends. "You look like hell."

"You should see the other guys."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and died there. She dismounted, slow, deliberate, showing him empty hands, and walked the last ten paces alone. Up close she smelled of horse and leather and something underneath it—incense, faint and expensive. Vaelric's incense. Wendell's stomach turned.

"When did you cut your hair?" he asked, because he couldn't think of anything else to say.

"Last winter." She touched the ends of it absently. "It got grabbed in a fight in Kellin's Ford. Seemed easier." She looked past him at Sera, then back. "You're a hard man to find, Wendell. You've always been good at disappearing. I taught you half of it myself."

"The other half you learned from me."

Another almost-smile. Then it fell away entirely, and what was left underneath was worse than anger. It was tiredness. Deep, structural tiredness, the kind that lives in a person's bones.

"We should talk," she said.

"So talk."

"Not here." She glanced at the sky, at the ridgelines, at the four riders who were very carefully not looking at anything. "Walk with me. Just the two of us. Your friend can keep my riders company—they won't touch her. You have my word."

"Your word," Wendell said. "Is that worth more than it was when Vaelric bought it?"

Maren flinched. It was small—a tightening around the eyes, a half-breath held—but Wendell had spent eleven years learning to read her, and he saw it plain as a wound.

"That's fair," she said quietly. "That's completely fair. Walk anyway."

* * *

They walked along the stream, far enough from the others that their voices wouldn't carry, close enough that either could turn and run if it came to that. Neither ran. The water was snowmelt, loud and cold, filling the silence between them the way it always had on long marches.

"Ten years ago," Maren said, "you walked into my tent and told me Ember had gone silent, and you asked for leave to go sit with it. I gave you the leave. Do you remember what I said?"

Wendell did remember. He said nothing.

"I said grief is a country with no roads, and you can't garrison it, so don't try." She picked a piece of bark off a birch as they passed, rolled it between her fingers. "I've thought about that a lot lately. About countries with no roads."

"Maren."

"Vaelric sent for us fourteen months ago." She said it flatly, like reading a supply manifest. "Not an offer, at first. A contract. Garrison duty in the free cities. Good pay, honest terms, protection for the towns we already bleed for. You know what the free cities are, Wendell. You've seen Cinderhold burn. You know what fills that vacuum when there's no army standing in it."

"And the price?"

"The price came later." She stopped walking. Turned to face him fully, and he saw that her hands were steady but her jaw wasn't. "Loyalty. Discretion. And any documents, maps, or persons traveling east through the passes bearing certain seals." She paused. "Certain family seals."

The stream roared. Wendell made himself breathe.

"You knew it was me."

"I hoped it wasn't." Her voice cracked, just barely, and she pressed on before it could break. "When the description came through—the height, the sword work, the scar on the left forearm—I told myself it was coincidence. There are a thousand sellswords with scars. But then the outriders burned Cinderhold, and someone crossed the mountains ahead of the sweep pattern, using old smuggler roads nobody living should know, and I thought: that's Wendell. That's exactly how Wendell moves."

"Why are you telling me this? You could have taken the map already. Four riders, Wendell. You brought four riders and you're talking to me beside a stream."

"Because I'm offering you amnesty first." She held his gaze. "Because eleven years counts for something, and I'm going to spend whatever it counts for before I let this go wrong. Give me the map, Wendell. Come back with me. Vaelric will pardon everything—the desertion, the courier, all of it. You'll stand with the company again, under colors, with pay and purpose, and whatever's under Thornfell stays sealed where it belongs."

"And if I say no?"

Her face did something complicated. Grief and love and iron discipline all fighting for the same ground.

"Then I do my job," she said. "And I will hate every minute of it, and I'll do it well, because that's what I raised you to be. Someone who does the hard thing well."

* * *

They stood there while the stream filled the silence, and Wendell looked at her—really looked—and understood the worst part.

She believed it.

That was the thing that made his chest hurt. If Maren had sold out, if she'd taken Vaelric's gold cynically, he could have hated her cleanly and been done with it. But she believed serving Vaelric protected the free cities. He could see the whole architecture of it behind her eyes: the burning towns, the refugees on the roads, the memory of every contract where the Graywaters arrived too late or not at all. Vaelric had offered her order. After a lifetime of watching order fail, she'd grabbed it with both hands and refused to look too closely at what it cost.

"You've seen what he pays with," Wendell said. "Souls, Maren. Lanterns full of people."

"I've seen rumors."

"I've seen a map. I've seen a man die pressing it into my hands. I've seen—" He stopped himself before Liora. Not yet. Not until he understood more. "There's a court beneath Thornfell. People go in and don't come out. Whatever Vaelric is paying you with, it isn't money."

"It's stability." Her voice hardened. "You want to talk about what people pay? Talk to the widow in Kellin's Ford whose town still stands because Vaelric's garrison was there. Talk to the grain merchants running the passes because someone finally cleared the bandits out. I have two hundred people who eat because I sign contracts, Wendell. I don't get the luxury of believing rumors about lanterns."

"No," Wendell said. "You get the luxury of not having to believe them."

She hit him.

It was fast and professional—a short right cross, the kind she'd drilled into a hundred recruits—and it caught him on the cheekbone and snapped his head sideways. He took it. Didn't raise his hands. Stood there tasting copper while the stream roared and the four riders watched from a distance without moving.

"That's for Cinderhold," she said, breathing hard. "For every town that burned while you played hermit in the foothills for ten years. You don't get to lecture me about looking away, Wendell. You wrote the book. You turned your back on the whole world because your god got quiet, and now you come back with a smuggler and a stolen map and you want to tell me I'm the one who sold out?"

"Yes," Wendell said. Blood dripped from his chin. He wiped it with the back of his hand, calmly. "Because I came back. That's the difference, Maren. I turned away, and when the truth finally caught me, I turned around and started walking toward it. You're walking away from it right now, and you're calling it duty."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Somewhere above them a bird called, thin and lonely, and the snowmelt stream kept on with its indifferent noise.

"You always were the stubborn one," Maren said at last, and her voice had gone quiet again, quiet the way it got after battles, when she counted the dead. "I used to tell the recruits: give me a hundred Wendells and I'll hold any pass in the world. Give me a hundred men who won't bend." She shook her head slowly. "I never told them the other half. That a hundred men who won't bend will break the thing they're trying to hold."

"Maybe some things need breaking."

"Maybe." She looked at him for a long time, and he watched her weigh it—all of it, the eleven years, the four riders, the contract in her pocket, the incense smell she probably couldn't notice anymore because she'd been living inside it too long. "I can't hold the hunt off forever, Wendell. Vaelric doesn't accept reports of 'no contact' indefinitely. Sooner or later he sends someone who isn't me. Someone who doesn't owe you their life twice over."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stepped closer, and lowered her voice to almost nothing, beneath the stream's noise. "Then hear this, and hear it once. The sweep pattern goes north of here tomorrow. South is closed—Vaelric's regulars, real soldiers, not mine. There's an old shepherd track above the tree line, west fork of the pass. Hard climbing. It'll put you ahead of the hunt instead of behind it." She straightened. "And Wendell—if you find what you're looking for under that mountain, and it's half as bad as the rumors, you send word. Not to the company. To me. I'll decide then which side of this I'm standing on."

"You already know which side."

"Don't presume to know me." But her eyes said otherwise, and they both knew it.

* * *

They walked back to the horses together, and it was the strangest march of Wendell's life—eleven years of shared bread and shared graves compressed into fifty silent paces. At the edge of the meadow Maren stopped and raised her voice back to its command register, and just like that she was the captain again, flat and official.

"The map, Ishikawa. Final offer."

"You know my answer."

"I do." Something flickered across her face—pride, maybe, buried deep where it couldn't do any harm. "Then get off my contract land. Both of you. West fork of the pass, if you value your lives. And if anyone asks, I found no trace of you. No tracks, no fires, nothing. You were ghosts."

Sera joined him at the meadow's edge, eyeing his split cheekbone with a question she was smart enough not to ask aloud. Behind them, Maren mounted, gathered her riders, and turned north without a backward glance—which was itself a kind of message, because Maren Voss never left a battlefield without looking at what she'd lost.

They walked west, toward the tree line, while the hoofbeats faded north.

"She let us go," Sera said finally.

"She let us go." Wendell touched his cheekbone, winced. "And she drew us a map with her own mouth, which means somewhere between the stream and the horses, she decided Vaelric's contract was worth less than whatever we are to each other." He looked back once at the empty meadow. "Which means when Vaelric finds out—and he will find out—she's going to need somewhere to run."

"You'd take her in? After today?"

"After today especially." He shifted his pack and started climbing. "People who hit me and then save my life tend to be worth keeping around."

But that night, high on the shepherd track with the stars sharp as spearpoints, Wendell lay awake and admitted the truth he'd hidden from Maren and mostly hidden from himself: the ledger was waiting at his father's house, and the house was on the way, and he had promised himself he wouldn't stop there.

He was going to stop there.

Some doors, once named, cannot stay shut—and his father's confession was sitting behind one of them, dated in ink, waiting for him like a debt.
