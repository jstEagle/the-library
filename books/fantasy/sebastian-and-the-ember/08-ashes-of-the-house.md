## 8. Ashes of the House

The turnoff wasn't on Sera's map. It was on the one in Wendell's head, worn into him like a scar: left at the split birch, down through the alder stand, along the creek that used to run clear before the mining camps poisoned it. He didn't tell her where they were going. He just stopped at the birch and stood there, rain dripping off his hood, and she read his face the way she seemed to read everything.

"That's not on the way," she said.

"No."

"How far off the way?"

"Half a day. Maybe more, if the bridge is out."

Sera looked back down the smugglers' road, north toward the pass, then at him. Behind them Cinderhold was a smudge of smoke still visible in the valley's throat. Ahead, somewhere in the high country, Vaelric's hunters were moving faster than they were. Every hour counted. She knew it as well as he did.

"You're going whether I like it or not," she said.

"I'm going either way."

"Then we'd better move." She pulled her hood tighter and started down through the alders without waiting for him. "But when this costs us, Ishikawa, I want it noted that I objected."

"Noted," he said, and followed her into the trees.

* * *

The house had been burned twice.

He could see it from the ridge—first by whatever mob came in the year after Liora vanished, and again later, slower, by rot and weather and the patient vandalism of farmers stripping stone for walls. What remained was a blackened skeleton: two chimneys standing like sentries over a collapsed roofline, the great hearthstone cracked down the middle, the orchard gone to chokecherry and briar. His father's workshop had been the east wing. There was nothing left of it but a foundation full of nettles.

Wendell had imagined this moment a hundred times over ten years, usually at night, usually drunk enough to let himself imagine it. In every version he felt something clean—rage, or grief, or the simple animal relief of a man pissing on a grave. He felt none of those things now. He felt like a courier delivering a package to an address that no longer existed.

"You grew up here," Sera said. Not a question.

"Until I was fourteen."

She walked the yard slowly, reading it the way she'd read the map—professionally, thoroughly. "Burned from the inside first," she said, crouching at the doorway. "See how the lintel charred outward? They fired the door. Then the roof went after." She glanced up at him. "People did this. On purpose."

"People did this on purpose," he confirmed.

"Because of your sister."

"Because of what my father was. Or what they decided he was. The story changed depending on who told it. Some said he sold her to slavers. Some said he gave her to the old gods. Nobody agreed on anything except that the house deserved fire." He stepped over the threshold into the ruin. "They were more right than they knew. That's the joke."

Inside, the floorboards had rotted through in sheets, and he moved on memory alone—three steps in, turn left, avoid the third joist. The kitchen was a tangle of fallen beams. The stairs were gone entirely. But the pantry door still hung on one hinge, and behind it, exactly where a boy had once watched his father hide coin from tax collectors, there was a gap in the stone where the wall met the floor.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it with detached interest, the way you notice someone else's hands.

"What are we looking for?" Sera asked from the doorway.

"A ledger."

"Accounts?"
