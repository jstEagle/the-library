## 9. Whispers in the Deep Pass

The pass began to speak three days before Sera understood what it was saying.

At first she thought it was the wind. High altitude did strange things to sound—she'd crossed the Spine twice as a smuggler, and both times the mountains had muttered like old men with grudges. Wind through the narrows, wind over the ice fields, wind funneling down from the glacier's broken crown: it all came out sounding almost like language, almost like a voice trying to remember how words worked.

But on the third morning, crouched over a cook fire wedged into a hollow below the tree line, Sera heard her own name.

*Sera.*

Not Wendell's name. Hers.

She dropped the tin cup. It rang against stone and rolled away into the scree, and Wendell looked up from sharpening his knife with that flat, watchful expression he wore like armor.

"What?"

"Nothing." She retrieved the cup. Her hands weren't steady. "Dropped it."

He went back to his blade. He'd been quieter since the house—the ruined Ishikawa homestead with its rotted beams and its ledger full of dates and prices. Sera had read enough of that ledger to understand why he barely slept now. *Two souls sold,* the father had written, in a hand gone shaky with either age or shame. *One was meant for the boy.* Wendell hadn't spoken of it since. He just walked faster, ate less, and sharpened things that were already sharp.

Sera settled back beside the fire and listened past the wind.

*...hear me... hear me, little ash-tender...*

Her breath caught. Ten years of whispers—fragments, leaks, half-syllables that rose out of cold shrines and dying coals—and never once this clear. Never once this *directed*. The voice was thin, stretched, like a song heard through a wall, but underneath the distortion there was something vast and warm and terribly patient, and it was speaking to *her*.

*I know you,* she thought back, the way she'd prayed at her hidden shrine in Cinderhold, before Cinderhold burned. *I've heard you for years. I thought you were dead.*

*Buried,* said Ember. *There is a difference. Buried things can be dug out.*

The fire popped. Wendell glanced at her again, sharper this time. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The face you make when you're listening to something that isn't there." He sheathed the knife. "You did it at the shrine. You did it in the tunnels under Graywater. Now you're doing it here, and here is a long way from any shrine."

Sera weighed lying against telling. She'd known him two weeks; she trusted him more than anyone alive, which said less about him than about the state of her life. But the voice had said *hurry*, or it would say it soon—she could feel the words gathering behind the wind like weather—and Wendell deserved them straight.

"It's clearer," she said. "The voice. Up here it's coming through like—" She searched for the comparison and found an ugly, accurate one. "Like a leak becoming a crack. Something's getting closer to breaking open, and every mile we climb, I hear more of it."

Wendell stared at her. In the firelight his eyes had the color of wet slate. "What does it say?"

She hesitated.

"Sera." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Wendell Ishikawa—he never raised his voice, because he'd learned young that loud men died first. "What does it say?"

"It hasn't said anything yet. Not really. Just my name." She fed a stick to the fire rather than look at him. "And that it knows me."

Something moved across his face—not fear exactly. Recognition. "It used to talk to me," he said quietly. "Before. At the altar outside Thornfell. I'd sit there after contracts and it would..." He stopped. His jaw worked. "It doesn't matter. It stopped."

"It didn't stop," Sera said. "It was taken. You know that now. The refugee told us what the lanterns are."

"I know what the refugee said." He poked the coals apart with a stick, hard, scattering sparks. "I also know that gods don't get caged by one ambitious man with good tailors. And I know that if your voice-in-the-wind wants us to hurry somewhere, it might be worth asking *where*, and *why us*, before we follow it off a cliff."

As if summoned, the wind rose.

It came down the pass in a long exhale, bending the stunted pines, and inside it—threaded through it, unmistakable now, no longer deniable—Ember spoke.

*WENDELL ISHIKAWA.*

Sera flinched. Wendell went rigid, hand snapping to his sword hilt, scanning the ridgeline for the source of a voice that had no source.

*Wendell Ishikawa, son of Tomas, brother of Liora. Little flame who sat at my altar until your knees ached. I remember you. Do you remember me?*

"Hear that?" Sera whispered. "Tell me you hear that."

Wendell's face had gone bloodless. "That's not possible," he said. "That's not—it sounds like—" He couldn't finish it. He didn't have to. Sera knew what he was hearing: not a stranger's voice but an old one, a voice from before the silence, from the years when a boy had knelt on cold stone and been answered.

*Hurry,* said Ember, and the warmth in it cracked, and underneath the warmth was agony, ten years of it compressed into a single word. *Hurry, little flame. He is feeding. Every day he feeds, and every day I grow thinner, and your sister grows dimmer. Hurry. Hurry. HURRY—*

The voice cut off like a snuffed candle.

Silence rushed back in—ordinary wind, ordinary mountain. Wendell stood motionless in the middle of camp, chest heaving, staring at nothing.

"Sera." His voice came out scraped raw. "How long has it been able to do that?"

"That was the first time. For both of us, I think." She got up, legs unsteady, and closed the distance between them. "It knows your name. Your whole name. It knew your father's name. Wendell—it's been waiting for you specifically. This whole time. Whatever your father built down there, whatever deal he made—you weren't just collateral. You were *meant*."

"Don't." He stepped back from her touch. "Don't build meaning out of it. Gods use people. I've killed for companies that called themselves families; I'm not sentimental about the vocabulary." But his hands weren't steady either, and Sera saw that he was holding himself together the way he held everything together—through discipline, through refusal, through sheer bloody-minded denial of everything soft.

"The voice said hurry," she said. "So we hurry."

They broke camp in four minutes flat. Sera had learned to read Wendell's urgency by now: the fewer words, the worse the news. They climbed.

* * *

The Deep Pass earned its name by noon.

The trail narrowed to a shelf of rock barely wide enough for two, cut into the flank of the mountain with the glacier's blue-gray bulk rising sheer on their left and a drop into cloud on their right. Old smuggler marks—Sera's people's marks, faded ochre arrows chiseled into stone—pointed the way up and warned, in the shorthand of the trade, *no shelter, no water, no turning back.*

Halfway along the shelf, Sera heard the voice again. Softer now. Weary.

*Forgive the shouting. I have so little left to shout with.*

"You're welcome," Sera murmured, and Wendell shot her a look, and she shook her head: *not talking to you.*

*You are the ash-tender. The one who kept the small fires burning when keeping them was a crime.* A pause, and then, with something like wonder: *Do you know how many prayers reached me in ten years? Three hundred and eleven. Nearly all of them yours. You fed me when I was a prisoner, child. No one has ever done that.*

Sera's throat tightened. She kept walking, because stopping on the shelf felt like dying, but her eyes burned. Ten years she'd tended illegal shrines in cellar rooms and mine shafts, lighting stubs of candle and whispering to a god everyone said was dead, and she'd told herself it was stubbornness, habit, the refusal to let go of the only beautiful thing her faith had ever given her. She hadn't let herself believe anyone was listening.

Someone had been listening. Someone had been counting.

"Why me?" she asked under her breath. "Why can I hear you when no one else can?"

*Because you listened when listening cost something. Faith is a door, little ash-tender. Most people nail theirs shut. Yours has been open for years—I simply lacked the strength to walk through it. Here, this high, close to my cage, the wall between us wears thin.*

Ahead, Wendell climbed in grim silence, and Sera watched the set of his shoulders and understood something she hadn't before. The god wasn't calling to her. She was the door. Wendell was the destination. Ember had spent ten years whispering through cracks to anyone who would listen, and the one voice it had been straining toward all along was the mercenary trudging ahead of her, the man who knelt at altars and pretended he didn't care.

*Ask him,* Ember said, gentler now. *Ask him what he traded at the altar. Ask him what he promised me, the last night he heard my voice.*

"I don't pry into other people's wounds," Sera said.

*Then ask him what his sister taught him about fire.*

She opened her mouth—and stopped, because Wendell had frozen mid-stride, one fist raised, the universal signal of every soldier and smuggler alike.

*Stop. Listen.*

She listened. Above the wind, above the groan of the glacier, she heard it: the thin metallic clink of shod hooves on stone. Many horses. Coming up the pass behind them.

Wendell turned, and whatever communion had cracked him open that morning was sealed away again, packed down behind the flat gray calm of a man going to work. "Backtrail," he said. "How far?"

"Half a day. Maybe less if they're pushing." Sera strained her ears. "Twelve riders? Fifteen?"

"Fifteen." He said it without inflection, which meant he'd counted too, which meant it was true. "Company formation. Spread in pairs, scouts forward. That's Maren's drill."

"Maren let us go."

"Maren isn't leading." Wendell drew her off the shelf into a fold of rock, and they crouched there while the wind moaned past. His face, in the shadow, had aged years since morning. "Maren gave us a head start and a warning. Someone else took the leash. There's only one person it would be."

"Who?"

"Dace." The name came out of him like a splinter. "Corrick Dace. Maren's second. He wanted Vaelric's contract from the start—said the free cities were already dead, just too stupid to lie down. He thinks loyalty means picking the winner early." Wendell checked his blades, one after another, a ritual so practiced it was almost prayer. "If Dace has the hunt, we're not being chased anymore. We're being *run down*. He doesn't take prisoners unless prisoners are worth coin, and he knows we are."

*Hurry,* breathed the wind, fainter now, exhausted. *Little flame. Ash-tender. Hurry. The dark behind you is faster than the dark ahead.*

Sera looked back down the pass. Far below, where the trail threaded through the tree line, she caught it: a flicker of movement. Steel catching sun. The glint of a banner she couldn't quite resolve at this distance but knew anyway—Vaelric's colors, carried by men who had once, maybe, drunk beside her around company fires.

"They'll reach the tree line by dusk," Wendell said. "We've got maybe two hours on them across the ice field, less if the weather turns. After the ice field there's the split rock, and past that—" He stopped. His eyes went distant, reading a map in his memory. "Past that is the door."

"Then we make the ice field in two hours," Sera said.

"On this shelf? With fifteen riders behind us?" He almost smiled, and it was the worst smile she'd ever seen. "Sera. If Dace catches us in the narrows, we die tired. If we make the open ice, we die slower. Either way—"

"*Either way we're not dead yet,*" Sera snapped, and the force of it surprised them both. She grabbed his collar, hauled him close, and put every year of smuggled cargo and evaded toll and survived betrayal into her next words. "Listen to me, Wendell Ishikawa. I have spent ten years carrying coal to a god in a cage because quitting was the only sin I couldn't stomach. I am not going to lose you to Corrick Dace on a mountainside. The voice said *hurry*. So we *hurry*, and we fight when hurrying runs out, and we do not stand in a crevice cataloguing ways to die. Understood?"

For a long moment he just looked at her. Then something shifted behind the slate of his eyes—a crack, a thaw, the first heat of the morning's fire returning.

"Understood," he said.

They ran.

* * *

The ice field nearly killed them anyway.

The glacier's surface was a chaos of pressure ridges and hidden crevasses, blue ice slicked with meltwater, the sun glaring off it hard enough to blind. Sera followed Wendell's broad back and the smuggler marks he'd learned to read in three days, lungs burning, boots finding purchase by luck more than skill. Behind them, distantly, the drumbeat of hoofbeats had given way to something worse: silence. Dismounted. On foot. Gaining.

*They climb well,* Ember murmured, so faintly Sera had to strain. *Soldiers always climb well. It is the walking away afterward they never learn.*

"Not helping," Sera gasped.

*Then let me help differently.* The voice gathered itself, and Sera felt it spend something precious—felt the effort like a hand pressed flat against a wall from the far side. Ahead of them, high on the glacier, a seam of old snow hissed and slumped, sliding loose in a white ribbon that crossed the route Dace's men would have to take. Not an avalanche. Nothing so generous. Just enough to slow fifteen soldiers by twenty minutes.

*Twenty minutes,* Ember said, and its voice frayed at the edges like cloth wearing through. *That is most of what I have left. Spend it well.*

Sera stumbled and caught herself on an ice axe, heart hammering. "It's spending itself for us," she told Wendell. "Do you understand? It's *dying*, and it's buying us time with what's left."

Wendell stood on the blue ice, chest heaving, and looked back the way they'd come—at the ribbon of snow settling over the trail, at the tiny figures of men he'd trained with struggling through it—and Sera watched the last of his denial burn away. What replaced it wasn't hope. It was something harder and more useful than hope.

"Then we don't waste it," he said.

They crossed the ice field as the light went long and gold, and at its far edge the mountain split before them—the Split Rock, twin towers of granite with a slot of shadow between, the map's final landmark before the descent to the door. Wendell paused at the mouth of the narrows, breathing hard, and Sera came up beside him.

"We rest here," he said. "Ten minutes. Then the narrows, then down."

Behind them, across the ice, the pursuit had emerged from the snow-slide's debris. Even at this distance Sera could see them spreading into a skirmish line, moving fast, unhindered by doubt. And at their head, a figure in company leathers with a red scarf at the throat—Dace's colors, Dace's stride—raised one arm, and pointed, unmistakably, directly at them.

*Hurry,* whispered Ember, one last thread of sound, worn almost to nothing. *Little flame. Ash-tender. The door remembers blood. The door will open. But oh—oh, children—what waits in the narrows is not only behind you.*

Sera went cold. "What does that mean? What else is in the narrows?"

The wind gave no answer. Only, far off beneath the glacier's groan, a sound neither of them could name—deep, rhythmic, patient—like something vast beginning, at last, to wake.
