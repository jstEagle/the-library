## 10. Ambush at the Split Rock

The pass narrowed to a throat of stone two men wide, and Wendell felt the ambush before he saw it.

Ten years of mercenary work had taught his body things his mind no longer bothered with. The way birdsong stopped in a certain pattern. The way loose scree sat on a slope that hadn't been walked. The way the cold pooled differently when bodies were hiding above you, breathing shallow, waiting.

He put out an arm and Sera stopped mid-stride, her boot hovering over a patch of ice.

"What," she whispered.

"Split Rock." He nodded at the formation ahead—a granite wedge the size of a chapel, sheared down its center by some ancient violence, leaving a gap just wide enough for the smugglers' road to thread through. "It's the only choke point for six miles. If I were hunting us, this is where I'd wait."

"If *you* were hunting us."

"I am hunting us. That's how I know."

Sera's hand drifted to the knife at her hip, then past it, to the small iron charm at her throat—the ember-shrine token she'd carried out of Cinderhold's ashes. Her lips moved without sound, the way they did when she was listening for something he couldn't hear. He'd watched her do it a hundred times since the tunnels. Sometimes her face went soft afterward, like someone had spoken kindly to her. This time it went tight.

"Four," she breathed. "Maybe five. Above and behind."

"You can hear that?"

"I can hear their hearts. They're afraid of us." A ghost of a smile. "Ember says fear is loud."

Wendell almost laughed. Almost. Then he looked up at the ridge line where the morning fog was peeling back from the rocks, and he saw the glint—brief, professional, quickly stilled. A blade catching light because someone had shifted their grip.

He knew that grip. He'd drilled it into a hundred recruits: *blade low until the last second, boy, shine kills more scouts than arrows.*

His stomach dropped through the mountain.

"Company," he said quietly. "That's company positioning. That's *our* positioning."

"Your old captain?"

"Maren wouldn't—" But even as he said it, he remembered chapter seven of his own life: Maren standing in the snow at Graywater Ford, telling him Vaelric had bought the company's loyalty and meaning every word of it, believing it was mercy. "Maren said she couldn't hold the hunt forever. She didn't say who'd be holding it instead."

"Her second-in-command."

"Dace." The name came out of him like a splinter. "Dace Orlan. He trained under me. I taught him how to read terrain." Wendell's jaw tightened. "I taught half these bastards how to read terrain."

"So we go around."

"There is no around. Six miles of cliff face on the north side, a drop into the glacier on the south. Split Rock or nothing." He crouched, pulling Sera down with him, and studied the gap with a soldier's eye while something older and sicker worked at his chest. "They'll let the first man through. They always do. Take the second, seal both ends."

"Then I go first."

"No."

"Wendell—"

"They want me. The map, me, whatever Dace thinks he's retrieving. You're a complication to them. I'm the objective." He met her eyes. In the grey light of the pass her face was all sharp planes and stubbornness, ash still ground into the creases around her knuckles from Cinderhold. "You go first, they take you, and I trade everything I have left to get you back. So I go first, and you run the moment I'm through."

"And what moment is that? When they're killing you?"

"When I'm *winning*," he said. "There's a difference. Watch for it."

She stared at him for a long three seconds. Then she did something he didn't expect: she took off the iron charm and pressed it into his palm, folding his fingers closed over it.

"Then carry this. Ember watches its own."

"It's yours."

"It's a loan." Her voice was flat as slate. "Interest rates are murder. Now go."

* * *

Wendell went through the gap at a dead sprint, low, shoulder rolling, exactly the way he'd taught recruits to breach a choke point—and exactly wrong, deliberately wrong, because Dace would be reading for textbook form.

The first crossbow bolt cracked off the granite where his head would have been if he'd moved like himself. The second never fired; its shooter flinched at the wrongness of him, one heartbeat of confusion, and that heartbeat was all Wendell needed.

He came up inside the first man's guard before the man could reset. Close work now, ugly work, the kind that happened in spaces too narrow for swords. Elbow, knee, the short blade from his belt. The first soldier went down clutching his thigh, screaming a name Wendell knew—*Pietr*, Pietr who'd cried during the winter march two years ago and been teased about it for a season, Pietr whose wedding Wendell had attended in a borrowed coat.

Don't think. Thinking was for after.

Two more dropped from the rocks above, ropes and all, company-trained fast-ropes into the narrows. One was Dace himself—broader than Wendell remembered, beard gone grey at the edges, but the same balanced stance, the same weight on the balls of his feet. The other was a woman Wendell didn't recognize, which meant she'd joined after he'd left, which meant she was young, which meant—

Which meant nothing. Not yet.

"Dace." Wendell backed toward the far mouth of the gap, blade up. "Walk away. You know what I am. You know what I taught you. Don't make me spend it."

"Captain's orders." Dace's voice was steady, regretful, terrible. "Maren stood down. The rest of us didn't. Vaelric pays, Wendell, and the free cities eat, and you're carrying a piece of paper that's going to get a lot of good people killed."

"The paper's not why he wants it. Ask yourself why a lord needs a smuggler's map so badly he'd buy an army."

"I ask myself a lot of things." Dace sighed. "You were the best of us. That's the worst part."

He came on.

Fighting Dace was fighting a mirror with ten years of dust on it. Every feint Wendell tried, Dace had learned from him; every counter Dace attempted, Wendell had invented. They broke apart and came together twice, three times, blades ringing in the stone throat, breath steaming, boots skating on wet rock. Behind them, Wendell heard the unrecognized woman moving to cut off the far exit—flanking, patient, well-taught.

Well-taught by a dead man's habits.

Wendell gave ground. Gave it again. Let Dace believe the pattern was resolving the way it always resolved in training yard bouts, the senior man yielding, the junior pressing home. And when Dace committed to the finishing line—a beautiful, textbook thrust, *blade low until the last second*, gods, he'd even kept the lesson—Wendell wasn't there anymore. He was inside the arc, inside the reach, and his short blade went up under Dace's arm into the lung.

Dace looked down at it with something like surprise. Then something like understanding.

"Told them," he wheezed, sliding down the rock face, "you'd... read it wrong..."

"I'm sorry," Wendell said, and meant it, and hated that meaning it changed nothing.

Movement at the edge of his vision. The young woman, coming hard for the exit where Sera should have already been through—but Sera hadn't gone through. Sera was there, stepping out of the shadow of the rock wall directly into the woman's line, arms spread, putting her body between the blade and the gap.

"*No*—" Wendell lunged.

The sword took Sera across the ribs—not deep, turned at the last instant by bone and by Sera's own twist away, but deep enough. Blood sheeted dark and sudden down her side. She went down hard against the stone, and the woman raised the blade again, and Wendell arrived like something falling.

He didn't remember deciding. Later, he would search for the decision and find only the result: the woman on the ground, his hands doing what his hands had been trained across twenty years to do, and stopping—one inch short, one heartbeat short—of the place where training became execution.

"Yield," he rasped. "Yield and live."

She yielded. Wide-eyed, bleeding from a broken nose, seventeen maybe eighteen years old, staring up at him the way recruits stared at legends. He stripped her weapons, bound her wrists with her own belt, and dragged her clear of the kill zone because he could not make himself do anything else.

Then the mountain shook.

Not earthquake-shake. Deeper. A single vast concussion from beneath the glacier, rolling up through the rock into their bones, followed by a sound like a cathedral of ice groaning awake. Down-slope, a seam split open in the glacier's flank—a cave mouth, blue-lit from within, exhaling air that smelled impossibly of warm ash.

Sera, white-faced and pressed against the rock, one hand clamped over her wound, was laughing. Actually laughing, wet and disbelieving.

"He's loud today," she gasped. "Ember. He's *loud*. That was him. That was him clearing the road."

Wendell got her arm over his shoulders. Through the gap behind them, Pietr was crawling toward help and Dace lay very still, and ahead of them the glacier had opened a door that hadn't existed an hour ago.

"Can you walk?"

"Ask me again when we stop walking."

They walked.

* * *

The cave ran farther than any natural fissure should. Blue ice walls thick as fortress stone, veined with threads of faint gold that pulsed slow as sleeping breath—Ember's light, leaking through the mountain's bones, Sera said, the cage sweating warmth. Refugees had found it before them: dozens of them, huddled in alcoves carved smooth by meltwater, cooking over tiny fires that gave no smoke. Families. Old men. A girl asleep with a wooden toy gripped in her fist.

They made space for Sera near the largest fire, and a grey-haired woman with a field surgeon's competent hands cleaned and stitched the wound while Wendell held his tongue and his guilt in equal measure.

"Shielding me," he said finally, when the stitching was done and Sera lay propped against a pack, grey-lipped but alive. "That wasn't the plan."

"The plan was stupid." She winced as she shifted. "Plans usually are. It worked, though. She'd have opened your back."

"She'd have opened your *ribs*."

"And I'd heal. You'd be dead, and the map dies with you, and Liora stays in the dark forever." She said it simply, without drama, the way she said most true things. "Don't waste my blood feeling sorry for yourself, Ishikawa. Spend it getting us to the door."

He looked at his hands. Dace's blood was still under the nails, in the creases, dried brown in the lines of his palms. Men he'd trained. Men who'd shared his fires, his rations, his jokes in the dark before dawn watches. He'd killed four of the company in his life before today—all enemies, all strangers in the end. Today he'd killed family.

And the worst of it, the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone, was that he understood them. Dace had believed serving Vaelric fed the free cities. Pietr had believed following orders kept his new wife safe. Good men, decent men, marching loyal into hell because someone they trusted told them it was heaven.

That was what Vaelric bought. Not soldiers. *Certainty.* And certainty, Wendell thought, watching the refugees huddle in the gold-veined dark, was the most expensive thing in the world, because nobody ever checked the price until it was paid.

"Wendell." Sera's hand found his wrist. "Wherever you just went—come back. We need you here."

"There was a boy," he said. "In the narrows. Eighteen years old. I tied her up and left her alive and I don't know if that was mercy or cowardice."

"Yes you do."

He did. That was the problem. Mercy, and it would cost him later, because she'd report everything—the cave, the refugees, the direction they'd gone. He'd traded tactical advantage for a clean conscience, and somewhere behind them, Dace's replacement was going to collect on that debt.

An old man shuffled over from the nearest alcove, drawn by the mention of the map. He had the look of someone who'd once been important—scholar's stoop, ink-stained fingers despite the road grime—and he lowered himself beside their fire with the care of a man whose joints had opinions.

"You're going to the court," he said. Not a question.

Wendell's hand drifted toward his knife out of pure habit. Sera caught his forearm.

"How do you know that word?" she asked.

"Everyone fleeing Vaelric knows it eventually. The lanterns." The old man's eyes were distant, fixed on the gold veins pulsing in the ice. "I was an archivist at Thornfell before the usurper came. I catalogued what the under-court paid for. Souls, yes—but not all of them. Some souls are *fuel*. Burned to keep something caged. And the thing that's caged..." He leaned closer, firelight catching the hollows of his face. "It isn't what Vaelric tells people it is. The wards, the protection, the great sacrifice—all of it built on a lie written in ledger-ink. I've seen the ledgers."

Something cold walked up Wendell's spine. "What lie?"

The old man glanced around the cave—at the families, the sleeping children, the tiny smokeless fires—then back, and his voice dropped to almost nothing.

"The god isn't silent because it abandoned us," he said. "It's silent because it's screaming. And everything Vaelric has built—the armies, the courts, the wards over the free cities—is powered by keeping its mouth shut."

Beside Wendell, Sera went very still. He felt her listening—not with her ears, but with whatever had heard hearts through forty feet of granite—and when she spoke, her voice was strange and quiet.

"That's what the whispers are," she breathed. "All this time. It's not guidance. It's leaks."

Above them, muffled by a hundred yards of ice and stone, the mountain groaned again—that long cathedral sound, deepening now, taking on something almost like rhythm. Almost like speech.

Almost like a name.

The old archivist's eyes went wide. "It knows you're here," he whispered. "Child, it's been calling your name since you entered the ice."
