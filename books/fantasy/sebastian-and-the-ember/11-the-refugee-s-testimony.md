## 11. The Refugee's Testimony

The cave smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and too many people breathing the same cold air.

Sera counted them by habit—thirty-one, maybe thirty-three if the shapes at the back were people and not piled gear. Refugees from the low valleys, mostly. Farmers with farm hands and farm eyes, the kind of folk who'd never been more than a day's walk from their own doorsteps until Vaelric's outriders burned those doorsteps down. They'd fled up into the high pass behind Wendell and her, following the same logic that had driven them all winter: *up is away, away is safe.*

Up was not safe. Up was just slower dying. But nobody said so out loud.

Wendell sat apart, near the cave mouth, cleaning his sword with the methodical patience of a man who needed his hands busy or they'd start shaking. He'd killed six of Maren's soldiers in the narrows below. Men he'd trained beside. He hadn't spoken since.

Sera left him to it. She had her own work.

An old woman sat near the fire pit at the cave's center, wrapped in three layers of patched wool, feeding sticks to the flames one at a time like each one cost her something. The others gave her space—not deference exactly, but the careful distance people give to someone who has seen something they don't want described over dinner. Sera had noticed it on the climb in. The refugees orbited the old woman the way iron filings orbit a lodestone, close but never touching.

She settled onto the stone beside her without asking. That was the trick of it. Ask permission to sit and you got a story shaped for company. Sit first, and you got the truth.

"Fire's good," Sera said.

"Mm." The old woman didn't look at her. "You're not one of us."

"No. I'm passing through."

"Nobody passes through the high pass in winter. You're running from something or toward something." She fed another stick to the flames. "Which is it?"

"Toward," Sera said. "Mostly."

The old woman turned then, and Sera got her first real look at her face. It was a face like a dried riverbed—all cracks and channels where water used to run—and her eyes were pale as skimmed milk, though Sera didn't think she was blind. Just somewhere else, most of the time. Somewhere far south and far down.

"You're the one who talks to herself," the old woman said. "On the trail. My granddaughter heard you."

Heat crawled up Sera's neck. "I pray."

"You argue." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Praying sounds different. Praying is one voice begging. What you do is two voices, and one of them isn't yours."

Sera went very still.

Down the cave, Wendell's whetstone paused mid-stroke. He'd heard. Of course he'd heard; the man missed nothing anymore, now that everything made him flinch.

"I don't know what you mean," Sera said.

"Girl." The old woman patted the air near Sera's knee, missing it by a hand's width. "My name was Odile Vess, before names stopped mattering. For eleven years I kept the lantern-vaults beneath Thornfell. I was a warden's clerk. I counted what came in and I counted what went out, and I will tell you what I saw, because I am old and my feet are done and the only weapon left to me is telling."

The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the cave, a child whimpered and was hushed.

"The court beneath the mountain," Odile said, "keeps souls as lanterns."

* * *

Sera had heard the stories. Everyone had. They were the kind of stories you told children to keep them from wandering—the usurper's halls lit by the dead, the throne room glowing with stolen light. Campfire rot. Superstition dressed up as politics.

But Odile Vess didn't tell it like a campfire story. She told it like an inventory.

"There are vaults," she said. "Long galleries cut into the living rock, shelved floor to ceiling, and every shelf holds lanterns. Not lamps. Lanterns of glass and silver, and inside each one—" her milk-pale eyes fixed on some middle distance "—a soul. You can see them. Little lights, each one a different color, because no two burn alike. Some gold, some blue-white, one I remember that burned green as pond ice, poor thing. They flicker when their owners would have laughed. They gutter when their owners would have wept. After a while you stop thinking of them as people. That's the first sin of the place. Not the taking. The *counting.*"

"How many?" Sera asked. Her voice came out flat, professional, smuggler-flat, because the alternative was shaking.

"When I left? Nine thousand and change. They add more every season. Vaelric buys them, same as any commodity. Debts forgiven for a father's signature. Pardons sold to condemned men who don't understand what pardon means. Whole villages assessed for tax and paid in persons when coin runs short." Her mouth twisted. "Your free cities think the mountain takes by force. It doesn't need to. It *purchases.* People sell each other gladly, given the right price and the right fear."

Sera thought of Wendell's map, sealed in wax, marked with his family sigil. Thought of the name it pointed to. Thought of how he'd gone white when she'd mentioned the seal, and how he still hadn't told her the whole of it.

"And the god," she said quietly. "The stories say Ember went silent ten years ago. Every altar in the land, cold at once."

Odile's stick-hand stilled over the fire.

"Silent," she repeated. "Yes. That's the word they use up here. Silent, like a man who's stopped talking. Like a hearth gone out." She shook her head slowly. "It didn't go silent. It was *taken.*"

The words landed in Sera's chest like a dropped stone hitting well-water.

"I processed the requisitions," Odile went on, softer now, the way people speak when they've carried something heavy for a long time and finally set it down. "Eleven years ago, before my time even started, there was a great working beneath the mountain. The old warden—Ishikawa, that was his name, the one who built the outer gate—he designed a cage. Not for a soul. Souls fit in lanterns. This was bigger. This was built to hold a *presence.* And when it was finished, Vaelric's people lured the god into it—or dragged it, I never learned which—and they've been draining its light ever since."

"Draining it how?"

"Into the wards, they say. The great wards that protect the free cities from whatever waits beyond the mountains. That's the official accounting, and maybe there's a ledger entry for it somewhere." Odile's lip curled. "But I'll tell you what I counted instead. The god-light comes off that cage in threads, fine as spider silk, and the threads run *up.* Not outward to the ward-stones. Up, to the upper courts, to Vaelric's own halls. He drinks it, girl. Whatever a god's light does for a man, he's been drinking it for ten years, and the cage leaks besides. It leaks through the rock, through the deep ways, through anything hollow enough to carry a whisper."

The fire cracked. Sera's hands had gone cold despite the heat.

Because she knew about whispers.

She'd heard them since she was nineteen, since the night her brother died and she'd prayed so hard she broke something open in herself, and something had answered. Fragments. Scraps. A voice like wind through a keyhole, saying things that almost made sense—*the door, the counting, hurry, the door*—never enough, never whole, always fading before she could grip it. Ten years of listening through static. Ten years of tending illegal ember-shrines because the voice felt like Ember, tasted like Ember, and everyone told her the god was gone, dead or departed, and she was either blessed or mad.

Ten years of believing she was hearing a god across a vast silence.

"It's not silence," she said aloud, and her own voice sounded strange to her. "It's a wall. I've been standing outside a wall my whole life, hearing what bleeds through the cracks."

Odile turned her milk-pale eyes on her fully for the first time.

"You hear it," she said. Not a question.

"Fragments. For ten years."

"Then you're a leak-listener." The old woman said it the way a soldier says a rank. "We had a name for it down below. The cage isn't perfect—nothing the Ishikawas built was ever perfect, that was the joke of it, the great engineer built a flawless lock and a leaking box. The whispers come through the flaws. Most people feel nothing. A few dream strange. But once in a generation, someone hears it clear." She reached out and this time found Sera's knee, gripped it with fingers like bird talons. "Do you know what that makes you?"

"Spare me the chosen-one talk. I'm a smuggler with a bad ear for silence."

"It makes you a *keyhole,*" Odile said. "And keyholes are how doors get opened."

* * *

Sera walked to the cave mouth afterward because she couldn't sit still, because her skin felt two sizes too small, because ten years of her life had just been picked up and turned over like a stone revealing what lived underneath.

Wendell stood at the entrance, sword sheathed again, arms crossed against the cold, watching the snowfield below catch the last grey light. He didn't look at her when she joined him. He'd been doing a lot of not-looking lately.

"You heard," she said.

"Some." His jaw worked. "Lanterns. A caged god. A clerk who counted nine thousand souls." A pause. "Ishikawa built the gate."

"Yes."

"My family name." He said it carefully, like handling a blade by the edge. "The warden's name. The man who built the cage for your god was an Ishikawa."

Sera looked at him—at the hollows under his eyes, the split knuckles, the way he held himself like a man braced for a blow that hadn't landed yet—and understood, suddenly, why the map had terrified him. Why he'd lied to his own company scouts. Why he wouldn't say the name written at the map's end.

"Wendell. What's at the end of that map?"

He was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence, keening off the glacier above.

"A door," he said finally. "Sealed with my bloodline. And a person." His voice roughened. "Someone my father sold. Someone I thought was dead."

He didn't say the name. But Sera had read the map by candlelight in a waystation while he slept, and she remembered the single word inked beneath the sigil, and she kept it behind her teeth because some knowledge a person has to give themselves.

Instead she said: "The god's alive, Wendell. Caged, but alive. And it's been calling for help for ten years, and I'm apparently the crack in the wall it calls through." She laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob. "All this time I thought I was praying to nothing. I thought I was—mad, or cursed, or making it up to feel less alone. And it was *leaking.* It's been leaking toward anyone who'd listen, and I listened, and I did nothing but run contraband and tend little illegal fires—"

"You tended shrines," Wendell said. "For ten years. When the faith was banned and the altars went cold and every priest in the land shut their mouths, you kept lighting candles to a silent god." He turned and looked at her straight on, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—something that wasn't grief. "That's not nothing. That's the whole reason it could reach you at all."

The words went through her like warmth through frozen hands. She looked away first, out at the snowfield, because looking at him felt suddenly dangerous.

Below, the refugees' fire threw its small orange defiance against the dark. Above, the pass climbed toward the spine of the mountains, and beyond the spine, according to the map worn soft in her pocket, the way bent down—down through rock older than kingdoms, down to a door sealed with Ishikawa blood, down to a cage that leaked.

"We leave at first light," she said. "Ahead of the hunt. Through the deep pass."

"They'll follow. Kessler doesn't stop."

"Then we'd better be faster than grief." She pushed off the stone. "Get some sleep, Wendell. Tomorrow we go answer a prayer."

Behind them, deep in the cave, Odile Vess began to sing—an old vault-hymn, low and cracked, a song for counting lights. And threaded through it, faint as breath on glass, Sera heard the whisper rise to meet it:

*hurry.*

And beneath the whisper, closer than it had ever been, something else—a second sound, wrong-footed, human, coming up the trail through the dark.

Hoofbeats. One rider. Alone.

Sera knew, before Wendell's hand went to his sword, exactly who rode alone into a refugee camp at night, and what it would cost them all when she arrived.
