## 12. Maren's Choice

She came alone.

Wendell knew it before he saw her—knew it the way you know a storm by the smell of the air. He was on second watch at the mouth of the cave, Sera asleep behind him with her wounded side bound in moss and linen, when the snow on the switchback below shifted in a rhythm too deliberate for wind. One set of footprints. Heavy boots, company-issue, the left heel worn on the outside edge the way Maren's always had been since he'd known her, because she walked like she was leaning into a fight that hadn't started yet.

He didn't draw his blade. That was the first strange thing. Ten years of mercenary instinct said *blade out, back to stone*, and his hands stayed loose at his sides.

"Maren," he said to the dark.

"You always did watch like a man expecting to be shot." Her voice came up the slope with the snowflakes, rough and familiar as an old scar. "Don't move. I'm coming up slow."

She emerged into the grey pre-dawn with her hood back and her sword still sheathed, which from Maren Kestrel was either a peace offering or an insult so refined it circled back around to courtesy. Snow clung to her shoulders and the iron-grey braid she'd worn the same way for twenty years. Up close there were new lines around her eyes, and something else—a hollowness under them, like a woman who'd been marching on a decision she hadn't finished making.

"Where's your escort?" Wendell asked.

"Dead end of the pass, nursing their pride and a broken axle. I told them I'd scout ahead." She stopped six feet away, close enough to talk low, far enough that neither of them had to pretend this was friendly. "They'll believe that for about four hours."

"So we have four hours."

"We have whatever I say we have." She glanced past him into the cave's darkness, where Sera slept. "The priestess. How bad?"

"Ribs. A gash. She'll walk."

"She'd better." Maren crouched, forearms on her knees, and looked out over the valley below—the refugee trail winding down through the pines, thin threads of woodsmoke rising where the desperate had dared small fires. "You've seen them, then. The ones coming up from the lowlands."

"I've been walking among them for two days."

"Vaelric's tax collectors burned three villages on the south road last week." She said it flatly, but her jaw worked around the words like gristle. "Not rebels. Villages. Farmers. The company was there, Wendell. We stood in the square and watched, because our contract says we hold the pass roads and nothing else, and I signed my name to *nothing else*."

"That's why you're here."

"That's why I'm here." She rose. "That, and because you lied to me at Cinderhold."

The word landed harder than any blow could have. Wendell held her gaze and didn't flinch, though every muscle in him wanted to. "I did."

"In nineteen years, you never once lied to me. Not even when lying would have saved your hide. You were the one soldier in my whole company I never had to check." Her voice didn't rise; that was worse. It dropped instead, quiet as a closing door. "So either you're not the man I raised, or something happened that scared you more than I do. And I've decided I need to know which."

"Maren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't give me the smooth version. Don't tell me it's company business or free-cities business or any other kind of business. I led sixty men and women across the Split Rock narrows three days ago looking for two fugitives, and I found four of ours dead and Dain Corvane standing over them with that smile he thinks I can't see through. Dain is telling Vaelric's liaison that you murdered our own. Dain is telling *me* you murdered our own. And I have spent three days not deciding whether to believe him, because believing him means the boy I pulled out of a gutter in Saltmarsh grew up into a traitor, and I would rather cut off my own hand than—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was level, and that cost her more than shouting would have. "Show me it isn't true. Give me something. Anything."

"The truth," Wendell said. "All of it. But not standing in the snow."

* * *

Inside the cave, Sera was awake. Of course she was—she'd probably been awake since his boots shifted on the scree. She sat propped against the wall with her knife in reach and her breathing carefully even, and when Maren filled the entrance she took her measure in one long look, the way smugglers measured everything: threat, value, exit.

"So you're the captain," Sera said.

"And you're the woman who got my best soldier killed." Maren said it without heat, testing.

"He's standing right here, alive enough."

"For now." Maren lowered herself onto a rock near the small fire, close enough for its warmth, angled so both of them stayed in her sightline. Old habits. Wendell remembered teaching recruits to sit that way and realized, with a small cold shock, that she'd taught him. "Talk. Start wherever you want. I'm listening."

So he started at the beginning—the courier bleeding out on the smugglers' road, the wax-sealed map, the name Ishikawa pressed into his hands with a dying man's last strength. He told her about the waystation and the sigil, about Cinderhold burning behind them, about the ledger in his father's ruined house. And when he reached the ledger he stopped talking and simply took it from his pack and put it in her hands.

Maren read the way she did everything: fast, thorough, missing nothing. He watched her face while she did it. He'd spent years learning to read officers' faces across negotiation tables, and what moved across hers now was not disbelief. That was the terrible part. Disbelief he could have fought. What moved across her face was recognition—the slow, sick settling of a woman watching scattered pieces assemble into a shape she'd half-seen for years without letting herself look at directly.

"Payments to the under-court," she said finally. "Quarterly. For eleven years." Her finger rested on a line near the middle of the page. "This date. The eleventh of Deepmonth."

"What about it?"

"That's the week Vaelric's wards went up over Thornfell. The week the mages' guild announced the great working that would shield the free cities forever." She looked up. "Everyone celebrated. There were bells."

"There were no wards," Sera said quietly from the wall. "There's a cage under the mountain, Captain. Your guild masters built a prison and called it a shield, and the thing they locked in it has been screaming through the cracks ever since. I've heard it my whole life. So has every priest they burned for hearing it."

Maren's eyes went to Wendell. "And you believe her."

"I've heard it too. Since I was a child. Ember used to speak to me at the old altars, and ten years ago it stopped, and everyone told me the god had gone silent, and I believed them because the alternative was unthinkable." He crouched by the fire, turning his hands toward the heat. "It didn't go silent, Maren. It was taken. And my father helped build the lock, and paid for it with souls—one of them Liora's. One of them meant to be mine."

The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, water dripped with the patience of stone.

Maren closed the ledger. Opened it again. Closed it, as if the thing might behave differently on a second inspection. "Your father," she said at last, "was the most careful man I ever met. He counted his cups after dinner. He kept accounts of everything—grain, nails, candle stubs." Her voice had gone somewhere distant. "I remember thinking, when your sister vanished, how strange it was that a man who recorded every egg his hens laid never wrote down a single thing about where his daughter went."

"You knew him?"

"I negotiated the company's winter contracts with him for six years. He fed us when the free cities wouldn't, and I never asked why a grain merchant in a dying valley could afford that generosity." She laughed, short and bitter, without any humor in it. "Because I didn't want to know. Same as now. Same as every contract we've signed since Vaelric came to power—generous terms, easy terms, terms that made me think someone upstairs liked us. And I didn't ask, because the company eats, and fed soldiers don't ask questions."

"Maren—"

"I'm not finished." She set the ledger down between them, deliberately, like laying a blade on a table. "Dain says you're a traitor. Vaelric's liaison says you're a traitor. Half my officers are ready to hang you from the nearest tree and the other half are ready to follow you, and I have spent three days trying to find the truth before the company tears itself apart over your corpse." She leaned forward, firelight carving deep the lines of her face. "Here's what I see. I see a soul-trade dressed up as civic defense. I see a guild that lies and a lord who burns villages and a company being used as a whip. And I see one man who walked away from all of it—not for gold, not for glory, but because a dying stranger said his family's name." Her jaw tightened. "Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me there's some version of this where you're the villain, because the villain is easier. Villains you can fight."

"I can't tell you that," Wendell said. "I wish I could."

Sera pushed herself upright against the wall, wincing, and her voice came out low and certain. "Captain Kestrel. You swore an oath to your company. I understand oaths—I used to wear vestments before wearing them became a hanging offense. But ask yourself what your oath serves. A company is people. Is Dain Corvane people? Is a contract signed with a man who lights lanterns with children's souls?" She let that sit. "You said you watched them burn a village and did nothing. How many more times can you stand in a square and watch before there's nothing left in you that remembers why you took the oath at all?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then at Wendell. And Wendell saw it happen—the moment the decision landed, not with triumph but with grief, the way all real decisions land. Her shoulders came down half an inch. Twenty years of command weight shifting onto a new axis.

"I can't turn the company," she said. "Not yet. Not on a ledger and a priestess's word. They'd call it mutiny, and Dain would be right there with rope and a speech." She stood, and when she spoke again each word came out placed and deliberate, like stones laid across a river. "But I'm the one who writes the hunt reports. And the hunt report I write today will say the trail went cold above the Split Rock, that the fugitives likely died in the icefall, and that further pursuit is a waste of company strength needed on the pass roads. That buys you days. Maybe a week."

"A week," Wendell repeated.

"Dain won't stop. He smells promotion in this, and Vaelric wants you badly enough that failure will cost Dain more than he can afford to lose, which makes him dangerous and stupid, the worst combination there is." She pulled her hood up. "When he comes—and he will come, with forty men and no patience—you need to be through the high pass and at whatever door you're looking for. Because I cannot hold the hunt forever, Wendell. I can bend it. I can slow it. I can lose maps and misfile orders until someone notices. But the day Vaelric decides I'm more useful replaced than obeyed, I'm done, and everything I've bent snaps straight."

"Maren." He rose too. There were a hundred things to say and none of them were big enough. "Nineteen years. You pulled me out of the gutter and you never once made me feel like gutter filth. Whatever happens under that mountain—"

"Stop." Her voice cracked, just barely, just once. "If you thank me properly I'll start believing this is goodbye, and I refuse. This is a delay. You come back. You come back with your sister and your god and whatever impossible thing you're going to do down there, and you stand in front of the company and tell them yourself, because they'll follow you if you're alive to ask, and I am too old to lead a rebellion and too stubborn to die in one." She jabbed a finger at his chest. "Alive, Ishikawa. That's an order."

"Yes, Captain."

Something eased in her face—the ghost of the woman who'd shared watch-fires with him for two decades. She turned to go, then paused at the cave mouth, snow swirling in around her boots.

"Wendell." She didn't turn around. "The eleventh of Deepmonth. The bells." A breath. "I was in Thornfell that day. I stood in the cathedral square with the whole city, and when the wards lit up I felt something go out of the world, and I told myself it was just the cold." Her hand tightened on the doorstone. "I felt it leave. All those people cheering, and I stood there grieving something and couldn't say what. Ten years I've carried that and called it sentiment."

She stepped out into the falling snow.

"Bring it back," Maren said, and was gone down the switchback, one set of footprints filling slowly behind her, until the snow took even those.

* * *

They broke camp within the hour.

Sera moved stiffly, ribs bound tight, packing with the economy of someone who'd fled things before. Neither of them spoke much. There wasn't much to say that the mountain silence wasn't already saying better. But at the treeline, shouldering her pack, Sera paused and looked back the way Maren had gone.

"She'll pay for this," Sera said. Not a question.

"When Dain figures it out. Yes."

"Then we'd better make what she bought worth the price." Sera settled the straps and nodded up-slope, toward the white shoulder of the high pass, where the wind sang through the rocks in a register almost—but not quite—like a voice. "The whispers are stronger up there. Stronger than they've ever been." She met his eyes, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before: fear, yes, but underneath it, urgent and bright, hope. "Wendell. Last night it said your name again. Twice."

Above them, far up in the pass, the wind rose—and for one impossible moment it sounded less like wind than like someone calling, someone who had been calling for ten years, someone who was running out of time.

They climbed.
