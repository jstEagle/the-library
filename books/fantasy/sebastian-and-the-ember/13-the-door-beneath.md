## 13. The Door Beneath

The map's last leg took them down.

Not along the mountain—*into* it, through a seam in the rock that Sera found by pressing her palm flat against the stone and waiting, the way she did when she was listening for something the rest of the world couldn't hear. Three days past the high pass, past the frozen waterfall and the shrine of stacked bones where the refugees had turned west toward Cinderhold's ruins, the smugglers' road simply ended at a wall of black granite, and Sera said, "Here," as if that settled everything.

"Here is a cliff face," Wendell said.

"Here is a door." She traced a line in the frost with one finger, and for a moment he saw it too—a vertical seam in the rock, thin as a hair, running from their feet to somewhere above the reach of torchlight. "Feel it."

He didn't want to feel it. He'd spent three days feeling things: the ache in his shoulder where an arrowhead had grazed him at Split Rock, the cold that had worked into his bones and seemed to have opinions about when it left, the weight of his father's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart. But he pressed his hand to the stone because Sera was watching, and because some part of him—the part that had been listening to her whispers for weeks now without admitting it—already knew what he would find.

Warmth. Faint, deep, like a hearth on the far side of a thick wall.

"The mountain's warm here," he said, and hated how much his voice wanted to shake.

"No," Sera said quietly. "It isn't."

* * *

They made camp in the mouth of a side fissure, out of the wind that came shrieking down off the glacier, and Wendell read the map again by candlelight while Sera slept—or lay still with her eyes closed, which amounted to the same thing most nights. The final leg was drawn differently from all the others. Every route before it had been rendered in the careful cartographer's hand of whoever had copied the thing, but the last stretch was different ink, different pressure, a line that trembled slightly at its end where it stopped at a symbol no mapmaker would use.

A keyhole. Drawn freehand. And beneath it, in the same unsteady script:

*Only blood remembers.*

Wendell had turned that phrase over so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones, and they still cut him every time. Only blood remembers. His father had drawn this—not the courier, not some anonymous copyist, but Kenji Ishikawa, who had sat at this same fire thirty years ago or forty and pressed this wax seal with hands that must have been shaking too. His father had built something under Thornfell. Had locked it. Had left the only key written into the bodies of his children.

And then, when Liora vanished, he had said nothing. Had let Wendell grow up believing she'd run away, or died, or been taken by bandits—anything but the truth that she was behind a door her own blood could open, a day's walk from the house where they'd eaten dinner every night of their childhood.

He fed another stick to the fire and watched the flames do what flames did. Somewhere behind the black granite, something warm was breathing.

"You're not sleeping either." Sera's voice, soft in the dark.

"I'm reading."

"You've read it eleven times. It hasn't changed." She crawled over beside him, close enough that he could see the ember-glint in her eyes, the one that never quite went away anymore—the light that leaked out of her since the pass, since Ember had spoken through her clear as a bell. "Show me the sigil again."

He tilted the map toward her. In the corner, small as a thumbnail, was the mark he'd grown up seeing branded on crates and stamped on letters and carved into the lintel of the family house: two crossed lines over a circle, the old Ishikawa forge-sign, older than the name itself. His father had told them once, when Liora was seven and full of questions, that it meant *the flame kept*. That their ancestors had been hearth-keepers, back when the hearth-faith was legal, back before Vaelric's grandfather had burned the shrines and scattered the priests.

Liora had asked what flame we keep. Their father had gone quiet in a way that ended conversations, and Wendell had carried the question for twenty years without knowing he was carrying it.

"It's your family's mark," Sera said. Not a question.

"It's my family's cage." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "My father built the outer gate. That's what the ledger says. Payments, materials, dates going back decades—and then the dates stop the year Liora disappeared. He finished building it, and then he used it." He looked at the fire instead of at her. "I keep trying to make those two facts live in the same man. I can't do it."

"Maybe you're not supposed to." Sera pulled her knees up. "People aren't one fact, Wendell. My mother sold me to a smuggling crew when I was nine, and she cried while she did it, and she still did it, and both of those are true. I hated her for years for making me choose which one to believe. Then I stopped choosing. You can hold the whole man or none of him."

"And which did you pick?"

"Neither. Some nights I pick one. Most nights I just carry it." She glanced at him sideways. "You've been carrying yours alone since Cinderhold. That's why you keep almost-hearing things and panicking. You think if the god speaks to you, it'll be a verdict."

He didn't answer, because she was right, and because the wind chose that moment to moan through the fissure like something grieving, and because he was tired—bone-deep, soul-deep tired—in a way that sleep didn't touch anymore.

But in the dark, in the space between one gust and the next, he listened. Just for a second. Just to prove to himself he could.

Nothing came. Or nothing came *yet*, which was somehow worse.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn and stood before the seam as the sky behind them went the color of old ash.

Sera had prepared rites. He watched her chalk the signs of the hearth-faith onto the stone—circles within circles, the geometry of welcome—and chant under her breath in the old cadences, and the warmth behind the rock pulsed once, faintly, like a sleeper stirring. Frost retreated from the seam in a handspan-wide corridor. But the stone did not open.

"I can knock," she said, stepping back. "It won't be me it answers."

Wendell set down his pack. Drew his knife. For a long moment he stood there looking at his own hand—at the calluses built by thirty years of mercenary work, the scar across the knuckles from a fight in Cinderhold's alleyways when he was nineteen, the faint burn-mark on the wrist he'd gotten at six years old, reaching for a kettle on his father's forge. Every mark a life writes on a body. He'd never thought of any of them as words.

*Only blood remembers.*

He opened his palm with the knife and let his blood fall against the seam.

For three heartbeats, nothing. The blood sat on the stone like blood sits anywhere, already cooling, already beginning to look like a mistake. Wendell felt the absurd urge to wipe it off, to pretend he hadn't done this, to turn around and walk back down the mountain and find Maren and take whatever amnesty she was offering—

Then the blood sank in. All of it, at once, like rain into parched earth. And the seam lit.

Light ran upward along the hairline crack, gold-white, fast as a spark along a fuse, and the sound that followed wasn't grinding stone but something closer to a breath—a vast, slow exhalation from deep inside the mountain, air that hadn't moved in decades pushing outward past them, carrying a smell that hit Wendell like a fist to the sternum.

Woodsmoke. Forge-smoke. His childhood kitchen on a winter morning, bread baking, iron heating, his father's hands shaping something glowing on the anvil while Liora watched with her chin on her folded arms and Wendell pumped the bellows because his arms weren't big enough yet.

He staggered. Sera caught his elbow. "What is it? What do you smell?"

"Bread," he said stupidly. "I smell bread. There's no bread under a mountain."

"There might be," Sera said softly, "if someone in there remembers it too."

The seam widened. Stone drew apart from stone with a groan that rolled away down the valley behind them, loud enough that Wendell's whole body clenched—they'd been hunted across half the range; noise meant death—but nothing came. No riders, no horns. Maren had bought them a night and a day, maybe more, and they had spent them walking. The pass was empty. For now.

The door stood open. Not a gate, not a slab—a passage, tall enough for five men abreast, descending into the mountain in a gradual ramp lit from below by a light that had no visible source, gold and steady and warm, pooling on the ramp's first steps like water finding its level.

Wendell stood at the threshold and understood, with a clarity that felt almost cruel, why his father had designed the lock this way. Not out of cruelty. Out of the opposite. Anyone could be forced to give up a password. Anyone could steal a key. But you couldn't torture a man into being someone else's child. Blood remembered whether you wanted it to or not. His father—whoever else he had been, whatever else he had sold—had built a door that only opened for the people it was protecting. Or hiding. Or imprisoning. The verbs kept sliding.

"You don't have to go first," Sera said.

"Yes I do." He picked up his pack. "It's my name on the door."

* * *

The ramp descended farther than any ramp should. An hour in, Wendell lost count of the switchbacks. Two hours in, the air changed—warmer, thicker, tasting faintly of metal and something sweeter underneath, something organic, like the inside of a living thing. The walls smoothed as they went, rough granite giving way to dressed stone, dressed stone to surfaces that gleamed faintly in the sourceless light, veined with threads of gold that pulsed in slow rhythm.

"They're veins," Sera whispered, touching one. Her fingers came away glistening. When she held them to the light, the wetness on them glowed and faded, glowed and faded. "Wendell. These aren't veins of ore."

He looked, and looked away. He knew what leaked light and faded and leaked again. He'd seen it leaking out of Sera since the pass. "How far down does this go?"

"Farther than smugglers dug. Farther than miners dug." She wiped her hand on her coat, and her voice had the careful steadiness she used when she was frightened and refusing to say so. "This is old. This is *first*-age old. Your family didn't build this, Wendell. Your family built the *door*."

That landed strangely, and he turned it over as they walked. Hearth-keepers, his father had said. The flame kept. Maybe the truth of the family business went back further than ledgers and payments—maybe Kenji Ishikawa hadn't invented anything, just inherited it, taken up a post as old as the mountain, the way a son takes up a hammer or a sword. Warden. The word surfaced from nowhere and stuck.

Somewhere below them, faint as memory, he heard—for the third time in ten years, and the first time not through Sera—a sound like a voice trying very hard to be patient.

He stopped walking. Sera stopped a step later, watching him.

"What," she said. "What did you hear?"

"I don't—" His heart was slamming. The panic rose the way it always rose, hot and animal, the instinct to shut the listening part of himself like a door against weather. Ten years of silence had taught him that hope was the expensive emotion, the one you paid for in grief. But this time he made himself stand in it. Made himself listen the way Sera had taught him in the tunnels, head tilted, breath slow, attention turned inward and downward at once.

There. Beneath the pulse of the golden veins, beneath the mountain's vast slow breathing: a rhythm. Not random. Not geological. A rhythm like someone knocking. Like someone who had been knocking, patiently, at intervals, for a very long time, and who had just heard footsteps on the stairs.

"Wendell." Sera's eyes were wide, her hand pressed flat over her sternum where the whispers lived. "It knows you're here. It's known since the blood touched the stone. It's saying—" She closed her eyes, and her lips moved with words that weren't hers. When she opened them, they were wet. "It's saying *the heir comes down the stair*. It says it's waited. It says—" She stopped. Swallowed.

"Says what?"

"It says your sister taught it your name."

The words went through him like cold water. Liora. Down there, in the dark, for however many years—lucid, alive, and talking to a caged god about her brother. Teaching it his name so that when he finally came, it would know him.

His legs nearly gave. He put a hand on the wall to steady himself and the golden vein pulsed under his palm, warm as skin, and he snatched his hand back as though scalded.

"We keep moving," he said, and his voice came out rough. "We keep moving until we're standing in front of whatever's down there, and then we decide what kind of people we are. Agreed?"

Sera nodded. But as they started down again, she said quietly, "One more thing. While you were listening—it wasn't only knocking. There was something else under it. Farther down. Slower." She hesitated. "It sounded like breathing. Something very large, asleep. And the knocking isn't coming from the cage, Wendell. I think the knocking is coming from whatever's between us and it."

They descended in silence after that, boots ringing on ancient stone, following the golden veins deeper, while behind and above them the mountain door hung open against the grey morning like a wound that had finally been allowed to speak.

And far below, patient as winter, the knocking continued.
