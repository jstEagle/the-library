## 14. Lantern Court

The door closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting.

Sera had expected stone grinding on stone, dust, the groan of something ancient waking. Instead there was only that soft, final click, as if the mountain had swallowed them and found them palatable.

The passage beyond was not dark.

That was the first wrongness, and it took her a moment to name it. She'd spent her life in the dark places of the world—smugglers' tunnels, cellar shrines, the undercrofts where the ember-faith hid its altars when the hearth-laws turned against it—and she knew every species of darkness the way a fisherman knows weather. But this light was wrong in its texture. It came from everywhere and nowhere. It had no direction. It fell on her hands without casting shadows from them.

It came from the walls.

No—from things *in* the walls, she realized as they descended the first stair. Small alcoves, spaced at intervals like votive niches, each holding a lantern. And each lantern held a flame that was not fire.

She stopped walking. Wendell took two more steps before he noticed, then turned back to her, hand already at his sword.

"Sera."

"Look at them," she said. Her voice came out thin. "Wendell. *Look* at them."

He looked. He'd been looking since they entered; she could see it in the set of his jaw, the way he refused to let his gaze rest too long on any single niche. A soldier's discipline: don't stare at the thing that will unmake you. But Sera was not a soldier. She was a priestess of a god whose whole theology was built on one idea—that a soul is a small warm light, and that it deserves to burn where people can see it.

And the lights in these niches were souls. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat.

Each flame had a shape, if you let your eyes soften. Not human shapes exactly—more like the way a person looks when you remember them rather than see them. One flickered in quick, nervous pulses, like someone perpetually about to speak. Another burned low and steady and terribly patient. A third guttered in rhythms that were almost, almost like weeping.

"They're screaming," Sera said.

"You can't know that."

"I can." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, where the whispers lived—the fragments she'd carried for years, the leaks from whatever cage held Ember. Now, down here, those whispers weren't whispers anymore. They were a chorus. Every step deeper multiplied them. "I've heard pieces of this my whole life. Little scraps of voice in the ash. I thought it was Ember, broken up. It's not just Ember. It's everyone. The god's voice and the voices of—" she gestured at the niches, at the hundreds of them receding down the stairwell into impossible depth, "—of all of them. Mixed together. That's what I've been hearing. That's what the shrines catch. Screams, filtered through a god's throat."

Wendell stood very still. In the shadowless light his face looked younger and older at once, the scars standing out pale.

"Liora," he said. Just the name. Just checking that it still existed in the world.

"Not yet," Sera said. "But close. I can feel—" She stopped herself before she said *her*, because she couldn't actually feel Liora specifically, and she would not give him false comfort down here, where false comfort might be a currency someone was collecting. "We keep moving. Eyes forward. Don't touch the lanterns."

"I wasn't going to touch the lanterns."

"You were going to touch the lanterns," she said gently. "So was I. That's why I'm saying it out loud."

They went down.

* * *

The stairs ended at a landing that opened into a hall so long its far end dissolved into haze, and the haze itself glowed. Sera counted forty niches across the ceiling alone before she gave up counting. The architecture was human in proportion but inhuman in intention—corridors branched at angles that felt correct until you tried to hold their geometry in your head, and then it slipped away like water. Everything curved slightly upward, or seemed to, so that walking felt like perpetually beginning an ascent you never completed.

Her grandmother had told stories about the under-court. Smuggler's tales, fireside stuff, the kind of story you told to scare children away from certain mine entrances. *The court beneath Thornfell, where the old kings paid their tithes in blood and the new king pays in something worse.* Sera had always assumed the stories were embroidered. Every smuggler embroidered. Half her livelihood depended on embroidery.

Nothing in the stories had prepared her for how *polite* it all was.

That was the second wrongness. The halls were clean. The air was cool and faintly sweet, like a chapel after incense. There was no rot, no dungeon stink, none of the decay you'd expect from a place that imprisoned gods and trafficked in souls. Everything was maintained. Everything was tended.

It was a place where someone took pride in their work.

"Oh no," she murmured.

"What?"

"It's nice down here, Wendell. Look at it. Someone dusts these halls. Someone trims the wicks—" she caught herself, horrified at her own word choice, "—someone keeps this place running, and they think of it as *keeping*. As stewardship. This isn't a dungeon. It's an *estate*."

Ahead of them, where the hall bent toward a colonnade, something moved.

Sera's hand found the knife at her hip out of pure habit, though she suspected a knife would be about as useful down here as a polite letter. The figures resolved out of the glow slowly, the way figures do in dreams—not approaching so much as becoming more present where they had perhaps always been.

There were three of them. Courtiers, if she had to call them something. They wore the shapes of people the way the flames wore shapes of memory: approximately, affectionately, wrongly. The nearest was tall and wore court robes of deep grey that flowed like smoke settling, and where its face should have been there was a suggestion of a face—features you could name if you insisted, eyes, mouth, the line of a nose—but the suggestion kept drifting, as if the face were being remembered by someone who hadn't seen the man in decades.

Half-faded, she thought. Half-here. People who had given so much of themselves to this place that the place now wore them like livery.

Wendell stepped forward—of course he did, he put himself between her and them without seeming to decide to—and Sera saw his hand hovering near his blade and prayed silently, uselessly, that he wouldn't draw. You did not draw steel in a church unless you meant to blaspheme, and whatever this place was, it was a church.

The tall courtier inclined its drifting head.

"Heir Ishikawa," it said.

Its voice was the worst thing yet. It was a beautiful voice—measured, courteous, pitched like a chamberlain announcing dinner—and it came out slightly wrong, layered, as though two speakers shared one throat and had agreed long ago not to compete.

"The court has expected you," it continued. "Long has the wardenship stood empty. Long have we kept the lights against your return." It gestured with a sleeve that contained more arm than arm. "Your father's seat awaits. Your father's duties await. Come. The Regent Vaelric receives you at his pleasure."

"We're not—" Sera started, and Wendell's hand brushed hers: *wait.*

She watched him work. Watched the mercenary surface over the grieving brother, the professional mask sliding down over ten years of silence and three weeks of hell. When he spoke, his voice was level and cold and utterly convincing.

"My father is dead," Wendell said. "I've come to claim what's mine."

*Don't tell them anything true,* Sera wanted to say. *Every truth down here is a coin in someone's purse.* But it was done, and the courtier bowed—a motion like a tree bending in wind that wasn't blowing—and turned, and led them into the colonnade, and the other two courtiers fell in behind them like honor guards.

Like escorts. Like servants welcoming home the young master.

Sera walked beside Wendell through the glowing halls of the Lantern Court, and felt each soul they passed scream silently against the inside of her skull, and understood with sickening clarity that to everything around them, they were not intruders.

They were family.

* * *

They walked for what felt like hours. Time behaved strangely; her legs ached as though they'd climbed a mountain, but the halls never tilted downward again, never rose, just flowed onward through that gentle false ascent. The courtiers made small talk.

That was the third wrongness, and somehow it was the one that got under her skin deepest.

"The harvest was modest this season," the lead courtier remarked, in the tone another man might discuss rainfall. "The Regent is displeased, but the mountains give what the mountains give."

"There was trouble at the passes," said the second, a squat shape whose face was mostly forehead and suggestion. "Free cities' refugees, wandering where they shouldn't. Some were collected. Useful ones."

"Collected," Sera repeated.

"For the walls," the courtier said pleasantly, indicating a niche they passed. Inside, a flame the size of a thumb pulsed fast and frantic, and Sera had to bite the inside of her cheek hard enough to taste copper to keep from reaching in, from doing *something*, from tearing the little prison open with her bare hands even though she had no idea what that would release or what it would cost. "The border wards require renewal. It is the purpose of the court. It has always been the purpose of the court. The free cities sleep safe because we are diligent."

She glanced at Wendell. His face was carved wood. But she'd marched beside him for three weeks, fought beside him, bled beside him in a narrows full of men he'd trained with, and she knew the difference between his calm and his calm. This was the second kind. The kind with a body count waiting behind it.

"You said the wardenship," Wendell said. "My father held a post here."

"Held? No." The courtier sounded genuinely puzzled, and its puzzlement was somehow obscene, a dead thing performing confusion. "He *holds* it. Wardens do not resign, heir Ishikawa. The gate requires a keeper of the keeper's line. Your father built the outer door. Your father sealed the cage. Your father's blood is the hinge of everything." Its drifting face angled toward Wendell with something like fondness. "You have his hands."

"I have my own hands."

"Yes," the courtier agreed warmly. "That is what makes you suitable."

Sera's skin crawled so badly she nearly missed the change in the corridor ahead—the light thickening, the niches giving way to taller alcoves, and in the tallest of them, set apart on a dais like an artifact in a museum, a lantern larger than any other they had seen.

It was the size of a brazier. And it didn't flicker.

Every other flame in the court moved—pulsed, guttered, wept, screamed in silent light. This one burned perfectly, horribly still, a single unwavering point of gold-white brilliance, and the stillness of it was louder than all the screaming. Sera stopped without meaning to. Her ears popped. The chorus in her chest, the endless leak of voices she had carried her whole life, went suddenly, utterly quiet—as if every voice in the cage had turned to look at her.

Then, from the still flame, one voice separated out. Not a whisper. Not a leak. A word, spoken directly into the marrow of her bones:

*HURRY.*

She grabbed Wendell's sleeve. "That one. Wendell. *That's*—"

"I know." His voice had gone rough. He was staring at the great still flame with an expression Sera had only ever seen on mourners. "I know what that is."

"That's Ember," she breathed. "That's the whole of it. Caged and *displayed*—they put it on a dais like a centerpiece—"

"The Heartlight," the lead courtier said, catching up to them with courteous patience, mistaking her horror for admiration. "The court's pride. It burns without consuming. The Regent calls it the most efficient soul ever harvested." The drifting face produced something that was almost a smile. "You may look, heir Ishikawa. Your family has always been permitted to look."

Wendell's jaw worked. For a moment Sera thought he would say something unforgivable, something that would start a war in that hallway, and part of her—ugly, exhausted, furious—wanted him to.

Instead he said, quietly: "Take me to Vaelric."

* * *

The throne room doors were black iron, twice a man's height, worked with a sigil Sera recognized from the map's wax seal and from the ruined lintel of a burned house in the foothills: the Ishikawa mark, a flame within a circle within a flame.

Two more courtiers flanked it. As the party approached, they drew the doors open without a word, and warmth rolled out—actual warmth, hearth-warmth, the first honest heat Sera had felt since descending—and beyond, at the end of a hall lit by a thousand captive stars, a figure rose from a chair that was not quite a throne.

Vaelric was elegant. Of course he was. Tall, silver-haired, dressed in charcoal and white with a single ember-red sash, he moved like a man who had rehearsed grace for centuries and finally gotten it right. He spread his hands in welcome, and his smile reached his eyes, and his eyes were wrong—too still, too old, lit from within by a gold-white light Sera recognized instantly, horribly, as the same light that burned on the Heartlight's dais.

"Wardenslayer," he said—or *heir*, the words braided together, both meanings offered at once. "At last. We have so much to discuss."

Beside Sera, Wendell drew breath.

Behind them, softly, the iron doors began to swing shut.
