## 15. The Usurper's Offer

The courtiers led them down a corridor that should not have existed.

Wendell counted his steps the way he'd once counted paces before an ambush—two hundred and forty, then two hundred and fifty, then past any number the mountain's exterior could have allowed. The walls were black stone shot through with veins of pale light, and the light moved. Slowly. Like something breathing under skin.

Sera walked close enough that her shoulder brushed his arm. She hadn't spoken since the lantern hall, since she'd pressed her palm against her mouth to keep from screaming at whatever the captive souls had done to her senses. Her hand found his now—not tenderly, but the way a drowning woman finds driftwood.

"They think you're the heir," she breathed.

"I know."

"Do they know what that means? What your father *built* here?"

Ahead of them, the half-faded things in their court dress turned in unison, as if they'd heard her through the stone. One of them smiled with a face like smoke over water and gestured them onward.

"No," Wendell said. "But I'm about to find out who does."

* * *

The throne room was not what he expected.

He had imagined Vaelric's seat as something monstrous—a pit, a charnel house, a temple built from stolen bones. Instead they entered a long gallery lit by ordinary firelight, hung with maps and tapestries, furnished like the study of a scholar-king. Bookshelves lined one wall. A table held decanters of wine and two glasses already poured.

Vaelric stood at the far end, examining a chart of the northern passes, and when he turned, Wendell felt the floor tilt beneath him.

Because Vaelric looked *reasonable*. That was the horror of it. He was tall and gray-templed and dressed plainly, in the manner of a man who had better things to do than vanity. His face was kind around the eyes. He looked like someone's uncle. He looked like the officer who'd taught Wendell to read terrain, like the quartermaster who'd slipped extra rations to orphans in bad winters. He looked like every decent man Wendell had ever followed into a fight.

"Master Ishikawa." His voice was warm gravel. "You look like your father. Sit, please. Both of you. You've walked a long way through my mountain, and I imagine no one has offered you anything to eat since Cinderhold."

"We're not hungry," Sera said.

"You are. Hunger makes men stupid, and I need you sharp." Vaelric poured wine himself, unhurried, and set the glasses within their reach without insisting. "I'll come to the point, because I suspect you're the sort of man who respects that. Ten years ago, your god went silent. Everyone in the free cities has grieved that silence—the altars gone cold, the hearth-fires needing tending by hand, the prayers falling into nothing. You personally have grieved it more than most. Am I wrong?"

Wendell said nothing.

"Your sister told me about you, actually." Vaelric smiled faintly. "Liora speaks of her brother often. She's been waiting for you a long time."

Something in Wendell's chest lurched toward the sound of her name, and he hated how easily it moved him, how transparent he must be to this man. "Then let me see her."

"In time. First, the truth—which you've crossed a mountain to hear, so hear all of it." Vaelric came around the table and stood where they could both see him, hands loose at his sides, a man making a case before a jury. "Ember did not go silent. Ember was *loud*. Do you know what it was saying, ten years ago? Do you know what the god of hearth and flame woke up screaming about?"

"The thing beyond the mountains," Wendell said slowly. "The old stories."

"Old stories, yes. Stories your grandmother's grandmother dismissed because no one alive had ever seen it. But gods see farther than we do. Ember saw it waking. And Ember's answer was to pour itself out—to burn across the whole sky of the world, to spend everything it had, screaming a warning no mortal ear could survive hearing directly." Vaelric's voice dropped, gentle as a physician's. "It would have destroyed itself doing this. And worse—it would have drawn the thing's attention. A beacon burning that bright, on that border, calls the dark straight to us."

"So you caged it."

"I *saved* it." For the first time, something flickered behind the reasonable eyes—not anger, but weariness, deep as bedrock. "I built the cage with your father. The wards that hold Ember also hold the boundary. Every soul in this court is a lamp in a wall against the night. It is not cruelty, Master Ishikawa. It is masonry. And masonry requires stones."

"And my sister is a stone."

"Liora is the keystone." Vaelric said it without flinching, and Wendell understood suddenly why the man had survived so long: he did not lie. Whatever else he was, whatever rot lived underneath the reasonableness, he told the truth the way a blade tells it—clean, and meant to cut. "Her soul anchors the central ward. Free her carelessly and the wall comes down. Not metaphorically. The mountains open. The thing your god died screaming about walks into the free cities while they sleep beside their cold altars."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, Wendell realized, the light-veins pulsed in slow rhythm—and for the first time he wondered if the pulse wasn't the mountain breathing, but the wards holding. Heartbeats of the imprisoned, keeping time against the dark.

Sera's hand was shaking. "You expect us to believe you're a shepherd. That all of this—" her voice cracked toward the lantern halls, toward the screams she'd felt through the stone, "—is mercy."

"I expect you to believe your own god." Vaelric turned to Wendell. "Ask it. When we're done here, put your hand on the cage and ask Ember whether the dark beyond the mountains is real. It will tell you yes. It has been trying to tell everyone yes for ten years, and its voice has been leaking out in fragments ever since—that whisper your priestess friend hears, the scraps the shrines catch. The god isn't silent. It's *muffled*, and even muffled, it spends what voice it can spare warning us." He spread his hands. "I am not Ember's enemy. I am its warden. As your father was before me. As your father intended you to be after him."

"That's a lie," Wendell said. "My father sold us. There's a ledger in the ashes of our house. Two souls, bought and paid for. Me and Liora. That's not wardenship, that's debt collection."

"Yes," Vaelric agreed, and Wendell's certainty staggered. "Your father sold you both. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. He was a frightened man in a dying family, and I gave him a terrible price for a terrible necessity, and he took it, and he hated himself until the day he died—I know, because I held the ledger too. But understand what the sale purchased, Wendell Ishikawa. Your father didn't sell his children to save his house. He sold them to help hold up the sky."

He let that sit. Then he stepped closer, and his voice softened into something almost paternal.

"Now. Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and I will make it honestly. Take up your father's post. Learn the wards. Become warden after me—there are perhaps twenty good years left in my body, and the work needs younger hands, steadier ones. In exchange, I release Liora. Fully. Her soul unbound, her freedom given, and she walks out of this mountain beside you. She can live her life in the sun while you keep the watch she was sacrificed for. One stone removed from the wall—but replaced by a willing one, which is stronger than any taken stone ever was. The wards run truer on consent. Your father's mistake was buying. Yours can be choosing."

Wendell's mouth was dry. "And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out of here with the map and the truth and my compliments, and the wall stands as it stands, and Liora remains a keystone until the wall fails or I find another way. I won't punish you. Punishment is for children and tyrants. I'm offering you a *post*." Vaelric picked up his own glass and drank, watching Wendell over the rim with those kind, tired eyes. "Think of it this way: your whole life, you've been a mercenary. You've killed for coin, guarded caravans, held passes for men who paid you and forgot your name. Here is a contract at last worth the word. Defend the free cities. Keep the dark out. Save your sister. All for the price of staying."

* * *

They were given a chamber afterward—comfortable, warm, absurdly civilized—and left alone, which was either confidence or contempt.

Sera waited until the door had sealed before she spoke. "He told the truth."

"Some of it."

"All of it, Wendell. I've spent my life listening to liars in border towns; I know what truth sounds like coming out of a rotten mouth. He believes every word. The wards are real. The dark is real. Maybe the thing beyond the mountains is real." She paced the length of the room, arms wrapped around herself. "That's what frightens me. A liar I could fight. This man is building a cathedral out of people and calling it architecture, and he *believes*."

Wendell stood at the window—if it was a window; it showed stars that might have been real or might have been another vein of light in the stone—and turned the offer over in his mind the way he'd turn a blade, checking for flaws.

It was a good offer. That was the trap of it. Every part of him that had crawled across the mountains on grief and fury wanted to say yes—wanted Liora free, wanted the years of silence explained, wanted a purpose big enough to justify everything his father had done. Say yes, and the ledger balanced. Say yes, and the sin became service.

But.

"He showed me the truth," Wendell said slowly, "and the truth was a sales pitch. Real wards, real dark, real stakes—all of it arranged to funnel me exactly where he wants me. You don't build a cathedral out of people, Sera. Even for a good reason. The moment you start, you stop being able to tell the difference between masonry and murder. He's been alone with that difference so long he can't see it anymore."

"Then what do we do? Because I felt those souls in the walls. If we tear the cage open and he's right about what's beyond the mountains—"

"Then we need to know what he's *wrong* about." Wendell turned from the false window. "He told the truth about the dark. So there's a lie somewhere else—in the ledger, in the arithmetic, in what the souls are actually *for*. My father built the outer gate. Liora has been inside this machine for ten years, mapping it from the inside, if the map was honest about that too. Before I take any post, before I trade anything, I want to see her."

A knock sounded at the door. Soft. Courteous.

When Wendell opened it, a courtier stood in the corridor—smoke-faced, patient, its faded eyes holding something that might once have been kindness.

"The master says," it intoned, "that you may see your sister now. He thought you would ask." It paused, and the smile that crossed its ruined face was almost human. "He also says: be careful what she tells you. Prisoners see only the bars."

Wendell took Sera's hand, and they followed it down into the dark.
