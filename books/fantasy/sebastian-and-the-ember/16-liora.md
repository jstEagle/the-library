## 16. Liora

They took him down alone.

Two of the half-faded courtiers flanked him through a corridor of pale stone that hummed like a struck bell, their footsteps making no sound at all. Wendell had counted doors on the way in—eleven, then nine, then the count dissolved into a rhythm that refused to hold numbers. The lanterns overhead drifted when he wasn't looking directly at them. He'd stopped trying to decide whether that was the light moving or his mind.

"You will have a quarter hour," said the taller courtier, its voice like wind through a keyhole. It stopped before a door of black iron banded with copper, and Wendell's stomach dropped, because he knew the bands. He'd seen them as a child, wrapped around the strongbox his father kept beneath the floorboards—the one with the ledger, the one with everything.

His father hadn't just sold to this place. He had *built* for it.

"Quarter hour," Wendell repeated. "And if I need longer?"

The courtier tilted its head, and where a face should have been there was a suggestion of one, like a face remembered by someone who hadn't seen it in years. "Then you will be finished anyway." It drew back into the wall—into the wall itself, stone closing over it like water—and was gone.

Wendell put his hand on the iron. It was warm. Everything down here was warm, faintly, the way a body is warm. He thought of Sera's voice from three nights ago, low in the dark of the smugglers' tunnel: *Every soul they take, they hang it up and call it light.* He thought of her hand on his arm. *Don't go looking at what you can't unsee.*

He pushed the door open.

* * *

The room beyond was small, dry, and utterly ordinary, which after the lantern halls felt like a kind of violence. A bed. A table. A chair. A window—a real window, cut deep into the mountain rock, showing not sky but a smooth curve of stone, so that whoever sat beneath it could look out at nothing forever.

She was standing at that window with her back to him, and she was thin—not lantern-thin, not faded, but the thin of someone who had spent years eating exactly enough. Her hair was cropped short and uneven, self-cut. She wore grey, plain wool, no ornaments, no chains.

But her hands were moving. Even as he stood there, even before she turned, her fingers were tracing something against the windowsill, small and ceaseless, and he understood with a lurch that she was writing. Counting. Keeping tally of something only she could see.

"Liora," he said.

She didn't startle. That was the first wrong thing—or maybe the first right thing. Ten years, and she did not whirl around, did not cry out, did not do any of the things a sister stolen at fourteen should do when her dead brother's voice found her in a cell under a mountain. She simply went still, all at once, like an animal deciding whether the sound was wind or wolf.

Then she turned.

She had their mother's face. That was the second thing that broke him—not that she'd changed, but how much she hadn't. Older, yes. Harder at the mouth. But the eyes were the same grey-brown, Ishikawa eyes, his own eyes looking back at him from a stranger who wasn't one.

"Wendell," she said. Her voice was steady. Almost amused. "You're older than I expected."

"I came—" His throat closed. He tried again. "I came as soon as I knew."

"I know when you came." She crossed the room in four strides and took his face in both hands, and her palms were cool and dry and trembling just slightly, and for one moment the steadiness cracked and he saw the girl underneath, the girl who used to fall asleep against his shoulder during the long cart rides south. "I know *everything* that happens here," she whispered. "That's the whole point of me."

Then she stepped back, wiped her eyes once, briskly, like a soldier, and gestured to the chair. "Sit down. We have perhaps twelve minutes now, and I need you to listen faster than I can talk."

He sat. His legs were not entirely trustworthy.

"How are you—" he began.

"A prisoner, not a lantern. Yes, you've worked that out already; you have that look." She pulled the second chair around so it faced him backward, straddled it, arms folded across its back. "Vaelric keeps me apart because lucidity is contagious. One person in this mountain thinking clearly is a disease. Two would be an outbreak." A thin smile. "I've spent ten years being a very careful infection."

"Liora—"

"Listen. Please." And there it was again, the crack, quickly papered. "I've waited ten years. You can give me five minutes of silence."

He gave her silence. Outside, somewhere far above, thunder rolled through the stone—no, not thunder. Something else. He chose not to think about it.

"The wards are a lie," Liora said.

"I know. Vaelric told me himself. Last night, in the throne hall—he offered me Father's old post. Warden. He said the souls power protections for the free cities, that without them something worse comes down from beyond the passes. He was very reasonable about it. That's what frightened me most."

"Of course he was reasonable. Reasonableness is free when you're lying." She leaned forward. "Here's what he didn't tell you. The wards exist—I've seen the workings, I've mapped every corridor of this cage—but they aren't fed by the lanterns. They're fed by *Ember*. Directly. The god isn't a battery for the city walls, Wendell. The god is the *wall*. Every ward in this mountain is a splinter of Ember turned inward, bound to hold Ember, and the drain goes one direction: into Vaelric."

"Into him."

"He eats." She said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report the weather. "Slowly, carefully, over decades, so no one notices the shape of him changing. You saw him up close. Tell me—when he moved, did the light in the room move with him?"

It had. Wendell had filed it away as some trick of the lanterns, some glamour. Now the memory rearranged itself, cold and complete: the way the flame-light had leaned toward Vaelric like grass bending to wind. Like worship.

"He's not entirely human anymore," Wendell said slowly. "Or he started as something else wearing a man."

"Does it matter which? What matters is arithmetic." Liora held up her fingers, ticking off. "One: the souls in the lanterns are not powering anything except Vaelric's appetite. Two: the wards holding Ember are powered by Ember, which means the cage is a knot the god could theoretically untie—if it had the strength left to pull the threads. Three: it doesn't, because it's been drained for thirty years. Four—" she paused, and her fingers stilled for the first time since he'd entered, "—four. There is a mechanism. A vault below the throne hall, behind a door my father designed, because my father designed everything. The outer gate, the inner lock, the vault mechanism. All of it. Our family built the prison, Wendell. We just weren't told what for."

The room seemed to tilt. Wendell gripped the chair's arms. "Father built the cage."

"Father built the *lock*, believing he was building a seal against the thing beyond the mountains. I think he learned the truth too late—that's why he sold us. Not just for money or survival. Payment in kind. He owed them two souls, and when he couldn't pay, they took his children instead. Me for the debt. You as collateral." Her voice never rose. Somehow that made it worse. "There's a line in the confession he hid in the ledger. Did you find the ledger?"

"At the house. Ashes of it. I read it twice."

"Then you know he wrote *forgive me* eleven times." She almost smiled. "I used to lie awake counting them."

*Liora,* he wanted to say. *Liora, I'm sorry, I'm sorry I didn't come sooner, I'm sorry I stopped listening to Ember, I'm sorry for every year—* but none of it was useful, and she had said listen faster than she could talk, so he swallowed all of it and asked instead:

"The vault. What's in it?"

"The binding. The actual knot. And here is the part you must carry out of this room, Wendell, the part worth dying for:" She reached across and gripped his wrist, hard, her nails biting. "The binding can be opened. It has to be opened *from inside* the vault, and opening it will release everything at once—the god, the light, every stolen soul, all of it in one flood. Vaelric can't allow that. He also can't stop it if it starts, because stopping it requires the warden's key, and the warden's key—" her eyes searched his face, "—is Ishikawa blood. Ours. Which is why he wants you to accept the post. He doesn't want a warden. He wants our blood, willingly given, to re-tie the knot tighter than ever. A willing heir makes the lock permanent. An unwilling one can break it."

"So I refuse the post."

"And he takes your blood unwillingly, and it works almost as well, and you die badly." She let go of his wrist. "Unless the vault opens first. Unless someone reaches the mechanism before he completes whatever rite he's preparing—you've noticed the preparations? The courts gathering? He's timing something. I don't know what. I know it's soon."

"How soon?"

"If I knew, I'd tell you. I know it's soon the way you know a storm's coming—by the pressure." She stood abruptly, crossed to the window that showed nothing, and pressed her palm flat against the stone. When she spoke again her voice had changed, dropped into something rawer. "Ten years, Wendell. Do you want to know how I survived them? I mapped. Every corridor, every guard rotation, every meal, every word spoken within earshot of my door—I built the whole mountain inside my head, floor by floor, and I checked it against itself a thousand times until there were no errors left. Because as long as I was mapping, I was doing something. As long as I had the map, I wasn't a payment. I was a spy."

She turned back to him. In the strange warm light her face looked carved.

"I can get you to the vault. I know a route the courtiers don't watch because they don't believe anyone could know it—it runs behind the lantern-vault, through a service gallery from before Vaelric's time, back when this was a temple and not a larder. My map has never been wrong. But I can't open anything. The locks answer to warden's blood and priestess rites, and I have neither." She paused. "But you brought a priestess. Don't look surprised—I hear things. Fragments leak through these walls, especially near the lantern halls. A woman's voice, praying in smuggler's cant. I've been listening to her for three days wondering when you'd arrive."

"She's above. In the guest quarters Vaelric assigned her. Under guard, probably."

"Loosely guarded. He *wants* her close—priestesses make useful hostages and better bait. Which means getting to her is possible." Liora came back to the chair, gripped its back, and looked at him levelly. "So here is the conspiracy, brother, and you will forgive me for having rehearsed it alone for a decade: tomorrow, at the evening court, you will accept Vaelric's offer."

"What?"

"You'll accept. Kneel, swear, whatever theater he requires. It buys you freedom of movement inside the mountain, access to the lower galleries, and time. Meanwhile Sera finds the vault entrance from the temple side—there's a sealed rite-door in the old sanctum that answers to hearth-faith words, and I can teach them to you tonight if we have minutes left. Then you open the mechanism, and everything happens very fast, and either we win or the mountain comes down on all of us."

"That's not a plan, that's a coin flip."

"Yes." She smiled, and for a moment she was fourteen again, grinning at him across the cart bench on the road south, before everything. "Welcome to my decade."

Somewhere beyond the door, a bell tolled—deep, bronze, wrong. The quarter hour, spent.

Liora moved fast then. She caught his hands and pressed something into them, folded small: a scrap of cloth, covered edge to edge in tiny inked characters. The map. Ten years of mapping, compressed into a square of linen.

"Memorize it tonight. Eat it in the morning." She kissed his cheek once, hard, fierce. "You got tall," she said, absurdly, and her voice finally broke all the way through. "You got tall and you came. That's more than I let myself hope for, some years."

"I'll come back," he said. "With Sera. Tomorrow night. I swear it on—"

"On nothing," she said sharply. "Swear on nothing. Promises are currency here; spend them only when you can pay." She straightened, composed, the prisoner again, the spy. "Now go. Walk slowly. Look dazed. He expects you shaken from seeing me, so be shaken—it costs you nothing and buys me another day of being considered broken."

He reached the door. Her voice stopped him, quiet:

"Wendell. One more thing. When you kneel tomorrow—when you swear—he will ask you to say your name as part of the oath. Full name, blood-name, the old formula." Her fingers had resumed their silent writing against the chair-back, fast now, urgent. "*Refuse that part. However he frames it, however reasonable it sounds—do not speak your name into his keeping. Names are the one coin he cannot counterfeit. If he holds yours, he holds the lock on you forever.*"

"I understand."

"You don't yet. But you will." She exhaled, and the steadiness settled back over her like armor. "Go, brother. Twelve minutes was never going to be enough. Come back tomorrow and we'll finish the conversation we should have had ten years ago—at the top of a freed mountain."

The door opened for him. Behind him, he heard her sit, heard the chair scrape, heard her begin—softly, steadily—to recite the map aloud to herself, checking it against itself, floor by floor, error by error, the way she had every day for ten years.

He walked back up through the humming corridors, the linen scrap burning against his chest, and rehearsed the only speech that mattered:

Tomorrow, he would kneel. Tomorrow, he would accept.

And when Vaelric asked for his name—

he would refuse.
