## 17. The Warden's Key

The courtiers gave Sera a room with no door.

Not a locked one—a doorway without a door, an arch of black stone hung with curtains that didn't quite touch the floor, so that anything passing in the corridor could see her bed, her washstand, her body if it chose to turn over in its sleep. She understood the design immediately. It wasn't cruelty. It was accounting. In the Lantern Court, everything was watched because everything was worth watching, and a smuggler-priestess from Cinderhold was either a guest or inventory depending on how the next three days went.

She lay awake the first night listening to the halls breathe.

That was the only word for it. The mountain inhaled and exhaled around them, slow as a sleeping man's chest, and beneath the breath she heard the whispering—the same whispering that had followed her since Cinderhold, grown from thread to rope now that they were close to its source. *Hurry,* it said. *Hurry, hurry.* Sometimes it said Wendell's name. Once, horribly, it had said hers, in her mother's voice, dead eleven years, and she had sat up in the doorless dark with her knife out until dawn.

On the second morning, a courtier came for Wendell.

It called itself Vess, and it had been human once—Sera could see the ghost of the man in the long face and careful hands—but now its skin had the translucent quality of paper held up to flame, light moving behind it that wasn't light. It bowed to Wendell with genuine reverence. "The heir will dine with the Regent. There are matters of inheritance to discuss."

Wendell caught Sera's eye across the corridor. They'd agreed on nothing explicit—there hadn't been time, and doors here presumably listened—but twenty days on the road had built a language between them: a flick of his eyes toward the east galleries meant *stay clear*, a touch of two fingers to his sternum meant *I'll come back*. He touched his sternum now.

"I'll be fine," he said, which was what he always said before walking into something that was not fine.

Vess led him away down the lantern-lit hall, and Sera watched until the black arches swallowed them both, and then she stood alone in a corridor of screaming light and got to work.

* * *

The rites were old ones. Old enough that when Sera had learned them, huddled in a cellar shrine in Cinderhold with a woman named Osha who smelled of tallow and secrets, they'd been illegal twice over—once for the god they honored, once for the things they let you do. Osha had taught her the hearth-rites first: kindling, keeping, blessing. But she'd taught the others too, late at night, when the wine was gone and the watchfires low. The rite of the unlit hand, which made your warmth invisible to things that hunted by heat. The rite of borrowed silence, which wrapped sound in sound until even a scream came out muffled. And the last one, the one Osha had made her swear she'd never use unless the alternative was dying:

The rite of the wick.

*A soul is a candle,* Osha had said. *Most people never learn they can tip their own flame. Pour a little out, and you can walk past anything that feeds on souls—it tastes you and finds you already spent, already ash, not worth the taking. But the flame doesn't refill, child. Every drop is yours, and you don't get more.*

"How much does it cost?" Sera had asked.

"That depends," Osha said, "on how much of yourself you're willing to never feel again."

Sera had used it once, crossing the Salt Straits with a hold full of refugees while a Vaelric patrol boat swept the water with soul-sensing lanterns. She'd poured out a memory—she'd chosen carefully, a bad one, a night she wanted to be rid of anyway—and she'd felt the absence ever after like a tooth pulled from the back of her mouth. Not pain. Just a smooth gap where something used to be.

Tonight she would need more than one bad memory. Tonight she was going to walk into the vault where a god was caged, and everything in that place would be hungry.

She dressed for it. The courtiers had left clothes in her room—fine ones, gray silk, the wardrobe of someone they expected to keep—and she ignored all of it and put her own road-worn leathers back on, and over them the patched cassock of the hearth-faith, ember-red at the hem, the symbol stitched by her own hands: a flame inside a cupped palm. If she died down there, she'd die dressed as what she was.

Then she knelt on the stone floor, closed her eyes, and began to pour.

* * *

The vault was below everything.

She'd mapped it from whispers over three days of playing the meek guest—asking Vess innocent questions about the architecture, trailing her fingers along walls while courtiers weren't looking, counting stairs. The Lantern Court was built like a shell around its secret: throne room above, banquet halls above that, and beneath it all, reachable only through a service passage the faded things no longer bothered guarding, because who would steal a god? Who could carry it?

The passage stank of cold iron and old smoke. Her candle—one real candle, wax and wick, the last honest thing she carried—threw a bubble of amber light against the pressing dark. She walked with the unlit hand rite burning in her blood, her body heat folded inward, invisible, and with every hundred steps she felt the other rite working at her edges, the borrowed silence drinking her footsteps, her breath, her heartbeat.

And she felt herself thinning.

Not weakness—thinness. As though she were becoming less present, a sketch of a person rather than a person, and she understood with cold clarity that this was the price of the deep rites compounded: each layer of concealment shaved something away. She tested herself as she walked, the way Osha had taught her. Recite your name. Recite your mother's name. Recite the worst thing you ever did.

Her name: Sera. That came easy.

Her mother's name: Maren-of-Cinderhold, no relation to the captain, a cook with burn-scarred forearms who sang off-key. Easy.

The worst thing she ever did—

Nothing came.

She stopped walking. Stood in the dark with her candle guttering, and reached for the shape of a memory she knew she owned, the way you reach for a stair in the dark and find flat floor. She knew she had done something unforgivable at nineteen; she could feel the weight of it, the way an amputee feels the missing limb. But the thing itself was gone, and she realized, standing in that iron-smelling dark, that she must have poured it out years ago and never missed it, the way you don't miss a color you can no longer see.

"Keep going," she whispered, and her voice sounded strange to her, and she kept going.

The passage ended at a wall.

No—that wasn't right. It ended at a door, but the door was not a door. It was a lattice, a screen of interlocking iron bands forged into the shape of a knot with no beginning and no end, and through the gaps in the knot came light. Not the pale harvested glow of the courtier-lanterns upstairs. This light was gold and orange and red, alive, shifting, and it moved like breathing, and Sera stood before it and felt tears start down her face without her permission because she knew that light.

Every cold altar she'd ever prayed at had been reaching toward this.

Every whisper she'd heard for ten years had leaked through this knot.

"Ember," she said, and the word broke in half coming out.

The light surged. The whole lattice brightened, and the whispering that had been rope became roar—not sound, exactly, but pressure, meaning forced through a keyhole too small for it, and out of the flood she caught fragments the way you catch fragments of a shout through a closed window:

*—small one—you came—you CAME—*

"I brought him," Sera said. "Wendell. He's here. He's upstairs, he's—he's doing what he has to do. Can you open it? Can you open it yourself?"

The light dimmed, and the grief that rolled off it nearly put her on her knees.

*—bound—bound-bound-bound—the knot is the lock and the lock wants a key and the key is—*

The pressure shifted. An image forced itself into her mind, huge and precise: a hand. A living hand, pressed flat against the knot, and running through the hand—through the wrist, the arm, the whole body behind it—light. Soul-light, freely given, flooding the mechanism the way water floods a dry channel. The image held, repeated, insisted, and Sera understood with a sick lurch what the god was telling her.

The lock opened from the outside. It required a soul poured through it, willingly, completely. Not a drop. Not a bad memory. All of it.

"That's why my map marked me," said a voice behind her, "isn't it."

* * *

Sera spun, knife out, candle nearly dying in the sudden draft—and there was Liora.

She'd seen her only once, in the meeting Wendell had been granted, and even then through a doorway, at distance, under lantern-light that made everything look drowned. Up close, in the god-light leaking through the knot, Liora Ishikawa looked like her brother the way a river looks like rain: the same elements, differently arranged. Sharper. Thinner. With the particular stillness of someone who had spent years being careful in a place where carelessness meant becoming furniture.

"You followed me," Sera said.

"Lady Vess thinks I'm cataloguing the eastern reliquary." Liora's voice was dry as old paper. "I've spent six years learning which of these creatures can be lied to. Vess believes whatever flatters its sense of order. I told it I'd be checking seals until the second bell." She stepped closer to the lattice, and the light washed over her face, and Sera saw that she was shaking—but steadily, like a wire under load, not like a frightened woman. "You asked it if it could open itself. What did it say?"

"It showed me a hand. A soul, given freely, all of it, poured through the knot like water through a lock."

Liora closed her eyes. "Yes," she said. "That matches."

"Matches what?"

"Six years of mapping, priestess. You think I've been sitting in a cell reciting prayers?" She turned, and in the gold-orange light her eyes were fierce and exhausted and terribly young. "I know this cage better than the thing that built it. My father designed the outer gate—Ishikawa blood opens it, that part of the map was true—but the inner lock, the knot, that's older than my family. Vaelric found it. Or it found him. And it needs feeding to stay shut, which is why the lanterns exist, why the harvest exists—" She pressed her lips together, mastered herself. "But a lock that eats souls can also be broken by one. Given freely. Completely. The mechanism can't tell the difference between theft and gift, only volume. That's its flaw. I've known about the flaw for four years and there has never once been anyone I trusted enough to tell."

"And now?"

"Now there's a man upstairs having dinner with a monster," Liora said, "wearing our father's ring, planning to trade himself for me. Because that's what Wendell does. He walks into fires for people." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, Sera could see her hating it. "So we have a problem, you and I. The lock needs a whole soul. And everyone I love keeps volunteering."

* * *

They worked by the knot's own light, Liora sketching in the dust with a stick of charcoal she'd stolen from the reliquary, and what she drew made Sera's stomach drop through the floor.

"The cage isn't just holding Ember," Liora said. "Look. Here—" the outer gate, Ishikawa-sealed. Here—" the knot, the soul-lock. And here, wrapped around both, underneath everything, a third layer she'd drawn in jagged strokes: "the reason Vaelric tells his wards-and-protection story. Because it isn't entirely a lie."

"What's under it?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. The courtiers won't go near the lowest galleries—they say the mountain dreams badly there. But I've heard it." For the first time, Liora's steady wire of a voice trembled. "Some nights, through the floor. Something turning over in its sleep. Whatever it is, Ember isn't its jailer—Ember is its *lid*. The god's light presses down on it. That's why Vaelric cages the god instead of killing it, if he even could. He needs the weight. He just doesn't need the god awake to provide it."

"So when the knot opens—"

"Everything comes out at once. The god. The light. Every stolen soul in the lanterns upstairs, released in a single flood." Liora met her eyes. "And the thing underneath wakes up with all of us standing on its lid."

Sera looked at the knot. At the beautiful, suffering light bleeding through the iron, the god that had whispered *hurry* to her for twenty days and *hurry* for ten years before that, and understood the full shape of the trap. Freeing Ember meant freeing everything else. Leaving Ember caged meant leaving every lantern in the court burning forever, every soul in this mountain spending eternity as fuel. There was no clean choice. There was only the choice between two different catastrophes, and the only variable anyone controlled was *when*.

"When," Sera said aloud. "We choose when. On our terms. Not his."

Liora stared at her. Then, slowly, she smiled, and it transformed her face into something startlingly like her brother's. "Maren said you were the stubborn one."

"Maren talks about me?"

"Constantly. Mostly complaints." Liora crouched over her dust-map again, brisk now, a general assembling a battle from charcoal and dirt. "Here's what matters. The knot takes a soul given freely—volume, willingness, nothing else. But Wendell can't do it."

"He'll try."

"He'll try, and he'll fail, because his soul is already promised." Liora tapped the outer gate on her map. "The gate answers to Ishikawa blood. When he opened it, he paid a toll—didn't he? Felt it leave him?"

Sera remembered. Wendell had gone gray as ash stepping through that door, and for a full day afterward he'd been forgetting small things—the name of the pass they'd crossed, the taste of a fruit he loved—and laughing it off, and she'd known, and let him laugh, because they'd been too close to stop.

"The gate took its portion. What's left of him is spoken for." Liora's voice went quiet. "If he pours what remains into the knot, there won't be enough. The lock would take the payment and jam half-open, and a cage half-open is worse than a cage sealed—we'd have a dying god and an angry thing underneath and a mountain full of panicking courtiers. No. The mathematics only work one way."

"Then we find another soul," Sera said. "There are thousands in those lanterns—"

"Stolen souls can't pay. That's the point of *given freely*. Theft won't turn the lock." Liora stood, and brushed the dust from her knees, and looked at Sera with an expression that Sera recognized, because she'd seen it in mirrors before hard jobs, on the Salt Straits, in burning towns: the look of a person measuring the distance to a cliff and finding it acceptable. "One free soul. Willing. Whole. Standing right here."

"No," Sera said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"I don't need to hear it. The answer is no."

"Sera." Liora said her name gently, and somehow that was worse than anger. "I've been in this cage six years. I know its every bolt. I am the only person alive who can guide what happens after the lock turns—who can steer the flood, keep the freed souls together, keep them *moving upward* instead of scattering into the dark where the sleeping thing can drink them. That's my job. It has to be. Which means the lock needs someone else." She spread her hands. "A priestess who's been carrying Ember's whispers since Cinderhold. Who already knows how to pour."

"I'm not giving you my soul so your brother can have his sister back."

"My brother," Liora said, "is going to give his soul to get his sister back, within the day, unless we act first. Those are your options, priestess. Me in the knot, or him. Someone is going to burn tonight. The only question left is whether it's the person who's spent six years preparing for exactly this, or the person who still thinks he's going to walk out of here."

The knot blazed. The whispering rose around them like wind through a canyon, and for a moment—just a moment—Sera could have sworn the god was saying her name again. Not her mother's voice this time. Her own. Calling her forward the way you call someone home for dinner, the way you call someone you've been waiting ten years to meet.

Osha's voice, in memory, eleven years dead: *How much of yourself are you willing to never feel again?*

All of it, Sera thought. Apparently all of it.

"Not yet," she said hoarsely. "Not tonight, not without telling him, and not before I've seen the mechanism with my own eyes—if I'm going to pour myself into a lock, I want to know exactly what I'm pouring into. Take me deeper. Show me the works."

Liora studied her for a long moment. "You're stalling."

"I'm negotiating. It's what smugglers do."

Something that might have been relief crossed Liora's face—relief, and gratitude, and guilt at feeling them. She shouldered her charcoal stub and turned toward the lowest gallery, where the air grew colder and the god-light thinned and somewhere far below, something vast turned over in its sleep.

"Stay close," Liora said. "And whatever you hear through the floor—don't answer it."

Above them, faintly, through miles of stone, the second bell began to ring.

Lady Vess would soon discover an empty reliquary. And somewhere upstairs, a man in his father's ring was finishing dinner with a monster, running out of time neither of them knew he'd lost.
