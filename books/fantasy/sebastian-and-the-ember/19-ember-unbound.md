## 19. Ember Unbound

The vault was a throat, and Wendell was standing in it.

He had no other word for what he saw. The lantern-vault opened beneath the throne hall like the inside of a bell—circular, ribbed with black stone, and hung with light. Thousands of lanterns, thousands of souls, each one burning in its glass prison with a color that had no name. Gold and green and a blue like deep winter water. They swayed without wind. They hummed without sound. And at the center of it all, sunk into the floor like a heart in a chest of stone, was the cage.

It did not look like a cage. It looked like an altar—the twin of every dead altar Wendell had ever knelt at, down to the scorch-marks where prayers had once landed hot. But it was wrapped in chains of a metal that drank the eye: look at it too long and your gaze slid off, found something else to do. The glass above it pulsed in slow rhythm, and each pulse made the thousand lanterns dim a little, as if the whole vault were breathing around a stolen heartbeat.

*That's him,* Sera breathed beside him. Not aloud. He felt it more than heard it, the way he'd learned to feel her thoughts across ten days of tunnels and one night of blood. *That's Ember.*

Wendell's hands were shaking. He looked down at them and made them stop.

Behind them, Maren stood in the shattered doorway with her sword drawn on her own company, her breath ragged, her face gray with the effort of having chosen. "I can give you minutes," she said. "Not more. The guard will regroup. Whatever you're going to do—do it now."

Vaelric came through the far arch alone.

He had stopped pretending. That was the worst of it. All the way up through the court he had worn his elegance like armor, the reasonable smile, the patient eyes of a man doing necessary arithmetic. Now the elegance was still there but it sat wrong on him, the way a coat sits wrong on a scarecrow. His skin had gone translucent at the edges. When he moved, the lantern-light bent toward him, hungry, and Wendell understood with a lurch that every soul in this room had passed through that hunger on its way to the glass.

"Wendell Ishikawa." Vaelric's voice was warm. It was always going to be warm; that was the trick of him. "You've seen the wards. You know what stands beyond the mountains. Take the post. Keep the light. Be the warden your father refused to be at the end."

"He wasn't refusing," Wendell said. "He was ashamed."

"Shame is a luxury of small men." Vaelric drifted closer, and the nearest lanterns flinched. "You want your sister. I have already given you Liora. Take the office instead of the girl. One soul keeps the door; two would only waste it."

"You keep saying *keep*," Wendell said. "Like it's yours to keep."

"It is mine." And there—for half a heartbeat—the warmth cracked and something looked out from behind Vaelric's face, something old and vast and patient as erosion. "I built the silence you prayed into for ten years, mercenary. I drank the god's voice note by note while you blamed yourself. Do you understand? Your grief was my income."

The words went into Wendell like a blade finding the gap in armor. Ten years. Ten years of kneeling at dead altars, of telling himself he'd imagined it all, that the god who had spoken to him as a boy had been a child's fancy or a fever or a punishment. Ten years of grief disguised as professionalism.

And it had been theft. It had been harvest.

Something in him went very quiet and very clear.

"Sera," he said.

"I see it." She was already moving along the vault's rim, reading the mechanism the way she'd read it in her vision—her fingers tracing channels in the stone, following the flow of light from lanterns to cage. "The binding runs through the altar-stone. Break the cage and everything comes out at once. Every soul. Every year of stored voice. It'll come out like a dam breaking." She looked back at him, and her face told him she'd done the same arithmetic he had. "There has to be an anchor. Something to hold the shape of the release, or the mountain doesn't just crack—it opens. Everything in here goes up, and Thornfell, and the pass, and the refugees in the glacier caves, and—"

"And someone holds the line," Wendell said.

"*Someone*," Sera said, and her voice broke on the word, because they both knew what kind of someone could hold a flood of that size. Not flesh. Flesh would burn like paper. It needed a vessel with room in it. A hollow thing. A soul-shaped absence big enough to pour a god into.

Or a man willing to become one.

"No," Maren said from the doorway. She'd gone white. "No, whatever you're thinking—"

"The rites exist," Sera said quietly. She wasn't looking at Wendell anymore. She was looking at the floor, at the old scorched circle before the altar, where a worshipper could kneel and offer. "They were never meant for this. But the shape is the same. You give the god what you are so it has somewhere to land. A hearth instead of a house fire." Her jaw tightened. "My shrine taught the small version. Candle-offerings. A memory of your mother for a blessing. This is—" She stopped. Started again. "This is the whole self. Name, history, face. Everything he is, poured out to make a bed for the light."

"And what's left?" Maren demanded. "After?"

Sera didn't answer. She didn't have to. Wendell had seen the answer in the under-court halls, in the courtiers who had given pieces of themselves away across centuries until nothing was left but courtesy and appetite. Except those had been takings. This would be a giving.

Vaelric laughed.

It was not a human laugh. It came out of him like gas from a fissure, and the lanterns guttered with it. "Oh, splendid. Yes. Offer yourself. The cage drinks one more soul and I gain a warden after all—you'll hold the light forever, Ishikawa, anchored in the stone, aware enough to know what you lost. Your father designed the lock. I'll design the rest." He spread his translucent hands. "Or refuse, and we all die together when the mountain bursts. Choose. You're good at choosing, aren't you? You chose the company over the truth. Your father chose survival over his children. Everyone chooses, and the ledger balances."

"That's the difference," Wendell said.

Vaelric paused.

"My father sold. I'm giving. Same coin, opposite direction." Wendell walked forward, past the swaying lanterns, past the flinching light, until he stood at the edge of the scorched circle. Up close the cage-hum was audible now, a note held too long, a voice pressed flat for a decade. He knew that note. He'd spent ten years missing it. "You harvested a god's voice and called it warding. My father sold his daughter and called it survival. Everyone in this mountain has a story about why the taking was necessary." He turned to look at Vaelric, and he felt his own face go calm in a way that surprised him. "I'm done with taking."

"Wendell." Sera caught his arm. Her grip was strong; her hands were shaking. "Listen to me. There might be another way. Give me an hour, give me—"

"There isn't, and you know it, and you're brave enough to say so even now." He covered her hand with his own. Her fingers were cold. "You carried the whispers for years when everyone called you mad. You got me here. You kept me listening when listening hurt." He smiled, and it cost him, because he could feel the edges of himself already loosening, the way a knot loosens when you finally stop gripping it. "So I need you to do one more thing. After. Carry what I can't. If I forget him—if I forget all of it—I need someone who remembers for me."

"Don't," she whispered.

"You once asked me why I never talked about my sister." His voice stayed steady. He was proud of that, later, in the fragments that remained to be proud with. "Because it hurt. That was the whole answer. And I have spent ten years being careful with my hurt, keeping it, rationing it, like it was the last thing I owned." He looked up at the cage, at the pulse of imprisoned light. "It's not the last thing I own. It's the first thing I can give."

He stepped into the scorched circle and knelt.

The stone was warm. Of course it was warm. It had been drinking heat for a decade.

"What are you doing?" Vaelric's warmth had cracked again, and beneath it Wendell heard something new: alarm. "The anchor must be willing, yes, but the rite requires a name freely surrendered—and I hold the mechanism, boy, I decide what—"

"You don't," Wendell said. "That's the thing about gifts. You never controlled them. You only ever stole."

He bowed his head, and he began.

He didn't use Sera's rites. He used the oldest one, the one every child in the free cities learned before they learned letters, the one his mother had murmured over the hearth before the silence fell: *Ember, who warms the dark, who sees the small hours, who waits at every cold altar—here is my hand, here is my name.*

The cage screamed.

There was no other word. The note it had held for ten years broke upward into a chord, and the chord became a voice, and the voice was vast and cracked and furious and *glad*, and it filled the vault like sunrise filling a cellar.

***HERE.***

One word. It went through Wendell like a spear of heat, and then—

Then he gave.

It was not pain. That was the strange mercy of it. It was pouring. He felt his mother's kitchen go first—the smell of cedar smoke, her humming off-key—and he let it go the way you open a fist. His father's face, stern and tired and guilty. The company yard, Maren teaching him to fall properly, laughing at him. Rain on the foothills contract. The dead altar. The courier's blood on his hands, warm and failing. The map. Cinderhold burning behind them. Tunnels. Sera's laugh, sharp and startled, the first time he'd made her laugh. The name Ishikawa. The name Wendell. The word *brother*. The word *son*. The word *man*.

Each memory left warm. Each one lit something on its way out—he could feel them catching below him, kindling, feeding the rising pressure of a decade of stolen light, and the vault began to shake, and the lanterns blazed, and Vaelric was shouting something in a language that predated language, and Maren was roaring orders at her own men, and none of it mattered, none of it touched him, because he was busy becoming an empty room with the doors thrown wide.

Last of all, almost last, he held one thing back—not to keep, just to carry to the threshold. Liora, age six, missing teeth, holding up a drawing of a dragon made of fire. *This one's you,* she'd said. *When you grow up.*

*I hope so,* he'd told her.

He gave that too. He gave it hardest, and it burned brightest, and he thought—or the thing that had been him thought, in the last instant where thinking was still shaped like a person—*let that one land close to her.*

Then Wendell Ishikawa poured himself out, and Ember came home.

***

The mountain did not burst. But it sang.

Every chain on the cage snapped at once—not broken, *released*, the way a bowstring releases—and the stored light of ten years came up out of the stone like a geyser of dawn. It did not burn the vault. It *filled* it, and the thousand lanterns shattered in sequence, a chord of breaking glass rolling outward through the dark, and the souls rose free—gold and green and winter-blue, thousands of them, spiraling upward through the broken ceiling like sparks off a bonfire, and where they passed, the half-faded courtiers straightened and remembered their faces and wept.

Vaelric burned.

He tried to run—that vast thing behind his face tried to fold itself back into the elegant man-shape and flee up through the court—but the light knew him. It had been counting what he took. It came down on him like a verdict, and for one moment the usurper's mask tore entirely away and what shrieked in the throne hall of the under-court was old and starved and small after all, small as greed, small as a hole that could never fill itself. Then it was a shadow on the wall. Then it was less than that.

Maren stood in the doorway with her sword lowered, tears cutting through the ash on her face, watching souls rise past her like weather.

And in the center of it, in the scorched circle where a man had knelt, the light gathered and pooled and took a shape—a shape like a dragon made of fire, like a child's drawing brought magnificent and true—and it stood over the empty place where Wendell had been, and it bowed its head, and the whole mountain rang with a voice that was grief and gratitude braided into one sound.

Then it rose. Through stone, through rock, through miles of mountain, unhurried, unstoppable, home.

The vault went quiet. Glass dust drifted like snow. The souls were gone—upward, outward, toward the surface, toward every cold altar in the world.

Sera knelt in the wreckage with her arms full of nothing.

She crawled to the center of the circle. There was no body. There was no ash. There was only a faint warmth in the stone, fading, and the shape of two knees pressed into the scorched rock, and beside them, unburned, untouched, a single object: the courier's map, its wax seal cracked, its final destination now a blank space where a name had been.

She picked it up. She held it against her chest, and she did not make a sound, because some griefs are too large for noise.

Footsteps. Liora came down through the broken arch, limping, ash-white, alive, her eyes going everywhere at once—reading the vault the way she'd read it from inside for years, mapping the ruin of her prison. She saw Sera. She saw the empty circle. She understood, because she had spent seven years learning exactly what it cost to give yourself away in that place.

She knelt beside Sera, and took the map from her hands gently, and folded it closed.

"He's not gone," Liora said. Her voice was hoarse from disuse and iron anyway. "Look up. Look at what's rising. Every spark that passes us—he's holding the door for them. He's the shape they're pouring through." She swallowed. "I know what it looks like. I lived in the arithmetic of this place. Nothing is ever destroyed here. It's only... rearranged." Her hands tightened on the map. "We get him back. However long it takes. Whatever it costs. We get him back."

Above them, faint through miles of stone, the mountain was still singing.

And far overhead—at the surface, at the pass, in the glacier caves, in Cinderhold's ruins, in a hundred towns where altars had stood cold and silent for ten years—the first fires of the returning god were beginning, one by one, to light.
