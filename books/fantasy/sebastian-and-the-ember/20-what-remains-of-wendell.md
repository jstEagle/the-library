## 20. What Remains of Wendell

The mountain stopped screaming before dawn.

Sera felt it happen the way you feel a fever break—one moment the stone beneath her was shuddering with everything that had been caged inside it for ten years, and the next it simply exhaled, long and slow, like a body finally allowed to rest. The tremors faded down through the vault floor, down into whatever deep veins of rock ran beneath Thornfell, and then there was only silence.

Not the old silence. Not the dead-altar silence she had grown up with, the silence of a god gone missing from the world. This was the silence after a shout—the ringing kind, the kind that meant something enormous had just been said.

She got to her feet. Her legs nearly refused her. She let them refuse, and knelt there a moment longer on the cracked obsidian of the vault floor, one hand pressed flat against the warm stone, listening with the part of her that had spent a lifetime listening.

*Ember?*

Nothing came back in words. But warmth came—actual warmth, rising through the floor into her palm, into her wrist, spreading up her arm like sunlight through water. And threaded through it, faint as smoke, something that was not language but was unmistakably *attention*. Being noticed. Being known.

Her eyes stung. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, smearing soot across her face, and laughed once—a broken, astonished sound that echoed strangely in the ruined vault.

"You're awake," she whispered. "You're actually *awake*."

Around her, the lantern-vault was coming apart in the gentlest possible way. The iron frames that had held the captive souls—hundreds of them, thousands, each one a person sold or stolen or traded away by Vaelric's court—were splitting along their seams, and what came out of them was light. Not firelight. Not the cold harvested glow Sera had walked past for hours, feeling each lantern like a scream against her teeth. This was different. This was *them*, whole, unbound, rising.

They rose slowly, the way embers rise from a banked fire—not fleeing, not scattering, but climbing, drawn upward toward some call only they could hear. Sera watched a woman-shaped brightness drift past her shoulder, close enough that she felt its warmth on her cheek, and for half a heartbeat she would have sworn the light paused. Turned. Touched her hair the way you'd touch a stranger who'd been kind to you.

Then it rose on, joining the slow river of souls streaming up through the shattered ceiling, up through the galleries of the under-court, up toward the surface and the sky.

Sera stood in the middle of it with her arms at her sides and wept without any dignity at all.

It took her several minutes to remember Wendell.

When she did, the grief arrived ahead of the memory, a cold hand closing around her throat, and she turned in a full circle, scanning the vault, the rubble, the blackened place where Vaelric had burned—

He was standing by the cage.

Or what had been the cage. The great mechanism his father had built, the outer gate with its Ishikawa locks, was open now, its doors flung wide, its workings dark and cooling. And in front of it, exactly where he'd been when the light took him, stood Wendell Ishikawa.

He was upright. That was the first thing she noticed, and the last good thing. He stood straight, hands loose at his sides, head slightly tilted as if listening to something far away. His eyes were open. They were the same grey-green they had always been, and they were looking directly at her, and there was nothing behind them. No recognition. No wariness. Not even the polite blankness of a stranger meeting a stranger's gaze. Just the empty, patient attention of a man watching weather.

"Wendell." She crossed the vault floor fast, stumbling over a fallen lantern-frame, catching herself on her palms and pushing up again without slowing. "Wendell. Hey. Look at me—it's Sera. It's *Sera*."

He watched her come. He didn't step back when she reached him, didn't flinch when she grabbed his shoulders—his jacket was scorched, she saw now, one sleeve burned away to show skin that was unmarked, impossibly unmarked—and he didn't answer. He just looked at her with those emptied eyes, and after a moment he said, in a voice that was his voice and yet somehow not, mild and curious:

"Is that my name?"

The words went through her like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

"No," she said, too fast, and then, because lying to him now seemed like the worst sin available: "I don't know. Maybe not anymore. But it was. It was your name for thirty years, and you paid it, and it *mattered*, do you understand me? You bought a god out of a cage with it. You bought—" Her voice cracked. "You bought all of this."

He considered that. She could see him considering it, the way you'd watch someone consider a puzzle box, turning it over, finding no seam. Then he smiled—a small, courteous smile, the smile of a man being patient with a distressed woman—and said:

"I'm sorry. I don't remember buying anything."

Behind them, the last of the souls streamed upward, and the vault grew dim, lit now only by the residual glow in the walls, the deep ember-light of a god settling back into the bones of the world.

* * *

Liora found them there.

Sera heard her before she saw her—boots on stone, running, uneven, and then Liora Ishikawa came around the flank of the dead mechanism with a stolen guard's cloak over her prison rags and ten years of captivity written into every line of her body, and stopped dead at the sight of her brother.

Sera had met Liora less than an hour ago, in the chaos after the burning, and even in that chaos she had understood immediately where Wendell's face came from. The same sharp jaw. The same dark hair, though hers was streaked prematurely with grey. But where Wendell's face had always been guarded, closed, a door kept locked on principle, Liora's was open—had to be, Sera thought, to have survived that place. You couldn't live ten years as a prisoner mapping your own cage if you didn't keep every part of yourself alive and reachable.

Now that openness broke.

"Oh," Liora said. Just that. She crossed the distance between them in four strides and took Wendell's face in both hands, tilting it down toward hers, searching his eyes the way Sera had searched them, and getting the same nothing back. "Oh, no. No, no—Wendell. *Wendell.*"

"Is that my name?" he asked again, the same mild courtesy, and Sera watched Liora absorb the blow. Watched her go white. Watched her hands tighten on his jaw and then, deliberately, gently, loosen.

"Yes," Liora said. Steady. Sera marveled at how steady. "Yes, it is. I gave it to you. You were born in the second week of harvest month, during a storm, and Papa wanted to call you Aldous and Mama said absolutely not, and I was six years old and I said he looked like a Wendell, and Mama laughed for the first time in three days, so that was that." She was talking fast, the words tumbling, and Sera realized she wasn't talking to Wendell at all, not really—she was talking *over* the emptiness, laying planks across a chasm. "You hated the name until you were nine and then you loved it and wouldn't let anyone shorten it. You used to sign it on everything. Papa's ledgers. The frost on windows. Once you signed it on the neighbor's goat and we never heard the end of it."

"Liora," Sera said softly. "He can't—"

"I know what he can't do." Liora didn't turn around. Her thumbs moved against Wendell's cheekbones, small steady strokes, an anchor-line. "I spent ten years in a cell learning the difference between what people can't do and what people won't try. Sit down, both of you. We're going to be here a while."

There was nowhere to sit except rubble, so they sat on rubble. Wendell folded himself down beside them with the easy grace of muscle memory—his body remembering things his mind had surrendered—and looked out at the dim vault with placid interest, waiting, it seemed, to see what would happen next.

Liora took his hand.

Sera watched her do it and understood, with a lurch, that this was the bravest thing she had seen in a campaign full of brave things. Braver than the vault. Braver than Maren turning her blade on her own company. Because Liora was reaching into the dark for a hand that might not reach back, and doing it anyway, and smiling while she did it.

"Do you want to hear about the goat?" Liora asked.

"I don't mind," Wendell said.

"The goat's name was Duchess. She was a terrible animal. She ate Papa's accounting ledger, which honestly improved it, and she bit the mail carrier twice, and you—you were seven—decided she was your best friend in the world." Liora's voice wavered and steadied. "You fed her apples off the tree by the well. You'd stand on the fence rail and hold them way out over your head so she had to stretch, and you'd laugh so hard you fell off the fence, every single time, and every single time Mama would come running out thinking you'd broken your neck, and every single time you were fine, just lying in the grass laughing with apple juice all over your face."

Something crossed Wendell's face.

It was nothing. Less than nothing—a flicker, a twitch at the corner of his mouth, gone almost before it arrived. But Sera saw it, and she saw Liora see it, and Liora made a sound low in her throat like a woman taking a wound and deciding to keep walking.

"There," Liora whispered. "Did you feel that?"

"I don't know what I felt," Wendell said. He sounded troubled for the first time, genuinely troubled, and Sera thought: *good, trouble means a door, trouble means something in there still moves.* He pressed his free hand flat against his chest, over the sternum, as if checking whether something had knocked. "It was like—warmth. Without a source."

"That's the apples," Liora said. "That's you falling off the fence. Hold onto it."

"I can't hold onto it. It's already—" He frowned, and the frown was so purely, so heartbreakingly *him*, the exact frown he'd worn studying maps and ledgers and Sera herself whenever she said something he was turning over, that she had to look away. "It's already going. Like water through fingers."

"Then we'll pour more," Liora said. "We have years. We have the rest of our lives, which you gave us, so you don't get to complain about how we spend them."

* * *

They stayed in the vault until the light in the walls began to fade, and then they climbed.

The way up through the under-court was a pilgrimage through a dying city. The halls that had been lit by captive souls were dark now, truly dark, and the courtiers—the half-faded things that had served Vaelric, sustained by proximity to the harvested light—stood motionless in their alcoves and doorways as the three of them passed. Some had collapsed entirely, reduced to drifts of pale ash in the shapes of people. Others remained upright, and as Sera walked past them she felt them noticing the warmth she carried, the borrowed ember-glow that lived in her now the way it had lived in her whispers for years, and she felt them *wanting* it, hungering after it, and she kept her hand near her knife and did not run.

One of them spoke as they passed the last gallery before the ascent. Its voice was dry paper, barely a voice at all.

"Heir," it said. "The heir returns the light."

"Not the heir," Sera said. "The son. There's a difference."

The thing tilted its head, considering, and did not answer, and they climbed on.

The tunnel up was narrow and long and lit, increasingly, by daylight leaking down from above. Sera went first, then Liora, then Wendell, and twice on the climb Sera looked back and caught Liora mid-sentence—*and the winter the river froze we skated all the way to Miller's bend, you were terrified, you insisted on bringing a pole*—feeding her brother's emptiness story by story, plank by plank, building a bridge across ten lost years and one sacrificed self. And twice Sera saw the flicker again. The twitch of almost-memory. Each time it faded. Each time Liora poured more.

Near the top, Wendell spoke unprompted for the first time since the vault.

"It's strange," he said. His voice echoed in the shaft. "I don't remember any of it. The apples, the goat, the ice. But I notice that when she talks, I want her to keep talking. Is that—is that something? Or am I just being polite?"

Liora stopped climbing. Sera heard her breath catch, heard her master it.

"That's something," Liora said. "That's the biggest something there is. Keep walking."

* * *

They came out of the mountain into morning.

Sera had expected ash, ruin, the aftermath of a god detonating through stone. What she found instead was light—ordinary, golden, early-morning light pouring down the mountainside, and air that tasted clean in a way she hadn't known air could taste. Below them, the pass was crowded with refugees, hundreds of them, the people who had sheltered in the glacial cave and fled down the valleys ahead of Vaelric's advance, and every one of them had stopped. Every one of them was looking up.

At the sky.

No—at the *mountain*. At the thin bright threads rising out of Thornfell's peak and drifting upward, hundreds of them, thousands, catching the sunrise as they climbed. The freed souls, Sera realized. They hadn't scattered. They were ascending, unhurried, luminous, and the people below were watching them go with their hands over their mouths, and somewhere down in the crowd someone began to sing—an old hearth-hymn, one of the ones that had been illegal for a decade, sung raggedly and off-key and gathering voices as it went.

Sera stood on the mountainside with the wind pulling at her hair and felt the warmth in her chest swell until she thought it would crack her ribs open.

*Thank you,* she said—not aloud, not in words, just opening herself the way she'd learned to open herself to the whispers, offering everything she had.

And Ember answered.

Not in fragments now. Not in leaks and whispers through a cage. The voice that filled her was vast and warm and whole, a hearth the size of the horizon, and it said—or sang, or simply *was*, meaning pouring through her like blood through the heart:

*Little listener. You carried my voice when I could not carry it myself. Whatever you ask of me, ever, it is yours.*

Sera laughed and cried at once, standing in the sun, and made no request at all, because there was nothing left to ask for.

Except.

Except the hollow-eyed man sitting on a rock behind her, patient as furniture, while his sister told him stories he couldn't keep.

*Him,* she said. *Can you give him back?*

The warmth hesitated. She felt it hesitate—felt the god weigh something, the way a person weighs a debt they desperately want to pay and cannot.

*I took what he gave,* Ember said, and the vastness of it gentled. *A name. A life's worth of memories. These are not coins I can mint anew, little listener. They were his, and he spent them, and the spending was the point.*

*But.*

*But,* Ember agreed, and the warmth in her chest flickered, brightened, took on a new quality—something almost like mischief, almost like hope. *What is spent may yet glow. What is ash may yet hold the shape of what burned. I will gather what I can find of him, piece by piece, flame by flame, and carry it back to him as I am able. It will take time. It may take years. And some pieces are gone beyond any finding.*

*But some aren't.*

*Some aren't.*

Below them, the hymn swelled. Above them, the last of the souls crested the peak and dissolved into the bright air like sparks into a night sky, and the mountain stood quiet and warm and free.

Sera turned back to the Ishikawas. Liora had Wendell's hand again and was deep in some new story—Sera caught the words *festival* and *you cried* and *you absolutely did cry*—and Wendell sat listening with his empty eyes and his slight courteous tilt of the head, and then, abruptly, he looked up.

He looked at Sera. Directly. And his brow furrowed—that familiar furrow, the map-reading furrow, the *something here doesn't add up* furrow—and he said:

"You. I'm sorry—I don't know you. But you're looking at me like I owe you something, and everyone else here looks at me like I've given them something, and I can't make those two things fit together." He paused. "Who am I to you?"

Sera walked over and sat down on the rock beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and felt him not pull away.

"You're the most infuriating man I ever met," she said. "You lied to me about why you wanted passage. You nearly got us killed about nine times. You traded away the only thing that made you *you* without asking anyone's permission, including mine, which I intend to be angry about for a very long time." She swallowed. "And you're my friend. My best one. So we're going to sit here, and your sister is going to tell you stories, and I'm going to tell you worse ones, and between the two of us we're going to build you back whether you like it or not. Understood?"

Wendell Ishikawa studied her for a long moment with those emptied eyes. Then, slowly, uncertainly, like a man testing ice:

"That sounds like something I would do."

Liora's laugh cracked in the middle and became a sob and became a laugh again. Down in the valley the hymn reached its final verse. And high overhead, faint but unmistakable, a thread of gold light detached itself from the bright morning sky and began, slowly, to drift back down the mountain—toward them, Sera thought. Toward *him*.

The first flame, coming home.
