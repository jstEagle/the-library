## 21. The Free Cities Stand

Sera felt the god come back before anyone told her.

She was standing on the mountainside above the shattered door, holding Wendell's hand because Liora had put it there, and the light that poured out of Thornfell passed through her like a bell being struck. Every whisper she had ever heard—every fragment, every leak from the cage—arrived at once and then resolved into a single voice, vast and warm and unmistakably *awake*.

*You listened,* Ember said. *When no one else could hear me, you listened.*

She went to her knees in the snow. She wasn't ashamed of it. Half the refugees on the slope did the same.

The light didn't stop at the mountain. She watched it go—watched it pour down the valleys like water finding old channels, following roads she recognized from her smuggling days, following the buried arteries of the land itself. It ran toward Cinderhold. Toward the border towns. Toward every cold altar in every cellar and back room where people like her had kept a forbidden flame alive for ten years out of nothing but stubbornness.

"Look," Liora said softly.

Down in the valley, on the far ridgelines, fires were kindling. Not one or two. Dozens. Hundreds. Points of orange light blooming across the dark country like a fever breaking, each one an altar that had been stone and silence for a decade suddenly remembering what it was for.

Sera laughed, and it came out cracked, and she didn't care.

"He's really back," she said. "He's really—"

Above them, the sky over Thornfell's peak shimmered, and for one heartbeat she saw it: a shape in the light, wings made of heat-haze, eyes like the coals of a forge seen through a keyhole. Then it was gone, folded back into the mountain, into the world, into everything.

But she felt it settle behind her ribs like a coal placed there by careful fingers.

*Rest,* the god said—not to her, she realized. To Wendell, who stood empty-eyed among the rising souls, wearing his own face like a borrowed coat. *I will carry what you gave. And I will give it back.*

* * *

They came down off the mountain over two days, because the freed souls came with them.

That was the part no one had planned for. Thousands of them—the harvested, the sold, the stolen—rising out of the under-court and drifting up through the rock like sparks through a chimney. They weren't ghosts, exactly. They were brighter than that, warmer, and they moved with purpose: upward, outward, home. Sera walked at the head of the refugee column and watched them pass overhead all night, a slow river of embers flowing against the stars, and the refugees below stopped being afraid of the dark entirely.

Some of the souls paused. A few of them circled down to touch the shoulders of people in the column—a woman whose husband had vanished eleven years ago went silent when a point of light settled against her cheekbone and then rose away again, and whatever passed between them, she wept for an hour and afterward walked differently. Lighter.

"They're saying goodbye," Liora said. She hadn't left Wendell's side since the mountain. She kept his hand in both of hers as they walked, talking to him steadily—childhood things, small things, the name of their dog, the shape of the kitchen, the way their father used to burn the rice. He didn't respond. But he didn't pull away either, and once, only once, when Liora mentioned the dog, his mouth twitched at the corner like a man hearing a song he'd forgotten he knew.

Sera carried the rest. That was the arrangement, unspoken but absolute: Liora rebuilt what she could, and Sera held what couldn't be rebuilt yet—the map of who Wendell Ishikawa had been, kept safe in the memory of a woman who had known him for six weeks and loved him anyway. She turned those memories over at night like coins. The way he lied badly. The way he checked exits before he sat down. The way he'd looked at her in the shrine at Cinderhold when she'd refused his coin, like a man being handed something he didn't believe he deserved.

Ember had taken his name. She intended to be there when it was given back.

* * *

Cinderhold was ash when they reached it, but it was not empty.

Vaelric's outriders had burned the town in the first hours after the mountain opened—she learned this from a tanner with a bandaged arm who'd hidden in a root cellar—and then the outriders themselves had fled east when the altars lit, because you cannot fight a god with cavalry. What remained of the town's population had crawled out of cellars and ditches and stood in the ruins looking at the shrine.

Her shrine. The illegal one, the one in the smuggler's cellar beneath the chandler's shop, the one she'd tended alone for years with a candle stub and a stolen relic. It had not burned. The fire had gone around it—she saw the scorch marks stop at the doorway as if the flames had met a wall—and inside, the little altar blazed with a flame the size of a fist that gave light like a bonfire.

They were all standing outside it, afraid to enter.

"It's yours," Sera said to them, when she understood why they were waiting. Her voice came out strange, formal, like someone else's. "It always was. I just kept the wick trimmed."

An old woman touched her sleeve. "You're the one who heard him. In the bad years. They said the priestess of Cinderhold heard whispers."

"I'm not a priestess," Sera began.

"You are now," said the tanner, and the crowd murmured agreement, and Sera felt the word settle onto her shoulders like a yoke—familiar weight, heavier than any pack she'd ever carried, and somehow not unwelcome.

That night she stood before the altar and let Ember fill the silence she'd spent ten years filling herself.

*What do you need of me?* she asked.

*A voice,* said the god. *I am flame. Flame does not speak. It burns, and others say what the burning means. You said what my whispers meant for ten years when the whole world called you mad. Say it louder now.*

So she did.

* * *

Word traveled faster than armies, faster than souls, faster than anything except fear—and this was not fear. Riders went out from Cinderhold within days: the tanner, the old woman, three smugglers who owed Sera favors and repaid them gladly now. They carried a simple message, and Sera wrote it herself, sitting in the ruins of the chandler's shop with ink frozen in the pot until she warmed it over the altar flame:

*Ember is free. The altars are lit. Come to your shrines. Come to Thornfell. The free cities have a god again, and a war to win.*

The answers came back as fires.

Every cold altar that lit sent up a answering glow visible for miles, and the smugglers' network she'd spent her whole life navigating became something else overnight—the same hidden roads, the same cellar chapels and mountain waystations, but carrying pilgrims instead of contraband, and news instead of secrets. From Greywater: the granary guild had seized the docks from Vaelric's harbor-master and hung his tax ledgers from the crane. From Marrow's Ford: the ferry-men were running free passage east for anyone willing to carry a spear. From the salt coast, a week later, half a message that arrived singed and half-legible: the tide-priests had read the flames on the water and declared the old covenant renewed.

Ten years of Vaelric's quiet, reasonable darkness—ten years of *the wards must be fed, the cities must be protected, sacrifice is order*—and it took the free cities nine days to remember they had never actually agreed to any of it.

Sera stood on Cinderhold's ruined wall at dusk and counted the fires on the horizon until she lost track past forty.

"We're going to win," Liora said beside her. She'd come down from the mountain camp where Wendell rested under the tanner's roof, blank-eyed and gentle, letting himself be led. "You know that, don't you? I spent seven years mapping that cage, and I know exactly how much of Vaelric's power walked out of it as smoke. He's a husk with an army. We're a country with a god."

"Armies still have to be fought," Sera said.

"Yes." Liora smiled, and it was her brother's smile, and Sera's chest hurt. "But isn't it better, fighting with the wind at your back?"

* * *

Maren Kestrel came to Cinderhold on the twelfth day, and she brought four hundred soldiers with her.

Sera saw the dust of the column from the wall and felt her stomach drop—four hundred was a company strength, and Maren's company had been bought, and amnesty had been a trap once already—but Liora put a hand on her arm.

"Wait. Look at the banners."

The company colors flew at the head of the column, but torn. Deliberately torn, ripped from the staffs and knotted around the spears of individual soldiers like armbands, like badges of defection. And behind the torn colors, on new-cut poles, rode banners Sera hadn't seen in ten years: the hearth-mark, the crossed flame, the sigil of a faith that had been outlawed so quietly most people had forgotten it was ever illegal.

Maren rode up to the gate alone and dismounted, and Sera went down to meet her, and for a long moment the two women just looked at each other across the trampled snow.

"The last time I saw you," Maren said, "I was pointing a sword at you in a vault full of screaming lanterns."

"And then you pointed it the other way," Sera said. "I remember that part too."

Maren's jaw worked. She was thinner than she'd been at the Split Rock, and there was a healing cut along her hairline, and her eyes had the flat shine of a woman who had done arithmetic she didn't like. "After the mountain, Vaelric ordered the company to burn the refugee camps at Greywater Pass. Containment, he called it. Souls leaking from the mountain might be corrupted, he said. Every soul that rose had to be accounted for, and anyone sheltering them was a collaborator." She spat. "I've been a soldier thirty years. I've burned villages for pay. But I looked at that order, and I looked at the sky full of lights going *home*, and I finally understood what I'd sold."

"What happened to the ones who wouldn't turn?"

"About sixty followed my second. They're Vaelric's now, gods help them. The rest—" she jerked her thumb at the column behind her, at the four hundred, at the hearth-banners, "—the rest remembered whose children they are. Half of them grew up under cold altars. Half of them had grandmothers who whispered to Ember when the priests said he was dead." She straightened. "We're not mercenaries anymore. Mercenaries take coin. We're taking an oath. If you'll have us."

Sera looked past her at the ranks. Hard faces, tired faces, faces she'd last seen trying to kill her in a glacial cave. And here and there, younger faces—camp-born kids, company brats raised on the road, staring at the burning shrine of Cinderhold with their mouths open.

"You understand what you're swearing to," Sera said. "Not to me. To him. Ember doesn't want soldiers who kneel. He wants soldiers who *tend*. There's a difference."

"Then teach us the difference," Maren said. "You're the voice of the hearth-faith now. Everyone from here to the salt coast says so. So say it to us."

And that was how, on a burned town's muddy main street, with a scrap of altar-flame carried out in a brazier, Sera Reyes swore four hundred soldiers into a faith that had legally not existed for a decade. She had no rites written down—the old ones had burned with everything else—so she spoke from the ten years of whispers, the fragments she'd hoarded like a miser, and found that Ember fed her the words as she needed them, line by line, like a partner who'd been waiting a long time for his cue.

It was not a complicated oath. She kept it short on purpose.

*Keep a flame. Feed a stranger. Tell the truth even when the lie would keep the peace. When the dark comes—and it will come—stand between it and the ones you love, and don't stand alone.*

Four hundred voices repeated it back to her, raggedly at first, then together, and the brazier-flame leapt so high on the final word that the soldiers flinched and then laughed, startled, like children, like people remembering how.

* * *

They dug the pass defenses for six days.

Thornfell's approach was a single road through a granite throat—the same narrows where Vaelric's hunters had ambushed them at the Split Rock, and Sera walked it with Maren and the company's surviving sergeants and showed them where the rock funneled, where the dead ground lay, where she and Wendell had nearly died. Maren marked it all on slate without sentiment.

"He'll come up this road," Maren said. "He has maybe eight thousand effectives left—desertions have gutted him since the mountain, but eight thousand is enough to burn every town between here and Greywater if we let him pass. And he'll come desperate. Desperate men attack terrain they should flank."

"Can we hold?"

"With four hundred? No." Maren said it flatly, without drama. "With four hundred and a god and every farmer between here and the coast deciding he's done kneeling? Maybe. Ask your altar."

So Sera climbed to the high shrine—the refugees had built it in the weeks since, a ring of stones at the pass mouth with a flame that never guttered no matter the wind—and she knelt, and she asked.

The answer was not words. It rarely was, anymore; Ember had grown more godlike since leaving the cage, less conversational, more weather. But she felt it: a warmth moving through the rock beneath her knees, patient and immense, like a banked fire the size of a mountain range. And underneath the warmth, threaded through it, something she recognized.

Wendell. What was left of him, woven into the god's heart like a gold thread through black cloth. His stubbornness. His refusal to check whether he deserved things before doing them anyway. Ember had anchored itself in the gift of a hollowed man, and the god's presence now carried his fingerprints, and Sera knelt there on the freezing stone and cried a little, quietly, the way you cry at good news.

*He'll hold,* she told Maren, coming down. "The pass will hold. Whatever else happens—he'll hold."

* * *

On the ninth night, the watchfires on the eastern ridge began to answer each other wrong.

Sera was teaching evening prayers to the newest soldiers—forty farm boys from Marrow's Ford who'd never held anything sharper than a sickle—when the runner came down from the heights, breathless, frost in his beard. Lights in the dark country beyond the pass, he reported. Moving. Organized. Too regular for refugees.

By midnight the ridge scouts confirmed it: Vaelric's army, eight thousand strong, marching on Thornfell. And at its head, the scouts said, something that made the hardened ones cross themselves with the new hearth-sign: a figure that glowed faintly, that cast no shadow, that the horses refused to approach. The usurper had stopped pretending to be human altogether.

Sera stood on the wall of Cinderhold one last time and looked east. Far off, past the dark miles, a smudge of wrong-colored light sat on the horizon like a bruise. Against it, behind her, the whole length of the free country glittered with altar-fires—dozens, hundreds, more every night, a constellation answering the bruise point for point.

Liora joined her at the parapet. Behind Liora, wrapped in a blanket against the cold, stood Wendell, watching the fires with his empty eyes—and then, slowly, he raised one hand and pointed east, at the bruise-light, and frowned, and said his first word in three weeks:

"Hurry."

Not a command. A memory surfacing, maybe—or Ember speaking through what remained of him, or both, indistinguishable now. Sera took his pointing hand gently and lowered it and held it, and felt his pulse beat steady and warm as a banked coal.

"He's coming," she said to Liora, to the wall, to the four hundred soldiers drilling by torchlight below, to the god in the mountain and the man inside the god. "Let him. We'll be ready."

Below, Maren's voice rang out over the yard, calling the companies to morning formation, and four hundred throats answered as one—and far to the east, the bruise on the horizon began, very slowly, to grow.
