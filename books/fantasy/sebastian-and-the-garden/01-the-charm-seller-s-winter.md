## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

There are three rules to surviving as a charm seller in Greyhollow, and I have kept all of them for twenty years: never sell anything that works, never look anyone in the eye longer than politeness requires, and never — under any circumstances — listen.

The first rule sounds like fraud, but it's really mercy. A luck-knot that actually worked would get us both hanged. What I sell is hope with a pretty knot tied around it, and hope is the one commodity the Ministry of Order has never managed to outlaw, mostly because they can't figure out how to tax it. I braid cord from the cheap wool, whisper nothing into it at all, tie a pattern my grandmother taught me that means *the road bends but does not break*, and charge four coppers. The buyer walks away feeling lighter. I walk away feeling like a man who has just sold someone a candle made of shadow, and we are both, in our separate ways, satisfied.

The second rule keeps me forgettable. In Greyhollow, being remembered is a disease with a high mortality rate. Informants are paid in bread here — actual bread, the dark rye kind, which tells you everything about how hungry this city is — and a man who meets your eyes twice becomes a story, and a story becomes a report, and a report becomes a knock on your door at an hour when no honest knock is ever made.

The third rule is the hard one. Because I can listen. Have always been able to. When I was nine years old I heard the miller's horse complain about his teeth, and when I was eleven I heard a sparrow describe the color of my mother's laugh, and by the time I was fourteen I understood that what lived in me was not a quirk or a blessing but a death sentence with a very slow fuse. Beast-speaking. The old gift. The thing the Ministry's founders burned out of the world two hundred years ago, or believed they had, and wrote laws to make certain of.

So I stopped listening. Or rather, I taught myself the trick of hearing without attending, the way you learn to sleep beside a busy road. The animals still spoke — they never stopped, poor things, chattering their hunger and their small philosophies at every window and gutter — but I let their words slide past me like rain off oiled canvas. Twenty years of practice. I could stand in a market square full of begging dogs and gossiping crows and feel nothing but the cold.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole shape of my life: a shop the width of a coffin, a kettle that whistled flat, a gift folded up small enough to fit behind my ribs where nobody could see it, least of all me.

I tell you all this so you'll understand what it cost me, the morning the dog spoke and I answered.

* * *

The frost that morning had come down the river the way frost always does in Greyhollow — first along the water, then up the pilings, then across the cobbles until the whole market square looked like something a god had breathed on and left unfinished. The stalls wore their awnings like hunched shoulders. The fishmonger's wares had gone stiff as cutlery. Even the pigeons looked offended, if pigeons can be said to look anything other than smug; these ones managed it.

I set up my little table between the rag-and-bone man and the woman who sells candles she swears are beeswax and I swear are tallow with ambitions. My stock that day: eleven luck-knots, six hearth-charms (wool and wire, meaning nothing), a jar of river-stones I'd labeled *protection, various*, and one love-token I'd been unable to sell since midsummer because whoever braided it — me — had been having a bad week and it showed.

Business was the usual winter business, which is to say a study in people wanting things they're ashamed to want. A dockhand bought a knot against drowning and didn't meet my eye while he did it, because to admit you need a charm against drowning is to admit the river has already picked you out. An old man bought protection stones for his doorway and asked me twice whether they worked, and I gave him the answer I give everyone, which is the only honest sentence in my inventory: "They work the way you need them to."

And then, near midmorning, when the light had finally climbed high enough to make the frost steam faintly off the cobbles, the widow came.

I knew her before she reached my table, the way everyone in Greyhollow knew her. Maren Ostler. Her husband had been a net-mender on the south bank, and he'd gone through the ice three weeks ago, and they'd pulled him out too late and laid him under ground that had to be pickaxed open first. She walked through the market the way grief makes some people walk — very straight, very careful, like a woman carrying a full cup of something that must not spill.

She stopped at my table. Her eyes went over the knots, the stones, the jar. Her hands were red at the knuckles and she held them together like a woman holding her own wrists to keep them steady.

"Which one," she said, "for a house that's too quiet?"

I want you to understand that I have heard a thousand versions of that question, and I have a script for all of them. There's the knot for sleepless houses, the knot for empty chairs, the hearth-charm that is really just permission to keep lighting the fire. I opened my mouth to deliver the appropriate line — warm, vague, four coppers — and what came out instead was the truth, because grief does that to liars; it strips us down to the studs.

"The knots don't work," I said quietly. "You should know that before you buy. They're wool and good intentions."

Something flickered across her face. Not anger. Relief, maybe, or its cousin.

"Then why do you sell them?"

"Because the tying helps," I said. "Mine, and yours. There's a pattern my grandmother used — she said the road bends but doesn't break. It's just words. But sometimes words are load-bearing."

Maren Ostler looked at me for a long moment, and I broke my second rule and looked back, because she had earned it. Then she put four coppers on the table, and I took the best cord I had, and I tied the pattern slow, letting her watch each crossing of the strands, and when I handed it to her I said, "For the quiet. So it knows it isn't the only thing in the house."

She closed her hand around it. For a moment her face did something complicated and private, the way faces do when a stranger accidentally says the true thing, and then she thanked me in a voice mostly under control and went on into the market, straight-backed, carrying her cup.

I sat there feeling, for once, like maybe the shop wasn't entirely a fraud. Which should have warned me. Pride is the sound a trap makes just before it closes.

* * *

It was after noon when the dog came.

He came down the middle of the square like he owned it, which no stray in Greyhollow does — strays here move along walls, apologetic, ready to bolt. This one was big, some shepherd mix gone thin over the ribs, gray about the muzzle, with one ear that stood and one that had given up. He planted himself in front of my table and looked at me, and I did what I have done ten thousand times: I let my gaze slide off him like water off glass. Heard without attending. The trick behind the trick.

But he didn't beg. That was the first wrongness. Strays beg. This one sat, deliberate as a magistrate, and waited.

And then he spoke, and twenty years of careful silence cracked down the middle like river ice.

*"Cyrus Amberdale."*

My name. Not the word *food*, not the word *cold*, not the endless simple vocabulary of hunger and weather that street animals live inside. My name, spoken in a voice like gravel turning over in a stream bed, patient and old.

Every hair on my arms stood up under my sleeves. I kept my hands on the table. I kept my face doing the boring work of a bored shopkeeper, because there were forty people within earshot and at least three of them would sell me for a loaf.

"I think you've mistaken me for someone else," I said, to the air, in the tone of a man muttering at a nuisance animal.

*"No,"* said the dog. *"I haven't. You heard me the moment I entered the square. Your breathing changed."*

Damn the animal. He was right; it had.

"Strays aren't welcome at market stalls," I murmured, rearranging stones that needed no rearranging. "Move along."

*"I would have,"* he said, and there was something in the voice now that hadn't been there before — a strain, a fraying, like rope taking weight it wasn't spun for. *"Believe me, speaker, I would rather have died quietly than come to you. But the river sent me, and the river does not ask politely twice."*

The word went through me like a fishhook. *Speaker.* Nobody had called me that in twenty years. Nobody alive had called me that ever — the last voice to use it had belonged to a one-eyed gull on the roof of my mother's house, the summer before I learned to stop listening, and I had spent two decades pretending that gull was a dream.

"Go away," I whispered.

*"Listen to the river."*

"There is nothing wrong with the river."

*"There is everything wrong with the river,"* the dog said, and now his voice dropped into something lower, and I felt it in my teeth the way you feel thunder. *"It has started talking in its sleep. It says names it shouldn't know. It says the ice on the far bank is singing. It says—"*

He stopped. His whole body shuddered, head to tail, like a man swallowing back sickness.

*"It says the old ones are waking up on the other side of the water. And it says you are the only one left who can hear them coming."*

The market went on around us — haggling, laughing, the candle-woman shouting about beeswax, a cart grinding over frozen ruts — and none of it touched me. I was somewhere very cold and very quiet, looking at a scruffy stray with one proud ear, understanding that my life, the small careful life I had built stitch by stitch out of silence, had just been read its own name.

"You're mad," I told him softly. "Or I am. One of us has been out in the cold too long."

*"Both of us,"* he agreed, with a ghost of dry humor that I hated him for, because humor meant kinship and kinship meant danger. *"Come to the bank tonight. After the lamps. Listen. If the river says nothing, I'll leave you to your knots and your quiet, and you'll never see me again."*

"And if it says something?"

The dog rose, shook the frost from his coat, and looked back at me with eyes the color of wet slate.

*"Then you will wish it hadn't,"* he said. *"But you'll listen anyway. It's what you're for."*

He trotted off down the row of stalls, unhurried, and the crowd parted around him without knowing why, the way crowds part around bad news.

I stood at my table with my useless charms and my flat-whistling kettle waiting at home, and I did not sell another thing all afternoon. I watched the light go long and orange over the rooftops, and I watched the frost creep back out of the shadows as the sun lost its argument, and I thought about the third rule, the one I had kept for twenty years, the one that had kept me alive.

Never listen.

But that night, when the lamps were lit and the market had emptied and the river lay black and steaming between its white banks, I pulled my coat on, turned the shop's sign to *closed*, and walked toward the water with my heart going like a fist on a door.

Because here is the truth under all three rules, the one I'd never let myself say aloud: I hadn't stopped listening because it was safe. I'd stopped because it hurt. Every silenced voice was a small grief, and I had stacked twenty years of them behind my ribs, and I told myself it was caution, but it was only a different kind of loneliness, the kind you build yourself and lock from the inside.

The dog was waiting at the foot of the pier, one ear up, one ear down, patient as winter.

"Well?" I said. My breath came out in a white flag of surrender.

He turned his head toward the water, and the river — my river, the dull working river I had lived beside my whole life, the river that had never said anything more interesting than *cold* and *mud* — began, very softly, to speak.

What it said first was my name.

What it said next is why I did not sleep again that night, and why, though I did not know it yet, I would never truly be safe in Greyhollow again.
