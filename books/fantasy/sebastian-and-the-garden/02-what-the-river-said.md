## 2. What the River Said

The river spoke to me the next morning, and I want it on the record that I did not go looking for it.

I had gone to the bank of the Grey for the same reason half of Greyhollow goes to the river in winter: because the fishmongers dump their ice there, and a man with more charm than coin can sometimes trade a knotted cord for a herring that's only mostly frozen. This is called economy. My mother would have called it scavenging, but my mother also called my gift "the family embarrassment," so her taxonomy is not authoritative.

The dog from the market had not spoken again. I'd spent the night telling myself I'd imagined it — that grief and cold and a widow's tears had conspired to make a stray's whine sound like words. This was a comfortable theory. It lasted until roughly the moment I crouched at the water's edge with a herring in one hand and heard the river say my name.

Not *Cyrus*. It didn't know my name. What it said was older than names, a pressure against the inside of my skull like a thumb pressed on wet clay: *you-who-hear, you-who-hear, come down to the water.*

I stood up very fast, which in icy conditions is its own small catastrophe. My boots went out from under me, the herring flew, and I sat down hard on the frozen mud with all the dignity of a sack of turnips. A fishwife two stalls down laughed at me without malice. That was fine. Let her laugh at the clumsy charm seller. Better she laugh at that than wonder why the charm seller had gone white as the ice.

*You-who-hear,* said the river again, patient as water, which is to say infinitely patient and utterly without mercy. *Listen.*

"I'm listening," I muttered into my collar. "Against my better judgment."

Twenty years I had kept this rule: never answer them where anyone can see your lips move. Twenty years of hearing sparrows complain about cats and horses grumble about their riders and once — memorably — a pig deliver a forty-minute oration on the moral failings of its owner, delivered with such rhetorical force that I had briefly considered freeing it out of respect. And through all of it, I had kept my face blank and my mouth shut and my gift folded up small, like a letter passed hand to hand that no one must open.

But the river is not a sparrow. The river does not gossip about cats.

*I have carried things down from the mountains,* it said, and its voice moved through me the way cold moves through boot leather, slow and total. *I have carried meltwater and blood and the ash of burned temples. Now I carry waking.*

"Waking," I repeated, and hated how my voice cracked on it. "Waking what?"

*What was put down.* A pause, and in the pause I felt something vast shift its attention toward me, the way you feel a large animal turn its head in a dark room. *The gods are stirring, little hearer. They are stirring wrong.*

Now, a word about my theology, such as it is. The Ministry teaches that the old gods were never real — that they were stories told by ignorant ancestors to explain weather and death, and that the Ministry's founders, bless their enlightened hearts, simply helped everyone grow up. There are no gods, say the pamphlets. There is Order, and there is Chaos, and Chaos is whatever the Ministry hasn't catalogued yet.

But rivers don't lie. That's not piety; it's hydrology. A river has no reason to embellish. It just carries what it carries.

"Wrong how?" I asked. I had stopped caring about the fishwife. I cared about her exactly as much as I cared about anything, which was suddenly very little, the way you stop caring about small debts when the house is on fire.

*They wake hungry,* the river said. *They wake with their names half-torn away. Something has been eating them from the far side of the water, hearer, and what it leaves behind comes up my current like silt. Prayers are getting through now. The dam the gray men built has cracks in it. And what answers—*

It hesitated. Rivers do not hesitate. When a river hesitates, you should be afraid.

*What answers is not entirely what was prayed to.*

Somewhere behind me a cart rattled past. Somewhere behind me the ordinary world continued its ordinary morning, and I knelt in the mud at the edge of an impossible conversation, feeling twenty years of careful silence develop hairline fractures.

"The far side of the water," I said. "You mean — across? The sea?"

*I mean the other side,* said the river, and I understood that these were not the same thing, and that I did not want to know what the difference was. *I mean where they were put. I mean the half-world, the starved country, the place with no name because naming it was part of the putting-down. Something walks there now. Or something wakes there. The beasts feel it first — they always feel it first — and they are afraid, hearer. The birds will not roost on the north side of the city. The rats are leaving the granaries, and rats do not leave grain. Ask them yourself if you doubt me.*

"I don't doubt you," I said. "Doubting you seems like a hobby for people with better prospects."

A sound moved through the water then that might have been laughter, if laughter could be made of current and gravel. It was not comforting. Nothing about that morning was comforting, including the herring, which by then had been stolen by a gull who offered no commentary whatsoever, which in retrospect makes the gull the most trustworthy party present.

*You-who-hear. You are one of very few left, and fewer every winter. The gray men pay bread for informants, and some of my creatures can be bought cheaper still. So I will say this once, and you will hold it or you will drown in it:* the pressure against my mind deepened until my teeth ached. *The gods are waking broken. Broken things answer prayers badly. And when the gray men notice — and they will notice, they count everything, counting is their religion — they will look for the doors prayers come through. They will look for hearers. Be small, hearer. Be nothing. Be a stone in the shallows.*

And then it was just water again, sliding grey and indifferent over its stones, carrying its cargo of ice and secrets down toward the sea, and I was a grown man kneeling in frozen mud talking to it, alone, except that I was not alone, because a Ministry clerk was watching me from the far end of the fish stalls.

I didn't see him at first. I saw him the way you see anything important — too late, and then all at once. He stood by the barrel of pickled eels with a ledger under his arm and a pencil behind his ear, and he had the particular stillness of a man whose profession is noticing. Not staring. Staring is amateur work. He was doing the professional version: eyes angled slightly off me, attention settled on me like snowfall, soft and accumulating.

He was maybe thirty-five, thin in the way of men who forget meals rather than skip them, with ink stains on his fingers going all the way back to the second knuckle, which told me he wrote for hours daily and had done for years. His coat was Ministry grey but a good coat, mended carefully rather than replaced. Everything about him said: *this man is not dangerous the way a knife is dangerous. This man is dangerous the way a filing system is dangerous.*

I have been afraid of knives my whole life. I should have been more afraid of filing systems.

I did what twenty years had trained me to do. I became boring. I fished another coin from my pocket, bought a second herring from the fishwife — who was now watching me with the frank enjoyment of someone who suspects a story — and held it up to the light like a connoisseur, turning it, inspecting the frozen gills. Then I frowned at it, shook my head, and handed it back.

"Too much ice in this one," I announced to the fishwife, loudly enough to reach the eel barrels. "You're selling me water at herring prices."

"You're buying luck-knots at copper prices," she shot back, "so we'll call it even, Amberdale."

"Harsh but fair." I touched my hat brim, turned, and walked away at the pace of a man with absolutely nowhere urgent to be, while every nerve in my body screamed at me to run. Never run. Running is a confession with legs.

Behind me, I heard the clerk's boots on the cobbles — unhurried, unhurried — and then stop. I did not look back. Looking back is also a confession, though a smaller one, the kind you make with your neck.

* * *

The walk home took eleven minutes and cost me approximately four years of life.

Greyhollow in deep winter is a city pretending to sleep. Smoke stands straight up from the chimneys because there's no wind, which the old wives call a hanging sky, though they won't say hanging *what*, because saying it invites it. The Ministry's criers were out at the corners reading the morning notices in their flat official voices: curfew unchanged, grain ration unchanged, rewards for information regarding unlicensed gatherings unchanged. Unchanged, unchanged, unchanged. The Ministry loves that word. Change is chaos, and chaos is whatever they haven't catalogued yet.

At the corner of Tanner's Row, a cat sat on a wall and watched me pass, and I felt its attention brush against my gift like a finger testing a bruise.

*You smell of the river,* it observed, in the bored tone cats reserve for observations they consider beneath both of us. *You smell of fear. These go together often.*

"Noted," I murmured, barely moving my lips. "Thank you for your report."

*The dogs are gathering by the north gate,* the cat continued, washing one paw. *Sitting in rows. Like they're waiting for something. It's undignified.*

My stomach dropped somewhere around my boot heels. Dogs sitting in rows, waiting. Birds refusing the north side of the city. Rats abandoning full granaries. The beasts feel it first — they always feel it first — and they are afraid.

I made it home. I locked the door, threw the bolt, wedged a chair under the handle out of habits I told myself were about burglars, and sat down at my workbench among my little knots and braids and bundles of dried herbs, and tried to think like a stone in the shallows.

Here is what I knew, laid out on the bench alongside the red thread and the dried rowan:

One. The old gods were real, and they were waking, and they were damaged.

Two. Something on "the far side of the water" had hurt them, and the river's phrasing suggested the hurting was old, deliberate, and possibly ongoing.

Three. Prayers were getting through, and being answered badly, and the Ministry counted everything, and answered prayers were the kind of thing that got counted loud.

Four. A Ministry clerk had watched me talk to a river.

Five. I had talked to a river.

I put my head in my hands. Above the bench hangs a little wooden sign I carved myself years ago, when I was younger and thought irony was armor: AMBERDALE CHARMS — LUCK, LOVE, SMALL MERCIES. Small mercies. I'd meant it as a joke about my inventory. Now it read like a prayer request.

Because here is the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I've never said, the thing I keep in the same sealed room as my gift itself: I remember the stories. Not the Ministry versions — the real ones, the ones my grandmother told with the shutters closed, her voice dropped low the way you speak in a sickroom. She told me the world used to be a conversation. Gods spoke to humans, humans spoke to beasts, beasts spoke to the land, and everything answered everything, and it was — she always paused here, searching — *loud*. Loud and warm and crowded, like a market day that included the sky. And then the founders came with their ledgers and their silence, and the conversation was ended, and the gods were put somewhere, and the door was locked, and the key was—

She never said what the key was. She died before I thought to ask, and I have spent twenty years being grateful I never asked, because questions are how people get noticed.

*You are one of very few left, and fewer every winter.*

I looked at my hands. Charm-seller's hands, rope-burned and ink-stained, ordinary hands, hands that had spent two decades performing ordinariness so well that sometimes I believed it myself. And underneath the performance, faint as a scar under a sleeve, the thing my mother called the family embarrassment hummed like a struck bell that wouldn't quite stop ringing.

The dog had begged me to listen. The river had begged me to listen. Every instinct I owned, every survival strategy honed over twenty careful years, said: *be a stone, be nothing, let the world break somewhere else.*

And underneath that, quieter, older, in a voice I recognized with horror as my grandmother's: *when the beasts ask a hearer for help, hearer, and he says no — that's the day he stops being a hearer and starts being furniture.*

Someone knocked on my door.

I came out of the chair so fast it clattered to the floor. For one lunatic heartbeat I considered answering with a knot of silence, some charm to make whoever it was hear only an empty room — and then I remembered rule one, the rule above all rules: *never use the gift where the gift might be seen working.* Magic noticed is magic reported. Magic reported is magic paid for in bread by somebody's starving neighbor.

So I opened the door like a normal frightened man.

Nobody there. Just the street, the smoke standing straight up in the hanging sky, the snow beginning again in fat lazy flakes. And pinned to my doorframe at eye level, neat as a tax notice, a slip of Ministry paper:

**NOTICE OF OBSERVATION. Citizens are reminded that loitering near public waterways without declared purpose may be subject to inquiry under Statute 12. Report unusual behavior to your district office. Rewards available.**

Rewards available. Paid in bread.

Below the printed text, in pencil, in a small precise hand — the hand of a man who writes for hours every day — someone had added a single line:

*You were at the bank a long time, Mr. Amberdale. Long enough to hear something. — H.*

I stood in my doorway holding the paper, snow collecting on my shoulders, and felt the first thread of something tighten around my life — not a rope yet, not a snare yet, just a thread, fine as the red thread I used for luck-knots, but threaded through a needle, and needles are for sewing, and sewing is for making things hold their shape.

He'd signed it. A clerk who wanted to frighten me would have stayed anonymous. A clerk who signed his initial wanted me to know he was courteous, thorough, and coming back.

I burned the notice in my stove, watched the pencil line curl and vanish, and told myself the fire solved something.

Then, from the cellar beneath my feet — from the dark under the floorboards where I store my twine and my dried herbs and, apparently, my future — came a sound I had never heard before and would spend the rest of this story trying to save: a voice like banked embers finding air, weak and dry and unmistakably noble, speaking words that were not meant for any beast.

"Hearer," it said. "Little hearer. I have crossed a great distance to die somewhere quiet, and your cellar smells of rowan and old promises. Will you come down? I would ask properly, but I am afraid I am using my last strength to knock."

The fox-god had arrived, and it had chosen my door, and the thread around my life pulled one notch tighter.
