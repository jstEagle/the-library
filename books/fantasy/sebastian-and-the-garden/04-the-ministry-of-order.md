## 4. The Ministry of Order

There are three kinds of clerks in the Ministry of Order. The ones who believe, the ones who don't but are good at pretending, and the ones who stopped asking questions somewhere around their tenth year and now simply move paper from one side of a desk to the other with the serene efficiency of a millstone.

I am, on most days, the third kind.

My name is Halvers. I have worked in the Bureau of Deviations for nineteen years, which means I have spent nearly two decades cataloguing the ways in which the citizens of Greyhollow fail to be properly ordinary. Unlicensed charms. Back-alley blessings. A woman in Tanner's Row who swore her hens laid eggs with crosses on them (they didn't; she was bored; her husband was dull). Most of what crosses my desk is nothing. Most of what crosses my desk is *supposed* to be nothing. The Ministry does not actually want magic to exist — that would require trials, and trials require evidence, and evidence requires work. What the Ministry wants is the *appearance* of vigilance, maintained at the lowest possible cost, like whitewash on a damp wall.

I understood this early. It is why I still have my teeth.

But this winter, the paper coming across my desk has changed character, and I do not care for it at all.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with a discrepancy in the numbers.

Every parish in Greyhollow files a monthly report of prayers offered at licensed shrines — the Ministry keeps eleven of them, grey little rooms with grey little altars where you may address the Approved Providence in approved language, though everyone knows the Approved Providence has never once answered anybody. The reports include a column for "anomalies." For years that column has been filled with the same tired entries: a drunk who claimed he heard singing, a child who swore her doll blinked. Routine noise. I file it under N and forget it.

In the last six weeks, the anomaly column has grown teeth.

Forty-one reported instances of answered prayer. Not vague ones — specific, verifiable, *documented* ones. A fisherman on the Weep prayed for calm water and got it, three days running, while every boat around him pitched in swells that shouldn't exist on a river. A laundress prayed for her sick mother and found the woman's fever broken by morning, the sickroom smelling inexplicably of summer meadows. A man in Copperlane prayed for rain for his dying garden and received rain — rain that came sideways out of a clear sky, rain that fell only on his street, rain that would not stop until the Ministry itself sent men to break up the clouds with poles and prayer-hooks, and even then it took two days.

That last one cost the Ministry four hundred crowns in damages and produced a report so frantic it was written partly in verse. I read it twice. Not because of the poetry, though the poetry was memorable. Because of the detail nobody else seemed to have noticed: the rain had fallen *only where it was asked for*. Answered prayers are supposed to be impossible. But if they were going to happen anyway, they should at least have the decency to be sloppy. This was precise. This was *aimed*. Something out there was listening carefully, and careful listening is worse than careless miracles, because careless miracles are accidents, and accidents can be survived.

I took the file to my superior, Undersecretary Corvane, a man built entirely of neck and disapproval, and set it on his desk the way one sets down something that might bite.

"Answered prayers," I said. "Forty-one confirmed instances in six weeks. Up from a yearly average of zero."

Corvane looked at the file the way he looks at everything — as though it had personally inconvenienced him. "Coincidence and hysteria."

"Four hundred crowns of coincidence, sir. The rain report is signed by three witnesses from the Office of Public Works."

"Hysteria with good penmanship." He flipped a page. Then another. I watched his neck redden slightly, which in Corvane is the equivalent of another man going pale. "Where are these concentrated?"

"Along the river, mostly. And—" I hesitated, because the next part sounded mad even written down, and I was about to say it aloud. "And there's a pattern in the petitions themselves. The ones that get answered aren't the formal prayers at the shrines. They're the informal ones. Muttered. Overheard. Prayers said to no one in particular."

Corvane looked up. "You're suggesting the Providence is answering unauthorized petitions."

"I'm suggesting *something* is. And whatever it is, it isn't us."

The silence that followed had a texture to it, like frost forming on glass. Corvane closed the file with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a door on a room where something was sleeping.

"Take this to Director Sallow," he said. "Tell him nothing else. Let him draw his own conclusions."

Which was Corvane's way of saying: *this is above my pay grade, and I would like it to be above yours too, quickly.*

* * *

Director Sallow's office is on the top floor of the Ministry, behind a door with no nameplate, because men like Sallow believe that power announced is power diminished. He is small, neat, and old in the way that furniture is old — worn smooth in the places people touch, hard everywhere else. He received the file without comment and read all forty-one reports in full, which took him an hour, during which I stood at parade rest and contemplated the painting on his wall: a grey field under a grey sky, by an artist whose name history has mercifully misplaced.

When he finished, he did not say "coincidence and hysteria." That was the first bad sign.

"Do you know what the founders called the year the covenant broke?" he asked, without looking up.

"The Severance," I said. Everyone knows the word. It's carved over the Ministry gates.

"They called it the Severance publicly. In their private correspondence they called it the Quieting." He tapped the file. "Because that is what it was. Not a war. Not an uprising. A silencing. The gods went quiet, the beasts went dumb, and the world became legible. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Halvers?"

"That history is more interesting than the official version, sir."

"That *silence* is policy." Now he looked up, and his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of old ice. "For two hundred years this city has functioned because the world answers to reason, labor, and the Ministry, in that order. Every miracle is a debt incurred against that order. Forty-one miracles in six weeks is not a curiosity. It is a *creditor at the door*." He slid the file back across the desk. "I want listeners identified."

"Listeners, sir?"

"People the things on the other side can hear. Whatever is answering those prayers must be hearing them through someone — or reaching toward someone. There will be signs. Animals behaving strangely. Individuals lingering near water. Persons who receive information they should not possess." He said it flatly, like reading a shopping list. "Compile a register. Names, addresses, associations. Quietly. If we announce a hunt, we get a thousand false confessions from cranks and a thousand true denials from the guilty."

"And when we find them?"

Sallow returned to his papers. "Detain for questioning. Standard procedure."

Standard procedure. I have seen standard procedure. Standard procedure involves a room in the sub-basement with excellent acoustics and no windows, and men whose job title is "interlocutor" and whose actual job is best not described in a document that might someday be audited. I nodded, because nodding is free, and left.

In the corridor outside, I stopped by the window — third floor, overlooking the Ministry yard — and watched the snow come down on the flagstones where, two hundred years ago, they had burned the last public priestess. There's a plaque. It says ORDER RESTORED. It doesn't mention her name. It doesn't mention that she was twenty-three.

I want to be clear about something, since I am apparently keeping a record: I am not a good man. Good men do not last nineteen years in the Bureau of Deviations. I have filed reports that ruined bakers and midwives and one extremely unlucky puppeteer. I have told myself, each time, that the machine moves whether I push it or not, and that a clerk who refuses to push is simply replaced by a clerk who pushes harder. This is true. It is also, I have recently begun to suspect, the exact reasoning every cog in every terrible machine has ever used, right up until the day the machine grinds *him*.

But knowing a thing and being able to act on it are separated by a distance measured in daughters' school fees and a wife's opinion of lodgings in the Low Quarter. So I went back to my desk, and I began compiling a register of listeners, and I hated myself with the quiet, practiced efficiency of a man who has gotten very good at it.

* * *

The register grew faster than I expected.

The animal reports alone filled a folder. Dogs howling at the river at dawn, in unison, across four districts — not the ragged chorus of normal dogs but coordinated, *congregational*, as if attending a service. Crows gathering on the Ministry roof in numbers that made the guards nervous. In Shambles Lane, a butcher's cat sat outside a locked shop for three days, and when the constable finally broke the door, the butcher was alive inside, having been trapped under a collapsed shelf — and the cat, according to the constable's report, "appeared to have been keeping watch." The constable had appended, in smaller handwriting, the phrase *as if posted there*, and then crossed it out. I un-crossed it in my copy. Clerks have privileges.

Then there were the people. A lamplighter in Bellwether Street who claimed the river spoke to him — dismissed as drink, except he'd been sober eleven years, verified by his temperance society, of all things. Two children in the Weep district playing a game they called "telling secrets to the water," which their mothers found charming until both children began repeating things back that the water had supposedly said, including, in one case, the location of a drowned body that had been missing since autumn. That report I read three times. The constable who wrote it had added a personal note: *the child drew a map before anyone told her where to look.* Someone senior had stamped it DELUSION and moved on. I put it in my own folder instead. My folder was getting thick. My folder was becoming a problem.

On the ninth day of the compilation, I went down to the river myself.

I tell myself it was professional diligence. Site verification. A clerk should see the geography of his cases. But the truth, which I am recording here precisely because no one will ever read it, is that I wanted to hear it too. Nineteen years of filing other people's wonders, and some starved thing in me wanted a turn.

The Weep was frozen at the edges, open at the center, moving dark and slow under a sky the color of dishwater. I stood on the bank near the old ferry steps, hands in my pockets, feeling like an idiot. Gulls screamed somewhere. A barge groaned against its moorings. The river said nothing, obviously. Rivers do not talk. Rivers are water with a current and a reputation.

But I stayed an hour. Longer than any site verification required. And while I stood there, a dog came down the frozen bank — a big brindled stray with ribs like a washboard — and it did not beg, and it did not scavenge. It walked to the edge of the ice, faced the open water, and *waited*, the way a man waits at a door. And after a while it turned and looked directly at me, with an expression I can only describe as assessment, and then it looked back at the river, and then at me again, as if to say: *well? Are you going to do something, or just stand there?*

I left. Quickly. A grown clerk of the Ministry of Order, retreating from a starving dog, and I wrote in my log that evening only: *site inspected. Nothing to report.*

Nothing to report. The three most expensive words in this city.

* * *

The order came down on the eleventh day, and it was worse than I feared.

Not because of what it said — I had guessed most of it. Detention of suspected listeners. Seizure of animals displaying "irregular cognition." Authority to search premises without warrant, under the Emergency Sanctity Statute, which had not been invoked since my grandfather's time and which everyone had politely pretended was a historical curiosity rather than a loaded weapon in a drawer.

No, what was worse was the addendum, in Sallow's own hand, at the bottom of the distribution list:

*Priority: the charm-sellers. Listeners cluster around those who traffic in belief. Begin with the registered vendors in the market quarter. Cross-reference the animal incidents.*

Charm-sellers. Of course. When the gods wake up, arrest the men selling luck-knots. It has a certain logic — if you cannot find the well, poison the sellers of buckets — and it is exactly the sort of logic this Ministry produces the way a rotting log produces beetles. Registered charm-vendors were a tolerated nuisance, licensed, taxed, inspected twice yearly, permitted to sell knots and dried herbs and small brass amulets of no demonstrated efficacy. Half of them were frauds. The other half were frauds with better manners.

I pulled the vendor rolls and began cross-referencing, because that was my job, and because the alternative to doing my job was thinking about what my job was for.

There were thirty-one names. I worked through them methodically, matching against the animal incident reports, the prayer anomalies, the parish records. Most fell away quickly — charm-sellers whose shops sat nowhere near anything strange, whose only crime was charging a widow two coppers for knotted string.

One name kept surfacing.

Amberdale, C. Charm-seller, Greyhollow Market, stall and premises off Candlemaker's Row. Licensed fourteen years, no violations. And yet: his stall sat three streets from the ferry steps where the dogs gathered. His district accounted for four of the forty-one answered prayers. And there was a note in his inspection file from six years ago, written by an inspector long since retired, that I had to read four times to believe:

*Vendor's premises noted for unusual quiet. No vermin. No rats in cellar despite adjacent grain stores. No mice. No insects. Recommend routine follow-up.*

No rats. In Greyhollow, where every cellar has rats the way every sermon has hypocrisy. A building the animals avoided — or a building the animals *respected*. Or a building belonging to a man the animals knew something about.

I sat with the file for a long time. Outside my window the snow kept falling, patient as bureaucracy. Somewhere below, in the sub-basement, the interlocutors were oiling whatever it is interlocutors oil. Somewhere out in the city, a brindled dog was waiting by the river for an answer, and a charm-seller named Amberdale was presumably selling hope to widows at two coppers the knot, minding his own small life, hurting no one.

*Begin with the registered vendors,* the order said.

I thought about the plaque in the yard. ORDER RESTORED. No name. No age. Just the clean grey lie of it.

I opened Amberdale's file again, and beside his name, in the column marked PRIORITY, I wrote — slowly, deliberately, in my best bureaucratic hand — *low. Insufficient corroboration. Recommend continued observation.*

It was a small thing. A delay, not a rescue. A week, maybe two, bought with a clerk's pen instead of a priestess's fire. Somewhere upstairs, Sallow would eventually notice the gap, or he wouldn't, and either way the machine would grind on, and I would keep feeding it, because I am not a good man, only — as of eleven days ago — a man who is no longer entirely comfortable.

I filed the register at dusk. As I locked the cabinet, I heard it clearly through the frosted window, rising from the direction of the river: dogs. Dozens of them, maybe hundreds, howling in unison at the darkening water — one long note, held together, like a congregation singing.

Like a congregation calling for someone.

And beneath the howling, so faint I might have invented it, something else — a sound like a voice under ice, saying a word I could not make out, addressed, I was almost certain, to somebody in the market quarter.

Somebody with a shop off Candlemaker's Row.
