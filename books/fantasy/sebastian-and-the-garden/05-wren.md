## 5. Wren

The bell above my door had a cracked throat. It had rung half a note for as long as I'd owned the shop, and I'd never fixed it, because a charm seller whose bell announces him properly is a charm seller who gets remembered properly, and being remembered is the one luxury Greyhollow does not sell.

So when the bell gave its broken little cough that morning, I was already reaching for my best lie — the pleasant, forgettable smile of a man who sells knots and dried lavender and absolutely nothing else.

The woman who came in out of the cold was not forgettable.

She was maybe forty, maybe fifty; winter had gotten into her face early and made its opinion known. Grey hair cut short and practical, like she'd done it herself with a knife rather than waste coin on scissors. A long coat patched at the elbows but clean, which in Greyhollow meant either discipline or desperation, and often both. She stood in the doorway a moment longer than a customer would, letting her eyes do what eyes do in my shop: cataloguing.

I've been catalogued before. Ministry clerks catalogue you with their chins up, bored, already writing your name down inside their heads. This woman catalogued me the way a fox looks at a henhouse — not hungry, exactly. *Assessing.* Deciding whether the latch was worth the trouble.

"Closed for inventory," I said, though the sign said nothing of the kind and my counter was visibly stacked with luck-knots.

"No, it isn't," she said, and shut the door behind her.

There are perhaps three correct responses to a stranger locking herself into your shop at dawn, and all of them involve shouting. I did none of them. Partly because shouting draws informants, and informants in Greyhollow are paid in bread, and bread has been dearer than blood these two months. And partly — I'll be honest, since no one's grading me — because something about her made the back of my neck prickle in a way I hadn't felt since I was a boy kneeling in the reeds, listening to things I wasn't supposed to hear.

"I want a charm," she said, unwinding a grey scarf from her throat. "Against forgetting."

I kept my hands busy sorting knot-cords, which is what they're for, besides looking busy. "Luck-knots, love-charms, traveler's blessings. Against forgetting, I've got rosemary. Tie it over the door. Folk say it helps."

"Folk say." She came closer, boots quiet on my floorboards. Too quiet. People who move that quietly learned it somewhere with consequences. "Rosemary keeps memories *near*. I want them gone."

"Gone," I repeated.

"All of them. Every year of them. I want to wake tomorrow and not know my own name." She said it the way other people ask for the time. Flat. Practiced. Which meant she'd said it before, probably to herself, probably nightly, and had worn the horror off it through repetition until only the shape remained.

Now, I have sold charms against grief. Against bad dreams, against a husband's fist, against the tax assessor. Grief is the commonest thing in my shop and I've learned to tell its many dialects. But this wasn't grief. Grief wants to hold on even while it begs to let go. This was a woman asking me to help her amputate something, and she wanted the blade clean.

"That's not a charm," I said. "That's a wound with a price tag."

Her mouth did something that in a younger face would have been a smile. "You sound like someone who's thought about it."

"I sound like a shopkeeper protecting his stock. Forgetting charms don't exist. If they did, the Ministry would have cornered the market and we'd all be cheerful idiots by now."

"They exist." She set both hands flat on my counter, and that's when I saw it.

The brand.

It sat on the inside of her left wrist where the pulse lives — an old burn, silvered with years, shaped like a circle broken by a single vertical line. I knew that mark. Everyone in Greyhollow past forty knew that mark, from the years when knowing it was safe and the years after, when knowing it was a hanging offense. The sigil of the Threshold Rites. The mark they pressed into the wrists of priests and priestesses of the old gods, back when the old gods were merely *neglected* instead of outlawed.

They hadn't branded her to shame her. That was the ugly joke of it. They'd branded her so she could never pretend she hadn't believed. The Ministry's founders understood something important about mercy: it's easier to police people if you make their own skin testify against them.

She watched me look. She didn't cover it. Covering it would have meant admitting it needed covering, and this was clearly not a woman who granted anyone that satisfaction.

"Well?" she said.

"Well *what*?"

"You know what that is. You looked at it the way a man looks at a debt he thought was forgiven." Her grey eyes didn't blink enough. "So let's stop pretending, charm-seller. Because I didn't come here for rosemary, and you didn't flinch at my wrist. You flinched at the word *forgetting* — before I ever explained it. Like you knew there was more than one kind."

The stove ticked in the corner. Somewhere outside, a cart groaned past through the slush, and I heard the driver swearing at his mule, and the mule — this is the part I don't put in my ledger — the mule answered him back, low and resigned, in the way animals talk when they think no one's listening. *Heavy. Heavy today. Heavy.*

Twenty years of practice closed that door in my head before it opened more than a crack. Twenty years of keeping my gift folded up small as a moth in winter, wings together, breath held. I turned back to the woman and gave her my second-best lie, the one about being a simple tradesman.

"Madam, I tie knots. I dry herbs. Whatever you think I—"

"The river spoke to you."

The stove stopped ticking. Or I stopped hearing it. One of those.

"Three nights ago," she went on, relentless as frost, "a stray dog sat outside this shop until midnight, staring at your window. Dogs don't do that. Not for charm-sellers. And yesterday morning, the fisher-folk at the weir say the river ran backward for the space of ten heartbeats, and every bird on the bank went silent at once — except one wren, singing its head off, facing your street." She tilted her head. "I asked myself: what's on this street that the river would want to talk to?"

I should have laughed. I should have said *the river runs however it pleases, madam, and birds are birds.* I have a laugh for that. I've used it. It's a good laugh, warm and slightly stupid, calibrated over two decades to make listeners feel foolish for having suspected anything at all.

Instead I heard myself say: "Who told you about the dog?"

And that, as my mother used to say, was that. My mother said a lot of things, most of them about the danger of answering questions with questions, and she died poor and unheard and uncaught, so draw your own conclusions about how seriously I took her advice.

The woman exhaled slowly through her nose. Not triumph. Something wearier. Relief, maybe, with dread folded inside it like a letter.

"My name is Wren," she said.

"Of course it is."

"It's the truth. My mother named me for the bird before I ever took the rites. The priests thought it was funny. Birds carry prayers, in the old understanding. Small brown messengers nobody notices." She touched the brand on her wrist, absently, the way you touch an old scar while talking about something else. "I was a priestess of the Threshold for eleven years. I kept the dawn office. I fed the shrine-stones honey and ash. And then the Ministry came with their hammers and their ledgers, and I learned that faith is very easy to confiscate when it's been illegal to write it down."

"I don't want to know this," I said. "Whatever you're about to ask me, I don't want to know it, and you should take your rosemary and go."

"What I'm about to *ask*," she said, "is whether you've heard it too."

The silence stretched. Outside, the mule and its driver argued their way around the corner and out of my life. Inside, I stood in my shop among my harmless knots and my harmless lavender, twenty years of careful smallness stacked neatly on shelves around me, and I felt the whole arrangement trembling like ice on a puddle.

Because yes. Yes, I'd heard it.

Three nights ago a dog had spoken to me — actually spoken, words in my head with the taste of another mind behind them — begging me to listen to the river. And the river had told me things I've written down nowhere and told no one: that the old gods were waking, that something was wrong on the far side of the water, that the long sleep was ending badly. I had reported none of it. I had gone home and drunk half a bottle of plum brandy and told myself the dog was a dog and the river was meltwater and the shaking in my hands was the cold.

But the frost hadn't lifted. And neither had the shaking.

"Heard *what*," I said, giving it one last, dignity-preserving try.

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and set something on my counter between us, gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping animal.

It was a prayer-token. Old work, older than the Ministry, older than the city walls maybe — a disc of river-clay baked hard, stamped with the broken circle, worn nearly smooth by centuries of thumbs. Except it wasn't worn smooth. It was *cracked*, a fine black fissure running through the sigil, and from the fissure, impossibly, came a faint warmth I could feel from where I stood. Like holding your hand near an oven door.

"Shrine-stone fragment," she said. "From the chapel by the weir. It cracked three nights ago. All of them did. Every stone, every token, every threshold-marked doorpost in Greyhollow — cracked at the same hour, same minute." Her voice dropped. "The gods aren't waking, charm-seller. Waking is gentle. This is something else. They're waking *wrong* — like men pulled too fast from deep water. And when broken gods open their eyes, they answer prayers. All of them. Badly. Have you seen what's happening at the tanneries? The widow on Tanner's Row who prayed for rain for her garden?"

I had heard rumors. I'd chosen, skillfully, not to listen harder.

"And the beasts," she continued. "The beasts feel it first and worst. They're gathering, did you know? Out past the walls. Deer and wolves standing together in the snow like they've forgotten to eat each other. Waiting for something. Someone." Her eyes came up and pinned me. "A speaker. Someone who can hear them and be heard. The old covenant needs a voice on the human side, and the stones are cracking because they're calling one, and every sign I can read says he lives on this street and sells knots and lies beautifully."

The word *covenant* went through me like a fishhook. I didn't know why. I'd never heard it before — or I had, somewhere, in some childhood reed-bed or grandmother's muttering, and it had sunk so deep I'd forgotten the shape of the wound.

"I'm not anyone," I said. My voice came out rough. "I'm a shopkeeper. I keep my head down. Do you know what happens to people like what you're describing, in this city? There's a bounty. There's a clerk named Halvers who smiles like a man doing arithmetic. They pay informants in *bread*, Wren. Bread. That's what a neighbor's soul goes for these days."

"Yes," she said simply. "I know. I've been hunted before. It's why I want a charm against forgetting — because the last time they caught me, they didn't kill me. They made me *help*. Eleven years of rites, and in one afternoon I helped them seal the last of the shrine-doors, and I have remembered every detail of it every day since, and I would give anything—" her voice caught, just once, and she mastered it the way you master a dog on a lead, "—anything, to set it down."

Something in my chest moved that I didn't authorize. Twenty years of solitude makes a man's chest full of unguarded rooms.

"I can't give you that charm," I said quietly. "It doesn't exist. Believe me — if it did, I'd have used it on myself long ago. Forgetting isn't a knot you can tie. It's a knot you have to *untie*, and untied knots leave marks in the rope."

She stared at me. Then she laughed — short, startled, rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in nine years."

"Don't get attached. I bill for honesty at double rate."

"Do you." She picked up her prayer-token, wrapped it back in its cloth, and tucked it away. Then she paused, hand still inside her coat, and studied me with that fox-assessment again — except the hunger had gone out of it, replaced by something more dangerous. Hope. "The stones will keep cracking. The beasts will keep gathering. And the Ministry will keep hunting whoever they gather *to*. You can stay hidden, charm-seller. Maybe you'll even live. Hidden men usually do — right up until the world ends around them and hiding stops mattering."

"Cheerful."

"I'm an ex-priestess. Cheerfulness was never in my training." She moved to the door, then stopped with her hand on the latch, her back to me. "When you decide — and you will decide, the river doesn't ask twice — come find me. The blue door on Candlemaker's Lane with the crow painted on it. Knock twice and wait. If I don't answer, knock a third time anyway. I'll be deciding too."

The bell gave its broken half-note as she left, and the cold came in with her going, and then the door shut and the shop was mine again, small and orderly and full of harmless things.

I stood there a long while.

Then I went to the window and looked out at the frozen street, at the ordinary people hurrying through the ordinary morning, and I listened — really listened, for the first time in twenty years, wings unfolding, breath released — to the city underneath. The dogs murmuring to each other about the strange pressure in the air. The rats in the walls saying *deep, deep, it comes from deep.* And beneath it all, faint and patient as a heartbeat under floorboards, the river, repeating the same thing it had told me three nights ago, over and over, like a prayer no god was answering:

*Come to the water. Come to the water. Before the ice takes even us.*

On my counter, where Wren's token had rested, the wood was still warm.

* * *

I didn't go to Candlemaker's Lane that day. Or the next.

I told myself it was caution. Caution is my native language; I speak it fluently, with no accent. I tied knots. I sold a traveler's blessing to a carter and a luck-knot to a girl with a wedding coming, and I smiled my calibrated smile, and every hour the warmth on my counter faded a little more, and every night the dog came back and sat across the street, watching my window with eyes like coals, not begging now. Just waiting. Dogs are patient the way debts are patient.

On the third evening, the frost climbed my window glass from the *inside*.

I watched it happen. Ferns of ice blooming on the interior pane, in a heated room, spelling nothing and everything, and out in the street the dog stood up, shook the snow from its coat, and spoke one word into my head, clear as a bell with no crack in it:

*Soon.*

I got my coat. I blew out the lamp. And I went out into the falling snow, toward the blue door on Candlemaker's Lane, telling myself the whole way that I was only going to tell this Wren woman, firmly and finally, that I was nobody — that I was a shopkeeper, that whatever the river wanted, it would have to want it from someone else.

The blue door had a crow painted on it, and a light burning behind the shutter, and as I raised my hand to knock twice, the door opened before I touched it.

Wren stood in the doorway, lantern in hand, and behind her — limping into the lamplight, red as embers, thin as a sermon — came a fox.

It looked up at me with ancient, starving eyes, and it said:

"At last. Signatory. We have very little time, and I have even less."

The door stood open. The cold breathed at my back like a pursuer.

I went inside.
