## 6. Prayers Answered, Badly

### I. Marta Quill, fishwife, of Salt Row

I want it written down somewhere that I prayed *properly*. Knees on the cold stone, coin in the offering bowl — well, cup, because the chapel's been a tannery these twenty years and the bowl went to the tanner's wife as a wedding gift, but the intent was there. Intent is what counts. That's what my grandmother said, back when saying such things out loud could get you a fine.

I prayed for rain. Three weeks of hard frost had salted the fishing boats to their moorings, and the herring had gone deep and sullen, and my boys were hungry in that quiet way children are hungry when they've learned not to mention it. So I knelt by the frozen gutter outside the old chapel door and I said, *whoever's listening up there now that nobody's listening down here — send us rain.*

It came that night.

It has not stopped since.

Nine days now. Nine days of rain like the sky's been split with an axe, rain that comes sideways and upward, rain that fills your boots if you look at them wrong. The gutters of Salt Row have become rivers; the rivers have become something with opinions. My cellar's a pond. My neighbor Odell's roof came off whole on Tuesday and sailed two streets before lodging in the bell tower of the Ministry garrison, which everyone agrees is the funniest thing to happen in Greyhollow in a decade, and everyone also agrees we'd best never say so where a clerk can hear.

And here is the part I keep turning over like a stone looking for what's underneath: the fish came back. The herring came up silver and stupid and thick enough to walk on, filling every net we own and several we borrowed. My boys eat until they groan. We are rich in fish and drowning in our own house, and both of those things arrived together, in answer to one prayer, as though whatever heard me understood the *words* but not a single thing about wanting.

My grandmother also said the gods were like a great wheel — turn them gently and they grind your grain. I think somebody has taken a hammer to that wheel. I think it still turns. God help us all, I think it still turns.

### II. Halvers, clerk third class, Ministry of Order

The rain was not, strictly speaking, my department. Weather fell under Civic Maintenance, and Civic Maintenance had sent three memos and one increasingly hysterical junior engineer to inform the Registry that the storm systems over Greyhollow did not correspond to any pattern in forty years of records, and would the Registry please explain why prayers logged at the abandoned chapel sites had increased four hundred percent in nine days.

Prayers logged. Yes. We log them. This is the part of my job I did not put on the recruitment poster, because there was no recruitment poster; there was a notice nailed to my father's door informing him his son had been assigned useful work, and my father read it twice and said, "Well. It's bread."

Here is how you log a prayer in Greyhollow: informants report gatherings, whisperings, kneeling. A woman on Salt Row was seen with her knees in a puddle, mouth moving. A man in Tanner's Ward buried a copper under a dead oak and spoke to it. Children skipping rope in Copperlane were chanting something old — the informant couldn't transcribe it but said it "had a shape." Each report goes in a ledger. Each ledger goes in a drawer. For twenty years the drawers stayed shut and full, a kind of museum of human hopelessness, and I confess I found the whole apparatus faintly embarrassing, the way you're embarrassed by an uncle who still polishes a uniform from a war nobody won.

Then the drawers started mattering.

Deputy Overseer Corvane summoned me on the ninth day of rain. Corvane is a tall man built like a folded document, and he has the gift — rare in the Ministry — of making you feel that any question you might ask has already been answered, unfavorably, elsewhere. He laid out maps of the city. On each map, small red pins.

"Answered prayers," he said. "Confirmed manifestations. Tell me what you see."

I looked. The pins clustered around the old holy places — the drowned chapel by the river, the foundation stones of the Grove shrine, a well in Copperlane that had been sealed since before my birth. And the pins were getting closer together. Closer to the center. Closer, though I didn't say this, to the charm shops, the fortune-tellers, the people who remembered how to kneel.

"I see a pattern," I said carefully.

"I see a crisis," said Corvane. "Something is answering them. Something was supposed to be incapable of answering. The founders of this Ministry made certain arrangements, clerk, and those arrangements are failing, and when arrangements fail, the failure is always carried by a person. There is a word for people through whom the old powers move." He looked at me the way you look at a sum that won't balance. "*Listeners.* Find them."

"Find them how?"

"That," he said, "is why you're a third class and I'm a deputy overseer."

So now I walk the wet streets with a notebook growing soft at the edges, and I ask my questions, and here is the thing I have not written in any ledger yet: yesterday an old dog looked at me across a flooded square with an expression I can only call *urgent*, held my eyes for three full seconds, and then ran toward the river as though it wanted me to follow. Dogs do not hold your eyes, clerk. I know animals. Animals avoid clerks on principle.

I did not follow. I wrote down the time and the weather and moved along, and my hand shook while I wrote it, and I have decided — provisionally, pending further review — that the shaking was the cold.

### III. Odell Brack, lamplighter, Tanner's Ward

You'll have heard about the Tam boy. Everyone's heard about the Tam boy. What everyone hasn't heard is what it was like from across the street, which is where I stood, holding a lamp I no longer needed because the sky itself had gone strange and bright, like the light was coming from underneath everything.

Serra Tam lost her boy to fever in the autumn. Little Pip, six years old, gap-toothed, forever underfoot. She took it the way most of us take things in Greyhollow — quietly, and then not quietly once, at night, behind a closed door, and then quietly again forever. She stopped lighting her window. I noticed, because lighting windows is my trade and unlighting them is apparently also my trade, this year.

On the eleventh night of the rain she lit it again.

And there he was. In the window. In the window where a boy should not be, waving at me with that gap-toothed grin, and I dropped my pole in a puddle and I am not ashamed to say I shouted the name of a god I don't believe in, or believed in, or — you understand my difficulty.

He came out into the street. Serra came after him, sobbing, calling his name, and he turned to her and smiled, and I saw it clear as lamplight: the smile was right but the *timing* was wrong. It arrived half a breath after it should have, like a puppet worked by someone remembering the strings. And when she gathered him up and he put his arms around her neck, his little hands hung open. Empty. The way hands hang when they've forgotten what arms are for.

She didn't see. Of course she didn't see. You don't see, when the thing you'd trade the world for walks back into it wearing your son's face.

He stayed three days. He ate nothing — she told everyone he'd had no appetite since his illness, poor lamb, and the neighbors nodded and brought honey cakes he never touched. He didn't sleep. She'd find him standing in corners at 3 a.m., facing the wall, perfectly still, and when she called his name he'd turn with that delayed smile and say, in a voice that was almost right, "Almost, Mama. Almost done coming back."

On the fourth morning he was gone. Not dead — gone, the way frost is gone off a window when the room warms. Serra sat in the doorway in the rain and said, over and over, "But he was warm. He was warm, I felt him, he was warm."

He was. That's the horror of it, if you want my testimony, and I suppose you do. Whatever came back wasn't cold. It was warm the way a coal is warm after the fire's out — warmth without anything left to be warm *of*. The gods gave her exactly what she asked for. Her boy, returned. They just hadn't kept the boy. Only the shape of her loving him.

The Ministry came that afternoon. Took statements. Took Serra's name. Took the window — pried the whole frame out and carted it off, which struck me as thorough even for them.

I relit her lamp for her last night. She watched me do it and said thank you, polite as a duchess, and then she said, "Odell, if you ever pray — and I know you don't, none of us do, not properly — pray *small*. Pray for rain, pray for fish. Don't pray for anything you couldn't bear to receive wrong."

I've thought about that every day since. I've thought about it especially hard because last week, walking home past the sealed well in Copperlane, I heard — and I'll swear this on my mother's grave — I heard singing from down inside it. Small voices. Many. Like children skipping rope somewhere very far below the city.

I did not look down. I walked faster than I have ever walked in my life.

### IV. Brother Anselm, defrocked, of no fixed parish

They took my collar eleven years ago for the crime of keeping a candle lit, so understand that I speak as a man with no standing whatsoever, which in Greyhollow makes me either worthless or the only honest witness available.

I tend the old chapel by the river. Not officially — officially it's condemned, flooded to the second step, home to eels and worse. But I go. I sweep what can't be swept. I sit where the altar was. An old man's habit, nothing more, I told myself, for eleven years, nothing more.

Three nights ago the water in the nave recited the Litany of the Deep.

Not metaphorically. The floodwater rose and fell against the stone in rhythm — the actual rhythm, the call and response, the verses I learned as a novices and haven't spoken aloud in decades. And in the pauses between the water's lines, I heard myself answering. My mouth moving. The responses arriving in my own voice before I'd decided to give them.

When it finished, the water went flat and ordinary, and I stood in a condemned building soaked to the waist, laughing and weeping in equal measure, because I had just conducted vespers with a river.

It tried to tell me something afterward. It has no face anymore — I understood that much, that whatever speaks through that water is worn down to almost nothing, a god the way a stub of candle is a candle. But it pressed at me, image upon image, the way you press a seal into wax that's nearly cooled: a garden. Green beyond reason. A road. And a warning, repeated until I thought my skull would crack — *the gate takes a toll, the gate takes a toll, the gate takes a toll.*

Toll of what? I asked. Out loud, like a madman, to standing water.

The water showed me a memory that wasn't mine: hands letting go of other hands. That was all. Hands opening, fingers uncurling, empty air where a grip had been.

I've spent two days trying to decide whether to carry that message anywhere. To whom? The Ministry would burn me and the chapel both. The people wouldn't understand, and shouldn't — wonder is dangerous cargo in a city this frightened.

But tonight a fox followed me home. Sat across the lane from my door in the rain, which no sensible fox does, and looked at me with eyes like banked coals, and when I said "What do you want?" it said — and I am sober, I had one glass of plum wine, one —

It said: "Find the one who hears. Before the winter finishes falling."

Then it left. I'm writing this by candlelight with the door barred, an old man in a city that has outlawed everything I know to be true, and tomorrow I will go looking for a listener, because I am apparently still, after everything, a priest.

Gods forgive me. Or rather — gods, I know you can hear me now. Please stop helping.

### V. Cyrus Amberdale, charm seller — recorded later, reluctantly

Marta wants rain stories. Odell wants someone to confirm the Tam boy's hands. Half of Salt Row has come through my shop this week buying luck-knots and river-charms, and I sell them, and I hate myself a little more with each coin, because I know what they're really buying and it isn't string.

They're buying the feeling that someone is listening.

Someone is. That's the problem. I've heard it all week through the walls of my own skull — the beasts first, dogs and gulls and one appalled cat reporting the same news in a dozen accents: *the old ones are waking, the old ones are waking, and they are wrong, they are broken, they answer like men drunk or dreaming.* And beneath the beasts, lower, older, the river itself muttering its litany to anyone with ears, gathering strength, pressing at the seams of a world stitched shut twenty years before I was born.

I have told no one. I have sold knots. I have smiled at widows.

But last night I dreamed of a garden, green past all sense, ringed in ice, and hands letting go of other hands, and I woke with tears on my face for people I have never met, and when I went down to open the shop there was mud on my doorstep — a line of it, deliberate, winding, the tracks of some animal that had come in the night and stood at my door long enough for the frost to take its prints.

Fox prints. Leading to my door. Leading nowhere else.

Whatever is waking in this city knows my address.

And that morning, as I stood staring at those tracks with my heart going like a drum, a Ministry clerk crossed the square toward my shop — a tired, damp, ordinary-looking man with a notebook — and I understood, with the total clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that my twenty years of silence were over.
