## 8. Hunted

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a customer's knock.

I'd learned the difference over twenty years. Customers knocked twice, apologetic, like they were sorry to interrupt whatever a charm seller did before opening hours. Ministry men knocked the way winter knocks — once, hard, and already inside your walls.

I was in the cellar with Vessel-of-Embers when it came. The fox had eaten half a pigeon I'd bought from the market — paid for, mind you, with coin, because even now I couldn't bring myself to steal for him — and he was curled on my coat, looking less like a god and more like something that had been dragged behind a cart for a hundred miles. His ribs made a little fence of himself.

*They are coming,* he said, without lifting his head. His voice, when he spent the strength to use it, was like embers settling in a grate. *Three of them. Boots that do not try to be quiet.*

"How do you know?"

*I can smell iron and paper. Iron is the man who carries the chains. Paper is the man who writes down what the chains find.* He opened one eye, gold as a struck match. *You have a back way out of this burrow, speaker?*

"I have a sewer hatch," I said. "It's not dignified."

*Nothing about gods is dignified anymore. That is rather the point of us.* He rose, unsteady, and I saw how much the pigeon had cost him just to stand. *Open it.*

My hands were shaking so badly I fumbled the latch twice. Twenty years of caution, and here I was, sweating over a rusted grate while a starving fox-god gave me instructions in the voice of my childhood nightmares. There's a version of me — the smart version, the version I'd built brick by careful brick since I was nine years old — who would have shoved Vessel-of-Embers out into the snow three nights ago and told myself it was mercy. Let him die warm somewhere else. Let someone else's door get kicked in.

That version of me had apparently died somewhere between the riverbank and the cellar, and I hadn't noticed the funeral.

"Listen," I whispered, as the knocking came again, harder. "There's a drain channel that runs under Tanner's Row. It comes up behind the tannery vats — the stink will cover your scent if anything follows you. Go east. East until you're past the wall, then keep going east."

*And you?*

"I'll handle them. I've handled them for twenty years."

*By being nothing,* the fox said. Not cruelly. That was somehow worse. *I have watched you do it, speaker. You fold yourself small and you wait, and it works, because they are hunting something large and you have made yourself into a keyhole.* His tail brushed my ankle, light as an apology. *But you are not a keyhole. You never were.*

Above us, the front door splintered.

I got the grate open. Vessel-of-Embers dropped through the hole like water finding its level, and the last thing he said to me was not thanks or blessing or any of the things a god ought to say. It was:

*Lie beautifully. It is the only prayer your kind still knows how to answer.*

Then he was gone, and boots were on my stairs, and I had perhaps four seconds to become a keyhole again.

* * *

They took the shop apart with the efficiency of men who had done it many times and enjoyed it only moderately.

The tall one went through my shelves, holding luck-knots up to the gray window light as though treason might be stitched into the twine. The big one stood by the door doing nothing, which was his whole job; some men are furniture with fists. And between them moved a third man — slighter, older, with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who reads everything and believes very little.

He didn't introduce himself. He picked up a charm off my counter — a widow's knot, the same pattern I'd sold to poor Marta Ellery weeks ago — and turned it over like a jeweler assessing a fake.

"Cyrus Amberdale," he said. It wasn't a question. "Charm seller. Licensed." He produced a paper and consulted it without much interest. "No priors. No reported irregularities. No informants' claims against you." He looked up. "Which is itself slightly irregular. A man in your trade goes twenty years without a single informant? In Greyhollow?"

"Informants cost bread," I said. "I sell knots. Thin margins."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A clerk's smile is a dangerous thing; it means he's decided you're interesting, and interesting is the first stage of condemned.

"My name is Halvers," he said. "Ministry of Order, Bureau of Deviations. We're conducting inquiries regarding—" he consulted the paper again, though I'd have bet my shop he knew it by heart— "unlicensed spiritual activity. Answered prayers. Unnatural communications." His eyes came up and stayed on mine, patient as a ledger. "Have you heard anything, Mr. Amberdale? Voices, say. Animals speaking. Rivers with opinions."

"Rivers with opinions," I repeated. "Is that a category now?"

"It became one Tuesday."

I laughed. I want that on the record, whoever keeps it: I laughed, easily, naturally, at a joke made by the man sent to destroy me, and the laugh cost me nothing. That's what I hated later. Not the fear — fear you can survive, fear is honest. What I hated was how *good* I was. How the lie assembled itself in my mouth the way other men assemble sentences, smooth and load-bearing, no gaps showing.

Because there were gaps. Of course there were gaps. There was a fox-shaped gap in my cellar and a god-shaped hole in my chest where twenty years of silence used to be, and Halvers was standing four feet away reading me like a customs manifest.

"The animals have been strange lately," I offered. "That part's true enough. Dogs won't come near the market square. Crows sitting on the rooftops all night, quiet as debt collectors. Folk say it's the cold."

"Folk say a great many things." He set the widow's knot down, aligning it precisely with the counter's edge. "Your cellar, Mr. Amberdale. May we?"

"Do I have a choice?"

"You have the appearance of one."

So they went down my stairs, and I followed, and I stood at the bottom watching two strangers search the place where a god had slept last night, and I performed the single greatest act of my career: I looked bored.

The tall one found the straw I'd laid down. Found the pigeon feathers. Found — my stomach dropped — a smear of blood on the stone where Vessel-of-Embers had collapsed the first night, when he'd torn himself coming through the coal chute.

"Fox," I said, before anyone asked. "Got in through the chute. Hurt. I fed it a few days and put it out. It's the season for it — they come down off the hills when the ground freezes. Ask any farmer."

Halvers crouched by the bloodstain. He touched it, rubbed thumb and forefinger together, and said nothing for a long time. The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above us, the big one shifted his weight, and the floorboards complained.

"A fox," Halvers said finally.

"A fox."

"Not a dog. Not a cat. Not a raven, or a rat, or any of the other creatures currently behaving strangely across this city." He straightened, brushing red dust from his fingers. "A fox. Which, according to certain texts our bureau keeps — texts most people have the good sense not to know exist — has a particular significance." He turned to face me fully, and for the first time I saw that beneath the clerk's tiredness there was something else, something almost like reluctance. "In the old rites, Mr. Amberdale, the fox was the messenger of the boundary gods. The go-between. The thing that crossed between worlds carrying words." He tilted his head. "A fox, bleeding, in the cellar of a man with no informants and no priors and no history at all, which is its own kind of history."

For one heartbeat — one long, ringing heartbeat — I considered telling him the truth. I don't know why. Maybe because he'd said *texts most people have the good sense not to know exist*, which meant he knew them, which meant somewhere under the ink stains there was a person who had read old things and felt something stir. Maybe because I was tired, bone-tired, of folding myself into a keyhole while the world outside cracked open like river ice.

Maybe because lying well is its own kind of loneliness, and I'd been lonely for twenty years.

"If I tell you it was just a fox," I said slowly, "you won't believe me. If I tell you it was something else, you'll take me in chains. So let me save us both the paperwork." I met his eyes. "I'm a charm seller, clerk. I sell string and superstition to people who need comfort more than they need truth. If there are gods waking up out there, they've got no business with a man like me. I'm nobody. I've worked very hard at being nobody, and I'm rather good at it."

Halvers looked at me for a long moment. Then he did something I didn't expect. He closed his notebook.

"Cellar's clear," he called up the stairs. "Stray animal, nothing more."

The tall one came down, disappointed. The big one simply left, the way weather leaves.

At the top of the stairs, Halvers paused. He didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth, Mr. Amberdale — and it isn't worth much — I don't think you're nobody. Nobody doesn't feed a wounded animal for days. Nobody doesn't look at me the way you just did, like you were measuring exactly how much of yourself to spend on the lie." He adjusted his collar. "We'll be watching the shop. Consider that a professional courtesy. Some of my colleagues wouldn't bother mentioning it."

Then he was gone, and the door hung crooked on its splintered frame, and I stood alone in my ruined shop with the morning coming up gray through the window, shaking so hard I had to sit down on the floor among my scattered charms.

* * *

I found him at dusk, behind the tannery vats, exactly where I'd told him to go — except he hadn't gone east. He'd gone thirty feet and stopped, and when I demanded to know why, he looked at me with those match-gold eyes and said:

*You told me to run. You did not tell me to leave you. Those are different instructions, speaker, and I am old enough to know the difference.*

"You should have gone east."

*Yes.* He didn't move. *And you should not have lied for me. Yet here we both are, having failed each other beautifully.*

I sat down in the reeking dark beside the vats and laughed until it turned into something else, and the fox leaned his warmth against my side and let me. Above us the frost climbed the tannery wall in patterns too deliberate to be natural, and far off toward the river I could hear dogs barking — not at anything, just barking, the way animals bark when the world has stopped making sense to them.

"He knows," I said finally. "Halvers. He knows what I am. Or close enough. He'll be back, and next time he won't close his notebook."

*Then we do not wait for next time,* Vessel-of-Embers said. *There is a woman, speaker. Ex-priestess. Branded, disgraced, hiding in this city the way you hide — badly, and with great dignity. She has been asking after charms against forgetting. She will find her way to your shop within days, because the gods are waking and waking things pull their servants like tide pulls driftwood.* His muzzle rested on my knee. *When she comes, listen to her. And Cyrus Amberdale — when she asks what you are, this time, do not lie.*

The frost crept another inch down the wall. Somewhere beyond the city, past the walls and the watchers and the long white silence gathering at the edges of the world, something old was calling beasts toward a garden neither of us could name yet.

I put my hand on the fox's cooling back, and I waited for the woman who would change everything, and I hated — gently, thoroughly, with twenty years of practice — how good I'd gotten at waiting.
