## 9. The Long Winter Falls

### Cyrus

The frost came up the river like a debt collector.

I noticed it first on the morning after the raid — of course I did; guilt makes a man observant. Vessel-of-Embers was gone into the dark under the city, and I had walked out of the Ministry annex with my hands free and my stomach sour, and the river that had spoken to me in October now spoke in a different voice entirely. Not words. A kind of grinding silence. Ice forming on moving water, which water does not do, not in Greyhollow, not in any winter I'd known. The current still ran beneath it — you could hear it muttering through the gaps around the bridge pilings — but the surface had gone white and ridged, like a scar over a mouth.

By noon the frost had climbed the embankment stairs. By evening it was in the gutters of Tanner's Row, furring the iron with white lace that did not melt when Wren held her candle to it.

"That's not cold," she said, crouched at my shop door, breath fogging. "Cyrus. Look at it. It's not *cold*."

"I can see that."

"No, I mean — feel it." She took my wrist and pressed my fingers flat against the doorframe, against the frost. It wasn't bitter. It was nothing. The ice sat there like paint, like something *applied* rather than arrived. "Cold is a thing the world does," she said quietly. "This is a thing being done *to* the world."

I thought of the fox-god spending itself in my cellar, keeping some door propped open with its own body. I thought of the river saying *something is wrong on the far side*, back when wrong was a word with comfortable distances attached to it.

"Then someone's doing it badly," I said, because wit is cheaper than prayer and I've never been able to afford either honestly.

Wren didn't laugh. That was how I knew it was serious. She laughed at everything — at my charms, at the Ministry, at death, probably, when it came for her she'd make some joke about the queue.

The next morning the frost reached the market square, and the widow Marta's winter cabbage — the one crop in Greyhollow that laughs at weather — stood in her allotment behind the tannery, blackened and glassy, every leaf frozen from the inside out. Not wilted. *Preserved.* Like a saint's relic. Like something meant to be looked at rather than eaten.

That was the week the crops failed. All of them. In seven days, in high autumn, while the sky stayed a mild grey and nobody's breath should have been fogging at all.

### Halvers

There is a particular quality to paperwork generated by catastrophe, and I have come to know it intimately: it multiplies. A good harvest generates perhaps four forms per district. A dead harvest generates forty, then four hundred, and somewhere past the thousandth requisition-for-emergency-grain-allocations a clerk stops reading the forms and simply feeds them forward like coal into a furnace, which is what the Ministry believes clerks are for.

I fed the furnace. I am not proud of this, but I am honest about it, which is more than can be said for anyone above the fourth floor.

The grain numbers were impossible. Greyhollow's stores should have lasted until Thawmoot even in a bad year. Instead the ledgers showed spoilage — entire granaries reporting rot and frost-crack within days of each other, as though the cold had walked from building to building with a list. And always, always, the reports clustered near the river first, spreading outward in rings, like something dropped into water.

Like something dropped into water. Yes. I wrote that phrase in my private notebook, the one I kept between the pages of the *Codex of Municipal Ordinances*, where no supervisor would ever look, because no supervisor had ever read the Codex either.

Deputy Director Corvane summoned me on the fourth day of the failure. His office smelled of ink and lilies — he kept fresh lilies year-round, which in a starving city is its own kind of confession — and he did not ask me to sit.

"The saboteurs," he said, "have escalated."

"Sir?"

"The frost. The crop failure. This is not weather." He said it with the flat certainty of a man who has decided a thing and therefore requires no evidence for it. "Weather does not respect municipal boundaries. This pattern does. Someone is doing this deliberately, and they are doing it with—" he paused, choosing his word the way a man chooses a knife, "—unlicensed means."

Unlicensed means. Magic, in other words, though we had all agreed decades ago to call it something bureaucratic enough to be deniable.

"You'll draft the proclamation," Corvane said. "Sabotage of civic provisioning. Informant bounty doubled — bread ration doubled, actually, we're calling it a bounty so people feel heroic about informing on their neighbors rather than hungry." He almost smiled. "And you'll reopen the listener files. Every name. Every family with a history of it. If someone is working unlicensed means, they will need to *hear* things to work them. Find me listeners, Halvers."

I said yes, sir, in the tone that means *no* without costing anything, and went back to my desk, and drafted the proclamation, and hated every sentence of it, and wrote it beautifully anyway. That is the trick of surviving inside an institution: you learn to do the terrible thing well enough that they stop watching you, and you tell yourself you're doing it well so that you'll be positioned to do less of it later. Later. The great promise every functionary makes to his own conscience. My conscience and I had that arrangement going back years, and lately I had begun to suspect it was the conscience doing the surviving and not me.

That night I pulled the old listener files. Names, mostly dead or scattered. One charm seller on Cooper Lane flagged twice for "loitering with intent to observe hydrological anomalies." I looked at that phrase for a long time. Loitering with intent to observe hydrological anomalies. We had written a man into our records in the language of a disease.

I burned nothing that night. But I copied one page, and hid it, and told myself it was for later.

### Wren

I have watched people starve before. In the temple years, in the famine districts, we kept the rites going partly because the gods were supposed to prevent exactly this and partly because ritual is what you give people when you have nothing else to put in their hands. I know the choreography of hunger: first the queues get quiet, then they get organized, then they get dangerous, and somewhere in the middle of all that the government starts feeding them somebody else's name instead of bread.

The Ministry moved fast. Faster than I expected, and I had low expectations; I'd helped build that machine once, in my way, believing I was merciful. By the second week there were criers on every corner: *sabotage, sabotage, reward for information leading to the arrest of persons employing unlicensed means.* The bounty was bread. Of course it was bread. You don't pay informants in coin during a famine; coin doesn't keep children alive, and the Ministry understood hunger the way a surgeon understands flesh.

What struck me, walking the city, was how the frost chose. It ignored the rich quarter entirely — I checked, I walked up through Hillgate and the fountains there still played, absurdly, greenly, throwing water into air that had no business permitting it. The frost clung to the poor streets, the riverside wards, the tanneries and the tenements and the market. It spread outward from the water in perfect rings, and everything inside those rings grew quieter. Not colder. Quieter. Dogs stopped barking. Gulls left the bridges. Even the rats seemed to have taken some advice and gone.

Animals know things. That was the whole of my old faith, before I helped bury it: the beasts are the covenant's memory, and when the world goes wrong they leave first.

They were leaving. And Cyrus Amberdale stood in his little shop on Cooper Lane selling luck-knots to people who could no longer eat, and every night the animals gathered outside his window in the dark — I saw them, cats and crows and one enormous shaggy sow with intelligent eyes — gathered like mourners outside a house where someone important was dying.

"They're asking him," I murmured to myself, on the third such night, watching from across the lane with my collar up. "He hasn't said yes yet."

Because I knew the shape of it. I had read the confiscated rite-books before the Ministry burned most of them; I had helped burn some myself, God forgive me, mercy, I called it mercy. When the covenant stands, the beasts gather to their speaker and the speaker leads them, and there is only one place they would be pulling him toward. Only one place with a name old enough to survive in beast-memory when it had been scrubbed from every human book.

The Garden.

I went home and packed a bag I pretended was for ordinary reasons. Two blankets, dried apples, the rite-book I'd stolen back from my own bonfire twenty years ago. Then I sat with it between my knees and admitted what I'd known since the moment I walked into his shop asking for a charm against forgetting:

We were leaving soon. All three of us. And the frost was coming faster than my courage was.

### Cyrus

On the sixth day the frost reached my cellar, and the animals came to the door openly, in daylight, which is the one thing they had never done in twenty years of my careful, cowardly silence.

It started with the dog — the same brindled stray who'd broken my quiet back at the river, weeks ago now, another lifetime. He sat on my doorstep in the mild grey morning with frost climbing the wall behind him, and he did not speak this time. He just looked at me, and behind him the street filled: cats on the fences, crows on the eaves, a milk-cart horse straining against its harness to face my shop, pigeons down off the roofs in numbers that made the few pedestrians cross to the other side and hurry.

*Speaker,* the dog said at last, and his voice was thin, stretched, like a man calling across a widening ice. *It's taking the green places. It's taking the warm dens. The small ones are dying first.*

"I know," I said aloud, because there was no longer any point pretending otherwise, not with half the street's fauna watching me move my lips.

*You know where the door is,* the dog said. *She knows the road. The burning one knows the way, while he lasts.* A pause, and the whole assembled multitude of them leaned in — I felt it physically, like a change in pressure. *We are asking you, speaker. We are asking you to lead us out before the rings close.*

"And if I say no?"

The dog looked at me with the terrible patience animals have, the patience of creatures who have never once lied and therefore cannot comprehend why I might.

*You won't,* he said. *But we needed to hear you choose it.*

I closed the shop. I hung the CLOSED sign — the plain one, not the charming one with the painted wren on it; there was no charm left in anything — and I went upstairs, where Wren was already standing with her packed bag beside the door, wearing the expression of a woman who has been ready for six days and is trying not to look smug about it.

"The frost hit the cellar," I said.

"I know. I felt it through the floor."

"The animals want me to lead them to the Garden."

"I know that too."

"Of course you do." I sat down heavily on the stairs. Twenty years of hiding, and here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain: stay, and watch the rings close over everything alive in this city, hiding successfully in a dead world. Or go, and be hunted by the best-informed police apparatus on the continent, across country that was freezing from the inside out, guided by a dying god and a woman who used to burn books like mine.

"There's a third option," Wren offered. "We wait for the Ministry to connect the loitering-with-hydrological-anomalies dots and arrest us in our sleep. It's very restful, comparatively."

"Halvers," I said. "The clerk. He let me walk. He asked me questions he already knew the answers to and then he let me walk."

"Or he's building a case slowly."

"Or," I said, "he's a tired man in a machine that's starting to eat its own hands, and he wanted to see if I'd lie to his face." I rubbed my eyes. "I did. Beautifully. I keep thinking about that."

Wren crossed the room and sat beside me on the stairs, shoulder to shoulder, the way you sit with someone when the conversation has gotten too large for eye contact. Outside, the frost crept visibly along the gutter — you could watch it move, a slow white tide against the iron, patient as bureaucracy.

"When?" she asked.

"Soon," I said. "Before the rings close. Before the roads—" I stopped. Through the window, far off toward the river, I heard it: hoofbeats, many, unhurried, the particular rhythm of riders who are not chasing anyone yet because they already know where everyone is.

Wren heard it too. She stood, and picked up her bag, and for a moment neither of us moved, two people listening to the sound of their ordinary lives ending at a canter.

"Well," she said. "Blizzard's coming in tonight, if the clouds mean what they usually mean. Bad weather for riders."

"Good weather for us, you mean."

"I mean," said Wren, "that we should decide in the next hour whether we're travelers or prisoners, because the sky is about to make the choice easier for one of those options and much harder for the other."

Down in the cellar, faint through the floorboards, something warm flickered — I felt it the way you feel a hand take yours in the dark. Vessel-of-Embers, holding on. Spending himself. Keeping the door propped open.

The hoofbeats turned onto Cooper Lane.
