## 10. Beasts at the Gate

I want it on the record that I did not invite them.

Whatever happens next — whatever the Ministry writes in its ledgers, whatever Wren says with that look she has, the one that means *you knew this would happen and you let it* — I want someone, someday, to write down that a man can spend twenty years being small on purpose and still have the world decide otherwise.

It started, as most catastrophes in my life do, with the dog.

Not the same dog from the market — that one had gone wherever stray dogs go when they've delivered their message, which is to say nowhere good. This was a different animal entirely: a grey-muzzled sheepdog with one clouded eye, standing in my doorway at dawn three days after the frost began eating the riverbank, looking at me with her good eye as though I were late for an appointment I didn't know I had.

*"Speaker,"* she said. Her voice was old and gravelly, like a cart wheel that needed oiling. *"They're coming."*

"Who's coming?" I asked, because apparently I had given up on pretending I couldn't hear talking animals. Twenty years of discipline, undone by a fox in my cellar. Vessel-of-Embers slept upstairs now, in front of my hearth, taking up more room than a fox should be able to take up, the way dying gods do.

*"All of them,"* said the sheepdog, and turned, and trotted off down the frozen street without another word, leaving me to stand in my doorway in my nightshirt feeling like a man who has just been told his own funeral will be well attended.

*

By midmorning I could feel them before I saw them.

I don't know how else to describe it. It was like the moment before a storm breaks, when the air goes thick and your teeth ache — except it wasn't the air, it was the ground, humming faintly through the soles of my boots. A vibration. A gathering. When I pressed my palm flat against the floorboards of the shop, I felt it there too, rising up through the foundations of Greyhollow itself, as if the whole city had become a drumhead and something enormous had begun walking across it.

Wren felt nothing. She stood at my counter grinding dried nettle for a fever charm — we'd been selling fever charms all week; half of Greyhollow had taken sick since the frost came, and the other half was hoarding candles against a dark that arrived earlier every day — and she watched me crouch there with my hand on the floor like a madman and said, "Cyrus."

"They're coming," I told her. "The animals."

"I know." She kept grinding. "The sparrows left the temple district yesterday. All of them, at once. Halvers' people noticed. There's a bounty notice going up this afternoon — I heard it from the baker's wife, who hears everything." She set down the mortar and looked at me, and her face did the thing it does where all the softness drains out of it and what's left is something hard and old and very tired. "You need to understand what's about to happen outside those walls. Because whatever it is, they'll say you called them."

"Did I?"

"No." She said it quickly, and then, more honestly: "Probably not. But it won't matter. The Ministry doesn't deal in probably."

Vessel-of-Embers stirred by the hearth. The fox-god had grown thinner every day since we'd crossed into the deep cold — each mile of winter cost him something, he'd admitted once, in the flat tone of a man reading his own diagnosis aloud — but when he lifted his head, his eyes still burned banked-orange, embers under ash. *"They are not coming because you called, signatory,"* he said. *"They are coming because there is nowhere else. You are the last lit window in a very dark house."*

"That's a terrible thing to say to a man before breakfast."

"It is a true thing. Truth rarely keeps convenient hours." He rose, stretched, winced. "Come and see them. They have walked a long way to see you, and some of them will not survive the waiting."

*

We went up through the tannery district, past the smoke-houses where the workers stared at us — at Wren's brand half-hidden under her scarf, at me, the charm seller who everyone in Greyhollow knew was harmless, which is the most dangerous reputation a man can have — and out through the Dung Gate, which is exactly as pleasant as it sounds, and up the low rise beyond the walls where the city's dead had been buried back when burying people outside the walls was allowed.

I stopped walking.

Wren stopped beside me. For once, she said nothing at all.

The fields below the rise, all the way to the frozen river and past it, all the way to the grey line of the thorn-country on the horizon, were full of animals.

I have tried, in the days since, to find words for it, and I keep failing, so let me simply list what I saw, because the list itself is the only honest account: wolves, standing calmly among sheep, and the sheep unafraid. Deer in such numbers that the frost-crusted wheat beneath them had been trampled to chaff. Foxes, dozens, their coats dull with hunger. Bears, two of them, sitting on their haunches like pensioners waiting for a bus. Crows beyond counting, a black shimmer over the whole eastern field, and beneath the crows, rats — and I want you to understand that I am a shopkeeper, and my feelings about rats are commercially traditional — rats sitting in neat rows beside the cats who would ordinarily have eaten them, both species watching me with the same expression.

Waiting. Every one of them, waiting. The silence was the worst part. Thousands of animals, and not a sound. No barking, no birdsong, no lowing. Just breath steaming in the cold air, thousands of small clouds rising toward a sky the color of dirty wool, and every pair of eyes — brown, gold, black, the flat mirror-eyes of the geese standing in the shallows of the half-frozen millpond — fixed on the rise where I stood.

On me.

"There must be ten thousand," Wren breathed.

"More." My voice came out strange. "I can hear all of them. All at once." And I could — that was the terrible wonder of it. Not words, mostly. Not yet. But a pressure, a tide of wanting, the way you can stand near a crowd and feel the murmur of it without making out a single sentence. Except this murmur had a shape, and the shape was my name — not *Cyrus*, they didn't know that name, but *speaker*, *speaker*, *speaker*, rolling through ten thousand minds like a stone dropped in a well.

A stag came forward out of the deer. Old, gray about the muzzle, one antler broken off at the base, scarred across the shoulder where a wolf or a snare or a hard life had marked him. He climbed the rise with the unhurried dignity of a creature who has decided that fear is a luxury, and stopped six feet from me, and bowed his head.

*"Speaker."* His voice was deep and slow, like tree roots growing. *"We have come to ask you a question."*

"I'm —" I cleared my throat. Somewhere behind me I heard Wren sit down carefully in the snow, which I think was her version of screaming. "Ask."

*"The gods are waking, and they are waking wrong, and the winter is their sickness spilling over into the world. We have known this in our bones since the first hard frost. The beasts of the field and the birds of the air and the fish under the ice — we all know it, the way you know a storm is coming in your joints."* He raised his head, and his dark eye held mine, and I swear to you the eye was wet. *"Our question is this: is it true? Are you the one? The last signer? Can you lead us to the Garden?"*

And here is the thing I could not explain to him, and cannot fully explain to you, and have stopped trying to explain to myself: I wanted to lie. Lying is my oldest skill. I have lied to Ministry clerks and tax assessors and neighbors and, most faithfully and consistently, to myself, for twenty years, and I am good at it, and in that moment every instinct I possessed screamed at me to say *no, you've got the wrong man, I sell luck-knots, I'm nobody.*

But ten thousand pairs of eyes were on me, and the tide of them pressed against my mind, and underneath the word *speaker* I could feel the other things they carried — the hunger, the cold, the young ones already dead in the drifts, the fish gasping under ice that would not thaw, the whole suffering animal weight of a world going wrong — and I found that lying to them was like trying to lie to the sea.

"Yes," I said.

The word came out of me like a tooth coming out. It hurt, and then there was a strange lightness where it had been.

The murmur changed. I felt it move through the fields like wind through wheat — *he says yes, he says yes, the speaker says yes* — and ten thousand animals made a sound I had never heard before and hope never to hear again, because it was too much: relief and grief and joy all at once, a sound like the whole world exhaling.

Then the stag spoke again, and the joy went out of the air.

*"Then lead us, speaker. Lead us to the Garden, before the winter takes what remains."*

*

Here is what I knew about the Garden, at that point: almost nothing. A word from a fox-god. A direction, roughly north and east, into the thorn-country that no map in Greyhollow bothered to draw because nothing lived there worth taxing. A story Wren told in fragments, reluctantly, usually at night, usually after she'd had enough of my questions and answered one just to buy silence.

And here is what I did not know: how to feed ten thousand animals through a blighted wasteland in the middle of an unnatural winter. How to keep wolves and lambs from remembering what wolves do to lambs. How to move a living river of creatures across country where the Ministry had riders, and informants paid in bread, and gallows that had been getting steady work all season.

"You can't," Wren said, when I'd come down off the rise and told her what I'd agreed to. We stood apart from the animals, near the wall, our breath steaming between us. "Cyrus. Listen to me. You cannot march ten thousand beasts across the thorn-country. The Ministry will call it an invasion. They'll call *you* a warlock leading a beast-army at the gates of a loyal city, and every frightened soul in Greyhollow will believe them, because frightened souls always believe the story that lets them keep their fires lit."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you just said yes to a stag."

"He asked if I was the one. What was I supposed to say?" I heard my own voice crack and hated it. "No? Look at them, Wren. Look at them!" I gestured at the fields, at the patient, starving multitude. "There's a bear over there sitting next to a goat. A *bear*. Do you know what that costs a bear? They came here because there was nowhere else. Because someone — some clerk with a ledger and a warrant — broke the covenant that used to hold the world together, and everything alive has been paying the interest ever since, and now the debt's come due and the collector is winter itself, and I'm the last name on the contract." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "My mother sold buttons. Buttons, Wren. How did her son end up owing a debt to creation?"

She was quiet for a while. Below us, the stag watched, patient as stone.

"When I was a priestess," she said finally, "before —" she touched the brand through her scarf, didn't finish the sentence — "the elders taught us that the covenant was never really a contract. That was the Ministry's word for it, because the Ministry thinks everything is a contract. The old rites called it a *promise*. And a promise isn't a debt, Cyrus. Nobody collects a promise. Somebody keeps one." She looked at me, and for a moment the hardness went out of her face and I saw the girl she must have been, before. "You said yes because you couldn't do anything else. That's not a debt. That's just who you are. It took you twenty years to admit it, but there it is."

"And what do I do with who I am?"

"The same thing anyone does with an impossible task." She pulled her scarf up against the wind. "One step at a time, and lie to yourself about the odds. Come on. If we're doing this, we start tonight, before the Ministry's riders get here. And Cyrus —" she glanced back at the fields, at the black shimmer of the crows, at the bears and the goats and the patient, starving multitude — "we're going to need the fox. Whatever strength he has left. I'm sorry. But we will."

*

That night I stood at my shop window and looked out at the dark, and the dark looked back with ten thousand eyes.

Word had spread through the city faster than I'd thought possible — the baker's wife again, presumably, or maybe just the raw fact of ten thousand animals visible from every rooftop in Greyhollow. People had come out to the walls all afternoon to gawk. Some had thrown bread down to the wolves, which tells you everything hopeful and foolish about people. Others had run to the Ministry, which tells you everything else.

Through the glass I could see lanterns moving along the wall-top. More lanterns than usual. And down the frozen street, a knot of men in grey coats, Ministry grey, standing in the snow and looking up at my windows, not approaching, not yet, just — watching. Taking notes. Building the case one careful line at a time.

In the cellar, Vessel-of-Embers lay curled so small now that I could have held him in two hands. When I brought him water he drank a little and said, *"Tomorrow, then."*

"Tomorrow," I agreed. "North and east. Into the thorns."

*"Into the thorns,"* he echoed, and there was something in his voice I pretended not to recognize, because recognizing it would have meant admitting what it would cost him, and I wasn't ready. I am not sure I ever was.

I banked the fire, and sat down in the dark with my back against the door, listening to the city breathe and the fields beyond it breathe, two great lungs of the world, and somewhere out in that multitude a wolf began to sing — low, mournful, rising — and one by one, across the frozen miles, the others answered, until the whole night rang with it, and inside the walls dogs began answering too, and then, God help me, so did I, or wanted to, some old thing in my chest straining toward a note I didn't know I'd lost.

At dawn, a rider in grey came down my street at a canter, and did not slow down.

He stopped at my door, dismounted, and hammered on the wood hard enough to wake the dead, and when I opened it he thrust a folded paper at me — heavy stock, official seal, the works — and said, in the bored voice of a man delivering a hundred of these a day:

"Cyrus Amberdale. By order of the Ministry of Order, you are summoned to appear before the magistrate within the hour, to answer charges of unlawful assembly, sedition, and traffic with proscribed powers." He glanced past me, at the fields, at the multitude, and for just a moment his professional boredom slipped and I saw the fear underneath. "Bring a coat," he added, almost kindly. "It's a long walk to the magistrate's, and the roads are watched."

Behind me, upstairs, Wren began quietly pulling up floorboards.

The paper trembled in my hand. Out past the walls, ten thousand throats waited on my answer, and the winter leaned in close to hear it, and I understood, finally and completely, that the man who had spent twenty years hiding in a charm shop was done existing — that whatever walked out that door next would have to be someone new, someone enormous, someone I had no practice being.

I put on my coat.
