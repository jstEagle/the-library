## 11. Halvers' Choice

There are three kinds of orders in the Ministry of Order. The kind you follow, the kind you pretend to follow while doing something easier instead, and the kind that arrives on cream-colored paper with a wax seal and a signature you've only ever seen at the bottom of things that made your stomach hurt.

The order to arrest the charm seller was the third kind.

*Detain Cyrus Amberdale, proprietor, premises on Lantern Row, for questioning regarding suspected auditory deviance. Discretion advised. Report directly to Undersecretary Voss.*

Discretion advised. That's Ministry for *don't let anyone see what we're about to do to him.* I'd carried enough of those orders to know the grammar by heart. I'd written some of them myself, back when I still believed the paperwork meant something other than what it did.

I sat at my desk with the order in front of me and did not move for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, in the records vaults, a junior clerk was dropping something heavy and swearing about it with the creativity of the very young. Outside my one narrow window, Greyhollow was freezing in ways that frost had no business freezing — ice climbing the walls of buildings from the ground up, like the cold had changed its mind about which direction it came from.

I should have gone straight to Lantern Row. That was the job. That had always been the job: find the thing the Ministry feared, put it in a room, ask it questions until it stopped being a thing and became a confession.

Instead I went down to the vaults.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about working for a regime built on the principle that magic is a lie: you spend your whole career filing evidence that it isn't.

Eleven years I've been a clerk of the Second Bureau, Prayers and Petitions Division. Eleven years of cataloguing answered prayers as "coincidence, mass" or "coincidence, weather-related" or my personal favorite, "coincidence, unexplained." There's a whole drawer of those. Coincidence, Unexplained. It's the fullest drawer in the building. Some nights I used to stand in front of it and think: *this drawer is the truest history of this city anyone has ever written,* and then I would go home and drink cheap wine and not think about it anymore.

But lately the drawer had been overflowing. Rain that wouldn't stop over the Tanner's Quarter — three weeks now, falling on six streets and nowhere else. A woman in Copperlane whose dead son sat at her table every evening for four days before dissolving into river water. A baker whose ovens lit themselves and wouldn't go out until they'd burned the whole shop flat. Every incident report crossed my desk, because every incident report was a prayer first, and tracing prayers back to their askers was my particular, tedious gift.

And every prayer that had come true badly in the last month traced back, eventually, to the same place.

Not to a person. To a date.

The ninth day of Deepwinter, forty-one years ago.

I found it the way you find most important things: by accident, while looking for something else. I'd pulled the founding-era files because Voss wanted a summary of "historical precedent for auditory deviance" — which is Ministry for *find me a reason the charm seller deserves what we're going to do to him* — and historical precedent meant the Covenant Trials, and the Covenant Trials meant boxes nobody had opened since before I was born.

The box was labeled in faded iron-gall ink: **ACCORD OF ROOT AND RIVER — SUPPRESSED MATERIALS — DO NOT CIRCULATE.**

I want to be honest here, since honesty seems to be happening to me whether I like it or not: I hesitated. Not out of virtue. Out of fear. Fear is the Ministry's real currency; bread is just what they pay informants with. I stood in that cold vault with the box in my hands and I thought about my pension. I thought about my sister in the country and the money I send her. I thought about how easy my life has been precisely because I have never once opened a box marked DO NOT CIRCULATE.

Then I thought about the woman in Copperlane, who had asked the old rites — quietly, in her kitchen, the way her grandmother taught her — to see her son once more. And the thing that answered had worn her son's face the way a thief wears a coat.

Somebody had done that. Somebody had broken whatever used to keep the answers honest, and then spent forty-one years filing the wreckage under *coincidence.*

I opened the box.

* * *

You expect forbidden history to be dramatic. Sealed letters, dying confessions, a map to something buried. Mostly it's minutes. Meeting minutes. The Accord of Root and River had been negotiated over eleven days between representatives of the human settlements, the beast-speakers' conclave, and — this part was written in a hand that shivered even on the page — "the Undying Who Keep the Springs." Three parties. One covenant. I read the terms twice because I didn't believe them the first time.

The gods kept the seasons turning and the rivers clean and the dead gently sorted. The beasts kept the wild places open and carried messages between realms and — this was the clause that made me sit down on a crate of forty-year-old dust — *lent their ears to those humans born able to hear them, so no prayer would go unanswered and no speaker would stand alone.* And the humans kept the gardens, tended the boundary shrines, and swore — signed, in blood, names listed — never to seal the ways shut.

There were nine signatories for the humans. Nine names. Eight were followed by dates of death, entered in a later hand, respectful, almost tender.

The ninth name was Amberdale. And beside it, where a death date should have been, someone had written: *line continues. See Addendum K.*

Addendum K was the last document in the box, and it was not minutes. It was an after-action report. I recognized the format — we still use it. Cold, procedural, numbered. It described, in the flat voice of men proud of their tidiness, an operation conducted in Deepwinter forty-one years past. A manufactured drought. Poisoned shrine-wells, blamed on beast-speakers. A staged desecration — the report actually used the phrase *theatrical blasphemy* — designed to turn the settlements against the conclave. And then, when the signatories gathered to renegotiate the covenant in good faith, the doors of the meeting-house were sealed and burned, and the rite inscribed on its foundation stones — the rite that anchored the gods to this side of the world — was chiseled out and carted away.

The founders of the Ministry of Order did not outlaw magic because magic was a lie.

They outlawed it because it was a witness.

The gods hadn't abandoned us. We'd bricked them into a starving half-realm and posted clerks at the door for two generations to file their knocking under *coincidence, unexplained.* And now the door was rotting through, and everything coming through it was mad with hunger, and the Ministry's answer was to arrest a charm seller on Lantern Row because he might be able to hear them coming.

I read Addendum K three times. Then I took off my gloves, which was stupid, because the vault was freezing, and I held the pages for a while without reading anything, just feeling the paper, as if it might tell me it wasn't true.

It didn't say anything. Paper never does. That's the whole trouble with paper. It just waits for you to decide what you are.

* * *

I took two documents up to my office. Everyone does this — carries files upstairs "for review" — so nobody looked at me twice, which is the most damning sentence I can write about the place I've worked for eleven years.

The first was Addendum K. The operation report. Forty-one years of winter, famine, twisted prayers, drowned children wearing their own faces — all of it downstream of that one numbered list of tidy atrocities, authorized by signatures I knew. Two of them still sign things. One of them signs my orders.

The second was the covenant itself. The original terms, with the nine names. I copied the ninth name onto a scrap of my own paper — *Amberdale, line continues* — and then I sat there holding both documents and understanding, with terrible clarity, that I was holding a choice shaped exactly like two pieces of paper.

Burn one. Keep one.

If I burned the covenant, the arrest order stayed simple. Cyrus Amberdale, auditory deviant, detain and question. I could walk to Lantern Row tonight and do my job and sleep the way I've always slept, which is to say poorly but without nightmares about fire. The Ministry would grind on. The winter would deepen. The drawer marked *Coincidence, Unexplained* would fill until the drawer broke, and then they'd get a bigger drawer, because that is what institutions do instead of thinking.

If I burned Addendum K, the crime disappeared. No proof the founders manufactured the breaking. No proof the war on magic began as a cover-up. Just my word against forty-one years of official history, and my word, I am aware, is worth roughly one informant's loaf of bread.

But if I burned the covenant —

I kept trying to make the arithmetic work the other way. I'm good at arithmetic; it's most of my job. And the numbers kept refusing. Because the covenant wasn't just history. It was a *terms sheet.* It said what the world was supposed to look like when it worked. And somewhere out there, a man who sold luck-knots to grieving widows was walking around with the last living blood of the people who signed it, and the Ministry wanted him in a windowless room before he could figure out what his own name meant.

Voss thinks Amberdale is a threat. Voss is right, in the way that a man who burns a bridge is right that the far shore is now inaccessible.

I fed Addendum K into the stove. It caught quickly, the way old paper does, eager almost, and I watched the numbered list of atrocities curl and blacken and become indistinguishable from any other fire, and I felt — I'll admit it — nothing like relief. Relief implies the matter is settled. What I felt was more like the moment you step off a ledge and discover you're committed to finding out whether you can fly.

Then I folded the covenant into quarters and put it inside my shirt, against my chest, where it sat like a coal that refused to burn down.

* * *

Undersecretary Voss received me at the ninth hour, which is when he receives everyone, because Voss believes unpredictability is a vice. He's a neat man with neat hands and eyes like ledger lines, and he listened to my preliminary report on the Amberdale surveillance with his fingertips steepled, and when I finished he said:

"You've read the historical file I sent you?"

"I have."

"And?"

"It confirms auditory deviance clusters along bloodlines," I said. Which was true. It also confirmed approximately ten thousand other things I did not say, and Voss looked at me for a moment longer than he needed to, the way a man checks a ledger line he half-suspects doesn't sum.

"The arrest order stands," he said. "Tonight. Quietly. If he resists—" He made a small gesture, the one that means *discretion,* the one that means the rest.

"He won't resist," I heard myself say. "He's a charm seller. They're trained to please."

Voss smiled at that, a thin administrative smile, and dismissed me, and I walked out of his office with the covenant crackling softly against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I understood that I had just lied to the face of the man who signs my wages, and that it had been easy.

That frightened me more than the fire did. Lies should cost something. Mine haven't cost me anything in years, and I'd always told myself that was because I never told any big ones.

Now I knew better. I'd just never had a lie worth telling before.

* * *

I didn't go to Lantern Row.

I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of my own skull, which is rapidly becoming the only archive in Greyhollow I trust. I did not go to Lantern Row. Instead I went home, and I took out the covenant, and I read the ninth name again by candlelight, and then I did the single most reckless thing of my eleven-year career, which was this:

I wrote a warning.

Not much of one. I'm a clerk; brevity is my sacrament. A line about the arrest order. A line about discretion meaning worse than questioning. A time. Unsigned. I addressed it the way you address something you don't want traced: no name, just *the charm shop, Lantern Row,* and I paid a street kid two coppers to leave it under the door before midnight, and I watched from across the square as a small grubby hand slid the paper into the gap, and I felt like a man watching himself fall and discovering, mid-air, that the view is better than the office.

Then I went back to my rooms and packed a bag, because I'm not a fool. A clerk who warns a target is a clerk with a short future, and the Ministry always finds out — not because it's clever, but because everyone informs on everyone, and the bread is good.

I sat by my cold window with my bag packed and the covenant against my chest and I waited to feel afraid.

What I felt instead was the strangest thing. The frost on my window had climbed all the way to the top pane, and in the moonlight the patterns didn't look like frost at all. They looked like branches. Like a garden seen through ice, waiting.

Somewhere across the city, a charm seller was either reading my note or already gone, and either way, tomorrow morning Voss would ask me why the shop was empty, and I would need to have an answer ready, or a horse.

I blew out the candle.

In the dark, with the frozen garden branching silently across the glass, I caught myself saying the ninth name out loud — just once, just to hear it — *Amberdale* — like a man testing whether a bridge will hold.

Then I picked up my bag, and left the lamp burning behind me for whoever came looking, which was as close to a confession as I intended to give anybody.
