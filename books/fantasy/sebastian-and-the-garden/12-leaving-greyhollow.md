## 12. Leaving Greyhollow

**Cyrus**

The blizzard came the way bad news always comes to Greyhollow: sideways, and all at once.

I had spent twenty years learning every trick of invisibility a man can buy, and none of them worked on weather. Snow piled against my shutters like a creditor who wouldn't leave. The Ministry riders would be slower in it — that was the one mercy — but so would we, and Vessel-of-Embers was in no condition to argue with drifts.

"Tell me again why we're doing this tonight," I said, packing dried fish into a satchel with the tenderness of a man defusing something.

Wren didn't look up from wrapping her hands in strips of wool. "Because Halvers let you walk out of that interrogation room, which means someone above him decided you were more useful followed than held. Because there are three men across the street pretending to repair a cart wheel that hasn't needed repairing since the last frost. And because," she nodded at the cellar door, where the fox lay curled around his own dwindling warmth, "he has maybe two days of winter left in him. Maybe three."

I hated when she was right. It happened often enough that I'd developed a whole liturgy of hating it.

The dog from the market had come back, too. He sat by the cellar stairs now like a hired guard, and when I looked at him he said, *The river says go north along the ice before it breaks. The river says hurry.* Dogs have no gift for suspense.

So we went. Hood up, lantern hooded darker, Wren's hand on my sleeve because she couldn't see well in whiteout and refused to admit it. The snow erased Greyhollow behind us street by street, which should have felt like freedom and instead felt like being unwritten. I kept thinking of the shop. Twenty years of luck-knots and fever-charms and small mercies, abandoned to whoever kicked the door in first.

*You can have a life or you can have a secret,* my mother used to say, though she said it about dancing with boys, not about treason. She'd have been furious. She'd have packed us sandwiches first.

Behind us, faint through the wind, a whistle. Then another, answering. Riders.

"Hurry," I told Wren, and felt absurd saying it to a woman already half-running.

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what they don't tell you about guilt: it weighs exactly as much as a memory, and you carry it in the same place, and after enough years you cannot tell which is which.

Cyrus walked ahead of me through the blizzard with the fox bundled against his chest like a stolen infant, and I watched the set of his shoulders and thought: *he doesn't know yet. He trusts me, and he doesn't know.*

Three men on the cart-wheel corner. I'd counted four, actually; the fourth was the one pretending not to count us. They wouldn't follow into the open country in this weather. The Ministry preferred its cruelty indoors, where the paperwork stayed dry.

We reached the river gate just past midnight. The ice groaned under the wind like an old man asked to move furniture. Cyrus knelt and put his bare hand flat on the frozen surface — fool, but a faithful fool — listening the way other people pray.

"She says the middle channel is thick," he reported. "She says stay off the eastern bend." A pause. His mouth twitched. "She says the Ministry dredged the western shallows for runaways two winters ago and never filled them back in."

"The river gossips."

"The river holds grudges. There's a difference." He stood, and for a moment the wind dropped, and I saw him clearly: a tired shopkeeper with snow in his lashes, carrying a dying god and a stranger's salvation and not one complaint he'd spoken aloud. My chest did something complicated and unhelpful.

We went out onto the ice.

Halfway across, the whistles found us again — closer now, three notes, the Ministry's signal for *fugitives sighted, take alive.* Take alive. That was new. When they'd posted the listener-bounties, the notices hadn't specified condition. I suppose even informants charge extra for damaged goods.

"Cyrus." I caught his arm. "If they take us, they'll take the fox. You understand what they do with—"

"I understand." His voice was very quiet and very steady, the voice of a man who has rehearsed his own courage in private for decades. "Keep walking."

And then, because secrets are currency and we were spending fast, and because if we died on this ice I did not want to die owing him, I said:

"I helped seal them away."

He stopped walking.

"The gods. Twenty-two years ago. I was nineteen and a novitiate of the Ember Rite, and the founders of the Ministry came to us with a story — that the gods were starving the world with their hunger, that severing them would heal both realms. And I believed it. Gods know why. I was young and the rites had grown cruel and I wanted them to mean something gentler." The words came out of me like teeth. "There were nine of us who laid the binding. Eight are dead. I ran before the ninth rite closed, and I've spent my whole life since telling myself it was mercy. That I saved them from something worse."

The wind screamed. Somewhere behind us, hooves on the embankment road.

Cyrus stood very still. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off. When he finally spoke, it wasn't anger. Somehow that was worse.

"The fox is dying because of that seal."

"Yes."

"And the river's grief, and the twisted prayers, and the child who came back wrong—" He stopped himself. I watched him stop himself, watched a man fold his fury small enough to fit inside a coat pocket. "You could have told me in the shop. In the chapel. Anywhere with walls."

"You'd have left me on the ice."

"Probably," he admitted. And then, horribly, gently: "But you told me here, where I can't. That's either cowardice or strategy, and I haven't decided which."

"It's arithmetic," I said. "A dead confessor is worth nothing to anyone."

Something moved in his face — not forgiveness, nothing so finished as that. Recognition, maybe. Two people standing in the same storm, holding the same kind of weight.

"Walk," he said again. "Confess the rest when we're not about to be shot at."

* * *

**Cyrus**

They shot at us. Of course they shot at us. The Ministry of Order had never once in my lifetime done anything as interesting as I feared, but they were reliable.

Arrows in a blizzard are mostly theater — the wind ate every one of them — but the sound made Vessel-of-Embers stir against my chest, and his voice came thin as smoke: *North. Past the drowned chapel. Do not stop for the riders, speaker. They are men. Men freeze. We do not have that luxury.*

"He's getting poetic," I told Wren. "That's a bad sign."

"It's a dying sign," she said, and put on more speed.

The far bank took us into scrub pine, and the pines took us from the riders' eyes, and for an hour there was only the storm and our breathing and the fox's heartbeat slowing against my ribs like a clock running down. I talked to him without words, the old silent speech, just warmth pushed outward. He answered once: *You spend yourself carelessly, signatory. Save it. You will need every hour of yourself where we are going.*

I didn't ask what he meant. I was learning that the Garden kept its prices close, like a moneylender, and I wasn't ready to see the ledger.

We stopped in a hollow beneath a leaning spruce, and Wren built a fire the way she did everything — efficiently, resentfully, perfectly. The dog flopped down beside the fox with a sigh that contained several opinions.

"They'll lose us till dawn," Wren said. "Horses won't hold a line in this. But they know the only road north, and they know we know it."

"So we don't take the road."

"So we take the river's road." She glanced at me sideways. Firelight did strange things to her face; it made the brand on her jaw look less like a wound and more like a keyhole. "Your river. Does she know the drowned chapel?"

I put my hand to the ground, to the water moving somewhere beneath the frost, and listened until my ears ached. *Yes,* said the river, in her long gray voice. *Yes. Tell the priestess the chapel remembers her. Tell her the water there has been waiting to forgive or to drown, and has not yet chosen.*

I decided not to translate that part.

"There's a chapel," I said. "Two days north. Off every map the Ministry keeps."

"Nothing is off every map," Wren said. "I should know. I helped burn most of them."

* * *

**Wren**

He didn't sleep. I knew because I didn't either, and because he sat with his back against the spruce holding the fox and watching the dark with the expression of a man doing sums he doesn't like.

Near dawn I gave up pretending and sat beside him. The fire had burned to a red patience.

"My turn to trade," he said, without preamble. "Fair's fair. You gave me your worst sin on the ice. Here's mine: I lied to Halvers. In that room. About everything. And it was easy." He turned his hands over, studying them as if they belonged to a stranger. "Twenty years I told myself my silence was virtue. Caution. Humility. And then a clerk with ink-stained fingers asks me questions and the lies come out of me smooth as water, and I realize I'm not humble. I'm practiced. There's a difference, and it's the same difference as yours."

"Between mercy and desertion."

"Between keeping a secret and building your whole self out of one." He laughed, short and without much humor. "My mother used to say I could talk a fish onto a cart. I thought she was complimenting me."

"Maybe she was. Maybe both things are true at once. That's usually how sins work — they're virtues that traveled too far." I fed a branch to the fire. "I believed sealing the gods away was mercy. I still believe mercy was the intention. The road between intention and result is where I've lived for twenty-two years. It's crowded there."

The fox opened one eye. Ember-colored, banked low, but open.

*Listen to them,* he murmured, to no one, to the storm, to whatever gods still bothered eavesdropping on fugitives. *Trading sins like coins because they think coin is all a soul is worth. Foolish children. Sins aren't currency. They're seeds. What you plant them in decides everything.*

Then he slept again, and neither of us spoke for a while, because we had both heard him, and because some truths arrive better in silence, like guests who take off their own boots.

* * *

**Cyrus**

Dawn came gray and merciless, and with it, distantly, the horns. Not the three-note signal anymore — a longer call, repeated, spreading east to west along the treeline. They'd brought dogs of their own. Real ones, deaf to my kind of speech, all nose and appetite.

"We have maybe an hour," Wren said, kicking snow over the fire. "Less, if their handlers are good."

The dog lifted his head and said, *Their dogs are good. But their dogs are also cold, and hungry, and their masters beat them, and one of them is mine from before.* He stood, shook the snow from his coat, and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as professional. *I will go be found. I will lead them along the eastern bend, where the river says the ice is rotten over the old dredge-cut. Dogs swim. Handlers swear. It buys you a day.*

"No," I said. "Absolutely not. That ice will kill—"

*It might,* he agreed placidly. *Most things might. You are going to save the world, speaker, or so the fox insists, and the world includes dogs who were beaten by the men who are chasing you. Let me spend myself where it counts. It is what beasts have always done for your kind. You simply were never permitted to know it.*

He pressed his broad head once against my knee — the first time any animal had touched me freely in twenty years, and it undid something in me I'd bricked over so thoroughly I'd forgotten the brick was there — and then he trotted away south into the pines, tail high, toward the horns.

I stood there like a man watching his own heart walk off on four legs.

"Cyrus." Wren's hand was on my elbow. "We have to move."

"I know." My voice came out wrecked. I cleared it. "I know. North. The chapel."

We went. Behind us the horns changed pitch — found scent, found dog — and then, minutes later, changed again to something sharper, angrier, and then to muffled shouting swallowed by wind, and then to nothing at all. I didn't look back. Looking back felt like a luxury purchased with the dog's courage, and I meant to pay attention to the bill.

By midday the blizzard broke apart into a hard clean cold, and the land opened northward into a whiteness so complete it looked manufactured. Somewhere under it ran the river, whispering directions. Somewhere ahead waited a drowned chapel that remembered Wren, and a god too weak to speak, and — if the fox's silences meant what I feared — a price already written down in a ledger I hadn't been allowed to read.

Vessel-of-Embers woke briefly in the crook of my arm and said, with the ghost of his old iron humor: *You travel well for a man who spent his life hiding, signatory.*

"I had practice," I said. "I'm hoping to retire from it."

*Mm. Hope is a beast too. Feed it carefully.* His eyes closed. *The chapel keeper watches for us. And, Cyrus Amberdale — the priestess. Whatever she tells you next, remember that shame makes liars of the honest. Ask her what she was running from before you decide what she is.*

And then he slept, and Wren walked beside me with her secrets stacked like cordwood, and the horns sounded once more, far behind and growing no closer, and I understood that we had not escaped the Ministry at all.

We had only gotten ahead of it. And ahead of us, in a chapel under the ice, something was waiting that frightened me more than any rider ever could — because the river, when I listened, had stopped giving directions.

She had started giving warnings.
