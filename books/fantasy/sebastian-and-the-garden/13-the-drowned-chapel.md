## 13. The Drowned Chapel

I had been a priestess for eleven years before the Ministry burned my temple, and in all that time I never once slept in a chapel I actually liked. The old rites demanded grandeur — vaulted ceilings, incense thick enough to chew, gods addressed from a respectful distance. Grandeur is easy. Grandeur is what you build when you're afraid of intimacy with the divine.

The chapel we found on the third night of flight was intimate to the point of drowning.

Cyrus spotted it first, or rather Vessel-of-Embers did and Cyrus translated, which is its own kind of miracle I still haven't gotten used to. The fox had been riding in the cart like a bundle of red rags for two days, spending itself thin to keep the Ministry's hounds confused about our trail, and when it finally lifted its head and said — through Cyrus, in that flat exhausted way of his that meant the words weren't his own — *water, roof, old stone*, we turned off the road without argument.

The chapel sat in a hollow below what had once been a village and was now a suggestion of chimneys. The river had come for it decades ago, patient as everything else in this country, and settled into its nave like a congregation that refused to leave after the service ended. Water stood knee-deep between the pews. The altar was an island. Stained glass survived in fragments along one wall, and the moonlight came through the broken saints in pieces — a blue hand here, a gold halo there, a face nobody could identify scattered across the flooded floor.

"Charming," I said, because someone had to.

"It's dry above the waterline," Cyrus said, which was true, and which was Cyrus all over: he heard the worst of what the world said and answered with the practical part of it.

We carried the cart's contents up onto the pew-backs, spread blankets, got a small fire going in a brazier Wren — I — had thought to pack, because eleven years as a priestess teaches you two things: how to keep a flame alive in bad conditions, and how to lie beautifully to men with authority. The Ministry taught me neither. They only taught me what happened when you stopped.

* * *

Vessel-of-Embers drank melted snow and slept by the fire like something ordinary, and for an hour I let myself believe we were just three fugitives in a ruin, and that the strangeness of the last weeks might pause long enough for me to sleep.

Then the fish came.

They arrived singly at first, out of the dark water beneath the pews — small silver things, minnows or young dace, rising to hang just under the surface where the firelight reached. They didn't behave like fish. Fish school, scatter, flicker. These arranged themselves. Six of them formed a line facing us. Then they moved, slowly, deliberately, and I watched Cyrus go very still beside me in the way he does when he's listening to something the rest of us can't hear yet.

"They're not talking," he murmured. "There's nothing to hear. It's just — arrangement. Position." His brow furrowed. "It's trying to spell something out and it doesn't have letters."

"A god?" I asked, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. Old habit. Priestesses learn early that your fear feeds the congregation faster than your faith does.

"I think so." He crouched at the water's edge, careful not to let his shadow fall across the fish. "A big one. Or — no." He winced. "A big one that's been made small."

I knew then, before anyone said it, who we were looking at. You don't forget the names you were raised on, even when the Ministry burns them out of the liturgy and replaces them with civic virtues. And there was only one power whose domain ran through this valley, whose chapel this had been before the drowning — though even then, even as a girl in white robes, I'd understood that the chapel wasn't built *for* the river-god. The chapel was built *by* people who wanted to feel they'd caught it. Gods don't live in buildings. Buildings are just where humans go to pretend the conversation is on their terms.

"The Merrow-of-the-Long-Bank," I said quietly. "The river itself."

Cyrus looked at me. "You know it?"

"I know its name. Everyone used to know its name. That was rather the point of names." I took off my boots, because some things you do properly or not at all, and stepped down into the flooded aisle. The water was cold enough to make my breath catch, but I knelt in it anyway, among the pews, in the ruins of a rite I hadn't performed in twenty years, and I gave the greeting of the old covenant — the one I'd helped bury, the one I'd told myself was mercy.

"Hail, keeper of the long bank. Hail, old neighbor. We are strangers at your door, and we ask leave to shelter."

The fish scattered.

For a horrible moment I thought I'd done it wrong — that the words had rotted in my mouth over the years, that the god had heard the seal-breaking in my history the way you can hear a lie in a voice you know too well. Then every fish in the chapel rose at once, hundreds of them, the whole drowned nave suddenly silver underneath, and the reflections came.

That's the part I will never be able to explain to anyone who wasn't there. The water went mirror-still — impossibly still, given the cold and the current that must have been moving somewhere beneath us — and in it we saw not the ceiling, not the broken saints, but the road.

* * *

It showed us the way north.

Not as a map, exactly. Reflections don't work like maps. It showed us images that followed one another like beads on a string: a ford where two dead oaks leaned together over the water; a ridge of black thorn against a sky the color of dishwater; a valley floor where frost lay in patterns too regular to be natural, spirals and lattices, winter written in a hand; and then — at the end of the string of images, held longer than the others, glowing faintly green at its center — a ring of ice around a heart of impossible summer.

"The Garden," Cyrus breathed.

Beside him, Vessel-of-Embers had woken. The fox stood on the pew-back with its ears forward and its ribs showing through its coat, and it made a sound I had never heard from it — not speech, not quite, but a low keening, the sound of a creature seeing home from very far away.

The reflections shifted. The image of the Garden faded, and in its place came others, slower, graver.

We saw the ford again, and this time riders crossed it — grey cloaks, Ministry steel, lanterns swinging. We saw the thorn country, and in it shapes that moved wrong, that came apart as they walked, beast-shapes unraveling like rope ends. We saw frost climbing a stone gate. And then the water showed us a figure standing before that gate — a man-shaped absence, a silhouette with the details scrubbed out of it, smooth where a face should be — and the fish began to die.

Not dramatically. Not in blood and thrashing. They simply rolled over, one by one, silver bellies up, drifting in the still water like coins dropped in a well, and the reflection dimmed with each one, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the god was paying for every image it showed us out of whatever remained of itself.

"Stop," I said. "Stop, you're killing yourself, we've seen enough—"

One more image rose. The largest yet. It filled the whole nave: the gate of the Garden seen close, ice-sheathed, carved with three interlocking rings — god, beast, human, the covenant-sign I'd been branded for betraying — and above the gate, in letters of frost that formed and reformed because the god had no voice left to speak them, a single phrase:

*A TOLL IS ASKED.*

And beneath it, smaller, almost gentle:

*IT CANNOT BE PAID TWICE. IT CANNOT BE REFUSED. KNOW BEFORE YOU KNOCK.*

Then the water shivered, broke, and went ordinary — black, rippling, full of nothing but moonlight and dead minnows — and the presence that had filled the chapel like pressure behind a dam withdrew so completely that the silence afterward rang.

* * *

Cyrus pulled me out of the water. I hadn't realized my hands had gone numb until he wrapped them in his and I felt how warm his were, how badly they shook.

"You know what it meant," he said. "The toll. You knew before it finished showing us."

I wanted to lie to him. I want that noted, for honesty's sake: I wanted it the way a thirsty woman wants a well she knows is poisoned. Twenty years of practice, and lying to Cyrus Amberdale would have been easy, because he wants so badly to believe people are decent that he meets them more than halfway.

But I'd spent the whole day watching him feed half his supper to a starving god, and I'd spent the whole night kneeling in freezing water apologizing to a river for my part in its imprisonment, and there is a limit to how much hypocrisy a person can carry before it starts to cost more than the truth.

"The old covenant had terms," I said. "Everything sacred does. When it was first sworn — god, beast, human, three voices, one binding — the signatories gave things. A god gives dominion. A beast gives its wildness, its freedom to be only itself. And a human—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "A human signatory gives their memory of everyone bound to them by love. That was always the price. That was why there were so few willing to sign, and why those who did were honored like martyrs. You don't get to hold the covenant together and keep the reasons you'd want to."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark water, the last of the dead fish drifted against a pew leg.

"And the gate takes that toll," he said. "From whoever reforges it."

"If the warden holds to the original terms. Yes."

"From a living signatory." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "And I'm the last living signatory."

"You don't know that the warden will demand the full price. Terms can be negotiated. There were always dispensations, mercies—"

"Wren." He said my name gently, the way you set something fragile down. "I sell luck-knots. I have spent twenty years making sure no one noticed me, and I'm good at it, and the one thing I've learned is that powers like this don't offer discounts. They offer exact change." He looked toward the water, toward the drowned altar, toward the place where a god had just spent its last strength spelling out a warning in dying fish. "It didn't show us that to frighten us. It showed us because it couldn't speak, and it wanted us to walk in with our eyes open. That's a courtesy. That's a god being kind with its final coin." His jaw tightened. "So yes. I know what it meant."

"And you're going anyway."

He didn't answer right away. He looked instead at Vessel-of-Embers, curled again by the brazier, watching us with eyes like banked coals. Then at the water. Then, briefly, unbearably, at me.

"There's a dog in Greyhollow," he said, "who talked to me for the first time in years because the river told it to find me. There are animals outside the walls right now waiting for me to lead them somewhere they only know in their bones. There's frost coming up out of the ground in spirals, and a fox who's dying by inches keeping our trail clean, and a clerk back in the city who I think might be worth saving if anyone saves him first." He laughed, short and without much humor. "I spent twenty years pretending to be ordinary, Wren. It turns out the pretense was the most expensive thing I ever bought. I can't afford it anymore. So yes. I'm going anyway."

I should have argued. Part of me, the priestess part, the part trained in disputation and doctrine, stood ready with a dozen counterarguments about sacrifice and necessity and the sin of despair dressed up as duty. But I had helped seal the gods away because I believed it was mercy, and I had watched mercy curdle for twenty years, and I had just knelt in the ruins of everything I'd broken while a river killed its own fish to warn us honestly.

So instead of arguing, I sat down next to him on the wet pew, shoulder to shoulder, and said the only true thing left in my mouth:

"Then I'm coming with you. All the way to the gate. And when you come out the other side—" I made myself say it, made the words real by giving them air, "—when you come out the other side and you don't know me, I'm going to tell you everything. Every story. Starting with tonight, and the fish, and how brave you pretended not to be."

He smiled at that — tired, crooked, real. "Pretended?"

"Don't ruin the eulogy, Amberdale."

Above us, through the broken saints, the moon kept moving. Below us, in the black water, the last silver belly of the last messenger-fish drifted slowly toward the altar, coming to rest at its base like an offering left by no one, for a god too weak to receive it.

We slept in shifts after that. I dreamed of frost writing itself across a gate, and woke before dawn with Cyrus already awake, already packing, already listening to the fox describe the road ahead in a voice gone thin as paper.

Three days to the ford, Vessel-of-Embers said through him. Then the thorns.

Neither of us said the other thing we were both thinking. Neither of us had to.

Three days, and then the country where the beasts were coming apart — and beyond it, a gate that would take from my friend everything he was, and give back only the world.
