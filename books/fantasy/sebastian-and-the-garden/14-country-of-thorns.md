## 14. Country of Thorns

I had always assumed that if I ever heard a thousand voices at once, it would be at a market on festival day, and it would be mostly complaints about the price of eels.

Instead it was the thorn country, three days past Greyhollow's walls, and the thousand voices were dying.

They didn't speak in words, most of them. Words are what beasts use when they want something from you specifically — *feed me*, *open the door*, *the river says listen*. What I heard now was older than words: the raw hum of souls under strain, like ropes creaking before they part. A hedgehog's soul sounds like a small dry rustle. A crow's is all edges. A deer's is a long low note you feel in your back teeth. And every one of them, across the whole grey sweep of the borderlands, was fraying — unraveling thread by thread as the winter reached deeper into things winter has no business reaching.

"Stop walking," I said, for the fourth time that morning.

Wren stopped. She'd gotten good at stopping without asking why, which I suspected was a skill she'd learned somewhere unpleasant. "Which one is it now?"

"The hares." I pressed my palm against my sternum, as though I could muffle the sound from inside. "There's a warren under the old orchard wall, maybe half a mile east. They're... going out. Not dead yet. Going *out*."

"We can't dig up every warren between here and the Garden," she said gently. Which was true, and which was also the kind of sentence that should be illegal to say aloud.

"I know."

"You know, and you're still looking at the orchard."

"I'm still looking at the orchard," I admitted.

Vessel-of-Embers limped up beside me and leaned his thin weight against my boot. He'd been doing that more often — leaning, borrowing warmth he no longer generated enough of himself. When he spoke, his voice had the crackle of a fire burned down to coals. "The hares know what is happening to them, charm-seller. They would tell you not to come, if coming cost you nothing. But it costs. Everything costs, now. That is what the breaking meant."

"That's a terrible comfort."

"I am a god," he said. "Comfort was never my office. I was embers. I was the warmth left after the burning — the thing that says the fire happened, and mattered, and may happen again." He looked east toward the orchard with eyes gone dull as old pennies. "Go to your hares if you must. But understand that each mile you spend on mercy is a mile I do not have left to spend on the path."

That was the arithmetic of our journey, and nobody had put it to me so plainly before. I stood there hating him a little for it, and loving him entirely, and knowing both feelings were correct.

We went east to the orchard. Of course we did. I dug the warren open with my hands while Wren kept watch on the road, and forty hares poured past me into the deep burrows where the frost hadn't reached, and one old doe touched her nose to my wrist and said, in the wordless way, *you smell like the Garden, garden-man, don't let it take too much of you* — which made no sense, because none of them could know about the price, and I hadn't told anyone, and I filed the moment away in the growing drawer of things I refused to think about.

Then we walked west again, back to the road, having spent an hour and a piece of Vessel-of-Embers I couldn't see but could feel, the way you feel a tooth go quiet before it goes bad.

* * *

The thorn country earned its name honestly. The Ministry's maps called it the Border Marches, which was the way bureaucrats described anything they'd decided not to govern. Once there had been villages here — we passed the bones of two by noon, roofless houses with their doorways standing open like mouths mid-word. The exodus had happened generations ago, when the covenant broke and the land turned sullen. Nothing grew right anymore. Trees came up twisted, bark split into spirals. The grass was the color of old dishwater. And everywhere, everywhere, the thorns: blackberry gone feral into hedgerows taller than a cart, bramble choking the road itself, thorns as long as my thumb and black as iron nails.

Wren walked through them and they bent aside. Not dramatically — she wasn't performing anything — but I watched a bramble actually lean away from her shoulder as she passed, the way a dog leans away from someone who once kicked it.

"They remember me," she said, without turning around.

"The thorns?"

"The rites. I carried the cutting-shears for eleven years." Her voice did the thing it always did when she talked about her priesthood — went flat and careful, like someone crossing ice. "Pruning was sacred work, once. You cut the bramble back from the shrine paths so the gods could walk among their people. Every cut a small kindness. Every kindness a small wound." She pushed a branch aside and let it swing back behind her; it caught my sleeve instead, politely, almost apologetically. "Then the Ministry said the pruning was idolatry, and the shears were melted down, and the bramble grew wild because nobody was kind to it anymore. This whole country is what happens when you stop tending. It isn't evil. It's just unloved."

"And the gods?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. "The gods are the same," she said finally. "Just unloved on a scale you can't imagine."

Behind us, Vessel-of-Embers made a sound I'd never heard from him — a low, cracked note that might have been a laugh in a healthier throat. "She flatters us," he said. "We are also vain, and petty, and we hold grudges the way rivers hold rain. Do not let the priestess make saints of us, charm-seller. You will be living with us soon enough. Better to know us now."

"I'm not living with anyone," I said. "I'm reforging a covenant and then presumably retiring."

He looked up at me with those penny-dull eyes, and he didn't argue, and somehow that was worse than any argument.

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a waystation chapel — not drowned like the one Wren knew further along, just abandoned, its little bell still hanging in the gable, green with age. Wren built a fire small enough to hide and cooked barley in a tin pot while I sat with my back against the cold altar stone and tried very hard not to listen.

It's difficult to explain what not-listening costs when listening is what you are. Twenty years I'd practiced it in Greyhollow: the muffled hearing, the careful blankness, the discipline of letting a beggar-dog's plea slide off me like rain off waxed canvas. Out here the practice failed completely. There were simply too many souls, too frayed, crying out in the register below language, and my gift drank it all whether I consented or not.

A fox's soul, since you ask, sounds like banked embers settling. I knew that one intimately by now.

"Cyrus." Wren handed me the pot. "Eat. You've been somewhere else since the orchard."

"I'm always somewhere else. It's my best feature."

"Eat anyway."

I ate. The barley was over-salted, which meant she was worried; Wren salted food according to some private emotional ledger I'd never cracked. Across the fire, Vessel-of-Embers lay with his chin on his forepaws, and I watched his ribs move, and counted how visible they were, and hated the number.

"How far to the Garden?" I asked. "Truly. Not the answer you gave me to keep me walking."

"Four days," Wren said. "Five, the way we're traveling. The drowned chapel is tomorrow night, or the night after. Then the last stretch through—" she gestured at the dark beyond the doorway, where the thorns made a sound like slow applause whenever the wind moved, "—that."

"And him?" I nodded at the fox. "Can he make four days?"

The silence that followed had a shape, and the shape was an answer.

"He can make it," Wren said at last, "if the path stays open. You've seen what he does. The thorns part for him. The frost pulls back a hand's width wherever he walks. He's spending himself to keep us a road, Cyrus. Every mile we travel easily is a mile he paid for."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you keep digging up warrens."

"Yes," I said, "and I know exactly what each warren costs, because he told me to my face, because gods are apparently incapable of letting a man have one decent act of stupidity without itemizing it." My voice came out harder than I intended. Softer: "Yes, Wren. I know. I've known since the orchard. I dig anyway. That's not ignorance, that's just — who I am. If I start rationing my mercy by the mile, then the Ministry won, even out here. They made a man who counts."

Wren studied me across the fire for a long moment. In the ember-light she looked younger and older at once, the brand on her wrist catching orange. "My teacher used to say something like that," she said quietly. "Before. That mercy isn't a resource, it's a direction. I stopped believing it for a long time." She poked the fire. "You make it easy to start again. It's very annoying."

"Thank you. I practice being annoying daily."

From the shadows by the altar, Vessel-of-Embers spoke without lifting his head. "Charm-seller. Come here. There is something I would show you while I still have the strength to show it."

* * *

What he showed me was the country's soul.

He had me sit cross-legged on the chapel floor and lay his muzzle against my knee, and he said, "Open. All the way. I will carry what comes."

I opened — really opened, wider than I had since the day the dog spoke to me in the market, wider than I'd dared since childhood — and the thorn country poured in.

It was not horror. That surprised me most. I'd expected horror; I'd been marinating in horror for days, hare by hare, crow by crow. But seen whole, all at once, through the god's steadying presence, it was something else. Grief, yes — grief in strata, centuries of it, the land's memory of shrines torn down and rites forbidden and names struck from records. But underneath the grief ran something stubborn and patient and green. Roots, going down through frozen ground toward water that was still there. Seeds in the bellies of dead orchards, waiting with the specific arrogance of seeds, absolutely certain the world would eventually make sense again.

"They are not broken," Vessel-of-Embers murmured, his voice coming from very far away and very close, both at once. "Frayed, yes. Starving. But a soul frays toward something, charm-seller. Look where theirs fray toward."

Toward the Garden. Every fraying thread in the whole blighted country, thousands upon thousands of beast-souls unraveling like rope in a current — and the current ran west, toward the place we were going. They weren't just dying. They were leaning. The whole country leaned toward the Garden the way plants lean toward light, and had been leaning for two hundred years, and the weight of all that leaning was what had worn the road we traveled, faint and ancient, beneath the thorns.

"Why show me this?"

"Because in four days you will stand in front of a warden-god who will name a price, and you will be afraid, and you will be tempted to believe the fear means the price is wrong." His breath rattled slightly. "So look at this instead. Remember this. They have been leaning toward you for two hundred years, Cyrus Amberdale. Not toward a hero. Toward a signatory. Toward someone who counts." A pause, and then, with the ghost of his old dry humor: "Someone who digs up warrens."

When he lifted his muzzle from my knee, I saw what the effort had cost. The white at his muzzle had spread to his throat. His flanks, when he settled by the fire, rose and fell like something barely holding its shape.

I didn't sleep much. Neither, I think, did Wren, though she had the grace to pretend.

* * *

On the third morning we found the stag.

He stood in the middle of the old road a quarter-mile ahead, motionless in the grey light, and even at that distance I could hear what he was — or rather, what was left of him. A great soul, a lord's soul, worn down to a whisper. His coat hung on him like a sack. His antlers had shed, and the new growth had come in black and brittle.

He didn't run when we approached. He lowered his head instead, the way beasts do when they've decided to trust you with the killing blow, and spoke in the wordless register, and what he said was: *brother-speaker. We held the line as long as we could.*

"Held what line?" I asked aloud, because Wren was watching and I wanted her to hear it too.

Images came, fragmentary, frost-edged: the borderlands' beasts organized — not the loose gathering outside Greyhollow's gates, but organized, companies, lines, the old war-shapes from before the covenant broke. Holding the thorns back from the road. Holding the frost back from the seed-banks. Dying in the ditches by dozens so that a path might exist at all for three travelers and a failing god. The stag had been their general. Now his army was scattered, frozen, spent, and he had walked out alone to find us and report.

*The Garden gate opens for the signatory,* he said. *But the country between is ours to give, and we have given it. Tell them. Tell the ones who come after. We were here. We held.*

"I'll tell them," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I didn't care. "Every one. I swear it by the covenant, old and new."

The stag breathed out — a long plume of steam, like a sigh two hundred years overdue — and walked past us, west, toward the Garden he would never reach, and sat down in the bracken with the dignity of a king abdicating, and was gone within the hour. Wren buried him. Vessel-of-Embers stood over the grave and said words in a language that predated language, and the frost around the grave pulled back a full arm's length, the only warm ground for miles, and I understood he had paid for that too, out of what remained.

We walked on. None of us spoke for a long time.

* * *

Near dusk, Wren spotted it first: smoke, thin and disciplined, rising from a hollow two ridges east. Campfire smoke, but wrong — too small, too controlled, the smoke of someone who knew about hiding fires.

"Riders?" I asked.

"Maybe. Maybe trappers. Maybe nothing." But her hand had gone to the knife at her belt, and she scanned the ridgeline with the flat professional attention of someone reading a battlefield. "We'll avoid the hollow, keep to the streambed, and—"

Then Vessel-of-Embers stopped walking.

He stood in the middle of the road with his ears pricked forward, staring back the way we'd come — south, toward Greyhollow, toward everything we'd fled through the blizzard — and his ruined voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"Charm-seller. How many days has it been since the city?"

"Six. Seven. Why?"

"Because something is following us," he said. "It has been following us since the drowned road, and I told myself it was wolves, and I am done lying to myself. It does not smell like wolves." His penny-bright eyes found mine. "It smells like ink and boot leather and a man who reads other men's papers. It smells like the Ministry, Cyrus. And it is half a day behind us, and gaining."

Wren swore softly, fluently, in a manner that suggested her eleven years of priesthood had not been entirely liturgical.

And far to the south, faint on the wind, I heard it too — not hooves, not voices, just the small cold click of a mind doing sums about us. A clerk's mind. Patient. Methodical. Closing the ledger.

Half a day, I thought. Four days to the Garden. The arithmetic of the journey had changed, and every soul in the thorn country, fraying and leaning westward, seemed to hold its breath to see what we would do with it.
