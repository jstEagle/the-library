## 16. The Garden Found

The thorns ended the way a fever ends — not gradually, but all at once, in a single cold sweat of light.

One moment Vessel-of-Embers was limping ahead of us through the last black hedge, his coat gone the color of old ash, and I was carrying him when he let me, which he hated, which meant he was worse off than he admitted. The next moment the hedge simply stopped being hostile. It parted like a curtain drawn by a bored servant, and the air that came through smelled of — I want to say *green*. Not grass, not leaves. Green itself, the idea of it, bottled and uncorked.

Wren stopped so suddenly I walked into her.

"Is that—" she started, and didn't finish, because there wasn't a word for it. She'd been a priestess of the old rites; if anyone should have had the vocabulary, it was her. The fact that she didn't told me everything.

Beyond the last of the thorns lay a ring of ice.

I don't mean snow, and I don't mean frost on a windowpane. I mean a wall of it, a crown of white rising out of the earth in jagged spires, some of them taller than Greyhollow's bell tower, all of them leaning inward like an audience craning toward a stage. And inside that ring of winter, impossibly, criminally, *green* — meadow going down to a lake the color of bottle glass, trees heavy with blossom, a light that came from everywhere and nowhere and cast no shadows at all.

"It's spring," Wren said. "It's been *spring* here the whole time."

"The whole time," I agreed, "while we froze our fingers crossing a country of dead brambles." My voice did something wry and unconvincing. It does that when I'm terrified. Twenty years of practice, that particular skill: charm as armor. "Someone should write a strongly worded letter to whoever's been hoarding."

Vessel-of-Embers lifted his head from where he hung against my shoulder. His eyes were still bright — gods burn down to embers, not to nothing — but his voice came out thin as paper.

"Do not mock the gate," he said. "It is listening."

* * *

We stood at the ice for a long time before any of us worked up the courage to touch it.

Here is what nobody tells you about awe: it's mostly fear wearing nicer clothes. I'd spent my whole life being small on purpose. Small shop, small voice, small footprint in a city that paid its informants in bread. Being small had kept me alive. And now I stood in front of a wall of ancient winter surrounding a pocket of impossible summer, and every instinct I owned screamed at me to turn around, go home, sell luck-knots until I died quietly in my bed with my secrets intact.

But the animals were behind us. That was the thing I kept coming back to. When we'd crossed into the country of thorns, they had followed — hundreds of them, then thousands, a slow river of beasts flowing along our path at a respectful distance: wolves and milk-cows together, crows walking because the wind up there could kill a flyer, a blind old cart-horse that had somehow kept pace for three days. They hadn't come for me, exactly. They'd come because something in them knew the way, and I was the one who could hear them say it. *To the Garden, speaker. To the Garden, where the promises were made.* Their voices had gotten quieter as the land got colder, frayed like rope worn against stone. Some of them had stopped along the way. We didn't look back at those. There was nothing to see except more winter.

I put my bare hand flat against the ice.

It didn't feel cold.

It felt like a held breath. Like standing outside a door where someone you love is sleeping, hand raised to knock, knowing that whatever you do next changes everything. Under my palm, deep in the ice, something moved — not physically, but the way a thought moves behind a forehead. Attention. Enormous, slow attention, turning toward me the way a whale turns in dark water.

Then the ice opened. Not cracked — *opened*, folding apart along seams I couldn't see, leaving a passage wide enough for the blind mare, which seemed like a courtesy aimed less at me than at what I was bringing with me.

"After you," I said to Wren, because gallows humor is also a skill, and mine was getting a real workout.

She looked at me — tired, frost-burned, ex-priestess, woman who had once helped seal the gods away and had spent three weeks of travel paying for it in confession — and said, "You know I'm not going in front of you. You're the signatory. This is your door."

"My ancestor's door," I corrected. "I just have the same face."

"Cyrus." She used the voice she saved for when I was being deliberately stupid, which, fine, was often. "Walk through."

So I did.

* * *

Crossing the threshold felt like being remembered by someone who had never met you.

That's the closest I can get to it. A warmth went through me, head to heels, thorough as a bath, and in it I heard — felt? — a vast murmuring attention take note of details about me I hadn't known were details. The calluses on my hands from twenty years of knot-work. The specific grief-shape of my mother's absence. The exact pitch of the fox-god's breathing against my shoulder. It catalogued me the way Halvers' Ministry clerks must catalogue suspects, except this catalogue was gentle, almost tender, and infinitely patient.

Then we were inside, and the light had no source, and it was green, green, *green*.

Grass underfoot so thick it swallowed our footsteps. Trees in full blossom though the petals never fell — or rather, they fell upward sometimes, drifting lazily back to their branches like things that had forgotten which way the world worked and weren't in any hurry to remember. The lake at the center lay perfectly still, holding a sky that didn't quite match the one overhead. The air tasted sweet without being cloying, like water after a long walk.

And the Garden was *full*.

Not of people. Of sleepers.

They lay in the long grass and against the tree roots and half-sunk in the warm shallows of the lake, and at first glance you'd swear they were hills and boulders and fallen logs, because scale means nothing out here. A god the size of a hillside, furred, breathing so slowly the grass around its flank rose and fell in tides. Something serpentine coiled beneath the lake's surface, visible only as a darkness with a heartbeat. Smaller ones — small as dogs, small as birds — slept curled in the crooks of branches, and when we passed, some of them stirred and made sounds in their sleep. Not words. More like the memory of words, the way a dreamer mumbles fragments of a conversation that made sense only while dreaming.

Wren sank to her knees in the grass. I genuinely don't think she decided to; her legs simply resigned.

"I trained for this," she whispered. "Twenty years ago, I memorized the rites, the names, the proper forms of approach. And now I'm kneeling in a meadow and I can't remember a single word of any of it."

"Maybe that's the point," I said. "Maybe they got tired of the proper forms."

"Don't," she said sharply, and then, softer: "Don't joke at them. Please. Not here."

I looked at the hillside god, breathing its tidal breath, and shut up.

Vessel-of-Embers slid down from my shoulder onto the grass. He walked a few paces on legs that shook, lifted his muzzle, and breathed in the Garden air like a man coming home after a war.

"Brothers," he said quietly. "Sisters. I am late."

None of them woke. But the whole meadow sighed — I swear to you, the entire Garden sighed, grass and blossom and still water — as if a roomful of sleepers had shifted toward a familiar voice.

* * *

We walked toward the center, because there was clearly a center, and centers are for going to.

The ghost-echoes began maybe halfway to the lake.

I say ghosts, but they weren't pale or translucent or any of the things ghosts are supposed to be. They were more like gaps shaped like people. You'd be looking at sunlit grass and then notice that a man-shaped piece of sunlight was standing slightly differently than the grass around it, and if you unfocused your eyes — Wren taught me this trick within the first minute, whispering, "Look past them, not at them" — you could see them. Figures moving among the trees. Sitting in circles. Standing in pairs, talking, laughing, arguing. They cast no shadows, made no sound, and left the grass unbent beneath them, and there were dozens of them, maybe hundreds, all drifting through the same few hours on repeat like flies caught in amber.

"The original signatories," Wren breathed. "This is where they swore it. The Garden remembers them."

One of the echoes passed close to us — a tall woman with her hair braided in a style four hundred years out of fashion, walking beside a shape that flickered between woman and wolf depending on how you looked at it. As she passed through the space where I stood, something brushed through me, cold and quick, like swimming through a stranger's memory. For half a second I saw what she saw: the lake, the circle of gods, a stone table, a knife, blood offered freely —

And names. Being spoken aloud, each one carved somewhere permanent.

I turned, following that pull, and found the stones.

* * *

They rose from the meadow near the lake's edge: a crescent of standing stones, maybe forty of them, each about my height, weathered smooth except for the carvings. Names, cut deep in an alphabet older than Greyhollow's walls. Wren read them aloud in a hush, translating as she went, her priestess training surfacing at last like a swimmer who'd only been pretending to drown.

"Aldous Brightmere, who spoke for the rivers. Sefa of the Nine Herds, who spoke for the beasts of field and fold. Marrow-and-Mirth, who spoke for the burrowers..." Name after name. Human names, beast-names, god-names, mixed together in no hierarchy I could detect, as if the carver had considered them all equally worth remembering. Beside many of the human names, smaller marks: lines of descent, children listed, grandchildren, the covenant passing down bloodlines like an inheritance nobody would want and everybody got.

And there, near the middle of the crescent, a name I recognized in my bones before Wren translated it.

*Amberdale.*

My name. My family's name, worn by four centuries of charm-sellers and quiet men, carved into a standing stone in the place where the world's oldest promise was sworn.

Beside it, the descent-lines ran down, generation by generation, each one a smaller carving, and Wren traced them with her finger, lips moving, counting. Her finger stopped. I watched it stop. I watched her go very still in the way she went still before saying true things, which was a habit of hers I'd learned to dread.

"Cyrus," she said. "Come look at this."

I came and looked.

The last line of descent ended with a name — mine, though I'd never seen it written in stone before, and seeing it there made it feel less like a name and more like a sentence already handed down, awaiting only its execution date. And beside my name, the stone was blank. Smooth. Un-carved.

Except it wasn't blank, exactly.

There was a space there. An absence with edges. And the absence was shaped like a person — my height, my shoulders, the slight forward lean of a man who has spent his life making himself small. As I stared at it, the hollow in the air beside the stone seemed to deepen, to wait, to *recognize* me. The way a coat recognizes the shoulder it was cut for. The way a grave —

No. I didn't finish that thought either.

"That's why the thorns let us through," Wren said softly. "That's why the ice opened. It wasn't me. It wasn't even really you, Cyrus. It's the shape. The Garden's been keeping a seat empty for four hundred years, and you're the first Amberdale to walk through that gate since the breaking." She turned to look at me, and her face did something complicated — pity and fear and a terrible kind of tenderness all fighting for the same ground. "Every signatory line died out or scattered or was hunted down by the Ministry's founders. Yours is the last. Which means—"

"Which means the empty space fits me," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "I noticed."

Behind us, Vessel-of-Embers limped to the base of the stone and sat down heavily, looking up at the carved names with an expression I couldn't read — grief and hunger and something like apology.

"You asked me once," he said, "why the gods chose to wake *now*, after two hundred years of silence, when waking hurt us so badly. I never answered you honestly." His ember-bright eyes held mine. "We woke because the last signatory line was thinning, little merchant. Because a covenant needs a living voice to hold it, and yours was the final thread, and threads fray. We are not waking to demand anything of you." He lowered his head, and for the first time since I'd known him, the fox-god looked ashamed. "We are dying. All of us, out there in the cold half-realm, starving in the dark. We woke because we are *dying*, and the covenant was our last hope, and you were its last key. I am sorry. I would have told you sooner. I told myself there would be a better moment, and then there was never a better moment, because there never is."

The meadow was very quiet. Somewhere in the blossoming branches, a sleeper god murmured in its dream, and the sound rolled through the grass like distant thunder that meant no harm.

I looked at the empty space beside my name. It fit me. It had always fit me. Four hundred years of Ambersdales selling charms and keeping their heads down, and the whole line had been a corridor, and I was the room at the end of it.

"You know," I said, aiming for wry and hearing something else entirely, "when the dog talked to me back in Greyhollow, I thought the worst part would be the Ministry finding out." I touched the stone, right at the edge of the hollow, where the air was cool and expectant. "Turns out the worst part is that it fits."

Wren reached out and took my hand. Her grip was fierce.

The lake, behind us, went utterly still — stiller than still — and across its surface, from the drowned dark where the great serpent-god slept, ripples began moving *inward*, toward the center, as if the water were drawing breath.

Something in the Garden had noticed that its last guest had arrived.

The empty space beside my name grew warmer under my fingertips, and waited.
