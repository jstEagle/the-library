## 17. The Price Named

I have stood before gods before. I want that on the record, because it changes what I'm about to admit, which is this: standing in front of the warden of the Garden was nothing like standing in front of the old altars, and I hated it immediately.

The altars had been stone and smoke and theater — a priestess in good boots reciting words she'd memorized at fourteen while congregants wept on cue. Manageable. A god on an altar is a god with a job description.

This one had no job description. This one had a garden.

It rose out of the central green the way weather rises out of a valley — not walking, not arriving, simply becoming more present than everything else until presence was all there was. It wore a shape the way a river wears a shape: loosely, and only because the banks insisted. Bark like birch gone silver, eyes like two holes cut through to the sky behind the world. When it moved, the grass around it leaned to follow, and the sleeping gods in their ring of ice stirred without waking, murmuring in their frost like children talking in sleep.

Vessel-of-Embers, who had spent the last hundred miles dying by inches so we could stand here, lifted his head off Cyrus's shoulder and said, with the last of his god-voice: *Warden. We come for the covenant.*

*I know,* the warden said. Its voice didn't arrive through the ears so much as through the ribs. *You have been announced since the thorns began. Every step you took north, something here turned over in its sleep.* The sky-holes that served it for eyes settled on Cyrus. Then, to my considerable alarm, on me. Then back to Cyrus. *Which of you is the signatory?*

"I am," Cyrus said. And then, because he is who he is, he added: "Sorry about the wait."

Twenty years of hiding, and the man apologizes for the delay. I could have killed him. I nearly did, later, but we'll get to that.

*The terms are known,* the warden said. *They were known when your blood first signed. They have not changed, because they cannot change, because they are not rules but the nature of the thing, the way a flame cannot choose to be cold.* It paused, and I understood — with the particular dread of someone who has read enough scripture to know how this part goes — that the pause was mercy. It was giving us one more breath of ignorance before it took even that away.

*Speak them anyway,* Vessel-of-Embers said. *She will want them spoken. She will want to fight them.*

"She's right here," I said. "Stop discussing me like I'm a horse you're appraising."

*The covenant binds three peoples,* the warden said, as if I hadn't spoken, as if gods had ever once let anyone finish a sentence. *Gods, beasts, and humans. To reforge it requires a living hand of the human line — a signatory, blood-carried, willing. There is one such hand left in either realm.* The eyes did not blink. Eyes like that don't need to. *And the covenant is not ink. It is memory. It was sworn in remembering — every god, every beast, every human who stood on this grass remembered aloud what they owed the others, and the covenant grew from those rememberings like a root from seed. So it must be reforged the same way. In remembering.*

"Then let him remember," I said. "He remembers plenty. Ask him about his mother's hands, ask him about—"

*Not his own remembering.* The warden's voice went quiet, and quiet, from a thing that size, is worse than thunder. *A covenant made of one person's memory would be one person's covenant — small, crooked, human. No. The reforging must be fed. And the only food worthy of it, the only currency this ground accepts, is everything the signatory remembers of those bound to him by love.*

The Garden was very still. Somewhere far off, under the ice, a sleeping god sighed.

*Every face,* the warden said. *Every name. His mother. The fox who carried him here. The woman standing beside him, whose name he learned four weeks ago and has already begun building a life around, because that is what men like him do, they build, they cannot help it, it is their whole cursed species' gift. He will walk out of this garden knowing how to speak, how to charm, how to sell luck-knots to grieving widows. He will not know why he knows any of it. He will not know a single soul who loved him, nor a single soul he loved. That is the price. That has always been the price. That is why the covenant has lain broken for two hundred years — because no one came who was willing to pay it.*

* * *

I want to describe my reaction accurately, and accurately means admitting it wasn't noble.

My first thought was: *no.* Not the reasoned no of an argument, the animal no, the kind that comes up from somewhere below language. My second thought was that there had to be another way, and my third — arriving hot on its heels, shameless — was that if there wasn't another way, then maybe the winter should just win. Let the long cold take both realms. Let the gods starve in their half-world and the beasts freeze at the gates and Greyhollow go white and silent forever. Any of it was preferable to watching Cyrus Amberdale walk out of this green place as a stranger to me.

Understand what he was to me. Four weeks. That's all. Twenty-nine days since he'd sold me a false charm against forgetting with steady hands and a liar's gentle eyes, and in twenty-nine days I had — what? Told him things I'd never told anyone. Slept sounder in his cellar than I'd slept in twenty years of exile. Learned the exact way he hummed when he was nervous, three notes, always flat on the third. Built, brick by brick, in less than a month, a life with a man inside it.

And the price of saving everyone was to unbuild it. Unbuild it in him, where it lived.

"No," I said again, out loud this time, and my voice cracked on it like ice on a pond. "No. Find another price. You're a god — invent another price."

*We do not invent,* the warden said. *That is your people's gift, not ours. We remember. And the covenant eats memory the way your body eats bread.*

"Then feed it mine." I stepped forward, into the leaning grass, close enough that I could smell the warden — rain on hot stone, green things rotting sweetly, the smell of spring itself. "Take my memories. All of them. I've got twenty years of guilt in here, it's excellent stock, it'll keep the covenant fed till doomsday. Take my memory of him too, fine, take it, but leave *his* side intact. He forgets me; I don't forget him. Asymmetric. Lopsided. Do gods care about symmetry?"

The warden regarded me. The sky behind its eyes had clouds in it now, moving slowly, and I had the horrible sense that I was being weathered, read, worn down the way water wears stone — patiently, without malice, without any possibility of appeal.

*Covenant-forging is not a ledger,* it said. *It does not accept payment from the unbound. You are bound to him, yes — I see the thread, it is young and strong and already load-bearing, which is precisely the tragedy. But the debt runs from the signatory's hand. Only his hand pays. Only his remembering feeds the root.*

"There's no other signatory," Vessel-of-Embers said softly. It wasn't a question. He'd known, I think, since the cellar. Since before the cellar. Gods always know the ending early; it's the rest of us who get to live in suspense.

*None living,* the warden agreed. *The last other candidate died eighty years ago, unwed, childless, and unremembering of her own gift, thanks to your Ministry's careful work. There is one hand. There is one price. There is no version of this where the arithmetic comes out kind.*

"Then I refuse it on his behalf," I said. "I'm his — I don't even know what I am to him, counsel, conscience, whatever — but he doesn't get to agree to this. He'll say yes because he's *good*, that's the whole problem with him, he'll do the arithmetic and decide everyone else outweighs him, and I am telling you as an ex-priestess who has watched men sacrifice themselves on altars my whole cursed life: it never buys what they think it buys."

*Wren,* Vessel-of-Embers said.

"Don't you *Wren* me. You're dying anyway; you don't get an opinion."

*Everyone dying gets an opinion,* the fox said. *It's the one perk of the position.*

* * *

Here is what I did instead of listening to reason: I fought.

I fought the way I haven't fought since the night the Ministry burned the temple, when I argued theology with a captain of guards until he stopped looking bored and started looking frightened. I threw everything at the warden. I cited the old liturgies — I quoted the Rite of Substitution, verse and gloss, the passage that says *what one owes, another may carry* — and the warden answered, gently, that the Rite of Substitution governed debts between mortals, and a covenant between realms was not a debt but a marriage, and no one can be married in your stead.

I offered it my memories anyway. Formally. On my knees, in the wet grass, with the liturgical gestures coming back to my hands like they'd never left. The warden looked at me for a long time, and something in the vast weather of its attention was almost tender.

*You misunderstand what you're offering,* it said. *Your memories of him are yours. If you gave them, you would simply be a woman who had loved a stranger once and couldn't recall why her chest hurt. He would still forget you. The covenant would remain broken. You would have paid grief's price and received none of the purchase. Love is not transferable, priestess. That is its entire meaning. If it could be handed off, it would be currency, and currency is the thing your Ministry built in its place.*

"And look how well that went," I said, and my voice came out small and vicious and defeated.

Somewhere in the middle of all this — I couldn't tell you when, because I wasn't watching, because I was on my knees arguing metaphysics with a tree that had opinions — Cyrus walked to the center of the green and stood beside the warden and said, quietly, in the voice he used for telling widows the truth about their luck-knots:

"I'll pay it."

* * *

The Garden went silent in a way that felt structural, like the silence after a bell stops ringing rather than before one starts.

"You don't even know what you're agreeing to," I said, rounding on him. "You heard maybe half of it. You were watching the fox breathe. Cyrus—"

"Wren." He looked at me. He had dirt on his cheek from the thorn-country and exhaustion carved under his eyes and frost in his hair that hadn't melted since we crossed the borderlands, and he looked calm, which was the worst thing, because I knew that calm. It was shopkeeper's calm. It was the calm of a man who has spent twenty years doing hard arithmetic in secret and is very, very good at it.

"It's everyone or it's me," he said. "That's not a hard sum. The river's been dying since before we met. The beasts outside Greyhollow are standing in snow up to their bellies waiting for me. Vessel-of-Embers—" his voice caught, just slightly, on the fox's name, and he steadied it, and I hated how well he steadied it, "—is spending his last weeks keeping a path open for a man who's going to stand at the gate and haggle. I'm not going to haggle. I've done enough haggling for one life."

"One life," I repeated. "You're about to lose it. Do you understand what forgetting *means*? It isn't sleep. It isn't rest. Everything you are is made of the people in it. Your mother taught you the knots. Your father's silence taught you the listening. The fox—" I gestured at Vessel-of-Embers, who was watching us with his ember-colored eyes going dim and bright, dim and bright, like a fire deciding. "The fox chose you. Out of every human alive, he dragged himself to *your* door. And you'll stand here tomorrow morning and not know his name."

"Yes," Cyrus said.

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What else is there?" And then, softer, with the terrible gentleness he used on everyone but himself: "You told me once, in the drowned chapel, what the river-god showed you. You said the gate takes a toll. You didn't ask me then whether I'd pay it. You knew. I think you've known since the thorns started, since you started telling me stories at the fires like you were packing a bag for me. Don't pretend you're surprised, Wren. You're just angry. Those aren't the same thing, even though they wear the same coat."

I opened my mouth to destroy him. To say something so precisely cruel it would undo the agreement, shatter his calm, make him fight for himself for once in his insufferable selfless life.

What came out instead was: "Who's going to tell you about your mother?"

And that undid me more than it undid him, because he smiled — small, real, wrecked — and said, "You are. If you stay. If you want to. I'm told I make an excellent listener."

* * *

The warden had waited through all of it without moving, which I suspect is the oldest and saddest skill gods possess: patience with mortal grief, practiced over millennia, polished smooth as river stone.

*Is the signatory's consent freely given?* it asked.

Cyrus looked at me. I want to be honest about this moment, because later — after, in the bad nights — I tried to remember whether I'd given him any sign, any escape hatch, any last chance to walk away. And the truth is I didn't. I stood in the leaning grass with my fists clenched and my heart going like a trapped bird, and I looked back at him, and whatever he found in my face was apparently enough, because he nodded.

"Freely given," he said.

*Hear then the terms, spoken whole, so that no one in this garden may claim ignorance.* The warden's voice dropped into cadence — old cadence, liturgical cadence, and despite everything my spine straightened, because some things live in the body deeper than betrayal does. *The signatory shall place his blood upon the stone and speak the remembering: gods, beasts, humans, each what they owe the others. The covenant shall take root and hold. And the price shall be taken in full: every memory of every soul bound to him by love, drawn out entire, fed to the root, gone beyond all reaching — beyond prayer, beyond magic, beyond the craft of any god or priest or speaker of beasts now living or hereafter born. He shall retain his skills, his speech, his craft, his kindness. He shall retain nothing of whom that kindness was for. This is the whole of the law. Are the terms witnessed?*

"I witness," said Vessel-of-Embers, and his voice was very thin now, a thread of ember in the dark.

I should have said it. Witnessing is what priestesses do; it was the one office left to me, the one dignity the Ministry hadn't burned out of me. Instead I stood there with my jaw locked and my eyes burning and said nothing, because witnessing meant agreeing, and I could not make my mouth build that sentence.

The warden, damn it, understood. *Unspoken witness counts,* it said to me, almost kindly. *It always has. Grief was the first liturgy, priestess. You are performing it adequately.*

"I hate you," I informed it.

*Most do, at first. Come spring, fewer.*

* * *

We walked back to the edge of the green together, the three of us, because the reforging would happen at dawn — the warden was explicit: the covenant must be fed on a day newly born, no other hour accepted, the old law of beginnings — and there was one evening left, and it belonged to us.

To him.

Vessel-of-Embers curled against Cyrus's boots the way he had in the cellar that first night, back when the biggest decision in my life was whether to trust a charm seller with my name. The fox was fading fast now, spending warmth he didn't have, and Cyrus sat with his hand resting on the red fur, light as you'd touch a candle flame you wanted to keep.

"The stories," Cyrus said to me, quietly. "Tonight. Tell me everything. Start with the market — start with the luck-knot. I want to hear it from the outside, the way you saw it. I want—" and here his composure slipped, just once, a crack running through the calm, and underneath it was a twenty-nine-day-old man terrified of losing a twenty-year-old loneliness, "—I want to know what I'm leaving in you. So some of me goes somewhere safe."

I sat down beside him in the impossible grass, under the ringed ice and the sleeping gods, and I thought about arguing one more time. About finding one more loophole, one more rite, one more desperate clause in a dead religion's footnotes.

But he had asked me for stories. And telling stories is the one thing I have never once refused anyone.

"All right," I said. "But you have to correct me when I get you wrong. You're the only one who knows."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and said, "For one more night, I am."

Above us, the frost on the ring of sleeping gods began, very slowly, to glow.
