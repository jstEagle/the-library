## 18. Night Before the Forgetting

The warden-god had said *tomorrow* the way a butcher says *Tuesday*, and then it had left us alone in the heart of the Garden with nothing but a fire that burned without wood and the sound of ten thousand sleeping gods breathing in their rings of ice.

I want to report that I spent my last evening as myself in dignity. That I composed letters in my head to people I loved, or knelt in prayer, or stood at the edge of the green and watched the frost retreat like a tide going out.

What I actually did was ask Wren to talk.

"Not about the covenant," I said. "Not about gods or gates or the price of anything. Tell me stories. About me. So some of it lives in you even after it stops living in me."

She looked at me for a long moment across the fire. Wren's face is not an easy one to read on a good day — twenty years of disgraced priesthood had taught her to keep her features locked like a strongbox — but that night the lock was off, and what I saw behind her eyes frightened me more than the warden-god had. It was grief arriving early. Grief with nowhere to put itself yet.

"You understand," she said, "that if you forget, and I remember, we'll be strangers who happen to know each other."

"That's why I'm asking now. While I can still be greedy about it."

* * *

She started with my mother, which surprised me, because I'd never told Wren much about my mother. But she'd been there when Vessel-of-Embers told the story, back in the drowned chapel, and apparently the fox-god had been more thorough than I'd realized.

"Your mother's name was Odile," Wren said. "She sold charms too — did you know that? Not out of a shop. Out of a basket, at the fish market, before the Ministry cleared the stalls. Luck-knots and fever-wards and little woven birds for children who were teething. She had the gift quieter than you have it. She could hear sparrows, and they mostly talked nonsense, and she thought that was funny."

"She did think that was funny." The memory came up warm, like a stone held from the fire. "She used to say the sparrows were the only honest gossips in Greyhollow."

"There you go. You've still got it. Good." Wren leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the brand on her wrist — the old rites' mark, a circle broken by a line, that had cost her everything and saved us both. "Tell me something back. That's how this works. I give you a story, you tell me whether it's true."

"It's true. She wove a bird for every child on our street. When the fever came through — I was six — she sat up three nights weaving wards until her fingers bled, and every child on the street lived except—"

I stopped. There was a name at the end of that sentence. I could feel its shape, the way you feel a tooth that's gone: a socket where something used to sit. My brother. I had a brother. Had.

"Cyrus." Wren's voice was very gentle. "You don't have to say it."

"No," I said. "I do. That's the whole point of tonight. If I skip the hard ones, then tomorrow I lose them clean, and tonight I lose them cowardly, and I'd rather—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "I'd rather lose them honestly. His name was Tam. He was four. And my mother wove him a bird anyway, after, and hung it over our door, and it stayed there until the Ministry took the house. And that's why I sell luck-knots to grieving widows at cost, and why I never once charged full price for a charm against forgetting, even though half of Greyhollow would have paid a month's wages for one. Because I know exactly what forgetting costs, and it's more than anyone can pay, and I'm about to pay it anyway."

Wren didn't say anything for a while. In the ice beyond the firelight, one of the sleeping gods turned over in its long dream, and the ground hummed like a struck bell.

"The shop," she said finally. "Tell me about the shop. Your version. I only ever saw it from the outside."

* * *

So I told her about the shop, and here is roughly what I told her, because I find I want it written down somewhere, even if the somewhere is a page no one will ever connect to me:

The shop on Candlemaker's Row had a door that stuck in wet weather, and I never fixed it, because the sticking was the best security system in Greyhollow. Anyone who wanted to rob a charm-seller had to shoulder the door open with a noise like a cart crash, which gave me time to hide the fox.

Because by then there was a fox. A red fox, half-starved, speaking with the cadence of a god, asleep against my boots most evenings like he'd always lived there. Vessel-of-Embers. Ember. He weighed less than a sack of flour and contained the dying ember of a god who had once — so he claimed, and I believed him because his voice made the cellar stones sweat — kept the hearth-fires of nine thousand households lit with a word.

"He used to sleep against your boots," Wren said. "He told me that. He said your boots smelled like honesty, which is a strange thing for boots to smell like."

"He also said my cooking smelled like penance, so his judgment wasn't flawless."

That got a laugh out of her — short, startled, real. I counted it. I was counting things all night: laughs, silences, the number of times she almost reached across the fire and didn't. I don't know what I thought the counting was for. Maybe I thought if I noticed everything hard enough, noticing itself might survive the gate. Some trace of me made entirely of attention.

I told her about the customers. Widow Marsh, who bought a luck-knot every month for a son lost at sea and knew perfectly well the knots did nothing and bought them anyway because the buying was the prayer. The baker's boy who wanted a charm to make a girl love him, and whom I refused, and who stormed out, and who came back two years later to thank me, married to someone else, having learned the difference between wanting and loving the hard way. Old Fenwick, who came in every week asking for a charm against his neighbors and every week bought one against loneliness instead, and neither of us ever acknowledged the switch.

"And the dog," Wren said. "The first one. On the market morning. Tell me that one, because Ember told it differently every time and I want the true version."

The true version was this: I was freezing, and broke, and lonely in the specific way that comes from twenty years of silence, and a stray dog with mud in its ears looked up at me and said, *Listen to the river.* Just that. And I stood in the snow like a man struck by lightning that hadn't finished striking yet, because I hadn't heard an animal speak in years — I'd made sure of it, clamped the gift down so hard and so long I'd half convinced myself I'd imagined my whole childhood — and there it was again, the oldest voice in my life, asking me for help.

"I went to the river," I said. "Of course I went. Twenty years of caution and one muddy dog undid it in a sentence. And the river told me the gods were waking, and that something was wrong on the far side of the water, and I stood there on the bank knowing — *knowing* — that listening was going to cost me everything. And I listened anyway."

"Why?"

I thought about it honestly, which took a while. The fire popped its woodless fire-pop. Somewhere out in the rings of ice, a god dreamed of spring.

"Because it was quiet," I said finally. "Twenty years of holding my breath, and the moment I heard the river, I could breathe. Whatever it cost — and it's costing everything, Wren, everything, I know the terms better than the warden does — those few months since the dog spoke have been the only months of my life I haven't spent hiding. I'd rather have this year than the twenty before it. Even ending like this."

"That's either very brave or very stupid."

"In my experience those are usually the same thing wearing different coats."

* * *

Somewhere past midnight — though midnight means little in a place where the sun hasn't moved in days — Wren ran out of questions and I ran out of stories, and we sat in the kind of silence that isn't empty. Ember had come up from wherever foxes go to be solemn, and arranged himself across my feet, a living hot-water bottle with the smell of woodsmoke and old temples. His breathing was slow. Too slow. I'd stopped pretending not to notice days ago; each mile of the thorn-country had taken something out of him he couldn't replace, warmth spent like coin to keep our path open, and now there wasn't much left in his purse. Tomorrow, whatever happened at the gate, I suspected the fire would take him too. Gods burn out. Even small ones. Especially small ones who love too hard.

"Ask me something," Wren said suddenly. "Fair's fair. All night I've been taking inventory of you. You should get some of me to carry into — into whatever you are tomorrow."

"All right." I considered. "Why did you really come to my shop? The day you asked for a charm against forgetting. You already knew what I was. You said so yourself — you recognized me before I admitted it. Nobody walks across Greyhollow to buy a charm they don't need from a man they've already identified. So why?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Because I helped seal them away. The gods. I told you that on the road — I believed it was mercy, starving them gently instead of letting the war burn both realms down. I carried that for thirty years. And then the prayers started being answered badly, and I understood that mercy done without consent isn't mercy, it's just violence with good manners. And I needed—" Her hands closed around each other, the brand white in the firelight. "I needed to find someone the covenant still recognized. Someone whose name was still written somewhere that mattered. Because if it was, then maybe it could be rewritten. And if it could be rewritten, then maybe what I did could be *undone*, and I could stop being the woman who starved the gods and start being the woman who—"

She stopped.

"Wren," I said carefully, "you do know that tomorrow, when I walk through that gate, I won't remember any of this? Including the part where you're forgiven. If you were waiting for me to forgive you, you've scheduled it terribly."

"I know." She laughed, wet and cracked. "Believe me, I've thought about the scheduling. But forgiveness isn't a thing you keep, Cyrus. It's a thing that happens. It happens tonight, and it changes tonight, and tomorrow you'll be someone new who doesn't owe me anything — and that's the gift of it. Everyone else in my life remembers what I did. You'll be the only person in the world who gets to meet me without it. Do you understand? You're the only clean slate I will ever be given. I intend to earn it."

"That's the loneliest, bravest, stupidest thing I've ever heard."

"Same coat," she said. "Different weather."

* * *

Toward the end — and there was an end; dawn comes even to places without sun, as a greying of the ice, a thinning of the dark — I asked her for the one I'd been circling all night and afraid to land on.

"Tell me the story where I'm worth saving."

Wren frowned. "Which one is that?"

"Any of them. All of them. Because here's what I know about myself, Wren, and I know it the way you know a limp: I have spent twenty years being small on purpose. Careful, hidden, cheap with the truth, expensive with trust. I lied to Halvers and it was easy and I hated how easy it was. I nearly let Ember die in my cellar because helping him meant breaking my rules. Everything good I've ever done, I did dragging my heels. So tomorrow, when the gate takes me — I need to believe there's something underneath the hiding. Something worth the price. And I can't build that belief out of my own materials, because my own materials are all secrecy. So you tell it. You tell me who I am when I'm not looking."

For a long moment she just looked at me, and I watched her decide how much truth I could hold.

"You want to know what I see?" she said. "I see a man who sold a luck-knot to a widow at cost on the worst morning of his life and lied to her about the price so she wouldn't feel like charity. I see a man who heard a god begging in his cellar and complained about it for exactly four minutes before bringing it soup. I see a man who walked into the Ministry of Order with a fox-god's scent on his coat and lied so smoothly that a career interrogator believed him — and then went home and was sick about it, because the lying worked. Cyrus. The hiding was never what you were. It was just the coat. Underneath it there's a man who cannot watch a living thing suffer without offering it his own warmth, and that man signed a covenant before he was born, and tomorrow he's going to keep a promise older than kingdoms. That's who walks through the gate. Not the small careful man. The other one. The one who's been waiting twenty years for permission."

Ember stirred against my boots. When he spoke, his voice was thinner than I'd ever heard it, a coal wrapped in ash, but it was still his voice, still carrying that cadence that made the air feel older.

*"Signatory,"* he said. *"It is almost time. The gate opens with the light. Will you come?"*

I looked at the fire. At Wren, memorizing her face the way you drink water before a desert — greedily, all at once, knowing it won't last. At the fox on my feet, who had given everything and asked for nothing but sanctuary and gotten it, and who would not see the spring he was buying.

"One more story first," I said. "A short one. Wren — tell me the one about tomorrow. Tell me how it ends. Because tomorrow I won't know, and you will, and I want the last thing in my ears to be the ending."

Wren took a breath. Out past the rings of ice, the first true light of morning touched the Garden's frozen edge, and the ice began — very softly, like distant bells — to sing.

"It ends with winter breaking," she said. "It ends with spring."
