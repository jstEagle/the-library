## 19. The Reforging

I have sold luck-knots for twenty years, so I know a thing or two about knots. The worst ones are not the tangled kind. They are the kind you tie yourself, deliberately, around something you cannot afford to lose — and the tighter you pull them, the more certain you become that someday you will have to cut them.

Dawn came to the Garden gray and gentle, which felt like an insult. You would think the morning of the end of the world would at least have the decency to be dramatic. Instead there was just light coming through the ice-ringed trees, pink and slow, and frost smoking off the grass like breath, and Wren sitting beside me on the covenant stone with her knees drawn up, watching me not-sleep.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"Neither did you."

"I slept. You watched me sleep, which is different and slightly unsettling."

"It's called keeping watch."

"It's called being afraid," she said, "and I'm not going to take it from you. It's yours. You get to keep everything today that you want to keep." She stopped. I watched her hear her own words come back to her. "That was cruel. I'm sorry."

"It was true," I said. "Keep it anyway."

The warden-god had told us the terms plainly enough, the way honest things are always plain: *a living signatory, who surrenders all memory of those bound to him by love.* Not his memories of them — his memory of them. The difference mattered, Wren had argued, for three hours, in four languages, two of them dead. The warden had listened with the patience of a mountain and repeated itself like one too.

So here is what I knew when the sun cleared the wall of thorns: today I would forget my mother's hands. Today I would forget the smell of the shop in winter, tallow and dried lavender. Today I would forget Vessel-of-Embers entirely, every word, every night he spent shivering against my boots while I pretended to read. And I would do it gladly, because the alternative was a world where no one remembered anything, ever again, because everyone was dead of winter.

I had made my peace with it somewhere around the third hour of the night, in the small hours when peace comes cheap because you're too tired to argue. What I hadn't made peace with was how much I wanted to spend the last hours hoarding. Every minute I caught myself doing it — memorizing Wren's crooked brand, the exact gray of her eyes, the way she said *Cyrus* like she was testing whether the name could hold weight. Hoarding. A charm seller to the last. Tying knots.

"Cyrus." Vessel-of-Embers limped out of the bracken, red fur gone nearly white at the muzzle, one eye clouded. He had spent himself badly and generously on our road through the Country of Thorns, burning god-stuff to keep the path open, and now there wasn't much left of him but voice and stubbornness. He sat down heavily against my boot, exactly where he always sat, and looked up at me with his good eye. "You're doing the thing where your face lies."

"My face never lies."

"Your face is a shop counter. Everything on it is arranged for sale." He sighed, a small gusty sound. "Stop arranging. I want to see you before —"

"Don't say it."

"Somebody has to say it. That's the whole trouble with dying gods; we've got nothing left but saying." He laid his chin on my ankle. His weight was almost nothing now. When had it become almost nothing? Somewhere in the thorns, probably, some mile I'd walked without noticing the cost. "Listen to me, beast-speaker. Last lesson."

"I didn't ask for a lesson."

"No one ever does. That's why they're valuable." His tail moved once across the stone, a slow metronome. "You have spent twenty years believing your gift was a secret you kept. Wrong. Your gift was a door you kept shut. There's a difference. A secret is a weight. A door is a choice. Today you choose open, and whatever they take from you after — the choosing was still yours. No one can unmake that. Not gods. Not forgetting."

I put my hand on his head, between the ears, where the fur was coarse and warm. "You'll be gone too," I said. "When it takes you. I won't remember you were ever here."

"Yes," he said, comfortably. "But you'll be warmer in spring and you won't know why. I intend to be extremely smug about it from wherever dead fox-gods go. You'll feel it. Smugness travels."

Wren laughed — wet, cracked laughter, the kind that's mostly grief wearing a disguise — and then stood, because the sun had cleared the thorns fully now, and the stone beneath us had begun, very faintly, to hum.

* * *

The covenant stone sat at the heart of the Garden like a heart indeed: a slab of black rock older than the city, older than Greyhollow's walls, older maybe than the idea of walls. Names were carved into its face in ranks, hundreds of them, human names and beast-names and god-names interwoven, and beside each human name a hollow — a space shaped like the person who had sworn, worn into the rock by centuries of standing. My ancestor Amberdale's name was there. Beside it, the hollow shaped exactly like me. I had stood in it yesterday and felt the stone take my measure like a tailor.

Now the warden-god came, as it had promised, walking out of the green heart of the Garden. I will try to describe it and fail, but here goes: it was like a tree that had decided, late in life, to also be a person, or a person who had been a tree all along and saw no reason to mention it. Its face was bark-soft. Its eyes were the brown of deep soil. When it spoke, the words arrived already understood, the way warmth arrives.

*Signatory,* it said. *Are you resolved?*

"I am."

*Then hear the terms once more, that there be no error. You will give us every memory bound to you by love. Your mother. Your friend the clerk. The priestess. The fox. All whom you love, and all who love you, will become strangers to you. This is the toll. The gate does not bargain.*

"I heard you the first time," I said. "And the second time. And Wren's version, which was longer and included swearing."

*Cyrus,* Wren started.

"Wren." I turned to her. Here is the thing I had rehearsed all night and still couldn't get right, so I just said it plain. "Don't let me be a stranger to myself. That's all I ask. You told me once you helped seal the gods away because you thought it was mercy, and you've spent every day since carrying it. Carry this instead. It's lighter. It's just — stories. Tell me my own stories until some of them stick. I'll be a man made of whatever you tell me, so make me a good one. Lie if you have to. I won't know the difference."

"I won't lie," she said. Her jaw was set the way it got when she was about to argue with a god. "You deserve better than a comfortable story. And Cyrus — you won't be made of what I tell you. You'll be made of what you *choose* to believe. That was always true. It's just going to be more obvious now."

"That," said Vessel-of-Embers, "is the second-best thing anyone has said at this stone in eight hundred years."

"What was the best?"

"The oath. Obviously. Keep up, priestess."

The warden raised a limb like a branch in wind. *It is time. Signatory, stand in your place. Speak the names. Blood to bind, breath to loose. We will do the rest.*

I stepped into the hollow. It fit the way my own name fit — completely, and with a strange sense of having always known it would.

I drew the knife Wren had sharpened for me the night before, though neither of us had said aloud what it was for. I opened my palm along the old line, the one every charm seller knows, the small honest cut we use to seal the strongest work, and pressed my hand to the stone.

The stone drank. I felt it drink — a slow pull, like thirst, like the river under ice.

"Names," the warden said. *Speak the names of the bound.*

And this was the part I had not prepared for, because how do you prepare for it? You speak the names of everyone you love, and speaking them is the giving. Each name spoken aloud loosened something in me and handed it over. I felt it go.

"My mother," I said. "Serafina Amberdale." And her hands went first — I felt them leave, the memory of her flour-dusted knuckles, the way she hummed off-key on market mornings — and I spoke through the loss because stopping was the only unforgivable thing. "She burned the bread every Sunday and we ate it anyway and called it tradition."

The stone warmed under my palm.

"Halvers," I said, and the tired clerk's face flickered and faded, the man who had raided my shop and then, later, burned a page and kept one, the man whose conscience had cost him everything and who had walked into the Garden behind us anyway, limping, carrying records like a penance. "He reads too much and regrets too little lately. He's better than his uniform. Remember that about him even if I can't."

Warmth. A crack, somewhere far away, like lake ice shifting.

"Wren." Her name caught in my throat like a fishbone. I looked at her. She was crying without any noise at all, which was somehow worse than sobbing. "Wren Halvorsen-doe — no, that's not — her name is Wren, just Wren, she gave up her other names when they branded her. She was a priestess of the old rites and she sealed the gods away believing it was mercy and she has been sorry every single day since, and her sorry is worth more than most people's faith. She argues with gods. She wins sometimes. She—" The memory was pulling free now, I could feel it sliding, and I gripped the words like rope. "She asked me for a charm against forgetting. In my shop. Before any of this. And I didn't understand until right now that she was asking for both of us."

*I have her,* said the stone, or the warden, or the Garden itself, gentle as snowfall. *Let go.*

"Vessel-of-Embers," I said, and my voice broke clean in half. "Fox. God. Freeloader. He slept against my boots for a whole winter and pretended it was for my benefit. He taught me that a gift kept shut is a door, not a weight. He is dying and he is smug about spring and I love him like — like family, like the family you choose, which is the only kind that ever chose me back—"

The pull became a flood. I felt the shop go — tallow and lavender, the creak of the third floorboard, the widow with the luck-knot, the dog by the river begging me to listen. I felt Greyhollow go, its crooked streets, its frozen market, its informants paid in bread. I felt my own name begin to thin, *Cyrus* becoming a word in a language I was losing letter by letter —

*Enough,* said the warden, and there was something in its voice I had not heard before, and it took me a moment, dissolving as I was, to recognize it.

Reverence.

*Signatory Cyrus Amberdale,* it said, using my whole name like a bell. *The covenant remembers you. Even when you do not. Especially then. Be bound.*

Blood on stone. Breath loosed. And the world —

* * *

— the world broke open.

Not loudly. That was the terrible and beautiful thing. The long winter did not shatter; it *melted*, all at once, everywhere, the way a held breath lets go. The ice ringing the Garden groaned once, deeply, like a ship changing its mind, and then ran with water. Frost smoked up off the grass and did not return. Far away — I would learn this later, from others' telling, because I was lying on black stone with an empty palm and an emptier head — far away the river cracked its skin from bank to bank, and the crops that had failed in a week stood up out of the thawed ground like sinners rising at a revival, and in Greyhollow the Ministry's criers fell silent mid-proclamation because nobody was listening anymore, everybody was outside, faces turned up to rain that fell soft and straight and mercifully ordinary.

At the stone, the carved names began to shine. One by one, rank upon rank, eight hundred years of signatories lit like embers catching — and among the beasts and gods, new lines wrote themselves, fresh-cut, glowing: the covenant remade, three-way and whole. Gods to keep the sky honest. Beasts to keep the earth speaking. Humans to keep the door between them open, and this time the door had a name carved under it, and the name was mine, and I did not know it.

Because I did not know anything.

I lay on warm stone in a green place and felt sunlight on my face and had no notion whatsoever of how I had come to be there, or what the shining carvings meant, or why an old red fox was dragging himself toward me across the grass with enormous effort and enormous satisfaction.

He reached me. He laid his head on my chest, over my heart, which was beating steadily and without any particular story attached to it.

"There," he said. "Told you. Warmer in spring." His breath rattled, slowed, gentled. "Smugness travels. Feel it? ...Good."

And Vessel-of-Embers, last vessel of a waking god, died the way a fire dies when its work is done: not extinguished but *finished*, sinking down into warmth, into the ground, into the roots of everything green. Where he lay the grass grew a foot in a heartbeat. Spring came up around his body like a tide. If you had stood there — and someone did — you would have sworn the whole Garden leaned toward that small red shape the way flowers lean toward light, taking him in, keeping him, making him useful one final time.

The woman came and knelt beside me. Her face was ruined with crying and she was smiling anyway, which should not be possible, and yet.

"Hello," I said, because it seemed polite. My voice sounded strange to me. New. "I'm sorry — do I know you?"

She laughed, that wet cracked laugh, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read — grief and devotion and iron resolve all braided together, like a knot tied deliberately around something she could not afford to lose.

"No," she said. "Not yet. But you're going to. My name is Wren, and I have a very long story to tell you, and we're going to start with a charm shop in a city called Greyhollow, and a man named Cyrus, who was —"

She paused. Chose her words the way you choose stones to step across a river.

"— who was the bravest coward I ever met. Can you walk?"

I could. I didn't know where we were going. I went anyway.

Behind us, the Garden rang with light, and the long winter retreated northward like a debt being paid, and somewhere in the warming ground a fox-god's smugness settled in for the season — patient, permanent, and, though I could not have said why, familiar.
