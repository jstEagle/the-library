## 20. Spring, Without Him

I have stood at a lot of altars in my life. I was raised on them, in fact — kneeled on cold stone before gods who never answered, recited words I didn't understand for men I trusted too much. So you would think that when the world's longest winter finally cracked like an egg and the thaw came roaring down the river valleys, I would have felt something holy about it.

What I actually felt was cold. Cold, and wet, and furious with grief, standing in the ruins of the Garden while the ice let go of everything except the one thing I wanted it to hold.

The covenant broke open on the third morning after the Reforging. I heard it before I saw it — a sound like a bell the size of a mountain being struck once, deep under the earth. Then every frozen branch in that impossible place shuddered, and the snow began to slide off the sleeping gods like blankets pulled from children, and green came up through the frost in a wave so fast I could watch it move, grass unfurling toward my boots as though the ground had been waiting twenty years — or two hundred — to stretch its legs.

And Cyrus was gone.

Not dead. I want to be very clear about that, because there is a difference and I spent that whole first morning learning exactly how wide it was. He lay where the warden-god had set him down, at the base of the great stone where the signatories' names were carved, breathing slow and even, his face smoothed of everything. The lines around his eyes. The small scar above his brow he'd never explained and I'd never asked about. The look he got when Vessel-of-Embers talked too long — fond, exasperated, helpless. All of it gone, wiped clean as a slate held under water.

He looked younger. That was the cruelest part. Twenty years of hiding had aged him around the edges, made him careful and quiet and slightly stooped, the way a man gets when he spends two decades listening for footsteps behind him. Now all of that was scoured away, and what remained was a stranger wearing his face, asleep in the springtime, peaceful as the dead but warm.

Vessel-of-Embers died an hour later, when the sun cleared the eastern wall of thorns. I want to record that properly, because foxes deserve better than footnotes. The old god dragged himself across the new grass — each step cost him visibly now, his ember-light guttering like a candle in a doorway — and curled himself against Cyrus's side, the way he had slept against his boots for a thousand miles. And then, instead of dying the way things die in winter, he simply... opened. His fur went gold, then translucent, then light, and the warmth of him sank into Cyrus's coat and into the ground beneath, and everywhere it touched, flowers came up. Small white ones. The kind that grow in churchyards, if anyone is still allowed to have churchyards.

I sat with both of them until midday. Then the warden-god came, moving through the Garden on root-legs, vast and patient and smelling of loam, and it regarded me with eyes like wells.

*He kept nothing,* it said, in that voice that arrives inside your chest rather than your ears. *You may keep what he gave you.*

"He didn't give me anything," I said. "I stole it. Every story he told me the night before — I stole them fair and square."

The warden did not smile. I don't believe it could. But something in the air around it loosened, the way a room does when someone decides not to press a point.

*Then carry them carefully,* it said. *Memory is heavier than stone and breaks easier.*

I know. Believe me, I know. I've been carrying other people's memories since I was seventeen years old, back when I helped seal the old gods away because I genuinely believed it was mercy, and I have spent every year since learning what mercy costs when you buy it on credit. I was not going to make the same mistake twice. Whatever else I had done wrong in my life — and the ledger is long, and written in a hand I don't like looking at — I was going to do this part correctly.

So I waited for him to wake.

* * *

He woke near dusk, when the Garden had gone amber and the sleeping gods were murmuring in their beds like a dormitory of old men. I was sitting three feet away, close enough to catch him, far enough not to frighten him. I'd rehearsed this. I want you to appreciate that: I, Wren, disgraced priestess, oath-breaker, woman who has faced down Ministry clerks and starving gods and her own considerable cowardice, sat in a field of resurrected grass rehearsing how to say hello to a man who no longer knew me.

His eyes opened. They were the same grey-brown they had always been. That undid me a little, right at the start, before I'd even gotten a word out — the sheer unfairness of the universe leaving a man his eyes and taking everything behind them.

"Hello," he said. His voice was hoarse with disuse. He looked at me the way you look at weather — politely, without recognition, cataloguing whether I was dangerous. "Am I — forgive me. Do I know you?"

"No," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Not anymore."

He sat up slowly. Vessel-of-Embers' white flowers scattered off his coat, and he caught one, turned it over in his fingers with a small frown — something reaching for a shape in the dark and finding only smooth wall.

"I don't remember anything," he said. Not panicked. That frightened me more than screaming would have. He said it the way you report a fact about the room: the door is closed, the sky is grey, I don't remember anything. "My name. Where I'm from. Whether I have — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Was there someone? A family?"

"There was a mother," I said. "She sold charms in Greyhollow and she taught you knots before she taught you letters. She died eight years ago, and you put a luck-knot on her grave marker every season, and you told me once that you did it because you couldn't bear the idea of her being forgotten by everyone at once."

He stared at me. And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave is eventually dug for me: he listened. Gently, attentively, the way he used to listen to dogs and sparrows and half-starved foxes — with his whole face. As if a stranger telling him his own grief were simply another animal asking to be heard.

"That sounds like someone worth being," he said. "I'm sorry. I wish I remembered her." And then, because the blankness in him had a kindness in it that the old Cyrus would have recognized as kin: "Do you? Remember her, I mean. You're crying."

"I'm not," I lied, with the fluency of a former priestess. "It's pollen. The whole Garden is full of it. It's a botanical ambush."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was a ghost of the shape of it, and I thought: good, that survived, some part of him is structural, load-bearing, built into the bones rather than the memories — and then I hated myself for auditing him like a building inspection, and then I did it again anyway, because grief makes accountants of us all.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"It's more name than most people get out of me. Count yourself lucky."

"And mine?"

I took a breath. Somewhere behind us, one of the sleeping gods rolled over, and the ground hummed.

"Cyrus Amberdale," I said. "Charm-seller. Beast-speaker. Last living signatory of the covenant that just saved the world. And the single most stubborn man I have ever met, which is saying something, because I've met bishops."

"Cyrus," he repeated. He tried it like a coat that might fit. "Cyrus Amberdale." Nothing lit behind his eyes. He looked at me with polite apology, this man who had given away his entire self so that dogs could bark and rivers could run and gods could stop starving, and he said, "Thank you. For telling me. I can tell none of it means anything to me yet, but — I'd like it to. Eventually. If that's allowed."

"It's allowed," I said. "In fact, it's the plan. Get up, Cyrus Amberdale. We're walking home."

* * *

Here is what the world looked like while we walked, because I promised myself I would notice it, since he couldn't.

The thaw moved ahead of us like a rumor. Rivers that had been grinding ice since autumn let go all at once; I watched the Thornwash throw off its skin of frost in a single convulsion and run brown and loud and alive. In the borderlands — the Country of Thorns, where we had heard thousands of beast-souls fraying like rope ends — the blight peeled back from the fields in patches. Not healed. Scarred. Winter leaves marks the way any long illness does, and the land will wear those marks for a generation. But wheat came up through the scars anyway, reckless and early, as if the soil intended to make up lost time through sheer spite.

And the animals. Gods, the animals. They came out of the hedgerows and the culverts and the burned barns, blinking, gaunt, disbelieving their own paws on softening ground. A hare stood in the middle of our path outside the drowned chapel and looked at Cyrus for a long moment — looked at him the way you look at a door you'd stopped believing opened — and then bolted, and I swear on every rite I ever botched, it was laughing.

Cyrus noticed none of it. Or rather, he noticed all of it, because he was Cyrus underneath whatever the Garden had left of him, and his attention went to small moving things the way water goes downhill. But it was a stranger's noticing now — aesthetic, unhaunted. He watched a fox kit tumble out of a bramble and said, "Look at that," with pure delight, and felt nothing tear in his chest, because there was nothing left in him that remembered tearing.

I carried both reactions for him. Mine and his. It was exhausting. I recommend it to no one.

At night he slept close to the fire — closer than politeness strictly required, which I chose to read as the body remembering what the mind had surrendered. Once, near the drowned chapel, he woke from a dream with his hand already half-raised in a knot-shape, fingers looping a pattern I recognized: the binding-knot, the one his mother taught him, the one he'd tied around Vessel-of-Embers' paw when the god was too weak to walk. He stared at his own hand for a moment.

"I know how to do that," he said, wondering. "Why do I know how to do that?"

"Some things go deeper than memory," I said. "Into the hands. Into the blood. Your family signed a covenant older than any kingdom in this country. Maybe the signing soaked in."

He flexed his fingers, tying and untying the ghost of the knot. "Who signed it? Me?"

"You," I said. "Your ancestor, technically. But the Garden doesn't split hairs, and neither do gods. It wanted a living Amberdale, and it got one."

"Did it hurt?" he asked. Simply. No dread, no self-pity — curiosity, the way you ask a doctor about an old scar. "Whatever it took?"

I looked at the fire. I thought about the night before the Reforging, when he had asked me to tell him stories of his own life so that some trace might survive inside mine, and we had talked until dawn — his mother, the shop, the fox asleep against his boots — and at the end of it he had thanked me, thanked me, for the loan of his own history.

"Yes," I said. "But he paid it gladly. He'd want you to know that. He wasn't tricked, and he wasn't brave about it in the way songs pretend people are brave. He was tired, and he loved specific, particular things — a city, a shop, a dead woman's knots, a talking fox — and he decided those things were worth more than his remembering them. That's all. That's the whole heroism. He did arithmetic."

Cyrus was quiet a while.

"The fox," he said finally. "Tell me about the fox."

So I did. All of it — the collapse in the cellar, the god-cadence voice, the thousand miles, the way he slept against Cyrus's boots like a sentry, the way he spent himself mile by mile keeping the road open, and the end of him, in the grass, opening into warmth and white flowers. Cyrus listened with his chin on his knees, firelight moving on his face, and when I finished he reached down and picked up one of the little white blossoms that had lodged in his cuff — they were everywhere now, growing wherever Vessel-of-Embers' warmth had passed, a whole continent of tiny memorials.

"He loved me," Cyrus said. Not a question.

"With the entirety of his ridiculous divine heart," I said. "And you loved him back. And neither of you ever said it in plain words, because you were both cowards in complementary directions. It was very tedious to travel with."

That got the almost-smile again, wider this time. "You're funny," he said, "for a priestess."

"Ex-priestess. And I'll thank you to remember I was funny before you forgot me."

"I wish I hadn't," he said, and it landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there. "Forgotten you, I mean. You seem like someone it would be a real loss not to know."

I fed the fire and said nothing, because if I had said anything it would have been either too much or a lie, and I had sworn off lies somewhere around the Country of Thorns.

* * *

Greyhollow announced itself two days later by smell — river-mud and coal smoke and the particular sourness of a city that has been afraid for months and is only now daring to hope. The walls rose up out of the morning mist the same as always, squat and grey and patched, and I felt something in my chest loosen that I hadn't realized was clenched, because however much that city had failed its beasts and buried its gods, it was still the place where Cyrus Amberdale had hidden a miracle behind a charm counter for twenty years, and some part of me considered it his.

He stopped on the road and looked at it.

"I live here," he said. Testing the sentence. "Don't I."

"You do. Above the shop. Third door past the cooper's, the one with the crooked awning. Mind the third stair; it complains."

He walked beside me through the gate without incident — the Ministry checkpoints had dissolved along with the informant bounties, their clerks suddenly finding themselves employed by a government very interested in forgetting its own recent behavior — and I watched Greyhollow take him back without recognizing him. The market was reopening. Stall-frames going up, awnings shaking off snow, a fishwife arguing with a cooper about prices with the joyous viciousness of people who had survived something together. Nobody looked twice at the tall man with the empty eyes and the white flower in his buttonhole. Greyhollow had always been excellent at not looking twice.

A dog trotted past us in the lane — a scruffy yellow thing, ribs still showing — and it stopped. Turned. Stared at Cyrus with its head cocked, ears forward, tail making small uncertain movements, the way dogs do when they've caught a scent that means everything and nothing.

And I saw Cyrus stop too. Saw him tilt his head, that same angle, that same listening posture I had watched a hundred times on the road — except now there was no answering light in it, no gift, just a man pausing beside a strange dog in a street.

The dog whined once. Came closer. Put its nose against his hand and pressed, hard, insistent, the way animals press against a door they know opens.

"I'm sorry," Cyrus said to it gently. "Is something wrong? Are you hungry?"

The dog looked at him. Looked at me. Looked back at him, and made a sound low in its throat that I have no translation for, though I have spent my whole life translating — something between confusion and mourning, a creature checking a lock it was certain had been open yesterday.

"He can't hear you," I told it quietly, kneeling. "Not yet. But he's home now, and he's staying, and I am going to spend every day telling him who he was until some of it sticks. So come back. Come back tomorrow, and the day after. He was the best listener this city ever had, and I refuse to believe the Garden got all of him."

The dog held my eyes for a moment — and I would swear, I would stand in any court in the land and swear, that it understood. Then it licked Cyrus's hand once, decisively, like a signature, and trotted off down the lane toward the river.

Cyrus watched it go. Then he looked at me, and there was something in his blankness that was not quite blank — a question forming, patient as a seed.

"Why does it feel," he said slowly, "like I've been somewhere? Like the whole street is familiar in the wrong direction?" He touched the white flower in his buttonhole. "Like I'm remembering forward instead of backward?"

I looked at my charm seller, my beast-speaker, my last signatory, standing in his own city like a ghost attending its own funeral — and I smiled, because someone in this story had to keep believing in impossible things, and I had the seniority.

"Because you are," I said. "Come inside. I'll put the kettle on, and I'll start at the beginning. It's a long story. It has a fox in it, and a river, and the worst winter in a hundred years — and you're in every part of it. You're not going to believe a word of it."

He followed me through the crooked door of the charm shop. Behind us, out in the thawing street, the yellow dog barked once — bright, hopeful, certain — and ran toward the river to tell the others he was home.

The kettle went on. The third stair complained. And upstairs in the cellar, in the dark under the floorboards, something small stirred in the straw — something with red fur and new-opened eyes, born of warmth that had pooled there all winter, waiting with a fox's perfect patience for the sound of a voice it already knew.
