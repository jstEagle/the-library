## 21. Telling Him His Own Story

He didn't know his own name, so I gave him the wrong one first.

That's how it started — an accident, or maybe not. He woke in the Garden gentle and blank as new snow, and when I said "Cyrus," he looked at me the way you look at a stranger who's using your cup without asking. Polite. Faintly alarmed. So I tried again: "Sebastian." And something in his face unclenched, like a knot loosening that he hadn't known was tied.

I've thought about that a great deal since. The gods took his memories, but they couldn't take the shape of him, and apparently the shape of him had been leaning toward Sebastian for years without either of us noticing. Names are funny that way. They fit or they don't, and no amount of paperwork changes it.

We walked home from the Garden through a world that was thawing by the hour. Snowmelt ran loud in every ditch. Birds were making absolute fools of themselves. And Cyrus — Sebastian — kept stopping to listen to things I couldn't hear, and smiling, and saying nothing, because whatever they were saying to him, he seemed to feel no obligation to share it with me. Twenty years of hiding what he was, and even with the memories scraped out of him, the habit of discretion survived. Some things go deeper than memory. That was the first thing I learned that summer, and honestly it terrified me more than any of the rest of it.

Because if the gift survived, what else had?

* * *

We reopened the charm shop in the third week of spring, mostly because standing in an empty house staring at each other was becoming unbearable for both of us. He swept the floor. I dusted the luck-knots. He held up a half-finished braid of red thread and asked me, very seriously, what it was for.

"Luck," I said.

"Does it work?"

"It worked once on a widow named Marta whose husband drowned. She came back the next winter and bought three more."

He turned the knot over in his hands. "Did she need them?"

"No," I said. "But she needed to buy them. Sometimes the charm is just permission to keep going."

He nodded slowly and set it down with the care you'd give a sleeping animal, and I had to turn away and pretend to reorganize the dried lavender, because that was exactly the sort of thing Cyrus used to say, except Cyrus would have said it with a shrug and a joke at the end, and this man said it plainly, like scripture. Same soul, different voice. I was going to have to get used to mourning someone who was still standing in front of me.

Halvers arrived the day after we opened, carrying a leather folder and the expression of a man arriving at his own execution and finding it postponed indefinitely. He'd fled the Ministry in the autumn; officially he was a deserter, unofficially he was nothing at all, which in Greyhollow is a much more dangerous category. But the Ministry had larger problems than one clerk with a conscience. Half their archives had flooded when the river rose during the long winter, and the other half had been burned by clerks covering their tracks, and the new government — such as it was — had neither the appetite nor the staff to hunt down everyone who'd ever done anything.

"I kept the page," Halvers said, setting the folder on the counter between us. "The one I didn't burn. The original covenant-breaking order. Signed, dated, sealed. It proves the Ministry manufactured the exile of the gods. It also proves your family signed the covenant in the first place." He looked at Sebastian, who was watching him with open, guileless curiosity. "It proves everything you told us. Everything she's going to tell you."

"Tell me?" Sebastian said.

I took a breath. Here it was, then. The whole ridiculous, enormous thing, laid out on a charm-shop counter next to a jar of dried nettle.

"Yes," I said. "Sit down. This is going to take all summer."

* * *

I want to be honest about how strange it was, telling a man his own life.

It wasn't like telling stories to children, where the listener leans forward and gasps in the right places. Sebastian listened the way scholars listen — attentively, skeptically, asking questions I couldn't always answer. When I described the night the fox collapsed in his cellar, he interrupted immediately.

"You let a wild animal into your home? You, who sold charms to people who believed magic was real, and you never once wondered if it might be?"

"You didn't wonder either. You broke twenty years of caution in one night because a starving fox looked at you with god's eyes and asked."

"That doesn't sound like me."

"It was exactly you," I said. "It's still you. You just don't remember doing it."

He frowned at that, and I watched him do the thing he did all summer: reach inside himself for some corroboration, some echo, and find only smooth blankness. Then he'd look at his own hands, or the cellar door, or the red thread, hunting for evidence. The body remembered. The habits remembered. Only the man was missing, and we meant to rebuild him out of whatever materials we could find.

Some nights it went well. I told him about his mother, who'd taught him beast-speech in secret behind the shop when he was small, using the market cats as practice partners, and he laughed — actually laughed — and said, "She sounds terrifying," and I said, "She was," and for a moment it was easy, it was almost pleasant, two people trading stories over tea while the evening light came gold through the window.

Other nights it went badly. The night I told him about the raid — Halvers kicking in his door, Vessel-of-Embers fleeing through the sewers, Cyrus lying to the interrogators with a fluency that had sickened him — Sebastian went very quiet and then said, "You're describing a coward."

"I'm describing a survivor."

"He abandoned the fox."

"He spent the next month trying to find it again, and then he crossed a country of thorns to carry it to the Garden, and it died warm and content in his arms while the winter cracked apart. That's not abandonment. That's a man who made the best choice available in a terrible moment and hated himself forever afterward for it."

Sebastian stared at the table. "You loved him," he said. Not a question. "The man you're describing."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're telling me about him like he's dead."

"He is," I said, before I could stop myself, and then I saw his face and wished the gods had taken my tongue instead of his memories. "No. That's not — I'm sorry. That's cruel. He's not dead. He's sitting across from me. I just —"

"You miss him," Sebastian said quietly. "And I'm not him."

"You're him," I said. "You're him with the pages torn out. We're going to write them back in. Some of them will be different. That's not a tragedy, Sebastian. That's just authorship."

He considered that for a long time. Outside, the summer insects were singing themselves hoarse, and somewhere down the street a dog barked twice — and I saw Sebastian tilt his head toward the sound, listening, and nod slightly, as though agreeing to something. Whatever the beasts said to him now, he answered gladly. That part had come back whole.

"All right," he said finally. "Tell me about the fox again. From the beginning. Slower this time."

* * *

Halvers came every Thursday.

He was an odd addition to our little storytelling circle — a former Ministry clerk, dry as August grass, who corroborated my accounts with the precision of a man reading minutes aloud. Where I gave the story its heart, Halvers gave it its bones. Dates. Distances. The exact wording of the Ministry directive ordering the hunt for listeners. The number of prayers logged as answered in the month before the winter fell (four hundred and twelve; he'd counted).

"Why do you remember all this?" Sebastian asked him one evening.

"Because remembering was my job," Halvers said. "For eleven years I filed other people's lives into boxes so the Ministry could decide which ones mattered. When I burned that page, it was the first time I'd ever chosen what to forget." He tapped the folder. "Keeping this is the first time I chose what to remember. I find I'm rather protective of the habit."

Sebastian nodded slowly. I could see him filing that away too — the idea that memory isn't just what happens to you, but what you decide to keep. He was learning to think of himself as a curator rather than a ruin. I'd like to claim I taught him that, but I suspect he figured it out on his own, probably around the time he started arguing with my versions of events.

Because he did argue. That was the surprise of the summer, the thing that turned grief into something almost like joy. He pushed back. When I told him he'd been lonely — twenty years of silence, twenty years of pretending — he said, "Was I? Or were you lonely for me?" And I opened my mouth to object and found nothing there, because he was right. I had built a whole Cyrus in my head, complete and pitiable, and part of my storytelling was an attempt to reinstall that Cyrus, my Cyrus, the one I'd grieved. He caught me at it more than once. He was gentle about it, usually. Usually.

"You keep giving me the sad parts first," he complained in midsummer. "The hiding, the fear, the guilt. Didn't anything good happen to this man?"

I blinked at him. "Of course it did. He — the mornings. He loved the mornings at the market, before the crowds, when the frost was still on the awnings and the fishmonger's cat would sit on his counter and gossip. He loved terrible wine. He loved —" I stopped.

"Go on," he said, with the ghost of a smile. "He loved what?"

"You," I said. "He loved you, whoever you were going to become. He talked about it once, the night before the reforging. He said he wanted to be someone who chose his own name. I thought it was just bravery talking. Turns out he was making plans."

Sebastian was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "Tell me about that night. The last one. All of it. Every story he asked you to tell."

So I did. I told him how we'd sat up until dawn in the shadow of the Garden, and he'd asked me to hold his memories the way you'd ask a friend to water your plants while you're away — casually, as if it were a small thing, as if he weren't handing me his entire self. I told him about the fox asleep against his boots. About his mother's hands. About the shop, and the luck-knots, and the widow Marta. And Sebastian listened with his eyes closed, and when I finished he said, "Thank you," and then, "I think I'll keep all of those," and I understood that something had shifted — that he had stopped being a man being told a story and become a man selecting one.

Choosing. That was the word for what he did all summer. He chose.

* * *

By late August he was keeping a notebook.

Not a diary — he was explicit about that. "A ledger," he called it, in a tone that suggested Halvers had been a bad influence. In it he wrote the truths he'd decided to adopt, and — this was the part that undid me — the truths he'd decided to decline. There was an entire page titled *Things I Am Not*, and I confess I peeked at it once when he stepped out to the market. *I am not a coward,* it began. *I am not the Ministry's enemy, whatever Halvers says about the archive. I am not Wren's dead friend.* And beneath that, in smaller letters, almost apologetic: *I am not obligated to grieve on schedule.*

He came back in with a paper bag of plums and caught me holding the notebook, and instead of being angry he just looked tired and fond. "Well?" he said. "Any corrections?"

"One," I said. "You are obligated to eat more than plums. I've seen your larder."

"Noted for the ledger," he said, and sat down across from me, and for a while we just existed together in the golden late light, two people who had carried each other through a winter that had nearly ended the world, sharing fruit.

"Do you ever wish they'd left him alone?" he asked suddenly. "The old him. Do you wish the gods had taken someone else?"

I thought about lying. I thought about how easy it would be, and how he'd catch me anyway, because catching people in small kindnesses had apparently survived the forgetting along with everything else.

"Every day," I said. "For about a month. And then I watched you reopen this shop, and answer the river when it spoke, and choose your own name out of thin air, and I stopped wishing. He'd have hated being wished back. He told me so, that last night. He said, 'Don't you dare resurrect me out of pity. Let me be a story you tell well.'"

Sebastian smiled. "You tell it well," he said. "Mostly. Your fox needs work. Vessel-of-Embers wouldn't have said 'please' that many times."

"Oh, he absolutely would."

"He wouldn't. Gods don't beg."

"This one did. He begged you for sanctuary, and you gave it, and it cost you everything, and he repaid you by dying warm." I leaned forward. "That's the whole story, Sebastian. That's the covenant in miniature. Everything costs, and everything repays, and the trick is deciding what's worth the price."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached over and turned the notebook around, tore out a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in careful letters: *Worth the price.* Underneath, he wrote my name. Then Halvers'. Then, after a pause, *Vessel-of-Embers*. Then his mother. Then Marta the widow, which made me laugh despite myself.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Signing," he said. "You said the covenant bound gods, beasts, and humans. Well. I'm human, the beasts talk to me daily whether I like it or not, and as for the gods —" he glanced toward the cellar door, where a fox kit had been sleeping against the warm stones all afternoon, dreaming whatever kits dream — "I expect they'll make their position known."

As if summoned, the kit stirred, stretched, and trotted up the stairs. It sat down in front of Sebastian and looked at him with bright amber eyes, and I saw him go still — that particular stillness, the listening stillness, the one I'd know anywhere.

"Well?" I asked. "What does it say?"

Sebastian's expression changed. Slowly. The warmth draining into something older and heavier, the look of a man hearing news he'd hoped was finished arriving.

"It says," he said quietly, "that the river wants to speak with me. Tonight. And that it says the Garden has sent a messenger." He looked up at me, and there was something new in his face — not the blankness of spring, nor the careful curation of summer, but something chosen, something his. "It seems the story isn't over. It seems I'm being asked to sign something else."

He stood, and picked up the notebook, and tucked it inside his coat like a covenant of his own.

"Come on, then," said Sebastian, who had once been Cyrus, who was nobody's copy and entirely his own. "Let's go hear what the river has to say."
