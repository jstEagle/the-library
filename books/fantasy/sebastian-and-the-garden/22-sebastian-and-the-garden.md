## 22. Sebastian and the Garden

They tell me I used to be called Cyrus.

I say "they" because that's how it works now. Wren tells me things, and Halvers corroborates, and sometimes a neighbor leans over the fence and adds a detail nobody asked for, and all of it goes into the ledger I keep in my head — or rather, into the new ledger, because the old one burned. That's the arrangement. That's the price. I paid it in the Garden with blood on stone and my own name spoken backward, and what came out the other side was a man with no past and an alarming amount of skill at tying luck-knots.

Here's what nobody warned me about: being empty doesn't feel like being empty. It feels like being full of someone else's furniture. I knew how to braid a charm against colic before anyone taught me. My hands went to the right shelf without instruction. When Wren first said *Cyrus* to me, back in the spring, something behind my ribs turned over like a dog hearing its name from a stranger's mouth — recognition without ownership.

I didn't want to be Cyrus. Not because the name was bad, but because it wasn't mine. It was a coat left on a hook by a man who'd gone somewhere I couldn't follow. Wearing it felt like theft.

So we're going to talk about the day I chose a different one.

* * *

It happened in high summer, on a morning so green it looked rehearsed. The long winter had cracked and retreated months ago — Vessel-of-Embers had seen to that, spending himself down to nothing so the thaw could come — and Greyhollow had responded the way Greyhollow responds to any miracle: by complaining about the mud and then charging admission to look at it.

The river ran loud and bright past the market district. Children fished in it now. Actual fish, actual children, neither of them frozen. There were days I still caught myself staring at moving water like it was a trick.

Wren walked beside me carrying a basket of early apples, which she did not eat, because Wren treats food as a rumor she'll investigate later. She'd been telling me about myself again — the Tuesday installments, as I'd come to think of them.

"You hated mornings," she said. "Cyrus, I mean. He'd open the shop late and blame the ice on the canal."

"Did the ice ever actually come from the canal?"

"Once." She smiled at the basket instead of at me. "The rest of the time he just wanted another hour alone with his thoughts. He was very good at being alone. He practiced for twenty years."

I turned that over. "And you loved him anyway."

"I loved him *because*," she said. "Any fool can love someone easy."

We crossed the market square, past the charm stalls — real ones now, licensed, the Ministry having discovered after everything that outlawing magic was like outlawing weather — and I stopped at the corner where a shop stood shuttered. My shop. *His* shop. Halvers had the deed in a drawer somewhere, along with a single scorched page from the Ministry archives that he guarded like a relic, which I suppose it was.

"I want to reopen it," I said.

Wren looked at me for a long moment. "You don't remember the shop."

"I remember what you've told me. And I remember standing in it last week, and my hands knowing where everything went." I shrugged. "That's more than most people get. Most people inherit their lives secondhand too — they just call it family."

She set down the basket. She does that when she's about to take something seriously; the basket is her excuse to stop walking.

"It won't be his shop," she said carefully. "If you reopen it, it will be yours. Are you ready for that? People will look at you and see him."

"People see what they need to," I said. "I'd rather they learned to see me."

* * *

The fox kit arrived three days before the shop did.

I found her in the cellar — *my* cellar now, though the stones remembered other feet — curled in the exact spot where a dying god had once collapsed and asked a stranger for sanctuary. She was red as a struck match, half-starved, and she looked up at me with the particular insolence of her kind.

"Well," she said. "You're not what I expected."

I sat down on the cellar steps, because you don't loom over a talking fox; it's rude. "What did you expect?"

"Bigger. Sadder." She yawned enormously. "Embers told us about you. Before the end. He said the speaker would be remade, and that we should find him when he woke, and that he'd be grumpy about it."

"He wasn't wrong about the grumpy."

"No," she agreed. "But he said to tell you it's all right. That forgetting isn't the same as losing. That a story retold is still true." She licked a paw, elaborately casual. "He said a lot of things while he was dying. Gods are chatty at the end. You'd have liked him."

"I'm told I did."

"Mm." She considered me. "I'm going to stay, by the way. In the cellar. It's warm and it smells like embers, which is either sentiment or indigestion, and I refuse to examine which."

"What do I call you?"

"Nothing yet," she said. "Names are earned. Ask me again when I've done something worth one."

And that, apparently, was that. I went upstairs and told Wren we had a tenant, and she laughed — really laughed, the first time I'd heard it since I'd known her, which is to say since she'd known me twice — and brought the fox a sausage she claimed was for herself.

* * *

The shop reopened on a Thursday, because Thursdays are honest days; nothing expected of them.

I kept the name off the sign for a week. Customers came anyway — word travels fast in a city that spent a winter learning what prayer costs — and they bought luck-knots and rain-charms and the little braided bells against nightmares, and my hands made all of it without asking my opinion first. An old woman bought a knot for her grandson's boat and told me I looked exactly like the previous owner, and I said, "People keep saying that. Was he any good?"

"He was the best," she said, and pressed my hand, and left before I could ask anything else, which is the Greyhollow way.

On the eighth day, Wren arrived with paint and a ladder and an expression that meant business.

"You can't leave it blank forever," she said. "A shop with no name is a promise nobody keeps."

"I know what I want to put there. I just haven't said it out loud yet."

"Then say it."

I took the brush. And here is the thing I understood, standing on that ladder with the whole street pretending not to watch: names aren't found, they're *made*. Cyrus Amberdale hadn't been born; he'd been assembled — out of secrecy, out of twenty years of silence, out of a gift buried so deep it nearly died. If a man could build himself out of hiding, then a man could build himself out of something better. Out of truth. Out of the story Wren had spent a summer telling him, every word of it offered freely, none of it demanded back.

Not erasure. Authorship.

I painted four letters, and stepped down, and looked at them until they stopped looking strange:

**SEBASTIAN**

"Sebastian," Wren read aloud, testing it. Then, softer: "Why that one?"

"There was a boy in one of your stories," I said. "Chapter six, if your Tuesday installments have chapters. A boy who fed a stray dog in the snow even though meat was scarce, and the dog followed him home, and he pretended to be annoyed for a year." I looked at her. "Cyrus never admitted he loved that dog. But you told it like it mattered. So. Sebastian. The man who feeds strays."

Wren didn't cry. Wren almost never cries. But she held the paintbrush very tightly for a while, and when she finally spoke her voice was steady in the way that costs something.

"He would have liked that," she said. "Being remembered as kind. He never believed he was."

* * *

Autumn came, and the shop became mine in the way that matters — not through paper but through habit. The fox kit (still unnamed, still insufferable) slept against the cellar stove and critiqued my inventory. Halvers came by on rest days with confiscated records he was slowly, quietly declassifying, reading me the parts of my own history I hadn't heard yet. "You once sold a love charm to a Ministry captain," he told me, deadpan. "It worked. On him. He married the woman he already loved and credited the knot. We never arrested you because the paperwork would've been embarrassing."

"That's the only reason?"

"The paperwork," Halvers said, "was always the only reason for anything."

And Wren came every evening, and told me Tuesdays, and stayed later each week, until staying stopped being an event and became simply what evenings contained.

Then, on the first cold night of the season, the river spoke.

* * *

I was locking up when I heard it — that voice beneath the water-sound, old and patient and glad. The same voice that had started all of it, a lifetime and one death ago, when a man named Cyrus had stood on a frozen bank and listened to something impossible.

*Speaker,* the river said. *Sebastian.*

Both names. It gave me both, the way you'd hand someone their old letters and their new ones together, because both belong to them.

*I'm listening,* I said. Twenty years ago, those words would have cost Cyrus everything. They cost me nothing. That's the difference hiding makes versus choosing: one drains you, one fills you.

*The salmon run is early,* the river said. *The pike are confused. And there is a heron downstream who has been asking for you by name — your new one, which amuses her greatly.*

"What does she want?"

*To complain. Herons complain. But also—* the current paused, the way a person pauses when the next words matter— *the young gods are settling. The covenant holds. They remember who reforged it, even if he doesn't. They would like to thank him.*

"Tell them—" I started, and stopped, and thought about it properly. Tell them what? That thanks weren't necessary? That was a lie dressed as modesty. That I wanted reward? Also a lie, and a smaller one.

"Tell them I'll come to the bank tomorrow," I said. "Tell them the speaker keeps his appointments. Both of him."

The river laughed — a sound like light on moving water — and receded, satisfied.

I stood a while in the dark outside my own shop, under my own name, breathing cold air that smelled like woodsmoke and apples and the green memory of summer. Behind me, through the floorboards, I heard the fox kit stir and mutter something derisive in her sleep. Somewhere across the city, Wren was probably awake, drafting tomorrow's installment. Somewhere in a drawer, a burned page kept its testimony.

Cyrus Amberdale had hidden for twenty years and paid for the world with everything he was. Sebastian had been handed the remainder and asked to make something of it.

I intend to. I intend to make something *good*.

Tomorrow I'll walk to the bank and answer the river gladly, in front of whoever happens to be watching, because I live in a city where that's allowed now, and because I spent — *he* spent — long enough being careful.

And next Tuesday, when Wren arrives with her basket and her stories, I'm going to ask her to start from the beginning again. All of it. The widow and the luck-knot, the dog in the snow, the fox at the door.

Because a story retold is still true.

And this one, at last, is mine.
