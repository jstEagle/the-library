## 1. The Quiet Trade

The widow Grell arrived at my door with her grief folded up small, the way people in Vellamere carry everything illegal — tucked against the ribs, wrapped in ordinary cloth, passed hand to hand like contraband.

"Marchetti," she said. Not a greeting. A transaction opening.

"Mistress Grell." I stepped aside to let her into the shop, which was not a shop, because shops selling what I sold had been burned twice in the last decade and the Wardens kept excellent lists. Officially, I was a bone-carver of household goods: buttons, combs, knife handles, the little carved saints nobody prayed to anymore but everybody still hung over their doors out of habit. Unofficially, I was the reason half the Ashmarket district slept through the night.

She sat where I pointed, on the stool by the window that caught the last grey light, and she unwrapped her bundle with the care of someone handling an egg that might already be broken. Inside: a knucklebone. Human, by the size of it, though she'd have told me it was from a pig if I asked, because even now, five years after the Hollowing, people lied to me about bones as though the lie were a courtesy.

"My husband," she said. "Tobias. The fever took him eleven days ago."

"I'm sorry for it."

"Don't be sorry. Be quick." Her hands were steady. Her eyes weren't. "I can't sleep. Every time I close my eyes I hear him moving about downstairs, and when I go down there's nothing, and I know — I *know* — it's just my head, but knowing doesn't stop it." She pushed the knucklebone across my worktable. "They say you make charms. Charms that quiet things."

"They say correctly."

"And they work?"

I picked up the bone and turned it in the light. "They work."

What I did not say — what I would never say, not to her, not to anyone with coin or without it — was this: your husband is not haunting you, Mistress Grell. Your husband is standing behind your left shoulder at this very moment, asking me to tell you where he hid the second ledger, the one with the real accounts, because he's worried you'll sell the shop too cheap. He has been saying it for eleven days. He will keep saying it until someone carves him quiet.

That was the trade. That was always the trade. Grieving families came to me wanting silence, and I sold them silence, and the dead got silence whether they wanted it or not, and I got to keep breathing on the right side of the law.

Survival, in Vellamere, meant shrinking. I'd learned that lesson young and relearned it daily, the way you relearn a limp.

* * *

Let me explain what the Hollowing did, briefly, because everyone in this city explains it differently and most of them are wrong.

Five years ago, magic died. Not faded — died, the way a man dies, suddenly and messily and in front of witnesses. The court sorcerers went silent mid-incantation. The great workings of the realm — the weather-wards, the healing springs, the lamps that burned without oil — guttered out all in the same hour, an hour no two accounts agree upon, which should have been our first clue that something stranger than simple death had occurred. King Aldous appeared on his balcony the next morning wearing his crown and his face and nothing else of himself, and he announced the Edict of Ash: magic was outlawed, its practitioners marked, its relics confiscated and burned.

The city, being terrified and recently deprived of miracles, agreed enthusiastically. We are a practical people, Vellamere. We burn what frightens us and then we build nicer buildings on the ash and call it progress.

What the Edict didn't account for — what the Wardens of Ash still haven't accounted for, thank every indifferent god — is that some gifts don't live in spells. Some gifts live in the marrow. Mine lives in mine. I hear the dead. Always have, since I was six years old and my grandmother's ghost corrected my table manners from across the kitchen. It isn't spellwork. There's nothing to confiscate. It's closer to hearing than to sorcery — an ear tuned to a frequency the living pretend doesn't exist.

So I survived the purge by making myself small. I stopped answering the dead aloud. I stopped telling widows what their husbands wanted said. I learned to carve charms that did one narrow thing — muffled the dead's voices near a sleeper, eased the pressure of a haunting down to a whisper — and I priced them like trinkets and called them folk remedies, old wives' comfort, nothing the ledger-men needed to trouble themselves over.

A gift shrunk to a whisper. A life shrunk to fit inside it. Some nights I lay awake doing the arithmetic of erasure and wondering which of us was winning: me, or the silence I'd built around myself like a second set of walls.

* * *

Mistress Grell watched me work, which clients always do, as though watching might hurry the carving along or catch me cheating.

The knucklebone was Tobias's, she'd admitted once the door was shut — dug from the grave, which was itself a hanging offense, corpse-theft being one of the Edict's favorite categories. Grave-robbing for love. The Wardens would call it desecration. I called it Tuesday.

I set the bone in my clamp and took up the finest of my gravers, the one with the worn rosewood handle my mother had held before me, and I began.

Here is what nobody understands about carving a quieting charm: you're not carving the bone. You're carving the *conversation*. Bone remembers — that's the whole secret of my trade, the thing the Edict never grasped because the Edict was written by men who thought memory lived in paper and crowns and official seals. Bone remembers everything the body ever did and much of what it witnessed, and the dead who cling to their bones cling to those memories the way drowning men cling to spars.

So I cut the channels that let a voice run along the surface of the thing, and then I cut the grooves that dam those channels, and the result is a bone that holds its dead the way a jar holds smoke: present, but going nowhere.

"You're good at that," Grell said. It wasn't praise exactly. It had the flat tone of a woman confirming she hadn't wasted her money.

"I've had practice."

"How long does it take? To learn."

"Longer than you'd think. Shorter than you'd fear." I blew bone-dust from the first groove. "It'll be ready tomorrow evening. Two silver, and you'll tell anyone who asks that you bought a comb."

"A comb."

"The finest comb in Vellamere. Carved from ox horn. You'll show it to your neighbors and complain about the price. This is important, Mistress Grell. Complain loudly."

Something moved through her face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile used to live before grief redecorated. "And the charm. Will it hurt him? Tobias. Will it hurt him to be quieted?"

There it was. The question under the question, the one they all carried and almost none of them asked, because asking meant admitting the dead were *somewhere*, and admitting that meant the Wardens might come asking why you believed it.

I set down my graver. "No," I said. "It doesn't hurt. It's more like..." I searched for the honest comparison and found one I'd never given a client before. "You know how a room full of talkers goes quiet when the candles are snuffed? Everyone's still there. They just agree, all at once, to rest."

Her breath caught. "He talks? You can—"

"I carve buttons," I said. "Combs. Knife handles. Saints for over doors. Two silver, tomorrow evening, and you bought a comb."

The shutters came down over her eyes, slow, obedient. She knew the game; she'd simply forgotten, for a moment, that we were playing it. She stood, gathered her shawl, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame.

"He was a good man," she said, to the doorframe, to the street, to no one. "Whatever the priests say happens after. He was good."

Behind her left shoulder, faint as frost on glass, Tobias Grell said: *Tell her the ledger's under the third floorboard. Tell her I never once regretted marrying her, not even the year of the goats.*

I said nothing. I carved buttons. I had gotten very good, these five years, at carving buttons while the dead recited their unfinished business directly into my skull, and if that sounds like a small cruelty, I assure you it is a large one, practiced daily, until it wears a groove in the soul the way water wears stone.

The widow left. I locked the door behind her and stood a moment in the grey light with Tobias's message sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal.

Then I did what I always did with the coals. I banked them. I told myself the third floorboard was none of my affair, that the year of the goats was between husband and wife, that a woman who sold whispers could not afford to start handing out whole sentences. I told myself survival meant shrinking, and shrinking meant exactly this: carrying other people's last words like stones in the shoe, forever, and walking anyway.

Most days I believed it.

* * *

That evening I made supper of barley and onions and ate it alone in the back room, which doubled as my workshop, my bedroom, and my fortress. The dead gathered in the corners the way cats do — not demanding, just present. Old Marta from three doors down, dead nine years, humming a lullaby nobody living remembered. A soldier with half a jaw who'd wandered in from some battlefield two generations back and never located the exit. My mother, who had died with my name in her mouth and had apparently decided to spend eternity finishing the sentence.

*Rosalind, lock the door, Rosalind, wear your scarf, Rosalind, don't trust the tall ones—*

"I don't trust anyone, Mama," I said to my soup. "You raised me in Vellamere."

The soldier laughed, or made the sound that remained of laughing. Marta hummed louder. This was my other life, the one that existed nowhere but inside these four walls: a crowded room that looked empty, a family assembled entirely of the departed, a woman talking to her soup and meaning every word of it.

If the Wardens ever heard me — truly heard, not the muttering they'd dismiss as a carver's habit but the *answering*, the call-and-response of it — I'd burn in Ashfall Square with the rest of the marked. So I kept my voice low and my shutters closed and my answers short, and I told myself, again, the arithmetic of erasure: a small life is a survivable life. A whisper cannot be prosecuted. Shrink, Rosalind. Keep shrinking until there's nothing left to hang.

I was halfway through believing it when the knocking started.

Not at my door. At the *street door*, the heavy one, past the shopfront — past midnight, during curfew, when every honest soul in Vellamere was behind barred windows and every dishonest one had better sense than to announce themselves.

Three knocks. Then silence. Then three more, softer, the knock of someone who has begun to hope nobody answers.

The dead went still. All of them at once, the way birds go quiet before weather turns. Marta stopped humming mid-note. My mother's voice cut off with my name half-spoken. Even the jawless soldier, who had not been fully present since the reign of Aldous's father, lifted whatever remained of his attention toward the door.

In five years of this hidden life, the dead had never once gone silent for anything.

I put down my spoon. I picked up my heaviest graver — not a weapon, but the closest thing this workshop owned — and I crossed the dark shop, and I opened the street door onto the cold.

A boy stood on my step. Barefoot, in this season. Nine years old, perhaps ten, thin as a candle-end, with the particular stillness of a child who has learned that stillness is safety. His eyes were the worst part. Not frightened — I'd have known what to do with frightened. They were *full*. Full the way a cup is full, filled to the brim with something older than his face, something that had no business looking out through a child's eyes at a bone-carver at midnight.

He opened his mouth.

And he spoke — not Vellamere's tongue, not any tongue I'd heard in thirty years of listening to the accumulated dead of a whole city — but a language like river-stones turning under water, like the sound bone makes when it grows, like the first sentence ever carved into the first grave. I didn't understand a word of it.

Every dead soul in my workshop understood every word.

I know because they answered. All of them. At once. In his language, fluently, joyfully, the way exiles answer in their mother tongue — and the sound of it coming through my own walls, my own hidden ears, was so sudden and so vast that I gripped the doorframe and forgot, for the first time since the Edict of Ash, to be afraid of being heard.

The boy looked past me into the dark shop, at the crowd only I could see, and his brimming eyes went wide with recognition.

Then he stepped inside, uninvited, and closed the door behind him with the careful hands of someone who had done it many times before, in many cities, always just ahead of something worse.
