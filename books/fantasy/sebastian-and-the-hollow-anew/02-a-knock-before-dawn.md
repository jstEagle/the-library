## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The dead keep terrible hours.

I'd like to tell you they visit at dignified moments — candlelit vigils, solemn anniversaries, the respectable hush of a churchyard at noon. They don't. They come at three in the morning, when I'm elbow-deep in a femur that refuses to hold its charm, and they murmur at me like neighbors complaining about a fence. That night it was old Tam Wicker, drowned eleven years in the Vellamere canal, going on about the cold and how nobody had ever found his good boots. As if I could do anything about his boots. As if I hadn't heard about the boots forty times already.

"Tam," I said to the empty workshop, "I will carve your boots into your next charm if you let me finish this one."

Silence. The particular offended silence of the recently deceased who have been offered a compromise.

The charm on my bench was for the widow Halloway, whose husband had died of the lung-rot two weeks past. She'd paid me three coppers and a jar of pickled onions, which tells you everything about the economy of grief in Vellamere. What she thought she was buying was a trinket: a little bone bird that would sit on her sill and feel, she imagined, faintly warm when the wind blew right. A whisper of comfort. Nothing more. Magic was outlawed after the Hollowing, and what I sold was legal precisely because it was nearly nothing — bone shaped with patience, carrying less power than a grandmother's lullaby.

What she didn't know — what no one in this city knew, because knowing would get me ashed — was that every piece I carved carried an actual voice. Not much of one. A thread of it. Enough that the widow Halloway might someday hear her husband humming over her shoulder while she mended, and think it was memory, and be glad.

That was the trade. I took the whole shout of the dead into myself so my customers could have the whisper. Survival meant shrinking my gift down to nothing, whittling myself smaller every year, until the woman selling charms at the corner stall was just a tired craftswoman with steady hands and no history worth mentioning.

I was mid-cut on the bird's wing when the bells started.

Not the hour bells. The *other* bells — the ones the Wardens of Ash rang when they wanted the streets emptied and every door barred. Curfew raid. My knife stopped against the bone. Outside my shuttered window, torchlight smeared orange across the gap in the boards, and boots hit cobblestones in that unhurried rhythm the Wardens used, the rhythm of people who knew no one would dare run.

I put down the knife. I banked the coals. I did all the sensible things a law-abiding charlatan does when the law comes knocking, and then I stood very still in the dark of my workshop and listened to the dead, who had all gone quiet at once.

They always go quiet when the Wardens pass. Even Tam Wicker, who wouldn't shut up about his boots. Whatever the Wardens carry in those iron lanterns of theirs, the dead want no part of it. It's the only opinion the living and the dead share without argument.

The boots went by. Three pairs, four. Then a fifth pair stopped.

Then came the knock.

Three raps, soft as a sparrow landing. Nobody knocks softly during a curfew raid unless they are either very stupid or very desperate, and in my experience the desperate ones knock softer than anyone, because they've learned that loudness is the first thing that gets you killed.

I stood with my hand on the door bar and said, through the wood, "If you're a Warden, you're supposed to announce yourself. If you're a thief, the till's empty. If you're dead—" I almost said *you know where to find me*, which is the sort of joke I make to an audience of one, "—then you're early."

"It's not safe out here," said a voice.

A child's voice. Thin, cracked at the edges, the voice of someone who had been running long enough to wear the sound out of himself.

Every sensible instinct I owned told me to stay behind that door. Sensible instinct has kept me alive through five years of purges and quiet disappearances, and I have learned to trust it the way other people trust their mothers. But there is another thing in me, older and stupider, the thing that hears the dead, and it was pulling at me like a fish on a line. Because under the boy's voice, faint as breath on a winter window, I could hear something else. Something that wasn't a child at all.

I lifted the bar.

He was small — ten years old, maybe eleven, though hunger had edited him downward. Barefoot on my frozen doorstep, in a nightshirt gone grey with road-dirt, hair cropped short and unevenly, the way you cut hair yourself with a knife when you can't risk a barber asking questions. His feet were cut and blue. He was shivering so hard his teeth clicked between words.

And he was looking at me like I was the last lit window in a burning city.

"The Wardens," he whispered. "They were following me. Please. Just until the bells stop."

Every rule I lived by said turn him out. harboring a runaway during a raid wasn't a fine or a night in irons; it was a name in a ledger, and names in ledgers ended up as smoke in the square. I looked at his feet. I looked at the torchlight sliding along the street. I looked at his eyes, which were the color of wet slate and held something no child's eyes should hold — a kind of patient, ancient watchfulness, like a bird that has learned exactly how long a cat will wait.

"Inside," I said, and hated myself, and pulled him in by the arm before the word finished leaving my mouth.

* * *

I sat him by the banked coals and wrapped his feet in flannel while he watched me with that unnerving stillness. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and river water and something else underneath, something like the air in a room that hasn't been opened in a hundred years.

"What's your name?"

He considered the question longer than most children consider whether to steal bread. "Sebastian," he said finally. Carefully. Like the word was borrowed.

"Rosalind. This is my shop. You'll touch nothing on the shelves, especially the things that look boring. Those are the expensive ones." He didn't smile. Most children smile at that. "Where are your parents, Sebastian?"

"I don't remember them."

Children say that. Children say it lightly, the way they say *I don't like cabbage*, and usually it means grief too large to sit still inside. But the way he said it — flat, factual, like reading off an inventory — made the back of my neck prickle.

"You don't remember them? Or you don't remember anything?"

"I remember things," he said. "Just not mine."

I should have sent him away then. I want that on the record, wherever records go. There was a moment, kneeling on my own floor with flannel in my hands, when every alarm I possessed was ringing, and I ignored all of them because his feet were freezing and because I am, despite five years of careful self-erasure, still the kind of fool who opens doors.

Outside, the boots came back. Slower now. Pausing at doors. I heard the scrape of a ledger being consulted, the low murmur of Warden Corvane's patrol checking names against houses. My heart did its usual cowardly gymnastics. I put a finger to my lips. Sebastian nodded once, gravely, like a soldier accepting orders.

The knock, when it came to my door, was not soft.

"Open. Wardens of Ash."

I opened it the width of a hand, letting the cold in and the lamplight out, and gave the Warden on my step my best face — the tired, honest, slightly stupid face of a tradeswoman woken at an indecent hour. He was young, this one, with the chapped lips and resentful posture of a man assigned the poor districts in winter.

"Charmseller. Anyone come to your door tonight?"

"You woke me to ask. So, no."

His eyes went past my shoulder into the dark shop. Behind him, his partner held one of the iron lanterns, and from inside it came a faint, hungry hum that made the bones on my shelves itch in a way I can't describe except to say it felt like being scratched.

"We're looking for a boy," the young Warden said. "Barefoot. Your size in age. Seen anything?"

"Barefoot children in Vellamere in winter," I said. "Tragedy upon tragedy. No."

He stared at me a beat too long. Then the partner said something low — a name called from further down the street — and they moved on, boots ringing, the lantern's hum fading like a receding tide.

I closed the door. I put the bar back. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, because I hadn't been afraid for myself in years and apparently all that saved-up fear had just been waiting for a reason.

When I turned around, Sebastian was standing at my workbench.

"No," I said. "No, no — I told you to touch nothing—"

But his fingers were already resting on the widow Halloway's half-finished bird, light as a moth landing, and the world changed.

The dead went silent.

You have to understand what that means. For twenty years, since I was a girl, the voices have been with me — murmuring, weeping, gossiping, demanding, a tide I learned to sleep inside the way sailors learn to sleep in storms. Silence, for me, is not peace. Silence is deafness. And in one heartbeat, every voice in Vellamere — every ghost within reach of my gift, hundreds of them, thousands — stopped speaking at once.

I grabbed the bench. The floor seemed to tilt.

Then the silence broke, and the dead spoke again — but not to me.

They spoke through *him*.

Sebastian's mouth opened, and what came out was not a child's voice. It was layered, many-voiced, a chord instead of a note — Tam Wicker's drowned rasp braided with a hundred others, men and women and accents dead before I was born, all of them pouring out of this barefoot boy in a language I did not know and yet understood the way you understand weeping, the way you understand a mother's tone across a crowded room. Old words. Older than Vellamere. Older, some treacherous part of me whispered, than any kingdom standing.

The bone bird on the bench began to glow, faintly, the way embers breathe.

And Sebastian — Sebastian was crying. Tears cutting clean lines down his dirty face even as the voices moved his mouth, his own eyes wide with terror above them, a passenger in his own skull, watching himself speak.

It lasted perhaps ten heartbeats. Then it stopped, all at once, and he sagged against the bench, gasping, and the workshop was just a workshop again, cold and dim and ordinary, and the dead came flooding back to me in a confused babble — but different now. Hushed. Awed. The way a crowd goes quiet when a king passes.

Tam Wicker said, in my ear, clearer than he'd spoken in eleven years: *"The tongue. She's found the tongue."*

I crossed the room on legs that belonged to someone else and knelt in front of the boy. He flinched when I reached for him, so I stopped, hands open, the way you approach a wounded animal.

"Sebastian. Look at me. What was that? What did you say?"

"I don't know." His voice was his own again, small and wrecked. "It happens. Words come and I have to say them or they — they press. They push from inside. I didn't mean to touch your carving, I'm sorry, I saw it and my hands just—" He swallowed. "Did I do something bad?"

"You did something impossible," I said.

And then, because I am a fool and a bone-carver and a woman who has spent five years pretending to be less than she is, I did the test. I don't know why. Fear does strange arithmetic. I reached to the shelf behind him and took down the oldest piece I owned — a knuckle-bone, brown with age, bought from a peddler a decade ago and never carved, never sold, kept only because the dead clustered around it like moths and I liked having somewhere in my shop where the noise was *thick*. A relic. I pressed it into his palm.

"Say whatever comes," I whispered.

He closed his fingers around the bone. His eyes unfocused. And the chord rose in him again — deeper this time, steadier, the dead lining up behind his tongue like choristers finding their places — and the knuckle-bone began to recite.

It recited a coronation.

I heard trumpets. I heard a crowd roaring in a square I recognized as Cathedral Square, before the Wardens' gallows stood there. I heard a priest's voice intoning blessings in that same ancient language, and beneath the blessing, a younger voice answering — a man's voice, warm and frightened and trying to hide the fright, saying vows in words that predated the vows themselves.

*"King Aldous,"* the bone said, in a hundred dead voices through a child's mouth, *"crowned in the sight of gods and ancestors, keeper of the inheritance, bearer of the—"* 

And then the recitation changed key. The warmth drained out of it. The chord thinned to a single voice, dry as paper, and the bone said:

*"—and something entered the crown."*

The glow died. Sebastian gasped and dropped the knuckle-bone like it had burned him, and it rolled across my floorboards and came to rest against the leg of my chair, ordinary and brown and silent.

Neither of us moved for a long time.

Outside, the curfew bells rang the all-clear, distant and tinny. Somewhere a dog barked. The coals ticked as they cooled. The world resumed being the world, indifferent and gray, and I knelt on my floor holding a boy's shoulders, watching him shake.

"They told stories," he whispered. "After. In the villages I passed through. About the night the king went mad. About how magic died in one night and the Hollowing came, and how it was nobody's fault, it just... happened. Like weather." His slate-colored eyes fixed on me. "It didn't happen like weather, did it."

Five years I had survived by not asking questions. By carving whispers and keeping my head down and letting the city believe its comfortable story about a madness that fell from the sky. And now the answer was sitting barefoot on my floorboards, wearing a stolen nightshirt, with a dead king's coronation caught in his throat like a fishbone.

"No," I heard myself say. "It didn't happen like weather. It happened like a door being opened from the inside."

Sebastian looked down at his own hands, turning them over, as if checking whether they still belonged to him.

"There's more," he said quietly. "In my head. So much more. It doesn't stop, Rosalind. It never stops. And lately..." He hesitated, and the fear in his face shifted into something worse than fear. "Lately I think something in there knows I'm listening. Lately I think it's starting to listen back."

The knuckle-bone lay still against my chair. The dead in my shop had gone utterly silent again — not absent, but attentive, the silence of a full courtroom waiting for a verdict.

I looked at the boy. I looked at the bone. And I understood, with the calm clarity that arrives only at the exact moment you realize your life is over, that I had not opened my door to a runaway.

I had opened it to a beginning.
