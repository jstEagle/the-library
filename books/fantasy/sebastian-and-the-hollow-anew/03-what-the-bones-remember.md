## 3. What the Bones Remember

The boy slept in my chair.

Not the bed — I'd offered the bed twice, and both times he'd looked at it as though I'd suggested he lie down in a river of eels. So he curled up in the chair by the cold hearth with his knees to his chest and his bare feet tucked under him like a bird roosting, and he slept the way soldiers sleep: lightly, one ear open for the world's next betrayal.

I spent the night not sleeping either, which was nothing new, but for once not because of the dead.

Here is what nobody tells you about hearing the dead: they are mostly boring. My mother wants the shutters fixed. A cooper who drowned in the Vell nine years ago still complains about his apprentice. The widow Hesse's late husband has opinions about her second marriage that I have politely declined to pass along. The dead are a village, and villages gossip, and after twenty years of it you learn to let them wash over you like rain on a roof — there, always there, but not *in*, if you're careful.

Except last night, when Sebastian touched my carvings, the rain had stopped. All of it. Every voice. And then it had come back through *him*, in a language that made my teeth ache and my mother's ghost go quiet as a held breath.

I put water on to boil and watched him sleep and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since dawn.

Magic was outlawed eleven years ago. The Hollowing, they call it — the year the king went mad on his coronation day, or near enough, and the Wardens of Ash were born from the wreckage. I had been carving charms since before that, back when a bone-carver could say *charm* out loud without lowering her voice. I knew what old words sounded like when they came off a relic. I'd heard them maybe four times in my life, each time by accident, each time enough to make me sick for a week.

Sebastian spoke them in his sleep. I'd heard him at three in the morning, murmuring into the chair's arm, and the dead had gathered around him like cats around a warm stove — I could feel them pressing close, dozens of them, listening.

So. Arithmetic. Either the boy was a relic himself, which raised questions I didn't want to ask about who had carved him, or he was carrying something that relics carry, which raised worse ones.

Either way, I needed to test him properly. Not with trinkets. With the real thing.

* * *

Under the floorboards beneath my workbench, wrapped in oilcloth inside a box that had once held fine salt, I kept the femur.

It had come to me eight years ago from a grave-robber named Pell who drank too much and talked too much and eventually said exactly one sentence too many to a Warden, but that's another story and mostly his. He'd pulled it from a barrow north of the city, pre-Hollowing, pre-everything, and he'd sold it to me cheap because it made his hands go numb when he carried it. It made my hands go numb too. That was how I knew it was honest. Most relics are just old bones with a little echo caught in them, like a seashell holding the sea's opinion. This one was different. This one had been *listening* for a very long time.

I had never used it. Using it felt like opening someone else's mail, if the mail were two thousand years old and addressed to the dead. But I took it out now, unwrapped the oilcloth, and set it on the workbench in the grey light, and even before I turned around I felt Sebastian wake behind me.

He didn't gasp or startle. He simply stopped being asleep, the way a lamp stops being dark. When I glanced over my shoulder his eyes were open and fixed on the femur with an expression I recognized from mirrors: hunger and dread sharing a plate.

"You know what this is," I said.

He shook his head. Then, slowly, he nodded. Then he pressed both hands flat against his own thighs as if steadying himself, and looked at me with such naked pleading that I nearly put the bone away right there.

"No," I told him. "You don't get to be frightened yet. Frightened comes *after*. First we find out what you are."

He said something in the old tongue — soft, questioning. And the dead in my shop, the whole murmuring village of them, went silent again. All of them leaning in.

"Right," I said, mostly to myself. "That's going to keep happening, isn't it."

* * *

I want to be clear about what I expected, because expectations are the bones that horror breaks.

I expected an echo. A name, maybe. A snatch of a lullaby, a fragment of a prayer, the way relics give things up — grudgingly, in pieces, like a miser counting coins through cracked fingers. I planned to hold the femur while Sebastian touched it, note whatever surfaced, and spend the rest of the day telling myself it meant less than it did. That's a skill of mine. Telling myself things mean less than they do. I've kept myself alive with it.

I sat across the workbench from him. I laid the femur between us on a square of clean linen, because some habits survive even the end of the world. His hands hovered over it without touching, trembling.

"It won't hurt you," I said, and then, because lying to children is a habit I'm trying to break: "It might hurt you. I don't know. Nothing like you has ever sat at my table."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. There was a ghost of a smile in him somewhere, buried under all the running, and I found myself — absurdly, inconveniently — wanting to dig it out someday.

"Touch it," I said. "And whatever happens, don't let go until I tell you. If the words come, let them come. Don't fight them and don't chase them. You're a door, not a mouth. Doors don't chew."

Where I got that phrase I couldn't tell you. It felt older than I was. Everything about that morning felt older than I was.

He touched the bone.

* * *

The silence came first.

You have to understand what that silence is like, for someone like me. Twenty years of voices, and then — nothing. Not quiet, which is just absence of sound, but *silence*, which is presence of absence, a thing with weight and edges. The dead didn't fade. They were *taken*, swept out of my head like a hand clearing a table, and in the space they left I could feel the femur waking up underneath Sebastian's fingers.

Then the boy opened his mouth, and it wasn't his voice that came out.

It wasn't one voice at all. It was many, layered, braided — men and women, young and ancient, all speaking in unison in that language that predates every kingdom whose coins I've ever forged a charm against. And beneath the language, faint as a watermark, I could hear the shape of meaning, the way you can hear the shape of a melody through a wall. I understood almost none of it.

But I understood the bones' habit of ceremony. Old relics remember rituals the way rivers remember rain. Whatever was coming, it came in order, in procession, with the gravity of something rehearsed for generations.

And then, threaded through the layered voices, words shifted. Not translated — *surfaced*, the way a drowned body surfaces, bloated and changed but recognizable. The relic was reciting something, and parts of it were rising into my tongue so I could hear.

"*On the first day of the Reaping Moon,*" said a woman's voice, formal and ringing, a herald's voice, "*Aldous, son of Aldous, was crowned in the Hall of Salt before the assembled peerage of Vellamere—*"

My skin prickled. I knew that date. Every child in Vellamere knew that date, though not for the coronation. The first day of the Reaping Moon, five years ago. The day the king went mad. The day magic died.

The relic rolled on, inexorable, a tide coming in.

"*—and the crown of Aldous was set upon his head by the Hand of the Realm, and the bells rang from the Salt Spire, and the people cheered in the square below.*"

Sebastian's eyes had gone white-rimmed, unfocused, his lips moving in sympathy with the voices. His fingers stayed on the bone. Good boy. Door, not mouth.

"*—and the crown was old, older than the hall, older than the line, and it had been remade for the new king with gold from the deep vaults—*"

Here the voices faltered. Not stopped — *faltered*, the way a choir falters when it reaches a passage no one wants to sing. The layered sound dropped away until only one voice remained, thin and clear and terribly close, the voice of someone standing at the front of a crowd.

"*—and I saw it enter.*"

I leaned forward despite myself. The shop was very cold. I hadn't lit the stove.

"*I was third among the bearers, and I saw the crown before it was set down, and there was a moment — a breath, no more — when the light passed through it wrong. Gold does not drink light. This gold drank. And in the drinking there was a turning, like a key, like a lock that had waited in the metal for a hundred years for the right head, the right hour, the right grief—*"

"*Grief*," I repeated aloud, stupidly, and Sebastian's mouth shaped the word with me in the old tongue.

"*—for the new king came to his crowning already mourning. His queen, dead that spring. His daughter, dead before her. They crowned a hollowed man and called it dignity. And when the band of gold touched his brow, the thing that had waited in the vault-metal rose up through the crown and into the king, the way water rises through salt, and I saw his eyes change, and I said nothing, because what does one say? Who would believe a bearer over a king?*"

"*By evening he stood at his window and did not know his own name. By midnight he knew everyone's name — every ancestor, every bloodline, every debt of memory owed to the dead — and he began to call them in. And the calling did not stop. And that is the truth of the Hollowing, which the histories will not record, because the histories are written by the palace, and the palace was eaten first.*"

The voice stopped.

Sebastian slumped forward over the workbench, catching himself on his elbows, breathing hard, sweat shining on his forehead. The dead rushed back into my head all at once — the whole village of them, clamoring, frightened, asking questions in a dozen registers of panic — and for the first time in twenty years I wanted them to be quiet. I wanted silence back. I wanted the other thing, the terrible clear thing.

Because I had heard it. We had all heard it, all these years, the official story worn smooth by repetition: the king went mad, madness breeds fear, fear breeds law, and the law says magic is ash. The Wardens didn't burn witches; they burned *contagion*. Magic had made the king mad, so magic must die, and every charm and whisper and gift along with it. Five years of ash, five years of my life shrunk down to trinkets and lies, all of it built on one sentence:

*The madness came from the king.*

But the bone said otherwise. The bone said the madness came from the *crown*. From the vaults. From something that had waited in the metal like a seed in winter ground, patient, planted — and I am a carver, I know what it means when a thing is *planted*. Seeds don't happen. Seeds are placed.

Someone had made that crown. Someone had fed it into the vaults and let it wait. And the king — Aldous, mourning, hollowed by grief before anything ever touched him — the king hadn't gone mad at all.

The king had been *eaten*. From the outside in. On the holiest day of his life, in front of the whole assembled peerage, and no one had said a word, and the whole city had spent five years burning the innocent for the crime of the guilty.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small. "Oh, you poor bastard."

Sebastian raised his head. His eyes were wet, and focused now, and looking at me with an expression I couldn't read — until I realized I could. It was recognition. He'd known. Some part of him had known all along, carrying it around in his skull like a stone in a shoe, and here was the first person in five years who had finally heard the same stone hit the same pavement.

He spoke, and for once it wasn't the old tongue. It was broken, accented, halting Common — the first words I'd heard him say in my language, scraped out syllable by syllable like a man digging through rubble:

"The king. Is in — " He pressed his fist against his own sternum. "In here. The king is in here. He is *screaming*."

* * *

I gave him tea. It seemed inadequate. It was inadequate. I gave it to him anyway, because ritual is what we do when the alternative is screaming ourselves, and because his hands needed something warm to hold.

He drank it in small sips, hunched over the cup, and I sat across from him and rebuilt the world in my head, which takes longer than building it the first time.

Five years ago, something entered King Aldous through his crown. Something that eats inheritance — that much the bone had implied and my own ears, over years of whispers from the newly dead, had begun to suspect. People forget their mothers' faces. Bloodlines lose their stories. Whole families wake unable to say where their name came from. I'd blamed grief, war, the general rot of a city under occupation by its own law. Everyone blames the usual suspects.

But if the thing in the crown ate memory, and it had been feeding for five years from the throne of Vellamere —

Then the Wardens weren't the cure. They were the symptom. Burn the charms, burn the old words, burn anyone who carries the past too loudly — you're not stopping the disease. You're cutting away the body's memory of being ill. You're doing the eater's work for it, house to house, ledger to ledger, bonfire to bonfire.

And sitting in my kitchen was a boy who fled that palace — I was sure of it now, sure in the marrow the way you're sure of weather — carrying the one thing in Vellamere the Hollow wanted most. The king's own memories, sealed away, walking around on bare feet, knocking on the door of the only bone-carver in the market district who still heard the dead.

Which meant the Hollow knew he existed. Which meant it was looking.

Which meant the knock I'd answered before dawn was only the first knock.

Sebastian set down his cup. He looked at the femur on its linen square, and then at me, and said — in Common, laboriously, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"They are coming. For the words. For *me*." A pause. His jaw worked. "You should give me to them."

"No," I said.

It came out faster than thought, flat and absolute, and I surprised us both with it. I'm not a brave woman. I've built a whole life on the careful architecture of not being brave — small charms, small lies, small rooms. But I looked at this half-starved child who had been running for five years from a monster wearing a king, and I heard myself say no the way my mother used to say it, the way the dead say it, the way bone says it when it refuses to break.

"I don't give people to monsters," I said. "It's the one trade I don't practice."

He stared at me. Somewhere in my shop, my mother's ghost — who had been agitating for the shutters for six years — went suddenly, completely quiet, in the way of the dead when they witness something worth witnessing.

Outside, far off toward the palace district, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. Not the alarm. The *summons* bell, the one they rang when the Wardens moved through the city in force, when ledgers came out and doors came down.

It was ringing for someone. In a city full of forgetting, it seemed, somebody had finally started remembering to look for us.

I blew out the candle, though it was barely past noon, and reached for the floorboard.
