## 4. The Law of Ash

The dead do not knock.

That's the first thing you learn when you're carrying a king in your skull like contraband sewn into your ribs: the dead don't knock, don't wait their turn, don't have the decency to arrive at reasonable hours. They simply *arrive*, the way weather arrives, the way fever arrives, and you stand there in whatever room you happen to be standing in — a cellar, a hayloft, once a chapel that had burned down before I was born and whose ghost still smelled faintly of smoke — and you are suddenly someone else.

This morning it happened over bread.

Rosalind Marchetti kept her kitchen dark except for one candle she'd stubbed into a saucer, and she'd given me the heel of a loaf so stale it could have been used as a weapon against small animals. I was chewing. That's all. Chewing, minding my own business, being a boy with bread in his mouth, and then —

*— the great hall hung with banners of every house sworn to the crown, forty-one of them, and the herald's voice cracking on the name Aldous because he was weeping too, everyone weeping, and the crown lifted on its cushion of white velvet, heavier than anyone expected, heavier than gold should be —*

I was standing. I didn't remember standing. My hands were out in front of me like I was receiving something, which in a way I was, because my hands were the king's hands for about four seconds, and they remembered the weight of a crown I had never touched.

When I came back to myself, Rosalind was watching me from across the table. She hadn't flinched. She hadn't dropped anything or backed into a corner or made the sign against possession that people make, thumb folded under two fingers, mouth moving through the old ward-words they barely remember anymore. She just sat there with her own bread in one hand and her carving knife in the other and looked at me the way you look at weather. Noted it. Waited for it to pass.

"You were somewhere else," she said.

"The hall where he was crowned," I said. "Forty-one banners. The herald cried."

"Which house was missing?"

I blinked at her. "What?"

She set down the knife. "If there were forty-one banners, one house wasn't there. Kings count these things. It matters to them." And then, softer, almost embarrassed by her own curiosity: "Which one?"

I closed my eyes and let the memory come up instead of fighting it, which is another thing you learn — fighting it is like fighting a river; you just get water in your lungs. "House Verren," I said. "The eastern marshes. They sent no banner because the heir had died of marsh-fever three weeks before, and the old lord said his family would mourn rather than celebrate."

Rosalind was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "You know that like you were told it yesterday," and I said, "I know it like I *was* told it yesterday, because as far as the part of me holding it is concerned, I was," and she nodded slowly, and went back to her bread, and that was all.

Do you understand what that means? Maybe not. Let me tell you what it usually means, when someone watches me go elsewhere and come back speaking with borrowed certainty.

Usually they run.

* * *

Here is how the world works now, for people like me, which is to say: here is the Law of Ash.

Five years ago magic ended. Everyone knows this the way everyone knows the sun rises — it's not a fact so much as a piece of furniture in the mind. The Hollowing, they call it, though nobody agrees on what was hollowed. The king went mad, they say. The king spoke words that unmade things, they say. Magic turned on us, they say, and the Wardens of Ash rose to protect us from ourselves, and ever since, any word older than the oldest living grandmother, any charm, any carved bone that hums, any child who talks in their sleep in a language that makes candles gutter — that gets reported. Written down. Taken away.

Nobody says what "taken away" means, but everybody knows. There's a courtyard behind the Warden barracks on Salt Row where the ground never grows moss. You can figure out the rest yourself. I did, when I was eight, from a window across the street, watching men sweep it every morning with a diligence that told me everything the silence didn't.

The Wardens wear grey cloaks powdered with actual ash — it's ceremonial, some vow they take, though I've heard they renew it from the burnings, which if true is the most honest uniform in Vellamere. They carry ledgers. The ledgers matter more than the swords. A sword ends one life; a ledger ends a whole line, because a name written in a Warden's book means the bloodline gets watched for generations, marriages checked, children questioned, until the old words are scoured out of the family like rot cut from a beam. They aren't hunting people, exactly. They're hunting inheritance. They are trying to make forgetting permanent, official, enforced.

And I am — I want to be precise here, because precision is the only dignity I have left — I am a walking archive of everything they are trying to burn. In my head lives King Aldous the Third: his coronation, his wedding, his wars, his griefs, the names of every servant who ever brought him wine, the smell of the palace stables, the sound his mother made when she laughed (a snort, undignified, immediately covered), and the last five years of him too, or pieces of them, seen from inside a body that isn't mine anymore, wearing a face I sometimes catch in reflective water and don't recognize.

I am eleven years old. I have been alive for eleven years, and I contain roughly sixty-three years of someone else's living, plus fragments of others besides, because the sorceress who sealed him inside me didn't seal *only* him. She sealed the whole court's memory of him, I think, or maybe memory is sticky and he came coated in it. Either way, when I sleep, I dream in a voice deeper than mine. When I wake, I sometimes reach for a beard that isn't there.

The Law of Ash says I should be ash myself. So I run. Or I ran. Running has rules, and I knew them well:

Rule one: never sleep in the same place twice. Rule two: never let anyone hear you talk in your sleep. Rule three: when the other voice comes up your throat, bite it back, always, because the old language sounds like nothing else and there is always, *always* someone listening who knows what it means. Rule four: don't get attached to anyone, because attachment makes you slow, and slow gets written in ledgers.

I broke rule four in about six days, which for me was practically a record of discipline.

* * *

It was the not-flinching that did it. I want to be clear about that. It wasn't the bread, though the bread helped. It was the not-flinching.

The third night I stayed with her — she hadn't invited me to stay, exactly; I had simply stopped leaving, and she had stopped pretending to notice, which is its own kind of invitation — I woke up screaming. Not boy-screaming. King-screaming, low and full-chested, a scream that belonged to a man watching something enter a crown, and I sat bolt upright on her floor pallet with the taste of white velvet in my mouth and the word coming out of me before I could stop it, the old word, the one that sounds like ice settling on deep water:

"*Vessha-kol. Anu-veth.*"

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Every mouse in the walls went silent. Somewhere outside, a dog began to howl and then thought better of it.

And Rosalind appeared in the doorway with a poker in her hand — not raised, not pointed at me, just held, the way you hold a tool when you're deciding what needs doing — and she looked at me kneeling there in the dark with a dead king's terror in my throat, and she said:

"Did you hurt yourself?"

That's it. That was the whole speech. Did you hurt yourself. Like I was a child who'd fallen off a wall, and not a vessel of forbidden memory that half the armed force of Vellamere had standing orders to collect.

"No," I said.

"All right." She set the poker down. She crouched, keeping a careful arm's length — she's careful, not fearless; I learned the difference later — and looked at me straight on. "Was it yours? The screaming."

I wanted to lie. Lying was rule five, right after rule four, and I was very good at both. But she was looking at me like an answer was a thing she could carve and keep, and I was so tired of carrying truth alone that it fell out of me like a stone out of a torn pocket.

"No," I said. "It was his."

"His," she repeated. Not *whose*, not yet. Just *his*, letting me decide how much to give.

"The king's," I whispered. "He's in me. What's left of him. Someone put him there the night everything went wrong, and I can't take him out, and the Wardens—" My voice cracked, and I hated that, eleven years old and cracking like cheap pottery. "The Wardens burn people for a single old word, and I'm made of them. Head to foot. I'm a book they'd burn the library to get."

Rosalind was quiet for so long I started cataloguing exits. Back window, shutter sticks. Cellar hatch, leads to the tannery drain. Front door, too exposed. I had done this inventory in a hundred rooms.

Then she reached out — slowly, telegraphing it the way you move near a spooked animal — and picked a splinter of bone shaving out of my hair, left over from the day's carving, and flicked it away.

"I hear the dead," she said.

I stared at her.

"Not loudly. Whispers. It's why I make charms — it gives the whispers somewhere to live besides my skull. It's illegal. It's been illegal for five years, and I've spent those five years making my gift smaller and smaller so no one would notice it, and I am very tired, Sebastian, and I am apparently a bad survivor, because when I look at you I don't see a reason to run. I see the first person in five years who might understand what the quiet costs."

The candle guttered between us. Neither of us moved.

"You should report me," I said, because rule five, because lying, because I had to offer her the out even though offering it felt like pulling my own tooth. "There's coin in it. Wardens pay informants. You could buy a year of safety off my name alone."

"I could," she agreed. "And then I'd go back to hearing whispers alone in the dark, and someday one of them would be yours, wouldn't it? If they take you to Salt Row." Her jaw tightened. "No. I've shrunk enough. Five years of shrinking. I'm done."

I don't cry easily. Sixty-three years of kingly memory teaches you that tears are currency and you spend them carefully. But I was eleven, and it was dark, and no one had ever looked directly at the whole of what I was — not the useful parts, not the dangerous parts, just the whole — and I cried anyway, silently, the way kings are taught, and Rosalind pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness, and after a while she said, "Come on, back to sleep, and if the king screams again, tell him the woman downstairs says he should try breathing," and that was so absurd that I laughed wetly, and the king — I swear this is true — the king settled, somewhere behind my eyes, like a dog circling twice before lying down.

* * *

Two days later, the Wardens came to Copperlane.

We heard them before we saw them — boots, many boots, the particular rhythm of a patrol that doesn't expect resistance, and beneath it the creak of the ledger-cart wheels. Rosalind was at the workbench with her back to the window, mid-charm, and she went still in a way I'd learned to read: not fear exactly, more like a deer calculating distance to treeline.

"Ashfall patrol," she murmured. "They sweep the market districts at month's end. Checking charms for authenticity. Most of what's sold in this quarter is fraud — dried herbs and pretty knots — so they rarely bother with the carvers." A beat. "Rarely."

"What happens if they bother?"

"They weigh it." She turned the half-carved charm in her fingers. "Real magic has weight to it. Not physical weight — well. Some physical weight. Enough that a trained hand notices. If they find a genuine working, the maker writes their name in the book, and the book goes to Salt Row, and..." She didn't finish. She didn't need to. I'd swept that courtyard in my imagination often enough for both of us.

"Hide me," I said. "Under the floor. I'll be quiet."

"And if they search?" She shook her head before I could answer. "No. Hiding looks like hiding. You stay in plain sight. You're my nephew, come from the coast after his parents' death, mute since the fever — grief does that, sometimes, everyone knows a case of it. You don't speak. You don't need to speak. Nod when spoken to, look sad, and for the love of every saint we're not allowed anymore, do *not* let anything come up your throat while they're in the room."

"Mute," I repeated. It was, I had to admit, elegant. The safest mouth in Vellamere is a closed one. "And if they ask you questions?"

"Then I'll answer them, because I've answered questions from worse men than these, and I'm better at lying than you'd credit. Carving charms that carry only a whisper of the real thing teaches you a great deal about making less look like more." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice — shame, maybe, at the shrinking she'd confessed to — and was gone. "Boots. Sit by the fire. Look orphaned."

I have range. I looked orphaned.

The knock came, three measured raps, and Rosalind opened the door to two Wardens in ash-powdered grey and a third behind them carrying the ledger under his arm like a scripture. The tall one had a face like a door someone had locked and lost the key to. He introduced himself as Warden Corvane — a woman's voice, clipped, tired around the edges in a way that surprised me; I'd imagined Wardens as stone all the way down, and here was stone with a hairline crack running through it.

"Charm-maker," she said, scanning the shop. Her eyes moved over the hanging charms, the workbench, the shavings, and then over me by the fire, and stopped. Half a second. A full second. I kept my eyes down and arranged my face into the specific blankness of a child who has learned that attention is dangerous.

"Relative?" Corvane asked.

"My sister's boy," Rosalind said. "From Merrow's Landing. Lost his parents in the winter fevers." She let a silence sit, the kind that invites pity. "He doesn't speak. Hasn't since."

Corvane's gaze stayed on me a moment longer. Wardens are taught to watch for the signs — the faraway look, the lips moving without sound, the candle behavior. I gave her none of it. Inside, sixty-three years of royal composure held me like a hand at the back of my neck: *be still, be small, be nothing.* For once, king and boy wanted exactly the same thing.

"Fevers took a lot this winter," Corvane said at last, and it wasn't sympathy, quite. It was something adjacent to sympathy, filed down flat. She turned back to the charms. Rosalind handed them over one by one — mourning knots, luck-bones, a birthing charm — and the Warden weighed each in her palm with those half-closed eyes, and each time, her hand paused a fraction, and each time, she moved on.

I noticed. Of course I noticed. I notice everything now; the king trained me without meaning to. Her pauses were the pause of a woman finding something she expected and choosing not to name it. And when she reached the last charm — a small carved vertebra Rosalind had made the day before I arrived, before she'd begun shrinking herself for my sake — Corvane held it a long moment, thumb tracing the grooves, and then set it down with a softness that didn't match anything else about her.

"Your work is unremarkable," she said to Rosalind. Flat. Almost bored.

"I do my best," Rosalind said, wry as dry bread.

"Do." Corvane wrote something in the ledger — I caught only the motion of the pen, not the words — and closed it. "Copperlane will be swept again within the month. Keep your work unremarkable." At the door she paused, and looked back once, at me, and there was something in her face I couldn't read then, though I can now, having learned what I learned later: it was the look of a woman checking whether a thing she dreaded had found her yet.

Then the door shut, the boots receded, and Rosalind let out a breath she'd been holding for approximately the entire visit.

"Well," she said. "Nephew."

"Uncle was a stretch," I said. "You're not nearly hairy enough."

Something like a laugh escaped her, quickly strangled. Then she picked up the vertebra charm Corvane had handled last, turned it over, and went pale.

"What?" I asked.

"She turned it over," Rosalind said quietly. "Twice. Weighing it. And then she wrote in the ledger before she left the shop — not after, *before*. Wardens write findings after inspection." Her knuckles whitened around the little bone. "Sebastian, she wrote something down in here. About this charm, or about us, or—" She stopped. Through the window, down the crooked lane, I could see the ledger-cart rolling away toward Salt Row, and beside it, the tall Warden with the tired eyes glancing back at our door exactly once.

Whatever she had written, she was taking it home to the books.

And the books, everyone in Vellamere knew, never forgot anything.
