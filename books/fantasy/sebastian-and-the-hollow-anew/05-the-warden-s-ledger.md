## 5. The Warden's Ledger

The bells woke the market district an hour before dawn, and I lay in bed listening to them and doing arithmetic.

Three tolls meant a Warden inspection. Three tolls before dawn meant they expected to catch someone sleeping beside evidence. And three tolls on the first cold morning of autumn, when every widow in Vellamere was buying charms against the dark half of the year — that meant they'd had a tip, and tips came from somewhere, and somewhere was usually a neighbor who wanted their rent lowered.

I got up, dressed, and counted my stock.

Not all of it. The real stock — the pieces where I'd actually listened to what the dead had to say and worked it into the carving — those lived under a loose floorboard beneath the hearthstone, wrapped in oilcloth, because selling true work was how you ended up in a ledger. The public stock sat in the display case by the door: little bone birds and knotted cords and smooth worry-stones with nothing in them but polish. Whispers of a gift, as the saying went. Enough to suggest craft. Nothing to prove crime.

Forty-one pieces. I'd sold nine since the equinox. If Corvane's people confiscated everything, I could eat for perhaps six weeks, assuming I ate like a person with sense instead of a person with feelings, which historically I did not.

Sebastian was asleep on the pallet behind the partition wall, curled up like a comma, one hand under his cheek. He'd been with me eleven days now. Eleven days of teaching him to hold his tongue — literally, the dead tongue rising out of him at night like water finding a crack, words pouring through him while he slept in a voice that was never his. Eleven days of me lying awake listening to a boy speak coronation oaths and burial rites and, once, a recipe for eel pie that had made me genuinely hungry.

He didn't stir when I shook his shoulder. He stirred when I said his name, though, and his eyes opened the way they always did: all at once, fully awake, a boy who had learned that sleep was something you could be ambushed in.

"Raid," I told him. "Wardens. You're going into the floor."

* * *

The space under my floorboards was not designed for comfort. It was designed for hiding contraband, and contraband does not complain about cramps. Sebastian fit because Sebastian was small and because he had spent five years, apparently, being places he wasn't supposed to be. He folded himself down among the wrapped carvings without a word, and I put a slat over him and the rug over the slat, and then, because I am not entirely a monster, I put the cat bed on top of the rug, even though I do not own a cat. A woman who owns a cat bed owns a cat, and a woman with a cat opens her door differently than a woman hiding something.

Then I swept, which is what my mother did when the Wardens came, and her mother before her, back through the whole shrinking line of Marchetti women who survived by making themselves smaller. Sweep. Open the shutters. Look bored. Boredom is armor. Fear is a confession.

They came at first light, four of them in ash-grey coats, boots ringing on my step like a verdict. And at the front of them, ledger under one arm like a hymnal, walked Warden Ilsa Corvane.

I want to say something fair about Ilsa Corvane, and this is difficult, because fairness is not the natural language between a Warden and a bone-carver. She is tall in a way that suggests she was made rather than born, grey-eyed, with a scar through one eyebrow she never explains and everyone invents stories about. Five years ago she arrested the charm-seller two doors down from me, old Marta Voss, and Marta was never seen again. I have hated her efficiently and at a distance ever since, the way you hate weather.

But here is the unfair thing I noticed, standing in my doorway with my broom: she looked tired. Not the tiredness of cruelty. The tiredness of a woman carrying a book full of names and knowing what happens to names in books.

"Rosalind Marchetti." She said it like a receipt. "Bone-carver."

"Carver," I agreed. "The bone is incidental."

Her eyes went past me into the shop, cataloguing. Display case. Workbench. The cat bed, which earned a flicker of something — not suspicion; almost amusement. Wardens are trained to look for fear, and I have made a discipline of giving them boredom instead, but Ilsa Corvane looked at the cat bed and I understood, in a cold slide of the stomach, that she knew exactly what it was for. That she had seen a hundred cat beds over a hundred rugs.

"Ledger says you're licensed," she said. "Whisper-work only. Let's see it."

I let her see it. That's the whole trick, really — you don't argue, you don't perform innocence, you just open things. Innocence performed looks like guilt; innocence offered like a chore looks like innocence. I unlatched the case and stood aside while she lifted each piece to the light.

She held the worry-stones longest. Turned them over. Held one near her mouth, which is the test — if there's anything alive in the carving, it wants to speak near breath, the way flowers turn to sun. Mine stayed silent, because they were silent, because I had made forty-one objects that were lies about what I am.

"Clean," she said finally, and wrote something in the ledger, and I felt the specific relief of a coin landing in a well.

Then, because Wardens travel in fours and the other three were turning my shop inside out with the gentle thoroughness of men who get paid either way, she asked the question.

"You live alone?"

"I live with my work."

"That wasn't the question."

"It's the answer I've polished." I smiled. "You can confiscate the smile too, if it's on the list."

Something moved behind her eyes — irritation, or its cousin, respect. "There's a boy been seen in this quarter," she said. "Barefoot, or near enough. Dark hair. Ten, eleven years old. Seen coming and going from this street." She turned a page, and I heard the paper the way you hear ice cracking on a pond. "Seen entering *this* house."

"He sounds handsome," I said. "I'll keep an eye out."

"Marchetti."

"Warden."

"The description isn't mine." She closed the ledger. "It came down from the palace itself, sealed, three days ago. Which means whoever this boy is, someone above me is frightened of him. And I have learned that when the palace is frightened, it pays for names." She looked at me then, straight on, grey eyes gone flat as coins. "I'm telling you this because your license is clean and your stones are dull and I would rather not write your name today. But I will write it tomorrow, and the day after, until the boy is found or the order changes. Do you understand the shape of what I'm saying?"

I understood the shape. It was the shape of a noose, drawn politely.

"I understand," I said, "that you've described a boy who doesn't exist, and threatened me about him, and now we're both standing in my shop pretending this is normal."

"Yes," she said. "That's Vellamere."

* * *

They left an hour later, taking nothing but my dignity's last shreds, having found a cat bed and no cat and a floor that sounded exactly as hollow as any other floor, thanks to a bag of sand I keep under the hearthstone for acoustics. I waited until the boots faded, then waited longer, then pried up the slat.

Sebastian climbed out stiff and silent, and I saw immediately that he'd heard everything. He hears everything; it's the curse of him. He sat on the edge of the pallet, rubbing his knee, and looked at me with those old, old eyes, and said nothing, because he still couldn't — not in any tongue anyone living should know, and he'd learned in eleven days that silence was safer.

So I talked enough for both of us, the way I do.

"Well," I said, "good news: the Wardens think I own a cat. Bad news: the palace knows your face. Worse news: Ilsa Corvane just gave me a deadline wearing a polite face, which in my experience is the most dangerous kind." I sat down across from him. "Here's where we are, boy. Every day you stay, the price of shelter goes up. Today it cost me a lie. Next week it might cost me my license. The week after, my name in her ledger, and names in ledgers don't come back out. So I need you to tell me — and I know you can't, so nod, or point, or draw it in the dust — I need to know what's worth this."

He reached out and took my hand.

And the dead went quiet.

Not silent the way my shop is silent, the ordinary absence of it. Quiet the way a room goes when a great door closes. All the murmuring presences that live at the edge of my hearing — the widow's husband still muttering about his boots, the child's voice in the femur upstairs, the thousand thin threads of Vellamere's dead that I pretend I cannot hear — they drew back, all at once, like water before a wave.

And then they spoke through him.

His mouth didn't move much. His eyes changed first — went distant, deep, a well filling — and the voice that came out of him was layered, many voices braided into one, and what it said was:

"*The ledger has our names in it already.*"

A pause. Then, softer, in a different braid:

"*We died in the Hollowing. We remember what ate us. Write us down again, carver, and we will testify.*"

The pressure lifted. Sebastian blinked, came back to himself, and looked at my face with an expression I recognized because I wear it myself most mornings: the apology of a person whose gift inconveniences people.

I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding.

"They've been reading your mail," I said. "The dead. They know about the ledger."

He nodded slowly. Then he picked up the poker and wrote in the ash of my cold hearth, careful letters in trade-common, which he was learning fast — too fast, learning everything fast, the way you drink when you've been thirsty for years:

**HOW MANY NAMES?**

I thought of Ilsa Corvane's ledger, the thickness of it, the way she'd carried it like a hymnal.

"More every day," I said. "That's what the Hollow does, boy. It doesn't just eat kings' memories. It eats *inheritance*. Whole bloodlines forgetting their dead. And every bloodline that forgets gets quieter in my hearing — and louder in hers." I rubbed my face. "Which means Corvane isn't hunting you because she's cruel. She's hunting you because somebody convinced her that you're the disease, when you might be the only cure walking around barefoot."

Sebastian looked at the hearth-ash a long moment. Then he wrote:

**SHE LOOKED SAD.**

"She looked tired," I corrected. "Sad is worse. Sad means there's something left to save in her, and saving Wardens was not in my plan." I stood, and went to the window, and looked out at the market street below, where the ash-grey coats were stopping at the next stall, and the next, checking licenses, turning charms to the light, writing in ledgers.

The price of shelter, rising daily. And somewhere above it all, in a palace of salt, something crowned and hollow counting its harvest.

Behind me, the poker scratched again.

**WHAT DO WE DO?**

"We," I said, turning around. "Listen to him. Eleven days and already using the plural." But my heart wasn't in the joke, because the truth was already settling over me like ashfall: whatever we were going to do, we would need help, and the only help I trusted was dead, and the only help available wore a grey coat and carried a book of names.

And I had a feeling — the feeling I get sometimes, the one that's never wrong and never convenient — that Ilsa Corvane would be at my door again soon.
