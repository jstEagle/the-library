## 6. Five Years of Silence

Rosalind keeps her house too warm. That's the first thing I noticed about it, back when noticing things was still something I did on purpose instead of having them done to me. The stove ticks all night like a patient insect, and the air smells of cedar shavings and tallow and, faintly, of the river stones she boils for reasons she refuses to explain. I have decided not to ask. There are questions you ask a person who has taken you in off a curfew raid with ash still on your feet, and there are questions you swallow along with her terrible barley porridge.

I am getting ahead of myself. Rosalind says I do that — says my head is "a house where every door is open at once." She says it kindly, the way you'd describe weather to someone whose house had no roof.

Here is what I remember, in the order I can bear it.

* * *

The night the Hollow came, I was seven years old, and my most pressing concern was that the feast had run out of honeyed figs before it reached my end of the children's table.

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because I do. Every day. Somewhere between the memory of King Aldous mounting the coronation dais and the memory of what entered his crown, there is a small boy reaching for a fig that isn't there, and that boy is me, and he has been sitting at that table for five years while everything else burns down around him.

The palace of Vellamere on that night was gold light and too much perfume. I was nobody — a scullery orphan, one of forty children the household kept for washing dishes and looking decorative near doors. I had no family name anyone respected. My mother had died scrubbing the west stairs when I was four; I remembered her hands and the smell of lye and nothing else, because grief at four years old is a coat too big, and you grow out of it without meaning to.

What I did have — though I didn't know it — was an ear. Not for music. For *words*. The old words, the ones the priests said had died with the drowned kingdoms. When the court sorceress passed me in a corridor and muttered under her breath in a language like riverwater over stones, I repeated it back to her. Perfectly. With the accent of a place that hadn't existed for nine hundred years.

She stopped walking. She looked at me the way you look at a coin you've found in a fish's belly.

Her name was Maren Solace, though everyone called her the Gray Lady because she wore the same gray dress every day and had opinions about everything else. She became, over three years, the closest thing I had to a mother, which is a sentence I could only ever say in the old tongue, because in any language people use out loud it would sound like begging.

And then came the fifth anniversary of Aldous's crowning, and the feast, and the figs.

I remember the exact moment it began, because I was watching the king's face when it happened. He was laughing at something the Lord Chancellor said — a real laugh, the kind that crinkles his eyes, the kind Maren told me he only gave when he'd forgotten to be a king for a second. And then the laugh stopped. Not faded. *Stopped*, like a door closing in another room.

His eyes went hollow.

I don't mean sad. I mean empty the way a cup is empty, the way a house is empty after the family leaves and takes even their shadows' habits with them. Something looked out through King Aldous's face, and whatever it was, it was very old and very hungry and it had come home.

Then the screaming started, and I will spare you the inventory of the next hour except for the parts that matter: the candles guttering blue, the banners above the dais rotting in seconds from silk to lacework to dust, and the guests — the guests forgetting. You could watch it happen. A woman would look at her husband and her face would smooth over like water after a stone, and she would say, politely, *do I know you?* And he would open his mouth to answer and find that he couldn't remember answering, or her name, or why he had come to a party, or whether he'd ever been anywhere at all.

The Hollow didn't kill anyone that night. Killing is quick, and it wastes material. It ate inheritance. It reached into bloodlines and drank backward through them, generation by generation, so that by dawn there were noblemen in the palace square who could not tell you their grandmothers' names, and then, as the thing settled into its new crown and learned its appetite, farmers who forgot the songs their mothers sang, and smiths who lost the techniques their fathers' fathers' fathers had hammered into the family bones.

Maren found me under the dessert table. I don't know why I ran there. Children run toward tables when the world ends; we are practical creatures.

"Sebastian," she said, and her voice was steady in a way that frightened me more than the screaming, because I understood even then that steadiness under screaming is a choice, and choices cost. "Come here. Quickly."

There was blood on her gray dress. I never learned whose.

She knelt, and she took my face in both her cold hands, and she said the words. All of them. In the old tongue, in a voice that cracked like ice on a pond, she poured King Aldous into me.

That is the truest way I can say it. She didn't put the memories *in* me the way you put coins in a purse. She poured him, the whole man — his childhood in the orchard country, his mother's lullabies, his father's funeral, his wedding, his doubts, the weight of the crown described from the inside, every secret he kept and every kindness he never got credit for. Five-and-forty years of being Aldous, decanted into a seven-year-old dish-boy kneeling on sticky flagstones.

"Why?" I asked her. In the old tongue, because I couldn't have said it otherwise. "Why me?"

"Because you're empty enough to hold him," she said, which sounds cruel until you understand it was the kindest thing anyone had ever told me: my poverty, my namelessness, my unrecorded little life — all of it made me a vessel. "And because you'll run. Look at your feet. They're already pointed at the door."

They were.

"One more thing," she said, and now the steadiness was fraying, threads of it pulling loose. "He cannot be allowed to keep the crown. The thing inside it eats what kings are. If Aldous forgets himself entirely, it won't stop at the palace. It will eat Vellamere's whole past, and then the country's, and then—" She coughed. Blood, I realized later. Just blood. "You must not let it find you. Never say the old words aloud where walls can hear. Never sleep two nights in the same place. And Sebastian—"

She pressed her forehead to mine.

"—when you're older, someone will need him back. Keep him safe until then."

She died before midnight. I know because I stayed long enough to see it, crouched behind an overturned chair, and because afterward I carried the moment with the rest of it, filed away in the archive in my skull between Aldous's first tooth and the name of his childhood dog.

I was seven years old, and I left the palace barefoot, carrying a king.

* * *

Running from your own head is a strange profession, and I held it longer than most men hold honest ones.

Here is how it works. The memories do not arrive politely, on request, in order. They arrive like weather. I would be stealing bread in Harrowgate Market and suddenly I was thirty-eight years old and signing a treaty I could smell the ink of, my hand moving through signatures I'd never learned, tears on a face that wasn't mine because Aldous had hated this clause and signed it anyway for the sake of the grain ships. Or I'd be asleep in a hayloft and wake up mid-dream in his father's funeral procession, and I would grieve a man I'd never met with a depth that made me dizzy, and then lie in the dark trying to remember which griefs were actually mine.

My own were hard to find. That was the worst of it. A child's memories are small — a dead mother's hands, a stairwell, lye-smell — and they get crowded. Imagine keeping one candle lit in a house where someone has installed a chandelier of a thousand flames. Which light is yours?

For a while I kept a list. Scratched into the bottom of a wooden bowl I stole in my third winter: *Sebastian. Seven. Mother washed stairs. Lye. Figs.* I recited it every morning like a prayer, and every morning the list seemed smaller against the flood, and every morning I hated the king a little more for taking up so much room, and every morning I hated myself for hating him, because he hadn't asked for this either. He was in there somewhere, buried, drowning, dreaming someone else's dreams. Sometimes at night I felt him at the edges of my mind like a man knocking gently on the far side of a wall. *Hello,* the knock said. *Hello. Is anyone there.*

"I'm here," I'd whisper. "We're both here."

Two ghosts sharing one body, haunting each other.

The Wardens made the running worse. After the Hollowing, the Church of Ash declared that the old tongue was the Hollow's doorway — that magic itself was infection, and memory its fever. They weren't entirely wrong, which is the trick of good lies. Warden patrols learned to listen for it in markets and taverns, in the mutterings of the mad and the mourning. Anyone caught speaking it was taken to the pyres in Correction Square. I saw a woman burn once, in the town of Dell's Crossing, for singing a lullaby her grandmother had taught her. She was singing it to forget, the townspeople said afterward. Grief-work, they called it, old as anything. The Wardens called it treason against the present.

So I learned silence the way other boys learn whistling. I learned to hold the words behind my teeth until my jaw ached. I learned to let Aldous's memories wash through me without gripping them, the way you learn to float in cold water — no struggling, or it pulls you under.

And I learned, above all, not to flinch. Because the ones who flinch get noticed. A boy who goes rigid when a Warden mentions the old days is a boy carrying something.

By the time I reached Vellamere — drawn back, though I swore I never would be, pulled by something in the king's memories that ached toward the city like a stump aches toward a lost hand — I had perfected the art of being nobody. Barefoot. Filthy. Mute by reputation; I hadn't spoken aloud in almost two years, and I'd begun to wonder if my real voice still worked, or if it had been quietly replaced, shelf by shelf, with a dead man's.

Which brings me, more or less, to a door in the tanner's row during a curfew raid, and a bone-carver with ink-stained fingers who opened it anyway.

* * *

Here is the thing about Rosalind Marchetti that I figured out before she knew I knew: she hears them. The dead. She thinks she hides it — the tilting of her head, the pauses in conversation with no one, the way she sometimes answers questions a heartbeat after asking them, as if checking with someone unseen. But I grew up in a palace full of people pretending, and pretense has a texture. Hers is threadbare in exactly the shape of a secret.

The night she took me in, when the Wardens' boots were still echoing down the lane and she was bartering her door shut behind us, I heard the dead in her house. Faintly. Through the wall of my own crowded skull. A woman humming. An old man counting something, coins maybe, or sins. And underneath them, so deep I might have imagined it, a vast low murmur like a crowd heard from across a city — thousands of voices, worn soft, saying nothing I could catch.

I sat on her floor among the wood shavings and I thought: she talks to the dead, and I carry one alive, and between us we make nearly a whole person.

It should have terrified me. It did, a little. But mostly — and this is the shameful, tender truth I've told no one — mostly what I felt was relief. Two years of silence, and the first person I met in Vellamere was someone I didn't have to perform emptiness for. She already had ghosts. Mine could queue.

She fed me barley porridge with too much salt. She asked my name and I wrote it in the ash on her hearthstone, and when she read it she went very still, the way Maren had gone still in the corridor six years ago, coin-in-a-fish-belly stillness.

"Sebastian," she said, testing it.

And inside me, deep down, behind the wall, the knocking started again. *Hello. Hello. We're here. Someone finally knows our name.*

* * *

I write this in the third week, by stove-light, on the backs of her charm-wrapping papers, while she sleeps and the dead hum in her rafters and the king dreams his orchard dreams somewhere beneath my breastbone.

Because today everything changed again, and I need to set it down while my hands still obey me.

Today she brought out a femur. Old, yellowed, carved over with script so fine it looked like frost damage — and when I touched it, the flood came, but ordered this time, channeled, *directed*, the way water behaves in a millrace instead of a burst dam. The bone spoke. It spoke Aldous's coronation. It spoke the crown going on, and the moment of the laugh, and the moment the laugh stopped, and I stood in Rosalind's kitchen reliving the worst hour of my life while a dead man's leg bone narrated it, and Rosalind's face went white as her shavings.

She knows now. What I carry. Where it came from. What's wearing the crown across the city and what it does to bloodlines when it gets hungry.

She didn't flinch. That's the part I keep turning over, like a coin I can't spend. She looked at me — at the whole impossible archive of me, seven years of stolen childhood and five years of flight and a buried king knocking on the inside of my ribs — and she did not flinch, and she did not reach for the door, and she did not look at me the way the Wardens look at pyres.

She looked at me the way Maren did. Coin in a fish's belly. Except warmer. Except like maybe the coin was worth something beyond its metal.

"You're going to have to learn to use it," she said. "Before it uses you."

And then, from the corner of the room where no one was standing, in a voice like riverwater over stones, something answered her.

It wasn't me. I want to be clear about that. My mouth was shut; my hands were full of wrapping paper; I was standing perfectly still.

But the voice that spoke was mine, and the words were the old words, and they came out in the cadence of a man giving orders from a throne, and Rosalind went pale all over again — and I felt, unmistakably, for the first time in five years, the wall inside me crack.

Not break. Crack. Like ice on a pond in early spring.

Like something on the other side, tired of knocking, learning to turn the handle.

Tomorrow, she says, we begin lessons. Tonight, I sit by the ticking stove and I listen to the king move around inside my chest, and I am afraid in a brand-new way — not of losing myself anymore, but of what happens if I find him.

The dead in the rafters have gone quiet.

Even they, apparently, are waiting to hear what I say next.
