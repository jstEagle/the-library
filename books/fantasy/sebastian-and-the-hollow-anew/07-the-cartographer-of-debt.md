## 7. The Cartographer of Debt

There are three kinds of people you can find in the Saltcellar district after dark, and two of them will rob you. The third is Odric Vane, who will do something worse: he'll make you understand exactly what you've lost, itemized, in a hand so beautiful it looks like an apology.

I went to find him on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were when the Wardens rotated their patrols toward the river gates and left the Saltcellar to its own damp devices. I told Sebastian to stay behind my shop with the door barred and the shutters drawn, and I told him it was because the streets weren't safe for a boy his age, which was true, and not because a barefoot child who spoke the First Tongue would be worth more to a Warden than my entire inventory, which was also true, and truer.

He'd watched me from the window as I left. There's a particular look a child gives you when he's memorizing your face in case it's the last time he gets to — I knew that look. I'd worn it myself, once, at an age no one should have to learn it. I didn't let myself think about that. Thinking about it meant admitting what I was doing, which was walking into the flooded underbelly of Vellamere to ask a disgraced scholar to confirm that the voice in my head wasn't madness but arithmetic.

The dead had been getting louder all week. That was the thing nobody warned you about hearing them — they don't whisper politely. They accumulate. Like water behind a cracked wall, like debt with interest. And lately their voices had a new texture to them, thin in places, frayed, as though someone had been going through the archive of the dead and tearing out pages.

I wanted Odric to tell me I was imagining it.

Odric lived above a tannery that hadn't operated since before the Hollowing, in rooms that smelled of old salt and older paper. His door had no lock, only a sign painted in careful script: *No debts collected here. Debts are only recorded.* Which was either philosophy or a legal disclaimer. With Odric it was usually both.

I knocked the way you knock on the door of a man who's been exiled from the University: twice, then waited, then once more, so he'd know I was patient enough to be dangerous.

"Marchetti." He opened the door already looking tired, which I took as a compliment. He'd aged since I'd last seen him — three years, maybe four — gone grey at the temples, stooped like a man who'd spent too long bent over maps of places that didn't exist anymore. "If you're here about the charm you owe me, the answer is still no, I don't need magic, I need money."

"I'm not here about a charm."

"Everyone's here about a charm. It's the only currency left in this city that isn't ash." But he stepped aside to let me in, because underneath the bitterness Odric Vane was still, at his rotten core, a scholar, and scholars can't resist a question the way drunkards can't resist a bottle.

* * *

His rooms were exactly as I remembered: floor to ceiling with ledgers, every surface buried under charts drawn on vellum, linen, and in one desperate case, the back of a wallpaper panel. Maps of Vellamere, but not maps of streets. Odric mapped something else entirely. Each family line in the city was a river on his charts, each dead ancestor a tributary, each inheritance — land, name, grief, gift — a current flowing downstream through the generations.

Except the rivers were drying up.

I saw it before he said anything. A quarter of the chart nearest the window had been marked over in red ink, whole tributaries struck through, and beside them, in Odric's immaculate hand, a single word repeated like a refrain: *severed. severed. severed.*

"When did that start?" I asked.

He didn't pretend not to know what I meant. That was the other thing about Odric — he was too proud to waste time on small talk and too honest, in his way, to lie about data. "Eighteen months ago. Maybe two years. I thought it was clerical error at first. My own records contradicting themselves." He poured me tea I didn't want, from a pot that had seen better decades. "A family in Tanner's Row — the Hallowells. Five generations of rope-makers, meticulous people, kept a household book going back to the founding. The grandmother died in autumn. By spring, the granddaughter couldn't tell you the grandmother's name. Not grief-fog. Grief passes. This was—" he searched for the word, and a man like Odric searching for a word is a frightening thing to watch, "—excision."

"People forget things all the time."

"People misplace things," Odric said. "This is different. I started asking. Quietly. Scholar to widow, widow to cousin. Do you know how many bloodlines in this city have lost their dead in the last two years? Whole branches. Names gone. Faces gone. One woman in the Weavers' Quarter told me her father had been 'a tall man, she thought,' and when I asked what he did for a living, she said, 'Didn't he?' " He set down his cup with a click too precise to be casual. "That's not forgetting, Rosalind. Forgetting has edges. This is amputation."

The dead in my head shifted, restless, and I kept my face still the way you keep a lantern shuttered in wind. "And you have a theory."

"I have a map." He crossed to the great chart on the far wall — the master one, the one I'd heard rumors of, the reason the University had stripped his title and the Wardens had a file with his name on it. It showed Vellamere entire, every bloodline rendered as light, or what had once been light. Whole districts had gone dark at their roots. Not everywhere. Not evenly. The darkness spread outward from a single point.

The palace.

"The Hollowing wasn't an event," Odric said quietly. "Everyone talks about it like weather. A storm came, magic died, we built a new city on the ruins. But storms don't eat selectively. Look." His finger traced the spreading dark. "It follows inheritance. Bloodlines first, then the things bloodlines carry — names, gifts, griefs, the old words passed mother to daughter. Whatever sits on that throne isn't wearing the crown, Rosalind. It's *drinking* through it. Five years of drinking. Every heir in this city is a straw."

I thought of Sebastian. Barefoot, mute by anyone else's accounting, carrying a king's memories in a skull barely big enough for his own childhood. Carrying the oldest inheritance in Vellamere.

"How much does it take at a time?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the Marchetti family talent — my mother could smile through a funeral, and her mother before her, and now me, presumably, until whatever was eating the city got to my branch of the river too.

"Slowly, at first. A name here. A face there. The dead go quiet in a family, and everyone says *well, that's grief settling.*" He looked at me sharply. "You'd know about the dead going quiet."

Careful, Rosalind. "Bone-carvers deal in remembrance. It's professional interest."

"Mm." He let it go, which cost him visibly; curiosity was eating him alive behind those scholarly eyes. "Here's what matters. The taking is accelerating. Two years to strip a dozen lines. In the last six months? Dozens. And the pattern's changed — it's not drifting outward anymore. It's converging. On anything old. Anything *carried.*" He tapped the chart where a few bright threads still ran, stubborn, through the darkening city. "Whatever it's building toward, it wants the deep reserves. The old words. The inheritances that go back past the founding."

The room felt colder. Or I did.

"And if something carried the oldest inheritance in the city," I said, watching his face, "walked into Vellamere last month. What happens then?"

Odric went very still, the way a man goes still when he realizes the map he's been drawing includes himself. "Then it stops sipping," he said, "and starts drinking."

* * *

I made him swear oaths before I told him anything — real ones, on his mother's grave-name, which for a man like Odric was worth more than any Warden's seal. Then I gave him the shape of it, if not the flesh: a boy, an inheritance, a crown. He listened the way scholars listen, which is to say he interrupted eleven times and took notes during my sentences.

When I finished, he sat back and rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment he looked less like an exiled academic and more like what he'd been before the fall: the youngest cartographer ever appointed to the Royal Archive, the man who'd mapped the magical debts of the entire kingdom before his twenty-fifth year.

"You understand what this means," he said finally. "For you. For the boy."

"That we're dead if the Wardens find us. Yes, I'd worked that out."

"Worse. You're not hiding a fugitive, Rosalind. You're hiding the *account book.* Everything that thing on the throne has eaten, everything it still wants — it's all in that boy's head, catalogued, indexed, alive." He leaned forward. "It won't send Wardens after him. Wardens are for smugglers and hedge-witches. For him it will unmake the city if it has to. Block by block. Bloodline by bloodline. Until the only inheritance left in Vellamere is the one it can reach."

Outside, somewhere across the rooftops, a curfew bell rang the second hour, and beneath its bronze voice I heard — faintly, frayed, thinner than last week — the dead murmuring in their thousands. And I noticed, with a cold that started in my wrists and worked inward, that one of the voices I'd carried for years, a weaver woman named Maren who'd died in my mother's time and never stopped chattering about loom-tensions —

Maren was gone.

Not quiet. *Gone.* Searched the whole crowded dark of my skull and found no trace. As if she'd never spoken at all.

"Rosalind?" Odric was watching me. "You've gone white as your merchandise."

"Nothing." I stood, and my knees cooperated out of sheer professional discipline. "I need to get home. Bar your door, Odric. And burn nothing — if they come for your maps, let them take the room, but the master chart goes in the floor cache. Swear it."

"On my mother's name." He hesitated at the door, and for one unguarded moment the exile fell away and the young cartographer looked out. "Rosalind. If the boy is what you say — don't teach him to hide. Teach him to *spend.* An inheritance hoarded is just a larger theft waiting to happen."

I walked home through the empty streets with my collar up and my heart going like a loom, counting the voices in my head like a miser counting coins after a robbery.

Sebastian was awake when I came in, sitting cross-legged by the cold hearth, and he looked up at me with those too-old eyes and signed, quick and anxious: *You learned something bad.*

I crouched down to his level. Behind him, on the shelf, my charms hung in their neat rows — and one of them, a mourning piece I'd carved three winters ago for a woman long since paid, had gone dark. Not faded. *Dark.* The whisper of the dead woman it held, snuffed out like a wick pinched between fingers.

"Yes," I said aloud, and took his hands in mine. "And I think it already knows you're here."
