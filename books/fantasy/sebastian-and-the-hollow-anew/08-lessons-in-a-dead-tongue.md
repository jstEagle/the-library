## 8. Lessons in a Dead Tongue

*Rosalind*

The first rule of teaching anyone anything is that you must pretend to know what you're doing. I had been pretending for thirty years — to customers, to Wardens, to myself — so I was, if nothing else, qualified.

"Again," I said.

Sebastian sat cross-legged on my workroom floor among the bone shavings, his hands flat on his knees the way I'd shown him, and looked at me with the particular patience of someone who has been dead-adjacent far longer than he's been alive. He was nine, maybe ten. It was hard to tell. Children who carry other people's lifetimes in their skulls tend to have eyes that finish aging without them.

"The word," I said. "Just the first one. Don't let it take your whole mouth."

He opened it — his mouth, not the door in it — and spoke.

The sound was wrong. Not ugly-wrong. *Old*-wrong, the way a cellar smells wrong when you realize the damp has been there longer than the house. The single syllable moved through my shop like a draft under a door, and three of my hanging charms swung on their cords though no window was open, and somewhere in the walls the dead stirred like dogs hearing their names.

I put my hand flat on the bench. "Good," I said, when my voice agreed to work. "That was good."

It hadn't been good. It had been *almost catastrophic*. But the first rule of teaching, as established, is pretense.

Here was the problem, laid out on my bench like a femur waiting for the saw: Sebastian didn't speak the old tongue. The old tongue spoke him. When it came, it came whole — a flood, a possession, a king's coronation pouring out of a child's throat while his own self stood aside and watched. And every time it happened, something of *him* got a little thinner, the way a riverbank thins under flood after flood until one day there's no bank left, just river.

So we would build him a bank. Stone by stone. Word by word.

"One syllable," I said. "You choose it. You open the door a crack, not off its hinges. Can you feel where it lives? In you?"

He closed his eyes. His brow furrowed — that small, ferocious concentration he wore like armor. Then he nodded, once, and pointed to the center of his chest, and then, after a moment's thought, to his throat, and then, with a small apologetic shrug, to everywhere at once.

"Everywhere," I said. "Of course it is. Nothing about you does things by halves."

He smiled. It was the first smile I'd seen from him that wasn't borrowed from someone dead, and I confess it did something structurally unsound to my chest.

We worked until the candle burned down to a thumb's width. One syllable, then two. He learned to hold the door open a crack — to let a single word through and then *shut it*, which took eleven attempts and left us both sweating, because each attempt was like watching a boy hold back a tide with a broom. But on the eleventh try, he said *vel* — just *vel*, a word I knew only as the sound bones make when they remember — and then closed his mouth, and the word died politely in the air instead of detonating, and Sebastian opened his eyes and looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the stunned face of a person who has just discovered they are allowed to be powerful.

"There," I said, and my voice cracked on it, damn it all. "There. Again tomorrow."

* * *

*Sebastian*

Rosalind Marchetti's shop smelled of dust and tallow and, underneath everything, of the sea — though Vellamere's harbor was half a city away. She said she couldn't smell it anymore. She said the dead had lived in her nose so long they'd worn grooves in it.

I liked her shop. That was the strange part. I had spent five years being afraid of every room I slept in, because rooms were where the memories came — they pooled in corners, they seeped up through floorboards, they ambushed me between sleeping and waking with other people's mothers' voices. But Rosalind's shop was full of the dead already, full to the rafters, and somehow that made it safe. A cup that is already full cannot overflow.

She taught me in the mornings, before the customers came. She taught me the way she carved: slowly, precisely, with small jokes filed down until only the sharp part showed.

"You're gripping it," she said, on the fourth morning. "The word. You're holding it like a knife you're afraid of."

"Aren't you afraid of it?"

"I'm afraid of plenty." She turned a charm blank in her fingers, not looking at me. "But fear makes you grip, and gripping makes it push back. It's a language, boy, not a wolf. Languages want to be spoken *with*, not *by*."

*I am spoken by,* I almost said. I didn't. Some truths were still too heavy to lift.

That afternoon, everything changed, and it changed because of a spoon.

Not a magical spoon. An ordinary pewter spoon, bent from years of some dead man's grip, that Rosalind kept on the windowsill because — she said — it amused her. I picked it up to move it out of the light, and the man who had owned it woke up.

*Careful with that,* he said, inside my mouth, inside my hands. *My wife gave me that. Third year of our marriage. She said a man should have one thing that bends and doesn't break.*

I froze. Rosalind looked up from her carving.

"He's talking," I managed. "The spoon man. He's — he's not taking anything. He's just *talking.*"

Rosalind set down her knife very carefully, the way she did when something mattered. "What's his name?"

*I'm Tam,* said the spoon man. *Tam Ferrow. Tell the bone-woman the spoon was my wife's idea, not mine. I wanted a sword. She said a sword never stirs the soup.*

"His name is Tam," I said. "He says the spoon was his wife's idea."

And Rosalind Marchetti — who did not flinch from me, who had never once flinched from me, which was the whole reason I trusted her, though I'd never found the words to tell her so — Rosalind laughed. A real laugh, startled out of her, rusty as an unused hinge.

"Well," she said to the spoon, "she sounds like she was right."

*She always was,* said Tam, warm as bread. And then, gentler, to me: *You held the door, lad. Did you feel it? You held it open on purpose.*

I had. For the first time, the dead hadn't come through me like weather. I had *let* them, the way you let a guest in — and a guest, unlike a flood, can be thanked and shown out.

"They can coach me," I said, and heard my own voice go thin with wonder. "Rosalind, they've been doing this longer than either of us. They know how it works from the *inside*—"

"I know what they are," she said, but her eyes had gone bright and fierce. "I've known since I was six years old. I just never thought to ask them for help. Thirty years of carrying this alone because it never occurred to me that the dead might be *good company*."

From the walls, from the beams, from the shavings on the floor, the dead of Rosalind's shop began — softly, tentatively, like people leaning over a fence — to laugh.

* * *

*Rosalind*

I want it recorded, somewhere, by someone, that for eleven days that winter my shop was the happiest place in Vellamere.

This matters because of what came after. Hope is not foolishness merely because it ends. A meal is not wasted because you get hungry again. For eleven days, a mute boy and a woman who talked to bones built something in that workroom that neither the Hollow nor the Wardens nor the whole ash-hearted law of this city could unmake, and I will not let what came later retroactively poison it. Memory is the one country the Hollow hasn't conquered yet. I intend to keep it.

Sebastian learned. Saints, how he learned. With the dead coaching him — Tam from the spoon, a drowned fishwife named Maren who had opinions about posture, an old Warden's grandmother who found the entire enterprise hilarious — he went from drowning in the old tongue to swimming in it in less than a fortnight. They taught him things I couldn't: how the words sat in the body, where they pooled, which doors stuck. Maren taught him to breathe from the belly before speaking, "so the words have a harbor to come home to." The grandmother taught him to *name himself first* — to say *I am Sebastian* in the old tongue before every lesson, a keel under a sail.

"That's the trick of it," I told him one evening, watching him anchor himself and then speak a full sentence — a *sentence*, controlled, chosen, released — without his eyes going distant. "You don't fight the river. You build the boat."

He wrote on his slate — he still couldn't say much plain Vellamere speech; whatever the sorceress had done to seal the king inside him had taken his born voice with it — and held it up:

*Will I ever hear myself again? Or only them?*

I thought about lying. I had gotten quite good at it. Instead I sat down across from him and told the truth, because he deserved a teacher who treated him as more than a vessel.

"I don't know," I said. "Maybe the voice comes back when the memories find their proper resting place. Maybe it doesn't. But Sebastian — listen to me. What you say isn't less yours for borrowing a mouth. Every word you choose is still a choice. Nobody can inherit your will. Not a king, not a Hollow, not a hundred generations of dead. That's the one thing that's only ever been yours."

He looked at the slate a long time. Then he wiped it, and wrote:

*Then I choose to stay here. With you. If that's allowed.*

"It's allowed," I said, gruffly, because if I wasn't gruff I was going to do something embarrassing like cry in front of a child, and I had a reputation as a hard-faced bone-witch to maintain. "It's allowed, you ridiculous boy. Now write it again. Slower. The dead are watching and Maren will correct your posture."

He laughed — silently, shoulders shaking, mouth wide — and outside the window the snow came down soft on Vellamere, and in the walls our congregation of the dead hummed something old and contented, and for eleven days, that was the whole world.

On the twelfth day, the bells rang the curfew-change early, which meant soldiers, which meant a raid, which meant that a knock came at my door at an hour when honest people were asleep and dishonest ones were gone.

Through the wood, a woman's voice. Clipped. Weary. Familiar from the market square, from the ledger, from every nightmare I'd had since Sebastian's description began circulating among the Wardens of Ash.

"Marchetti. Open the door. I know the boy is here."

I looked at Sebastian. He looked at me. Around us, very quietly, the dead went still — listening.

I blew out the candle and went to answer it.
