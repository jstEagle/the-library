## 9. Ilsa at the Door

I had always imagined that when Warden Ilsa Corvane finally came for me, it would be with boots and torches and a squad of ash-marked enforcers kicking my door into splinters. There would be shouting. There would be a ledger held aloft like scripture. There would be, if I was lucky, a moment to swallow something incriminating before they dragged me to the pyre.

What I got instead was three precise knocks at half past nine in the morning, while Sebastian was upstairs reciting the names of dead Vellamere fishwives in a language that predated the city's walls.

"Stay up there," I hissed through the floorboards. "And if you must talk to anyone, talk to no one."

The knocks came again. Patient. Measured. The knock of a woman who had never once had to break down a door because people simply opened them.

I wiped bone dust on my apron — pointless, since the apron was mostly bone dust by now — and opened the door.

Warden Corvane stood on my step in her grey coat, hands clasped behind her back, alone. No escort. No torches. Her face had the particular stillness of a pond that looks calm right up until you notice nothing living in it is moving. She was younger than I'd expected, or perhaps I'd spent five years imagining her older, the way you imagine all your nightmares taller. Grey shot through dark hair pinned severely. A scar bisecting one eyebrow. And eyes that did the thing Wardens' eyes were trained not to do: they looked *tired*.

"Warden," I said. "If this is about the charm I sold Magret Dunlow, I'll have you know that widow has been swindling charm-sellers since before the Hollowing. Whatever she told you, the truth is worse."

"I'm not here about charms." Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had learned that volume was a concession. "May I come in?"

That was wrong. Everything about that was wrong. Wardens did not ask. Wardens announced, entered, inventoried. Asking permission was what people did when they needed something, and Ilsa Corvane needing something from a bone-carver of my reputation was roughly as likely as the tide applying for citizenship.

"You may," I said, because refusing would have been more suspicious than admitting her, "but I should warn you the tea is terrible. I keep it for appearances."

"Then don't bother." She stepped inside, and her gaze swept the shop with an efficiency that made my skin prickle — the workbench, the hanging charms, the little stove, the curtain over the stairs. It paused on the curtain exactly one heartbeat longer than it paused on anything else.

One heartbeat. I'd sold enough mourning charms to know what one heartbeat of hesitation cost.

"Sit, if you like," I said. "Or stand. Standing suits you."

She stood. Of course she stood. She unclasped her hands from behind her back, and I saw then that she was holding something: a small cloth bundle, wrapped tight, the way people wrap things they can't bear to look at but can't bear to put down.

"My daughter's name is Anneliese," she said.

* * *

There is a particular silence that falls over a room when someone says their child's name to a stranger. I had heard it a hundred times across my workbench, from widows and fathers and once, memorably, a man mourning a horse he'd loved better than his wife. It is the sound of a person setting down something heavy on a table between you and saying: *here, look at it, because I cannot carry it alone anymore.*

Warden Ilsa Corvane set her daughter's name on my workbench and unwrapped the bundle.

Inside was a child's shoe. Small. Leather gone soft with wear. And tucked into it, the way mothers tuck things, a lock of hair tied with thread.

"She's eleven," Corvane said. "Ten when it started. She used to tell me everything. Every quarrel at school, every dream, every unfair thing the world did to her before breakfast. Then, two years ago, she began losing pieces." Her thumb moved over the shoe's worn heel, back and forth, a small unconscious motion, like worrying a charm. "Not like forgetting. I know what forgetting looks like. This is different. This is like pages being cut out of a book. Clean edges. She doesn't miss what's gone because she doesn't know there was anything to miss."

"What kind of pieces?" I asked, though I already knew. Gods help me, I already knew, because Odric's maps were spread across my table not four days ago, and the blackened bloodlines on them had names attached, and somewhere among those names I'd half-registered *Corvane* and thought nothing of it. Wardens had families. Wardens had daughters. The Hollow didn't care whose inheritance it ate.

"Her grandmother first," Corvane said. "My mother died when Anneliese was six. Last spring, Anneliese asked me who the woman in the portrait was. Not *who is that*. She looked at her own grandmother's face and felt nothing. Then her father —" A pause. The first crack in the even voice, hairline-thin. "He died in the salt floods, year before last. Two months ago she asked me if we'd ever had visitors who left a coat here, because there was a coat in the closet she didn't recognize. His coat. She wore it as a dressing gown last week and laughed about how it smelled of strangers."

I thought of Odric's finger tracing the spreading stain on his map. *It doesn't take memories whole,* he'd said. *It takes the threads that hold them. Pull enough threads and the tapestry comes apart on its own.*

"And you've been to physicians," I said.

"Four. They prescribed rest, tonics, and in one case a small hammer to the skull, which I declined." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite humor, but the fossil of it. "Then a colleague mentioned a rumor. That in the market district there's a carver whose charms are too good. Whose mourning pieces make the bereaved feel *accompanied*, which is against several ordinances, because accompanied grief tends to remember things it shouldn't." Her eyes came up to mine, flat and grey and merciless. "That's you, Marchetti. It has been you for years. I could have taken you any time in the last three. I have your file. It's thicker than most."

"Flattering."

"It isn't a compliment. It's arithmetic." She set the shoe down on my workbench, precisely, square to the edge, the way a woman squares papers before signing a death warrant. "Here is my arithmetic. If I arrest you today, I follow procedure, you burn or rot depending on the magistrate's digestion, and my daughter keeps losing her mother's face next. Because it's started on me now too, Marchetti. Yesterday she called me by the cook's name. The cook left in the spring. And I sat in my kitchen and realized that whatever is eating her is working its way backward through our bloodline, and when it reaches her entirely, it will start on whatever's left of me, and there is not one physician, priest, or Warden in this city who can stop it."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread.

"But you think I can stop it," I said carefully.

"I think you hear the dead." She said it without flinching, without lowering her voice, and I understood suddenly why she'd risen so high in the Wardens: she was constitutionally incapable of pretending. "I think the reason your charms are too good is that you're not selling echoes. You're selling the real thing. And I think whatever took my daughter's memories is made of the same substance as whatever you traffic in, and I am standing in the shop of the only person alive who might understand the mechanism." She drew a breath. "So. Here is my offer."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No one ever does before hearing terms. It's the hearing that ruins them." She reached into her coat and produced a slim ledger — not the great public ledger of the marked, the one that haunted the market district, but a private one, worn at the corners. She laid it beside the shoe. "This is my personal record. Names I've collected and never entered into the official rolls. People like you. People like the hedge-healer on Tanner's Row, the bell-ringer who remembers sermons in the old tongue, forty-one souls in this district alone whom I have protected by the simple method of writing their names down where only I could see them and then losing the page." Her jaw tightened. "You want a blind eye, Marchetti? I *am* the blind eye. I have been the blind eye for years, and it has cost me sleep I will never recover, and now I find myself asking whether the blind eye has been worth anything at all, because my daughter is being devoured and every ordinance I ever enforced has done nothing, *nothing*, to slow it."

She was shaking. Not visibly — her hands stayed clasped, her voice stayed level — but I could see it in the tendons of her neck, in the way her breath came a fraction too fast. Five years of holding a wall up with her spine.

"If you can save her," she said, "you get the ledger. All of it. My protection, formal and informal, for you and anyone under your roof. And I will burn my copy of the market survey that lists a barefoot boy answering to no registered household, seen entering this shop." 

The floor did not open up and swallow me, which I considered rude, given the opportunity.

"A boy," I repeated, aiming for baffled and probably achieving constipated. "Barefoot boys are common as pigeons."

"Common as pigeons, yes. But this one was seen by three witnesses, described as speaking words that made a fishwife drop her basket, and the description matches a circular that came down from the palace garrison eight days ago. Do you know what the palace wants with a mute child who suddenly speaks the old tongue?" She leaned forward, and for the first time her composure bent, just slightly, around something that looked almost like fear. "Neither do I. But the palace garrison doesn't issue circulars over pigeons, and the fact that *I* intercepted that circular before it reached my own wardens is the only reason your door still has hinges. So you see, Marchetti, we are not so differently situated, you and I. We are both hiding something the state would burn us for. The difference is that mine has a name and a shoe."

Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked.

Corvane's eyes went to the ceiling. To the curtain. Back to me.

"That would be the cat," I said.

"You don't own a cat. I've watched this shop for two years. You own a cat-shaped absence of cat, which is itself suspicious."

"In my defense, the cat is also illegal."

For one suspended moment, I saw something move behind her face — the ghost of a woman who might, in another life, in an unbanned world, have been funny. Then it was gone, filed away, and she was the Warden again.

"Marchetti." She picked up the shoe, cradled it against her chest like an infant, and I watched a woman who had spent five years enforcing the death of magic stand in a bone-carver's shop and choose, visibly, deliberately, to beg. "I have burned people. I have signed the orders and watched the smoke and told myself it was law, it was order, it was the price of surviving the Hollowing. I can live with that. What I cannot live with is my daughter looking at me one morning with polite, empty eyes and asking who I am — and knowing that I could have done something, and chose my oath instead." Her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "I am offering you everything I have. Tell me what you need. Tell me it's impossible. Just don't tell me nothing, because I have been telling myself nothing for two years and it tastes like ash."

And there it was. The thing I'd been circling since she said *Anneliese*: the recognition. Because I knew that taste. I had built a whole life out of swallowing it — carving whispers when I carried thunder, shrinking my gift to a parlor trick so the city wouldn't kill me for it. Weary self-erasure, my specialty, my daily bread. And here stood the woman sent to erase people like me, confessing that erasure had eaten her family from the inside, and asking me to do the one thing I'd sworn off doing.

Save someone. Loudly. With everything I had.

"The boy upstairs," I said slowly, "is not my son, my nephew, or my cat. He is a great deal more dangerous than all three, and helping him is the single most illegal act available to either of us. If I use what he carries to reach your daughter, I cannot promise she gets her memories back. I can't promise she survives the attempt. And I certainly can't promise the palace won't learn what we've done."

"I didn't ask for promises. I asked for terms."

"Then here are mine." I held out my hand for the ledger. "You give me that. You give me the circular. And you give me time — a week, maybe less — because what I have to try requires preparation I won't describe in a room I assume you've bugged with at least one listening charm."

A muscle jumped in her cheek. "There is no listening charm."

"There's a loose brick by the stove that hums when you hold it. Either it's a listening charm or my house is haunted, and frankly both options explain a lot about my life."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she placed the ledger in my hand — it was heavier than it looked, the way guilt usually is — and stepped back toward the door.

"One week," she said. "After that, I report the boy's description as unverifiable and let the palace send its own hounds. Understand me, Marchetti: I can shield you from the Wardens. No one can shield you from the palace."

"Understood." I walked her to the door, and as she crossed the threshold she paused, her back to me, the morning light catching the grey in her hair.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the rumor about your charms is true. My sister bought one from you, three years ago, after our mother passed. She said it was the first night she slept." A beat. "I reported the sale anyway. I want you to know that about me before you decide whether to help me."

"I already decided," I said. "About thirty seconds after you said *Anneliese*. I'm just resentful about it."

She left. I watched her go down the street with her straight back and her terrible arithmetic, and then I closed the door, leaned against it, and let out a breath I felt I'd been holding since the Hollowing.

From the top of the stairs, Sebastian's voice drifted down — careful, accented, learning my language the way he learned everything, hungrily. "Was that the woman who burns people?"

"Yes."

"Is she going to burn us?"

"No." I climbed the stairs to where he sat surrounded by scraps of carved bone, the king's old ghosts folded up inside his small body like letters in a drawer. "She's going to help us. Which is considerably more frightening."

"Why?"

I looked at the ledger in my hand, at forty-one names written in a Warden's secret mercy, and at the shoe-shaped hole in my heart where a stranger's grief had lodged itself and refused to leave.

"Because," I said, "she's going to introduce us to her daughter. And I have a horrible feeling that when we meet what's left inside that girl, we're going to find out exactly how fast the Hollow is feeding — and exactly how little time we have."

Sebastian went very still. And in the silence that followed, one of the dead spoke through him — an old woman's voice, thin and urgent, rising out of his throat like smoke:

"*The girl is not the first. The girl is not the worst. Ask the Warden how many children have stopped dreaming.*"

I sat down heavily on the stairs.

We had a week. It was beginning to look like a very generous estimate.
