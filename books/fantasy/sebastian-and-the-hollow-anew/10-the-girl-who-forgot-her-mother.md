## 10. The Girl Who Forgot Her Mother

The Corvane house smelled of beeswax and fear.

I want to be honest about that, because in the stories people tell afterward — and there are stories now, I've heard them — everything gets smoothed over. The brave bone-carver. The boy who speaks with the dead. Nobody mentions that when Warden Ilsa Corvane opened her door to us at dusk, her knuckles white around the frame, the first thing I noticed wasn't courage or destiny. It was the smell. Beeswax on the banisters, polish on the floors, a house scrubbed so clean it was screaming *nothing is wrong here*, which is what frightened houses always say.

And under it, faint as smoke: fear.

"Inside," Ilsa said. "Quickly. Before the neighbors decide your face is interesting."

Rosalind went first. She'd dressed for the occasion in her least memorable coat, the gray one that made her look like weather rather than a person, and she'd spent the walk over coaching me on how to behave in a Warden's home. *Don't touch anything carved. Don't speak the old tongue above a whisper. If anyone asks, you're my nephew from the coast and you're dull.* I had asked what I should do if the plan failed, and she had looked at me with those tired carver's eyes and said, "Then we improvise, and I apologize in advance for whatever I do."

I liked her enormously for that. It's strange, the things that make you trust a person. Not grand gestures. Just honesty about the likelihood of disaster.

* * *

The daughter's name was Maren, and she was eleven, and she did not know her mother.

That's not quite true. She knew *of* her mother the way you know of a historical figure. She could recite facts. *My mother is Warden Ilsa Corvane. My mother enforces the Law of Ash. My mother has gray eyes.* But when Ilsa came into the sitting room and said her name — just her name, softly, the way you'd set down something breakable — Maren looked up from her embroidery with the polite blankness of a child greeting a stranger who has been introduced too many times.

"Mother," she said. Correctly. Warmly, even, if warmth can be performed from memory of instructions. "You're home early."

"I wanted to see you." Ilsa's voice didn't crack. I remember being impressed by that, and then realizing it wasn't strength holding her together. It was practice. She'd been doing this for months, this nightly theater of being known by her own child. "How were your lessons?"

"Good. Mistress Hallow says my stitching is improving." Maren held up the hoop. A pattern of lilies, even and careful. "She says I have good hands. She says—" and here the girl paused, frowning slightly, like someone reaching for a stair that isn't where their foot remembers, "—she says I remind her of someone. She wouldn't say who."

I felt Rosalind go very still beside me.

Mistress Hallow hadn't said who because she couldn't have known. But the dead knew, and the dead had been trying to tell Maren through every mouth they could borrow, and each time the words arrived they must have dissolved before meaning took hold. *You remind her of someone.* Of course she did. She reminded everyone of herself. That was the whole horror of it. The girl was becoming a stranger wearing her own face, and the only people who noticed were ghosts.

Ilsa caught my eye across the room. The question in it was naked, and it cost her everything to let it be seen: *Can you help her?*

I wish I could say I answered honestly. What I actually did was nod, because she was a Warden and I was afraid of her, and because hope is easier than truth, and because some part of me — the part that had spent five years running from my own head — wanted desperately to believe that nothing eaten is ever entirely gone.

* * *

We did the work in Maren's bedroom, by candlelight, with Ilsa standing watch at the door like a sentry outside her own heart.

Rosalind laid out her tools on the coverlet: three charms, a sliver of relic bone no longer than my finger, and the small knife she used for fine work, its handle worn smooth by years of other people's grief. She explained it to Maren gently, in the voice she used on widows — I recognized it, the soft trade-voice, the one that promised comfort without promising miracles.

"This will feel like falling asleep in a warm bath," she told the girl. "You might hear voices. They're not going to hurt you. If you're frightened, squeeze my hand and we stop."

Maren, to her credit, was not frightened. Children rarely are, I've learned, until adults teach them what to fear. She lay back against her pillows and watched us with bright, curious eyes and said, "Will it hurt Mother if it doesn't work?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"No," Rosalind said, after a beat too long.

"That's good," Maren said, satisfied, closing her eyes. "Because she cries at night. I hear her through the wall. I don't know why she cries, but I've decided it's about Father, because that's what the neighbors think, and it seems rude to ask."

Ilsa turned her face to the door. Her shoulders did not move, which is its own kind of crying.

I sat on the edge of the bed and took the girl's hand, and I reached down into the place where I keep the old language — it isn't a place, exactly, it's more like a well behind my ribs, and things climb up out of it whether I invite them or not — and I spoke the word for *opening*.

Here is what I have never told anyone, not even Rosalind, though I suspect she guessed: when I open, I am afraid. Every time. Because opening means becoming a doorway, and doorways don't get to choose what walks through. Most nights it's small things. A grandmother's recipe. A soldier's last joke. The name of a dog dead two hundred years, spoken with such love that I sometimes have to stop and sit with it.

But tonight, reaching into Maren Corvane, I found almost nothing.

Not silence — silence I know, silence is just an empty room. This was worse. This was a room that had been *tidied*. Everything in its place, everything smooth, and nothing left of what had been there before. It was like walking into a house after a fire that someone had carefully repainted over. The walls were fresh. The walls were wrong.

"She's in," I whispered to Rosalind. "There's... structure. Memories leave shapes, did you know that? Even faded ones leave shapes, like—"

"Like footprints in ash," Rosalind said quietly. "Yes. Go on."

I went deeper. I called the old words the way she'd taught me — *gently, Sebastian, you're not prying a lock, you're asking a door* — and shapes began to rise. A birthday. A kitchen. A woman singing off-key while kneading bread, flour to her elbows. A hand braiding hair. A voice saying *my little wren, my little wren* —

And then, mid-word, the memory *folded*. Not faded. Folded, neat as linen, and beneath it nothing. I pushed. I found another shape — a festival, lanterns, a man lifting a small girl onto his shoulders — and it folded too, and beneath it, nothing. Again. A scraped knee, a mother's kiss, a lullaby half-remembered — fold, fold, fold, and underneath all of them, at the bottom of the girl like sediment at the bottom of a drained pond—

Something looked back at me.

I won't pretend I have better words than that. There was a presence in the deepest layer of Maren Corvane, coiled around the roots of her memories the ivy coils around a tree, and it was not eating anymore. Eating is messy. Eating leaves marks. This thing had *digested*, and digested, and digested, and what remained was not a wound but a garden — a garden growing in the shape of absence, beautiful and total, and it felt me touching the edges of its work and it did not panic.

It *purred*.

I pulled back so fast I nearly fell off the bed. Maren opened her eyes, blinking, sweet-faced, unharmed.

"Did it work?" she asked. And then, seeing my face, with the terrible kindness children deploy without knowing: "It's all right. Whatever you were looking for, it's all right. I don't miss it. You can't miss what you never had."

But she had had it. She had had a mother who sang off-key and called her *little wren*, and the thing inside her had taken that and grown flowers over the grave of it, and the girl would never grieve because the girl would never know there was anything to grieve.

I said, "Excuse me," and I made it to the washroom before I was sick.

* * *

Rosalind found me sitting on the floor with my back against the cold tile, shaking.

"It saw me," I said. "Rosalind, it *saw* me. It's in her. It's not — we thought it was in the palace, we thought it was in the king, one thing in one place, but it's in *her*, it's in a child in a Warden's house, it's everywhere, it's—"

"Breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe. It purred, Rosalind. Like a cat. Like it was *pleased* I came." I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "I couldn't get anything. There's nothing left. Five minutes of work and there's nothing left to save. Do you understand? I'm the one who's supposed to fix this. That's the whole — that's the entire arrangement. The boy with the dead tongue comes and speaks the lost things back. And I stood there and I had *nothing*. I gave her nothing. I gave her mother nothing."

Rosalind sat down on the floor next to me. She didn't touch me — she knows I flinch when I'm drowning in someone else's feelings, that borrowed grief sits badly on my skin — but she sat close enough that our shoulders nearly met, which is her way of holding on.

"How much was left?" she asked. Not *did you fail*. How much was left. She wanted the number. Numbers are how she survives things; she carves them into her thinking like marks on a tally stick.

"Scraps. Shapes with the middles gone. I got maybe — a dozen fragments. A song. The word *wren*. A kitchen." I laughed, and it came out awful. "A kitchen, Rosalind. I can give an eleven-year-old a kitchen."

"A kitchen is where mothers live," she said. "Don't sneer at kitchens."

And that undid me more than the failure had. Because she was right, and because she said it without pity, and because somewhere below my panic I understood what she was really telling me: *the scraps matter, the scraps are the whole job, nobody gets the whole person back, we work in fragments or we do nothing.*

"Ilsa's going to ask," I said finally. "In a minute she's going to walk in here and ask me if her daughter can be saved, and I'm going to have to tell a Warden of Ash that the Hollow has already finished eating her child, and that the best I can offer is a kitchen."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "You are. And you'll tell her the truth, because she'll know a lie, Wardens always know lies, it's the one magic they kept." She stood, brushed off her gray coat, and offered me her hand. "Come on. Cruel kindnesses shouldn't be kept waiting."

* * *

I told her the truth.

I told it plainly, the way Rosalind had taught me — no poetry, no cushioning, because cushioning is a debt you pay later with interest. I told Ilsa Corvane that the Hollow had been inside her daughter for longer than any of us had guessed, that it had consumed the memories whole and grown itself into the space they left, and that what I could recover was fragments. A song. A nickname. The feeling of a kitchen.

I expected rage. I braced for it — the ledger, the irons, the word *heretic* hissed through teeth.

What I got instead was Ilsa standing very straight in her scrubbed-clean sitting room, looking at her daughter's closed bedroom door, and saying, in a voice sanded flat:

"The song. Can she keep the song?"

"If I anchor it in a charm, yes. It'll fade slower. Years, maybe. Not forever. Nothing I make lasts forever."

"Then give her the song." Ilsa's jaw worked. "And the kitchen. Skip the rest. If she's going to lose me anyway, I'd rather she lose me cleanly than spend ten years grieving a ghost in pieces." She turned and looked at me, and her gray eyes were dry and terrible. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, boy? I am asking you to help my daughter forget me *faster*. Because the slow way — the way it's been doing it — is crueller than anything I could do with irons and a ledger."

I said I understood. I didn't, not fully. I was twelve years old and I understood nothing except that the world was crueler and more organized than I'd thought.

While Rosalind carved — and I watched her do it, watched her put the song and the kitchen into a small round charm like seeds into soil — Ilsa walked me to the door. At the threshold she stopped, and lowered her voice, and asked the question I'd been dreading.

"You felt it. Inside her. The entity." Not a question. "Tell me true, boy. Is Maren unusual? Or is this in other houses too?"

I thought of the city at night, all those windows, all those families. I thought of the market women who'd forgotten their grandmothers' names and laughed it off. I thought of Odric saying, weeks ago, *bloodlines are thinning, boy, the past is leaking out of this city like water from a cracked jug,* and how I'd nodded along without understanding.

"It's in other houses," I said. "I think it's in most of them. I think it's been in most of them for years, and we're only now learning to feel it."

Ilsa Corvane closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had changed in her face — not softening, exactly. Recalculating. A soldier discovering the war is not where she thought it was.

"Five days," she said. "That's how long I can keep Maren's name off the ledger, and yours, and the carver's. After that, the district reports come due and I can't hide a hole that big." She pressed something into my hand — a token, lead-stamped, a Warden's pass. "Whatever you and Marchetti are planning, plan faster."

* * *

We walked home through streets gone blue with evening, and neither of us spoke for a long time. The lamps were being lit. Somewhere a fiddler played badly. The city performed its ordinary evening, and beneath it, in thousands of houses, the Hollow sat digesting, patient as rot, tidy as a good housekeeper.

Finally Rosalind said, "You did well tonight."

"I failed."

"You failed *usefully*. There's a difference, and you're too young to know it yet, so write it down." She glanced at me sideways. "We learned three things. One: it's not in the palace alone. Two: it doesn't just eat — it replaces, it gardens, it makes the loss look like it never happened. Three: we have five days instead of the months we were pretending we had." She exhaled. "That's more than we knew this morning. Most victories are just failures that taught you something before they killed you."

I wanted to believe her. I tried to. But what stayed with me, walking home, was not the strategy or the tally of lessons. It was Maren's voice, bright and forgiving: *you can't miss what you never had.*

Because I could. Miss it, I mean. I had a childhood stolen out from under me, sealed away in my own skull by a dying woman's desperate spell, and I had spent five years missing things I couldn't name — waking with tears on my face and no reason, flinching from corridors I'd never walked, loving a woman's face I couldn't find in any crowd. I was the opposite of Maren Corvane. She had been emptied painlessly. I remembered *around* my loss like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

And the Hollow had shown me, in the throne room dark, that it knew exactly what it held of mine. My mother's face. My mother's voice. It had offered them to me once already, and I had said no, and I had been proud of saying no.

Tonight I understood, with a cold clarity that felt like falling, that pride was a luxury with an expiration date. The Hollow was not in one palace. It was in every house, working slowly, and it had already finished with an eleven-year-old girl, and it was patient, and I was not. My memories were still mine — most of them, the sealed ones, the king's and my own — but *mine* was a word that required a future tense to mean anything.

Five days, Ilsa had said.

At the door of the shop, Rosalind paused with her key in the lock and looked back down the street toward the palace hill, where the crown of Vellamere caught the last light like a ring of thorns.

"We need Odric," she said. "Tonight. Get your coat."

I got my coat. I was learning that in this war, the weapons were words and bones and scholars in exile, and the battles were fought at kitchen tables, and the casualties were things no sword had ever touched.

I did not yet know that one of the men we were about to ask for help was already counting what his own name was worth.
