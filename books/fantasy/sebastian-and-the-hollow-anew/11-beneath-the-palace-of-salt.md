## 11. Beneath the Palace of Salt

The dead do not give directions well. This is something no one tells you, because no one talks to the dead, and those of us who do have learned to keep our mouths shut about it.

"Left," whispered my mother's voice at the mouth of the storm drain, and then, a moment later, "no, other left," because my mother had never once in her life or death admitted to being wrong on the first try.

I adjusted course. Sebastian followed without question, which was either trust or exhaustion, and by that hour I couldn't tell the difference in him anymore. Three nights we had walked the city's underside — the culverts beneath Tanner's Row, the drowned chapel where the water ran knee-deep and cold enough to make your teeth file complaints, the long brick throat of the old aqueduct that had once carried clean water to the palace and now carried mostly rats and regret.

The boy moved through it all like he'd been born down here. Barefoot still — he refused shoes with a stubbornness I'd come to respect — stepping over broken glass as if the glass had agreed in advance not to cut him. And maybe it had. There were days I suspected the whole world was quietly negotiating with Sebastian, and only I hadn't been invited to the table.

"You're limping," he said. Not accusing. Observing. He observed everything; it was his one flaw besides being a walking archive of a murdered kingdom.

"I'm *pacing myself*," I said. "There's a difference. Limping is what happens when you're careless. Pacing yourself is what happens when you're forty-one and your knees have opinions."

He almost smiled. Almost. The almosts were getting rarer the closer we came to the palace, and I didn't like what that told me. For three days the dead had been growing louder around him — not just whispering but *pressing*, crowding in like mourners at an open casket, all of them trying to look at whatever lay ahead before the lid closed. And Sebastian carried them all without complaint, which is its own kind of terrible, because a boy who has learned not to complain is a boy who learned it somewhere bad.

"Tell me again why we're doing this the stupid way," I said, mostly to hear my own voice over the drip of water. "There's a perfectly good front gate."

"There are forty Warden checkpoints between the gate and anything that matters," Sebastian said. "And Corvane can only blind so many eyes before someone asks her why."

Right. Corvane. Our warden, our blackmailer, our reluctant ally-in-progress, currently upstairs in the living world burning entries out of her ledger and pretending she'd never seen two faces she could describe in her sleep. Every favor she did us was another stone in the wall of her own ruin, and I knew it, and she knew I knew it, and we had agreed — silently, in the way of people who dislike each other productively — not to say it aloud.

"The drain it is," I said.

*Down,* said my mother. *And mind the third step. It's a liar.*

* * *

The undercrofts beneath the Palace of Vellamere were not on any map, because maps are drawn by the living for the living, and the living prefer to believe palaces end at their foundations. But the dead remember the older city — the one built on salt flats, the one whose architects sank their cellars into the brine itself, thinking that whatever slept below would stay asleep if you kept it wet.

They had been wrong, of course. Architects are usually wrong about the things they're most confident of.

We waded in up to our thighs. The water was warm here — body-warm, which is worse than cold, because cold water has an excuse. Salt crusted the walls in white terraces, thick as candle wax, and the air tasted of old iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going off. My charms — the real ones, the ones I don't sell — hung against my chest and hummed like struck tuning forks. I hadn't felt them hum since I was a girl.

"They're loud," Sebastian said quietly. "The dead here. Louder than anywhere."

"How many?"

He was silent a while. When he answered, his voice had gone strange and layered, the way it did when the old tongue rose in him uninvited. "All of them. Rosalind — everyone who's ever died in this city is *here*. They've been pulled. Like — like water finding a low place." His hands came up and pressed against his own temples. "They're not talking to me. They're talking past me. To *it*."

"To the Hollow."

"To the Hollow."

I took his wrist. His pulse was going like a rabbit's, and mine wasn't much better, but there's a particular lie in steadying someone else — your hand believes it even when the rest of you doesn't — and I needed the lie badly.

"We go slow," I said. "We look. We do not touch, we do not speak to it, and above all—" I caught his chin and made him look at me. His eyes had gone glassy, the pupils wide, and behind them something vast was leaning close to the window. "Above all, you stay *Sebastian*. Whatever it shows you. Whatever it offers. You come back to me. That's the whole mission. Everything else is decoration."

"Come back to you," he repeated. The glassiness receded, a tide going out. "Yes."

"And if I get eaten—"

"You won't."

"If I *do*, boy, you run. Up the aqueduct, left at the drowned chapel, straight to Odric's, and you tell him to burn the maps and forget my name. Promise me."

He looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the face of someone agreeing to a promise they fully intend to break. I decided to accept it. Hypocrisy is the mortar that holds most families together, and we were family now whether either of us had signed the paperwork or not.

*Third step,* said my mother, as the stairs rose out of the water ahead of us, slick and white-rimmed. *It's a liar.*

I tested it with my boot. It held. Then, as I put my weight on it properly, it crumbled into slurry and pitched me forward into the dark, and I heard my mother sigh with the deep satisfaction of the eternally vindicated.

* * *

The undercroft opened like the inside of a throat.

That was the image that arrived and would not leave: a throat, ribbed with salt, descending in wide pale curves toward a chamber where the water pooled black and utterly still. No drips down there. No current. Even the surface of the flood lay flat as poured glass, and the silence had texture — a pressure against the ears, like the moment after a bell stops ringing when the bell hasn't actually stopped, only gone somewhere you can't follow.

And in the center of the stillness, on a throne half-sunk in brine, sat the king.

I want to be fair to King Aldous. I want to say we found him monstrous from the first glance, some obvious horror, teeth and shadow, a thing you could hate cleanly. But that isn't what we saw. We saw a man. Tall, broad through the shoulders, gray at the temples, dressed in robes that had once been magnificent and were now stiff with salt — a man seated with the terrible patience of furniture. The crown sat on his head, gold gone green-black at the joints, and it fit him the way a lid fits a jar.

His eyes were open. That was the worst of it, right at the start — that his eyes were open, and empty, and aimed at nothing, and every few seconds they *moved*. A small twitch, left to right, like a man reading. Like a man reading something very long, very fast, forever.

"Aldous," Sebastian breathed beside me.

"Not yet," I whispered. "Look first. Judge later."

But the boy had already begun to shake, and I understood why a heartbeat later, because the dead around us — the thousands upon thousands pressed into that salt-throated dark — went quiet all at once. Not faded. *Silenced.* The way a room goes quiet when the important person enters. And in that silence I felt, for the first time in my life, what it was like to be one of them: small, gathered, and *regarded*.

The king's head turned.

It turned the way a door turns — smoothly, completely, further than a neck should allow — until those hollow eyes found us across the black water. And though I have spent my whole life listening to the dead, I had never once been *looked at* by something that eats listening. The sensation is difficult to describe. It was like being read aloud. Like every memory I owned was suddenly printed on my skin in letters large enough to see across a room.

*Oh,* said the thing wearing Aldous's face, and its voice did not travel through the air. It arrived directly, the way the dead speak, except inverted — the dead speak *up* from below, and this spoke *down* from above, patient and amused and terribly, terribly interested. *The bone-carver. And the little library that walked away.*

Beside me, Sebastian made a sound I'll hear until I die — a small, bitten-off noise, the sound of a boy trying not to answer.

"Don't," I said, barely moving my lips. "Don't talk to it. Don't listen. Look at me. Sebastian. Look at *me*."

He dragged his eyes from the throne. They were streaming. "It knows my name," he whispered. "It never learned my name. How does it—"

"It eats inheritance, love. Names are the smallest thing it owns of you."

The Hollow smiled with Aldous's mouth, and the smile was wrong in a way I cannot improve upon with description — it was simply wrong, the way a portrait's smile is wrong, painted by someone who had been told about smiles but never had cause to wear one. Salt cracked along the king's cheeks as the expression moved them. Cracked, and did not bleed. Five years underwater and the man did not bleed.

*You came all this way,* the Hollow said, settling back, and the black water around the throne shivered with something that was not quite a ripple. *Through the wet dark, following your little ghosts. How devoted. How rare. Do you know how rarely anyone comes down here for* him? *They come for me. They always come for me, eventually. Kings, sorceresses, wardens. They climb down into the dark carrying their grievances like lanterns, and they set the lanterns down, and they stay.*

"He's still in there," Sebastian said. His voice cracked on it. "Aldous. I can feel him. He's — he's *pushing*, he's been pushing for five years—"

*Has he?* The hollow eyes flickered — that reading motion again, faster. *Let me show you what your king has been doing for five years, little library. Let me show you what he does while you sleep.*

And the crown *moved*.

Not much. A fraction of an inch, a settling, the way a bird shifts its weight. But every dead thing in that chamber flinched at once — I felt it pass through the water, through the salt, through the floor and up my legs and into my humming charms, a single convulsion of ten thousand souls — and in the half-second of that flinch, I *heard* him.

Aldous. Deep down under the stillness, under five years of being read, a voice worn thin as tissue paper, saying the same thing it had perhaps been saying since the night of the Hollowing:

*Don't let it finish the sentence. Don't let it finish the sentence. Don't let it—*

Then the crown settled back, and the king's face went smooth and vacant as a locked door, and the Hollow regarded us with fresh appetite, and I understood with total clarity that we had made a catastrophic error.

We had come to look.

It had been waiting to be looked at.

*Stay,* said the Hollow, and Aldous's salt-stiffened hand lifted from the arm of the drowned throne, palm up, an invitation five years in the making. *You've walked so far, both of you. The water is warm. I have so much to give you. So much you've lost. So much you never got to keep.*

The hand beckoned.

And Sebastian — my Sebastian, barefoot, brave, stupid with grief — took one step toward the black water before I caught his arm, and across the flooded dark, the Hollow began, softly, in a dead tongue older than any kingdom, to speak *his* name.
