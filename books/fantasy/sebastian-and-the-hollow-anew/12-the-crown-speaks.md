## 12. The Crown Speaks

I want to say I heard the Hollow's voice and knew it instantly for what it was — a monster's voice, a thing to run from. That would be tidy. That would be the kind of story Rosalind would carve into a charm and sell to someone who needed to believe monsters announce themselves.

The truth is that it sounded like my mother.

Not exactly. Not the voice from before the palace, before the sorceress pressed her palms against my temples and poured a king into me like wine into a cup too small for it. It was the voice from *after* — the one I'd half-forgotten, the one that had sung to me in the dark of the salt tunnels when I was seven years old and running, the one that had said *keep going, little bird, keep going* even though by then my mother had been dead three days and what was singing was something wearing her memory like a coat.

The Hollow had eaten her. Of course it had. It had eaten everything I loved first, because I was the last thing the king loved, and the Hollow always starts with what the vessel holds dear.

*Little bird,* it said, from everywhere in the flooded dark. From the water. From the walls of ancient brick sweating brine. From inside my own skull, which was frankly rude. *You've grown.*

Rosalind couldn't hear it. I knew that the moment I saw her face — she was watching me across the black water with that expression she got when her charms went quiet, the look of a woman listening hard and hearing nothing, wondering if the silence was hers or the world's. Her lantern shook slightly. She'd deny it later.

"Sebastian." Her voice came low, carved flat the way she flattened things when fear was in the room. "What do you hear?"

*Tell her nothing,* said the voice, warm as bread. *Or tell her everything. You were always going to tell her everything. That's your tragedy, isn't it? You're a boy made entirely of other people's words.*

"Nothing," I said.

And then, because lying to Rosalind Marchetti was like lying to a knife — technically possible, deeply unwise — "Everything."

* * *

Let me explain what the undercrofts did to a person, because nobody had warned me, and I've decided I'm allowed to complain about it retroactively.

Beneath the Palace of Salt there are halls where the kings of Vellamere used to speak with their dead. Everyone knows the palace has cellars. What the Wardens' histories don't mention is that the cellars go down nine levels, and the ninth is older than the city's name, and on the ninth level the water stands waist-deep and perfectly still, and the stillness isn't water being calm. It's water *listening*.

We had come down following the dead — or rather, following Rosalind following the dead, since she could hear them and I could only hear them when they borrowed my mouth, which is a different and considerably less convenient arrangement. The dead had led us true. Past the collapsed stair on the fourth level. Past the alcove where some long-drowned chamberlain's bones had been arranged, deliberately, into a pointing hand. Down, and down, and down, until the air tasted like old coins and the lantern light bent strangely, as if it were reluctant to go further.

And at the center of the ninth level, on a throne half-sunk in black water, sat King Aldous.

He looked, at first glance, almost peaceful. An old man in a rotted crown, eyes open and empty as two coins left in a fountain. His chest rose and fell. Five years underwater-adjacent and he breathed; the Hollow kept him alive the way you keep a jar of preserves — sealed, useful, slowly consumed.

Rosalind had gone gray around the mouth. "That's him," she'd whispered. "That's really him. The dead keep saying his name like an apology."

And then the water had begun to speak.

* * *

*You don't have to stand in the cold, little bird,* the Hollow said now, as we waded closer, as Rosalind's lantern found the king's hollow eyes and I felt her courage do the thing courage does when it meets something that shouldn't exist — bend, hold, bend again. *Look how tired you are. Look what they've done to you. A child, sent down into the dark to fight a war older than his own teeth.*

"I'm not a child," I said, which was undermined somewhat by my voice cracking on *child*, and I felt the Hollow's amusement roll through the water like a slow fish.

*No,* it agreed gently. *You're a library. Twelve years old and shelved full of dead men. Do you know what I remember about being twelve?*

"You were never twelve."

*I was everyone.* The voice rippled. *That's the difference between us, Sebastian. You carry one king. I carry ten thousand years. Every grandmother's lullaby. Every farmer's trick for reading rain. Every secret whispered to a deathbed and every promise made at every wedding since your people learned to make promises. I am not a thief, little bird. I am an heir. The only honest one this city has ever had.*

"That's not—" Rosalind started, and the water rose up around her ankles, gentle, almost apologetic, and her voice stopped. Not silenced. *Convinced*, somehow, that speaking wasn't worth the effort. I saw her jaw set. I saw her reach for the carving knife at her belt and find, with visible disgust, that her hand didn't want to move toward the throne. The Hollow wasn't holding her. It was just making the alternative seem unreasonable.

It did that. I would learn that about it. The Hollow never pushed. It only rearranged the furniture of your wanting until the door it wanted you to walk through was the most comfortable place left in the room.

*Ask her,* it said to me, soft as snowfall. *Ask the bone-carver what she hears right now.*

"Rosalind—"

"Nothing," she said tightly. "It's quiet. It's been quiet since we came down here and I hate it." And I understood, then, why she'd brought me — not just because I spoke the old tongue, not just because the unbinding needed both of us. Because down here, in the Hollow's house, she was deaf, and I was the only ears she had.

Which meant the Hollow could say anything it liked to me, and she would never know if it was true.

Clever. I hated how clever it was.

*Here is what I offer,* it said, and the water around my knees went warm. Actually warm, like bathwater, like the kitchen of a house I'd lived in when I was four, before the palace, before any of it. *You have spent five years carrying a dead man's coronation in your head. You know the weight of his crown better than your own hair. You know the taste of his wedding feast and the name of his first horse and the sound his father made when he died, and you have never once, not once, been permitted to simply be a boy named Sebastian.*

The warmth climbed. My chest. My throat.

*I can take it back.*

"What?"

*The memories. All of them. I'll draw the king out of you the way you'd draw a splinter — cleanly, completely. Five years of a stranger's life, gone, and in its place:* the water shimmered, and images rose in it like reflections that belonged to no one standing there — *your mother's kitchen. The smell of the bread she burned every single morning because she talked while she baked. Your dog — you had a dog, you barely remember him, his name was Bristle and he slept against your back because you were always cold. The street you lived on. The game you played with the cobbler's daughter, the one with the chalk lines. Your seventh birthday, little bird. You were going to have a cake with honey on it.*

I hadn't thought of Bristle in four years. I hadn't known his name in three. I stood in freezing black water in a drowned hall beneath a ruined palace and I *wept*, suddenly and horribly, over a dog whose name I had lost, and the Hollow held me in its warm attention like a parent watching a child cry over a broken toy it could fix.

*I ate them,* it admitted, and there was something in its voice that was almost tenderness, and that was the worst part, that was the part that made my skin crawl even as I leaned toward it. *When I took the crown, I took the palace's inheritance, and you were carrying yours, and some of yours spilled into me. I have kept them safe. I keep everything safe. That is all I have ever done — the city calls it devouring, but tell me, what is memory, kept from no one, shared by no one, except a meal going to waste?*

"You killed my mother."

*Your mother died of fever in a Warden's cart on the road out of Vellamere,* it said, without cruelty, which was crueler. *I cannot kill. I can only keep. And I kept her — every word she ever said to you, every song, the exact weight of her hand on your head. I can give her back. Not a ghost. Not a whisper in a charm. Her. Whole. In your head, where she lived anyway, where she belongs.*

The water showed me her face.

Not the way I remembered it — memory is a bad painter, and mine had been painted over with a king's coronation, a sorceress's dying spell, five years of running. This was better than remembering. This was *her*, laughing at something I'd said when I was five, flour on her cheek, the gap in her teeth that she covered with her hand when strangers were near and let show when they weren't.

"Stop," I whispered.

*Come here,* said the Hollow, through the mouth of the crowned and hollow-eyed king, whose lips moved for the first time, whose dead coin-eyes turned to find me in the dark with terrible gentleness. *Give me what was never yours, and take back what always was. That is fair trade, little bird. That is the only fair trade anyone has ever offered you. The bone-carver wants to use you. The dead want to ride you. The Wardens want to burn you. I am the only one in this whole drowning city who is asking what* you *want.*

And God help me — I started walking.

* * *

Later — much later, in the telling of this — Rosalind would ask me how close it came. How many steps. And I would have to tell her the truth, which is that I don't know, because I wasn't counting steps, I was counting the things I'd get back. Bristle. The burned bread. The chalk game. Honey cake. My mother's laugh, in stereo, in surround, forever, a museum of one small boy's stolen life with the doors thrown open and the lights on and *come home, come home, you've been gone so long.*

I think it was three steps. Maybe four. The water was at my thighs and rising, warm as blood now, and the king's face wore my mother's smile stretched over a dead man's bones, and somewhere behind me Rosalind was shouting my name in a voice stripped raw—

—and the dead stopped it.

Not Rosalind. Not her carvings, not her charms, not her clever knife-work. The dead themselves — the thousands upon thousands of voices the Hollow had swallowed and stored and called *kept safe* — they surged up through the water and through me, and for one moment I was not one boy but a chorus, and the chorus said, in the old tongue, in my mouth, in a voice like a landslide:

**"She is not yours to give back. Nothing he carries is yours to give back. You are not an heir. You are a *thief wearing an heir's coat.*"**

The warmth died. The water went cold so fast my breath punched out of me. The king's face collapsed back into blankness, and the Hollow's voice, when it came again, had shed every ounce of tenderness like a snake shedding skin, and underneath it was something vast and hungry and *annoyed*, the way a wolf is annoyed by a thorn.

*Clever,* it said. *They taught you that word in the tunnels. They sewed a guard into the binding. The sorceress was more thorough than I credited.*

Then, quieter, and this was the sentence I would carry out of the undercrofts like a stone in my boot:

*It doesn't matter, little bird. Ask your friend the scholar what he's been doing while you've been down here playing hero. Ask him what a man will sell when you offer him the one thing he's lost. I don't need to bargain with you. I already bought the door.*

The water went still. The king breathed. Somewhere far above us, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, a bell began to ring in the Warden's tower — once, twice, the pattern that meant *raid*, that meant *names taken*, that meant someone had told someone where we were.

Rosalind caught my arm. Her hands were shaking and her face was wet and furious and afraid, all three at once, which I would learn was simply her face. "Sebastian. What did it say to you? All of it. Don't spare me. *All of it.*"

"It offered me my childhood back," I said. "And then it told us Odric sold us."

Her grip went slack.

Above us, the bell kept ringing, and the water at our waists began, very softly, to pull us deeper in — not the Hollow's doing, I realized. Just the tide. Just the ordinary, indifferent sea, coming in through the drowned doors of the ninth level, patient as anything, certain as anything, the way all the oldest powers in Vellamere were patient and certain:

because everything, eventually, comes down to the water.
