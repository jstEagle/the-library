## 13. Odric's Betrayal

Odric Vane kept his office above a pickle shop in the Tanners' Quarter, which told you everything about how far the cartographer of debt had fallen. The smell seeped up through the floorboards like a confession — vinegar and brine and something older that I chose not to name. He'd once held a chair at the Collegium Arcanum. Now he mapped magical debts on scraps of vellum for people who paid in eggs.

"You look terrible," he said when I came in with Sebastian trailing behind me like a shadow that had learned to walk.

"Thank you. I've been working on it."

He gestured us into chairs that had been designed by someone who hated sitting. His study was exactly as I remembered from five years ago: shelves bowing under ledgers, ink stains mapping his fingers like a second set of veins, and everywhere — *everywhere* — paper. Maps of the city layered over maps of the city, each one annotated in his cramped hand with debts owed and debts collected and debts that had simply vanished, the way a candle vanishes when someone pinches the flame.

I'd come to him because he was the only man alive who understood what the Hollow ate. Not flesh. Not souls, whatever the priests shouted in the squares. It ate *inheritance* — the thread of memory and gift that ran down through bloodlines like water through limestone. And Odric had spent those same five years charting where the water had stopped flowing.

"Show me," I said.

He spread three maps across the desk, weighted at the corners with pickle jars because of course they were. Each map showed Vellamere's districts, and each district was marked with small crosses.

"The Saltmarket," he said, tapping the first. "Two hundred households. Five years ago, maybe forty of them could trace their family back past the Hollowing — names, stories, the little inherited knacks that used to pass quiet as eye color. A baker whose bread never burned. A midwife who could feel a fever coming a day early." He tapped the crosses. "Now? Ask anyone in the Saltmarket about their grandmother's grandmother and you'll get a shrug. The names are gone. Not forgotten — *gone*. Like they were never written."

"The second map?"

"The Weavers' Row. Same pattern. Third is the Docks." He sat back, and for the first time since I'd known him, Odric Vane looked frightened. "It's moving inward, Rosalind. From the edges toward the palace. Whatever it's doing, it's doing it in order. Deliberate as a man eating an apple around its core."

Sebastian stood at the window, watching the street below. He'd been quieter than usual since Corvane's daughter — since we'd failed her. He turned now, and his eyes had that faraway sheen I'd learned to dread and rely on in equal measure.

"There's a woman in the Saltmarket," he said slowly, in his own voice but with the cadence of someone else underneath it. "Maren Ostrewe. She had a son. She cannot remember his face anymore. She keeps a drawing she made before it faded, and she looks at it every morning, and every morning it means less." He blinked, coming back to himself. "The dead are angry about this one. They keep repeating her name."

"Maren Ostrewe," Odric repeated, writing it down. Then he paused, quill hovering. "Rosalind. There's something else. Something I found last month that I haven't shown anyone."

I should have known then. I should have read the way his hand trembled, the way he wouldn't quite meet my eyes, the way he'd positioned his chair between me and the door. But grief makes a poor reader, and I was carrying enough of it to fill a library.

"Show me," I said again.

He pulled a ledger from beneath the floorboards — actually beneath them, pried up and re-laid, which should have told me something too. Inside were pages of names in two columns. One column in his hand. One column in a hand I didn't recognize: sharp, angular, written with a ruler's help.

"What am I looking at?"

"A negotiation," Odric said quietly. "Or the beginning of one."

***

Here is what I knew about Odric Vane, or thought I knew: he had been the Collegium's finest scholar of inheritance-magic until the Hollowing made his entire field a hanging offense. He had lost his position, his library, and eventually his name — literally. When the Wardens purged the Collegium rolls, his family line was struck from the civic registers. As far as Vellamere's records were concerned, no Vane had ever existed. No property. No history. No grave plots in the family section of the Cinderfield cemetery. He was a ghost arguing with other ghosts about paperwork.

I had assumed, all these years, that his mapping of the Hollow's feeding grounds was resistance. Scholarship as defiance. The one weapon they couldn't confiscate.

I had not considered that a man might want his name back so badly he would trade anything for it.

"The Hollow doesn't only eat inheritance," Odric said, watching my face as understanding arrived. "It can also... restore it. Selectively. It holds everything it takes, Rosalind. Every name, every memory, every bloodline's whole archive. And it has offered to give mine back. All of it. The Vanes restored to the registers, our graves returned, my mother's face put back into my sister's memory where it belongs." His voice cracked on *sister*. "My sister woke up last spring and didn't know she'd ever had a brother. Do you understand? She looked at me in the market like I was a stranger trying to sell her something."

"And the price?" I already knew. Gods help me, I already knew.

"The price," he said, "is the boy."

The room went very still. Sebastian hadn't moved from the window. Either he hadn't heard or he was giving Odric the mercy of pretending.

"He carries the king's memories," Odric went on, faster now, the words tumbling like a man running downhill ahead of a landslide. "The Hollow wants them back — wants *him* — and in exchange it will un-eat my family. Don't you see? It's not even cruelty. It's bookkeeping. One boy against a whole bloodline. You of all people should understand balances—"

"Don't." My voice came out flat and cold, a blade laid on a table. "Don't you dare dress this in arithmetic."

"Rosalind—"

"How long have you been talking to it?" I stood. The chair scraped back. "Through the ledger? Is that angular handwriting yours or *its*?"

"It writes through the pages sometimes." He said it the way a man admits to a disease. "At night. It has my mother's handwriting, Rosalind. Did you know that? It took her memories and it kept her *hand*, and when it writes to me, it signs itself with the letters she taught me to make." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "You think I'm betraying you. I'm negotiating with my dead."

"No," I said. "You're selling a living child to buy back your dead. Those are different transactions, however beautifully you file them."

And that was when I saw it — the flicker of his eyes toward the window, toward the street, toward the corner where a Warden's grey cloak had been waiting since before we arrived.

I grabbed Sebastian's wrist. "Run."

***

We made it as far as the stairs.

They came up through the pickle shop itself — four Wardens in ash-grey, boots on the boards, and behind them a fifth figure I recognized with a sick lurch: Warden Ilsa Corvane, ledger under her arm, face carved out of duty. Our bargain hung between us like a rope bridge in wind. Her daughter's fading memories against her blind eye. I watched her weigh it, watch her jaw tighten, watch her choose — or begin to choose —

"Hold them," she said, and I couldn't tell if her heart was in it. I suspected not. I suspected her heart was somewhere else entirely, in a house across the city, with a girl who was forgetting her mother's face one morning at a time.

A Warden's hand closed on my shoulder. Another caught Sebastian's arm, and the boy cried out — not in pain but in *language*, that old tongue rolling out of him like floodwater, and the Warden holding him staggered back as though the syllables had weight, which, in fairness, they did.

"Sebastian!" I twisted free, drove my elbow into soft flesh, and did the undignified thing survival requires: I threw myself down the stairs.

We burst through the pickle shop, scattering jars, brine flooding across the floorboards like a small vindictive sea. The shopkeeper shrieked. Sebastian vaulted the counter; I went through the doorway into the alley behind, where the afternoon light fell grey through drying hides strung overhead like the flayed banners of some defeated army.

"They'll cut off the quarter," I gasped. "Corvane will seal the gates. We need—"

"The salt tunnels." Sebastian's eyes were clear, urgent, entirely his own. "The dead know them. The old smugglers' ways under the Tanners' Quarter — they run north toward the river. There's a man among the dead, a briner named Coss, who walked them every night for thirty years. He's *showing* me."

Of course he was. The dead had always loved Sebastian best. Sometimes I suspected they found me merely adequate.

"This way." He pulled me left, then right, then through a doorway I'd have sworn was a wall, down steps slick with a century of drippings, into darkness that smelled of stone and salt and time.

Behind us, boots. Shouting. The particular bureaucratic fury of the Wardens of Ash, who hated nothing so much as a target that moved.

***

The salt tunnels under Vellamere are older than the city above them. Before the kings, before the Hollowing, before anyone thought to outlaw magic, someone had cut galleries through the salt seams to harvest them, and the harvesters had left their marks — initials, prayers, crude drawings of children who died too soon. The walls remembered all of it. In the dark, with Sebastian's hand in mine and his borrowed knowledge steering us, I could almost feel the walls *wanting* to speak.

For once, I was grateful they couldn't reach me clearly. My head was too full of Odric.

"He showed me his mother's handwriting," I said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice in the dark. "That's how it got him. That's how it gets all of them. It doesn't threaten. It *grieves* at you. It offers you back the pieces of yourself it stole, one at a time, until you'd trade the world to hold just one more."

Sebastian squeezed my hand. "Is that why you're going to stop it? Because it might offer you something?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty. What would the Hollow show me? My mother's voice, singing while she worked? My father's laugh? The faces of everyone I'd ever charmed a farewell for, returned whole?

"Probably," I admitted. "But here's the difference between Odric and me, little shadow. He thinks his family can be given back. I know mine can't. Nothing eaten comes back the same. Even if it poured my mother's voice into my ears tomorrow, it would be a copy wearing her shape. Grief isn't a debt, Sebastian. It doesn't get settled. It gets carried." I squeezed back. "I'm carrying mine. Odric wanted his delivered."

We walked on. Somewhere behind us the pursuit faltered — the Wardens knew these tunnels existed but not their geography, and geography, tonight, belonged to a dead briner named Coss and the boy who listened to him.

Then Sebastian stopped so suddenly I walked into his back.

Ahead, the tunnel forked, and in the fork stood a lantern. Beside the lantern stood Odric Vane.

He looked terrible. Better than me, but then, he always had standards to maintain. His hands were empty. His eyes were wet.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said. "I told them the tunnel flooded. Bought you an hour, maybe two." He swallowed. "I need you to know that I didn't — that when it came to the moment, I couldn't—"

"Couldn't sell a child after all," I said. "Congratulations on clearing the lowest possible bar. Shall we carve it a plaque?"

"Rosalind." His voice broke. "My sister forgot me. *Forgot me.* Do you know what that is? To be erased from the only person who—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "The Hollow promised. It wrote to me in her hand. And I sat down at that desk and I opened the shutters and I let them in, and then I heard the boy speak in the stairwell — that language — and something in it..." He lowered his hands. "Something in it sounded like the Collegium, before. Like all of us, before. Like the world we were supposed to protect instead of survive."

I should have forgiven him. Some part of me notes, even now, that forgiveness was probably available somewhere in the building.

"An hour," I said instead. "You bought us an hour. Spend it telling me everything the Hollow has written to you. Every word. Its phrasing, its promises, its handwriting. If it negotiates through you, Odric, then you're not a traitor or a friend. You're a channel. And channels can be read."

He stared at me. Then, slowly, he nodded, and began to talk, and I listened, and beside me Sebastian listened harder still, his lips moving faintly, translating the dead man's living testimony into the old tongue where it could be kept safe.

We were half a mile underground when Sebastian suddenly went rigid, his grip crushing my fingers.

"What? What is it?"

His eyes had gone white-rimmed, distant, ridden. When his voice came, it came doubled — his own and something vast underneath.

*"It knows you heard,"* he whispered. *"It says: tell the bone-carver the offer stands for her too. It says it has her mother's voice. It says—"*

His knees buckled. I caught him before the salt floor could, and as I lowered him down, shaking, the last of the message spilled out of him in a whisper that wasn't his at all:

*"—it says it will sing."*

Above us, faint through a hundred feet of stone, the Wardens' horns began to sound.
