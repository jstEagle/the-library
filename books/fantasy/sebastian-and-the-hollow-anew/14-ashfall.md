## 14. Ashfall

### Rosalind

The fires started at dawn, and by noon the whole of Vellamere smelled like a funeral that had gotten out of hand.

I stood in the mouth of my shop and watched the smoke rise from Coronation Square, a grey column so thick it looked like the city was trying to hang itself. They'd built the pyres in the old fountain basin — clever, that, all that stone to catch the embers — and Warden Corvane's people were going stall to stall with iron pails and longer faces, confiscating charms from anyone too poor or too frightened to argue.

Maren the fishwife cried when they took her husband's luck-knot. Old Tamos handed over his mother's protection bead without a word, which was somehow worse. A girl of maybe ten had to be pried loose from a doorway charm her grandmother had hung before she was born, and she bit a Warden hard enough to draw blood, and I confess I cheered inside where no one could hear it.

They piled everything in the fountain and lit it, and the flames went up green.

Bone does that, when it holds enough of the dead. Burns green, sings a little, then goes quiet forever.

I felt every one of them go.

People think hearing the dead is like hearing voices. It isn't. It's more like living next to a river you've stopped noticing — a constant low murmur under everything, generations of small unfinished sentences, a grandmother still reminding somebody to wear a scarf, a soldier still counting his squad, a child still asking where the dog went. You stop hearing individual words after a while. You just know the river is there, and you are never, ever alone in your own skull, even when you want to be.

That morning, as the pyres caught, the river began to thin.

It wasn't silence yet. It was like watching a tide go out — channels emptying one by one, each severed bloodline a little stream cut off from the sea. The Marchettis still murmured; my own dead were stubborn that way. But the Vails of the Tanner's Row went quiet mid-sentence, someone saying *and tell him the blue thread, not the*— and then nothing, forever, because their last living descendant had died in the fever winter and now the Wardens had burned the only thing keeping the shape of them in the world.

Sebastian stood beside me with his fists clenched, and I knew he heard it too, and worse — I knew some of those voices by name.

"They can't do that," he said. His voice did the thing it sometimes did, the old language underneath the common tongue, so that his words came out with a strange doubled weight, like two people speaking in unison. "Memories aren't *property*. They're not — they're not *theirs* to burn."

"Everything's property in Vellamere," I said. "That's the founding principle. The Hollow just made it official."

He looked at me. Five years old when he ran from the palace, and he'd learned irony somewhere between there and my doorstep, God help us both.

"What do we do?"

Now, here is what I should have said. I should have said *we stay small, we stay shut, we wait for Odric to find us another path.* That was the plan. Plans are how people like me survive — carve small, sell quiet, keep your head below the windowsill. I have spent thirty years being small on purpose. Small is safe. Small is alive.

But the river was going out, channel by channel, and somewhere in that smoke was the last echo of a woman named Vail who had wanted to tell somebody about a blue thread, and nobody would ever hear it now, and I found that something in me had finished, quietly and completely, with being small.

"We stop carving trinkets," I said.

### Sebastian

Rosalind's workshop smelled different that night. Usually it was dust and tallow and the sweetish rot-smell of old bone. Tonight it was sharp — oil and whetstone and something hot, like the air before lightning.

She had cleared the workbench of charms. All the little things that kept her fed — the mourning beads, the traveler's knots, the tiny carved hands people hung over doors against bad dreams — they were swept into a drawer and shut away, and across the bare wood she had laid out bones the size of my forearm.

Not relics. Not keepsakes. Long bones, dense ones, still carrying marrow-shadow, the kind that hold a scream well if you carve them right. I knew that much already. I was learning faster than either of us liked.

"You're making weapons," I said.

"I'm making *arguments*," she said, not looking up. "Weapons are arguments you win quickly." Her knife moved, and a groove opened along the bone, fine as a hair, and I heard the faintest hum come up out of it, the sound of a dead thing agreeing to be useful. "A charm protects one person. This—" she turned it in the lamplight, the fresh-cut sigil catching orange, "—this will drop a Warden's ward-shield like a wet sock. Which means it's an argument."

"Against the Wardens? Or against the Hollow?"

She set the knife down and finally looked at me, and her face did something complicated. Rosalind has a face that does most of its talking sideways — the jokes live around her mouth, the fear lives around her eyes, and usually she keeps both behind the counter like merchandise she doesn't display.

"Both," she said. "Corvane's people think they're burning magic. What they're actually doing is clearing the table for it. Every bloodline they sever, the Hollow eats cleaner. They're not fighting the monster, boy. They're doing its dishes." She picked the knife back up. "So we fight everyone."

I should have been afraid. I want to be honest about this — part of me was. But underneath the fear there was something else, something I'd been carrying since the palace, since the sorceress pressed her bleeding hands to my face and poured a king into a child and told me to run. For five years I had run from what was in my head. And tonight, watching Rosalind carve war into bone by lamplight, I understood for the first time that I wasn't the only one hiding something. She had spent thirty years pretending not to hear the dead. Thirty years of shrinking. And the pyres had burned that pretense up along with the charms, green flame and all.

The dead were quieter tonight. I felt it too, the way you feel a tooth missing. But the ones still standing were loud, and they were angry, and some of them were mine.

An old man's voice — not mine, older than mine, a court voice, dry and precise — surfaced through me without asking. *Arm the left edge,* it said through my mouth. *The wards run counterclockwise. We built them; we can unbuild them.*

Rosalind didn't flinch. That's why I trust her, I think. Everyone else in my life has either feared what comes out of me or wanted to use it. She just reached past my lips, tapped the air like she was hailing a coachman, and said, "Thank you, whoever you are. Left edge. Noted."

*Master carver Aldous-Vane, mason's guild,* the dead man said, with dignity. *At your service.*

"Charmed," said Rosalind, and went back to carving, and I laughed for the first time in days — a real laugh, surprised out of me — and outside the window the smoke kept rising and the city kept forgetting itself, and in that little room we sharpened our argument.

### Rosalind

Word travels fast in a frightened city, faster than any Warden's ledger. By the third night of the purges, they were coming to my door.

Not customers. Refugees, of a kind. People who'd had charms confiscated and wanted more — better ones, hidden ones, things that could be swallowed or sewn into hems. And some who wanted nothing for themselves. An apothecary named Denna whose entire family line ended with her sister, dead two years, whose memory-charm had gone into the fountain. She stood in my shop with ash still on her sleeves and said, "I can't hear her anymore. I never could, not really, but I could *almost*, and now I can't almost. Make me something. Make me anything."

And I looked at her — this exhausted woman with burnt sleeves and no family left in the world except the hole where they used to be — and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for three decades.

If I gave her what she asked for, truly gave it, I'd be marked by sundown. If I refused, I'd be complicit in the second death, the one the Hollow was feeding on. There was no small option left on the menu. Ashfall had burned the whole menu.

"Sit down," I told her. "This will take an hour, and it will hurt a little, and afterward you must never speak of it."

She sat. I carved. And when I pressed the finished bone into her palm and let the whisper through — just a fragment, her sister laughing at some joke I couldn't hear, a gift passed hand to hand the way it used to be done before the Hollowing made gifts illegal — Denna made a sound I will carry to my own grave, whenever that arrives. Half sob, half prayer. The sound of someone getting back one minute of a person.

They came all that week. I slept four hours a night. My wrists ached; my stock of relic-bone ran dry and I started buying from slaughterhouses and grave-diggers, and I'm not proud of the grave-digger part, but the dead I consulted about it said, unanimously and with surprising good humor, *better used than forgotten.*

And through all of it, the ledger grew. Not mine — theirs. Corvane's people were compiling names. I saw the list myself on the fourth day, because a terrified apprentice copied it for me in exchange for a charm to keep his own grandmother's voice. My name was on it. Sebastian's description was on it twice, in two different hands, which meant two informants, which meant Odric hadn't been the only leak.

*"Boy, approx. 10-12 years, mute or near-mute, speaks unknown tongue under duress. Seen entering premises of R. Marchetti, bone-carver, Lantern Lane."*

Under duress. As if the dead needed forcing. As if I forced anything.

I read that entry eleven times, and each time the same cold fact settled deeper: they weren't looking for a boy anymore. They were looking for the vessel. Someone above Corvane — someone in the palace, wearing a crown five years hollow — had given orders that specifically described what lived in Sebastian's head. Which meant the Hollow knew about him.

Which meant we were out of time for careful.

On the fifth night I laid out every weapon I'd carved on the long table — forty-one pieces of worked bone, each one humming its small furious note — and Sebastian counted them with the king's memories moving behind his eyes like weather.

"It's not enough," he said.

"No," I agreed. "It's not. Forty-one arguments against a kingdom."

"So we get more?"

"So," I said, "we get the one person in this city who knows exactly where the Hollow nests, exactly how it feeds, and exactly what it's afraid of." I wrapped the weapons in oilcloth, my hands steady in a way that felt borrowed from someone braver. "We're going to need a Warden."

Sebastian stared at me. "Corvane wants you arrested."

"Corvane's daughter is forgetting her," I said. "Did you think I forgot that? A woman who burns other people's mothers to save her own child is a woman who can be turned. Fear cuts both ways, boy. It always has." I tied off the bundle. "Besides — she watched that little girl bite her Warden today. I'd bet my other wrist she didn't enjoy it."

Outside, another pyre went up across the district, and the river in my head went out another inch — a merchant family this time, the Ostrand-Hales, three centuries of ledgers and love letters guttering into nothing — and I stood in my doorway and listened to them go, every last murmuring one of them, until the silence where they'd been rang louder than the fires.

Then I shut the door, and blew out the lamp, and in the dark I said aloud, to no one, to everyone still left in the water:

"Hold on. I'm coming. And I am done being quiet."

Somewhere far beneath the Palace of Salt, something ancient shifted in its crown, and heard me.
