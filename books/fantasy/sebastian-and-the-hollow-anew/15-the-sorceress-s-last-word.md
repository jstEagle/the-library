## 15. The Sorceress's Last Word

The dead do not sleep. I want to say that up front, because everyone assumes they do — assumes death is a long dark nap and the rest of us are just waiting for our turn at the pillow. It isn't. The dead *wait*. They wait the way water waits in a cracked jug, seeping out through every fissure in the world, pooling in low places. In Vellamere, after five years of the Hollowing, there were more low places than high ones.

I had learned to walk through them.

Rosalind called it "the shallows." She meant it kindly — she had a whole vocabulary of kindnesses she used like bandages on things too deep for bandages — but the truth was less gentle. When I closed my eyes and let the old tongue rise in my throat, I sank. Down past the floorboards of her shop, down through the clay and the cellar-stone and the older stone beneath that, into a country with no borders except grief. The dead stood there in their thousands, dim as candles behind frosted glass, and every one of them was looking for someone who could still hear.

Most nights I gave them what I could. A word here. A name spoken aloud so it wouldn't finish dying. Rosalind said this was how you kept from drowning: you didn't fight the water, you became a ferry.

But tonight I wasn't looking for just anyone.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Rosalind said.

I looked up from the workbench, where I'd been staring at the same uncarved sliver of bone for the better part of an hour. "What face?"

"The one where you're about to do something brave and stupid." She didn't look up from her own carving — a small round charm, something for a living hand, the kind of thing she made now instead of the mourning pieces she'd built her survival on. Her knife moved the way it always did, sure and quiet, like it was having a conversation with the bone and she was only translating. "I've seen it twice before. Once when you decided to go back for my ledger during the raid, and once when you ate that entire meat pie off Odric's plate while he was mid-sentence."

"That pie was owed to me. He'd sold my location to the Wardens."

"He'd sold your location to the Wardens *and* eaten well. The man has no shame." She set down her knife. "So. Tell me."

I turned the bone sliver over in my fingers. Cow knuckle, sanded smooth, waiting for a purpose. Everything in this shop waited for a purpose. That was the whole trade.

"The sorceress," I said. "Maren Vael. The one who sealed me. I've been walking the shallows every night since the salt tunnels, and I can't find her."

Rosalind went still in the particular way she did when the news was bad and she was deciding how much of it to hand me at once. I'd catalogued all her stillnesses by then. This one was the *there's-a-reason-and-you-won't-like-it* stillness.

"Say it," I said.

"Five years is a long time." She picked her knife back up, which was what she did instead of wringing her hands. "And the Hollow eats inheritance. Maren Vael had no children, no students who survived the purge, no bloodline to carry her forward. If anyone's memory would be thin down there—"

"She'd be thin," I finished. "Not gone?"

"I don't know." And here was the thing about Rosalind Marchetti: she never lied to me. Not even when lying would have been a mercy, and she was a woman who understood mercies the way a surgeon understands knives. "The dead don't vanish when they get thin. They just get quieter. Harder to hear. You might have walked past her a hundred times without knowing."

"Then I'll walk slower."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the curfew bells rang the hour — ninth bell, the city holding its breath the way it had held it for five years, ever since the night the king went mad and magic died and the Wardens of Ash started keeping their ledger of names.

"Take the lamp with the green glass," she said finally. "And take this." She pressed something into my palm: a small carved bead, no bigger than a pea, warm from her hand. "If you sink too far, hold it and think of my voice telling you to eat something. That's an anchor if I ever made one."

"Wry to the last."

"It's the only register I have left." But her thumb brushed my knuckles once, quick, before she let go, and we both pretended that hadn't happened, because that was also the trade between us.

* * *

The shallows were worse than usual.

I want to be honest about that, because later — when people told this story, and they did tell it, they told it wrong — later everyone wanted the descent to sound noble. It wasn't noble. It was cold. It was the feeling of your own name getting slippery in your mouth, of remembering your mother's face and then, horribly, *not*, and then catching it again at the last second like a coin rolling toward a drain. Every time I went under, I gambled a little more of myself against the current, and every time I came back up I checked my pockets like a man leaving a pickpocket's alley.

Tonight I went deeper than I'd ever gone.

Past the recent dead — the purged, the burned, the ones whose names the Hollow had chewed until only the shapes of the names remained. Past the old dead, the plague-years and the flood-years, standing in their generations like rings in a tree. Down where the language lived, the true tongue, the one that predated kingdoms, the one that had come out of my mouth at Rosalind's door on the first morning like water finding its old channel.

Down there, the dead weren't people anymore. They were *weather*. Movements in the dark. Pressures. And somewhere in all of it, thin as a hairline crack in glaze, was a voice I'd been hunting for six weeks.

*Maren Vael.*

I found her by accident, the way you find anything down there. I'd stopped to speak a name for a dead dyer's apprentice — a boy, maybe fourteen, who'd been clutching the memory of his master's recipe like a talisman, and I spoke the formula aloud in the old tongue so it would stick somewhere, anywhere, in me, in the world — and when I straightened up, she was simply *there*.

Not the way the others were there. The others loomed or drifted or pressed. Maren Vael stood.

She was smaller than I remembered, though I should say: I remembered almost nothing of her. I remembered hands. Old hands, quick hands, pressing themselves flat against my chest the night the palace fell, and heat going through me like a brand, and her mouth moving fast against my ear — *hold this, hold this, don't let go, whatever comes, don't let go* — and then the door breaking and the crown-thing wearing the king coming through it and her turning to face it so I could run.

Everything else I knew about her, I knew secondhand, from the memories Aldous had left sealed inside me like letters in a wall.

Now she stood in the dark of the shallows, and she was translucent the way frost is translucent, and she was looking at me with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it on Rosalind's face a hundred times: the look of a teacher watching a student do something dangerous and mostly managing it.

"You got taller," she said.

Her voice was thin. Thin the way Rosalind had warned. Like a song played on a string missing two-thirds of its length.

"You remember me?" My own voice cracked down there, embarrassingly, echoing in a place where echoes shouldn't exist.

"I remember everything I chose to keep." She tilted her head. "That's not nothing, boy. That's the whole lesson. Are you ready to learn it, or do you need another month of flailing around down here waking up every ghost with a grudge? Half the palace guard thinks you're a poltergeist."

"They're welcome to think that."

"Mm. You have Aldous's stubbornness. And his terrible habit of answering rhetorical questions." Something moved across her face that might have been a smile, worn soft by five years of erosion. Then it faded, and what was underneath was grave. "You came for the binding. The seal I put on you. You want to know how to undo it — or rather, how to use it."

"How did you—"

"Because it's the only reason anyone comes this deep, and because you have the look of a boy carrying a plan he hasn't told his carver about yet." She stepped closer, and the dark bent around her like water around a stone. "Sit. Well — kneel, or float, or whatever you do down here. This will take as long as it takes, and I am running out of self to say it with."

* * *

Here is what she told me, and I'm going to tell it plainly, because plainness is the only honest container for it.

The Hollow cannot be killed. It isn't alive; killing is a category error, like trying to drown a fire. It can only be *housed*. Contained. Given a shape small enough and strong enough that it cannot spread, and locked there.

The shape is bone.

"Not any bone," Maren said. "Bone remembers. That's why your carver's charms work at all, why the whole trade of whisper-charms exists — bone holds what flesh lets go of. A prison-bone must be carved in the old tongue, in real time, while the Hollow pours in. Carve it before, and the Hollow reads the carving like a menu. Carve it after, and it's already loose. It must be simultaneous. Speech and knife together, one mind each, working as one."

"One mind each," I repeated. "Me speaking. Rosalind carving."

"Yes. And there's the flaw, boy, so listen, because I'm only going to be coherent for this part once." Her frost-thin hands closed around mine, and they had no temperature at all, which was somehow worse than cold. "The bone must be *willingly given*. Not taken, not bought, not tricked. The Hollow feeds on inheritance — on what passes freely down a line of blood and love. Bind it with stolen bone and it eats through the theft in a day. Bind it with given bone and the giving itself becomes the lock. Willingness is the ward."

I felt it coming before she said it. The way you feel a stair miss under your foot.

"And the giver loses—" I started.

"What the bone holds." Her voice didn't waver. Five years of being eaten, and it didn't waver. "Every bone carries its owner. Their memories, their gifts, their particular music. Give the bone, lose the contents. All of them. Permanently. The Hollow doesn't just move in — it consumes the house on arrival. That's the price of the lock. That's why I couldn't do it myself, that night. I tried. I reached for my own wrist-bone and I realized what I'd lose, and I *hesitated*, and hesitation is unwillingness, and unwillingness is a broken lock." Her laugh was dry as ash. "So I put the king in you instead, and bought you both time with my life, and hoped someone braver than me would come along."

The shallows were very quiet. Even the weather of the dead seemed to have stopped to listen.

"If Rosalind gives the bone," I said slowly, "she loses—"

"Her gift. Her hearing. Every voice she's ever carried in those ears of hers, every dead soul she's spoken with, every charm-whisper she's ever coaxed into bone. The whole country she's lived in since she was a child. Gone. Silence, forever, total." Maren's eyes — frost-pale, Aldous-pale, I saw now where the king's coloring had come from, some old shared line — fixed on mine. "And she'll do it anyway. That's the kind of person she is. I watched her type for thirty years from the other side of the veil, boy. Stubborn, self-erasing, generous to the point of pathology. She'll give the bone and smile about it and never once tell you what it cost."

"Then I won't let her."

"Oh?" One eyebrow rose, and it was such a courtly, ancient gesture on such a faded face that I almost laughed. "How, exactly? Hold her down? Carve it yourself? Can you carve, Sebastian? Has she taught you the knife-work, the grain-sense, the hundred small judgments that turn a bone into a sentence?" She shook her head. "It takes two. Speaker and carver. There is no version of this where you do it alone, and there is no version where the giver keeps what the bone holds. That is the law of it. I wrote the binding, and I'm telling you its price, and I am asking you — as the woman whose life bought yours — to hear it clearly."

I heard it. That was the worst part. I heard it perfectly.

* * *

Coming up was harder than going down. It usually was. But this time I carried something heavy along with me, and the heaviness made the ascent feel like climbing with wet wool tied to my shoulders.

I surfaced in the shop near dawn, gasping, the green-glass lamp guttered, the anchor-bead clenched so hard in my fist it had left a spiral bruise in my palm. Rosalind was already awake — she was always already awake; I suspected the woman considered sleep a personal failing — sitting at the bench with two cups of chicory steaming and her eyes on the door like she'd been counting my heartbeats.

"You found her," she said.

"Yeah."

"And?"

I sat down across from her. Took the cup. Held it for the warmth, because my hands were still shallow-cold. And I looked at this woman who had taken in a mute, barefoot, haunted boy because he touched her carvings and the dead went quiet, who had fed him and taught him and fought Wardens for him and never once asked what loving him might cost her — and I understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what I was going to have to do.

"We found the flaw in the binding," I said. "There's a way to imprison the Hollow. For good. In bone."

Her face lit — actually lit, the first unguarded hope I'd seen on it since Ashfall, since the square full of burning charms, since the dead began going quiet in droves. "Then we—"

"But the bone has to be willingly given. And the giver loses everything the bone holds." I kept my voice level. It took everything. "Rosalind. If you carve the prison, you lose the hearing. All of it. Forever. Every voice. Your mother. The dead you've talked to your whole life. The whole—"

"Country," she said softly. "Yes. I know."

"You *know*?"

"Boy." She set down her cup and looked at me with the patience of someone addressing a very bright child who has just discovered fire is hot. "I've been carving bones for twenty years. You think I never wondered what the giving costs? I make my living putting pieces of myself into objects. Of course I know the rule. I just never had a reason worth paying it." She smiled, and it was the wry one, the armored one, the one that made me want to shake her. "Ask me again tomorrow. Ask me properly. But tonight, drink your chicory, because you look like something the tide rejected, and tomorrow we have a Warden problem."

"A what?"

She slid a folded paper across the bench. A broadsheet, freshly printed, ink still smelling of the press. On it, above a woodcut of a stern-faced woman I knew far too well, ran a headline:

**WARDEN CORVANE TO ADDRESS THE ASHMARK DISTRICT AT NOON — LEDGER OF THE MARKED TO BE READ ALOUD**

And beneath it, in smaller print, a list of descriptions. One of which was mine. Barefoot boy. Speaks the dead tongue. Last seen in the company of the bone-carver.

"She burned her ledger," Rosalind said quietly. "Or so my sources say. And now she's calling a public reading of names she no longer intends to enforce — which means either she's turned, or she's baiting a trap with herself as the worm." Her fingers drummed once on the table. "Either way, Sebastian — noon is coming, and we're going to need to decide whose side the law is on before it decides for us."

Outside, the first grey light crept up the street, and somewhere beneath the city, in the flooded dark under the palace, something wearing a king's crown turned its hollow eyes toward the surface — and waited.
