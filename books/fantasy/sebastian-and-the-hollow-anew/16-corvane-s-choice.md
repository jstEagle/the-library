## 16. Corvane's Choice

I had prepared three speeches for the night Warden Ilsa Corvane came to my door a second time, and I delivered none of them, because she arrived already weeping.

Not sobbing. Warden Corvane did not sob. She stood on my threshold in the grey hour before dawn with her helmet under her arm like a hat she'd forgotten how to wear, and tears ran down her face in two neat, disciplined lines, as though even her grief filed reports.

"Marchetti," she said. "I need you to lie to me."

I looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, the street was empty except for the ash that never quite stopped falling now, sifting down through the lamp-light like the city itself was molting. Somewhere two streets over, a Warden patrol's boots kept their rhythm. She had come alone, or mostly alone, which from Ilsa Corvane was the same thing as naked.

"Come in before someone sees you being human," I said. "It'll ruin your reputation."

She came in. Sebastian was asleep in the back room — actually asleep, which after the girl in the warden's barracks had become rare enough that I'd stopped waking him for anything short of fire. He had drawn out what remained of Maren Corvane's memories three nights ago and found nothing there to hold. The Hollow had eaten them clean, down to the marrow of the girl's childhood, and Sebastian had come home gray-faced and silent and hadn't spoken above a whisper since.

Corvane saw the state of my workbench — the carvings half-finished, the bone dust, the little arsenal of rebellion I no longer bothered hiding under cloth — and something in her jaw set.

"You knew," she said. "When I came here. You knew what was happening to her."

"I suspected. You confirmed it." I poured her a cup of the barley tea that passed for hospitality in my house. "Sit down, Warden."

"Don't tell me to sit—"

"Your knees are shaking. Sit down before they make the decision for you."

She sat. That was the thing about Ilsa Corvane that I would come to understand properly only later: she was not a cruel woman doing a cruel job. She was an honest woman who had built her whole life on the belief that the law was a load-bearing wall, and she had just discovered the wall was made of ash.

"Maren remembered my face this morning," she said, staring into the cup. "For about four seconds. She looked at me and said, *You're the lady who visits.*" Her voice didn't crack. It went flat instead, which is worse. "Five years I've been visiting my own daughter, Marchetti. Five years since the Hollowing, and every year there's less of her, and everyone told me it was grief-madness, wasting sickness, bad blood — and now I know it has a name and an appetite and it lives under my feet in the palace I've sworn to protect."

"Yes," I said.

"I could arrest you right now." She said it like she was testing whether the words still worked. "The boy is sleeping in your back room. His description has been in the ledger since midwinter. I wrote the entry myself."

"I know. Your handwriting slants left when you're angry. It slants very far left on his page."

Something almost like a laugh moved through her and died. "You're impossible."

"I'm a bone-carver. We're all impossible; it's in the guild charter."

* * *

Here is what I learned in the next hour, while the tea went cold between us and the ash kept its patient fall outside: Ilsa Corvane had spent five years being the best instrument the Law of Ash possessed, precisely because she believed in it. She had not burned charms out of malice. She had burned them out of conviction — that magic had broken the king, that memory was safer in ledgers than in blood, that a city that forgot on purpose was a city that couldn't be hollowed.

And then she had watched her daughter forget her.

"There's a word for what I am," she said. "There's a word the people in the market use when I walk past. I've earned it. I don't ask you to take it back."

"No," I agreed. "You have."

Her chin came up. Good. There was the spine. "But I'm asking you this: is there anything left? In her. Anything at all that can be saved?"

I thought of Sebastian's face when he'd come back from the barracks. I thought of how he'd said, in that terrible gentle way of his, *It's like reaching into a room where the fire went out years ago. The shape of things is still there. The warmth isn't.*

"I don't know," I said. "And I won't lie to you, because you asked me not to. What the Hollow digests, stays digested. But—" and here I made the choice that shaped everything after, though it didn't feel like much at the time, just a woman deciding to spend her last coin — "the Hollow isn't finished eating. Nothing is finished until it's finished. If we cut off its feeding, if we reach the nest beneath the palace and bind it, then whatever remains in Maren stops eroding. And the boy — Sebastian — he carries more memory than any living person. He can't give her back what's gone. But he might be able to give her neighbors. Context. The shape of a life to grow into."

"That's not saving her."

"It's the only version of saving her that exists. Take it or leave it."

She took it. I watched her take it — watched the moment land in her like a stone dropped down a well, all the way to the bottom — and then she did the thing I will remember about Ilsa Corvane until whatever silence waits for me takes me too.

She stood up. She walked to my hearth. And she pulled the ledger out of her coat.

It was smaller than I expected. Ledgers usually are, the ones that hold whole lives. Bound in grey leather, corners worn soft, the Warden's seal stamped in wax gone amber with age. Every magically marked name in the market district. Every charm-seller, every hearth-whisperer, every grandmother who hummed the old lullabies a half-tone wrong. Sebastian's page. My page. Pages and pages of people who had done nothing worse than remember.

"What are you doing?" I asked, though I knew.

"My oath says this book protects Vellamere." She held it over the embers. "My daughter taught me otherwise this morning, in four seconds, by not knowing who I was." She looked at me. "If I burn it, there's no walking back. Wardens don't get forgiven twice. And if the Hollow wins — if you fail down there — then everything I've done tonight was for nothing, and my name dies with my daughter's memory, and honestly, Marchetti, at this point that trade doesn't frighten me nearly as much as it should."

"Then don't do it for strategy," I said. "Do it because it's yours to burn."

She struck the flint herself. Wouldn't let me help. The pages caught slowly at first — ledgers are reluctant things, full of other people's weight — and then all at once, the way guilt finally does when it goes. She fed it into the hearth page by page, and I noticed she read each name as it burned. Not skimmed. Read. A kind of funeral rite, one name at a time, giving each marked soul back to the unmarked dark.

When she reached Sebastian's page she paused.

"He's just a boy," she said.

"He's a boy carrying a king," I said. "Which is worse, some days. Ask him."

She burned the page. Then she stood there with her empty hands open at her sides, looking at them as if they belonged to a stranger, and said, quietly, "Now what am I?"

"Ours," said a voice from the doorway.

Sebastian stood there in his blanket, barefoot, hair a ruin, watching the last of the ledger curl into nothing. He must have heard everything — the boy heard everything, always, the dead talked in their sleep through him — but what struck me was his expression. Not fear. Not even forgiveness. Recognition, maybe. One ruined person identifying another across a room.

"The palace," Corvane said to him, hoarse. "You're going into the palace. Under it."

"Beneath the Palace of Salt," he said. "That's what the dead call it. They remember when the foundations were laid, and the sea got in, and never left."

"I can get you in." She said it fast, before courage could reconsider. "Above, I mean. There's a service stair from the salt-cellars up to the east range — sealed since the Hollowing, but I hold two of the three keys, and the third key belongs to a man who owes me his career and hates me slightly, which is the most reliable kind of debt. Once you're below, once you're past the wards — I can't follow. My oath may be ash but my face isn't. If I'm seen near the undercrofts, every Warden in the palace comes running, and you'll die in a corridor instead of finishing whatever it is you mean to finish."

"You're giving us the door," I said slowly, "to the place where your oath lives."

"I'm giving you the door to the place where my daughter's thief sleeps." Her eyes were dry again now, burned clean. "That's not the same building anymore. I understood that tonight."

* * *

We worked until dawn, the three of us, and it was the strangest night of my strange life: a Warden of Ash sitting at the bench of an illegal bone-carver, holding bones steady while I carved, learning the names of tools, asking questions in the flat competent voice of a soldier inventorying weapons. What does this one do. How long does the binding hold. What happens to the giver.

I answered the last question honestly, because she'd asked me not to lie, and I watched her decide not to press it. She knew. Wardens know cost when it's described to them; it's the one poetry they speak fluently.

Near the end, she paused over a small carving I'd set aside — a wrist-bone blank, unmarked, waiting. She turned it in her fingers.

"This is the prison?"

"That's the prison."

"And it has to be willingly given."

"So the sorceress's last word says. Bone taken by force holds nothing. The Hollow would slide out of it like water out of a fist." I held out my hand for it. She gave it back. "It's handled."

"Whose?" she asked, and I let the silence answer, and she looked at my left wrist, at the old scar I'd never explained to anyone, and for the first time all night she flinched. "Marchetti—"

"Don't," I said. "Please. I've carried worse than this. I carried it quietly for thirty years. Let me put it down somewhere useful."

She didn't argue. She was learning, I think, that some gifts are insults to refuse.

Before she left, as the sky went the color of dirty pewter and the patrols began their morning rounds, she stopped at the door with her helmet back on and her face rebuilt into the Warden's mask, and she said:

"Two nights from now. The feast of Saint Verene — the palace kitchens will be chaos, the guard rotations doubled and sloppy, everyone drunk on ceremony. East stair, third landing, I leave the door unbarred at the changing of the midnight watch. After that, I never saw you. After that, if they ask me, I burn your shop myself and weep convincingly."

"Your oath," I said. "Doesn't any part of it object?"

She considered. "Every part of it objects. That's how I know it's the right thing. Oaths only go quiet when you feed them lies." She settled the helmet. "Keep the boy alive, Marchetti. Keep yourself alive too — you're no good to my daughter dead, and neither am I."

And then Warden Ilsa Corvane walked out into the ashfall and became, to all the world, the law again — upright, implacable, incorruptible — while inside her coat, where the ledger used to sit, there was nothing at all, and the nothing weighed more than the book ever had.

Sebastian came to stand beside me in the doorway. The dead were murmuring around him; I could feel it even now, that faint pressure like weather coming. He watched her go.

"She read every name," he said softly. "Even the ones she arrested. She's going to carry those names now. That's what burning a ledger costs — you inherit it."

"Then she's one of us," I said. "God help her."

He smiled, faint and tired and old. "Two nights."

"Two nights," I agreed. And beneath our feet, beneath the streets and the cellars and the flooded dark where a crowned thing wore a hollowed king like a coat, I felt — or imagined I felt, the dead being quiet on the matter — something shift in its sleep, and turn toward us, and begin, patiently, to wait.
