## 18. What the Hollow Offers

I want to tell you that I was brave. That when the crown began to speak — really speak, not the whispering it had done through the king's ruined mouth — I stood my ground like a hero in one of Rosalind's carved stories.

I was not brave. I was drowning, and the thing wearing the throne room offered me air.

* * *

It started with a smell. Bread. Not the hard black rye that Rosalind keeps under her workbench and pretends is food, but soft white bread, warm from an oven, the kind I hadn't smelled since before the palace. Before the running. My mouth flooded and my eyes burned and I thought: *this is a trick.*

Then the memory arrived whole, the way they always do, except this time it wasn't mine.

A kitchen. A woman with flour on her forearms singing a song about a fisherman's wife. A boy — not me, some other boy, some boy dead two hundred years — burning his fingers on the crust and laughing. The song. The flour. The burn. All of it poured into me like water into a cup, and I knew the woman's name was Maretta, and I knew she died in winter with her hands still smelling of yeast, and I knew this because I had eaten her.

Not me. It. The Hollow. But the knowing lived in my head now, and there was no unliving it.

"You see," said the Hollow, from the throne, from the king's body, from everywhere at once, "how much there is."

The throne room beneath the palace of salt was lit by nothing — the water in the flooded galleries around us glowed faintly green, the way drowned things do, and the light moved across King Aldous's face so that he seemed to be underwater even standing on dry stone. His eyes were the worst part. Not cruel. Empty. Two doorways with no house behind them.

"Where did you get that?" I managed. My voice came out small. "That memory. The kitchen."

"From Maretta's line," the Hollow said. "Her great-great-granddaughter carried it until last spring. Then she didn't." It tilted the king's head, a gesture almost gentle. "You are carrying quite a lot yourself, little archive. Shall we compare?"

"I'm not comparing anything with you."

"No," it agreed. "You're going to take everything."

And it opened.

* * *

I have tried, since, to describe what happened next, and every attempt fails, because language belongs to one life at a time and what entered me belonged to thousands.

Rosalind says the dead speak to her in voices, one at a time, patient as rain. This was not that. This was every voice at once, and not only voices — hands, faces, fevers, weddings, the specific weight of a newborn against a chest, the specific cold of a grave dug in frozen ground. Generations. Whole rivers of people, all pouring toward the same low place, which was me.

A farmer named Halden who loved his ox more than his brother. A girl who invented, alone, in a tower, a way of writing music for the blind, and died before anyone believed her. A soldier who deserted at Vellamere's founding and lived forty years disguised as a woman named Bess and never regretted it once. A mother teaching her son to whistle. A poisoner. A saint. A child who spent his entire short life watching ants and died happy.

They weren't offered to me one by one. They were *in* me, already, settling like sediment, and each one dragged its own griefs and joys behind it, and each grief and joy was mine now, and I could feel myself — the shape of myself, the Sebastian-shaped space where I lived — being pressed thinner and thinner between them.

"Stop," I said.

"Why?" The Hollow sounded genuinely curious. "Does it hurt? Or does it feel like coming home?"

And that was the terrible thing. It felt like coming home. Every memory fit. That's what inheritance is — it fits, because you're made of the same stuff as the people who came before. The Hollow wasn't forcing anything on me. It was showing me what I already was, just... more. So much more. I was a cup being asked to hold the sea, and the sea was saying, *you were always meant to be open.*

Somewhere very far away, Rosalind was shouting my name. I heard it the way you hear rain on a roof when you're falling asleep — present, unimportant, belonging to another world.

* * *

Let me tell you about my mother.

I have been running from the palace since I was six years old. I have carried King Aldous's memories sealed in my skull since the night the sorceress pressed her dying hands to my face and made me a vault instead of a boy. In five years, do you know how many memories of my own mother I have? Three. Her hands turning me toward the light. Her voice saying my name through a door. The smell of rosemary, which might have been her or might have been a garden I invented later out of loneliness.

Three memories. I built my whole self on three stones.

The Hollow knew this. Of course it knew. It had been sitting in the palace eating inheritance for five years; it had eaten the royal line down to the marrow, and my mother was of the royal line, and so somewhere inside that vast devouring dark was everything I had lost.

It gave her to me.

Not a trick. Not a false image. It gave me a true memory, whole and perfect: my mother kneeling in a palace corridor, holding my face in both hands, telling me — telling six-year-old me, the night everything ended — *"Whatever happens, whatever they put in you, you are Sebastian first. Say it. Say it back."*

And small-me had said it back. *Sebastian first.* And she had smiled like the sun coming up over water, and then she had pushed me toward the servant stairs and gone back toward the sound of screaming, and I had never seen her again.

I was on my knees. I don't remember getting there. The flood of lives roared around me and I knelt in the middle of it clutching one true memory of my mother like a man clutching driftwood, and the Hollow leaned close over me in the king's borrowed body and said, with something that was almost kindness:

"I can give you all of her. Not three stones. The whole river. Every day of her childhood, every thought, every time she spoke your name. And your grandmother, and hers, and the first king who ever raised a wall on this hill. Everything your blood ever was. You would never be lonely again. You would never *lose* anyone again."

"And the price?"

"There is no price," it said. "That's the beautiful part. There's only subtraction. You give up the small thing — the single, narrow, frightened boy — and receive the vast thing. Everyone who ever lived in your line, alive again inside you. Is that not mercy? Is that not what the dead deserve? To be remembered perfectly, forever, by someone?"

And God help me, it sounded right. That's what I need you to understand, whoever finds these words someday. It didn't sound like temptation. It sounded like arithmetic. One boy against ten thousand souls. What kind of selfish creature chooses the one?

I felt my edges going. I felt the Sebastian-space thinning to paper. Somewhere in the flood, Halden's ox lowed, and the blind-music girl wrote a note, and Bess laughed at her own joke, and my mother said *say it back,* and I opened my mouth to say — what? My name? Whose name? Was there still a mouth that was only mine?

* * *

What saved me was bread.

Maretta's bread. The burned fingers. Because in the middle of the roaring flood, that small dead boy's small burnt laugh surfaced, and it wasn't grand, it wasn't holy, it wasn't a river of ancestors — it was a kid yelping over a hot loaf, and something in me recognized it. Not as inheritance. As *otherness.* That boy was not me. He was wonderful and he was not me, and if I took everything, I would lose the ability to know the difference. I would be everyone, which is the same as being no one.

The Hollow doesn't want your memories because it loves them. I understood that then, kneeling in the green water-light. It wants them because it has none of its own. It is the hungriest thing in the world precisely because it was born empty, and it is offering me fullness the way a drowning man grabs at a swimmer — not generosity. Hunger wearing mercy's face.

"You ate them," I whispered. "All those people. You didn't keep them safe. You *ate* them."

"They are not diminished," the Hollow said. "They are *held.*"

"Held by what? You don't hold anything. You're a hole. You're the shape of taking." I got one foot under me. The flood pushed at me, sweet and enormous, my mother's voice everywhere now, *Sebastian first, say it back, say it back* — and I realized, with a clarity that felt like breaking, that it was using her. Using her love. Turning the best thing I had ever owned into a hook.

So I said it back.

"*Sebastian first.*"

And I reached — not for the flood, not for the crown, but for the small hot coal of myself, the three-stone boy, the one Rosalind had taught, the one the dead coached, the one who had failed Corvane's daughter and carried that failure like a stone on purpose because it was *his* stone — and I held on.

The Hollow went very still.

"Ah," it said softly. "There you are. How disappointing. How *rare.*" The king's empty eyes fixed on me, and for the first time I saw something move behind them — not a soul, but the absence of one, moving. "Do you know what happens now, little archive? Now I stop offering."

The green light in the flooded galleries guttered, all at once, like a thousand candles pinched by one hand.

"Now I simply take."

* * *

It came for me the way winter comes — not fast, but total, from every direction, patient. The flood stopped being an offer and became a tide, and I stood in it holding my small coal, and I screamed for Rosalind without any words at all, just the raw animal sound of someone going under.

And far away, impossibly far, above the roar of ten thousand stolen lives, I heard bone creak.

Carving sounds. Quick, desperate, precise.

Rosalind was working. Rosalind was making something. And the last coherent thought I had, before the flood closed over my head and I became a crowd screaming in the shape of one boy, was:

*Hurry.*
