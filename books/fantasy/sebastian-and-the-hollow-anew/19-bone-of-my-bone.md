## 19. Bone of My Bone

There is a moment in every carving when the bone stops arguing with you.

Carvers know what I mean. Bone has opinions. It wants to curve where you want it straight, it wants to splinter along its grain when you ask it to hold a shape it was never meant for. You fight it for hours, and then—sometimes—you don't win so much as it decides to let you. The knife finds a line that was already there, waiting, and you realize the bone knew what it wanted to be before you picked up your tools.

The prison-bone wanted to be finished.

I sat on the salt-crusted floor of the undercroft with my back against Aldous's empty throne dais, the carving cradled in my lap like something newborn, because that is essentially what it was: a thing being born out of an old thing's death. It was beautiful, if I'm honest, and I have rarely been able to afford honesty about my own work. A hollow cylinder of pale bone latticed with channels no wider than a hair, each one cut in the old tongue, each one a throat for a single word. Sebastian had checked my lines twice with his fingertip, closing his eyes, and twice he had nodded—that small solemn nod of his, the one that made him look forty years older than his eleven or twelve or however many years a boy carries when half of them belong to a dead king.

*It will hold,* he had signed. And then, because the dead who crowded the flooded dark around us insisted on their opinion too: *They say it will hold. Most of them. One of them is a pessimist from the reign of Aldous's grandfather.*

"Tell the pessimist," I said, "that if this fails, we'll all be digested together, and he can gloat in whatever comes after digestion."

Sebastian smiled. It was a terrible time for smiling. That was exactly why I did it.

Because here is what I hadn't told him. Here is what I had told no one, not even Odric, who would have sold it within the hour anyway, and not even Corvane, whose ledger-burning loyalty was still new enough to be fragile. The binding required bone willingly given by someone who could hear the dead. Not any bone. Not a relic, not a purchased femur off Odric's maps of debt. The sorceress's last word—the words she'd spent her final breath stitching into Sebastian's memory five years ago—had been precise in the way dying people are precise, because they no longer have time to be vague:

*The Hollow can be imprisoned in bone willingly given. But the giver loses what the bone holds.*

And what did a wrist hold, in the grammar of bones? In my grammar?

Everything. Every charm I had ever carved, yes, but beneath that—the listening itself. The gift was rooted in my hands. It always had been. When I pressed my palm against a relic and the dead rose up through my wrist like water through a well, that well was not metaphor. It was anatomy. Take the wrist-bone, take the channel, and you took the riverbed. What remained would be a woman with quick fingers and a good eye for grain, selling pretty trinkets to people who didn't know they were buying nothing at all.

I had spent fifteen years shrinking my gift down to whispers so the Wardens wouldn't hear it. Now I was going to shrink it to zero, and the irony was so clean it almost felt designed. Somewhere, the Hollow was probably laughing. If it laughed. I'd never gotten a clear sense of whether it had a sense of humor, which somehow made it worse.

Above us, muffled through feet of stone and flood-water and the whole rotten weight of the palace, Vellamere was burning. Ashfall, day three. We had heard the bells at dawn, and then heard nothing, which was worse—the dead in the city above growing quieter by the hour as more bloodlines got severed, more names struck, more grandmothers forgotten by grandchildren who would wake tomorrow unable to say what had been taken from them. The Hollow wasn't even fighting us anymore. It was eating on schedule, confident as a tax collector.

Well. Confidence before the audit.

* * *

Sebastian found me checking the channels a fourth time, which was three times too many, and sat down beside me without a word. He had gotten good at knowing when I needed silence and when I needed noise. It was one of perhaps four hundred things he'd learned in our months together, and every one of them had been learned by a boy who should have been learning how to whittle or steal apples or do anything other than host a kingdom's worth of grief.

He touched my elbow. I looked up.

*Your wrist,* he signed. His eyes were doing the thing where they saw straight through me, which was insufferable, mostly because I'd taught him to do it. *You've been holding your left hand wrong since midnight.*

"Habit," I said.

*Rosalind.*

"It's a very convincing habit."

His mouth went flat. He reached over—not asking, he never asked, the dead don't ask permission either, maybe he'd caught it from them—and turned my wrist over. The bandage was thin and I hadn't changed it since the tunnels, and blood had seeped through in a line as neat as a scribe's rule. Where I'd opened it, two nights ago, alone, with the smallest knife I owned and a great deal of swearing, because I am many things but I am not a coward about pain, only about conversations.

Sebastian looked at the cut. Then at me. Then at the cut again, doing the arithmetic.

"You needed living bone," I said. "Relics are secondhand. The sorceress said—"

*I know what she said.* His hands were shaking. That frightened me more than the Hollow did; I had never once seen his hands shake, not during the raids, not in the throne room, not when the Hollow had shown him his mother's face and offered him back his childhood like a merchant offering a refund. *You were going to do it tonight without telling me. Without telling anyone. Just—carve yourself into a prison and let us find out afterward.*

"That was the plan, yes."

*It's a stupid plan.*

"It's the only plan."

*It's both,* he signed, furious, and then his fury collapsed all at once, the way a boy's does, into something younger and rawer. *You're the only person who doesn't flinch from me. You looked at me in your doorway with a curfew raid coming and you didn't flinch, and now you're going to—*

"I'm going to trade," I said. "That's all this is. A trade. I've been making trades my whole life, Sebastian. Charms for coin, truth for safety, hearing for hiding. This one just has a better exchange rate than most." I turned my wrist in the firelight, showing him the fine white seam of scar tissue where the first cut had closed. "Fifteen years of listening to the dead, in exchange for a city full of the living getting to keep their dead at all. Even the pessimist from the old king's grandfather's reign thinks that's fair."

*The pessimist says you're lying about it being fair.*

"The pessimist can bite me."

And Sebastian laughed—a wet, cracked, eleven-year-old laugh, the laugh of a boy who almost never got to be eleven—and threw his arms around my neck, and I held onto him with the arm I was about to lose part of, because if I was going to be sentimental I was going to do it properly.

* * *

We did it in the throne room.

Not because the ritual demanded it—I could have bound the thing in a broom closet if the bone was right—but because the Hollow deserved witnesses. Because Aldous deserved to come home to his own hall, even ruined, even salt-scoured, even with the banners rotted to gray rags on the walls. And because, if I'm truthful, and I keep saying I'm trying to be, some petty animal part of me wanted that thing to see our faces while it went into the dark.

Corvane held the doors. She stood in her scorched Warden coat with her ledger-ash still under her fingernails and a sword she clearly hadn't cleaned since the tunnels, and she watched me walk to the center of the floor, and she said, quietly, "Marchetti. Whatever you're about to do—"

"You don't want to know."

"—I know exactly what I'm about to do," she finished. "So do it fast."

Aldous lay where we had left him after the unbinding began, propped against the foot of his own throne, his body gaunt as winter branches, his eyes surfacing now and then through the Hollow's occupation like a drowning man's face through ice. Sebastian knelt beside him with both hands on the king's temples, speaking the unbinding in the old tongue, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe that sound and failing. It wasn't language the way I understood language. It was language the way a flood is water—layered, ancient, every word carrying ten generations of other words inside it like seeds. The dead of the whole city seemed to lean in through the stones to listen.

And the Hollow fought.

It came up through Aldous like smoke forced backward through a keyhole, and the temperature of the room dropped, and every torch guttered toward black, and it spoke—with Aldous's mouth, with the mouths of a hundred dead things wearing Aldous's face in the shadows—

*Bone-carver. Little listener. I have eaten queens who bargained harder than you.*

"Then you'll have indigestion," I said, and drew the knife across my left wrist, deep, along the line I had already mapped, and the pain was bright and clean and utterly mine, the last thing I would ever feel in that hand that belonged only to me.

I won't pretend it was dignified. I swore. I may have sworn in two languages, one of them possibly Sebastian's, absorbed somewhere in the last months without my noticing. Blood ran hot over my palm and I gripped the knife differently, shifted my grip to the awkward angle carvers use when working against their own body, and set the blade to my own wrist-bone where it lay close beneath the opened skin.

Bone willingly given. I gave it.

The carving sang.

There's no other word. The channels I had cut filled with light the color of candle-through-milk, and the bone in my lap woke up, and I could feel it drinking from my wrist—not the blood, the blood was incidental—it was drinking the *listening*, drawing the gift out of me thread by thread the way you draw marrow from a soup bone, and with every thread came voices. Mama, singing off-key in the kitchen. The widow's husband, complaining about the weather in his coffin. Ten thousand strangers' grandmothers. The pessimist, who at the very end stopped gloating long enough to say, in a tone of genuine surprise, *Oh. It's warm in there,* and then was gone into the lattice.

The Hollow screamed.

I mean it screamed. All its stolen centuries hit the walls of the throne room at once, a hurricane of eaten names, and Sebastian held the unbinding with tears streaming down his face and blood trickling from his nose, and Corvane braced the doors with her whole body as the pressure of that scream tried to peel the palace open like a boiled egg, and I raised the bone above my head, slick with my own blood, and spoke the binding.

In the old tongue.

My accent, I am told by everyone who has ever heard the recording of that moment in Sebastian's memory—which is to say Sebastian, retelling it, delighted, at least once a year—was appalling. I butchered vowels that predate the kingdoms. I pronounced a word for "prison" as though it were the word for "soup." But intent outranks pronunciation in that language, the way love outranks grammar in ours, and the channels were already singing, and the words came out of me sounding like something older than my mistake.

*Be small. Be held. Be remembered, and be done remembering.*

The Hollow poured.

I felt it pass through me on its way in—one instant of contact, one heartbeat of standing inside a thing that had eaten five hundred years of inheritance, one glimpse of what it carried: whole dynasties folded like letters, faces stacked to the horizon, mothers and millers and kings all compressed into one endless starving appetite—and I understood, in that instant, why it did what it did. It wasn't evil, exactly. It was lonely the way only a hoarder is lonely, surrounded by everything and owning nothing.

Then it went into the bone, and the channels sealed behind it one by one with sounds like small doors closing, and the light died to a faint pulse, slow and sullen, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

And the silence came down.

I had prepared for quiet. Everyone prepares for quiet. Nobody prepares for *this*. Fifteen years I had lived with the dead murmuring at the edge of everything—background weather, a radio static of grief and gossip and grievance—and I had complained about it, privately, constantly, the way you complain about a bad knee. I had built my whole weary identity around enduring it.

It was gone. All of it. The throne room was just a room. The stones were just stones. Aldous's ancestors were nowhere, and nobody anywhere was whispering, and the silence was not an absence of sound but a presence of nothing, vast and smooth and total, pressing on me from every direction like deep water.

I sat down hard on the salt-white floor. My wrist bled into my lap. My hands—my poor magnificent traitor hands—felt like gloves someone else was wearing.

* * *

Later—minutes, hours, I couldn't have said—Sebastian crouched in front of me. The king was breathing behind him, slow and human, actually asleep for the first time in five years, and Corvane had draped her Warden coat over him like it was the most natural act in the world, and outside, impossibly, the ash had stopped falling.

Sebastian took my bandaged hand in both of his. He didn't sign. He spoke aloud, in the old tongue, softly, and though I couldn't understand the words anymore—couldn't understand them, the meaning sliding off me like rain off wax—I knew what they were, because he'd told me once, when I taught him that language is a gift you give forward:

*Thank you. Rest now. You are remembered.*

And then, because he is Sebastian, and because he had learned from watching me that the truest comfort is often a little bit wry, he added, in plain Vellamere speech, his voice cracking:

"For what it's worth, your accent really was terrible."

I laughed until my wrist hurt, which didn't take long, and then I looked at the bone lying between us on the floor—small, pale, pulsing faintly, holding a god and a pessimist and every voice I had ever loved—and I realized I couldn't hear a single thing inside it. Couldn't feel the Hollow. Couldn't feel anything at all except the ache in my hand and the boy's grip on my fingers and the enormous, oceanic quiet.

But Sebastian was staring past my shoulder, toward the high broken windows, and his face had gone strange.

"What," I said. "What is it?"

"There's someone," he said slowly, "standing at the top of the stairs. Someone the dead just told me about. Someone who's been waiting five years for that bone to exist—" and his eyes widened, "—and she says she's your mother."
