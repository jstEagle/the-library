## 21. Silence, and What Grows There

The first thing you should know about silence is that it has a texture.

I had spent my whole life under the dead the way other people live under weather. My mother's voice in the rafters. The grumble of some long-dead carver critiquing my chisel work from inside a femur I'd bought at auction. The endless low murmur of Vellamere's departed, layered like sediment beneath every street — a city built on its own grief, humming.

Now there was nothing. Not quiet, which implies something waiting to resume. Nothing. A room after the musicians have packed up and gone home and taken the music with them.

People imagine they would welcome it. Peace, they call it. And I will admit there were mornings — the first few weeks especially — when I woke and lay still in the astonishing hush and thought, *so this is what sane people get all the time.* No voices cataloguing their regrets over my breakfast. No dead grandmother of some client two streets over complaining about the quality of my mourning charms.

Then the mornings got longer. Then the hush started to feel less like peace and more like a debt collector standing politely outside my door, waiting for me to notice what had been repossessed.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about giving up a part of yourself: you don't miss it the way you miss an object. You miss it the way you miss a limb. The ghost of it itches. I would catch myself mid-carving, tilting my head toward a bone as though it might speak, and then remember — with a small, clean horror, every single time — that it couldn't. That none of them could. That I had poured everything I was into a prison made of my own wrist, and the door had locked behind the Hollow on its way in, and it had locked behind *me* too.

Willingly given. That was the flaw in the binding, and also the whole point of it. The sorceress who sealed Sebastian had known: bone willingly given holds what nothing else can, but the giver loses what the bone holds. My wrist-bone held thirty years of listening. So now I was a deaf woman in a world where everyone else had gone conveniently mute.

Poetic, Odric would say. Odric says a lot of things for a man whose entire contribution to the war against the Hollow was a map and one spectacular act of betrayal, since repented of so thoroughly and so publicly that I sometimes suspect he enjoyed the repentance more than he'd enjoyed the betrayal.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Months have passed. Let me start where the story actually sits now: in my workshop, on a grey morning in the season the city has started calling the Thaw, carving an ordinary thing for living hands.

* * *

The charm was a birth-token. A new thing, in Vellamere — mothers commissioning them again, now that the Wardens had stood down and the ash-squares had been swept and someone had finally pried the Hollow-bone off the palace altar and put it somewhere sensible. Mine, technically. It sat on my mantel wrapped in wool, because even bound, even silent, nobody wanted it loose in a drawer.

Birth-tokens used to be illegal. Everything worth carving used to be illegal. Now a fishwife named Bette Hollis paid me three coppers and a heel of bread to carve her daughter's name into a knucklebone, "so the girl remembers herself," Bette said, "in case the city tries forgetting again."

I liked Bette. I gave her the bread back and kept the coppers.

The trouble with carving without the gift is that it's just — carving. Before, I could feel where a memory wanted to sit in the grain of the bone, could follow the dead person's own shape through the material like water finding a channel. Now I worked by craft alone, and craft alone is honest but lonely. The token came out clean. Pretty, even. A knucklebone with a baby's name spiraling down it in letters that meant nothing except what they said.

It would hold no whisper. It would carry exactly as much of little Maren Hollis as any stone carries any name: whatever love the living bothered to keep pouring into it.

Which, I am learning, might be enough. Might even be the whole lesson. But knowing a lesson and living with it are different trades, and some mornings my hands shook anyway, and I had to set down the chisel and press my ruined wrist against the table until the ache gave me something true to stand on.

Sebastian found me like that. He had a way of arriving at the exact moments I least wanted witnesses, which I suspect he learned from watching me do the same to him for a year.

"You're doing the wrist thing," he said from the doorway.

"I'm resting my wrist. It's called an injury."

"It's called brooding." He came in and sat on his stool — his stool, the one I'd stopped thinking of as mine sometime around the third month — and watched my hands. He was fourteen now, or the palace records said he was fourteen; the boy himself had stopped being sure of anything so simple. He'd grown half a head taller. He still went barefoot indoors out of habit, though he owned boots now, good ones, a gift from a king who abdicated and took up gardening with the terrifying enthusiasm of a man making up for five lost years.

"Bad night?" I asked.

He shrugged, which from Sebastian was a paragraph. Since the unbinding, the memories sat in him properly — not riding him, not flooding him, just *there*, the way a library is there. King Aldous's coronation. The court sorceress's last breath. Every bloodline the Hollow had eaten, returned to their rightful keeper, filed away in a boy's head with the patient, terrible completeness of something that intended to outlive us all.

"Whose was it?" I asked. With Sebastian you didn't ask *what*. You asked *whose*.

"The weaver family. The ones off Tanner's Row." He turned my spare chisel over in his fingers, not fidgeting — studying it, the way he studied everything, like the object might confess. "Before the Hollowing. There was a grandmother who sang while she worked, and the song had words in the old tongue, and she didn't even know they were old. She thought she'd made them up." A pause. "She hadn't. They were a lullaby from before Vellamere had walls."

"And?"

"And now nobody sings it." He set the chisel down with exaggerated care. "I know it. Word for word. I've known it for months. And there's no one left to sing it *to*, because the granddaughter moved to Saltmarsh and doesn't remember her grandmother's face, let alone the song, and—" He stopped. Breathed. Fourteen years old, carrying a nation's attic in his skull, and he did breathing exercises I'd taught him like a good student. "It's not grief," he said finally. "Grief is for things that ended. This is more like... inventory."

"Inventory," I repeated.

"Of everything the city almost lost." He looked up at me, and his eyes did the thing they did sometimes — went old and crowned and briefly not-his. "You'd think winning would feel better."

I laughed. It surprised both of us. "Welcome to adulthood, Your Majesty. It's mostly inventory."

He smiled, and he was fourteen again, and I went back to Maren Hollis's knucklebone, and neither of us said the thing that was sitting in the room with us like a third chair.

The thing being: I couldn't hear the dead, and he could hear *everything*, and some nights I woke up aching for my mother's voice the way you ache for water, and we both knew it, and neither of us had yet found a way to say it out loud.

* * *

Here is what the silence cost me, itemized, because I am a carver and I think in inventories too:

My mother, first. Her voice had been with me since I was six, since the fever, since the first time the dead leaned close to a frightened child and one of them happened to be mine. She'd hummed while I worked. She'd told me, every winter, to wear the scarf. Trivial things. That's what people don't understand — the dead don't haunt you with their deaths. They haunt you with their Tuesdays.

My father, whom I'd heard exactly once, the night before the binding, saying my name like an apology. I'd been saving that one. Rationing it. Planning to listen to it again on some future bad night, the way you keep a coin for emergencies.

Every client's beloved. Every whisper I'd ever sold in thimblefuls to keep myself fed. The whole drowned choir of Vellamere, gone from my ears in a single act of necessary treason against my own nature.

And here is the item that shames me most: some days, I missed the *work* more than the people. The hearing itself. Being needed. Being the woman who could fetch a voice back across the river. Rosalind Marchetti, bone-carver, ferrywoman of the dead. Take that away and what remained was a middle-aged woman with a bad wrist and a talent for pretty spirals, sharing a house with a boy who could do what I did, only infinitely better, only *for real* —

No. That's the lie the silence tells, and I promised myself I'd write it down honestly, so: the bitterness wasn't really about the work. The bitterness was that I had bought the city's future with my mother's voice, and the city had already moved on, and nobody — not Aldous in his garden, not Corvane with her restored daughter, not the grateful, bustling, remembering-again citizens of Vellamere — nobody needed a deaf ferrywoman anymore.

Except one person did need me. He just needed me in ways that didn't come with applause. He needed soup on inventory nights. He needed someone who flinched at nothing, because I had already lost everything flinchable. He needed, though he'd have died before admitting it, a mother-shaped thing in the next room, humming tunelessly because she couldn't remember how the songs went anymore.

Some inheritances skip a generation. Some go sideways entirely.

* * *

It happened on an evening in late spring, and it happened because of a scarf.

That's not poetry. It was literally a scarf. Cold snap off the salt marshes, the kind that gets into a house through gaps you'd swear weren't there, and I was heading out to the market with my collar up and my ears burning, and I heard myself mutter — aloud, to an empty room, out of thirty years of habit:

"Yes, Mother, I'll wear the scarf."

And the room stayed empty. And the scarf stayed on its hook. And I stood in my own doorway like a fool with my hand on the latch, and the silence came down on me all at once, the full weight of it, months of it, and I did not go to the market.

I sat down on the workshop stool instead, in the dark, because turning on a lamp felt like more than I could manage, and I put my head in my hands and I grieved. Properly, this time. Not the managed, wry, keep-calm-and-carve-on grief I'd been performing for everyone including myself, but the raw, ugly, snotty original article. For my mother's Tuesdays. For the hummed songs. For the version of me that died on the throne room floor with her wrist-bone in a stranger's ancient tongue.

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the cold came in and found me. Long enough that when the floorboard creaked behind me, I didn't even startle.

Sebastian didn't turn on the lamp either. He just crossed the room, pulled the scarf off its hook, and draped it around my neck with the grave ceremony of a boy who had once been a king's shadow and never quite learned to do anything casually.

Then he said, in my mother's voice: "*Rosalind. You'll catch your death.*"

I want to be clear about what that was. It wasn't mimicry. Mimicry is a trick, and tricks are cheap, and if he'd done it as a trick I'd have thrown him out of the workshop and loved him anyway but thrown him out regardless. This was different. This was the archive doing what archives do. He carried everything the Hollow had eaten and everything it hadn't, and somewhere in that vast, terrible, wonderful collection was a recording no device could make: my mother's voice, heard through the bones of the city, preserved in the memory of a language older than kingdoms. He had it the way the sea has rain.

"*You'll catch your death,*" he said again, gently, word for word, tone for tone, the exact exasperated fondness she'd spent on me for forty years, and I broke.

Not prettily. There was no dignified single tear. I made sounds I hadn't made since I was a child, and he held onto my shoulders and let me, steady as a pillar, fourteen years old and bearing it easily because he had borne worse, because he had been ridden by a god of hunger and walked away, because grief — ordinary, human, survivable grief — was nothing to him now except a country he knew well and visited often.

When I could speak, I said, "How long have you had that?"

"Since the unbinding. Since everything poured back." He hesitated. "I wasn't sure I should ever use it. It felt like stealing."

"It isn't stealing."

"No," he agreed. "I figured that out eventually. It's more like..." He searched, and the old tongue flickered across his face — I saw it, the syllables moving behind his eyes, the language that had nearly eaten him looking for purchase — and then he chose plain Vellamere speech, deliberately, and said: "It's more like being trusted with it. Somebody has to keep these things. And keeping a thing means being willing to hand it over when it's owed."

"It's not owed to me."

"Your mother hummed over your cradle for six years, Rosalind. Half the lullabies I know, I know because she learned them from *her* mother, who learned them from a weaver on Tanner's Row, who learned them from before the walls." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last of it. "Everything connects. That's the whole secret. That's what the Hollow never understood — it thought inheritance was something you could *eat*. But it's not food. It's a thread. You can cut it, but you can't digest it. It just waits for someone to pick up the ends and tie them again."

I looked at him — this mute boy who'd arrived at my door barefoot in a curfew raid, speaking a dead language, and who was now neither mute nor dead nor a boy in any simple sense — and I understood that the binding had cost me my hearing and given the city something better than my hearing had ever been. I had been a ferry. One voice at a time, crossing one river, for whoever could pay.

He was the whole archive. Free of charge. Forever, or as near to forever as a fourteen-year-old gets.

"Sing it," I said. "The weaver's lullaby. The one from before the walls."

So he did. In the old tongue, in the dark, in a voice that was his own and also a grandmother's and also, faintly, underneath everything, my mother's — because my mother had known that song too, had hummed it to me without knowing its age, and hearing it sung whole for the first time in a hundred years was like watching a river find its old bed.

I wore the scarf. Obviously.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, both of us sitting by the banked fire with tea going cold — he said the thing that turned the evening from an ending into a beginning.

"There are others," he said. "Like you. Like I was."

"Others how?"

"People who gave things up in the binding. Small ones, mostly. Corvane says her daughter hears ringing sometimes where the memories used to be. A tanner on Cooper Street lost his ability to dream. Little prices, scattered all over the city — the binding pulled from everyone who'd ever touched the Hollow's attention, not just from you." He turned his cup slowly. "They don't know why. They think they're cursed, or sick. But I *remember*, Rosalind. I remember every thread. I could tell them what they lost. Maybe even give some of it back."

I sat with that. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, the city hummed — the living hum, the only one I had now, and I found, testing it honestly, that it was enough. That it might always have been enough, if anyone had ever taught me to listen for it.

"You're proposing," I said slowly, "to open a school."

"I'm proposing to open a *door*." He grinned, suddenly, wholly fourteen, and it lit the dark room like a struck match. "You'll carve. I'll remember. Between us we might just—"

"—rebuild the entire inheritance of a city that outlawed magic to survive its own king," I finished. "Yes. Fine. But I'm doing the accounts, and you're sweeping the floor, and if one more nobleman's ghost shows up asking for special treatment, we're both going on strike."

He laughed, and the sound filled the silence — not filling it the way noise fills a room, but the way a seed fills a furrow. Something planted. Something that grows.

Outside, dawn was coming up grey over Vellamere, over the swept ash-squares and the mended roofs and the mantel where a hollow bone slept in its wool wrappings, dreaming whatever hollow bones dream. Inside, a boy who remembered everything fell asleep in a chair, and a woman who could no longer hear the dead covered him with a blanket, and hummed.

Tunelessly. But she hummed.
