## 22. Sebastian and the Hollow Anew

The school has eleven students, one roof that leaks in exactly two places, and a mantel we are all slightly afraid of.

I want to be clear about the mantel, because people ask. They come up the crooked stairs of Rosalind's old shop — my shop now too, though I still knock before entering my own home, a habit I refuse to examine — and they see the long white bone laid on carved rests above the hearth, wrapped at each end in silver wire, and they ask: *Is that it? Is that the thing?*

And I say yes. And they edge away from it like it might sit up.

It won't. That's rather the point of everything Rosalind did. But I understand the flinching. I spent six years flinching from things far less deserving of it than a bone that cannot hear you anymore.

* * *

Let me tell you how the school started, because it didn't start as a school. It started as an accident, which is how most true things start.

Three months after the unbinding — after Aldous walked out of his own palace in plain clothes and gave the crown to a council of clerks who had never wanted it — a girl named Maren followed me home from the fish market. She was maybe eight. She had ash-grey eyes and a stutter, and she had heard me muttering under my breath in the old tongue while arguing with a fishmonger about the price of eels, which even in Vellamere is a negotiation that requires either ancient languages or divine intervention.

She tugged my sleeve and said, in the old tongue, badly, beautifully: *"You speak wrong."*

I stopped in the street. A cart nearly took me. In five years of running, in three years of hiding, in one year of war against a thing made of everyone's forgotten grandmothers, no living person had ever spoken those words to me. The dead had corrected me plenty. The dead were insufferable about my grammar. But the dead don't count; the dead have nothing better to do.

"Where did you learn that?" I asked her, in Common, because my heart had climbed into my throat and taken the old language with it.

"My nan," she said. "Before she went strange." *Went strange.* That's what families said, in those years, when the Hollow ate someone's past out from under them. Nan forgot her daughter's name but kept singing lullabies in a language she couldn't identify. Nan went strange. And then Maren's mother beat her for repeating the sounds, because fear travels downhill faster than love does, and Maren learned to say the words only in her head, where they grew crooked, like a tree growing around a fence.

So I sat down on the fish-market steps and taught her to say it right. That's the whole origin story. No vision, no destiny, no dramatic music. A girl, some steps, the smell of eels.

By winter there were four children. By spring, nine. Now eleven, and a waiting list, and Rosalind threatening to teach a class herself if one more parent leaves a child on our doorstep with a note pinned to their coat like we're an orphanage for haunted infants.

* * *

The lessons happen in the front room of the shop, among the carvings. Rosalind never stopped carving — that was the other thing everyone got wrong about her sacrifice. They assumed that losing the dead would hollow her out the way the Hollow hollowed everything else. I watched her the first weeks, honestly frightened for her, waiting for the silence to crack her open.

Instead she carved a spoon.

A completely ordinary wooden spoon, sanded smooth, for a customer's kitchen. Then a doorstop shaped like a sleeping dog. Then a set of buttons, then a child's rocking horse with a mane you could actually grip, then a hundred small dumb beautiful useless-in-the-best-way things for living hands. She told me once, while sanding something that would become a bread board: "I spent thirty years making vessels for voices. Turns out wood is just wood, and it's lovely."

But she doesn't carve charms anymore. Not mourning pieces, not luck-knots, not the whisper-charms that used to keep us fed. When people ask — and they do, constantly, because half the city decided she's a saint and the other half decided she's a witch and both halves want merchandise — she says the same thing: "The dead aren't mine to carry anymore. Ask the boy."

Which is how I ended up as a living archive, thank you very much, a job title nobody applies for.

Here is what it actually means. When King Aldous abdicated — and I'll get to him, he insists I mention him, he's worse than the dead about being remembered — he formally entrusted his memories to me. Not transferred. Entrusted. There's a distinction, and the distinction is the whole moral of this story, so pay attention. The Hollow *took*. It consumed inheritance, devoured bloodlines, ate the past out of people like marrow out of bone. What Aldous did was the opposite verb. He *gave*. Willingly, formally, with witnesses, in the throne room where he'd spent five years as a puppet wearing his own face.

So I hold a king's coronation. I hold a queen's laughter, which sounded like a kettle. I hold the names of every servant in the palace in the year of the Hollowing, including a scullery boy called Pimm who stole candied ginger and once hid a cat in the wine cellar for a month. I hold the court sorceress Wren's last words, which were instructions, and her second-to-last words, which were an apology, and her third-to-last words, which were a joke so bad I refuse to repeat it even under torture.

And I hold the city. Because here's what nobody planned: once the Hollow was bound, everything it had eaten didn't come back to its owners. It came to *me*. Every severed bloodline in Vellamere, every grandmother gone strange, every name scraped from Odric's maps of debt — it all poured into the vessel that was already holding the king. I woke up the morning after the binding knowing forty thousand lives the way you know your own childhood: not as facts but as weather.

Rosalind lost the dead forever. So I became them. That was always the trade, underneath the trade. She sealed the Hollow into her own wrist-bone and paid with her hearing, and the payment didn't vanish into the dark — it landed in me, and I carry it, and I will carry it until I die, and then, presumably, some poor child will inherit *me*, and I have already written them a letter apologizing in advance.

* * *

Teaching the old language safely is harder than it sounds, mostly because "safely" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

The old tongue isn't magic, exactly. That was the first lie of the purge, the founding fiction of the Wardens of Ash: that words themselves were the danger. Words are just handles. Magic is what you hang on them, and most people can't hang anything, and the ones who can should learn while someone kind is watching. What the language actually does, for the marked few like Maren and me, is *listen*. You speak it and the world speaks back — specifically, the parts of the world that remember. Stone remembers footsteps. Rivers remember drownings. Bones remember everything, the gossips.

So rule one of my classroom, chalked above the mantel in letters I make the students copy until their hands ache: **THE DEAD ARE NOT YOURS TO COMMAND. THEY ARE YOURS TO COURTESY.**

Rule two: **NEVER SPEAK THE TONGUE OVER A BONE YOU DIDN'T MAKE OR BUY HONESTLY.** We had an incident. A boy named Tam thought it would be funny to address a chicken bone from lunch. For the record, chickens remember almost nothing, but what they remember they remember *loudly*, and Tam screamed, and knocked over the ink, and Maren laughed so hard she cried, and I pretended to be stern for a full ten seconds before joining her. Still. Rule exists now.

Rule three, the real one, the one I make them say aloud every single day: **WHAT YOU ARE GIVEN, GIVE. WHAT YOU TAKE, RETURN.** The Hollow's whole crime, distilled to a sentence a child can chant. The sorceress Wren figured it out dying on a palace floor: inheritance freely given binds; inheritance stolen devours. Everything else — the crown, the purges, the salt-flooded undercrofts, poor Odric selling us out for a family name he'd already lost — all of it grows from that one root.

The children think the rules are boring. Good. Rules should be boring. Excitement is for the mantel.

* * *

Ah, the mantel again. Fine. Let's talk about it properly, since I've been circling it like a cat around a bath.

The bone lies there in its silver wire, and it is quiet, and it will be quiet forever, and sometimes at night I catch myself talking to it anyway.

Not praying. Not commanding. Just... reporting. It feels important that somebody report to it. "Maren conjugated the past tense correctly today," I tell it. "Tam only screamed briefly at the chicken. Rosalind sold three spoons and a cradle, and she hummed while she worked, and if you could have heard her humming you'd know why I'm telling you this."

Because here is the thing about the Hollow that I have never said out loud, not to Rosalind, not to Corvane, not to anyone: for one moment in the throne room, mid-unbinding, I understood it. Not forgave it — understood. It was hungry the way a burned man is cold. It had been *made* from discarded memory, from everything a city decided wasn't worth keeping, and it ate because no one had ever given it anything. It offered me godhood and my mother's face in the same breath, and the terrible truth is that the offer was sincere. It genuinely believed it was being generous. It was the loneliest thing I have ever met, and Rosalind didn't destroy it, she *housed* it, gave it a room of its own in bone willingly given, and I think that mercy is the bravest thing anyone did in this whole story, and I include the swordfights.

So it lies on the mantel, and the children are afraid of it, and I let them be afraid, and then eventually I teach them what it's a warning *of*: not that magic is dangerous. That hunger is. That taking is. That a thing left alone with nothing of its own will eat the world trying to feel full.

Some nights I swear the bone is warmer near the fire. Rosalind says bones conduct heat. Rosalind is usually right. I let myself believe both things.

* * *

Corvane visits on Thursdays.

Warden Ilsa Corvane, terror of the market district, keeper of the ledger, the woman whose boots I still occasionally hear in nightmares — she comes for tea, sits in the chair nearest the fire, and pretends not to check that the Hollow-bone hasn't moved. Her daughter Sera comes with her. Sera is fourteen now and remembers her mother imperfectly, the way you remember a house you moved out of at five: warm, but the details are gone. The Hollow digested most of Sera's memories of Ilsa before we ever reached the undercrofts, and no unbinding undigests anything.

But here's what happens on Thursdays, and I confess I engineered it shamelessly. Sera sits with me, and I give her back what remains — not by magic, just by talking. "Your mother used to sing off-key on purpose to make you laugh. She kept a ledger even then, but hers listed your first words instead of criminals'. She was terrified of moths." Small change, counted out weekly. I can't restore what was eaten. Nobody can. But I can tell it forward, and Sera can hold it, and someday Sera will tell her own daughter, and the moth story will outlive all of us, which is more than any charm ever managed.

That's the whole technology, really. That's the discovery of my entire ridiculous life: memory survives not in bones or crowns or ledgers or bound monsters over a fireplace. It survives in *telling*. In who we choose to tell. A king's memories live in a mute boy from nowhere because a dying woman chose him. A warden's love lives in her daughter because a former weapon of the state spends Thursdays being a storyteller. Rosalind's mother's voice — the voice she traded away — lives in me, word for word, and last month, on the anniversary of the binding, I stood in her workshop and gave it back to her, every syllable, the lullaby and the scolding and the way her mother said her name like it was a good joke.

Rosalind cried. Rosalind, who faced the Hollow without flinching, cried at a lullaby, and then threatened me with sandpaper if I ever told anyone, and I am telling you now because she'll never read this, and because some inheritances are meant to circulate.

* * *

Yesterday a new student arrived. Not left on the doorstep, thankfully — brought by her father, a cooper from the Salt Quarter, who stood in the doorway wringing his cap and saying he knew it was dangerous, he knew what the law used to say, but his girl talked in her sleep in a language he didn't recognize and he'd heard, everyone's heard, about the place above the bone-carver's shop.

She's six. Her name is Odile. When I greeted the class in the old tongue, she answered without hesitation, fluently, in a dialect I recognized from the bones of a fishing village that burned two hundred years ago. She doesn't know where she got it. Neither does her father. Somewhere back in his line, someone kept the words alive in secret, whispered through generations of fear, passed hand to hand like contraband, like a candle through a blackout — all the way to a six-year-old who talks in her sleep.

Five years the Hollow wore the crown. Twenty years before that, the purges. A century of ash laws and silent graves. And the words survived anyway, in sleep-talk and lullabies and a fish-market argument, because you cannot burn a language out of the mouths that love it. The Hollow never understood that. It thought memory was a possession, something you could seize and swallow and own. It died — sleeps — whatever a bound thing does — never learning that memory is a *conversation*, and conversations only die when everyone stops speaking.

Odile starts Monday. I've already warned Rosalind that the class is twelve now, and she sighed the sigh of a woman who lost an argument with the world years ago and has made peace with the terms.

Then she picked up her knife and started carving the girl a name-plate for her desk. An ordinary thing, for living hands. Oak, probably. Smooth where small fingers will touch it.

And if you pressed your ear to it — I checked, because I'm still me — it holds no voices at all.

Just the sound of the knife, going forward.

* * *

They ask me sometimes — the parents, the curious, the occasional historian from the clerk-council — what I would call all this. The school, the bone, the Thursdays, the twelve children learning to courtesy the dead. They want a grand name. *The Restoration*, maybe. *The Age of Return.*

I tell them the truth: it's just a shop above a shop, with a leaky roof and a mantel and a woman who makes spoons. Memory doesn't need a monument. It needs a teller, and a listener, and the stubborn daily choice to pass the candle on.

Tonight the fire is lit, the bone is warm, eleven — no, twelve — children are coming tomorrow, and somewhere in my head a king is reminding me to mention that he approves of the curriculum, and a scullery boy named Pimm wants it noted that the cat in the wine cellar lived a long and happy life.

Consider it noted. Consider all of it noted.

Class is in session.
