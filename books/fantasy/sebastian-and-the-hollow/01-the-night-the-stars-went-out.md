## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with a town that has celebrated the same festival for two hundred years, Sebastian Vale thought as he hauled another crate of lantern-oil up the clocktower stairs, is that everyone assumes it will go on forever.

"Mind the third step!" called Master Aldous from below, his voice carrying the particular cheer of a man who had already sampled the festival wine. "It's been loose since before you were born and I'll not have you falling through it on Founding Eve."

"It's been loose since before *you* were born," Sebastian called back, stepping neatly over it anyway. "You could fix it, you know. You're a clockmaker. Fixing things is rather our whole trade."

"I am an artist, boy, and artists do not stoop to carpentry." A pause, then, more softly: "And there's no one left who'd know how to fix it properly, is there?"

Sebastian didn't answer. There wasn't really anything to say to that. The step creaked under his boot like an old man clearing his throat, and he climbed on.

From the belfry of the Hollowmere clocktower, the whole valley laid itself out below him like something from one of the storybooks he'd never owned: rooftops strung with festival lanterns, the millpond catching their light in ripples of gold, and beyond it all — rising on every side like the walls of a bowl held by careful hands — the mountains. Somewhere up in those blue-dark heights ran the wards. Nobody talked about them much. They were simply *there*, the way the weather was there, the way the stars were there. Two hundred years ago, the stories said, the founders had sealed the valley against the world outside, and whatever lived outside had stayed outside ever since.

Sebastian had lived in Hollowmere all seventeen years of his life, orphaned so young that even Master Aldous — who remembered everything, mostly twice — couldn't tell him more than that he'd been found on the clocktower steps one winter morning, wrapped in a blanket stitched with a crest nobody recognized. A bird, wings spread, holding a key in its beak. Sebastian had kept the blanket folded in the bottom of his trunk for years, taking it out sometimes at night just to trace the stitching, as if the thread might eventually decide to explain itself.

It never did. So he'd stopped asking, mostly, and become very good at gears instead.

He set the oil down beside the great lantern they hung from the belfry every Founding Eve — the Heartlight, the town called it, though Master Aldous insisted the proper old name was the Vigil Flame, lit "so the dark knows we're still here." Sebastian had always found that a bit dramatic. Tonight, for reasons he couldn't name, the phrase sat differently in his chest. He looked out past the mountains, where the first stars should have been pricking through the dusk over the western peaks.

The sky was empty.

Not clouded — he knew clouds; these were his skies, every inch of them, learned from years of sleeping on the belfry floor in summer when the tower got too warm. Clouds had edges, textures, moods. This was different. The sky above the peaks was clear and black and utterly bare, as if someone had taken a cloth to it.

"Master Aldous," he said slowly. "When did you last see the evening star?"

"The evening star?" Boots on the stairs, unhurried. "Haven't seen it since spring, boy, it sets behind—"

"No. I mean tonight. It's not there. None of them are."

Aldous emerged onto the belfry, wiping his hands on his apron, his white beard catching lantern-light. He squinted westward, and Sebastian watched the squint deepen into something else entirely — something he had never once seen on the old man's face in seven years of apprenticeship.

Aldous was afraid.

"That's not possible," the clockmaker said. His voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when a mechanism failed in a way it shouldn't be able to fail. "That's not — boy, the almanac says—"

"The almanac says a great many things." Sebastian's own heart had begun doing something odd, a slow heavy knocking that had nothing to do with exertion. "Look east too. Look *up*. They're all gone, Master. Every single one."

They stood together at the rail while below them the festival went on oblivious — fiddlers tuning in the square, children chasing each other between the market stalls, old Marta Brindle already arguing with the pie judge — and above them, nothing. The lanterns suddenly seemed very small. The whole glowing town seemed small, a handful of embers cupped in enormous dark hands.

And then Sebastian felt it.

Later, he would try a hundred times to describe what it felt like, and never quite manage it. The nearest he ever came was this: it was like being a clock that had run all its life at slightly the wrong speed, and someone, somewhere, finally set the pendulum true. A shiver started deep beneath his sternum — not pain, not quite warmth either, but a *rightness*, vast and terrible, spreading outward through his ribs like the first tick after a long silence.

The lantern beside him flared. Not guttered — *flared*, flame standing straight and tall and white, and every lantern in the belfry did the same, and far below, he saw the strings of festival lights along the square brighten all at once, a gasp of gold running through the town like a shiver through a horse's hide.

"Sebastian." Aldous had gone very still. "What did you do?"

"Nothing." His own voice sounded strange to him, doubled somehow, as if someone were speaking just underneath it. "I didn't — Master, I don't know what's happening."

The old man grabbed his arm, and Sebastian saw that his hands were shaking. "Two hundred years ago," Aldous said, "when they sealed the valley, the founders wrote that the wards would stand so long as the covenant held. And they wrote — my grandmother told me, her grandmother told her — that if the covenant ever broke, we would know it by three signs. The lights would flare." He swallowed. "The stars would go out."

"And the third?" Sebastian asked, though some part of him, some deep buried part that had waited seventeen years without knowing it was waiting, already knew.

Aldous didn't get to answer.

Because that was when the wards shattered.

* * *

There was no sound at first — or rather, the sound arrived late, the way thunder arrives after lightning, because the thing itself happened too high and too far and too *other* for ears to catch it cleanly. What Sebastian saw was this: a line of pale blue fire ringing the entire valley, following the mountain crests like a crown of light, flickering — stuttering — and then breaking, segment by segment, the way ice breaks on a pond in thaw. Great shards of it peeled away and fell inward, dissolving into sparks long before they reached the treetops, and where the ring had been there was now only ordinary darkness, and beyond the darkness, the world.

The sound caught up then: a vast crystalline groan, like a cathedral bell the size of a mountain cracking down its middle, rolling across the valley and setting every window buzzing. Dogs began to howl. The festival music stopped mid-phrase.

In the square below, hundreds of faces turned upward. For one long moment nobody moved, nobody screamed, the whole town simply stood in the lantern-light with its mouth open, staring at the broken sky like a congregation watching the roof come off their church.

Then the screaming started, and the running, and somewhere a bell began to clang — not the festival bell but the alarm bell in the meetinghouse, ragged and urgent.

"Stay here," Aldous said, gripping Sebastian's shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Bar the door behind me. I'm going down to the council—"

"You're seventy years old, you can barely manage the stairs—"

"*Stay here.*" And for once there was no twinkle in it, no artistry, just an old man's terror, and Sebastian, to his own lasting surprise, obeyed.

He watched from the belfry rail as Aldous descended into the chaos, a small white-bearded figure plunging into streets full of running people. He watched the council house doors bang open, lamplight spilling out, the town's worthiest citizens gathering in frightened clusters. He watched the millpond, and this was the thing that lodged in him like a splinter: the water had gone perfectly, impossibly still, flat as hammered silver, reflecting a sky with no stars in it — and in the reflection, faint as breath on glass, something *moved*. Shapes in the black water-sky. Tall shapes. Watching.

The knocking feeling beneath his sternum grew louder. Stronger. It wasn't fear, exactly, though fear was certainly in attendance. It was more like recognition — like hearing, after a lifetime of silence, footsteps coming up a road you'd forgotten existed, toward a door you'd forgotten you had.

*You're awake,* something seemed to say, from somewhere beneath his skin, in a voice that was not a voice. *At last. At last.*

"Who's there?" Sebastian whispered aloud, like a fool talking to an empty belfry. The lanterns flared again in answer, and this time he could have sworn the flames bent toward him, all of them, like flowers finding the sun.

Down in the square, the crowd's panic had changed pitch. People weren't running randomly anymore; they were pressing back from the north road, away from the valley's mouth, where the land narrowed between two shoulders of mountain. Sebastian leaned out over the rail, straining to see, and the strange new sense inside him strained with him — and *showed* him, the way a lens shows what the naked eye misses.

Riders.

They came down the old north road at a canter, a dozen of them, maybe more, in armor that drank the lantern-light instead of returning it. Their horses made no sound on the road, which was wrong, which was deeply, fundamentally wrong, and every hair on Sebastian's arms stood up as one. Banners hung limp and starless above them — black cloth worked with a crest he couldn't make out, except that it seemed to shift when he looked directly at it, refusing to be remembered.

No one had crossed the valley border in two hundred years. That was the whole point of Hollowmere. That was the founding of the founding. The wards had stood, and the world had stayed outside, and generations had been born and died never once wondering what lay beyond the pass because wondering about it was like wondering what lay beyond the sky.

The riders crossed the old boundary stones without slowing. Where their horses' hooves touched the ground, frost bloomed — in midsummer, frost, spreading in delicate feathering patterns that Sebastian's new-woken senses read like text: *ash and iron, ash and iron.*

Below, the town guard formed up in the square, a thin line of farm boys with pikes and one ancient sergeant, and the crowd pressed in behind them, and somebody was shouting for the council, and somebody was shouting for the wards, and somebody — a child's voice, high and clear over all of it — was shouting that the stars were coming back.

Sebastian looked up. His heart stopped.

They weren't stars. They were too low, too evenly spaced, moving with purpose — a constellation descending the northern sky in perfect formation, points of cold white light, and as he watched, they resolved into shapes. Lanterns? No. Eyes. No —

He understood all at once, with the terrible clarity of the newly awakened part of himself, what they were. *Keystones,* said the voice beneath his skin, and it sounded like grief. *Or pieces of them. Falling home.*

One of the lights detached from the others. It drifted down, down, unhurried as a leaf, over the rooftops, over the frozen millpond, over the upturned faces of the town — and stopped, hanging in the air not thirty feet from the clocktower belfry. From the rail where Sebastian Vale stood alone with his hands gripping the wood and his whole body humming like a struck bell.

Up close, it wasn't a stone at all. It was a shard of light wrapped around something dark, turning slowly, and inside the dark something glinted — metal, a curve of it, familiar—

His mother's locket. Or a locket exactly like the one from the blanket in his trunk, the crest of the spread-winged bird catching the shard's glow, and the voice beneath his skin rose to a roar that shook his teeth:

*Find it. Find them. Before they do.*

The shard flared once, blinding, and shot away northward over the mountains, gone.

Sebastian stood shaking in the sudden ordinary dark. Below, the riders had reached the square's edge. The sergeant's pike-line wavered. Somewhere Aldous was shouting his name — *Sebastian! Sebastian!* — and somewhere else, closer than any of it, right at the base of the tower, soft as a secret, someone was climbing the stairs.

The third step creaked.

Nobody knew about the third step except him and Aldous, and Aldous was down in the square.

Sebastian turned toward the stairwell door as the lanterns dimmed around him, one by one, like held breath — and listened to the footsteps come up.
