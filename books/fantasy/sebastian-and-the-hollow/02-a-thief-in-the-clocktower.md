## 2. A Thief in the Clocktower

Sebastian Vale had repaired the archive-crystal housing twice before the festival, and both times he had complained about it loudly enough for the whole of Hollowmere to hear.

"It's a clocktower," he'd told Master Aldous, holding up the stripped gear that governed the tower's great bell. "It tells time. It does not need to *also* tell memories."

"Everything in Hollowmere tells memories," Aldous had said, not looking up from his tea. "That's rather the point of the place."

He wasn't wrong. The clocktower was the oldest building in the valley, older than the chapel, older than the mayor's ridiculous hat, and its lower vaults held the archive — rows upon rows of crystals the size of goose eggs, each one humming faintly with somebody's remembered life. Weddings. Harvests. The flood of sixty years past. Old Marta's first kiss, which she had deposited voluntarily and visited every Tuesday like it was a shrine. Nobody quite knew how the crystals worked; they predated the Founding, and the Founding was two hundred years gone. You brought your memory to the tower, you set your palm against the glass, and something of you stayed behind, warm and safe, where magic couldn't touch it.

Because that was the other thing about Hollowmere. Magic couldn't follow you here. It stopped at the wards like rain against a window. Whatever else was true of the world beyond the valley — and nobody in Sebastian's lifetime had ever been able to check — inside Hollowmere, a memory was yours forever.

Which was why, at half past eleven on the night before the Founding Festival, with the whole town asleep and the stars already behaving strangely overhead, Sebastian stood in the doorway of the crystal vault and said, "You're doing it wrong."

The figure crouched among the shelves went very still.

Sebastian had come down to oil the escapement — festival morning meant the bell would ring forty times instead of twelve, and a dry escapement meant an ugly sound, and he had *standards* — and he had heard, from three floors up, a noise no clock should make. A soft, wet clicking, like a beetle trapped in honey. He had followed it down the spiral stair with a wrench in one hand and the reasonable assumption that a raccoon had gotten into the vault again.

It was not a raccoon.

The intruder wore dark clothes that were trying very hard to be shadows and mostly succeeding, except for the part where they were crouched in the light of Sebastian's lamp with a crystal cradled in both gloved hands like a stolen egg. The hood slipped back as they turned, and Sebastian found himself looking at a face he didn't know — sharp-boned, pale, somewhere between twenty-five and thirty-five, with the particular exhausted elegance of someone who hadn't slept indoors in a while. Their eyes were gray-green and currently wide with the specific horror of a professional caught by an amateur.

"You're doing it wrong," Sebastian repeated, because apparently his mouth had decided to handle this situation without consulting him. "The extraction. You're twisting counterclockwise. That's why it's clicking. You'll crack the matrix and the memory will bleed out all over the shelf, and then I'll have to explain to old Marta why her first kiss smells like burnt sugar for a week."

A pause.

"How do you know what a proper extraction looks like?" the thief asked. Their voice was low and pleasant and roughened at the edges, like velvet dragged over gravel.

"I don't," Sebastian admitted. "But I know what a *broken* mechanism looks like, and that's the same face everywhere." He stepped into the vault, set his lamp on a shelf, and held out his hand. "Give it here. Palm flat, fingers spread, turn *with* the grain. The crystals remember hands better than tools."

The thief stared at him. Then, with the air of someone deciding that the night had already gone so badly sideways that dignity was a luxury, they handed over the crystal.

It was warm. Warmer than it should have been, Sebastian thought, though he had no real basis for comparison. He turned it under the lamplight and saw the memory swimming inside: a flash of green hills that were too green, a sky with too many colors in it, a woman's voice saying words in a language that made the back of his teeth ache. Not a Hollowmere memory at all.

"This isn't from the valley," he said slowly.

"No," said the thief.

"These are all from outside." He looked up at the shelves around them and saw it now — the dust disturbed in patterns that didn't match any archivist's route, the gaps where crystals had been shifted, the faint wrongness of things touched by someone who didn't belong. "You've been taking them out? Putting new ones in?"

"I'm *tidying*," said the thief.

"You're hiding."

The thief's jaw tightened. In the lamplight their face did something complicated — guilt and defiance fighting for the same ground — and lost to exhaustion. "Fine. Yes. I'm editing the record. There are things in my head that certain people would pay a great deal to find, and I would prefer those people continue not finding them, so I've been... redistributing."

"Redistributing your memories into our archive."

"Your archive is the single most warded repository in the known world. Nothing gets in or out without—" They stopped. Winced. "Without the wards standing. Which is becoming a problem."

Sebastian frowned. "What do you mean, becoming a—"

And that was when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Went out* — all of them, at once, through the high slit windows of the vault, as if some enormous hand had pinched the sky's wick. The darkness that followed was total and pressing, broken only by the lamp and by the crystals themselves, which had begun, all together, to glow.

They had never glowed before. Sebastian was sure of it. Two hundred years of quiet glass, and now every crystal in the vault shone like a held breath, and beneath the glow he could hear them — feel them, more than hear — a vast murmuring, thousands of stored lives stirring at once like birds before a storm.

"Oh," said the thief softly. "Oh, that's bad. That's *very* bad."

"What is it? What's happening?"

"The wards are failing." The thief was on their feet, moving along the shelves with sudden urgency, checking seals, touching stone. "The valley's containment runs off the covenant — old magic, older than your town, older than me — and the covenant is anchored to the sky. To the stars specifically. If the stars go out, then the wards—"

Above them, through three floors of ancient masonry, came a sound like the world's largest wineglass breaking.

"—go," the thief finished.

The floor shuddered. Dust sifted down through the lamplight. Somewhere far above, the great bell rang once — unstruck, unprompted, a single deep note of pure alarm — and then the escapement seized, and the silence afterward was worse than the ringing.

Sebastian's chest felt strange. Tight, and hot, and full. Something was waking up behind his ribs, something that had been sleeping so long and so quietly he had never once suspected it existed, and it stretched, and it *noticed* the broken wards the way a dog notices an open door.

"That feeling," the thief said, watching him with an expression that had gone carefully blank, "that you're having right now. We should talk about that later. Preferably never."

"What feeling? I don't have a feeling."

"Lovely. Denial. My favorite coping mechanism." They snatched back the crystal, tucked it away, and grabbed Sebastian's sleeve. "Come on."

"Come on *where*? I live here. This is my town. Whatever's happening, we should wake the council, ring the bell, get everyone to the—"

The second crack came louder than the first, and this time it wasn't distant. It was the front doors of the tower, two hundred-year-old ironwood, splitting down the middle.

Boots on stone. Voices — hard voices, accented voices, voices that had never once worried about being heard in Hollowmere because until tonight nothing from outside could enter. Torchlight spilled down the stairwell, orange and violent.

"*Council house first,*" a voice barked from above. "Take them alive. Vey wants them breathing."

The thief went white. Not frightened, exactly — worse. *Recognizing.* "Vey," they breathed. "Cassian Vey. Here. He can't be here, the wards—"

"Were the only reason he couldn't," Sebastian said. His heart was hammering, but underneath the fear, that sleeping thing behind his ribs was stretching again, curious, almost eager, and that scared him more than the boots did. "Who is Cassian Vey?"

"A man who collects people the way I collect memories." The thief's grip tightened on his arm. "And if he finds me here, he will take apart everything I am to find what he wants, and then he'll do the same to you, because you—" They caught themselves. Shut their mouth. "Never mind. Later. Never, ideally."

The boots were coming down the stairs. There was no time to be sensible. There was barely time to be afraid.

So Sebastian did the bravest and stupidest thing he had ever done in nineteen years of careful, quiet, well-oiled living.

"There's a way out through the bell chamber," he said. "The old maintenance channel. It comes out behind the millrace. But you'll never find it in the dark, and the dark is about to be the least of our problems." He pulled free of their grip, picked up his lamp, and met the thief's eyes. "I'll show you. And in exchange, you tell me everything. Who you are. What Vey wants. Why the stars went out. All of it."

"That's blackmail."

"That's *negotiation*. I'm told it's what happens outside the valley. Consider this my first lesson."

Something flickered across the thief's face — surprise, and under it, absurdly, delight, like a candle catching. "You know what? Fine. Deal. Gideon Delacroix, at your service, and yes, I'll explain the name later, there's no time—"

There was, in fact, no time. The torchlight rounded the stairwell's curve, and a man in ash-gray armor filled the landing, sword drawn, face empty in a way that made Sebastian's skin crawl — not calm, but *hollowed*, like a house with the furniture removed.

Gideon moved. Sebastian would spend a long time afterward trying to reconstruct what happened in the next four seconds: a thrown crystal that burst into a blinding bloom of somebody's wedding day, a shoulder slammed into armor plating, the hollow-eyed soldier toppling backward down six steps with a clatter like dropped cookware, and Gideon Delacroix grabbing Sebastian's wrist and shouting "*MOVE*" in a voice that admitted no discussion.

They ran.

Up, not down — up the spiral stair two at a time, past the works, past the pendulum swinging wild in its great arc, into the bell chamber where the bronze giant hung silent and the maintenance hatch gaped in the wall like a missing tooth. Cold air poured through it, smelling of river and cut grass and, underneath, something else — something electric and wild and utterly foreign, the smell of a world with no lid on it.

Sebastian hesitated at the threshold. Behind him was Hollowmere: the clocktower, the forge, Master Aldous, the festival bunting already strung across the square, the whole small warm universe he had never once thought of as small. Ahead was a hole in the world.

Behind him, below, boots were pounding up the stairs, and more voices were shouting in the square, and through the bell chamber's louvers he could see riders — dozens of them, ash-gray, pouring across the meadows toward a border that had held for two hundred years and now lay shattered and glowing like a broken window.

His home was being invaded, and the council was being seized, and the stars were gone, and something in his blood was singing at the open door like it had waited all its life for exactly this.

"Gideon," he said.

"Yes?"

"If I go through this hole with you, I'm not coming back the same person, am I."

Gideon paused halfway through the hatch, silhouetted against the impossible open dark, and for a moment all the sharpness fell away and left just a tired human face, honest for exactly as long as honesty could be afforded.

"No," they said quietly. "I don't think anyone is."

Sebastian Vale took one breath of Hollowmere air — forge smoke and festival bread and the river his whole life had run alongside — and climbed through after the thief.

* * *

The maintenance channel was a crawl of old stone behind the millrace, built by founders who had feared siege, and it let them out into the reeds below the millpond with the shouts of the occupied town muffled behind them. Gideon moved like water finding its level — fast, low, certain — and Sebastian followed, soaked to the knees, lungs burning, running on panic and a stubborn mechanical conviction that forward was at least a direction.

They reached the tree line at the valley's edge as the last ward fell.

He felt it go. Everyone in Hollowmere must have felt it — a soundless concussion, a pressure dropping, like the moment before lightning strikes. Above the border stones, the air itself tore, shimmered, and unraveled, and beyond it the night opened up: real night, wild night, thick with trees and distance and a horizon that went on forever, black and starless and enormous.

Sebastian stopped at the tree line and stared, and the thing behind his ribs pressed up against the inside of his skin and *reached*.

"Oh no," he whispered. "Oh, that's — what *is* that? What's in me?"

Gideon caught his arm, steadying him, and their expression was the expression of someone reading a sentence they'd hoped never to read again.

"Later," they said. "I promise. Everything — the name, Vey, the wards, whatever's waking up in you — later, when we're not standing in an open field with an army at our backs. Right now, we walk. Fast. Can you walk fast?"

Sebastian looked back once. Hollowmere lay in the valley below, lit by scattered torches, its bell tower dark, its festival bunting drooping in the wind. Somewhere down there, soldiers were kicking in the doors of the council house. Somewhere down there, Master Aldous was probably still asleep, and would wake to a world with no walls.

He had lived his entire life inside a snow globe, and he had only just learned the globe existed.

"Yes," Sebastian said, and turned his back on the only home he'd ever known, and walked out of the valley beside a stranger who owed him answers — into the dark, and the wild, and the rest of his life.
