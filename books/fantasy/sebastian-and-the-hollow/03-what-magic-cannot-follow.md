## 3. What Magic Cannot Follow

The first thing Gideon Delacroix learned about the world outside Hollowmere was that it had a smell.

Two hundred years of stories said the outer realms smelled of ash and rot, of courts and cruelty. Nobody mentioned that they smelled like rain on hot stone, like pine sap, like something green and growing and utterly indifferent to human suffering. Gideon crouched at the treeline where the valley's broken wards still glittered in the dark — a ring of dying light scattered across the ground like a dropped chandelier — and breathed it in, and thought: *I have been living in a jar.*

Behind them, Sebastian Vale was being quietly, thoroughly murdered by the air.

"Is it always like this?" he managed. He was on his knees in the moss with both hands pressed flat against the earth, as though the ground might anchor him. His face had gone the color of candle wax. "Like — breathing through wool? Breathing through *wet* wool?"

"It's not usually like this for anyone," Gideon said.

Because it wasn't. Gideon had crossed the border twice before, once going out and once coming back, and both times the magic of the wide world had simply flowed around them the way water flows around a stone. That was what Hollowmere's wards did, after all. They didn't keep magic *out*. They kept one particular person *in*, and everything else slid off the valley like rain off oiled canvas.

Except the wards were shattered now, scattered in glittering ruin behind them, and the magic wasn't sliding off Sebastian Vale at all. It was pouring into him.

Gideon watched it happen and felt something cold settle into their stomach, because they'd suspected this ever since the boy had touched the archive-crystals without gloves and the crystals had sung. Ambient magic clung to everyone out here — a fine mist of it, harmless, the way dust is harmless. But Sebastian stood in the mist like a dry sponge standing in a river.

"You should have told me," Sebastian gasped, "that leaving home would feel like drowning."

"I didn't know you'd drown." Which was true, and also a lie, because Gideon had known *something*, hadn't they? Had seen the way the crystals sang, had chosen not to think about it, because thinking about it would mean admitting what the boy probably was, and admitting that meant the whole plan — such as it was; Gideon's plans were more accurately described as *directions of flight* — would have to change.

Sebastian retched, dryly, magnificently. Then he looked up, hair stuck to his forehead, and said with the particular dignity of the very ill: "You're a terrible rescuer, do you know that? You didn't even bring water."

"There's a stream half a mile east."

"Then we're going to the stream." He tried to stand, swayed, and sat down again with a thump. "In a moment. The moment will come when I can walk to the stream. I'm waiting for it."

Gideon bit down on a laugh, which was inappropriate, given everything, but the boy's stubbornness was doing something to the tightness in their chest. "Here." They shrugged off their coat — a good coat, stolen from a merchant in Emberhold two years ago, lined with ward-sigils that cost them three memories of their grandmother's kitchen — and dropped it over Sebastian's shoulders. "The sigils will filter some of it. Not all. But some."

Sebastian stared at the coat. Then at Gideon. "You're giving me your coat."

"I'm lending you my coat. There's a difference, and I'll be collecting."

"Why?" The question came out raw, stripped of the fever. "Why help me? You could've run alone. Faster, lighter. Instead you're dragging a sick clockmaker's apprentice through the woods in the middle of the night while armed riders burn down my town."

And there it was. The question Gideon had been avoiding since the clocktower, since the moment they'd grabbed a stranger's wrist and said *come with me or die here*, and the stranger — fool, absolute fool — had come.

"Because," Gideon said, "you saw my face, and I'd rather have you where I can watch you than where Cassian Vey's riders can find you."

It was a good lie. It had structure, motive, a satisfying cynicism. Sebastian looked at them for a long moment, and Gideon could see him turning it over, weighing it, finding the seams.

"That's not it," he said.

"No," Gideon admitted. "But it'll do for tonight."

* * *

They made the stream by moonrise — no stars, of course; the stars had gone out two nights ago and the sky above the trees was a smooth black nothing, like a ceiling painted over. Sebastian drank until his shivering eased, then slept like the dead beneath the sigil-lined coat, and Gideon sat up keeping watch and did not sleep at all, because sleep was where the memories lived, and lately the memories had teeth.

*Emberhold, six years ago. The market square under a white-hot sun. Gideon, nineteen years old, holding their little sister's hand too tightly, watching the Ashen Court's harvesters move down the line of petitioners with their silver extraction-crowns gleaming.*

*"One memory per family member," the harvester had said, bored, kind in the way that only true indifference is kind. "Standard tithe. Choose carefully. The crown takes what you offer — nothing more, nothing less. We are not thieves."*

*Mira had offered her memory of their mother's lullabies. She'd said it was the one she could spare. She'd been eight years old, and she'd smiled while she chose it, because she wanted to be brave, and afterward she'd looked at their mother's face in the crowd and asked, "Who is that lady?"*

*Their parents had fought. Of course they had. Their father had put himself between the harvesters and Mira, and the harvester had sighed and taken his memory of her birth instead, and when their mother had screamed, they'd taken hers too, and then, because the family had become "disruptive," the whole family had been remanded to the Court's bonded service — smiling, empty people in gray livery who waved at Gideon as they were led away, because they didn't remember why they shouldn't wave.*

*Gideon had run. Nineteen years old, one bag, one skill. And the skill was theft — not of purses, purses were beneath them — of memories. It turned out a person who could slip into an archive-crystal vault could also slip into the gray market, could sell slivers of their own past to collectors who paid in coin and asked no questions. A summer afternoon at the lake: four silver marks. Their grandmother teaching them to pick a lock: seven. The smell of bread in their mother's kitchen: nine, because everyone wanted kitchens, nobody could make them anymore.*

*They'd told themselves it was temporary. Survival first, rescue later. They'd get Mira back. They'd buy their parents free. Every sale bought a little closer to enough money, enough leverage, enough* something.

*Six years later, Gideon Delacroix owned a very good coat, a set of lockpicks, a false name, and not one single memory of their mother's face.*

Gideon sat by the stream with their knees drawn up and did the arithmetic again, the same arithmetic they did every night, which always came out the same: *everything I loved, spent one piece at a time, and nothing purchased.* Somewhere in Emberhold, Mira was waving at strangers. Somewhere in the gray market, a collector owned the sound of their father laughing and didn't even know whose father it had been.

And now — now there was a boy asleep ten feet away who made archive-crystals sing, who had walked out of a containment valley the night its wards broke, who was quite possibly the lastborn of the covenant line, the thing every faction in the outer realms had spent two hundred years pretending didn't exist so they wouldn't have to admit how badly they wanted it.

Gideon knew what they should do. The smart play, the survival play, was the one they'd been running for six years: keep moving, stay small, tell no one anything. Take the boy somewhere quiet, teach him to shield whatever was waking inside him, and never, ever let the word *covenant* pass between them.

But the smart play had a flaw, and the flaw was currently snoring gently into Gideon's borrowed coat, and had come with them *without hesitation*, a stranger, into a collapsing night full of armed riders, because Gideon had asked. Because that was apparently the kind of person Sebastian Vale was. The kind who opened doors for thieves and trusted liars and got sick on magic he didn't ask for.

*I'm going to get him killed,* Gideon thought. Or worse: *I'm going to fail to, and watch someone else do it properly.*

Across the dead fire pit, Sebastian stirred. "Gideon?"

"Sleep."

"Can't." He sat up slowly, wincing. The fever-color had faded; the sigils were working. "My head's too loud. Everything's — humming. Is that normal? Tell me that's normal."

"It's not normal for anyone else," Gideon said. "For you, I'm starting to suspect it's the most normal thing about you."

"What does that mean?"

And here it was, then. The conversation Gideon had been circling since the clocktower, since the wards shattered, since the moment in the archive when the crystals had sung under the boy's bare fingers and Gideon had felt six years of careful ignorance begin to crack.

"It means," Gideon said, "that you should hear what Hollowmere actually is. Not the festival version. The real one."

Sebastian hugged the coat around his shoulders and waited.

"Every child in that valley grows up on the same story," Gideon said. "Hollowmere is a sanctuary. Your ancestors fled the wars of the outer realms, and the founders raised the wards to keep the magic out, because magic corrupts, magic devours, magic is the reason the stars went dark over the rest of the world. Sanctuary. Refuge. A blessing." They turned a pebble over between their fingers. "It's a lovely story. It's even mostly true. The wards do keep magic out."

"But?"

"But wards that keep magic out of a valley are just walls. And walls don't care whether they're protecting the people inside or imprisoning them." Gideon tossed the pebble into the stream. "Your valley isn't a sanctuary, Sebastian. It's a container. The strongest one ever built. And containers aren't built for nothing — they're built for *contents*."

The silence stretched. Somewhere an owl called, and got no answer, because owls presumably also noticed the missing stars.

"My parents died in a cart accident," Sebastian said at last. Quietly. "When I was a baby. The whole village knows it. Mistress Corvane took me in."

"Mm," Gideon said. "And does the whole village also know why a cart accident left no bodies? Why no one ever found the wreck? Why an orphan with no family records whatsoever was simply... accepted, by a town that hasn't let a single outsider cross its border in two hundred years?"

Sebastian's jaw worked. "You're saying you know something about my parents."

"I'm saying I know something about your *valley*, and your valley knows something about you, and neither of you has ever told you the truth." Gideon held up a hand before the boy could speak. "Not tonight. Tonight you need sleep, and I need to figure out which direction doesn't have riders in it. But tomorrow — tomorrow I'll tell you what I know. All of it. You've earned that much, walking out of your whole life on my say-so."

"You don't owe me honesty," Sebastian said. "You owe me a decent night's sleep and possibly a new coat."

"And yet." Gideon looked away, toward the black sky, because it was easier than looking at him. "Where I come from, people pay for things with pieces of themselves. Memories, mostly. It's the only currency the Court respects. I've been paying my whole life, and I've learned the hard way that the debts you hide grow the fastest. So. Consider this a payment on account."

Sebastian was quiet for a long time. When Gideon glanced over, the boy was watching them with an expression Gideon couldn't name and didn't enjoy — something soft and searching, like he was reading a locked diary through the cover.

"Gideon," he said. "Who did you lose?"

The question landed exactly where Gideon least wanted anything to land. They thought of Mira's hand in theirs, small and sticky. *Who is that lady?*

"People," Gideon said. "The usual number. Too many."

Sebastian nodded slowly, and — this was the part that undid Gideon, this was the part they'd be turning over for weeks — he didn't push. He just lay back down, pulled the coat to his chin, and said, "Tomorrow, then. Both debts. Yours and mine."

"Deal."

"And Gideon? The humming stopped. Mostly. Whatever you gave me—" he lifted the coat's edge, "—it helped. Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Gideon muttered. "I'm almost certainly going to get us both killed."

"Probably," Sebastian agreed comfortably, and was asleep within minutes, breathing slow and even, the hum of the world finally settling around him instead of through him.

Gideon kept watch until the sky went gray at the edges, and told themself the tightness in their chest was just exhaustion.

Near dawn, they heard it: hoofbeats, distant but deliberate, moving along the ridgeline to the south. Not random patrol movement. A search pattern. Someone knew roughly where they were.

Gideon stood, shouldered their pack, and woke the boy with a boot toe and zero apology.

"Up. Riders on our trail, and I know where we're going."

"Where?"

"Emberhold," Gideon said grimly, already hating it. "Seat of the Ashen Court. Home of everything I've spent six years running from." They pulled Sebastian upright by the collar of the borrowed coat. "Which means it's the last place anyone will think to look for us. Probably. If we're very lucky. Move."

Sebastian stumbled after them into the pale morning, muttering something about terrible rescuers and wet wool, and Gideon ran ahead and did not look back at the place where the valley had been, because if they looked, they might see the glittering ruin of the wards and understand, finally, that they hadn't escaped anything at all.

They'd just brought the contents with them.
