## 4. The Court of Cinders

The road to Emberhold was paved, which was the first wrong thing about it.

Sebastian had spent two days learning that the world outside Hollowmere was mostly mud, thorn, and Gideon Delacroix's relentless pessimism. So when the track beneath their boots smoothed into slate the color of cooled charcoal — fitted stones, swept clean, not a weed between them — he stopped walking.

"Roads don't get *swept*," he said.

"Roads leading to courts do." Gideon didn't stop. They'd pulled the hood of their travel cloak low over their eyes and kept it there since dawn, shoulders curled inward like a snail deciding whether to bother with its shell at all. "Keep up. And Sebastian — whatever you see in there, whatever anyone says to you — don't answer questions. You're my mute cousin from the fen country. Your name is Bram. You're very dull."

"I'm not dull."

"You are now. Dull people live long lives in Emberhold. Interesting ones end up on display."

Sebastian jogged to catch up. "What kind of display?"

"The kind with glass cases."

He decided not to ask further, mostly because his stomach had chosen that moment to remind him they hadn't eaten since a handful of bruised plums some thief-friend of Gideon's had shoved at them outside a tollhouse. The plums had been the first food he'd eaten since leaving the valley, and he'd nearly wept over them, which Gideon had pretended not to notice with the practiced skill of someone who noticed everything.

That was another thing he'd learned: magic saturated everything out here, but hunger was exactly the same as it had been in Hollowmere. That was almost comforting.

Emberhold announced itself before they saw it. First came the smell — woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, like sugar left too long over a flame. Then the light, a deep amber glow staining the clouds above the hills. Then the hills themselves parted, and there it was.

The city rose out of a caldera like a candle burning inside a black cup. Every building was dark stone shot through with veins of ember-light, as if the whole city were one enormous coal refusing to go out. At its center stood a tower — no, not a tower, a *spire*, tall and slender and crowned with a flame that burned without flickering, perfectly still, like a painting of fire rather than fire itself.

"It's beautiful," Sebastian said, before he could stop himself.

"It's a furnace wearing a crown," Gideon muttered. "Stay close. Eyes down."

* * *

They passed through the gates without being stopped, which should have been the second warning. Guards stood along the gatehouse walls in armor of blackened steel trimmed with copper, and each of them turned to watch Sebastian pass — not Gideon, him — their heads moving in slow unison, like sunflowers tracking something brighter than the sun.

Gideon's elbow found his ribs. "*Eyes down,* Bram."

"Sorry. Bram. Mute. Dull."

But he couldn't help one last glance back, and what he saw made his skin prickle: every guard had returned to staring forward, all at once, as if nothing had happened. As if they'd never looked at anything at all.

Inside, the city was worse and better than he'd expected. Better because it was alive — market stalls strung with lanterns, children chasing a mechanical bird that whirred and glittered through the air, musicians playing instruments that hummed harmonies with themselves. Worse because of how the people moved. There was a smoothness to it, a lack of argument. No haggling in the markets; prices sat on little brass plaques and were paid without comment. No children squabbling; they chased the bird with identical delighted laughter, dozens of mouths opening on the same sound.

"Gideon," he whispered. "Why does everyone look—"

"Bonded." Gideon's voice came flat and quiet under the hood. "Memory-bonded. It's how the Ashen Court keeps order. They take pieces of people — the sharp edges, mostly. Anger. Ambition. Grief that won't sit still. What's left is pleasant and obedient and doesn't ask why the price is carved in brass."

"That's—" Sebastian searched for the word and found only the ugliest one. "That's theft."

"Yes," said Gideon, in a voice that suggested the conversation was over. But their hand had drifted up to press briefly against their own temple, and Sebastian, watching, thought of the archive-crystals in the Hollowmere clocktower, and the thief who'd been crouched among them in the dark, and said nothing after all.

* * *

They had a plan, of sorts. Gideon always had a plan of sorts, usually delivered in a rapid murmur while walking briskly: enter the lower city, find a fence named Orla Quill who owed Gideon "a complicated favor," acquire papers and provisions, and be gone from Emberhold within two days, because "no one stays near the Court longer than they have to unless they want to leave in pieces."

The plan survived until the fountain.

It stood in a plaza at the heart of the lower city, a wide basin of black stone where water rose and fell in shapes that couldn't be water — spirals, then birds, then letters, dissolving before they could be read. Crowds had gathered around it, silent, watching. Sebastian watched too, and felt the strangest ache, because he could almost read the letters, could almost —

"There you are."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm as banked coals, and the entire plaza turned toward it. Even the water stopped mid-shape.

Descending the broad steps of the spire came a man dressed simply — or what would have been simply, on anyone else. Grey wool, plain boots, no jewelry. But the grey wool fit like it had been woven around him, and the air near him shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers above a forge. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with a face that would have been called kind in any portrait: laugh lines, soft eyes, the easy smile of a man who has never once doubted his welcome anywhere.

Every bonded servant in the plaza bowed. Not knelt — bowed, in unison, like wheat before wind.

Sebastian realized, distantly, that Gideon had gone rigid beside him, and that Gideon's hand had closed on his sleeve with a grip that meant *run*, and that his own legs — traitorous, curious legs — had not moved at all.

Lord Cassian Vey crossed the plaza unhurried and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Sebastian could feel the warmth rolling off him, gentle and enormous, like standing near an open oven door in winter.

"You walked past my guards," Cassian said, and his smile deepened by exactly one degree. "All forty of them. Do you know how long it's been since anyone did that?"

"I—" Sebastian's mouth, unschooled in dullness, answered before Bram the mute could intervene. "I didn't do anything."

"No," Cassian agreed pleasantly. "You didn't. That's rather the point." His gaze moved over Sebastian slowly, not the way men appraised strangers in Hollowmere's market — checking for weapons, for lies — but the way a clockmaker looks at a mechanism, finding every gear already turning, every spring already wound. "May I?"

He extended a hand, palm up. An invitation. A question. Sebastian heard Gideon breathe in sharply beside him.

And Sebastian — orphan, apprentice, boy who had spent seventeen years invisible in a valley that existed specifically so that things like this man could never see him — took the hand.

Warmth flooded up his arm. Not painful. The opposite of pain. It felt like the first sip of tea on a cold morning, like the moment a stubborn clock finally ticks, like being *recognized*. Somewhere in his chest, the thing that had woken on the night the stars went out rolled over and purred.

Cassian's expression flickered — just once, just slightly, the smile thinning into something older and hungrier — and then it was back, warmer than ever.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, well, well. Where did you find him?" This last was directed past Sebastian at Gideon, whose hood had slipped back in the crowd's bowing, exposing a face that had gone pale as bone.

"He's no one," Gideon said. "A cousin. We're passing through."

"Mm." Cassian released Sebastian's hand. The absence of the warmth was startling, like stepping out of sunlight. "Passing through. To where, I wonder? Everyone passes *through* Emberhold. Nobody ever quite manages to pass *out* of it again, but I admire the optimism." He clasped his hands behind his back and regarded them both with the benevolence of a landlord surveying tenants. "Here is what will happen. You will be guests tonight. Rooms, dinner, baths — you smell like the fen country, cousin Bram, I hope you'll forgive me saying so. Tomorrow you may continue your journey with my blessing and my protection, which in this world is worth more than gold."

"And if we'd rather not be guests?" Gideon asked.

Cassian's smile did not waver, but the temperature of the plaza seemed to drop several degrees. Around them, forty bonded faces continued smiling their identical smiles.

"Then you'd be the first people in eleven years to refuse me hospitality," he said, "and I confess I've forgotten what that feels like. It might be interesting. I do collect interesting things." He turned back to Sebastian, and his voice gentled into something almost paternal. "Rest, young man. Eat well. You've come a long way, even if you don't yet know how far — or from where. Some part of you does know. I can hear it ticking."

He knew about clocks. The observation landed strangely, intimately, a fingertip pressed to the exact center of Sebastian's chest.

"Dinner is at the eighth bell," Cassian said, and strolled away up the steps toward his spire of still-burning flame, servants peeling off to follow him like iron filings after a magnet. The plaza exhaled. The fountain resumed its impossible letters. Life flowed back into Emberhold as though nothing had happened, because for the bonded, Sebastian understood, *nothing had* — there was no fear to resume, no memory of it.

"*Bram*," Gideon said through their teeth, hauling him by the arm toward a side street. "Mute. *Dull.*"

"He offered us dinner," Sebastian protested. "What was I supposed to do, bite him?"

"A reasonable first option, yes."

* * *

The rooms Cassian gave them were beautiful, which was the third wrong thing about the day, and by now Sebastian had learned to count.

They occupied a corner chamber high in one of the spire's lesser towers: two beds with quilts soft as cloud, a window overlooking the caldera, a washbasin whose water steamed itself, and a plate of small cakes that refreshed whenever no one was looking. Everything smelled faintly of woodsmoke and sugar.

"We leave now," Gideon said, pacing. "Out the window, down the wall, through the lower city before the eighth bell. Orla Quill owes me—"

"Gideon."

"—and we can be over the rim by midnight if we push—"

"*Gideon.*" Sebastian was sitting on the windowsill, looking down at the glowing city, doing arithmetic he didn't like. "There are wards on this tower. Can't you feel them? I can feel them, and I've had magic for four days. It's like — like standing in front of a locked door and hearing the lock think."

Gideon stopped pacing. Closed their eyes. Sebastian watched them feel for it, watched their jaw tighten.

"...Fine. Then we wait until they bring dinner, overpower whoever carries the tray—"

"And then what? Walk out through forty guards who all turn their heads when I pass? Gideon." He said it gently, which surprised even himself. "He let us go. You saw it. He could have kept us — kept *me* — and he just... smiled and gave us cake. Why?"

"I don't know, and that's what terrifies me. Men like Vey don't give anything without a reason, and the bigger the gift, the bigger the hook inside it." Gideon dropped onto the edge of the far bed, and for a moment the mask slipped — exhaustion showing through, and something rawer underneath. "When he touched you, Sebastian. What did it feel like?"

Sebastian considered lying, decided against it, and was startled to realize why: because somewhere in the last four days, without either of them signing anything, they had become the sort of people who told each other the truth at night in strange rooms.

"It felt like being seen," he said quietly. "My whole life, I've been — background. The apprentice. The orphan. People looked *through* me. And he looked at me like..." He flexed the hand Cassian had held. The warmth still lingered there, faint as sunset. "Like I was the most interesting thing he'd collected in years. And the worst part, Gideon — the part I keep trying not to say out loud — is that I liked it."

Gideon studied him for a long moment.

"I know," they said, and there was no judgment in it, only a tired kindness that made Sebastian's throat tighten. "Power's like that. The first taste is free. That's how every trap in history works."

* * *

The knock came at the eighth bell precisely.

Sebastian opened the door expecting a tray-bearing servant. Instead, a girl of perhaps sixteen stood alone in the corridor, holding a covered dish, her livery immaculate, her face — Sebastian searched it carefully — her face *wrong*. Not empty, like the others. Her eyes moved. They were red-rimmed, and they darted from his face to the hallway behind him and back with quick, desperate calculation.

She stepped past him into the room before he could decide whether to allow it, set the dish on the table, and lifted the cover to reveal roast fowl and glazed roots arranged with surgical precision.

"Eat," she said, in a voice pitched barely above breath. "Both of you. The lord watches what his guests refuse."

Gideon was on their feet, a knife somehow already in their hand. The girl didn't flinch. She looked at the knife the way one looks at weather.

"You're not bonded," Sebastian said.

"Not entirely." Her hands folded neatly in front of her, the picture of a servant at ease, while her eyes did all the screaming. "They took the loud parts. They missed the quiet ones." She glanced toward the door, then back, and her voice dropped further. "You're going to run tomorrow. Everyone runs tomorrow. Don't take the north road — the wards sing there, and the lord hears everything the wards sing."

"How do you know we—"

"I carried your linens up myself. I know the smell of people who mean to run." Something flickered across her face — grief, maybe, or its fossilized remnant. "Listen. Before the covenant broke, there were three keystones. Three locks on the door between the realms. The lord holds one — you'll see it, it sits above his throne like a crown jewel, and it *eats*. The other two were scattered when the stars went out. Water and stone. If you're what I think you are, you'll need them, and you'll need to be gone before he decides you're ready to be useful instead of merely interesting."

Sebastian's mouth went dry. "What do you think I am?"

The girl looked at him then, really looked, and for one unguarded instant she was not a servant at all but a person, terrified and fierce and utterly alone.

"I think," she said, "that Lord Cassian Vey has waited eleven years for you, and that you should be very, very grateful he wants you whole." She straightened, smoothed her apron, and became a servant again, seamless as a clock's face closing over its gears. "Enjoy your dinner. Ring if you need anything. Someone will always come."

She was gone before he could ask her name.

Gideon stood motionless by the window, knife still in hand, watching the corridor through the gap in the door. When they finally turned, their expression had changed — stripped of irony, stripped of everything except a grim, dawning clarity, the look of a gambler who has just realized the game started long before they sat down.

"Three keystones," Gideon said. "Water and stone. And the third one sitting on that monster's throne." They laughed once, without any humor whatsoever. "Of course. Of *course* it's never simple with you."

"Me? None of this is my fault!"

"No," Gideon agreed, pulling their pack onto the bed and beginning, methodically, to repack it. "It's nobody's fault. It's prophecy's fault, or the covenant's fault, or the stars' fault. But somehow, Sebastian Vale, the entire architecture of two realms appears to be balanced on the point of your ridiculous head, and tomorrow we are going to walk down into a court full of smiling thieves and steal a keystone out from under the man who owns everyone in it." They yanked a strap tight. "So eat your chicken. You'll want your strength."

Sebastian looked down at the roast fowl, gleaming and perfect and paid for by no one.

Outside the window, the spire's crown-flame burned on, motionless, patient — and for the first time he understood that it wasn't decoration. It was a promise. Somewhere below it, a man who collected interesting things was waiting for morning with the contentment of someone who has already checked every exit, counted every guard, and wound every clock.

He ate the chicken. It was delicious.

That was the fourth wrong thing about the day, and he was starting to lose count.
