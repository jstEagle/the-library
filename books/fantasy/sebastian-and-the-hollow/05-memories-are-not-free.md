## 5. Memories Are Not Free

The ferryman's name was Brannock, and he had the eyes of a man who had counted every coin in the world twice and found them all wanting.

"The crossing to Mirrormere," he said, "is forty crowns."

Gideon looked at their purse. The purse, in fairness, looked back — if a purse could look empty, this one did it with flair. Three coppers and a button that might once have been silver, in better days, on a different coat.

"Thirty-five," Gideon said, out of habit rather than hope.

"Forty," said Brannock, "for you and the boy and the girl who keeps biting my deckhands."

"She doesn't bite."

Wren, perched on the piling behind them, bared her teeth at the nearest deckhand with the sincerity of a street cat defending a fish head. He took a prudent step back.

"Forty," Brannock repeated, unmoved. "Or you swim. The lake's deep enough that you'd have plenty of time to reconsider your budget on the way down."

Gideon did the arithmetic they had been avoiding since Emberhold. Room for the night: spent. Food for three: spent, mostly on Wren, who ate like a woodstove eats kindling. What remained would buy passage for one person across Lake Vesper to the drowned city — one person, arriving alone, with two companions waving from shore.

There were other ways. There was always one other way.

Gideon felt it sitting in their chest like a stone they'd swallowed years ago and never quite passed: the memory-broker's stall at the end of the market lane, its striped awning faded to the pink of an old scar. They had walked past it four times already today. Each time, they had walked a little slower.

"Give me an hour," Gideon said.

* * *

The broker's stall smelled of lavender and copper, which was the smell of memory extraction, though no one told you that up front. Lavender to soothe. Copper because memories, being electrical in their way, liked metal, and the extraction pans were polished until they gleamed like small moons.

The broker was a round woman named Odile Fenwick with spectacles on a chain and the gentle manner of someone who had learned that kindness moved more product than menace. She recognized Gideon on sight.

"Well," she said, folding her hands. "Gideon Delacroix. I wondered when you'd come back through. It's been — what, two years?"

"Three."

"Three. And what are we selling today?" Her eyes went over Gideon the way a tailor's go over cloth — assessing, measuring, finding the seams. "You've thinned. I can see it from here. You've been selling the bright things, haven't you? Everyone does, at first. The bright things fetch the best price."

"I know how it works, Odile."

"Of course you do." She gestured to the chair beside the extraction pan — a worn wooden thing with arms, shaped like a confession booth that had given up on religion. "Sit or don't. But sit knowing I'll ask what you're selling before I pay, and I'll pay honestly, and I won't pretend it isn't what it is."

"And what is it?"

Odile adjusted her spectacles. "A funeral with a cash box," she said.

* * *

Here is what Gideon remembered of their mother:

Not her face — faces went early, sold off in the first desperate year, along with the sound of her voice and the specific weight of her hand on Gideon's hair. What survived was smaller and stranger. A kitchen in autumn. The smell of apples cooking down with too much cinnamon because she always measured wrong and refused to admit it. The particular squeak of the third floorboard, the one she'd promised to fix for six years. And the laughing — not a sound anymore, Gideon couldn't hear her laugh, but the *shape* of it, the knowledge of how it lived in her shoulders, how she laughed with her whole body the way other people argued.

And this: Sunday mornings, when she would stand at the window with her tea going cold and say, "Look at the light coming through the glass, Gid. You could put that light in a bottle and sell it and be rich." And young Gideon, seven years old and literal-minded, would ask why she didn't, and she would laugh — the shape of it — and say, "Because some things are only worth having if you give them away."

That was the memory. Small as a seed. Warm as bread.

It was worth, according to Odile Fenwick's price list, sixty crowns.

* * *

Sebastian found them there.

Later, Gideon would replay the moment many times — lying awake in ferry berths and inn beds and once, memorably, a haystack — and each time they would arrive at the same unbearable conclusion: they had wanted him to find them. Some traitorous corner of them had heard his footsteps in the market crowd and angled their body toward the sound like a plant toward sun, so that when he pushed through the bead curtain into the lavender-and-copper smell of the stall, he would see. So that someone would see.

"Gideon?" Sebastian stood in the doorway with Wren peering around his elbow, both of them dusted from the road. His eyes went from the extraction pan to the chair to Gideon sitting in it. "What is this place?"

"A shop," said Odile pleasantly. "Can I help you, love?"

"What do you sell?"

"Memories, dear. We buy and sell. Best prices on the coast for childhood summers, first loves, and anything with music in it." She said it the way a baker describes loaves.

Sebastian's face did something complicated. "People *sell* their memories?"

"People sell everything eventually," said Odile. "Memories just happen to grow back thinner each time. Like grass. Or hope." She turned back to Gideon, all business now, because business was kinder than waiting. "Well, Delacroix? You came in here for something. What's the merchandise?"

And here was the moment. Here was where Gideon Delacroix, professional thief of other people's pasts, should have lied — should have smiled the smile that had gotten them out of a hundred tighter rooms and named some worthless trifle, a bad winter, a stranger's wedding, and walked out with thirty crowns and their mother's apples-and-cinnamon intact.

Instead they heard themselves say: "Sunday morning. Kitchen. My mother at the window."

Odile went still. Even Wren stopped fidgeting.

"You're sure?" the broker asked quietly.

"No," said Gideon. "Yes. Do it before I'm sensible."

"Gideon—" Sebastian started forward.

"Forty crowns gets us across the lake," Gideon said, and hated how reasonable their own voice sounded. "All three of us. That's the arithmetic, Sebastian. It's just arithmetic."

"That's not—" Sebastian looked at the pan, the chair, the neat little price list nailed to the wall, and Gideon watched him do the math himself, watch him arrive at the shape of what this place actually was. "You've done this before."

It wasn't a question. Gideon didn't insult him by pretending otherwise.

"How many times?" Sebastian asked.

"Does it matter?"

"Yes."

Gideon looked at the ceiling. Counted. It took longer than they expected; the count kept snagging on things. "I stopped counting at twelve," they said finally. "That seemed wise."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren, for once, said nothing at all, but her hand crept out and gripped the hem of Sebastian's coat, and she stared at Gideon with those feral, too-old eyes, and Gideon thought: *she knows. Whatever she lost to survive out there on her mountain, she knows exactly what this shop is.*

"All right," Sebastian said at last, and his voice had gone careful, the way you speak near a wound. "But I'm staying in the room."

"This isn't a spectator sport—"

"I'm staying," he said, "in the room."

* * *

The extraction itself was almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it — the whole industry built on this, entire markets fed by it, and the act itself took less time than tying a boot.

Odile fitted a circlet of thin copper wire around Gideon's temples, warm from the previous customer, and asked them to think of the memory — really think, hold it whole. Gideon held it. Apples. Cinnamon measured wrong. The third-floorboard squeak. The cold tea. The light in the glass worth bottling. *Some things are only worth having if you give them away.*

Mother, Gideon thought, I'm about to make a liar of you.

"There will be a pulling sensation," Odile murmured. "Like a splinter leaving. Then a small absence where the thing used to be. Don't fight the absence. Fighting makes it ache longer."

She turned a brass valve. The copper hummed.

It didn't hurt. That was the second obscenity. It felt like a long exhale after holding your breath underwater, like setting down luggage you'd forgotten you were carrying — relief and loss braided so tightly together you couldn't tell which was which. The kitchen dimmed. The apples cooled. The light through the window guttered like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and then—

Then it was simply not there.

Gideon sat in the chair in a shop smelling of lavender and copper, and there was a smooth, blank space inside them the exact size and shape of a Sunday morning, and they knew — abstractly, the way you know a country exists that you've never visited — that something had been taken out. They knew it had mattered. They knew this because they were crying.

They touched their own cheek, genuinely puzzled. Tears, running freely down their face, dripping off their chin onto their coat, and no reason for any of it. No image attached. No name. Just grief, orphaned from its object, rolling down their face like rain down a window with nothing outside.

"Why am I crying?" Gideon asked. Their voice sounded far away.

Odile's hands hovered, helpless, over the pan where a small silver-white shimmer coiled like breath on cold glass. "Oh, love," she said softly. "That happens, sometimes. The feeling runs deeper than the picture. We can take the memory but the roots—" She stopped herself. Professional. She counted sixty crowns onto the counter instead, and her hands weren't quite steady either, and Gideon understood that Odile Fenwick had roots of her own somewhere, pulled clean out.

Sixty crowns. Forty for the ferry. Twenty left over for food and whatever came next, purchased with a kitchen Gideon would now walk past in their own mind without ever turning their head.

"Delacroix," Odile said as they rose. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — whoever taught you that light-through-glass trick had excellent taste in lessons."

Gideon froze halfway to the door. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Odile agreed sadly. "You wouldn't."

* * *

Outside, the market noise hit like weather. Sebastian walked beside them in silence for half the lane, and Gideon kept wiping their face and being surprised each time the sleeve came away wet, a fresh little shock every time, a grief with no address.

Finally Sebastian said, "What was it? If you don't mind my asking."

"I don't remember."

"Gideon."

"That's the honest answer." Gideon laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, waterlogged. "That's the whole joke of the profession, Sebastian. I'm a memory-thief who can't hold onto a single one of their own. Everything I ever stole, I stole to fill holes I dug myself. And the holes don't fill. They just — wait."

Sebastian stopped walking. Wren bumped into him and glared up at both of them with the affront of the interrupted.

"You're crying," Sebastian said.

"Am I?" Gideon wiped again, absurdly. "Must be the wind."

"It isn't windy."

"Then it must be you. You have a very moving face."

"Gideon." He caught their wrist — gently, the way you catch someone on ice. "Whatever it was, I'm sorry. And I want you to know I noticed. Whatever it cost — I noticed."

And that, of all things, was nearly worse than the extraction. Because Gideon could lose a Sunday morning, a kitchen, a woman's laugh-shaped joy, and keep walking — that was just survival, survival was a trade they'd mastered — but being *seen* doing it, being witnessed by this strange earnest clockmaker's apprentice who still flinched at prices, that got under the wire armor and lodged there like a splinter no copper circlet could pull.

"Don't," Gideon said quietly. "Don't notice me. Notice the road, notice Wren, notice literally anything else. People who notice me end up paying for it."

"Too late," said Sebastian. "I've seen the invoice."

Wren tugged Sebastian's coat. "Why's the thief leaking?"

"It's the wind," said Gideon.

"There's no wind," said Wren and Sebastian, in perfect unison.

* * *

Brannock took the forty crowns with the expression of a man disappointed not to ruin anyone's day, and the ferry slid out onto Lake Vesper as the afternoon went gold. Wren claimed the bow immediately and hung over the rail watching fish. Sebastian settled beside Gideon amidships, close enough to be company, far enough to be polite.

The lake was beautiful in the way large bodies of water are permitted to be — all that light scattered and forgiven. Somewhere beneath it lay Mirrormere, the drowned city, though drowned wasn't quite the word the locals used. *Sunken* implied accident. The old-timers on the dock had called it *given*, which was somehow worse.

"Tell me about it," Sebastian said. "Mirrormere. You've been, haven't you?"

"Once. Years ago." Gideon watched the water. "Before your valley, before the wards. It was a covenant city, one of the anchor-towns where the old magic pooled. When the covenant started failing — really failing, centuries ago, not just lately — the keeper order there made a choice. Rather than let the city's keystone fall into the wrong hands during the wars, they sank the whole district. Intentionally. With everyone in it, depending on which historian you believe, or with everyone safely evacuated, depending on which historian you'd rather believe."

"Which do you believe?"

"I believe the water remembers them." Gideon nodded at the lake. "Drowned cities keep their dead lively, in my experience. You'll see."

Sebastian was quiet a moment. Then: "The keystone. The first one. It's down there."

"So the servant in Emberhold whispered, and so says every rumor from here to the Spire, and rumors are usually wrong except when they're expensive." Gideon flexed their fingers. The ghost-ache of the copper circlet still ringed their temples. "We'll need a diver, or a miracle, or — well. You may have noticed the water did something odd around you on the dock."

Sebastian blinked. "It rippled."

"It bowed," said Gideon. "Toward you. Like a dog greeting its master. I've been meaning to mention it, but you seemed busy being alarmed about other things."

Behind them, Wren called out — "Fishes! Big ones! They're all swimming the same direction!" — and Gideon turned to look, and they did see it: below the hull, a broad column of fish wheeling as one, and not following the boat, not fleeing it, but streaming in a long silver ribbon toward a point ahead and below, toward the drowned city, the way iron filings line up around a lodestone.

Or around something newly woken.

Sebastian leaned over the rail beside Wren, and the lake's light came up and lay across his face, and Gideon — watching, unnoticed, grieving something they could no longer name — thought: *the water bows to him, the fish run to him, and Cassian Vey saw it too, the day he let us walk out of Emberhold alive.*

Twenty crowns and a hole in their heart, Gideon thought. Cheap, considering everything else the world was planning to charge.

Ahead, far down through the gold-green water, something pale and vast shifted — rooftops, maybe, or ribs — and the fish streamed on toward it like pilgrims.

"We'll need a diver," Gideon said again, softer.

"Or a miracle," said Sebastian, without looking up from the water.

He was smiling. That was the part that frightened Gideon most.
