## 6. The Drowned City

The road to Mirrormere smelled of rain that had fallen a hundred years ago and never quite dried.

Sebastian noticed it first as a dampness in his lungs, then as a green-black shimmer between the hills — a valley within the valley, filled to its rooftops with water so still it looked poured rather than gathered. The drowned city lay beneath it like a sleeping thing: spires and bell towers breaking the surface at odd angles, their crowns furred with pale moss, windows staring up through the flood like the eyes of something patient.

"It used to be called the Jewel of the Lowlands," Gideon said, reining their borrowed mule to a stop on the ridge above. "Before the covenant-keepers flooded it themselves."

"They *flooded* it?"

"To hide what was inside it." Gideon's voice had gone flat in the way it always did when they were choosing which truths to hand over and which to keep buttoned under their coat. "A city is easy to search. A lake is not."

Sebastian studied them sidelong. In the week since Hollowmere — since the stars went out and the wards shattered and he'd run from armed riders with a memory-thief's cold fingers wrapped around his wrist — he had learned to read Gideon Delacroix the way he'd once learned to read clockwork: by what moved, by what hesitated, by the small catches in the mechanism where something had been filed down or replaced. Gideon gave him facts the way a miser gave coins, one at a time, each one counted twice.

But they had also, two nights ago, given him half their blanket without being asked, and grumbled about it so thoroughly that Sebastian understood it for exactly what it was: kindness wearing a disguise, because kindness felt too exposed to wear openly.

"Can you swim?" Gideon asked.

"I grew up in a valley with three rivers and a clockmaker who believed baths were a moral obligation." Sebastian swung down from the mule. "Can you?"

"I can *not* drown," Gideon said, with the particular precision of someone answering a different question than the one asked. "There's a difference, and you'll see it shortly."

* * *

They made camp that night on a shelf of stone above the waterline, beneath the ribs of an old boathouse whose roof had long since become a garden of reeds. Gideon built a fire small enough to cup in two hands and cooked river fish with a competence that surprised Sebastian until he remembered — remembered being told, rather — that Gideon had survived alone for years after the Ashen Court took their family. Some skills were carved into a person by necessity, and Gideon wore theirs the way Sebastian wore calluses from the lathe: without thinking, without pride, without ever quite looking at them directly.

"The ghosts," Sebastian said, when the fish was gone and the fire had settled to embers. "You said the streets hold ghosts. Actual ones? Or the poetic kind?"

Gideon poked the coals. "Actual. The covenant-keepers bound themselves to Mirrormere when they sank it. Wardens don't leave their work unfinished." A pause. "They won't hurt you."

"You say that very confidently for someone who also said the last guardian wouldn't hurt me, right before it tried to eat my memories out of my skull."

"That guardian fed on memory. These are just... echoes. Impressions." Gideon glanced up, and in the ember-light their face did something complicated — guilt and tenderness fighting for the same ground. "They've been waiting a long time, I think. For someone specific."

"For who?"

"Sleep, Sebastian."

He slept. But he dreamed of water rising up staircases, patient as time, and of a woman's voice singing somewhere below the surface — a song he almost knew, the way you almost know a word on the tip of your tongue.

* * *

In the morning, Gideon taught him the difference between swimming and not drowning.

It turned out to involve a charm — a sliver of bone, worn smooth, that Gideon strung on a cord and tied around Sebastian's neck with brisk, impersonal fingers that were not brisk or impersonal at all.

"Hold your breath normally," they instructed. "The charm does the rest. It borrows the water's patience, more or less. You'll have air where you need it and time where you need it. But—" they caught his chin, turned his face toward theirs, and their grey eyes were dead serious, "—you do not let go of me down there. Not for anything. Not if you see something wonderful. Especially if you see something wonderful. Wonderful things in Mirrormere are how people die."

"Has anyone ever told you that you'd be terrible at selling holidays?"

"Once. She drowned." Gideon shouldered their pack and walked into the shallows without another word.

The water took them gently. That was the strangest part — Sebastian had expected cold, resistance, the slap of it. Instead the lake received them the way the archive-crystals of Hollowmere had received light: completely, without argument. He sank past the moss-furred crown of a bell tower, past windows where silt drifted like snowfall in reverse, and the world above bent and wavered into a ceiling of hammered silver.

And the streets began.

They were whole, down here. Whole and silent and lit — though lit by nothing Sebastian could name — a soft blue-green glow rising from the cobbles themselves, from the ward-marks inlaid between the stones in patterns that made his eyes ache to follow. Doors stood open. A child's wooden horse lay on its side in a doorway, its paint miraculously unfaded. Laundry lines still stretched between balconies, hung with cloth that had become habitat: small silver fish darting through the sleeves of shirts, nesting in the folds of skirts.

"Oh," Sebastian breathed, and the charm caught the word and gave it back to him as a string of silver bubbles.

Gideon's hand found his in the dark water and gripped hard. *Not for anything.*

Then the ghosts came.

They didn't look the way he'd expected — no shrouds, no sorrow. They looked like neighbors. Figures of faint light moving through the flooded lanes with the unhurried purpose of people finishing a task: a woman sweeping a step that hadn't gathered dust in two centuries; two men carrying a beam toward a wall that no longer needed building; an old man seated on a bench, hands folded, watching the water with an expression of such mild contentment that Sebastian's chest tightened unexpectedly.

One of them turned. A young woman, her light flickering like a candle in a draft, and she looked directly at Sebastian — through the water, through the years — and her mouth formed words he couldn't hear but somehow understood anyway:

*You're late.*

His magic answered before he could stop it.

He felt it the way he'd felt it the night the stars went out — a waking, a turning-over, something vast rolling in its sleep inside his ribs. The ward-marks in the street flared. The glow in the cobbles brightened from blue-green to gold, and the gold spread outward from his feet in rings, racing down lanes and up walls, and everywhere it touched, the ghosts *turned*. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Sweeping women and bench-sitting old men and children mid-game with a hoop frozen against a wall, all of them rotating slowly to face him, all of them bowing —

— bowing, in the deep water, in the drowned city, to a clockmaker's apprentice from a valley where magic couldn't follow.

"Sebastian." Gideon's voice came strange and resonant through the charm. "Sebastian, what did you *do*?"

"Nothing! I said hello!"

"You *bowed back*, apparently—"

"I panicked!"

The ghosts straightened. And then, as one, they began to move — not toward him, but *with* him, flowing into formation around and ahead, an honor guard of light, beckoning down the great central avenue toward the heart of the city, where a domed building rose from the lakebed like a held breath.

"They're showing us the way," Sebastian said.

"I know." Gideon's grip on his hand had gone white-knuckled. "I wish they weren't."

* * *

The dome was a temple, or had been. Its doors stood open onto a hall where pillars rose through the water like the trunks of a stone forest, every surface carved with the same interlocking pattern Sebastian now realized he'd been seeing all week — on Wren's ward-tokens, on the keystone's casing, faintly, obsessively, in the margins of Gideon's maps.

The covenant-sign. Over and over, in stone that had waited two hundred years to be read.

At the hall's center, the floor opened into a circular well of clearer water, and the ghosts gathered around its rim — not pressing, not crowding, simply *attending*, the way a congregation attends. The old man from the bench was there. The woman with the broom. The young girl with the hoop, her light steady now, her expression solemn beyond her years.

Sebastian swam to the rim and looked down.

The keystone rested at the bottom of the well, cradled in a lattice of fused glass where the flooding heat of some ancient moment had melted sand into sculpture around it. Even from above, even through water, he could feel it — a hum in his teeth, a pull behind his sternum, the same note the first shard had sung when he touched it. Blue-white light pulsed at its heart, slow and steady.

*A heartbeat,* he thought. *It has a heartbeat.*

And wrapped around the keystone, holding it the way a parent holds a sleeping child, was a skeleton.

The bones were articulated with terrible care — arranged, not collapsed. Whoever this had been, they had come to this well deliberately, folded themselves around the stone deliberately, and stayed. Tatters of robe clung to the shoulders. And on the breastbone, pinned by a clasp of tarnished silver, was a crest.

Sebastian knew the crest.

He knew it the way you know your own handwriting. A tower beneath seven stars, with a key set into its gate. It hung above the workbench in Master Aldous's shop — no. It hung above the workbench in the shop where Sebastian had grown up, because it had been there before Aldous bought the place, because it had been painted on the beams and carved into the lintel and nobody, in nineteen years, had ever been able to tell him what it meant.

Seven stars. A tower. A key.

His family crest. His *family's* crest, on the bones of a covenant-keeper, guarding the keystone at the bottom of a drowned city, while ghosts bowed to him in the dark.

The water felt suddenly very close, and very large, and very cold.

"Gideon," he said. His voice came out small, even through the charm. "The crest. On the skeleton. It's—"

"I see it."

"It's *mine*. It's the mark from the shop, it's been over my head my whole life, why would it be *here*, why would—"

"Sebastian."

"—and the ghosts bowed, Gideon, ghosts don't bow, wards don't wake for strangers, the guardian called me *heir* before it tried to eat me, and you *flinched* when it said it, I saw you flinch—"

"*Sebastian.*"

He stopped. Turned. Gideon hung in the golden-lit water beside him, and their face was doing the thing it did — the careful, counted, filed-down thing — but underneath it, for once, Sebastian could see the machinery working too fast, catching, grinding. Gideon Delacroix, who lied beautifully and constantly, was trying to lie right now and failing at it, and the failure looked almost like pain.

"It's an old sigil," Gideon said. "Covenant houses used marks like that. Hundreds of families, hundreds of crests. Coincidence isn't impossible."

"You're lying."

"I'm *simplifying*." Their jaw tightened. "There's a difference, and someday you'll thank me for it."

"When? When will I thank you, Gideon? Because so far everything you've simplified has turned out to be the exact shape of something you didn't want me to know." He heard his own voice crack and hated it. "You knew the guardian would recognize me. You knew the ghosts were waiting for someone 'specific.' You've known things since Hollowmere — since *before* Hollowmere, maybe — and you keep handing me facts one coin at a time like I'm a child who'll drop the whole purse if you give it to me at once."

For a long moment Gideon said nothing. Around them, the ghosts waited with their terrible patience, and the keystone pulsed at the bottom of the well like a second heart, keeping time with his own.

"There are things I'm not telling you," Gideon said finally, quietly. "That's true. I won't insult you by denying it. But I need you to believe me when I say that every single one of them is a thing I'm not telling you *yet* — because the timing matters, because the truth in the wrong order is just another way of hurting someone." A breath, silver-bubbled. "And because some truths, once spoken, can't be unspoken. I've stolen enough memories to know that better than anyone alive."

"And which kind is the crest?"

Gideon looked at him. Really looked, the way they almost never did — guard down, count abandoned — and whatever Sebastian saw there frightened him more than the skeleton did.

"The crest," Gideon said, "is the second kind."

Then they kicked downward, into the well, before Sebastian could ask what that meant — down through the golden water to the lattice of glass, to the keystone and its silent keeper, and reached out with both hands to pry the stone free of a dead covenant-keeper's embrace.

The moment Gideon's fingers closed around it, every ghost in the hall went out at once, like candles in a wind.

And the keystone's light — that slow, patient heartbeat — stuttered, quickened, and turned toward Sebastian like a face recognizing its own.

Above them, far away, through two hundred feet of still silver water, the sky began to darken. Not with cloud.

With wings.
