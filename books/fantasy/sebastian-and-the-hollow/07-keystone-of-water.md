## 7. Keystone of Water

Gideon Delacroix had stolen a great many things in their life — memories, mostly, which were the easiest things in the world to steal if you had the hands for it. Memories didn't struggle. They didn't bite. They simply slid out of a person like a ribbon pulled from a spool, and afterward the person smiled at you and thanked you for your trouble, because they couldn't remember having any.

The drowned city of Mirrormere was not so cooperative.

It stood — or rather, it sank — at the bottom of a lake the color of hammered pewter, its towers rising out of the water like the fingers of something that had gone under and decided not to make a fuss about it. The covenant-keepers had built it two hundred years ago, back when the borders between realms were stitched tight and proud, and when the wards failed, the lake had come in through every window and door as though it had been waiting all along for an invitation.

"Tell me again," Sebastian said, "why we can't just swim."

They stood on the shore at dawn, or what passed for dawn in this part of the world, where the sky had been the color of dirty wool since the stars went out. Sebastian had his boots off and his trousers rolled to the knee, which did nothing whatsoever for his dignity but did wonders for Gideon's mood.

"Because," Gideon said, "the water remembers."

Sebastian gave them the look. He'd developed the look over the past week — eyebrows up, mouth flat, the expression of a boy who had spent his life around clockwork and therefore knew precisely how much nonsense he was being asked to believe. "That's not an explanation. That's a fortune-teller's stall."

"It's both." Gideon crouched at the waterline and let their fingers trail the surface. The lake shivered. Somewhere beneath, faint as a struck bell, something answered. "Everything magic touches keeps a record. This whole city is one long memory of people who thought they'd live forever. You walk into water like this carrying thoughts you're fond of, and the water will help itself. It's not cruel about it. It's just *hungry*."

"And you know this how?"

"I read." Gideon stood, brushing wet sand from their knees. "Extensively. In archives I may have technically been robbing at the time."

"You're a delight," Sebastian said, and there it was again — that warmth he kept offering, unguarded, like a coin held out to a stranger on the assumption that strangers were honest. Gideon had been many things in their life, but honest had never made the list, and every day Sebastian offered them that coin anyway, and every day Gideon took it without correcting him, and the weight of it sat somewhere behind their ribs like a swallowed stone.

Which was, they reflected, appropriate. They were here for a stone.

* * *

Getting down into Mirrormere required breathing, which Gideon solved the way they solved most problems: by paying someone. A ferry-witch worked the shallows in a coracle of woven reeds, her face tattooed with spirals that moved when she spoke. For three silver coins and a memory of a rainy afternoon — Gideon chose one deliberately dull, an afternoon of watching paint dry on a fence in a town whose name they no longer recalled — she agreed to wrap them in air-bubbles and point them toward the old keep.

"The keystone's in the Hall of Tides," she said, chewing on the memory like a sweetmeat. Her eyes went briefly, blissfully vacant, then refocused. "Lovely. What was I saying?"

"Hall of Tides," Gideon prompted.

"Mm. Yes. There's a guardian." She said it the way one mentions weather. "It doesn't want the stone. It wants what the stone costs. Feed it something before it feeds itself, or it'll take until there's nothing left of you but the parts that can drown." She smiled, wide and serene. "Have a lovely dive."

The bubble closed over Gideon's head, and then over Sebastian's, and then the lake folded them in.

Down they went, through water that grew darker and stranger, past streets where lamplight still burned green in flooded windows, past a marketplace where fish drifted between stalls as though browsing. Ghosts moved in the deep places — Gideon had warned Sebastian about those too, and watched him press his palm to the bubble's curve as a woman made of pale light paused to look at him, curious and sorrowful, before dissolving.

"They were the covenant-keepers," Sebastian had said earlier, wonder cracking his voice clean down the middle. "They *lived* here. They built all this to hold the realms together, and everyone forgot."

Everyone except, apparently, the lake.

The Hall of Tides opened before them like a cathedral that had lost an argument with the sea. Its vaulted ceiling had collapsed into graceful arches; its floor was silt and pearl; and at its center, on a pedestal of black coral, sat the first keystone.

It was smaller than Gideon expected. A teardrop of blue-white crystal, no bigger than a fist, turning slowly above the pedestal as though suspended in water even where there was no water. Light pulsed through it in slow rhythm — and Gideon realized, with a lurch, that the rhythm matched a heartbeat. Not theirs. Not Sebastian's. Something older, patient, buried.

"There," Sebastian breathed.

"Yes. And there—" Gideon pointed, because honesty about danger was the one kind of honesty they could currently afford, "—is our problem."

The guardian hung in the shadows above the pedestal. It looked, at first glance, like a jellyfish the size of a carriage, all trailing veils and drifting bells. At second glance, the veils resolved into faces. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Faces pressed into the translucent flesh like flowers in glass, mouths open, eyes wide, each one mid-scream or mid-song or mid-prayer — frozen moments culled from someone's head and worn like decoration.

"Oh," Sebastian said faintly. "Oh, that's horrible."

"It's a ledger," Gideon said. "Every memory it eats, it wears. That's how it counts."

The guardian turned. It had no eyes, but it turned all the same, and the pressure of its attention came through the water like a hand laid flat against Gideon's chest, patient and enormous, asking a question in a language made entirely of taking.

*What will you give?*

"Gideon." Sebastian's voice was thin inside the bubble. "Gideon, what do we do?"

What they did, Gideon decided later, was what they always did. What they had been doing since they were nine years old and the Ashen Court burned their family's name off the face of the earth: they paid.

"Stay behind me," they said. "Whatever happens, whatever you see — stay behind me."

"Gideon—"

"*Stay.*"

Gideon stepped forward, drew a breath of enchanted air, and opened themselves the way they'd learned to open themselves, the way a lock opens, the way a purse opens, the way everything they had ever loved had eventually opened and emptied out.

Memories rose in them like bubbles. Gideon sorted through them with the speed of long practice — not the mother-memory, never that one, that one was already sold and gone, God, don't think about it — looking for something they could bear to lose. A memory of a rooftop in Emberhold, aged fourteen, eating stolen plums while the Court's banners snapped below. A memory of learning to pick their first lock, their teacher's laugh, the click of the tumbler giving way like a small applause. A memory of—

The guardian struck.

There was no negotiation after all. Perhaps there never had been; perhaps the ferry-witch's advice had been a courtesy rather than a strategy. The great bell-shaped body descended, veils spreading, and the pressure became a pull, and Gideon felt memories tear loose — not the ones they'd chosen, not the ones they'd carefully budgeted, but *others*, ripped out wholesale, a decade of small bright things sluicing away into that hungry translucence—

No.

Not all of it. Gideon caught hold of the flow the only way a thief catches anything: by knowing the shape of what they carried better than anyone alive. They clenched down on the essentials — Sebastian's face, the clocktower, the road, the plan, the plan, *the plan* — and fed the guardian everything else in armfuls. Years of it. Whole seasons. A winter in a border town learning ward-script from a woman with ink-stained fingers. A summer of running. A hundred small kindnesses received and returned. Faces whose names went with them, peeling away like labels soaked off in rain.

When it ended, Gideon was on their knees in the silt, and the guardian hung above them, fat and luminous and satisfied, new faces blooming across its veils. Some of them, Gideon realized distantly, were wearing Gideon's own expressions. One wore them laughing. They could not remember what the laughing had been about.

The pedestal stood open. The keystone waited.

*Take it,* the guardian seemed to say, in the tone of a merchant closing a till. *We are square.*

* * *

Sebastian touched the keystone before Gideon could stop him.

In fairness, Gideon was in no state to stop anyone. They surfaced from the silt on legs that felt borrowed, waving off Sebastian's hands, insisting — with what they hoped was convincing briskness — that they were fine, just tired, the guardian had wanted payment and been paid, nothing to fuss over. They were aware, even as they said it, that they were smiling too easily. Smiling was what happened now when there was a hole where a reason should be.

"The toll?" Sebastian said sharply. "What did it take from you?"

"A few things I won't miss." Which was true, in the sense that Gideon could no longer remember them well enough to miss them. "Come on. Take the stone and let's get above water before I forget why we came."

Sebastian reached for the keystone — and the moment his fingers closed around the crystal, the light in it flared white, and he went rigid.

Gideon saw it happen and knew exactly what it was, because they'd seen memory move through a body before. It poured into him like floodwater into a cellar. His eyes rolled back. His grip locked. The bubble around his head trembled.

And Gideon, kneeling in the silt of a drowned hall, watched Sebastian Vale see something that had never belonged to him — and understood, with cold certainty, whose memory it was.

Because the keystone remembered its makers. And its makers had been Sebastian's family.

He came back gasping, sagging against the pedestal, the keystone clutched to his chest like a coal.

"What did you see?" Gideon asked. Carefully. Casually. The casualness cost more than the memories had.

Sebastian's face was wet, and not from the lake. "A woman," he whispered. "A room with wards carved into every wall, glowing blue. She's — she has my hands, Gideon, I *know* she does, the little scar on the thumb. She's sealing a baby into a cradle. Warded glass, like a lantern. The baby's crying and she's singing to it and her hands are shaking and she says—" His breath hitched. "She says, *'Forgive me. Live small and live safe. Forget me.'*" He looked up, and his eyes were terrible with wonder. "She put me in that valley. Didn't she. Someone sealed me into Hollowmere like — like a letter no one was meant to open."

The lie assembled itself in Gideon's mouth with the smoothness of long habit, and hated itself the whole way down.

"I don't know," Gideon said. "Old keeper families warded their children all the time, during the war. It might not be you. Stones like this show people what they need to see."

Even as the words left, Sebastian's gaze lingered on them a moment too long. Not suspicion, exactly. Something worse: the beginning of a question. The very first hairline crack in a trust that had been, until now, insultingly whole.

"Right," Sebastian said slowly. "Might not be me."

"We should go," Gideon said. "The bubble-air won't last."

They rose together through the darkening water, past the green-lit windows, past the browsing fish, past ghosts who watched them pass with expressions Gideon could no longer interpret — faces they might once have known, meanings they might once have carried, all of it paid out into the belly of a creature that wore stolen laughter on its skin.

Above the surface, the pewter sky had begun to bruise toward evening. Sebastian wrung out his sleeves and said little. Gideon counted what remained of themselves, the way a gambler counts coins by touch in the dark, and found the sum shorter than they'd admitted aloud — and told no one.

Two days later, on the mountain road east, they heard whistling.

High, tuneless, defiant whistling, coming from the rocks above the pass — and then a small figure dropped onto the path ahead of them, wild-haired and sharp-eyed, holding a slingshot the size of a threat.

"That's far enough," said the girl, "and also, you owe me a toll."
