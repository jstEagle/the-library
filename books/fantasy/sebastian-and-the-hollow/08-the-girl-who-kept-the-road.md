## 8. The Girl Who Kept the Road

The mountain pass had opinions about them.

Sebastian could feel it in the way the wind came at them sideways, deliberate as a pickpocket, and in the way the road itself seemed to narrow whenever Gideon walked at the front. Three days out of Mirrormere, with the water-keystone wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heartbeat, and the mountains had decided they were not welcome.

"Cheerful place," Sebastian said.

Gideon didn't answer. They hadn't answered much since the drowned city — since the skeleton wearing Sebastian's family crest, and the lie Gideon had told about not recognizing it. The lie sat between them now, small and sharp as a splinter working its way toward the skin's surface. Sebastian hadn't pulled it yet. He wasn't sure he wanted to see what was underneath.

Instead he watched the pass. That was the clockmaker in him: when something troubled him, he watched the mechanism instead. And the mechanism of this mountain was wrong.

"Someone's been through here," he said. "Recently."

Gideon glanced up. "What makes you say that?"

"The cairns." Sebastian nodded at the stacks of stones that lined the road every hundred paces or so, waist-high towers of balanced rock. "They're not erosion. Someone built them, and someone's been maintaining them. Look — this one's been rebuilt after a freeze-crack. New stone fitted into old. That takes regular attention."

"You're telling me there's a mason living on this mountain."

"I'm telling me there's *someone*," Sebastian said. "And whoever they are, they've been watching us since the switchback below the scree field."

Gideon went very still, the way they always did when a memory they'd sold or stolen brushed up against the edges of what remained. Then their hand drifted to the knife at their belt, and Sebastian realized how exposed they were — two travelers on an empty road, one of them carrying a relic three factions would burn kingdoms for.

He never heard her coming.

There was just a moment where the air above him went strange, like the hush before a clock strikes, and then a voice said, directly over his left shoulder:

"That's far enough."

Sebastian did not scream. He would maintain until his dying day that he did not scream. What he did was make a sound, a short involuntary bark of alarm, and stumble backward off the path, arms pinwheeling, and catch his heel on a cairn so that the whole careful tower of stones came down around him like a very slow, very geological applause.

The girl on the rock above him watched all of this with an expression of profound disappointment.

She was perhaps twelve years old, small and wiry, dressed in layers of mismatched wool that had been patched so many times the original garment existed only as a concept. Her hair was cut raggedly short — hacked, not cut, by someone who had used a knife and no mirror — and across her cheeks and forearms she had painted lines of pale ash mixed with something waxy. Ward-marks, Sebastian realized. Crude ones, child-drawn, but drawn with the unmistakable grammar of real warding. He'd grown up under wards his whole life. You learned to recognize the handwriting.

"You knocked down my marker," the girl said.

"Your—" Sebastian looked at the scattered stones. "I'm sorry. I'll rebuild it. I'm actually quite good at balancing things, it's most of my trade—"

"It took me a *morning*."

"It'll take me ten minutes."

"It took me a morning because each stone has to be *asked*." She scrambled down from her perch with the fluid economy of someone who had been climbing rocks since before she could form sentences, and began scooping up her fallen cairn with the wounded dignity of a general retrieving fallen flags. "You can't just stack them. They have to agree to be stacked. Everything on this mountain agrees to things or it doesn't work. That's the whole point."

Gideon had their knife half-drawn. Sebastian caught their eye and shook his head, very slightly. A twelve-year-old with ash warpaint and strong feelings about geological consent was not, whatever else she might be, an Ashen Court hunter.

"Who are you?" Gideon asked.

The girl looked up at them both, and Sebastian saw her do a thing he recognized from the archive-crystals back home — a quick flick of attention, reading them, cataloguing. Except where the crystals read memory, she read posture, gear, the oilcloth bulge at his chest, the way Gideon stood between her and the downhill path.

"Wren," she said finally. "I keep the road."

"Keep it how?" Sebastian asked.

"Against what?" Gideon said, at the same moment.

Wren stood, dusted her knees, and looked at them with the weary patience of an official addressing two unusually slow petitioners.

"Against everything," she said. "Obviously."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a shelf of stone that Wren informed them was called the Old Man's Shoulder, and which she had personally warded against "wind, wolves, worse, and weather," a list she delivered with the cadence of a much older person reciting a catechism learned too young.

Sebastian cooked. He'd discovered on the road out of Hollowmere that he was, without planning to be, the cook — Gideon ate like a debt collector, quickly and without pleasure, taking only what was owed. Sebastian cooked the way he worked on clocks, slowly, with small adjustments, and it turned out that a boy who could true a wheel could also make trail rations taste like someone had tried.

Wren appeared at the edge of the firelight exactly as the smell reached its peak, materializing with the silent professionalism of a cat who has heard a can opener.

"There's more if you want it," Sebastian said, not looking up.

"I don't want anything from you."

"Mm." He ladled out a third bowl anyway and set it on a flat stone between them, equidistant, neutral territory. Then he went back to stirring.

The silence stretched. Gideon, Sebastian noticed, had gone carefully still again — watching Wren watch the bowl, and Sebastian suspected that behind those grey eyes a whole ledger of debts and dangers was being balanced. But Sebastian had grown up in a workshop full of apprentices younger than him, hungry ones, proud ones, and he knew the exact weight of a bowl set down within reach of someone who insisted they didn't want it.

It took four minutes. He counted. He was a clockmaker; counting was the whole religion.

Wren took the bowl, ate with shocking speed and perfect manners — elbows in, no sound, nothing wasted — and set the empty dish down with the air of someone concluding a business transaction.

"That wasn't charity," she said. "That was payment."

"For what?"

"For information you're going to need." She wiped her mouth on her sleeve. "You're going to the Spire."

Sebastian felt Gideon go taut beside him like a plucked string. "We haven't said—"

"You don't have to say. You've got river-mud from Mirrormere dried in your boot seams, you've got keystone-sickness all over you—" she waved vaguely at Sebastian's chest, at the ache he'd been carrying since the water, the feeling of being a struck bell that wouldn't stop humming — "and nobody comes over the pass unless they're going to the Spire, because the pass doesn't go anywhere else except off a cliff." She shrugged. "Also you talked about it in your sleep last night. Both of you. Loudly. This mountain carries sound for miles. I heard you arguing about whether to tell me about the keystone while I was sitting right here eating your soup."

A long pause.

"We argued about whether to tell you about the keystone," Sebastian said carefully, "while you were listening."

"While you were *feeding* me," Wren corrected. "Which is worse. You fed me and then discussed secrets like I was furniture. My mother said you should never feed anyone you don't trust." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "Anyway. It doesn't matter. Everyone who goes to the Spire dies or turns around. Usually both, in that order."

"You've been there?" Gideon asked.

"I keep the road *to* it." Wren drew in the ash-dust with one finger: a spire, broken, ringed with marks. "My people kept it for six hundred years. The ward-keepers. Before the stars went out, there were forty-one of us on this mountain. Now there's me." She said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report the weather, and Sebastian understood suddenly and completely why she painted ward-marks on her face every morning, why she asked stones to consent before stacking them, why she kept a road for no one. Rituals were how you kept a door closed against a grief too big to look at directly. He'd wound the same dead man's clocks every day for two years after his master died. He knew the shape of it.

"The others," Sebastian said quietly. "What happened to them?"

"When the stars went out, the old wards broke. The deep ones, the ones that needed a whole circle to hold." Wren traced the broken spire in the dust. "The mountain has things in it that were only staying asleep because we asked them nicely. Forty-one keepers is a nice ask. One keeper is..." She considered. "A suggestion."

"And you stayed anyway," Sebastian said.

"Somebody has to keep the road." She said it like a law of nature. Like gravity. "Otherwise it stops being a road and starts being just a bunch of rocks with opinions."

Despite everything — the keystone against his chest, the splinter of Gideon's lie, the frozen battlefield waiting for them at the top of the world — Sebastian found himself smiling. "That's the most sensible thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

"I know," said Wren. "I say lots of sensible things. Nobody listens. That's the other reason I keep the road. Rocks listen."

* * *

She bargained hard for the second night, and harder for the third.

Food first — that was simple, transactional, almost comfortable. Two meals for the safe path through the scree. Four meals for the crossing at the Stone Ford, which could be forded at high noon or never. Sebastian paid gladly and Gideon paid grudgingly, and Wren pocketed the dried apricots Gideon surrendered with the solemnity of a tax collector.

But on the third evening, sitting with her feet nearly in the fire because she liked the sting of it, she changed currencies.

"I'm coming with you," she said.

Gideon's spoon stopped halfway to their mouth. "No."

"Yes."

"No. It's a battlefield frozen mid-collapse, it's crawling with things that eat memories, and the faction that wants the person carrying our keystone knows roughly where we're headed. No."

"I know the ways in. All nine of them. Well — seven now. Well — five." Wren held up a hand and began folding fingers down. "Two collapsed. One's got a nest of hollow-things in it. One I fell down and couldn't get out of for two days, so I don't recommend it on principle."

"That's four," Sebastian said.

"The fifth one I'm keeping secret in case you say no again." She folded the last finger with great satisfaction. "See? Sensible."

"Wren." Gideon set down the bowl, and Sebastian heard the tone they used — the negotiator's tone, the one that had talked them past checkpoints and through Emberhold's outer markets. Gentle, reasonable, final. "You're a child. You've survived alone on this mountain, which is extraordinary, but what we're doing isn't surviving. It's the opposite of surviving. It's walking straight at the thing that killed everyone you kept the road for."

Sebastian winced. It was true, and it was cruel, and it landed exactly where Gideon had aimed it.

Wren didn't flinch. She looked into the fire for a long moment, and when she spoke, the official voice was gone. What was underneath was smaller, and steadier than anything that small had a right to be.

"You think I don't know what dying looks like?" she said. "I buried forty people. I dug the graves myself because there was nobody else, and some of them were heavy, and I was eleven." She looked up. "You're going to the Spire to fix the thing that broke. That's keeper work. That's *my* work. You can walk past me and do it wrong, or you can take me and do it right." A pause. "Also I get a share of the apricots."

"That's not—" Gideon started.

"Negotiating," Wren said. "You were doing it wrong. You lead with feelings. Feelings are free. Lead with apricots."

Sebastian laughed — really laughed, the first time since the stars went out, a surprised bark of it that seemed to startle all three of them. Gideon shot him a look that contained an entire argument, and he met it with one of his own, and the argument went roughly like this: *we cannot protect her by leaving her on a haunted mountain alone, and you know it, and besides, she's right.*

Gideon looked away first. "Half share," they muttered. "And she does what we say when things go wrong."

"Nobody does what anyone says when things go wrong," Wren said. "That's what makes it wrong. Half share accepted. Shake on it."

She spat in her palm and held it out. Gideon stared at it as though it were a new species of trap. Sebastian, grinning, spat in his own and shook it first, and after a moment Gideon sighed the sigh of the defeated and followed suit.

"Sealed," Wren announced. "Now it's binding. Everything on this mountain agrees to things or it doesn't work."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, because Wren kept mountain hours and had simply begun kicking their boots at first light, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge they were climbing the last long shoulder of stone toward the place where the sky had been broken.

Sebastian felt it before he saw it — a pressure change, like descending into a cellar, like the moment in the workshop when a finished clock first begins to tick and the whole room joins its rhythm. The keystone against his chest warmed and hummed. Ahead, past a final ridge of black rock, something vast caught the early light.

The Shattered Spire did not look ruined so much as interrupted. It rose from the mountaintop like a blade driven into the world and snapped off at the hilt, and around its base the wreckage of a battle hung suspended — shattered towers frozen mid-fall, a bridge arcing upward in pieces, banners of stone rippling in a wind that had stopped two hundred years ago and never started again. The whole mountainside was a single held breath.

"Oh," Sebastian said. Very quietly.

"Told you," Wren said, and for once there was no officiousness in it, only a kind of fierce, grieving pride. "My people kept this. Six hundred years." She marched ahead of them onto the frozen battlefield, small and certain, ash-marks pale on her cheeks, and the mountain — Sebastian would have sworn it, then and ever after — seemed to settle around her footsteps, stones agreeing to be stepped on.

They had gone perhaps fifty paces when Wren stopped so abruptly that Sebastian walked into her back.

At the edge of the ruin, planted in the ground like a declaration, stood a black iron stake hung with a banner of ash-grey silk. And around the stake, pressed into the frozen earth, were bootprints. Dozens of them. Fresh. Leading in.

Leading out, none.

"Cassian's hunters," Gideon breathed, hand going to their knife. "They're already inside."

Wren crouched over the prints, touched the earth, and looked up at them with her chin set.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said. "Nobody keeps this road but me."
