## 9. Cassian's Offer

Gideon had always been a light sleeper. It was a professional necessity. A memory-thief who slept like the innocent did not remain a memory-thief for long; they remained a cautionary tale told in the back rooms of Emberhold's memory-markets, usually with an illustrative gesture and a knowing pause.

So when the horses shifted in the dark — not the drowsy shuffle of animals settling, but the sharp, rolling step of animals that had smelled something wrong — Gideon was awake before their eyes opened.

The camp sat in a hollow below the mountain pass, three bedrolls arranged around a fire that had burned down to a sullen orange eye. Sebastian slept on his back with one arm flung over his face like a man surrendering to a very small army. Wren slept curled into a ball so tight she looked less like a child than a fist, her knife — she had *a knife*, she was twelve, this was apparently normal where she came from — tucked under her hand.

And at the edge of the firelight, sitting on a fallen log as though he'd been invited, was Lord Cassian Vey.

He wore no armor. He wore no escort. He wore a coat of grey silk that seemed to drink the firelight rather than reflect it, and he was smiling the way a chess player smiles when his opponent has just realized the game ended four moves ago.

Gideon's hand found the knife under their bedroll. It felt about as useful as a spoon.

"Before you do anything dramatic," Cassian said, in the pleasant voice of a man discussing weather, "consider that I have been sitting here for the better part of an hour, and if I wanted the boy, I would already have him."

"If you wanted him," Gideon said quietly, "you'd have taken him while he slept. The fact that you didn't means you want something else. That's the only reason men like you ever talk."

Something flickered across Cassian's face — surprise, quickly folded away, but genuine. "You've met men like me."

"I've *sold* to men like you." Gideon rose slowly, keeping their weight low, angling themselves between the log and the sleeping forms behind it. Old habit. They weren't even sure anymore who they were protecting — Sebastian from Cassian, or Cassian from what Sebastian might be becoming. Both, probably. The truth was getting crowded lately.

"May I?" Cassian gestured at the fire.

"You may not."

"I'll take that as hospitality." He moved anyway, closer to the flames, and Gideon hated how gracefully he did it, how the firelight caught the silver threads at his temples and made them look like honors rather than age. "You've come further than I expected. Mirrormere is no easy dive, and the guardian beneath it has eaten better thieves than you."

"It tried."

"And yet here you stand, missing—" His eyes narrowed, reading Gideon the way Gideon read archive-crystals, tilting his head as though listening for gaps. "Ah. There. Something recent. Something you paid rather than lost. You've been feeding the road, Delacroix. Piece by piece. How much of yourself is left, I wonder? Do *you* wonder?"

"Get to the point."

"The point." Cassian leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the courtly polish dropped and something older showed underneath — something tired and hungry and terribly patient. "The point is that I know what you were, before the valley. Before the Court took your family. I keep records, Gideon. Records of everyone my hunters have ever touched. Your mother sang at the Emberfall festivals. Your father repaired the covenant-wards along the eastern road — badly, according to his supervisor, but with great enthusiasm." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "You had a sister."

The world went very quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, wind moved through the pines with a sound like slow breathing.

"Don't," Gideon said.

"I'm not threatening her. She's been dead eleven years." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse — the flat administrative kindness of a man reporting a ledger entry. "I'm telling you that I remember her. That I am, at this moment, possibly the only living soul besides yourself who remembers what she looked like. And I could give that back to you. All of it. Every memory you sold to survive — the market keeps its copies, Delacroix. Everything that passed through Emberhold's ledgers sits in vaults beneath my throne, whole and waiting. Your mother's face. Your sister's laugh. The name of the street you grew up on, if you've lost even that." He tilted his head. "Have you?"

Gideon said nothing, which was answer enough, and they both knew it.

*What was her name.* The question lived in Gideon now like a splinter worked too deep to dig out. They knew they'd had a sister. They knew it the way you know a room exists behind a locked door — by the shape of the absence. Some nights they woke with wet eyes and no idea why, and the not-knowing was its own kind of wound, a grief with the serial numbers filed off.

"I can restore all of it," Cassian said softly. "Every piece. Not copies — the originals, drawn fresh from the vaults, returned to the mind that made them. You would wake tomorrow remembering your whole life. Do you understand what I'm offering? Not comfort. *Wholeness.* No one else in either realm can buy that back for you. No one else would even try."

"And the price."

"The price is a delivery." He spread his hands, reasonable, almost apologetic. "The boy. Brought to Emberhold, unharmed, willing. That's all. I don't need you to fight him, trick him, or betray him in any way that would trouble your conscience — I simply need you to stop running, and let events arrive at their natural conclusion. He's going to Emberhold regardless, you know. The third keystone wears my crown. He cannot finish what he's started without walking through my door. All you'd be doing is choosing *when*, and choosing to walk in beside him instead of being dragged." A thin smile. "You might even think of it as mercy. You'd be there to soften what comes next."

"And what comes next?"

"That," Cassian said, "depends entirely on him."

Behind Gideon, Sebastian murmured in his sleep and rolled over. The sound went through Gideon like a hook through cloth — pulling everything taut. Three weeks ago, Gideon would have taken this deal. Three weeks ago, Gideon would have taken this deal *gratefully*, and told themselves a story about survival, and never once looked back, because looking back was a luxury for people who still had things behind them.

But three weeks ago Gideon hadn't watched Sebastian stand in the drowned water of Mirrormere holding a keystone fused to the bones of his own kin. Hadn't seen him give half his rations to a feral child who'd threatened him with a knife. Hadn't heard him say, with that infuriating clockmaker's patience, *"We'll figure out what it costs when we get there. But nobody pays it alone."*

Nobody pays it alone.

"No," Gideon said.

Cassian sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor who had rehearsed disappointment until it fit him like a glove. "No?"

"No. And I'll tell you why, since you're fond of records. You're offering me my memories back — every memory I *sold*. But you're not offering me the ones I stole. Are you." Gideon took a step forward, and now it was their turn to smile, thin and cold. "You keep meticulous books, my lord. So check them. Check what I took out of Emberhold myself, before I ran. I know what's in my head that you want back. And I know you'd love nothing more than to open my skull and read it. So no. Whatever you're really buying, it isn't a delivery. It's a retrieval."

For a long moment, Cassian Vey simply looked at them. The firelight moved on his face and none of it reached his eyes.

"There it is," he said at last, almost warmly. "The thief's instinct. Half right, as usual." He rose from the log, unhurried, brushing nothing from his immaculate coat. "Very well. The offer stands. It will stand for exactly as long as it takes you to learn what the boy actually is — because you don't know, do you? You've seen the crest on the bones. You've touched the stone. But you haven't told him what the covenant line *was for*." 

"He knows enough."

"He knows nothing." Cassian's voice dropped, and the warmth in it curdled into something ancient. "He is the lastborn, Delacroix. The final heir of the line that *built* the covenant — not its servants, not its wardens. Its architects. And the covenant does not ask its architects to hold it together. It asks them to *be* the mortar." He let that sit in the cold air between them. "You've felt it already, haven't you? The way magic leans toward him. The way the wards woke when he crossed the border. He wasn't smuggled into that valley to be safe. He was sealed there to *ripen*. Two hundred years in a jar where magic couldn't reach him, so that when the time came, he'd be clean. Unclaimed. Ready to pour."

Gideon's mouth had gone dry. "You're lying."

"Am I?" Cassian smiled, and it was the gentlest expression he'd worn all night, and the most terrible. "Then why haven't you told him about the cradle? Why does he still believe he was *hidden*? Ask yourself, thief — hidden from whom? A parent doesn't seal a child in a warded box and bury it in a magicless valley to protect it. A parent does that to protect *everyone else* from what the child is for." He stepped past Gideon, close enough that they caught the scent of woodsmoke and cold iron, and paused. "When he learns the truth — and he will, the stones will teach him whether you do or not — he'll look at you and ask one question: *did you know?* Think carefully, before that moment arrives, about which answer costs less."

He walked two steps toward the darkness, then stopped.

"Oh — and the girl." He didn't turn around. "Wren. Ward-keeper's blood, feral little thing. My hunters have orders regarding her. Bring her to me alive and I'll consider it a gesture of good faith. Leave her, and they'll collect her anyway. She ignites within the month — I can smell it from here — and unbonded ward-magic in a child is a wildfire waiting for a dry season." At last he glanced back over his shoulder, and his smile was almost kind. "You can't save everyone, Delacroix. Believe me. I've done the arithmetic."

Then the dark took him, the way water takes a stone — no sound, no ripple, just gone. The horses stamped once and settled. The fire crackled on, indifferent.

Gideon stood in the cold for a long time.

*He is the lastborn. He was sealed there to ripen.*

They thought of the skeleton in the flooded street, wearing Sebastian's crest. Of the woman in the foreign memory — the one Gideon had glimpsed through the keystone's touch, sealing a baby into a warded cradle with hands that shook. Gideon had assumed fear for the child. Now another reading offered itself, and it fit the shape of the memory far too well: not *I must keep you safe*, but *forgive me, I must keep you contained.*

And Sebastian — stubborn, generous, ridiculous Sebastian, who shared his bread with children who bit him — would hear this and break. Or worse: he would hear it and *believe it*, and start measuring himself against it, the way people measure themselves against the worst thing anyone ever said about them.

Unless someone stood between him and the story first. Unless someone gave him a version of the truth he could survive.

Which meant lying to him. Again. Carefully, again. For his own good, again.

Gideon looked down at their own hands in the firelight and thought, with a tired, bitter humor that was the last luxury they owned: *I used to steal memories for coin. Now I steal truths for love. The rates are worse and the clients are impossible.*

They returned to the bedroll. Sebastian had turned toward the fire in his sleep, face slack and young, one hand curled loosely near the keystone-pouch at his belt even unconscious, even dreaming — holding on.

Gideon lay down, stared at the wheeling, starless dark, and began, methodically, to compose the lie.

It would need to be a good one. It would need to hold through the Shattered Spire, through whatever waited in those frozen battlefields, through the day — coming sooner than any of them wanted — when Sebastian stood before the third keystone and the covenant finally spoke its terms aloud.

It would need to hold forever, ideally.

Gideon closed their eyes.

Behind them, soft as snowfall, Wren's voice came out of the dark — awake after all, awake perhaps the whole time, in the flat, unsentimental tone of a child who had survived alone by noticing everything:

"You knew him. The grey-coat man." A pause. "And you're going to lie to Clockmaker about it. I can tell. You've got the same face my mother got."

Up the pass, faint and far, a hunting horn sounded once and cut off.

Gideon opened their eyes and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, frosting the tent-flaps of canvas they'd strung between the horses. Sebastian woke cheerful, which Gideon found obscene. He built the fire back up, brewed the pine-needle tea Wren had taught them to make ("it tastes like punishment," Sebastian had said the first time, drinking a second cup), and was in the middle of explaining — animatedly, with sticks arranged on a flat stone — his theory that the Shattered Spire's collapse had been arrested mid-fall by some enormous act of warding, "which means the wards are still *there*, Gideon, still active after two hundred years, do you understand what that implies about—"

"Gideon," Wren said.

She was standing at the edge of the hollow, facing up-slope, very still. Her knife was in her hand. In the early light, the pass above them was printed with tracks — boot-prints, dozens, circling the camp in a wide, patient ring. Not approaching. Measuring.

"They were here in the night," Wren said. "More than the grey-coat. They counted us." She glanced back over her shoulder, and her eyes — old eyes in a young face — went from Gideon to Sebastian and back. "We should move. Now. And somebody should tell me what we're walking into, because I don't lead children into ambushes, and I'm starting to suspect I'm the only one here who'll say so."

Sebastian looked up from his stick-diagram, tea halfway to his lips, and his gaze found Gideon's — open, trusting, asking nothing yet.

Gideon smiled, and lied with their whole face.

"Packed in five minutes," they said. "I'll explain on the road."
