## 10. The Shattered Spire

The mountain had a name once. Wren said the ward-keepers used to sing it — *Vaelkorath, Vaelkorath, the spine that holds the sky* — but nobody sang it anymore, because nobody could agree on how many syllables it had, and the last person who'd tried to settle the argument had been eaten by something in the scree fields.

Sebastian decided not to ask what kind of something.

They climbed for two days through pine and then past pine, through snow and then past snow, until the trees gave up entirely and the world became rock and wind and a silence so complete Sebastian could hear his own heartbeat complaining about the altitude. Wren moved ahead of them like she was born to it, which, as far as Sebastian could tell, she might have been. She stepped on stones that looked identical to every other stone and somehow they held. She stopped at unremarkable patches of air and muttered words under her breath, and the air would shimmer faintly, offended at being noticed.

"Old wards," she explained, when Gideon asked. "Dead ones. Mostly." She paused. "Don't step there anyway."

"I wasn't going to," Gideon said.

"You were absolutely going to."

Gideon opened their mouth, considered their recent track record with ancient magical architecture, and closed it again.

Sebastian smiled despite himself. It was strange, how quickly Wren had become part of the shape of things — the way a coat becomes yours, or a scar. Two weeks ago he hadn't known she existed. Now her absence would have left a Wren-shaped hole in every day, and he suspected she knew it, and pretended not to, and was smug about both.

"There," she said suddenly, and pointed.

Sebastian followed her finger and forgot how to breathe.

The Spire rose out of the mountaintop like a wound that had decided to become a monument. It must have been beautiful once — a tower of white stone spiraling toward heaven, wide enough at the base to hold a town. But it had been broken. Not toppled, not crumbled: *broken*, the way a bone breaks, mid-motion, mid-scream. Half of it leaned against the other half at an impossible angle, frozen in the act of falling for two hundred years. Chunks of masonry hung in the air around it, suspended, drifting so slowly that you could watch one for an hour and never see it move. Shards of light — actual light, solidified into jagged glass-like splinters — jutted from the mountainside like the aftermath of an explosion someone had pressed pause on.

"It's a battlefield," Sebastian said quietly.

"It's *the* battlefield," Wren said. "Where the covenant broke. Where the war ended." She shrugged, the shrug of someone who had grown up playing among corpses. "Everything after this is just cleanup."

* * *

Getting inside was Wren's doing entirely.

The entrance — if it could be called that — was a gash in the Spire's flank where a wall had sheared away, revealing rooms like the cross-section of a dollhouse. But the gash breathed. Sebastian felt it before they reached it: a slow pull, like standing near an open oven door, except instead of heat it drew at something behind his ribs.

"Don't go in the front way," Wren said. "Front ways are for people who want to be digested."

"There are other ways?" Gideon asked.

"There are always other ways. Front ways are just the ones the builders wanted you to use, and builders always want something." She scrambled along a ledge barely wider than her boots, sure-footed as a goat, and stopped at a section of wall that looked exactly like every other section of wall. She pressed her palm flat against it and whispered — not words, Sebastian realized, but a rhythm. A counting rhythm. The kind children use for skipping rope.

The wall sighed and thinned to translucence.

"How do you know all this?" Sebastian asked, awed.

Wren glanced back at him, and for a moment the feral confidence flickered and he saw underneath it: a very small girl who had spent a very long time alone with dead people's magic because living people hadn't wanted her.

"The ward-keepers taught songs," she said simply. "I kept singing them after everyone stopped."

Then she stepped through the wall, and there was nothing to do but follow.

* * *

Inside, the Spire was a cathedral of interrupted violence.

They walked through halls where the last moment of the war still hung in the air like smoke. A table set for a meal no one finished — the food long since dust, but the chairs still knocked askew, one of them split down the middle by something that had come through the ceiling. A library with books floating in slow orbit around a shattered reading desk, pages open, as if the readers had simply stepped out and gravity had agreed to wait. And everywhere, everywhere, the frozen shards of light, drifting like snow that had forgotten which direction down was.

Gideon walked with their arms crossed tight, eyes moving constantly, cataloguing exits. Sebastian had learned to read that posture. Gideon Delacroix did not trust rooms. Rooms had histories, and histories had teeth.

But even Gideon slowed when they reached the great hall.

It stretched away into darkness, vast enough that torchlight couldn't find its far wall. And the walls themselves — the visible ones — were covered in murals.

Not painted. Sebastian touched one and his fingers came away dry. The images were *in* the stone, woven through it somehow, gold and deep blue and a red like embers, glowing faintly with their own light. They showed figures — tall robed ones, small plain ones, some that might have been neither — standing in circles, hands joined, and between their joined hands ran lines of light that flowed outward across the whole mural, across the whole wall, binding everything together.

"Binding realms," Gideon murmured. "This is it. This is the covenant being made."

Sebastian stepped closer. The mural marched along the wall in panels, a story told in stone. In the first panel, two worlds — rendered as two great spheres, one dark, one bright — drifted apart, unraveling at the edges like fraying cloth. In the second, the robed figures stood between them, straining, holding threads. In the third, the threads became a lattice, and the lattice became a bridge, and on the bridge stood —

He stopped.

There was a figure in the third panel unlike the others. Smaller. Plainer. Dressed in simple clothes — a workman's coat, the kind a clockmaker's apprentice might wear. And its face —

Sebastian's hand rose without his permission and touched the stone face.

It was his face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance, not the vague echo you might find in an old portrait. It was *his* — the same narrow jaw, the same crooked nose he'd gotten falling off the clocktower ladder at eleven, the same slightly-too-serious mouth that Master Aldous had always said made him look like he was auditing the universe and finding it short. Even the small scar above his eyebrow, the one he'd never been able to explain because he'd had it as long as he could remember.

The figure stood at the center of the lattice, arms out, and all the threads of light converged on its chest like rivers running home.

Beneath it, in letters of ember-red that seemed to pulse when he wasn't looking directly at them, was a single word:

**VESSEL**

"Sebastian?" Wren's voice, small. "You've gone funny-colored."

He couldn't answer. He was walking. His legs had decided without consulting him, carrying him along the mural, panel to panel, faster now. The fourth panel: the vessel-figure older, threads thickening around it, its expression — his expression — strained, tired, *used*. The fifth: the vessel kneeling while robed figures worked above it, and Sebastian understood with sick clarity that they weren't honoring it. They were maintaining it. The way you maintain a tool. The way you wind a clock.

The sixth panel was half-destroyed, sheared away by whatever had broken the Spire. What remained showed fire, and falling stone, and the vessel-figure reaching toward something — someone? — just outside the surviving edge of the image.

And the seventh panel was empty. Blank stone. Waiting.

"No," Sebastian heard himself say. It came out very quiet. "No, no, no—"

"Hey." Gideon's hand on his shoulder. "Hey. Breathe. What is it?"

"That's me." His voice sounded like someone else's. "Gideon, that's — that's my *face*. That's my scar. I got that scar falling off a ladder in Hollowmere when I was eleven years old, and it's in a mural that's two hundred years old, and—" He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I fell off a ladder. I have a whole life. I have splinters and bad winters and Master Aldous yelling at me about escapements. I'm not — I'm not a *panel*."

"Sebastian—"

"They built me." He turned to look at them and saw his own horror reflected, doubled, tripled. "Or they remade me, or they — I don't know, I don't know *what* I am, but I know what I'm *for*. Look at it. Look at what it says. All those threads going into one chest. That's not a person, that's a *keystone* with a heartbeat. That's the fourth keystone. That's the spare part they keep in case the other three break."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the drifting light-shards seemed to hang more still.

Wren, to her eternal credit, did the most practical thing anyone had done all day: she sat down on the floor, patted the stone beside her, and said, "Sit. You look like you're going to fall over, and I'm not catching you, you're heavy."

Sebastian sat. It was absurd and it helped. The cold of the stone came up through his clothes and gave him something true to hold onto.

Gideon crouched in front of him. Their face, in the mural-light, was carefully blank — too carefully, Sebastian thought distantly, the way a shopkeeper's face is careful when a customer asks about the thing in the back room.

"This doesn't change who you are," Gideon said.

"Doesn't it?"

"No. It changes what other people *wanted* you to be. Those aren't the same thing." Gideon's voice was steady, but their thumb was rubbing a slow circle against their own knee — the tell Sebastian had learned meant *I am choosing my next words very carefully.* "I've spent my whole life being what other people needed. A thief. A seller. A ghost. You know what I learned? The purpose they write on you isn't real until you sign it yourself."

"And if it's already signed?" Sebastian asked. "If it's carved into a wall in a language older than my valley? If the woman in the cradle — if whoever put me in Hollowmere — did it because I'm a *part*, Gideon, a component, and the whole time I thought I was a boy learning to fix clocks—"

"You are a boy who fixes clocks," Wren said firmly, from the floor. "I've seen you fix clocks. You fixed our camp kettle. Badly. A keystone wouldn't have fixed it badly."

Despite everything — despite the hollowed-out feeling behind his ribs, despite the mural watching him with his own eyes — Sebastian huffed something that was almost a laugh.

"She has a point," Gideon said. "I've seen your kettle work. It's a crime against kettles."

"My kettle work is *characterful*—"

"Your kettle screams, Sebastian. It screams like it remembers something terrible."

And just like that, the pressure in his chest loosened, a single degree. Not gone. Nothing about this was gone — the mural would still be there when the jokes faded, the word VESSEL still pulsing its patient ember-red, the blank seventh panel still waiting for its ending. But somewhere in the middle of being teased about kettles, Sebastian found his feet again, and found, tucked underneath the terror, a thin wire of anger. Anger was better. Anger had a direction.

"Whoever made me," he said, standing, "whoever wrote me into this wall — they don't get to finish the sentence. Whatever the seventh panel was supposed to show, we're going to make it something else."

Gideon looked at him a moment too long. Something moved behind their eyes — guilt, or fear, or arithmetic — and was gone before Sebastian could name it.

"We should keep moving," Gideon said. "The keystone of stone won't find itself."

* * *

They passed three more murals on the way out, and Sebastian made himself look at none of them. Some doors, once opened, needed propping shut again until you were strong enough to walk through on purpose.

It was Wren who found the last thing — because of course it was; the child collected secrets the way other people collected buttons. She stopped at a small alcove near the breach, where a side chamber had collapsed inward, and went very still in the way that meant *danger* rather than *wonder*.

"Sebastian," she whispered. "Come see. Bring the thief."

In the alcove, half-buried under fallen stone, lay a skeleton. Old, desiccated, curled as if asleep. Around its neck, on a chain gone green with age, hung a medallion: a circle bisected by a line, with a smaller circle at each end.

And clutched in the skeleton's bony fingers was a scrap of parchment, preserved by warding Sebastian didn't want to think too hard about. The writing on it was cramped, hurried, written by someone shaking:

*If you find this — the vessel lives. We hid the child beyond the wards, in the valley where magic cannot follow. Forgive us. It was the only door left.*

*Do not let them finish the seventh panel.*

Sebastian read it twice. Three times. The words refused to behave like words; they kept turning into a sound like his own heartbeat, very loud.

Beyond the wards. The valley where magic cannot follow. Home.

Someone had carried him through the night the stars went out in reverse — carried him *into* the dark, into Hollowmere, to hide him from the very purpose written on the wall behind him. Someone had loved him enough to make him small and safe and ordinary. Someone had begged whoever found this not to let the story end the way it was drawn.

And the first keystone had been fused to a skeleton wearing his family crest, in a drowned city, and Gideon had lied about recognizing it, and Gideon was looking at the parchment now with an expression Sebastian had seen only once before — the night in the clocktower, caught mid-theft, caught being exactly what they were.

"Gideon," Sebastian said quietly. "What aren't you telling me?"

Behind them, far off in the heart of the broken Spire, something that had been sleeping for two hundred years turned over in its sleep — and the frozen shards of light began, very slowly, to fall.
