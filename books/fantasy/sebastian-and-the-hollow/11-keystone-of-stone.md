## 11. Keystone of Stone

The monastery did not look like a place that had survived the end of the stars.

It clung to the mountainside above the pass like a barnacle — grey stone, grey roofs, a single bell tower leaning at an angle that suggested either great age or a structural disagreement. Smoke rose from its chimneys in thin, orderly ribbons. Lamplight glowed in the windows. And carved above the gate, in letters worn nearly smooth, were the words: *TRUTH IS THE TOLL.*

Wren read them aloud twice, frowning. "That's a trap," she said. "Anything that announces itself like that is a trap."

"Most things are traps," Gideon agreed. "This one's just honest about it."

Sebastian stood beside them in the thin mountain light, the first keystone wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a sleeping infant. He'd barely put it down since Mirrormere. Gideon had noticed. Gideon noticed everything Sebastian did these days, which was becoming a problem of its own.

"They'll want something from us," Sebastian said. "Everything wants something."

"Yes," Gideon said, and tried not to think about the drowned city, or the guardian made of other people's memories, or the hole in their own head where a Tuesday afternoon used to be. "Let's hope they want something we can afford."

* * *

The monk who met them at the gate was ancient and round and entirely bald except for one defiant white eyebrow, which he raised at Wren as though she were an unexpected bill.

"Pilgrims?" he asked.

"Travelers," Gideon said.

"Worse." The monk sighed with the weariness of a man who had said this sentence many times and enjoyed it every time. "I am Brother Anselm. You will eat with us, sleep under our roof, and pay the toll before you leave. Not before you arrive — before you leave. Truth keeps better when it's had time to settle."

"And if we refuse?" Wren asked, hand drifting toward her knife.

Brother Anselm looked at her with mild, bottomless patience. "Then you leave without the stone you came for. Yes, child, we know why you're here. Everyone who climbs this road is here for the stone. It has been two hundred years since anyone climbed this road for any other reason." He turned and shuffled inside, then paused. "There is stew. There is always stew. Do not ask what kind."

It was mushroom. It was possibly also several other things. Wren ate four bowls of it and declared it acceptable, which from Wren was a knighthood.

* * *

They learned the shape of the toll over the next two days, mostly by watching the other pilgrims.

There were only three others — a gaunt merchant who paced the cloisters muttering rehearsals to himself; a young woman with ink-stained fingers who stared into the fire as though it owed her money; and a broad-shouldered farmer who wept quietly at supper and was passed the bread without comment. Each would stand in the chapter house before the assembled brothers and speak aloud some truth they had spent a lifetime avoiding. The monks listened. They did not judge, or comfort, or nod. They simply listened, which Gideon suspected was the harder discipline.

"The stone doesn't care how pretty your lie was," Brother Anselm explained, catching Gideon watching. "Only that you set it down. Some truths are small. One man confessed he'd been pretending to like his brother's poetry for thirty years. We gave him tea afterward. Some truths are—" He looked at Gideon a moment too long. "Larger."

"You know what I am," Gideon said quietly.

"I know what the road brings me." Anselm smiled, and there was nothing gentle in it, only accuracy. "A memory-thief who flinches whenever a child laughs. You've been paying tolls your whole life, Delacroix. Ours are just cheaper."

Gideon walked away from that conversation and did not think about it for the rest of the day, which was to say thought about nothing else.

* * *

On the third evening, Sebastian found Gideon on the wall walk, watching the sun go down over the valley below — a valley, Gideon noted bitterly, that looked uncomfortably like Hollowmere. Green. Sealed. Safe from everything except itself.

"You haven't slept," Sebastian said.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"Your eyes are open."

"That's how I rest them."

Sebastian sat down on the cold stone beside them, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and said nothing, which was the most dangerous thing he could have done. Silence, around Gideon, had a way of pulling truth up like water into a well.

"The merchant goes tomorrow," Sebastian said eventually. "Then the farmer. Then..." He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. There were three pilgrims left, and one of them wasn't going to say anything, because Wren was twelve and had suffered enough and Gideon would burn the monastery down before letting her stand in that chapter house.

Which left two names, and only one of them had been lying professionally for a decade.

"Gideon." Sebastian's voice was careful, the way his hands were careful with clockwork. "What did you give the guardian? In Mirrormere. You fed it something to get me out, and afterward you went strange for half a day, and you won't tell me what it took."

"It took a memory."

"What memory?"

"A boring one."

"Try again."

Gideon watched the last light leave the valley. "You're learning to be stubborn. I'd be proud if it weren't aimed at me."

"I'm serious."

"I know you are. That's the worst part." Gideon pulled their knees up. Somewhere below, the bell tower creaked in the wind. "It doesn't matter what it was, Sebastian. That's the whole point of what I do. What I *did*. A memory leaves and the space closes over and you can't miss what you can't name. It's clean. It's the only clean way to survive that I ever found."

"But you *do* miss it."

Gideon didn't answer.

"You cry sometimes," Sebastian said softly. "At night. When you think we're asleep. You wake up crying and you don't know why, and I've watched you sit there trying to find the shape of it, and you can't, because you sold the reason." His voice cracked slightly. "How many, Gideon? How many times?"

The question hung there. Below them, lamplight bloomed window by window across the monastery, small and warm and utterly indifferent.

"I stopped counting," Gideon said, "because counting meant knowing, and knowing meant stopping, and stopping meant dying. My family was taken by the Ashen Court when I was fourteen. I had skills no fourteen-year-old should have, and a hunger no amount of skill could fix, and someone offered me bread for a memory. Just one. Something small. Something I wouldn't miss." Their laugh came out wrong. "Do you know what a fourteen-year-old considers small? I sold the smell of my mother's kitchen. Then I sold the kitchen. Then I sold her face, piece by piece, because each piece bought another week, and every week I told myself I'd stop, and every week I didn't. By the time I understood what I was doing, there wasn't enough of her left to grieve properly. I'd been grieving in installments."

Sebastian's hand found theirs on the cold stone. Gideon let it stay.

"So tomorrow," Gideon said, "when it's my turn in that chapter house, don't be surprised by what comes out. There's a lot of it. It's been backing up for years."

"I'm not surprised by you," Sebastian said. "Anymore. Mostly I'm just — glad you're still here to surprise."

"That's disgustingly sincere."

"I'm told sincerity is contagious."

"It's a disease," Gideon agreed, and discovered, to their private horror, that they were smiling.

* * *

The merchant went first, confessing that he had once let a bonded servant die rather than lose a shipment, and came out of the chapter house looking ten pounds lighter and twenty years older. The farmer confessed that his wife's death had been a mercy he'd helped along, and the brothers sat with him until dawn, saying nothing, which was apparently the correct medicine.

Then it was Gideon's turn.

The chapter house was smaller than expected — a round room, a ring of cushions, perhaps forty monks arranged like patient stones. No altar. No keystone visible anywhere, though Gideon could feel it now that they knew to feel: a low hum beneath the floor, like a heart beating through stone. The second keystone. Keystone of Stone, keeping its own counsel underground.

Brother Anselm gestured to the center cushion. "Whenever you're ready. Or never. Both are honest answers."

Gideon stood in the middle of the room and looked at all those waiting faces and felt, absurdly, like a pickpocket who had wandered into a hall of mirrors.

"My name is Gideon Delacroix," they began, and their voice came out steadier than they deserved. "I steal memories. Professionally. For eleven years I have taken things people cannot afford to lose and sold them to people who cannot be trusted with them, and I told myself every night that I had no choice, and every morning I chose it again."

Somewhere behind them, they heard Sebastian go very still.

"I stole from the archive in Hollowmere not to hide my past but to bury it deeper, because I'd finally stolen something I couldn't live with, and I hoped if I buried it under enough noise I'd forget I'd done it." The words kept coming now, a dam failing in stages. "I have sold pieces of my mother until she is a stranger wearing her name. I have sold my sister's laugh. I have sold the taste of rain on the roof of my childhood home, and the sound of my father arguing with a recipe, and the specific weight of being loved by people who are now ash, and every sale bought survival, and survival was supposed to be worth it, and some days it isn't."

The monks listened. They did not move. It was the listening that undid Gideon — no gasps, no pity, just forty pairs of eyes holding the truth up so it couldn't slither back down their throat.

"And there is one more thing," Gideon said, and here their voice finally broke. "One theft I have never spoken of, because speaking it makes it real. Years ago, desperate, starving, I sold a memory to a broker in Emberhold without asking what was in it. I never asked. I never wanted to know. But I've had time on this road to remember around the edges of the hole, and I know now what the edges hold. It was a location. A valley. A warded cradle. I sold the hiding place of the lastborn of the covenant line, and I have spent years running from that debt without once admitting I owed it." They turned, and found Sebastian's eyes, and did not look away, because looking away would have been one more theft. "I'm sorry. I didn't know it was you. But I didn't check, either, because checking might have cost me the coin, and I have been a coward in small ways my whole life, and this is the largest of them."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Sebastian crossed the room. Forty monks watched him do it. He took Gideon's face in both hands the way he handled the delicate insides of clocks — precisely, reverently, aware that everything important was happening somewhere invisible.

"You didn't know," he said.

"I didn't *ask*."

"You didn't know." He said it again, firmer, like tightening a screw. "And you've spent every day since Mirrormere feeding pieces of yourself into the dark to keep me alive. I've seen the gaps, Gideon. I've counted your Tuesdays." A wet, broken laugh escaped him. "We're even. We're more than even. And when we walk out of here, we stop keeping accounts. Agreed?"

Gideon, who had built an entire life on ledgers, nodded.

Behind them, stone ground against stone. The floor at the center of the chapter house had opened, and rising from it, on a column of pale rock, came the second keystone — smaller than Gideon expected, no bigger than a fist, grey as the mountain and shot through with veins of gold that pulsed slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Brother Anselm lifted it and held it out to Sebastian, not Gideon, which felt correct in a way Gideon declined to examine.

"Take it," the old monk said. "And take a warning with it, freely given, because you've paid honestly. The third keystone does not wait in some ruin or some deep. It sits in Emberhold, on the throne of the Ashen Court, and it feeds there. Cassian Vey wears it like a crown and calls himself its keeper." His eyebrow rose. "You mean to take a stone from the head of the man who destroyed your families. So eat well tonight. Sleep if you can. And whatever you three are to each other—"

He glanced at Sebastian's hands, still cradling Gideon's face, and at Wren in the doorway pretending she hadn't been eavesdropping and doing it badly.

"—keep it. It's rarer than keystones, and worth more."

* * *

That night, Gideon sat with Sebastian on the wall walk again, the keystone between them, warm as a loaf of fresh bread.

"He's going to offer you something," Gideon said. "Cassian. When we come for his crown, he'll offer you the world, and he'll make it sound like mercy."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's what I would have done," Gideon said, "back when I was the person who took things instead of giving them." They leaned, deliberately, until their shoulder rested against Sebastian's. "But you're not going to take it. And neither am I. We're done paying in the old currency, you and I."

Above them, the sky was still starless — had been since the night the wards fell — but the monastery lamps burned in their windows, and Wren was asleep inside with her knife under her pillow and, for the first time, her boots pointing toward companions instead of away from them.

Small lights, Gideon thought. Enough to steer by.

Tomorrow, Emberhold. Tomorrow, the man with the crown of borrowed light.

Tonight, for once, there was nothing left to sell, and nothing left to hide, and Gideon found that the emptiness where all those stolen years had been was not empty after all. It was full of the present tense: cold stone, warm shoulder, a boy who forgave debts no one should have to carry.

It was, they decided, a fair trade.
