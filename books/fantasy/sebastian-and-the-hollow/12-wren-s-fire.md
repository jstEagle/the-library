## 12. Wren's Fire

Wren had survived three winters alone on the mountain by following one rule: *never sleep deeper than a cat.*

She'd learned it the hard way, back when she was nine and still stupid with grief, when a rockslide nearly buried her because she'd curled up in a hollow like a tired rabbit instead of listening to the mountain first. The mountain always told you things, if you listened. The way birds went quiet. The way snow held its breath. The way her own ward-marks — the ones scratched into stone at every pass and hollow, the ones nobody had taught her to make but that came out of her hands anyway, crooked and stubborn as weeds — hummed a half-tone lower when something wrong was coming.

Tonight they were humming.

She sat up in the dark of the camp, knees pulled to her chest, and counted the sounds. Sebastian breathing slow and even in his bedroll. Gideon's breath, lighter, the breath of someone who'd never once slept all the way through — Wren recognized it because it was her own kind of breathing. The fire down to embers, ticking. The mule stamping once, twice.

And underneath all of it, far off down the pass: iron. Horses shod in iron, moving at a walk, which was worse than a gallop. A gallop meant panic. A walk meant patience.

Wren knew about patience. She'd been patient for three years.

She should have run then. That was the rule above all the other rules: *when the wards hum, you go.* She'd built her whole life on going — going before dawn, going without goodbyes, going light enough that nothing could ever catch her. It was a good rule. It had kept her alive when everyone else who'd kept rules like it was dead.

But Sebastian was asleep with his hand half outside his blanket, fingers curled loose, like he trusted the night. Like he trusted *them*. And Gideon was sleeping sitting up against a boulder, which was funny, actually, the way they'd argued for an hour about taking first watch and then lost the argument to their own exhaustion mid-sentence, and now their head kept tipping forward and jerking back like a bird drinking.

Wren looked at them for a long time.

*Never trust anyone enough to fight beside them,* that was the other rule. Because fighting beside someone meant staying, and staying meant they could be taken from you, and Wren had already been taken from once — taken *from*, she corrected herself, there was a difference, her mother had taught her the difference with her hands pressed over Wren's ears while the riders came up the valley road — and she did not intend to learn what it felt like from the other side.

The wards hummed again. Lower now.

"Idiots," she whispered, to both of them, to herself, to the whole stupid arrangement of people who had somehow become hers without asking her permission. "Absolute idiots."

Then she got up, quiet as frost forming, and went to wake them anyway.

* * *

They didn't believe her at first, which was insulting, because Wren had never once been wrong about the mountain. Not once. She said so, several times, while Gideon kicked dirt over the embers and Sebastian stood with his head cocked toward the pass like he could hear what she heard.

"I don't hear anything," he said.

"You wouldn't." She was already kicking snow over their tracks, erasing the camp the way she erased all her camps, fast and thorough. "You've been walled up in your little valley your whole life where nothing bad ever happened until last month. I've been listening to this mountain since before you knew how to shave. Iron shoes, walking pace, six horses maybe seven. They stopped at the shrine two miles down."

Gideon had gone very still in the way they did — the whole-body stillness of a person calculating exits. "Hunters?"

"Cassian's?" Sebastian asked.

"I didn't wait to ask their names," Wren snapped. "Move."

They moved. And here was the thing, the thing that made her chest hurt even while she was too busy to feel it: they moved *with* her. Sebastian didn't argue or blunder ahead. Gideon didn't take over and start issuing orders like they knew better. They just — watched her hands, and followed her lead, and when she pointed at the goat track that climbed behind the waterfall they took it without question, single file, Gideon's hand briefly steady on her shoulder as they passed into the narrow dark.

Nobody had ever followed her lead before. People followed her *rules*, sometimes, if they wanted to live. But following wasn't trusting, and this — the three of them climbing blind through freezing spray because a twelve-year-old said so — this was trusting, and it terrified her more than the hunters did.

The pass narrowed where the mountain had split itself in some old violence, walls rising sheer on either side, a throat of stone barely wide enough for the mule. There was no way around it. Wren had checked years ago, the way she checked everything, walking the whole ridge with her eyes shut and her palms open, learning the shape of the place.

Which meant the hunters would come through the throat. And the throat could be closed.

She'd known that for three years too. She'd just never had a reason to close it.

"Wren." Gideon had caught her arm. Their face was pale in the starless dark — the stars still hadn't come back, and Wren hated it, hated the naked sky like a ceiling ripped off a house. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking you two take the mule up the switchback and get over the saddle before dawn."

"And you?"

"I'm going to hold the pass."

Silence, except the waterfall. Sebastian said, "You mean hide."

"No," Wren said, "I don't."

She showed them instead of telling, because words were Gideon's tool, not hers. She pressed her palm flat against the canyon wall, against one of her marks — the crooked spiral she'd scratched there her first winter, when she didn't know what she was doing and the magic had come out of her anyway, wild and half-formed. Under her hand, the mark woke. Light crawled along the grooves like water finding a channel, faint gold, and the stone *sang*, low and deep, a note she felt in her teeth.

Sebastian made a small sound. Gideon said, very quietly, "That's ward-work. Old ward-work. Who taught you—"

"Nobody taught me anything." She pulled her hand back and the light died and she immediately missed it, the way you miss a fire after you've left a warm room. "My mother's people kept the roads. Ward-keepers. Before the Court burned them out. I'm what's left, and what's left is enough to drop a canyon wall on seven men with iron shoes, probably."

"*Probably?*" Sebastian's voice cracked upward.

"Definitely," she amended, because he looked like he might cry otherwise, and honestly, boys.

"We're not leaving you," he said.

"Yes you are."

"No," he said again, and stepped closer, and Wren discovered that being argued with by someone who actually meant to stay was a completely different experience than being argued with by someone trying to take something from you. He wasn't trying to take anything. He was just standing there, being stubborn at her, with soot still on his cheek from yesterday's fire and his ridiculous apprentice's hands hanging at his sides like they weren't the hands that had pulled a keystone out of a drowned city. "You said the wards hummed. You woke us up. You got us moving. Whatever you're about to do, we do it together — that's the deal, that's been the deal since the Spire."

"That's not a deal, that's you being reckless."

"It's both," Gideon said. "It's usually both."

And there it was again, that feeling, the awful warm one, the one she kept trying to evict like a squirrel in the woodpile. *Fight beside them.* Her whole body said run. Three years of running said run, said *go light, go now, go alone*, said the only person you can't lose is the one you never had.

The wards down the pass hummed again. Closer. The hunters had reached the mouth of the throat.

"Fine," Wren said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "But you do exactly what I say. Sebastian — the switchback, halfway up, where the ledge overlooks the throat. If I fall, you catch me. Can you catch me?"

He looked at the height, and at her, and swallowed. "Yes."

"Liar. Try anyway. Gideon — the mule, the packs, everything goes over the saddle and up the far trail. If we're not at the cairn by midmorning, you keep going and you don't look back."

"We will look back," Gideon said.

"Then look *while running*, idiot."

For a second — just a second — Gideon smiled. It transformed their whole sharp, guarded face into something younger. "Yes, ward-keeper."

Ward-keeper. Not *girl*. Not *feral child*. Ward-keeper. Wren turned away quickly so neither of them could see what her face did about it, and put her palm back against the stone, and began to work.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about holding a pass: it is not dramatic, at first. It is arithmetic.

Each mark she'd cut into the canyon over three years was a note, and tonight she had to make them into a chord. She ran the throat of stone heel to toe, waking them one by one — the spiral, the ladder-scratch, the ugly lopsided eye she'd carved when she was nine and furious — pouring herself into each one, and each one drank from her a little. That was the part nobody told you either. Wards didn't run on nothing. Wards ran on the keeper. Her mother had explained it once, kneeling in the snow, drawing lines in the frost: *the road remembers who keeps it, little bird, and memory is heavy, so carry only what you can lift.*

Her mother had carried a lot. Her mother had held a bridge for an hour against the Ashen Court's vanguard so that forty people could cross and scatter into the high country, and when the bridge finally fell, her mother fell with it, and Wren had watched from the treeline because her mother's last instruction had been *run, little bird, run and don't watch* — and Wren had run, and she had watched anyway, over her shoulder, the way you can't help looking at a wound.

Forty people lived because of that hour. Wren had spent three years deciding whether that was worth it. Tonight she stopped deciding. Tonight there were two idiots and a mule on a switchback above her, and seven riders coming up the throat, and the answer was suddenly very simple.

The first torchlight bloomed at the mouth of the pass.

They came in careful, the way professionals move — spread wide, torches high, checking the walls. Wren flattened herself into a crevice above the throat's narrowest point, heart slamming, and did the last thing, the big thing, the thing she'd never done because she'd never had anyone to do it *for*: she opened every mark at once.

The canyon lit up gold.

Light poured through her scratches and spirals like the stone had veins, and the whole throat became a cage of burning script, and the hunters' horses screamed and reared, and someone shouted a word — *ward-keeper!* — and Wren stood up on her ledge with her arms out and her hair whipping and felt, for the first time in three years, enormous.

"THE ROAD IS KEPT," she shouted, in the old formula, the one her mother used, the one that was supposed to be spoken by a circle of trained keepers and not one underfed twelve-year-old alone in the dark. "TURN BACK OR BE TURNED."

To their credit, they almost turned back. She saw the hesitation ripple through them, saw one rider wheel. Then a man in the center — gray cloak, gray eyes, a voice like a file drawn across iron — raised his hand and said something she couldn't hear, and produced a rod of black metal, and touched it to the nearest wall.

Where it touched, her mark *died*. Not faded. Died. The gold drained out of it like blood from a wound, and the stone beneath went gray and cold, and Wren felt it the way you'd feel a tooth pulled — a shocking absence, a hole in her own body.

Ashen counter-wards. Of course. Of course they'd brought tools for this; they'd burned her mother's people with these same tools, probably, these exact rods, these gray methodical men.

Fine. Fine. She was her mother's daughter, and her mother had held a bridge for an hour.

Wren stopped defending and started spending.

She dropped the first rockslide on them from the western wall — not killing anyone, she was relieved to notice later, mostly because she wasn't aiming, she was just *pulling*, hauling on the mountain's old broken seams the way you'd haul on a rope, and the seam gave and half a cliffside came down across the throat's entrance with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Two horses went down screaming. The riders scattered back.

The gray-eyed man walked through the dust and started killing her marks again, methodically, one touch at a time.

So she spent more. She pulled heat out of the stones and made ice slick the footing. She woke an old avalanche scar and let it grumble threateningly. She stitched light between the walls in nets that stung like wasps. Each working cost her — she could feel it going, warmth draining out through her fingertips, her vision going spotty and bright at the edges, her mother's voice in her memory getting fainter, *carry only what you can lift, little bird* — and she kept lifting, kept carrying, because the switchback above her was full of people she loved and she had only just admitted that word to herself and she would be damned if she'd lose them the same week she'd found them.

The gray man reached the middle of the throat. Behind him, more riders — eight now, not seven, she'd miscounted, she never miscounted, except she had — and the black rod came up toward the ladder-scratch, her oldest surviving mark, and Wren did the last thing.

She gave the mountain everything.

Not a working. A *giving*. Every scrap of warmth, every stored-up winter of loneliness and stubbornness and love-with-nowhere-to-go, poured down through her palms into the stone all at once, unshaped, unmeasured, the way her mother must have done at the end on the bridge. The whole canyon detonated into light. The eastern wall groaned and leaned. The gray man's rod shattered in his hand. Horses bolted back down the pass, riders clinging, and the throat filled with dust and thunder and gold—

—and then, quietly, the light went out of her marks forever, and the mountain let go of Wren, and Wren fell.

* * *

She didn't remember falling. She remembered falling *stopped* — an impact that knocked the world sideways, arms around her, a heartbeat hammering against her ear that wasn't hers, a voice saying her name over and over like a spell, "Wren, Wren, Wren, stay awake, stay with me, you absolute mule-headed—"

Sebastian. Sebastian had caught her. Liar, she thought fuzzily. He'd said he could catch her and he'd lied and then he'd done it anyway.

Everything was cold. That was the bad sign. She knew the bad signs; she'd catalogued them over three winters. Cold that starts in the fingers and doesn't leave. The loud silence in your ears. The way your own body feels like borrowed equipment.

"Her lips are blue," Sebastian was saying, somewhere above her, his voice shredded with panic. "Gideon, her lips are blue, what do I do—"

"Warmth. Fire. Now." Gideon's voice, stripped of all its cleverness, raw underneath. Hands on her face, her wrists. "Wren. Look at me. What did you spend? Tell me what you spent."

"Everything," she said. It came out as mostly breath. "Told you. Everything."

"Why would you—" Gideon's voice cracked clean through. "Why would you *do* that, we told you to run, we *told* you—"

"Because," Wren said, and the word cost her, and she paid it, "you'd have looked back."

Above her, the sky was still starless, but the dust was settling over the pass, and somewhere below, the gray man and his riders were retreating or regrouping or dragging their horses out from under a rockfall, and none of it mattered, none of it, because Sebastian had gathered her up off the ground like she weighed nothing — like she was something worth carrying — and started up the switchback at a stumbling run, and Gideon ran beside him with one hand splayed against Sebastian's back as if they could push warmth into him and through him into her.

Wren let her head loll against Sebastian's chest. His heart was going like a clockwork gone mad. She thought, distantly, that he was crying, and that he wasn't hiding it, and that nobody had ever cried over her before, and that she didn't know what to do with that except hold onto his collar with fingers that barely worked and refuse, absolutely refuse, to die now, because dying would mean missing whatever came next, and for the first time in three years there *was* a next. There were two idiots and a mule and a cairn at the saddle and a keystone waiting in a tower of ash, and Wren had chosen them, over the rule, over survival, over everything, and she intended to be insufferable about that choice for a very long time.

"Don't you dare die," Sebastian said into her hair. "You're the only one who knows where we're going."

"Rude," she whispered. "Reasons to live. Rude ones."

"Good," he said. "Stay rude."

The last thing she saw before the dark took her, gently, like a blanket drawn up, was Gideon's face above hers — pale, tear-streaked, terrified — and the last thing she thought was that they were all three of them liars, and cowards about the wrong things, and hers, entirely hers, and that the mountain had told her true:

Something wrong had been coming.

But something right had stayed.

* * *

She woke to firelight and the smell of burnt porridge, which meant Sebastian had cooked, which meant the crisis was truly over, because Gideon would rather starve than let him near a pot.

Her whole body ached like weather. When she tried to sit up, the world tilted politely and waited for her to reconsider. Gideon pushed her back down with two fingers, gentle as anything, and handed her a tin cup of melted snow with pine needles steeped in it, which tasted terrible and was the best thing she'd ever drunk.

"The pass?" she managed.

"Held," Sebastian said. He was sitting close, soot-faced, eyes red-rimmed, watching her like she might evaporate. "Collapsed the whole throat. They'll dig for days, and the far route adds four days to any pursuit. Gideon says it was the finest ward-work they've ever seen."

"Gideon says," Wren echoed, suspicious.

"Gideon is not allowed to say anything," Gideon said, "because Gideon spent the entire fight hiding a mule, and has been informed of this repeatedly." But their voice was rough, and they wouldn't quite meet her eyes, and Wren understood, suddenly, that they'd been frightened for her — properly frightened, the way you only get frightened for family.

She drank her terrible tea and let that settle into her bones alongside the ache. Family. Hers. Chosen, fought-for, rude.

"There's something else," Sebastian said, after a while. He'd gone strange and quiet, the way he did when he'd seen something in a keystone-memory he didn't want to say aloud. He turned his cup in his hands. "While you were... while we were carrying you, Gideon told me something. About Cassian. About why he wants me." He looked up, and his jaw was set in a way she'd never seen before, young and scared and decided all at once. "I'm not what they think I am. Or — I am, but I won't be it. And there's something Gideon's been carrying that they need to tell us both. Tonight. Before we reach Emberhold."

Gideon flinched. Actually flinched, the memory-thief, the smooth-tongued, unshakeable Gideon Delacroix, caught out in the open with nowhere to hide.

Wren looked between them, sipping her tea, and said, "Well. Don't keep the mountain waiting."
