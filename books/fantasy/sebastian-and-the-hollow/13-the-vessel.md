## 13. The Vessel

Sebastian woke to the smell of woodsmoke and someone burning porridge with great confidence.

"You're supposed to stir it," he said, before his eyes were fully open.

"I am stirring it," Wren said. She was stirring it with a stick she had clearly found on the ground and decided was a spoon now, by decree.

"That's a stick."

"It's a stick that's stirring," she said, with the unassailable logic of a twelve-year-old who had survived alone in the mountains since the stars went out. "You're welcome, by the way. For not dying."

He was, he discovered, lying under three blankets and what appeared to be an entire pine bough arranged over him like a roof, because Wren did nothing by halves. His ribs ached where the guardian's blow had landed — no, not the guardian's. Gideon's. Gideon had thrown themselves between Sebastian and the thing in the water, and had paid for it with another piece of their own memory, another hole scooped out of themselves like ice from a winter pond. They were sitting across the fire now, wrapped in a cloak, very still, watching him with the careful expression of someone who had been waiting for him to wake up and was now wishing he hadn't quite so soon.

"How long?" Sebastian asked.

"Two days," Gideon said.

"Two days." He sat up too fast; the world tilted and he let it. Two days of lying still meant two days of thinking, and he knew exactly what two days of thinking looked like on Gideon Delacroix's face. It looked like a locked door with the key already burned.

Wren shoved a bowl at him. The porridge was scorched on one side and raw on the other, which he privately felt took talent. He ate it anyway, because refusing food in front of Wren was apparently a crime punishable by being told about it forever.

"Good," she said. "Now you can walk. We're leaving the pass today. I don't like how quiet it is. Quiet means something's listening."

She went off to kick out the fire and pack her impossible collection of things — bird skulls, a tin whistle with no holes, a rock she'd named — and Sebastian was left alone with Gideon across the flames.

The Spire's murals had followed him into sleep. He hadn't dreamed anything else. Just the long gallery of painted stone, the ancestors binding realms together with threads of light, and then that last figure, standing apart from all the others, arms open, face turned upward as though waiting to be filled. *The vessel.* Painted in colors so close to his own that when he'd first seen it, some animal part of him had thought: *there I am.*

"Gideon," he said quietly. "Look at me."

Gideon looked at him. They always could do that — meet his eyes without flinching, which should have been reassuring and instead, this morning, felt like a duelist squaring up.

"The figure in the mural," Sebastian said. "The vessel. You knew. When we saw it, you already knew what it meant. You didn't even look surprised."

"Sebastian—"

"Don't." His voice cracked on it, which infuriated him. "Don't do the thing where you say my name like it's an answer. You've been doing it since Mirrormere. Since the cradle memory. Since the skeleton wearing my family's crest at the bottom of a drowned city, which you lied about recognizing, yes, I saw your face, I'm a clockmaker's apprentice, Gideon, my entire life is noticing small things that don't move the way they should."

For once, Gideon had nothing ready. That frightened him more than any answer would have.

* * *

They walked most of the morning before he said anything else, because Wren was there, and because some part of him — the cowardly part, he later decided — wanted to be wrong. Wanted Gideon to volunteer it. Wanted the truth offered freely instead of pried loose, because if it had to be pried, then it was true, and if it was true—

The pass narrowed between walls of gray stone furred with lichen. Somewhere below them a stream ran cold and quick. Wren ranged ahead the way she always did, walking the edges of things, touching old ward-stones with two fingers like she was greeting old friends who owed her money.

Sebastian stopped walking.

"Gideon. Tell me what the vessel is."

Ahead, Wren paused too, glancing back. She was learning, slowly, to read the weather between the two of them. She dropped back a few paces but didn't leave, and Sebastian found he didn't want her to. Whatever was coming, he didn't want to hear it in a vacuum. He wanted witnesses. Witnesses made it real, and real things could be fought.

Gideon stood very straight, hands empty at their sides, the way they stood when they were about to sell something.

"A covenant needs an anchor," they said. "That's what the murals show. The keystones are the lock, but a lock needs something living to turn it. Something bound to both realms. The old covenant-keepers used... a person. Made for the purpose. Born of the magic itself, so the magic would recognize its own."

"And when the covenant broke—" Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "When the war broke it. What happened to the anchor?"

Gideon's silence stretched so long that Wren, behind them, whispered, "Oh," in a small voice, and then clapped a hand over her own mouth as if she could take it back.

"The anchor isn't consumed all at once," Gideon said. "It's poured out. Slowly. Every year, a little more, until—" They stopped. Started again, flatter. "Until there's nothing left but the shape of a person. The court calls them hollows. You've seen them, in Emberhold. The smiling servants with nothing behind their eyes. That's what a spent anchor looks like."

The wind came down the pass and moved through the pines, and Sebastian heard it, distantly, from very far away, because he was standing inside the sound of his own heartbeat.

"And me," he said. "I'm—"

"You're the lastborn of the covenant line. Cassian told you that much, and Cassian doesn't lie about things that can be checked. Your mother sealed you in a warded cradle and sent you into Hollowmere, where magic couldn't reach you, where the covenant couldn't find you. She wasn't hiding you from danger, Sebastian. She was hiding you from *purpose*. From being used up."

"So if we restore the covenant." Each word came out level and cold, and he was proud of that, right up until the fourth word, which came out shaking. "If we gather the three keystones and reforge it. Then the covenant takes me. Pours me out. Year by year until I'm one of those smiling, empty—"

"Yes."

"*Yes?*" The coldness shattered all at once, and what came through the crack wasn't anger yet, it was fear, pure and enormous, and fear in Sebastian Vale had always come out sideways, as fury. "And you KNEW. You've known since—when? Mirrormere? Before Mirrormere? How long have you been walking me toward my own death with your little sad looks and your 'we'll find another way,' Gideon, HOW LONG?"

"Since before I met you!" Gideon's voice broke open too, and that stopped him, because he had never heard Gideon break. Not in the clocktower, not in the water beneath Mirrormere, not bleeding in the dark. "Since years ago, since I first read the archives, since I learned what the covenant line was and what happened to every lastborn before you. Do you think I stole my way across half a realm by accident? Do you think I ended up in Hollowmere, of all the valleys in all the world, by chance?"

The words fell into the space between them like stones into a well.

"What are you saying," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. He already knew. Some part of him had known since the clocktower, since the moment a stranger with stolen eyes had looked at him and chosen him, out of everyone, to run with.

"I was looking for you," Gideon said. "Before I ever met you, I was looking for you. Because I needed a way to restore the covenant without feeding it a life, and the only person alive who might matter enough to change the terms was the anchor himself. And then I found you, and you were—" Their voice did something complicated and gave up. "You were a person. A real one. Who makes clocks and burns porridge and laughs at bad jokes, and I thought, oh no. Oh no, I've been hunting a boy, and he's *kind*, and I'm going to ruin him."

Wren had gone very still by the ward-stone, her hand flat against it, watching them both with wide eyes. She didn't understand all of it — Sebastian could see that — but she understood fighting, and grief, and people standing too close to a cliff edge.

"You should have told me," Sebastian said. Quieter now. Worse, somehow, than shouting. "At Mirrormere. At the Spire. You should have let me choose whether to keep walking."

"If I told you, you'd stop. And if you stop, the realms keep failing, the wards keep falling, and everything your mother died protecting comes apart anyway. And if I tell you and you *don't* stop, then every step after that, you're walking to your own execution knowing it, and I—" Gideon pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "I've watched people get emptied, Sebastian. I grew up selling pieces of myself to avoid it. I could not bear to watch you do it knowingly. I know that's selfish. I know it's theft, of all things, from *me*, the thief. But I kept thinking: I'll find the way around it. There has to be a way around it. Covenants are just bargains, and bargains have terms, and terms can be—"

"Amended," Sebastian said.

Gideon lowered their hand. Looked at him.

"You said that once," Sebastian said. "In Emberhold. 'Every bargain has terms, and terms can be amended.' I thought you were talking about smugglers' tolls." He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and jagged. "You've been looking for a loophole in my death sentence this whole time."

"I haven't found it," Gideon said simply. "That's the truth you deserve, at least. I don't have the answer. I have fragments. The covenant accepts what is *given* and rejects what is *taken* — that's in the oldest records, the ones the Court burned copies of. Everything else is guesswork and hope, and I know how that sounds coming from me."

"It sounds like a thief's plan," Sebastian said.

"It is a thief's plan."

Something in him wanted to laugh again, properly this time, and couldn't quite get there. He turned away and walked a few steps along the trail, boots crunching on frost-brittle grass, and put his hands over his face, and breathed.

He was angry. He was so angry he could taste copper. And underneath the anger, doing all the actual work, was the fear — the vast, cold, patient fear of a boy who had just learned that his whole life in Hollowmere, every gear and spring and quiet evening in the clocktower, had been a shelf someone hid him on. That he had been made, or remade, or marked, for a purpose written before he could walk. That the woman in the cradle memory — his mother, *his mother* — had spent her last strength not saving him but *storing* him.

And underneath even that, worse than all of it: Gideon had known. Had carried it alone, all this way, paying piece after piece of themselves to keep him moving toward safety that wasn't safe, and had never once asked him to carry any of it.

That was the part that hurt. Not the lie. The loneliness of it.

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of a ridge, because Wren declared the sky "the wrong color" and Wren's declarations about weather had so far been annoyingly accurate. She built the fire with the efficiency of someone who had done it several thousand times and then sat close to Sebastian, closer than usual, shoulder almost touching his arm, which was how she said things she couldn't say.

"You're not going to do it," she said. It wasn't a question either. Everyone was having a day of statements dressed as questions.

"Do what?"

"The pouring-out thing." She poked the fire. "You're going to do what I did. When the hunters came for my village and the ward-keepers all ran. Everyone said the wards needed a keeper's whole life, bonded forever, and I believed them for a whole day, and then I got angry instead." She shrugged. "Angry is better. Angry finds doors."

"Wren—"

"I'm just saying." She jabbed the stick at him. "You're the angriest person I've met who pretends to be polite. Use it."

Then she lay down with her back to them both, radiating the message that the conversation was over and also that she would kill anyone who hurt him, in that order.

Night came down. The sky stayed starless — it had been starless since the night everything broke, and Sebastian had almost stopped looking up, almost. Gideon sat on the far side of the fire, mending a strap on their pack with small precise stitches, and Sebastian watched them for a long time.

Here was the thing he kept circling and couldn't land on: he understood. That was the trap of it. He understood why Gideon hadn't told him, understood it the way you understand a mechanism once you see the gears — fear driving guilt driving secrecy driving more fear, a clockwork of self-punishment with no off-switch. Gideon had sold memories to survive and hated themselves for it, and here they were, caught mid-transaction again, except the goods this time were the truth and the buyer was him.

"You're doing it again," Sebastian said at last.

Gideon's needle paused. "Doing what?"

"Deciding for me. Right now. Sitting over there deciding I need space, deciding you'll tell me things in measured doses, deciding what I can bear." He got up and crossed the fire circle and sat down next to them, close enough that their shoulders touched, and felt Gideon go rigid as a bowstring. "I'm not glass, Gideon. I'm a clockmaker. I know what I'm made of. Brass and stubbornness and a mainspring that won't quit. So here's how this works from now on: you don't get to protect me from the truth. You especially don't get to protect me from truths about *me*. If you've found a fragment, I hear it. If you've found nothing, I hear that too. We carry it together or not at all."

Gideon was silent for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was rough. "Together or not at all."

"Together or not at all." He exhaled, and some of the fury went out of him with the breath, leaving behind something sore but cleaner. "And Gideon? The fragments. The 'covenant accepts what is given.' Start from the beginning. All of it. Tonight."

"There's... more than fragments," Gideon said, and something in their tone made Sebastian turn to look at them properly. Gideon was staring into the fire, and their hands had gone still around the mending, and their face wore an expression Sebastian had never seen on them before — not guilt, exactly. Dread. The specific dread of a person approaching a door they had nailed shut from the inside a long time ago.

"What kind of more?"

"When I ran from the Ashen Court, the first time — before your valley, before any of it — I kept a cache. Memories I stole rather than sold. The ones I couldn't bear to lose and couldn't bear to keep in my head." Gideon's throat worked. "I hid it well. Too well. I've been telling myself for years I couldn't remember where."

"But you remember where."

"I think," Gideon said quietly, "that whatever's in it is the reason I started running in the first place. And I think it's time we opened it."

The fire popped. Behind them, Wren — who was absolutely not asleep, because Wren never actually slept, she just powered down like a banked stove — rolled over and said, "Finally. Some *plot*."

Sebastian didn't laugh. He was looking at the road ahead, down out of the mountains, toward wherever a thief hides the worst thing they've ever done — and at the woman in the cradle memory, turning over and over in his mind, sealing the warded lid, whispering something he'd never been able to hear.

He had a sudden, terrible feeling that she hadn't been whispering goodbye.

She'd been whispering instructions.
