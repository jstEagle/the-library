## 14. What Gideon Stole

The cache had traveled with Gideon for eleven years in one form or another — a locket once, then a hollowed prayer bead, then a sliver of warded glass sewn into a coat lining, and now a small black vial that hung against their sternum on a leather cord, warm from their skin, light as a lie.

They had never opened it.

That was the whole point of it. The cache was not for looking at. The cache was the shape of the hole they'd dug around themselves and called a life. Every memory too dangerous to keep loose in their head, every memory that might betray them if someone pried — sealed, stoppered, carried like a stone they'd convinced themselves was a talisman.

Tonight, sitting cross-legged on the floor of Wren's mountain shelter while Sebastian slept by the fire and Wren herself snored in the loft like a small avalanche, Gideon took the cord over their head and held the vial up to the flames.

It would be so easy not to.

They thought about that. They were, professionally speaking, an expert on easy things. Easy things were what they sold other people: the memory of a first kiss, smoothed and bottled; the memory of a funeral, dulled until it stopped cutting. People paid handsomely for easy. Gideon had built a career on the observation that nearly everyone, given the choice between carrying pain and setting it down, would set it down and never once look back to see who picked it up.

And here they sat, holding their own heaviest thing, having looked away from it for eleven years.

"Sentimental idiot," Gideon murmured, and unstopped the vial.

* * *

Memory, when you knew how to handle it, had texture. A stolen recollection poured out like oil or water or smoke depending on its weight. Gideon tipped the vial's contents into their cupped palms and let it rise, and it rose the way cold iron rises — slowly, reluctantly, with a smell of frost and old blood.

The oldest memories came first. They always did. Memory sorted itself by gravity.

A kitchen. Their mother's hands dusted with flour, tapping rhythm on the table — *one-two-three, one-two-three*, the counting song she used for everything, kneading and mending and rocking the baby next door when his mother needed sleep. Gideon, eight years old, standing on a stool to reach the counter. The window open. Rain coming down the mountainside in silver ropes.

Gideon braced themselves for this one. This was the grief they knew. They had walked past this kitchen a hundred times in the dark of their own skull, and it hurt the way an old break hurts, dully, familiarly.

But the memory kept going.

Because the cache wasn't one memory. It was everything they'd ever been afraid to hold at once, and gravity pulled the worst of it down beneath the flour and the rain, down into the deep strata where they'd buried the thing they'd spent eleven years not remembering that they were not remembering.

A market day. Gideon, nineteen, starving. Not hungry — starving, the difference being that hunger is a feeling and starvation is a decision your body starts making without you. Three weeks since the Ashen Court's collectors had come through the valley town where they'd grown up, three weeks since their mother had stood in the doorway with her arms spread and told Gideon to run, and Gideon had run, because they were fifteen and fast and cowardly in the way that keeps people alive.

A broker's stall in a border town. A fat man with kind eyes and a scale for weighing memory like fish.

"You'll get better rates for older," the broker said. "Childhood goes high. Everyone wants childhood."

"I'm not selling my childhood," Gideon said, with the fierce dignity of someone who had nothing else left to be fierce about.

"Then what have you got?"

And Gideon — this was the part, this was the part they had never let themselves see again — Gideon had thought about it. Had actually stood there in the cold and inventoried what they knew that might be worth coin. And what they knew, what a courier's daughter from a border family knew, what they'd overheard through thin walls and half-open doors their whole life, was talk. Covenant talk. Lastborn talk. A woman of the old line, fleeing east with a child wrapped in wards, heading for a valley where magic couldn't follow. Talk of safe houses. Talk of a name.

Gideon had sold the conversation. All of it. Every scrap of overheard covenant-whisper, bundled neat as kindling, for enough coin to eat for a month and a room with a lock on the door.

The broker had smiled. "Oh, this is good stock," he'd said. "This is very good stock." And he'd paid triple, which should have been the warning, and he'd asked no questions, which should have been the second warning, and Gideon had walked away with bread and cheese and a full belly and had slept twelve hours and dreamed of nothing at all.

The memory ended there. It ended there because that was where Gideon had cut it, years later, when they finally understood what they'd done — cut it clean at the moment of sale, sealed it in black glass, and told themselves the story ended with a full belly.

But memory doesn't end where you cut it. Memory ends where it ends.

Because Gideon knew what came after. They knew it the way you know a scar is there even through a sleeve. Somewhere east, a woman running with a warded cradle had found her road watched. Somewhere east, hunters who had lost the trail for two years found it again in a single afternoon, because a starving teenager in a border market had sold them the map to it. Somewhere east, the lastborn of the covenant line had made it to Hollowmere anyway — barely, narrowly, at some cost Gideon would never know — and had grown up in a clockmaker's shop with no idea why his dreams sometimes smelled of frost and old blood.

Sebastian Vale existed because his mother had outrun the people Gideon had fed.

Or — the thought arrived like ice water — Sebastian Vale was all that remained of his line because everyone else who ran that road had not outrun them.

Gideon sat very still. The fire popped. Above, Wren muttered something in her sleep about turnips.

Eleven years. Eleven years of thieving and running and selling pieces of themselves to stay ahead of the Ashen Court, and they had told themselves it was survival, it was spite, it was staying free. And it was. But underneath it, in the place they never looked, it had always been this: a debt with legs, running. You can't outrun a debt. You can only make sure it never catches up long enough to introduce itself.

Well. It had caught up. It was sleeping ten feet away, breathing softly, with soot on his cheekbones and a keystone in his pack and no idea at all.

* * *

"Gideon."

Gideon started so hard they nearly knocked the empty vial into the fire. Sebastian crouched across the embers, hair flattened on one side, watching them with the particular stillness he'd learned somewhere along this terrible road — the stillness of someone who has learned that people tell you true things when they think you're not asking.

"How long have you been awake?"

"Awhile." He nodded at Gideon's hands. They were still cupped, still holding the last gray wisps of the memory as it dissolved. "You were crying."

"I don't cry."

"You're crying right now."

Gideon touched their own face with the back of one wrist, discovered this was accurate, and hated it thoroughly. "It's smoke," they said. "From the fire. A known hazard of my profession."

"Mm." Sebastian came around the dead coals and sat beside them, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. He didn't ask. That was the thing about him that Gideon both treasured and could not bear — he waited, patient as a clock, until whatever you were hiding got embarrassed and came out on its own.

"The night we met," Gideon said finally, because apparently the debt intended to introduce itself tonight after all, "you asked me why I steal memories. I told you it was business."

"It was a very convincing business voice."

"I stole from the archive-crystals in your clocktower to hide my own past. That part was true. But I've been hiding more than mine." Gideon turned the empty vial over in their fingers. "I've been hiding yours too. Since before you were born, technically. Which sounds impressive until you learn how."

Sebastian said nothing. The fire settled.

"There was a woman," Gideon said. "Running east with a baby. The last of the covenant line. And there was a boy in a border market who was hungry enough to sell everything he'd ever overheard about where she was going." They laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He got a month of bread out of it. Best trade he ever made. Worst trade anyone ever made."

The silence stretched. Gideon stared into the coals and waited for it — the flinch, the recoil, the look. They'd seen the look before, on faces of people who'd learned what Gideon was. Thieves got the look. Memory-thieves got a worse one.

What they got instead was Sebastian reaching over and closing their fingers around the vial, folding Gideon's hand shut around it gently, like tucking something in.

"You were starving," he said.

"That's not an excuse. That's a price tag. There's a difference."

"I know there's a difference." His voice was quiet and steady and infuriatingly kind. "Believe me, I've had occasion lately to learn exactly what things cost. But Gideon — you didn't send the hunters. You were a child selling scraps to survive. The men who hunted my mother made a choice. You made a different one, later, every day since. You've been paying it back since before you knew what you owed."

"Have I?" Gideon's laugh came out bitter. "Because from where I sit, I've been running. There's a difference there too, and I've been an expert on pretending otherwise for eleven years." They finally turned to look at him. "Every mile of this — the keystones, the court, all of it — I told myself I was helping you. Some of that was even true. But I've also been trying to get to the end of this road before you found out what I did. And now you know, and we're not at the end, and I don't—"

"Don't," Sebastian said.

"—don't know what happens now," Gideon finished, because finishing cowardly sentences was apparently the one courage they had left.

"What happens now," Sebastian said, "is that we go to Emberhold tomorrow, and we take the third keystone off Cassian Vey's throne, and then we finish this. Together. Same as yesterday." He paused, and something flickered across his face — the mural, the vessel, the purpose written into him, all the weight he was carrying that Gideon hadn't yet fully answered for either. "We're even, anyway. You sold my location before I could walk. I was built before I could breathe. Neither of us chose what was done to us. We're just the ones left holding it."

"That's the bleakest accounting I've ever heard, and I once audited a smuggler's books."

"And yet." Sebastian almost smiled. Then he stood, and paused, and said, softer: "For what it's worth — I'm glad the boy in the market was hungry. Because the man who grew out of him found me in a clocktower and got me out alive. Whatever you owe the road, you've paid me back in full just by walking it."

He went back to his bedroll. Gideon sat alone with the fire and the empty vial and the strange, awful, unclenching feeling in their chest — guilt not gone, but named at last, and a named thing is a thing you can carry instead of a thing that carries you.

They were still sitting there when Wren's voice drifted down from the loft, thick with sleep and entirely too pleased with itself:

"If you two are going to have feelings, do it quieter. Some of us are guarding the pass."

"We're *not* having feelings," Gideon called up.

"Liar," said Wren, and rolled over.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold. Gideon packed the camp with hands that only shook a little, and did the last thing there was to do: took the black vial, and instead of stoppering the memory back inside, held it out to Sebastian.

"Keep it," they said. "If I hide it again, I'll start hiding other things. It's how I'm built. So you hold it. When this is over — if we're both still standing — give it back to me, and I'll look at it in daylight, on purpose, once a year. That's my penance. Witnessed."

Sebastian took the vial and hung it around his own neck, next to the faint pulse of the two keystones in his pack, and something in his expression said he understood exactly what he'd just been handed: not a trinket. A leash, freely given, held by the person least able to abuse it.

"Witnessed," he said solemnly. Then, ruining it completely: "Also I'm charging you storage fees."

"Noted and disputed."

Wren appeared at the mouth of the shelter with her ward-chalk already in hand, squinting down the pass toward the distant smudge of Emberhold's smoke on the horizon. "Road's clear," she reported. "Mostly. There's riders on the far switchbacks, but they're moving lazy. Patrol, not hunt." She glanced between the two of them, missing nothing, saying nothing, which was its own kind of grace. "So. We're really doing this. Walking into the ash-hole itself."

"We're really doing this," Sebastian said.

Gideon touched the space at their sternum where the vial used to hang, felt the absence like a pulled tooth — lighter, and sore, and honest — and followed them out onto the road.

Behind them, the mountain kept their secret. Ahead, the Ashen Court waited with its third keystone and its smiling, hollow-eyed servants, and somewhere inside those cinder-dark walls, Lord Cassian Vey was waiting too — though none of them yet knew how patiently, or for whom.
