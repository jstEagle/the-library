## 15. Into the Ashen Court

The collar itched.

That was the first thing Sebastian noticed about being a bonded servant of the Ashen Court — not the terror, not the enormity of what they were about to do, but the itch. The collar was a slim band of grey metal that sat snug against the throat, etched with a sigil like a closed eye, and it was apparently designed by someone who hated servants and loved suffering in equal measure.

"Stop fidgeting," Wren muttered beside him. She wore her own collar with the ease of someone who had worn worse things for longer. Her hair was bound up under a servant's cap, her usual scowl smoothed into something flat and obedient. She'd practiced the expression on the road for two days. It was very good. It made Sebastian's skin crawl every time he looked at her.

"I can't help it," he whispered. "It's like wearing a wasp."

"It's a leash," Wren said. "Leashes are supposed to remind you they're there." She glanced at him sidelong, and beneath the blankness her eyes were sharp as chipped flint. "The moment you forget it's there is the moment you're actually bonded. So itch away. Itching means you're still you."

Sebastian decided to take comfort in that. He took comfort where he could find it these days; the supply had grown thin.

They stood in the queue of supply carts winding up the causeway toward Emberhold, wedged between barrels of salt fish and crates of pale fruit from the orchards below the mountain. The city itself rose ahead of them out of the ash-grey plain like something grown rather than built — towers of black stone veined with faint ember-light, as if the whole structure were a banked fire someone had forgotten to put out. Heat shimmered above it even though the air around them was cold. The sky overhead was still starless. It had been starless since the night everything broke, but here, so close to the Court's seat, the darkness felt less like absence and more like a held breath.

"Remember," Gideon murmured from behind them. They were dressed as a cart-driver, hood up, hands wrapped in rough cloth to hide the ink-stains of a lifetime of theft. "You're Salt-and-Stock, transferred from the orchard houses. You don't speak unless spoken to. You don't look at anyone above your station. And if anyone asks—"

"We're tired, we're grateful, and we don't remember much," Sebastian recited. "Which won't be hard, because I barely remember anything anyway."

Gideon didn't laugh. That was the trouble with jokes about Sebastian's missing past — they landed wrong now, always, like stepping on a stair that wasn't there. Gideon had been quieter since the Spire, since the confession at the hermit order, since all the truths that had come pouring out of them like water from a cracked jug. Quieter, and watchful, in a way that Sebastian pretended not to notice because noticing would mean asking questions he wasn't ready to have answered.

"Wren," Gideon said instead, "you're sure about this?"

Wren rolled her shoulders. "I've been alone in the dark with things worse than Cassian Vey for three years. A week pretending to be empty-eyed is nothing." A pause. "Besides. I want to see inside. I want to know what we're breaking."

*What we're breaking.* Not *who*. Sebastian filed that away. Wren had a way of saying things that sounded like complaints and were actually promises.

The line moved. A gate-guard in ash-lacquered armor checked their collars with a rod of dull iron that hummed when it touched the metal band. The hum crawled down Sebastian's spine and settled somewhere behind his teeth.

"Orchard transfer?" the guard asked.

"Yes, honored one," Wren said, in a voice gone soft and vacant. It was remarkable. She sounded like a person who had never had an opinion in her life.

The guard looked at Sebastian. Sebastian looked at a point six inches left of the guard's face and thought, with great effort, about turnips.

"Move along."

And then they were inside Emberhold, and Sebastian stopped thinking about turnips entirely.

* * *

He had expected grandeur. He had expected menace — guards everywhere, doors of black iron, the architecture of fear. What he had not expected was how *pleasant* it all seemed, at first glance. Warm light spilled from high windows. Music drifted from somewhere above, delicate and sweet, a harp played by hands that never hurried. Servants moved through the halls in soft grey livery, carrying trays and linens and armfuls of white flowers, and they were all —

They were all smiling.

Not the strained smiles of people working a long shift. Not the tight smiles of people afraid. These smiles were easy, unguarded, serene. A young woman glided past them with a stack of folded towels and smiled at the wall. An old man polished a banister with slow, loving strokes and smiled at his own reflection in the wood. In a side corridor, a boy no older than Wren arranged flowers in a vase, adjusting each stem with perfect care, smiling, smiling, smiling.

"They're happy," Sebastian said quietly. It came out wrong — half question, half plea.

"No, they're not," Wren said. "Look closer."

He did. And once he looked properly, he couldn't stop seeing it. The smiles never reached anywhere. They sat on the faces like paint on glass. The eyes above them were smooth and untroubled and utterly vacant — not empty the way exhaustion empties you, or grief, but empty the way a house is empty after the furniture's been sold. There was room in those eyes. Room where a person used to be.

A woman carrying a tray of wine paused beside them, waiting for a door to open. Up close, Sebastian could see the fine scarring at her temple, a tracery of silver lines like frost-fern, the mark of harvested memory. Her smile was beautiful. Her eyes were beautiful. Nothing behind either of them was home.

"What's your name?" Sebastian asked her, before he could stop himself.

She turned to him with pleasant readiness. "I serve the Court," she said warmly.

"But what's *your name?*"

The smile flickered — just a stutter, just a skipped heartbeat — and for an instant something moved far back in her eyes, something small and drowning, reaching toward the surface of deep water. Then it sank again.

"I serve the Court," she said, and the door opened, and she glided through, and she was still smiling when it closed behind her.

Sebastian's hands had gone cold. Beside him, Wren stared at the closed door with an expression he'd never seen on her before — not anger, which he knew well, but something older and sicker. Recognition, maybe. She'd survived alone by being too stubborn and too wild to catch. These people hadn't been either.

"They gave it away," she whispered. "Their names. You don't give your name away. Your name is the first thing you fight for." Her hand drifted up toward her own collar and stopped. "Everything else, maybe. Never the name."

"That's what he does," Gideon said softly, from behind them. They kept their voice low, pitched under the music. "Cassian doesn't take memories by force, mostly. Force leaves cracks. He makes people *want* to give. He finds the grief or the debt or the shame, and he offers to lift it, and people say yes because the weight is unbearable and the price seems abstract. And then one day they can't remember why they ever minded." A breath. "It's very efficient. I would know. I did a smaller version of it to myself for years."

"You never smiled like that," Sebastian said.

"No," Gideon agreed. "Because I kept the pain. Pain tells you who you are. I only ever sold the good things." Their voice caught on something. "Turns out that's its own kind of hollowing. Just slower."

Somewhere above them, the harp changed to a brighter tune, and a hundred smiling servants turned in unison toward the sound, like flowers finding the sun, and Sebastian had to press his palm flat against the cold stone wall until the nausea passed.

* * *

They worked. That was the cover, and the work was real enough — hauling crates, scrubbing steps, staying bent and busy and invisible. Wren turned out to be disturbingly good at servitude; she moved through the halls with her head bowed and her eyes cataloguing everything, exits and guard rotations and the strange pulse of ember-light in the walls, storing it all in that fierce, hoarding mind of hers. Twice she redirected a supervisor's attention with a perfectly timed dropped crate. Sebastian began to understand that survival had given her gifts no academy could teach.

Gideon melted into the background the way only Gideon could, and by the second evening had assembled, from overheard scraps and a stolen duty roster, the shape of the palace: the Court's public halls above, the memory-vaults below, and between them — at the very heart of everything — the Throne Hall, where Lord Cassian Vey held audience and where, according to a whispering kitchen maid whose mind was still mostly her own, the third keystone sat in plain sight.

"In plain sight," Sebastian repeated that night, in the narrow servants' loft where they lay among strangers too hollowed to listen. "You're certain?"

"As certain as gossip gets," Gideon murmured. "It crowns the throne. Literally. Set into the headpiece, above his brow. He doesn't hide it." A pause. "He doesn't need to. Who would take it from him?"

Sebastian lay staring at the rafters and thought about the first keystone fused to bone at the bottom of Mirrormere, and the second pried from the honest hands of monks who demanded truth as toll, and how both times the stone had sung against his skin like something recognizing kin. Two-thirds of a broken promise, sleeping in his pack. One-third remaining, sitting on the brow of the most dangerous man in either realm.

"Tomorrow," Wren breathed from his other side. "There's a delivery of festival linens to the upper galleries. Big enough detail that extra hands get pulled in. We'll be in the Throne Hall before midday."

"And if he's holding court?" Sebastian asked.

"He holds court at dusk," Gideon said. "Mornings, he's in the vaults. Everyone says so." Another pause, longer. "Everyone also says the throne room is never truly empty. That the keystone keeps... watch. But that's superstition."

"Everything's superstition until it tries to kill you," Wren said, and rolled over, and was asleep within moments, the way she could sleep anywhere, anytime, like a knife returning to its sheath.

Sebastian stayed awake a while longer, listening to the breathing of the hollowed servants around them — dozens of sleepers, none of them dreaming aloud, none of them murmuring names or lost loves or old grievances the way ordinary sleepers do. Silence, row upon row of it. He thought of the clocktower in Hollowmere, of Master Aldous snoring in his chair amid ticking, of the town archive-crystals humming with everyone's stored days. Places full of remembered things. This place was a museum of forgetting, and tomorrow he was going to walk into its central gallery and steal the crown jewel.

He fell asleep smiling at the thought, and hated himself a little for it.

* * *

The Throne Hall of Emberhold was built to make you feel small, and it succeeded without effort.

Sebastian entered with the linen-detail, arms full of folded white cloth, head appropriately bowed — but he cheated. Everyone cheated; the trick was to cheat in rhythm, a glance here, a glance there, folded into the motion of walking. Enough to build the room piece by piece.

Pillars of black stone rising into gloom. Banners of ash-grey silk, each embroidered with the closed-eye sigil. A floor of dark glassy rock that reflected the ember-veins in the walls so that walkers seemed to wade through slow fire. And at the far end, atop seven steps, the throne: a mass of black iron shaped like grasping branches, or perhaps like veins, or perhaps like roots — Sebastian couldn't decide, and suspected that was deliberate — and above it all, set into the crown of the thing directly where a seated man's brow would rest—

The keystone.

Even at distance, even across a hall built to diminish everything in it, Sebastian *felt* it. It looked like a shard of frozen dawn, red-gold and pulsing faintly, and the pulse was not decorative. With each slow beat, the ember-light in the walls brightened and dimmed in answer. The whole court breathed with it. The whole court *fed* it — he understood that suddenly, horribly, watching the silver-scarred servants move through the hall with their serene smiles. Every harvested memory in Emberhold drained downward, inward, into that stone, and the stone poured power back out into the man who wore it. Cassian Vey had not merely collected the third keystone. He had married himself to it. It drank his court, and he drank the stone, and the arrangement was elegant and obscene and utterly stable, and Sebastian's skin crawled with the certainty that this was exactly what the old covenant had been designed to become in the wrong hands.

A life bound to the magic. Many lives, bound loosely, so no single death would ever be missed.

"There," Wren breathed, barely a shape of sound beside him. She was folding linens with mechanical perfection, eyes down. "Gallery stairs, east side. Vault access behind the tapestry — I saw the seam."

"Not yet," Gideon murmured from the linen cart. "Midday bell first. The guard changes, and—"

The doors at the end of the hall opened.

Every servant in the room went still — not frightened-still, which has a tremor in it, but the stillness of things that have been trained out of wanting to move. Sebastian bowed his head with the rest, heart slamming, and watched through the curtain of his own hair as Lord Cassian Vey crossed his hall.

He was not what Sebastian had expected. He was not robed in splendor, nor armored, nor shadowed by retinues. He walked alone, in simple dark clothes, a lean man with silver threading his temples, and he moved through his hall of hollowed servants the way a gardener moves through a garden — pausing here, touching a shoulder there, and every servant he touched smiled wider, briefly, like lamps turned up on dimmers. At the base of the dais he stopped. His gaze swept the hall, unhurried, and passed over the linen-detail, and returned.

Passed over. Returned.

Stopped.

Sebastian kept his eyes on the floor. He counted his own heartbeats and got to eleven before footsteps came down off the dais, unhurried, and halted in front of him.

"Look at me, child."

The voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a favorite uncle, a kind teacher, a man who remembered your birthday. Sebastian raised his eyes.

Up close, Cassian Vey's face was lined and human and almost gentle, except for his eyes, which held the same red-gold pulse as the keystone above his empty throne — as if the stone looked out through him whenever it pleased.

"I don't know your face," Cassian said pleasantly. "And I know all my faces."

Behind Sebastian, very slightly, the linen cart creaked — Gideon shifting weight, calculating distances, odds, escape routes. Wren had gone statue-still, her blank servant's mask perfect, her hand drifting with terrible casualness toward the linen knife at her belt.

Cassian tilted his head, studying Sebastian the way Sebastian had once studied a clock that ticked wrong — with fascination, and with the patience of a man who already knew what he'd find inside.

"How curious," he said softly. "You have your mother's way of standing."

The hall's ember-light pulsed once, slow and deep, and somewhere far below, Sebastian felt the vaults stir like something waking.
