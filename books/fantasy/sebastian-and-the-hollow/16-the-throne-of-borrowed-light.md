## 16. The Throne of Borrowed Light

Gideon had stolen into a great many places they had no business being — a duke's memory-vault in Vell, the dreaming mind of a sleeping archivist, once, memorably, a wedding to avoid a debt collector — but Emberhold was the first place that made them feel like a mouse walking into a cat's mouth on purpose.

The throne room did not help.

It was carved from black glass that had been poured, not built — rivers of it frozen mid-flow up the walls and across the ceiling, so that looking up gave you the vertiginous sense of standing at the bottom of a frozen waterfall. Braziers burned without wood or smoke, their flames the color of old bruises. And at the far end, raised on seven steps of ash-grey stone, stood the throne itself: a jagged thing crowned by a keystone the size of a heart, pulsing with a slow, greedy light.

The third keystone. Right there. Feeding.

*Feeding on what?* Gideon wondered, and then looked at the courtiers lining the hall in their grey-and-gold livery, smiling their smooth, vacant smiles, and stopped wondering.

Beside Gideon, Wren stood very still in her borrowed servant's uniform, her small hands folded the way she'd practiced for two days in front of a puddle. ("Like this? Do I look obedient?" "You look constipated." "That's what obedience *is*, isn't it.") She was doing admirably now, all things considered. Only Gideon could see the faint shimmer of ward-light crawling along her sleeves like ants preparing for war.

And ahead of them both, walking toward the throne with his chin up and his terror carefully hidden behind clockmaker's posture, went Sebastian Vale.

*Don't do anything brave,* Gideon begged him silently. It was rather like begging the tide not to come in.

"Cassian," Sebastian said, stopping at the foot of the steps. "We need to talk about your keystone."

Lord Cassian Vey smiled down from his throne, and Gideon's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for falling off cliffs. Because Cassian didn't look surprised. He looked like a man whose dinner invitation had finally been accepted.

"I wondered how long it would take you to come to me," Cassian said. His voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a favorite uncle, a kind teacher, someone who remembered your birthday. "Did you enjoy my city on the way in? The servants' quarters are quite comfortable. I imagine Wren knows — she wore the uniform beautifully."

Wren's folded hands twitched.

"Ah," Cassian continued, rising. Up close he was younger than his voice suggested, silver-templed and neat, the sort of man who would apologize before ruining you. "But then, you were always good at wearing other people's lives, weren't you, Delacroix?"

"You know me," Gideon said flatly.

"I know your work." Cassian descended one step. "I bought some of it, years ago. A location, as I recall. A valley. An infant." He tilted his head, savoring it. "Best coin I ever spent."

The floor did not move, but Gideon felt it move anyway. Beside them, Sebastian turned — slowly, the way a door turns when the hinges have rusted — and looked at them.

*Not now,* Gideon mouthed. *Please. Not now.*

Sebastian held their gaze for three full heartbeats. Then he turned back to the throne, and something in Gideon's chest cracked quietly, like ice on a pond in early spring.

"You knew what I was," Sebastian said to Cassian. "Before I did."

"The lastborn of the covenant line." Cassian spread his hands, magnanimous, delighted. "The vessel. The anchor. Do you know what you are, boy? You are the hinge of the world. The covenant is failing because its anchor was hidden away in a hole where magic couldn't reach it — left to grow up winding clocks instead of holding realms together." He took another step down. Two more and he'd be close enough to touch. "I can fix that. I can give you everything you were made for."

"I was made for winding clocks," Sebastian said. "I'm rather good at it."

Cassian laughed — genuinely, warmly — as though Sebastian had said something charming at dinner. "There it is. That spine. That's the blood in you. Listen to me." He stopped on the last step, and his voice dropped into something intimate, almost tender. "The covenant demands a life bound to the magic. Yes? You've felt it in the stones. But there is another way, and only I can offer it. Be the vessel *willingly*. Not consumed — *crowned*. Take the magic into yourself alive, whole, awake. Rule both realms from the space between them. My guidance, your power. No sacrifice. No death. Only ascension."

The bruise-colored flames flared. Somewhere above them, the frozen waterfall groaned.

"And what happens to everyone else?" Sebastian asked.

"Everyone else?" Cassian gestured gently at his courtiers, at their serene, hollow smiles. "Everyone else stops having to choose badly. That's all suffering is, in the end — choice gone wrong. I would take that burden from them. From *you*. No more watching people sell pieces of themselves to survive." His eyes flicked to Gideon, precise as a scalpel. "No more debts."

It was a beautiful offer. That was the horror of it. Gideon watched Sebastian hear it — watched him think of Mirrormere, of the woman sealing a baby into a warded cradle, of every empty-eyed servant they'd passed in these halls — and waited for the answer the way you wait for a surgeon's verdict.

"No," Sebastian said.

Just that. No flourish, no speech. A craftsman declining a bad commission.

Cassian's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was somehow worse than if it had fallen. "Think carefully—"

"I've been thinking carefully since the stars went out," Sebastian said. "It's mostly how I got here. The answer is no. You don't want to share power with me, Cassian. You want to hold the leash while I wear the collar. I've seen what your generosity costs." He glanced back — just briefly — at the courtiers. "You're not offering me a crown. You're offering to become the covenant. And the covenant eats people."

For the first time, Lord Cassian Vey's warmth went out like a snuffed candle.

"Yes," he said simply. "It does. And it will eat you whether you consent or not. The only question is whether you die useful or die broken." He lifted one hand. "Take the vessel. Kill the thief. The child, if she struggles."

* * *

What happened next happened in the space between two heartbeats, and afterward Gideon would remember it in fragments, the way shattered glass remembers the window it used to be.

Wren moved first.

She'd been waiting — Gideon realized later she'd been waiting the entire conversation, coiled under her servant's posture like a spring under a floorboard — and the moment Cassian's hand rose, her wards *erupted*. Light sheeted out from her sleeves and boots and hair, slamming down into the black-glass floor in a lattice of blue-white lines, and three guards who had been flowing toward them from the side doors hit the lattice and bounced like dolls thrown at a wall.

"RUN, YOU IDIOTS," Wren shrieked, in a voice nothing like obedience, "I HAVE THEM FOR MAYBE TEN SECONDS—"

They ran. Or rather, Sebastian ran, hauling Wren by the wrist, and Gideon ran backward, because Gideon had learned long ago never to turn their whole back on a man like Cassian Vey.

Which was why Gideon saw it happen.

Cassian didn't shout an incantation. He didn't even hurry. He simply reached up and laid his palm against the keystone crowning his throne, and the throne *answered*. Borrowed light — harvested memory, decades of it, the stolen inner lives of hundreds of hollowed servants — poured down through the black glass floor and rose around him in a cloak of shifting faces. Gideon saw the faces clearly as the wave of it gathered: a baker, a soldier, a girl with braids, an old woman laughing. All of them smiling. All of them *his*.

"Delacroix," Cassian said, almost kindly, and threw memory at them like a hammer.

Wren's lattice caught the first blow and screamed — actually screamed, a sound like a struck bell dying — and the girl herself dropped to one knee, nose streaming red, holding the warding with both bare hands pressed to the floor. "GO!" she sobbed. "I'M FINE! THIS IS FINE!"

"It is objectively not fine—" Sebastian began.

"SEBASTIAN." Wren's voice cracked across the hall like a whip. She looked up at him through the blood, and grinned, feral and blazing. "Go be the vessel somewhere *else*."

He went. Gideon grabbed his sleeve and they ran together up the processional aisle as the lattice behind them spiderwebbed, fractured, and came apart in a rain of dying blue light. Wren scrambled up and fled after them, and the last Gideon saw of the hall, glancing back over their shoulder, was Cassian Vey walking calmly down his own steps with the faces of the dead-and-drifting swirling around his shoulders like a mantle, unhurried as winter.

He wasn't chasing them. That was the thing Gideon understood a half-second too late.

He was *aiming*.

"GIDEON, DOWN—"

Gideon didn't go down. Later they would catalog all the reasons — pride, stupidity, the simple arithmetic of a body moving slower than a thought — but in the moment there was only the sound of gathered memory tearing the air, and Sebastian in front of them, and the blow meant for the lastborn of the covenant line arriving at exactly the height of a human heart.

Gideon stepped into it.

The world went white, then silent, then very far away. There was no pain at first — just an enormous pressure, as if someone had reached into their chest and taken hold of everything they were and *squeezed*. They heard themselves make a sound they didn't recognize. They heard Sebastian scream their name. The marble came up to meet them with the particular, impersonal courtesy of floors everywhere.

Faces. That was the strange part. As the borrowed light tore through them, Gideon saw faces — their mother's, laughing in a kitchen that no longer existed; the archivist, mid-snore; a hundred strangers whose memories they'd sold, one by one, to keep breathing. *So this is the ledger coming due,* they thought, with a distant, professional interest. *I always wondered.*

Then the pain arrived, all at once, like an ocean remembering a beach.

"Gideon." Sebastian's hands were on them, pressing hard below the ribs, and Sebastian's face was above them, and it was coming apart entirely. "Gideon, look at me. Look at *me*. You don't get to — you absolute idiot, you *thief*, you don't get to—"

"That's," Gideon managed. Blood in their throat; they swallowed it and hated the waste. "That's the nicest thing you've ever called me."

"Don't joke. Don't you dare joke."

"Wouldn't dream of it." The hall was tilting. Torchlight smeared. Somewhere Wren was shouting and the sound of pursuing boots was growing from a rumor into a fact, and none of it mattered even slightly, because Sebastian's hands were shaking against their wound and Gideon had spent their whole life taking things that weren't theirs and here, at the end, they had finally given away the only thing that ever truly belonged to them.

"Sebastian." They caught his wrist. Their fingers wouldn't close properly. "Listen. The stones. When you — when you get them together. It wants a life. It'll tell you it has to be yours."

"Gideon—"

"*Listen.* Find another way. Steal from the bargain itself. You're — you're the best student I never meant to teach." A breath that hurt going in and hurt more coming out. "Break its rules. It's the family trade, after all."

Above them, Sebastian was crying without seeming to notice, which Gideon found unbearably unfair, because they had wanted — foolishly, secretly, all this long road — to be the reason he stopped crying someday, not the cause of a fresh supply.

Boots. Shouting. Wren's voice, ragged: *"Side corridor — MOVE — I can't hold another one—"*

Sebastian slid his arms under Gideon's shoulders and knees and lifted, and Gideon, who had carried stolen memories across half the world without ever letting anyone carry *them*, discovered that they were too broken to protest.

"Hold on," Sebastian said. His voice had changed — gone quiet and level and absolutely certain, the voice of a man who has stopped asking the world's permission. "Hold on, Gideon. I'm not losing you. I've lost enough. I'm choosing, right now, and I choose *you*, and I don't care what the covenant, or Cassian, or the whole rotten architecture of both realms thinks about it."

The corridors blurred past. Cold air. Stone. Wren running ahead, bleeding, blazing, holding open a door with a ward that flickered like a guttering candle.

And in Sebastian's arms, sinking down into the dark with the ledger of their life finally balanced, Gideon Delacroix thought:

*Well. Look at that. The vessel chooses after all.*

Then the dark closed over them like water, and somewhere impossibly far away, three keystones began, very softly, to sing.
