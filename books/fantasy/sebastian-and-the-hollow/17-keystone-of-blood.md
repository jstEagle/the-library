## 17. Keystone of Blood

The throne room was burning.

Not all of it — not yet. The great banners of the Ashen Court smoldered where Wren's wards had torn through them, and the stained-glass windows had blown outward in a rain of colored shards that caught the light of the keystone and scattered it like a festival that had gone terribly wrong. Somewhere behind him, Cassian Vey was laughing, or maybe screaming; the two sounds had become difficult to tell apart.

Sebastian knelt on the floor of borrowed light with Gideon in his arms.

There was a hole in Gideon's chest where the killing blow had landed — a wound that was not entirely a wound, because nothing in this room was entirely anything anymore. The edges of it shimmered faintly, as though the magic that had made it couldn't quite decide whether Gideon was a person or a memory, and had settled on eating them from the inside out.

"Hey." Gideon's voice came out thin as wire. "Hey, clockmaker. You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you look at something broken and start counting gears." A wet, rattling laugh. "I'm not a clock, Sebastian. You can't fix me by taking me apart."

"I fixed Mrs. Halloway's mantel clock," Sebastian said, and his voice cracked straight down the middle like the wards had. "And that clock had been struck by lightning. Twice."

Gideon's hand found his wrist. Their fingers were cold. That was the thing Sebastian kept snagging on, the small terrible detail: Gideon Delacroix, who had always run warm, who complained about cold mornings and slept curled around heat like a cat, was going cold under his hands.

"Wren," Gideon said.

"Holding the door." Across the hall, Wren stood in the shattered doorway with her arms raised and her whole body shaking, holding up a wall of ward-light against the tide of Cassian's bonded servants. She looked twelve years old and eight feet tall at the same time. "She shouldn't be able to do that," Sebastian said. "She's going to burn herself out."

"She knows." Gideon coughed. "We both made choices today. Apparently mine was stupid."

"It was incredibly stupid."

"I know." Their eyes found his, and for a moment — just a moment — they were clear, sharp, entirely themselves. "Sebastian. Listen to me. The stone—"

Sebastian turned his head.

The third keystone sat where it had fallen when he'd torn it from its cradle above Cassian's throne: a shard of dark crystal the size of a heart, veined with red light that pulsed slow and patient, like something breathing. Like something waiting. It had been feeding on the court for two hundred years — on the harvested memories, on the hollowed servants smiling their empty smiles — and now that it was loose, it had turned its attention to the nearest living thing in the room.

Which was Gideon.

"No," Sebastian said aloud, to the stone, because he had run out of people to argue with.

*Yes,* said the stone, without words, directly into the marrow of him. And beneath the stone's voice there was another voice, older, vast, worn smooth as a riverbed — the covenant itself, waking now that all three keystones were gathered in one place. He could feel the first two against his chest, wrapped in cloth, humming in harmony with the third. Three notes of the same chord. Two hundred years of silence ending.

*Assembled,* the voice said. *At last. Assembled.*

"I didn't assemble you," Sebastian snarled. "I stole you. There's a difference. Ask my friend — she's a professional."

Gideon laughed again, worse than before.

*The terms are known to you,* the covenant said, gentle as a grandmother explaining why the porridge must be finished. *One life, bound to the magic. One life given freely, or taken by force — the covenant is indifferent to which. The stones are assembled. The vessel stands among us.*

"I'm not your vessel."

*You are what we made you.* The voice wasn't cruel about it. That was somehow the worst part. It said it the way a clockmaker might say *you are the escapement*, matter-of-fact, a part is a part. *But the terms have never required your consent. Only your presence. And a life.*

Sebastian looked down at Gideon.

He understood then — all at once, the way ice understands the lake — what the voice was offering. Not asking. Offering. Gideon was dying. The wound was beyond any craft he knew, beyond any medicine of either realm. But the keystone ran on lifebound sacrifice, exactly as designed, exactly as the monks had warned and the murals had shown and every step of this terrible road had whispered. A life poured into the stones would complete the covenant. The realms would realign. The wards would rise again, stronger than before, and the magic would flow where it was meant to flow.

And the covenant, being old and precise and utterly without malice, had noticed that a life was already pouring out right here on the floor. Half-spent. Nearly delivered. Why waste it?

*Give the thief to us,* the voice said, tender as rot. *And we will give you everything. Your purpose fulfilled. Both realms saved. Order restored to the border between worlds, and no more children smuggled into valleys, no more families taken by ash and cinder, no more stars going out. All of it, bought with one life that is already leaving. You need only stop holding on.*

The red light of the keystone pulsed. Beneath his hands, Gideon's breathing had gone shallow and irregular, a clock winding down.

"You know what the worst part is?" Gideon murmured. Their eyes were half-closed. "I did the math on this. Months ago. On the road, after the Spire. I lay awake doing the math." A ghost of their old crooked smile. "One life for two realms. Even I couldn't argue with the arithmetic. I was going to volunteer. Had a whole speech planned. Very moving. You'd have cried."

"Gideon—"

"But then—" a breath, hitching, "—then you went and did that thing with the memories at the water keystone. Freely given. And I thought, oh no. Oh no, he's going to ruin my perfectly good tragedy with *ethics*."

"You absolute idiot," Sebastian said, and his tears fell onto Gideon's face, and Gideon tried to catch one on their tongue like rain, because even dying they were impossible. "You were going to die and let me think it was my fault."

"It would've been very tidy."

"I don't want tidy. I want—" His voice broke. He pressed his forehead to theirs. "I want you stealing biscuits from the festival stalls. I want you complaining about my soldering. I want you telling Wren outrageous lies about how we met. I want the version of you that's *annoying*, Gideon, not the version that's noble, noble is useless, noble is just dying with better posture—"

"That's going on my headstone," Gideon whispered. "*Died With Better Posture.*"

*Choose,* said the covenant, and the word rang through the burning room like a bell through fog. *The vessel chooses. Save the thief, and the covenant fails, and the realms drift apart until they tear each other open — the wild magic will eat the borders, and everything either realm has built will come apart within a generation. Or complete the covenant, and save ten thousand lives for one. This is the arithmetic. It has always been the arithmetic.*

Sebastian closed his eyes.

And underneath the grief, underneath the roaring in his ears, he felt it — the thing that had woken in him the night the stars went out, the thing the valley had contained for twenty years, the thing he was *made* for. It rose in him now, quiet and enormous, and it looked at the arithmetic.

It was good arithmetic. That was the trap of it. One life for two realms. Every part of him that loved order, that loved clocks, that loved the clean click of a mechanism doing exactly what it was designed to do — every part of him wanted to say yes. To be the escapement. To make the mechanism work.

But he thought of the drowned city, and the skeleton wearing his family crest, still clutching a keystone after two hundred years because someone had decided *their* death was good arithmetic too. He thought of the hollowed servants, smiling. He thought of Gideon selling their mother's smile for passage and not knowing why they were crying. He thought of Wren, alone on a mountain for a year, because everyone she'd ever trusted had been spent like coin.

Every horror he had walked through since the stars went out had been built on this exact arithmetic. Someone always doing the math. Someone always finding that some lives balanced the scales.

"No," Sebastian said.

He opened his eyes and looked into the red heart of the keystone.

"I'm not giving you Gideon. And I'm not giving you me. Not my life, not anyone's life. You can keep your arithmetic. I've seen what it buys."

*Then the covenant fails—*

"Then the covenant fails," Sebastian agreed, and lifted the third keystone out of the air, and held all three stones in his hands for the first time, and the whole throne room shuddered as the chord completed itself. "Unless."

The vast attention of the covenant bent toward him, curious, hungry, ancient.

*Speak.*

"You run on sacrifice. Lifebound. Taken or given, you said — you're indifferent." Sebastian's hands were shaking. The stones were singing against each other, three notes becoming one sound, and the sound was trying to pour itself into the shape of the old bargain, and he could feel how easy it would be to let it. How *restful*. "But you also accepted an amendment once. At the water keystone. I gave memories freely and you took them. You *adapted*. So here are new terms. Final offer."

Behind him, Wren shouted something — the door was buckling — and Cassian's laughter had stopped, which meant Cassian was listening, which meant Cassian had understood what Sebastian was about to do, and was afraid of it.

"One life," Sebastian said. "That's your price. Fine. But you don't get to define what a life *is*. A life isn't blood. A life isn't breath. A life is everything a person remembers being. So I'll pay you a life — piece by piece, freely given, starting now — but you will release Gideon Delacroix first. Before I give you anything. That's the order of operations. I'm a clockmaker. I know how sequences work."

*The bargain requires—*

"The bargain requires a life bound to the magic," Sebastian said. "You'll have one. Mine. In installments. Take it or watch the stones go cold forever, because I will throw them into the sea, all three, and you can spend the next two hundred years learning to be indifferent about *that*."

Silence. The whole world seemed to hold its breath — Wren at the door, the fire, the dying man in his arms, the ancient voice weighing its options the way a merchant weighs coin.

And then, soft as a key turning:

*Accepted.*

The red light of the keystone flared — and flowed. Out of Gideon's chest, out of the terrible wound, drawing back like a tide going out, and Gideon gasped, a huge ragged drowning-man gasp, and their eyes flew open, and their hand clamped down on Sebastian's arm hard enough to bruise.

"Sebastian," Gideon rasped. "What did you just—"

"Shh." Sebastian was already reaching into himself, into the warm lit rooms of his own memory — the clocktower, the smell of oil and brass, Mrs. Halloway's mantel clock struck by lightning twice, the Founding Festival, the valley, the woman's hands sealing a warded cradle — gathering the first handful. "I'm busy."

"Whatever you're about to do, don't—"

"Too late." He smiled at Gideon, and it was the smile he'd learned from them, crooked and reckless and bright. "Hold still. This is going to hurt me a lot more than it hurts you."

He opened his hands, and gave.

* * *

It did not feel like dying. That surprised him most. It felt like a festival — like standing in the town square while lanterns were released, watching pieces of yourself float up and away, each one glowing, each one beloved, each one smaller and further away than the last.

He gave the winter he'd been seven and the pipes froze and Old Marta taught him to solder by candlelight. He gave the summer of the comet, when the whole valley had stood in the streets arguing about omens while he'd quietly corrected their arithmetic. He gave the taste of his master's terrible coffee. He gave the sound of the clocktower at midnight, forty gears keeping time like a heartbeat.

The stones drank. The chord deepened. Somewhere beyond the walls of the world, two realms that had been drifting apart began, slowly, to turn back toward each other.

And Gideon — Gideon was breathing. Whole. Sitting up in his arms with their hands pressed to a chest that had no hole in it anymore, staring at him with an expression Sebastian had never seen on them before, because it was terror, pure and undecorated.

"Stop," Gideon said. "Sebastian, stop, that's enough, you've paid enough—"

"There's more," Sebastian said, and found that his own voice sounded strange now, distant, like someone speaking from the far end of the tower. "There's so much more. That's the thing about a life, Gideon. Nobody tells you how much of it there is until you're spending it."

"Don't." Gideon grabbed his face, forced him to focus. Their eyes were fierce and wet. "Listen to me. Whatever's left when the stones are full — you hold onto it. You hear me? You hold onto *something*. Don't give them all of you. Promise me."

"Why?" Sebastian asked, and heard the honest confusion in his own voice, because already the question was getting harder to answer. "Why does it matter which ones I—"

And then he couldn't remember why he'd been crying.

* * *

The last thing he gave was almost the last thing he had.

The throne room was dark now, the fires burned down, the bonded servants fled or freed — Wren had gotten the door open somehow, and Cassian Vey was gone, fled into the ash-ways with his court collapsing around him, and none of that mattered, none of it registered as more than weather. What mattered was the boy sitting on the floor of a ruined hall with three cold keystones in his lap, looking at the two people crouched in front of him with polite, distant curiosity.

"Hello," he said. "I'm sorry — do I know you?"

Wren made a sound like something breaking.

Gideon didn't. Gideon went very still, the way they went still before a theft, and Sebastian — the stranger wearing Sebastian's face — watched them reach into their coat and draw out a small crystal vial, one of the archive-crystals, and hold it up to the light. Inside it, faintly, something glowed.

"Yeah," Gideon said quietly. Their voice was wrecked and steady at the same time. "Yeah, you do. You just don't remember yet." They closed their hand around the vial, and looked up, and their eyes were the eyes of a thief who had finally found the one thing worth stealing back. "But I'm going to fix that. Every single piece. I swear it on every memory I have left."

Somewhere far above the ruined court, past the shattered windows, the first star in two hundred years flickered uncertainly back into the sky.

The boy in the keystones' glow tilted his head, and smiled politely at strangers, and waited to find out who he was.
