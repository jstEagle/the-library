## 19. The Cost

Gideon had stolen a thousand memories in their life. They knew the weight of them — the way a memory sat in the palm like a coin, warm from someone's head, heavier or lighter depending on how much love was pressed into it. They had learned to appraise without feeling, to pocket without flinching.

They had never once held memories that belonged to someone who *gave* them.

Sebastian lay on a pallet of camp blankets in the ruin of the throne room, breathing. That alone felt like a miracle Gideon hadn't earned. His face was slack, younger somehow, as though whatever had anchored him to his years had been poured out of him along with everything else. The third keystone sat three feet away, dim now, its hunger quieted, looking almost embarrassed about the whole affair.

"You're staring," Wren said. She was sitting cross-legged by the broken window, sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen, because her hands needed something to do and her heart needed an excuse.

"I'm checking."

"You're brooding."

"I'm a professional brooder," Gideon said. "I have references."

Wren snorted, but it came out wobbly at the edges, and she turned back to the window fast, as if the ruins of Emberhold were suddenly very interesting.

Gideon looked down at their own hands. Between them, faint as breath on cold glass, shimmered a small cluster of light — Sebastian's childhood, every hour of Hollowmere he'd poured into the stones. It had passed through Gideon on its way out of him, because Gideon had been holding him when the magic moved, and magic, being lazy and literal-minded, had deposited the overflow wherever there was room.

There had been room in Gideon. There was always room in Gideon; they'd hollowed themselves into a warehouse years ago.

The light pulsed gently, patient, like a cat waiting at a door.

*Return them,* said the sensible part of Gideon, the part that had spent weeks learning what it cost to lose things you loved. *Open your hands and give them back.*

But Gideon had seen what happened when memories were returned wrong. They had watched Cassian's servants receive harvested scraps shoved back into half-made minds — smiling, empty, stitched together with someone else's thread. A memory given back carelessly could sit in a person like a splinter of glass. And these weren't just any memories. These were the foundations. The clocktower. The old woman who'd raised him. The valley itself. Pour them back into a man who had just been emptied to the walls, and who knew what would settle and what would shatter?

Or — said a smaller voice, one Gideon hated immediately and thoroughly — *keep them.* You know what it is to be missing pieces. Here are his. You could carry them. You could keep him safe from ever losing them again. You've been keeping things safe your whole miserable life; it's the only thing you're good at.

"That's theft," Gideon said aloud.

"What is?" Wren asked.

"Nothing. Talking to myself. Also normal."

"You talk to yourself *constantly,*" Wren said. "It's why I like you. You do all my thinking out loud so I don't have to."

Gideon almost smiled. Almost. Then Sebastian stirred, and every thought in Gideon's head fell off a cliff.

His eyes opened. They were the same eyes — grey-green, sharp, always slightly amused at the world's expense — but Gideon saw the instant they went from unfocused to searching, sweeping the ruined ceiling, the broken window, Wren's face, Gideon's face, with the polite thoroughness of a stranger taking inventory of a borrowed room.

"Hello," Sebastian said.

"Hello," Gideon said, very carefully.

"You were crying," Sebastian observed. "Your face does this thing. Like a storm deciding whether to commit."

"Do I know you?"

"No." He said it simply, without cruelty, which was somehow infinitely worse than cruelty would have been. He pushed himself up on his elbows, winced, took stock of his own body like it too was unfamiliar territory. "But apparently I owe you quite a lot. Everyone keeps telling me that. The girl — Wren — she's been filling me in while you were asleep. There's a great deal of 'you wouldn't remember this' in the account. It's very humbling. I hate it."

"Good," Gideon croaked. "Hate away. It builds character."

"It's just—" He gestured vaguely at his own head. "Empty. Not frightening-empty. Just... tidy. Like a house after the movers come. I know *facts*. I know I'm twenty-three. I know I was born somewhere called Hollowmere. I know I can mend a clock mechanism if you put one in front of me — my hands remember that much. But I don't remember *learning* it. I don't remember the smell of the shop. I don't remember—" He stopped. Something crossed his face, quick and lost. "There's a shape of something. A woman. Singing, maybe? I reach for it and it's like reaching for a stair in the dark that isn't there anymore."

Gideon's chest did something structural and painful.

"The woman," Gideon said. "Dark hair. Terrible singing. Used to let you wind the regulator clocks because you liked the sound they made at midnight."

Sebastian stared at them. "How do you—"

"Lucky guess."

"Gideon." Wren's voice had gone flat and warning.

"A very lucky guess," Gideon said, "based on extensive research," and stood up abruptly to go inspect a wall that did not need inspecting.

* * *

They made camp that night in the foothills below Emberhold, because no one had the strength to walk further and no one said so out loud. Cassian was unseated, the court scattered or freed — Wren had led a contingent of ward-keepers' survivors through the servant quarters, unlocking bond-collars with a ferocity that would become legend among people who had been legends for centuries. The realms were realigning overhead; even now the sky flickered with returning stars, tentative, like candles relighting in a drafty cathedral.

And Gideon sat apart from the fire, turning Sebastian's childhood over in their hands.

That was the thing nobody warned you about carrying memories that weren't yours: they *leaked*. Little moments rose up behind your eyes at idle times. Gideon kept seeing a boy of six standing on a stool in a clockmaker's shop, tongue between his teeth, fitting a gear the size of a fingernail into a movement, and the pride on his face when it ticked. Gideon kept hearing a woman's voice — warm, off-key, endless patience — saying *again, love, slower this time, the mechanism doesn't reward hurry and neither do I.* Gideon kept smelling wood polish and lamp oil and rain on slate roofs, and knowing, intimately, from the inside, what it felt like to be Sebastian Vale falling asleep in a valley where nothing magical could follow, safe, contained, loved, and utterly unaware of it.

Gideon had sold memories like these once. Warm ones. The kind that made a person who they were. They had told themselves it was survival, and it *was*, and it had also been a slow amputation performed one piece at a time until there wasn't much left to cut.

And here was a whole person's worth, sitting in their keeping. All they had to do was nothing. Keep them. Guard them. Never let Sebastian be hurt by them again — never let him learn he'd been made as a vessel, never let him grieve the mother-shaped hole, never let him feel the weight of a purpose written into his bones before he could walk. Gideon could curate his grief. Edit his past. Play archivist to his life.

It was exactly what Cassian had done to an entire court.

"Oh, you absolute hypocrite," Gideon muttered.

"Still talking to yourself?" Wren dropped down beside them, a bowl of something stew-like in each hand. She handed one over. "It's rabbit. Probably. It hopped like rabbit."

"Thank you for the eulogy."

She ate in silence a moment, then said, without looking at them: "You haven't given them back yet."

"How do you—"

"You hold your hands like they're full. You've done it since we left the throne room. You keep touching your thumbs together like you're afraid something will spill." She shrugged. "Ward-keepers notice where people put their weight."

Gideon looked at the fire. "What if giving them back breaks him?"

"What if not giving them back breaks him worse?" Wren said, with the brutal simplicity of twelve-year-olds everywhere. "You said he remembers facts but not the learning. That's not a man. That's a report about a man." She poked the fire. "My mum died when I was seven. I don't remember her face anymore. I used to. Someone should have *kept* it for me until I was ready. Nobody did." She glanced sideways. "Don't be nobody."

Then she got up and left Gideon alone with the fire and the worst advice they'd ever received, which was also the best.

* * *

Sebastian found them near dawn.

Gideon hadn't slept. They'd been practicing — opening their hands, letting the cluster of light rise, watching it hover, closing their hands again. Cowardice, rehearsed.

He sat down across the dead fire from them, wrapped in a blanket, hair doing something architectural. For a long time neither spoke. The sky above the pass was doing its slow, uncertain brightening, stars fading into a dawn that both realms were still getting used to sharing.

"Wren told me," Sebastian said finally. "About the memories. That you're holding mine."

"She has a big mouth."

"She said you've been awake all night deciding whether to give them back." He tilted his head. "Is that true?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"And I don't know." It came out rawer than Gideon intended. They pressed on anyway, because apparently honesty was contagious and they'd caught it somewhere on the road. "I've spent my whole life taking memories that weren't mine. Selling them. Hiding inside other people's pasts because mine was unbearable. And now the universe has handed me the one prize I actually want — a complete person, whole, undamaged — and the only way to get it is to give something back. Do you understand how obscene that is? The thief finally gets rich, and the wealth is worthless unless she pays it out."

Sebastian considered this. "That's not obscene. That's just irony. Obscene would be if you kept them."

"Don't tempt me."

"I mean it." He leaned forward. "Gideon. I woke up yesterday and didn't know my own name for an hour. Do you know what that's like?"

"No."

"Yes you do. You sold yours. Piece by piece, on purpose, to survive. At least I got a choice." He said it gently, but it landed like a hand on the sternum. "So here's mine: give them back. All of them. Even the ugly ones. Especially the ugly ones. If I'm going to be a person, I need the whole ledger, not the edited volume."

"They might break you."

"Then they break me and I mend." He smiled — tired, crooked, himself in a way that had nothing to do with memory. "I'm a clockmaker's apprentice. Broken mechanisms are rather my specialty."

Gideon laughed, and it cracked halfway through into something else, and before dignity could intervene they reached across the dead fire and took his hands.

"Close your eyes," Gideon said. "This is delicate work. If I fumble, you may briefly be seven years old again. Try not to make it weird."

"Too late. Everything about you is weird."

"Flattery," Gideon murmured, and opened their hands.

The light rose between them like a held breath. Gideon guided it the way they'd been taught by thieves and monks and one very patient ghost in a drowned city — slowly, in order, oldest first. And as it flowed home, Gideon watched it happen: the moment Sebastian's face changed.

First confusion. Then recognition — of the dawn, of the mountains, of Wren sleeping by the embers, of Gideon. Then grief, arriving all at once like water finding every low place at once — the clocktower, the woman, the valley, the cradle, all of it flooding back, and his breath hitching and his grip tightening on Gideon's hands hard enough to hurt.

"Ow," Gideon said. "Gratitude noted."

"Shut up," Sebastian whispered, wet-eyed, laughing, wrecked. "She sang so *badly.*"

"Terribly. I have witnesses' memories. Multiple sources confirm."

He cried for a while, and Gideon held on, and the sun came up over two realms at once, and it was ordinary, and it was the most extraordinary thing Gideon had ever been part of.

* * *

Later — hours later, when Wren had woken, demanded a full account, and pronounced herself "disgustingly vindicated" — the three of them sat on the ridge above the pass, looking down at the road that wound toward Hollowmere.

Sebastian was quieter now. Full, Gideon guessed, in the way a house is full after the furniture comes back — familiar, but requiring re-learning. Every few minutes he'd touch his temple absently, like a man confirming a wallet was still in his pocket.

"There's something else," he said at last. "When the memories settled. Something at the bottom that isn't mine. Or... is mine, but older." He frowned. "A door. In the covenant itself. Where the stones accepted what I gave — there was a seam. A clause. As if the original makers anticipated amendment. As if they *wanted* it amended, someday, by someone willing."

Gideon went still. "You're sure?"

"I built clocks," Sebastian said. "I know a mechanism designed for modification when I see one. Someone left us a hinge, Gideon. A lawful way to rewrite the terms." He looked out toward Hollowmere, toward the border where the wards stood open for the first time in two centuries, and where, in a week's time, delegates of both realms would gather to see what the new world would tolerate.

"And I think," he added quietly, "we're meant to bring it to them. Before someone less careful finds it first."

Below them, on the far road, small figures were already traveling toward the valley. Some carried banners. One banner, catching the new morning light, bore a crest Gideon recognized — and had hoped never to see again outside a burning court.

The work, apparently, was not finished.
