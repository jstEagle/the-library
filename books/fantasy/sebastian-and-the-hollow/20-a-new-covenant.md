## 20. A New Covenant

Wren had decided, somewhere around her third week as an apprentice ward-keeper, that adults talked too much.

Not all of them. Master Ilsa could say more with a raised eyebrow than most people managed in a sermon. But the ones who came to the border — the emissaries, the clerks, the men with sashes and the women with ledgers — they talked like water dripping into a bucket that never filled. They had been at it for two days.

She sat on the old boundary stone, legs swinging, watching Sebastian Vale and Gideon Delacroix do it differently.

The reforged border ran through the pass where she'd once held off Cassian's hunters with fire she hadn't known she carried. The wards here were new — Wren's wards, technically, though Ilsa had guided her hands for the first three and let her do the last seven alone. They didn't hum or glow the way the old ones had. They were quieter than that. A feeling, more than a wall. Like standing in a doorway between a warm kitchen and a cold night: you knew which side you were on, but nothing stopped you from stepping through.

That was the whole point. That was what Sebastian had built when he broke the old covenant and refused to build another one just like it.

"Again," Gideon was saying. They stood on the outer side of the line, arms crossed, wearing their thief's coat even though everyone present knew exactly who they were now. "From the top. Because your clerk wrote *in perpetuity* again, and we've been over this."

Sebastian sighed the sigh of a man who had been over this many times. He was holding a document that looked important — heavy paper, wax seals, the works — but he held it the way he'd once held clock parts, like something that might be fixed if you were patient enough. "Master Corvin, strike *in perpetuity*. Write *renewed each year by mutual consent*."

The clerk struck it out. His pen scratched like a nervous mouse.

"And the exchange rate," Gideon pressed. "One memory for one memory. Not one for three because someone's memories happen to be prettier. I've seen what happens when people start pricing memories. I was the market."

"You were the *black* market," Sebastian said.

"I was the *only* market. There's a difference, but it's smaller than you'd think." Gideon's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a twitch at one corner, quickly suppressed, like a secret trying to escape. Wren had learned to watch for it. It meant Gideon was enjoying themselves and would rather die than admit it.

Wren swung her legs. Below the stone, in the grass on the Hollowmere side, a knot of valley folk had gathered to watch — farmers mostly, and two of the archive-crystal keepers, and old Marta who claimed she'd been present at the founding festival two hundred years ago, which was impossible, but nobody argued with Marta because she brought pies. On the far side, past the border, the outer-realm delegation waited under a canopy: Ashen Court survivors, mostly, people Cassian's fall had left blinking in daylight like moles. Their representative was a thin woman named Sera Voss who had once been bonded and still sometimes smiled when there was nothing to smile about. Wren hated seeing that. She'd decided, privately, that fixing it was going to be part of her job someday.

"The terms," Sebastian said, and his voice changed — not louder, just steadier, the way it did when he talked about things that mattered. Wren had heard him use that voice over the keystone shards, over the ashes of Emberhold, over Gideon's sickbed. "No memory crosses this border without the giver saying yes. Out loud. To a warden. Every time."

"Every time?" one of the outer clerks protested. "For a basket of apples?"

"For a *memory*, yes. Apples are apples." Sebastian tapped the paper. "You want to trade the taste of your mother's bread for a song you heard as a child? Fine. You stand at the stone, you say yes, a warden witnesses it, and both of you walk away knowing exactly what you gave and what you got. No harvesting. No taking from people who can't refuse. Nobody wakes up hollow."

Sera Voss spoke for the first time in an hour. Her voice was soft and careful, like someone relearning how to use it. "And if someone changes their mind? After?"

Sebastian looked at Gideon.

It was a small thing, that look. Most of the delegation probably missed it. Wren didn't. She'd spent a winter learning to read people out of necessity, and she knew what it meant when Sebastian handed a question to Gideon without words: *this part is yours, because you know the cost better than anyone alive.*

Gideon was quiet for a moment. Then they said, "Then there's a return window. Seven days. If either party wants to give back what they received, the warden unwinds the exchange. Both memories go home." A pause. "I stole memories for eleven years. I know every trick there is for making a theft look like a gift. If any clause in this covenant can be bent that way, I'll find it before you sign it, so it's better I tell you now."

Silence. Somewhere down the slope, Marta whispered loudly, "That's the memory-thief? They look tired."

"They're always tired," Wren said, without turning around. "They're thinking."

Marta made a sound of profound respect, as though thinking were a rare disease.

*

The sun climbed. The negotiation went on.

Wren had expected to be bored. She wasn't, quite, because watching Sebastian and Gideon negotiate was like watching two people who had once tried to kill each other with words and had since discovered they were the only two people alive fluent in the same strange language. They interrupted each other constantly. They finished each other's clauses. When Sebastian wanted to give the outer realms more than the valley folk would stomach, he didn't argue for it — he just looked at Gideon, and Gideon made the argument for him, dressed up in thief's cynicism so it sounded like hard-headedness instead of kindness.

"That's cheating," Wren informed them during a break, when the delegations had scattered to eat and the two of them stood alone by the boundary stone sharing a flask of tea.

"What is?" Sebastian asked.

"You keep using Gideon to say the nice things so nobody thinks you're soft."

Sebastian choked slightly on his tea. Gideon looked delighted. "The child sees through us," they said. "We're doomed."

"I'm not a child. I'm an apprentice."

"An apprentice who set a mountain pass on fire to save our lives."

"*Held* a mountain pass. With wards. It's different, and Ilsa says if you keep calling it setting fires I'm allowed to hex you."

"She absolutely does not say that," Sebastian said.

"She says it with her eyebrows."

Gideon laughed — actually laughed, short and rusty, like a door that hadn't been opened in years but still worked. Wren counted that as a victory and filed it away. She kept a mental list of those. *Gideon laughed: 4 times since Emberhold. Sebastian slept a full night: 2 times. Marta's pie acquired: 6 slices.* It was, she had decided, her real apprenticeship. Wards were just chalk and intention. This other thing — keeping count of small good things until they added up to a big one — that was the actual work.

"Can I ask something?" she said, when the laughing had settled. "The old covenant needed blood. A life. You broke that. So why does the new one need anything? Why not just... no covenant? Everyone keeps their own memories, everybody goes home?"

Sebastian and Gideon exchanged a glance again, but a different kind this time. Wren was getting good at these too.

"Because walls are how we got here," Sebastian said finally. He sat down on the boundary stone next to her, which she permitted because he was basically family now, in the way that people who have bled together become family whether anyone asks or not. "The old covenant wasn't really a treaty. It was a scar. Two realms hurt each other so badly that they built a wall across the wound and called it peace. And the wall needed feeding. Blood, then lives, then—" he gestured vaguely at himself, "—me. Anything to avoid actually talking to each other."

"People are terrible at talking," Wren said.

"We are," Gideon agreed solemnly. "Historically catastrophic at it."

"So the new covenant isn't a wall," Sebastian went on. "It's a habit. A reason to come to this stone, every year, and say yes to each other on purpose. It'll be slow. It'll be messy. Someone will cheat, and we'll catch them, and there'll be a scandal, and Ilsa will make a face." He smiled a little. "But it's ours. We chose it. Nobody's feeding it anything they didn't decide to give."

Wren chewed on that for a while, the way she chewed on everything, thoroughly and with her whole face.

"My parents didn't choose to leave me," she said at last. It came out flatter than she'd meant. "The road chose. The war chose. I just... survived it." She kicked at a pebble. "I like that yours is chosen. The new one. I like being—" she hunted for the word, "—*asked*."

Gideon crouched down to her level, which they only did when they meant something. "You were asked, you know. Back on the pass. Ilsa asked if you'd apprentice. You could have said no. Gone feral again. Honestly, some days I think you miss it."

"I don't *miss* it. I miss the quiet. There's no quiet anywhere near Marta."

"There is no quiet anywhere near Marta," Sebastian agreed gravely, "and yet the pie flows."

Wren grinned despite herself. Then, because the moment was warm and she'd learned — slowly, painfully, one burned campfire at a time — that warm moments should be used while they lasted, she said: "When the stars come back tonight. Will they be right? Or dimmer?"

The warmth flickered. Both of them looked up at the pale daytime sky, as though they could see the night hiding behind it.

"Dimmer," Sebastian said honestly. "Maybe forever dimmer. Magic runs thinner now, in both realms. Some things won't come back. Some people's memories won't either." His hand moved, briefly, toward Gideon — toward the spaces in Gideon where sold memories used to live, the gaps they'd both learned to talk around. "But dim stars are still stars. That was the bargain. Less light, freely shared, instead of all the light taken from somebody."

"Freely given," Gideon corrected quietly. "You always say *shared*. It was given. By you."

"It was given by all of us. You gave plenty."

"I gave involuntarily, for years, to a black market. That doesn't count as giving, it counts as being robbed with extra steps."

"Gideon."

"Gideon," Wren echoed, in her best Ilsa voice.

Gideon held up their hands in surrender. "Fine. Given. Freely. By everyone. Happy?" But they were doing the mouth-twitch again, and Wren added a point to the list.

*

Dusk came early to the pass in autumn, sliding down the mountains like water poured from a jug. The delegations lit lanterns. The clerk's pen scratched its final scratches. And Wren, on Ilsa's nod, walked the line of the border and opened the evening wards.

It was the first time she'd done it in front of a crowd, and her palms sweated, and she told her palms to shut up. She laid her hand against the first marker stone and felt the ward wake beneath it — that doorway-warmth, that sense of *you may pass, and you will be seen passing, and that is enough*. Seven stones. Eight. Nine. At each one she said the words Ilsa had taught her, the ones that had replaced the old binding-oaths: *By consent, witnessed. By choice, kept.*

At the last stone she paused and looked up.

The sky over the pass was deepening from grey to violet to a blue so dark it was nearly black, and there — low in the east, shy as a girl at her own party — a star.

Just one at first. Then three. Then a scatter of them, faint, like embers raked over but not dead. The crowd on both sides of the border went silent, hundreds of faces tilting upward at once, valley folk and outer-folk together, and Wren heard old Marta whisper, "There you are," in the tone of a woman greeting children home late.

Dimmer. But present. Sebastian had promised that, and the sky was keeping his word.

Beside her, Sebastian let out a breath he'd maybe been holding for a year. Gideon stood very still, face tipped up, and Wren saw their lips move — counting, maybe, or naming constellations they'd sold and couldn't remember, or just saying *thank you* to nobody in particular. She didn't ask. Some things you let people have.

"Sign it, then," Ilsa said behind them, brisk as ever, because miracles were no excuse for dawdling. "Before the ink dries crooked."

They signed it at the boundary stone itself, because Sebastian insisted the covenant should live where it worked, not in some court. Sebastian first, in his careful clockmaker's hand. Then Gideon, whose signature was a beautiful illegible flourish that the clerk eyed with suspicion. Then Sera Voss for the outer realms, her letters shaky but unbroken. Then Ilsa, as witness. Then, when Sebastian turned and held out the pen with his eyebrows up in a question, Wren.

"Me? I'm not — I'm twelve."

"You're the warden who opened the border tonight," Sebastian said. "This is as much yours as anyone's. More. You're the one who'll be standing here when we're old."

Wren took the pen. Her hand didn't shake, which surprised her. She signed *Wren* — just Wren, the way she always had, the name the road had left her — and then, after a heartbeat, added underneath, in smaller letters: *ward-keeper, apprentice.*

"There," she said. "Now it's official. If you two mess it up, I'm allowed to say I told you so for the rest of history."

"Terrifying," Gideon murmured. "The next generation is already extorting us."

Below the stone, the crowds were mingling — carefully, awkwardly, the way strangers do, but mingling. A valley farmer was showing an Ashen Court survivor his lantern, of all things, explaining the wick. Marta was distributing pie across the border with the unstoppable authority of a natural disaster. Somebody started singing, badly.

Wren sat back down on her stone and watched the dim stars come out one by one over two realms that had decided, against all history and sense, to try talking.

It wasn't fixed. She knew that. Sebastian still woke some nights reaching for a childhood he'd poured into the stones; Gideon still went quiet at odd moments, touching the gaps inside themselves like a tongue finding a missing tooth; the outer realms still had courts full of hollow-eyed people learning how to want things again. It would take years. Maybe generations. Maybe it would fail, and need breaking, and rebuilding, the way all honest things do.

But the stars were out. The border was open. And the paper pinned to the stone said, in ink that four people had signed and one child had witnessed:

*Renewed each year by mutual consent.*

Wren decided that was the best sentence anyone had ever written.

She was still deciding that when Ilsa appeared at her elbow, followed by a small, mud-splattered boy of perhaps ten, who was clutching a battered tin whistle and looking at the border the way Wren once had — like it was a wall, and like he was already planning to climb it.

"Found him on the south road," Ilsa said. "Alone. Says he's looking for the ward-keepers." Her eyebrow did its work. "He has potential. And no manners whatsoever."

The boy looked up at Wren with the exact stubborn, hungry expression she remembered wearing in mirrors.

"Well?" he demanded.

Wren grinned, hopped off her stone, and stuck out her hand.

"Welcome to the worst job in two realms," she said. "Lesson one: never trust anyone who smiles at pie."
