## 21. What Sebastian Chose to Keep

The memories came home in the order they had left, which was not the order Gideon would have chosen.

They sat in the loft of a waystation inn three days' ride from Hollowmere, on a bed that creaked like the clocktower's old bellows, and between them lay Sebastian's childhood — every stolen year of it — folded into a sliver of keystone-glass no bigger than a thumbnail. It had been Gideon's to keep since the night at the border. A keeper's burden. And for three days Gideon had found reasons not to begin.

"You're stalling," Sebastian said.

"I'm being thorough."

"You've checked that glass eleven times."

"Twelve," Gideon said, "if you count the time you were asleep." They turned it over in their palm one more time anyway. The light inside it moved like water under ice — festival lanterns, the smell of solder and oil, a woman's hands cupped around a small brass gear. All of it waiting. All of it his.

Sebastian watched them with the particular patience of a man who had spent a year learning to live without his own past and had gotten, if not good at it, then practiced. He looked thinner than he had before the covenant broke. Calmer, too, in a way that unsettled Gideon more than any rage would have.

"We don't have to do all of it today," he said.

"No. We don't." Gideon closed their hand around the glass. "That's rather the point."

* * *

It had taken Gideon two days after the reforging to understand what they were holding.

The magic had accepted Sebastian's freely given memories as payment — but payment was not the same as destruction. The covenant, even broken and remade, kept its books. What Sebastian had poured into the stones had gone somewhere, and that somewhere, it turned out, was Gideon: the thief, the keeper, the one person in either realm who had spent a lifetime carrying other people's yesterdays and knew how to hold them without dropping them.

Wren had figured it out first, actually, with the blunt cruelty of twelve-year-olds everywhere. She'd looked at Gideon across the campfire — Gideon, who had been staring into the flames and seeing, unbidden, a boy of nine falling out of an apple tree in a valley where magic couldn't follow — and said, "You've got his face on. The thinking-about-something-sad face. Except it's *his* sad thing you're thinking about, isn't it?"

Gideon had not answered. Wren had nodded like she'd won something and asked for more stew.

So here they were. The mechanics were simple enough; Gideon had done the reverse often enough, taking instead of giving, that returning felt almost obscene in its ease. A touch. An opening. A memory sliding home like a key into a lock that had been standing empty.

The hard part was choosing which ones.

Because Sebastian had asked. On the second night, quietly, while Wren slept with her boots on as she always did, ready to run: *"There are some I don't want back. You'll know which ones when you hold them. The heavy ones. The ones that feel like being made for something."*

And Gideon did know. They could feel them even now through the glass — dense, cold shards wrapped in the warm bright stuff of an ordinary childhood. The woman in the warded cradle, sealing her son in with shaking hands and a word that meant *live*. The murals in the Shattered Spire. The figure labeled *the vessel*. The purpose written into his blood before he could walk, the weight he had carried in his bones his whole life without knowing its name.

Gideon had spent years selling pieces of themselves to survive. They knew what it was to lose a memory. They also knew — this was the part nobody warned you about — what it was to be *relieved*. To wake some mornings and reach for the grief over their mother's face and find only smooth blankness, and to feel, beneath the guilt of that relief, something dangerously close to peace.

Some prices should not be repaid. Some debts were kinder left unpaid.

But it wasn't Gideon's choice. That was the whole lesson of the last year, wasn't it? The old covenant had decided for everyone — who paid, who profited, who was born to burn. The new one ran on consent or it ran on nothing.

So they would ask. Every single time. Even when it hurt. Especially then.

"All right," Gideon said, and set the glass down between them. "Ground rules. I give one, you take it, we stop whenever you say stop. No heroics. If something hits wrong, you tell me, and we put it back in the glass until you're ready. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Sebastian held out his hand. Then, because he was Sebastian: "You know you don't have to look like you're handing me a live grenade."

"It's a reasonable approximation of my feelings."

* * *

The first memory was small, deliberately so. Gideon chose it with care: Sebastian at six, sitting cross-legged on the clocktower floor while Master Aldous pretended to be annoyed about the gears scattered everywhere and was not annoyed at all.

Sebastian took it with a sharp breath. His eyes went distant, then soft. When he came back, he was laughing — a startled, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut.

"He kept a tin of sweets behind the escapement cabinet," Sebastian said wonderingly. "He thought I didn't know. He'd give me one whenever I got a mechanism right on the first try and pretend it was punishment for something."

"He sounds like he was a terrible teacher."

"The worst. Made me measure everything twice." Sebastian's voice caught on the last word, just slightly. He pressed his palm flat against his own chest, as though checking that the memory had settled properly. "It fits. It's like — putting a gear back where it belongs. You can feel the teeth catch."

Gideon nodded and did not say what they were thinking, which was that they knew exactly how that felt from the other side. The shape of the absence. The way a missing memory left a hole that ached in bad weather.

They worked through the morning. The apple tree. The first festival Sebastian remembered, sticky with honey-cakes and lost for an hour and not frightened, because Hollowmere was the kind of place where a lost child was everyone's child. The night he'd finished his first clock alone, aged fourteen, and sat in the dark listening to it tick and felt — this came through clearly, Gideon felt it echo in their own chest as they gave it — *this is enough, this is exactly enough, I want nothing else.*

Each time, Gideon asked first. Each time, Sebastian said yes. Once, on the memory of Master Aldous's funeral — grey rain, a small crowd, a boy of sixteen standing too straight beside the coffin — Sebastian stopped halfway through and said, "Wait. Not yet," and Gideon drew it back without comment, and they drank tea in silence for a quarter hour until Sebastian said, "All right. Now," and finished it with tears running freely down his face and no shame in them at all.

By late afternoon the glass was lighter. Gideon could feel the difference in their palm — less weight, less cold. What remained was the hard core, the dense dark center. The cradle. The Spire. The vessel.

Sebastian looked at it for a long time.

"That's her, isn't it," he said. "The woman. My mother. Sealing me in."

"Yes."

"And the rest of it. What I was made for." He turned the phrase over like a flawed casting. "What I was *remade* for."

"If you want it—"

"I know what I want." He said it gently, but firmly, the way he refused things. Gideon had learned that tone across a year of watching him refuse gods and lords and bargains. "I want to ask you something first, and I want you to answer honestly, because you owe me honesty and you've been very good about paying that debt lately and I'd hate for you to break the streak."

Gideon braced. "Ask."

"When you hold those memories. The heavy ones. Do they feel like mine?" A pause. "Or do they feel like yours? Because you carried them longer than I ever did. You've been living with my mother's face in your head for a year, Gideon. You flinched at the word 'cradle' for three weeks. So I'm asking — when you give them back, will you be giving up something you need?"

The question landed harder than any blow Cassian Vey had ever thrown. Gideon looked down at the glass, at the cold dark heart of it, and told the truth.

"They feel like mine," they admitted. "A little. Not the way your grief over Aldous feels like yours. More like — a coat I've been wearing. Warm in places. But it isn't mine, and I know it isn't, and keeping it would be just another kind of theft. The last one." They met his eyes. "I'd rather return it. Truly. I'd just — need a moment after."

"Then we'll take a moment after," Sebastian said simply, as if it were the easiest bargain in either realm. Perhaps it was. It was certainly the first one Gideon had ever entered where both parties intended to keep their end.

* * *

The cradle-memory went last but one.

Gideon gave it slowly, carefully, the way you lower something fragile into water. Sebastian received it with his eyes open — he'd learned to do that, receiving with eyes open, so that whatever he saw wouldn't entirely take him away — and Gideon watched him watch his own beginning: the woman with the covenant-line eyes, the warded wood, the word that meant *live*, the last desperate kindness of a realm coming apart.

When it was done, Sebastian sat very still for a long moment.

"She was singing," he said finally. "Off-key. Badly. She knew it and sang anyway." His mouth trembled into something that was almost a smile. "I always wondered why lullabies made my chest hurt. Now I know it's hers. I have her voice and I never knew where I got it."

"Do you want the last one?"

The last one. The vessel. The purpose. The reason a baby had been sealed in warded wood and smuggled into a valley with no stars and no magic — not saved, *stored*. Set aside like a tool for a job two hundred years in the making.

Sebastian was quiet for so long that Gideon heard Wren downstairs, arguing cheerfully with the innkeep about the correct way to season porridge, and the wind in the eaves, and the tick of the inn's cheap clock on the mantel — slightly fast, Sebastian would have said, needed regulating, and the thought arrived with such ordinary domestic warmth that Gideon nearly laughed.

"No," Sebastian said at last.

Gideon waited.

"Not today. Maybe not ever. Maybe—" He exhaled. "Maybe that's the one I leave behind. Not because I can't bear it. Because I've already answered it. Whatever I was made for, I *refused*, and the realms are still turning, and the stars came back dimmer but present, and Wren is downstairs fighting about porridge. The purpose is finished, Gideon. It did its work, and it failed, and I don't want to carry the blueprint of a machine I dismantled." He looked up, and his eyes were clear. "Some prices shouldn't be repaid. You taught me that. Or — no. You showed me that, when you couldn't remember why you were crying. I decided it myself. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

Gideon's throat was tight. "Then it stays in the glass."

"For now. Keep it safe. You're better at keeping than anyone I've ever known." A pause, and then, with the gentle humor that was Sebastian's armor and gift alike: "Besides, someone should remember what I was almost sacrificed for. It'll keep you humble."

"Nothing keeps me humble. It's a character flaw."

"Mm. One of many."

* * *

They walked out the next morning, the three of them, into a world still learning its new shape.

Wren led, because Wren always led on the road — ahead by twenty paces, hood up, walking with the rolling gait of someone who had survived a mountain pass alone at eleven and would never entirely trust flat ground. She had appointed herself scout, quartermaster, and chief complainer, and performed all three offices with tremendous seriousness. Every few hundred yards she pointed out something: a ward-stone half-buried in the verge, newly carved with the open-circle sigil of the reformed keepers; a merchant cart heading toward Hollowmere with fair-trade memory-goods, the drivers relaxed in a way no traveler had been in two centuries; a hawk, which required no commentary but received some anyway.

Behind her, Sebastian and Gideon walked side by side, unhurried.

"How does it feel?" Gideon asked. "Having it all back. Mostly all."

Sebastian considered. "Like a house where all the furniture's been returned, but I arranged it differently while it was gone. Everything's mine. Nothing's quite where I left it." He glanced sideways. "There's a gap where the vessel-memories used to sit. I can feel the edges of it. Like a missing tooth."

"Does it hurt?"

"Only when I poke it." He smiled. "So I've decided to stop poking it. Which, I'm told, is called healing, and apparently takes longer than a fortnight. Who knew."

The road bent along the river, and there it was at last: the pass above Hollowmere, the valley spread below in autumn gold, smoke rising from chimneys, the wards standing open — *open by design*, the gates wide, the boundary marked now by nothing but a line of pale stones and the willingness of everyone on either side of them.

Two hundred years of walls, and the walls were down, and the town below had not burned. Had, in fact, planted extra fields this year.

Wren stopped at the crest of the pass and waited for them, arms crossed, in the pose of a ward-keeper surveying her charge — which, Gideon reflected, was precisely what she was, apprentice sash knotted crookedly at her waist, first of the new order, self-appointed and unanimously ratified because nobody wanted to argue with her.

"Well?" she demanded. "Are you going to stand up here being poetic all day, or are we going home? There's a festival in three days and I promised Marta I'd judge the pie contest and I am NOT backing out."

Sebastian laughed — full and easy, the laugh of a man whose furniture had come home. "We're coming, we're coming."

He started down the road. Wren fell in beside him, immediately demanding to know whether returned memories included the recipe for honey-cakes, and if so, could he prove it.

Gideon lingered a moment at the top of the pass, the keystone-glass safe in their breast pocket, holding the one memory Sebastian had chosen to leave behind. The weight of it was familiar now. Almost comfortable. A coat worn so long it had become a kind of promise: *kept safe, kept secret, kept until wanted.*

Down in the valley, the bells of Hollowmere began to ring — early, off-schedule, the way they always did when the watchtower spotted travelers on the north road, because the whole town had apparently decided that Sebastian Vale coming home was an event worth unscheduled bells.

Sebastian glanced back up the slope, eyebrows raised. "Coming, Delacroix?"

Gideon touched the glass once, through the fabric, and smiled.

"Right behind you."
