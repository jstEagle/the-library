## 22. Hollowmere, Under Stars

The clocktower smelled of oil and cedar shavings and, faintly, of the past — which Sebastian supposed was appropriate, given what he'd traded away to keep it.

He stood at the top of the tower with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, a gear the size of a dinner plate cradled against his chest, and listened. That was the trick of clockwork nobody told you when you were an apprentice: you didn't build a clock so much as you negotiated with it. Every gear wanted something. Every spring held its tension like a grudge. Your job was to find the arrangement where everything got enough of what it wanted that it agreed, all together, to tell the time.

It occurred to him — not for the first time, but with more warmth than usual — that he'd spent a year doing exactly this with two entire realms.

"Sebastian!" Mrs. Hollis's voice floated up the stairwell, breathless and imperious in equal measure. "The festival committee is here! They've brought questions! And pie!"

"The last time they brought questions and pie, I lost the whole afternoon," Sebastian called back down.

"Yes, but this time there are *two* pies."

He sighed, set the gear down gently on its cloth, and went to negotiate.

---

The wards stood open.

That was still the part that made visitors from the outer realms go quiet when they first crossed the border — not the valley itself, though Hollowmere in autumn was beautiful enough to stop a heart, all gold larches and woodsmoke and the river running silver over pale stones. It was the border stones themselves, unhooded for the first time in two hundred years, humming softly with a light that wasn't a wall at all. It was more like a handshake. More like a door left unlatched on purpose.

Children from Emberhold came through in twos and threes now, wide-eyed at trees that weren't cinders, at grass that didn't crunch underfoot. Hollowmere's farmers had learned to trade with them — eggs and honey and, strangest of all, memories. Fair trades only. That had been the first law of the new covenant, written into the reforged keystones in Sebastian's own hand before the magic took it: *nothing given that was not offered; nothing taken that was not paid for.*

Gideon had argued about the wording for three days. Gideon argued about wording the way other people breathed.

"'Nothing taken that is not paid for' sounds punitive," they'd said, pacing the archive with their ink-stained fingers laced behind their back. "Payment implies debt. Debt implies extraction. We're trying to *dismantle* extraction, Sebastian."

"What would you call it, then?"

"*Exchange*. Reciprocity. A gift acknowledged." They'd stopped pacing and looked at him with that expression they got — half scholar, half someone standing very carefully at the edge of an old wound. "I spent years taking things because no one ever asked if I wanted to give. The words matter. The words are the covenant."

So the words had become: *nothing given that was not offered; nothing received without thanks.* The keystones had accepted it with a sound like a bell being struck underwater, and the stars had come back — dimmer than the old stories promised, but present, patient, *theirs.*

---

Mrs. Hollis had indeed brought two pies, and also the entire festival committee, which numbered seven people and one opinionated goat named Duchess who had opinions primarily about eating things.

"The question," said Alderman Pike, who had been the loudest opponent of opening the wards right up until the moment it became clear it was going to happen, at which point he'd become its most vocal supporter with the seamless grace of a man who had never once been wrong in his own mind, "is the lanterns. Last year we released memory-lanterns over the square. Beautiful. Uplifting. But the delegation from Mirrormere says their flooded streets lit up like — well, like a drowned city full of ghosts having a party, and several of their covenant-keepers were *quite* put out."

"They're not ghosts," Sebastian said, for perhaps the hundredth time. "They're echoes. Residues. The old keepers wove themselves into the water when the city sank. They're not suffering, they're — remembering. Loudly."

"Well, can they remember somewhere else during the festival?"

"I could ask," Sebastian said. "Politely. They did help save both realms; it seems rude to also ask them to be quiet about it."

Duchess the goat ate a corner of pie off the table. Nobody stopped her. There was a general air of everyone understanding that some negotiations were unwinnable.

In the end they settled on lanterns on the eastern meadow, pointed away from the river, with a formal apology delivered to the keeper-echoes beforehand — which Wren volunteered to carry out, mostly because Wren liked any errand that involved walking alone through interesting places and coming back with stories.

"Wren's arriving today," Mrs. Hollis mentioned, in the tone of a woman pretending this was casual information. "Sent word ahead by ward-pulse. Says she has news and it's 'extremely good' and she refuses to say more until she can say it in person."

"That's very Wren," Sebastian said, and found himself grinning like an idiot anyway.

---

The archive had once been the town's memory-hall — rows of crystal shelves where Hollowmere kept its collective recollections, births and harvests and weddings, sealed against a world the valley had decided to pretend didn't exist.

Now it was Gideon Delacroix's kingdom, and it was open to anyone.

That had been the second great argument of the new age: whose memories belonged to whom? The council had wanted the archive locked again, guarded, access by petition. Gideon had stood in front of the doors with their arms crossed and delivered a speech that Sebastian privately thought should have been carved above the entrance. Something about how a vault of shared memory locked against the people who made it was just a tomb with better lighting.

So the doors stayed unlocked. There was a reading room now, with chairs that didn't match and tea that Mrs. Hollis sent over in a bottomless thermos, and people came in the evenings to sit with the crystals of their grandparents and their neighbors and sometimes, carefully, with fair-traded memories from the outer realms — a taste of the sea, the smell of a spice market in Emberhold, the feeling of flying on a wind-rider's shoulders. You could borrow them. You gave something small in return, freely, and the keystones hummed their approval, and the whole thing worked so simply that Sebastian sometimes wanted to shake the founders of Hollowmere and ask what exactly they'd been so afraid of for two hundred years.

(He knew what they'd been afraid of. He'd seen the murals. He carried, folded small and kept deliberately in the dark, the knowledge that the fear had once been aimed at *him* — at what he was made for. He chose, every morning, to leave that particular memory where he'd put it. Some prices should not be repaid. Some weights you were allowed to set down.)

He found Gideon at the archive desk that evening, cataloguing a crate of incoming trades, looking — as they always did these days — like a person who had finally stopped running and discovered that standing still required an entirely different set of muscles.

"You're smiling at a ledger," Sebastian said.

"I'm smiling at the *inventory*. We received a memory of the Mirrormere keeper-choir singing the old covenant verses, freely given, in exchange for a recording of your mother's lullaby — the one you let us copy last spring." Gideon looked up, and their expression did the complicated thing it always did around mentions of the woman in the warded cradle. "Twelve requests for it this month. She's becoming quite famous in realms that tried to erase her family."

"She'd have laughed at that," Sebastian said. He didn't remember her — not really, not anymore — but he remembered *knowing* her, the shape of the knowing, the way you remember a room after the lamp goes out. "She'd have demanded royalties."

"She'd have demanded the archive name a shelf after her, and then pretended to be embarrassed when we did."

"It's a good shelf."

"It's an excellent shelf." Gideon set down the ledger. For a moment they just looked at him — that look they'd never entirely lost, the one that checked, quietly, that he was still real, still whole, still *there*, a habit left over from the night they'd bled out on the Spire steps and woken up anyway because a boy had refused to let the universe take the easy bargain. "Wren's ward-pulse came through an hour ago. Did you get it?"

"She's bringing news. Extremely good news. Refuses to elaborate."

Gideon smiled slowly. "She's learned theatrical timing from you."

"She learned it from *you*, actually, and I've never forgiven either of you."

---

* * *

Wren arrived at dusk, on foot, as she always did, with dust on her boots and a grin that suggested she had personally arranged the sunset for dramatic effect.

She was fourteen now, and taller, and wore the grey-and-silver sash of the reformed ward-keepers across her chest like she'd invented the concept of sashes. Which, in fairness, she somewhat had — the order had been a handful of frightened old archivists before Wren had walked into their sanctuary two winters ago and told them, with the tactless clarity of the young, that wards built on fear were just prettier walls, and wouldn't they rather build something worth keeping?

They had rather, as it turned out. Wren had taught them how.

"So," she said, dropping onto the bench outside the clocktower between Sebastian and Gideon, producing from her pack a wrapped bundle that smelled of road-bread and one that smelled suspiciously of stolen pastries, "news."

"You could send it by pulse like a normal person," Gideon said.

"Normal people don't have news this good." She tore the pastry bundle in thirds with the efficiency of someone who had once fought a guardian over a heel of bread and never intended to lose food to diplomacy again. "The Ashen Court has dissolved."

Sebastian nearly dropped his third. "Dissolved? Cassian's faction just—"

"Cassian's *dead*, Sebastian, keep up. Two years ago. What's new is the Court itself — the councils voted last month. All the bonded servants freed, the memory-tithes abolished, the throne—" she paused for effect, because of course she did, "—the throne room is being converted into a public school."

There was a silence.

"A school," Gideon repeated, in the voice of someone testing whether a bridge will hold their weight.

"In the building where they harvested people. First class starts in spring. They're asking if the Hollowmere archive will lend teaching-memories. Freely given." Wren took a bite of pastry and spoke around it, because some habits survived even dignity. "And there's more. The ward-keepers want to make me a full keeper. Not apprentice. Full. Youngest in the order's history. And they want me to take the western border — my border, the pass where—" she waved a hand vaguely at her own history, the fire and the hunters and the night she'd chosen people over survival, "—where everything happened. My route. My wards."

"Wren." Sebastian found his throat unexpectedly tight. "That's — congratulations doesn't even—"

"Yeah, yeah." But she was glowing, and not from ward-light. Then her expression shifted, and she got the look she got when she was about to say the true thing underneath the true thing, the thing she'd learned from watching him and Gideon circle each other for a year before either of them said anything honest. "There's one more thing. Actually the main thing. I buried it because I wanted to see your faces on the rest first."

Gideon went very still. Old reflex. Sebastian reached over without thinking and put a hand on their arm, and felt them breathe again.

"The outer realms are asking for a gathering. All of them. Emberhold, Mirrormere, the free cities, Hollowmere. A covenant-moot, next founding season, here in the valley — the first one in history. They want the three of you to open it." She grinned. "They're calling it the Festival of Chosen Terms. Someone in Emberhold apparently thinks that's poetic."

"Someone in Emberhold has been reading Gideon's correspondence," Sebastian said.

"Someone in Emberhold stole a draft of Gideon's correspondence," Gideon corrected, "and I want it noted that whoever it was left *payment*, so technically it wasn't theft, it was—"

"A gift acknowledged," Sebastian and Wren said together, and Gideon threw up their hands while Wren cackled.

But underneath the laughter, Sebastian felt it settle into place — the thing he'd been building toward without quite naming it, gear finding gear finding gear, the whole mechanism agreeing at last to turn. A moot. A gathering. Both realms sitting down at one table, voluntarily, with nothing walled and nothing owed.

His terms. Their terms. Everyone's.

---

* * *

Festival night came bright.

The whole valley turned out — Hollowmere born and outer-realm visitors side by side, no one checking papers because there were no papers, just the soft handshake-hum of the open wards welcoming everyone home who meant no harm. The memory-lanterns rose over the eastern meadow, gold and green and one defiantly purple one that everyone suspected was Wren's. Downriver, the keeper-echoes sang anyway, audible as a far-off choir through the water, and instead of complaining the crowd had started singing along, badly, joyfully, the old covenant verses turned into something new by sheer weight of voices that hadn't existed when the verses were written.

Sebastian stood at the top of the clocktower — his tower, kept and keeping — and watched the lights drift.

A year ago he had poured his childhood into three stones to break a system built on taking. He'd done it freely, and that was the whole point, and he'd do it again, and he also — this was the part he'd only recently allowed himself — *missed it*. Missed the clockmaker's shop and the smell of solder and the Founding Festivals where he'd been a lonely orphan boy watching other people's families from the edge of the square. Missing it didn't undo the choice. Choosing grief freely given was still choosing.

But look what the choice had bought.

Below him, Gideon crossed the square with a lantern of their own, pausing every few steps to talk to someone — always someone, Gideon could not cross a room without collecting a conversation — and Wren perched on the ward-stone at the border with her legs dangling over two realms, telling a story to a ring of children from three different homelands who were united entirely by the universal conviction that whatever she was describing sounded made-up and they demanded proof.

And Sebastian felt it arrive, quiet as a gear seating into place: *free.*

Not safe — he'd learned safety was a wall, and walls were just fears with masonry. Not certain — certainty had been written into his bones before he was born, and he'd shattered it on purpose. Free. Free to keep the tower or leave it. Free to attend the moot or send word he was busy with a stubborn escapement. Free to hold his missing childhood in one hand and Gideon's returned memories in the other and decide, each morning, what to carry forward and what to lay down with thanks.

Free to choose his terms. Every single day.

The stars burned overhead — dimmer than the old songs claimed, brighter than the year of darkness, steady as a promise kept by people instead of imposed on them. Somewhere below, Gideon looked up and found him on the tower balcony, and raised their lantern, and didn't shout anything because Gideon knew he'd hear it anyway:

*"Come down when you're done being dramatic. There's pie. Duchess already claimed one."*

Sebastian laughed, and the sound rang off the clocktower like a bell struck clean, and he turned to go down to them —

— and stopped.

Because on the horizon, past the open wards, past the handshake-light of the border stones, in the sky above the Shattered Spire where nothing had moved in a year, a star that had not been there before flickered once, twice —

and began, slowly, deliberately, to fall.
