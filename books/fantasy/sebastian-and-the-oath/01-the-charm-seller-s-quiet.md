## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet

The fog came off the harbor at dusk the way it always did in Vellmark—slow, gray, and smelling of salt and old fish and things better left unnamed. Quentin Dravenmoor watched it swallow the crooked rooftops of Saltrow from behind her shop window, her hands busy with a task that required no thought: winding copper wire around an iron nail until it looked, to any passing eye, like exactly what she intended it to be. A trinket. Nothing more.

The bell above her door had not rung all day.

She told herself this was good. Silence was safety. Silence was the whole architecture of her life, built plank by careful plank over ten years—the shop with no name painted on its sign, only a small brass sun that meant *charms and mending* to those who knew how to read such things; the plain wool dresses with no color bright enough to be remembered; the habit of looking at people's chins instead of their eyes, so that no one could later swear the charm-seller had *looked* at them, truly looked, and seen what they carried inside.

Ten years since the pyres at Karth Hollow. Ten years since she had stood in the smoke and sworn herself quiet.

Her mouth remembered the oath even now, though she never spoke it aloud anymore. It lived in her teeth like a splinter of cold iron: *I bind my voice. I will not speak power into the world. What is broken in me stays broken until the world is safe to mend.* The words were old ones, oath-speech, and they had taken root in her the way oaths did—not as memory but as law. When she had spoken them, kneeling in the ash with her mentor's blood drying on her sleeve, something in her throat had closed like a door, and it had never fully opened since.

That was the cruelty of the binding, and its mercy both. She could not wield her gift. But neither could it be torn from her by force or fear, because fear could not make her speak what she had forbidden herself to say.

A knock at the door. Three soft raps, then two, then one—the rhythm dockworkers used when they wanted something they didn't want to ask for.

Quentin set down the wire, checked that her face held nothing but mild shopkeeper's patience, and opened the door on a man half-hidden by fog and his own hunched shoulders.

"Bram Tull," he said, though she already knew him. Everyone in Saltrow knew everyone; that was the trouble with Saltrow. He was a stevedore, broad through the chest, with rope-burned hands he kept curling into fists against his thighs. "You got a minute?"

"For paying customers? Always." She stepped back to let him in, and the bell gave a single muted clank, quickly smothered, as if it too had learned discretion.

Bram did not sit when she gestured to the stool. He stood in the middle of her little shop, among the hanging bundles of dried lavender and river-sage, the shelves of honest goods—needles, lamp oil, tallow candles, boot wax—and the dishonest ones tucked between them: knotted red thread for cramps and fevers, hag-stones on leather cords, small pouches of salt and iron filings that were, mostly, just salt and iron filings. The real work she did in the back room, after dark, alone.

"It's my girl," Bram said. His voice came out rough, like something dragged over gravel. "Mira. She's six. Fever's been on her four days now. The physicker bled her twice and it's worse." He swallowed. "They're saying at the docks you—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "They're saying you can fix things. That aren't broken in the normal way."

There it was. The sentence every frightened person in Vellmark eventually worked up the courage to say, and the one Quentin dreaded more than any other, because answering it truthfully would cost her everything, and answering it falsely might cost a child her life.

"Who says that?" Quentin asked quietly.

"Doesn't matter who."

"It matters very much who."

He shifted. "Old Wenna. Down at the Gull. Says you fixed her hip when the bone-setters couldn't. Says you did it without touching her."

*Wenna*, Quentin thought wearily. She had eased the woman's pain two winters ago with three words whispered under her breath while pretending to adjust a poultice—three words of unbinding, so small a working the oath had barely stirred, like a cat shifting in its sleep. She had told herself it was nothing. She had told herself the same thing every time, and every time the telling wore thinner.

"The fever kind," Bram pressed, "the kind that makes her talk to people who aren't there. Sees lights crawling on the walls. Wenna says that's not fever at all. Wenna says that's *touch*."

Touch. The word landed in the room like a dropped coin, ringing. Some children were born touched—thin-skinned to the world's hidden currents, able to hear what stones heard and see the threads that ran beneath things. In the old days, before the war, before the edicts, the order would have found Mira and trained her gently, taught her to carry it the way Quentin had once carried hers. Now, touched children were reported. Touched children were taken for examination. And examinations ended, more often than not, in smoke.

"She doesn't need fixing," Quentin said. "If what Wenna thinks is true, there's nothing wrong with your daughter at all. There's something wrong with a world that would burn her for it."

Bram stared at her. "Then what do I do?"

Quentin moved to the shelf and took down a small clay vial, stoppered with wax, filled with a bitter infusion of willowbark and meadowsweet and one other thing she ground fresh each month from a plant that grew only where dragon-veins ran near the surface—a plant that cooled touched-blood and quieted the crawling lights. To any inquisitor who ever searched her shop, it would read as a common febrifuge. It was. And it wasn't.

"Three drops in water, morning and night," she said. "It will bring the fever down. And when the lights come back—and they may, they may always come back—you tell her this: the lights aren't strangers. They're hers. Nobody gets to take them away from her, and nobody gets to make her ashamed of them." She paused. "And Bram. You tell no one where you got this. Not Wenna, not the physicker, not your own mother. If anyone asks, you bought willowbark tea from the apothecary on Cordwainer Street, and it helped, and that's the whole story."

"Why?" His voice cracked. "Why all the hiding?"

Because I am the last of an order of forty, and thirty-nine of them are ash. Because I have spent ten years being no one, and I am so tired of being no one that some nights I forget who I was. Because if I am seen, truly seen, everything I buried comes up out of the ground with me.

"Because helping is dangerous these days," she said aloud. "Even the ordinary kind. Especially the ordinary kind."

He paid her in coppers counted out one at a time, wincing at each, and she took less than the worth of the vial and let him believe he'd gotten a bargain. At the door he turned, hesitated, and said, "You're a good woman, mistress."

"No," Quentin said, closing the door softly behind him. "I'm a careful one."

*

The fog thickened after full dark. Quentin ate bread and hard cheese standing at her counter, listening to the harbor bells count the hours, and tried not to think about Mira Tull seeing lights on her walls. Tried not to think about how many other Miras there might be in Vellmark right now, thin-skinned and frightened, learning early the lesson Quentin had learned in blood: that what you are must be hidden, or it will be used, or it will be burned.

Loneliness was the tax on concealment, and she had paid it so long she'd nearly stopped noticing the weight. But some nights—foggy nights, nights when the bell didn't ring—it settled on her shoulders like wet wool. Ten years of conversations conducted entirely in code. Ten years of friendships measured in half-truths. Ten years of going to sleep with words dammed up behind her teeth, beautiful words, true words, *powerful* words, that would never again be allowed into the air.

She had been a swordspeaker once. The last one now, though the title meant nothing in a world that had forgotten the order ever existed. Swordspeakers didn't fight with blades—they fought with vows. An oath spoken truly could bend the world around itself: *I vow this bridge holds* made bridges hold; *I vow you shall find peace* had, twice in her life, actually brought peace. Her order had ended the Dragon War with a single spoken covenant, bought with their lives, and the realm had repaid them within a generation with fire and edict and forgetting.

She rinsed her cup, banked her stove, and was trimming the lamp when the voices drifted in through the shutters—two men passing along the street below, their words carried up on the fog.

"—edict's coming, that's certain. Man at the customs house saw the proclamation with his own eyes. Crown seal and all."

"Against magic, proper? Not just the hedge-witches?"

"All of it. They're sending inquisitors this time. Real ones, from the capital. Corvane's men, someone said. Or Corvane himself." A low laugh. "Better sell your luck-charms quick, eh? Might be worth more than gold soon. Or a hanging."

The footsteps faded. Quentin stood very still, the lamp scissors frozen in her hand, and felt the familiar cold begin at the base of her skull and slide down her spine, vertebra by vertebra.

Inquisitors. From the capital.

She had known this day would come. She had known it the way a condemned woman knows the date of her execution—in the abstract, comfortably far off, never quite real. For ten years Vellmark had been far enough from the throne's attention, a gray port city full of gray people, too busy smuggling tar and herring to bother with witch-hunts. She had built her whole quiet life on that indifference.

Now the crown was sending *Corvane's men*. Everyone knew the name. Sebastian Corvane, the crown's own hound, the inquisitor who had cleared the gifted out of three provinces without ever losing his reputation for fairness—which somehow made it worse. Fair men were thorough men. Fair men followed every thread.

Quentin set down the scissors. Her hands were steady—that was the discipline of a lifetime—but her heart was hammering against the cage of her ribs like something trying to get out.

*Stay invisible,* she told herself, and the words rang hollow even inside her own head. *At any cost.*

But what cost, exactly? Stop selling charms? Let Bram Tull's daughter burn with fever because the alternative was a whisper overheard? Every retreat shrank the world a little more. She had already given up her name, her order, her voice, her past. What was left to surrender except the last few small mercies that made the hiding bearable?

She crossed to the window and parted the shutter a finger's width. Fog lay over Saltrow like a burial shroud, and somewhere beyond it, out on the black water, a ship's lantern swung—coming closer, or going away, impossible to tell. Somewhere beyond that, weeks down the coast road, an inquisitor was packing his warrants.

Quentin let the shutter fall.

"One week," she murmured to the empty shop—to the lavender bundles, the honest candles, the dishonest knots of red thread. "One week to decide whether I run again, or—"

Or what, she didn't know. That was the terrible part. Ten years of silence had left her with no idea what she would do if she finally stopped running.

The bell above her door stayed still. The fog pressed close against the glass. And in her chest, behind the door that had not opened in ten years, the old oath lay quiet as a drawn blade—waiting, patient, for the hand that would one day be desperate enough to lift it.

Outside, far off across the water, a ship's bell rang once through the fog.

Someone was arriving in Vellmark.

Quentin blew out her lamp and stood in the dark, listening to it fade.
