## 2. A Room for the Inquisitor

The knock came at dusk, when the light through Quentin's shutters went the color of old honey and the harbor bells rang their slow, mournful count of six. She was grinding dried rowan bark at the back counter, and the sound of knuckles on wood—three measured raps, unhurried, official—stopped her hand mid-circular motion.

She knew before she opened the door. Some part of her had known since morning, when the ferry from the capital had come in riding low with soldiers' baggage and a wind that smelled wrong, like iron filings and cold ash. Ten years in Vellmark had taught her to read the weather of the city: gulls meant fish, brine meant rain, and a hush along the docks meant someone important had arrived.

Three raps. Not the desperate double-knock of a customer needing a charm by nightfall. Not the furtive scratch of someone who wanted what could not be asked for aloud.

Official.

Quentin set down the mortar, wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at herself in the small tarnished mirror by the door. A charm-seller looked back—middle-aged, gray threading her dark braid, hands stained with bark and beeswax. Harmless. Bored, even. She let that woman settle over her like a coat, buttoned it up, and opened the door.

The man on her step filled most of it. Tall, broad through the shoulders, dressed in traveling leathers gone soft with road dust rather than the polished black of the capital's inquisitorial guard. His hair was dark going silver at the temples, his face weathered into something that might once have been handsome and had settled instead for interesting. But it was the badge that held her eyes—a disc of white bronze at his collar, stamped with the crowned eye of the Inquisition.

*The crowned eye sees all,* the edicts said. *The crowned eye burns what it sees.*

"Madam." His voice was low, gravel-smoothed, the voice of a man who had learned to make himself pleasant to frightened people. "Forgive the hour. I'm told you keep a spare room."

"I keep a shop," Quentin said. Her voice came out steady. Good. The woman in the mirror would have been proud. "Charms against damp, against rats, against bad luck at cards. I don't keep rooms."

"The landlady at the Gilded Eel disagrees." He produced a folded paper, but didn't offer it—just held it, casually, the way a duelist holds a hand he's already won. "She says you mentioned it to her last month. A room above your shop, empty since your last lodger left. She thought—" and here something flickered across his face, dry as dust, almost humor, "—she thought an officer of the crown might be good for business."

The Gilded Eel's landlady was a gossip with a memory like a fishhook. Quentin felt the trap close around her with the gentle inevitability of a door swinging shut. To refuse now—to insist the room didn't exist—was to lie to a crowned inquisitor about a matter of no consequence whatsoever, which was precisely the kind of small, stupid lie that got quiet people noticed.

"The attic room," she said. "It's dusty. The roof leaks in the northeast corner."

"I've slept in worse." He stepped past her before she'd quite invited him, and paused just inside, taking in the shop with a single slow turn of his head. The hanging bundles of herbs. The jars of river-stones and salt. The little pewter charms on their ribbons, each one blessed with nothing more dangerous than patience and a soldering iron. "You sell luck," he observed.

"I sell *hope*, mostly," Quentin said. "Luck is just hope with better packaging."

Something moved behind his eyes—not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe, though of what she couldn't say. "Corvane," he said, offering no hand. "Sebastian Corvane."

She should not have known the name. She made sure her face showed only polite blankness as she gave her own—her false one, worn smooth as a river stone after ten years of use—and led him up the narrow stair.

* * *

The room was small and clean because Quentin had cleaned it that morning without knowing why, some animal instinct scrubbing surfaces against a coming inspection. Corvane set down a single travel case—lighter than a soldier's, heavier than a pilgrim's—and stood at the window looking out over the rooftops toward the harbor, where the evening lamps were coming alight one by one, scattered gold on dark water.

"You'll want to know my business here," he said, without turning. "Before rumors reach you anyway."

"Rumors travel faster than ferries in Vellmark."

"They do." He turned. In the lamplight his face was difficult to read—not guarded, exactly, but *arranged*, every feature placed where it ought to be. "There have been... incidents. Fires. An apothecary on Tanner's Row burned three weeks ago under circumstances the local watch found inconvenient. The crown has an interest in unexplained fire these days." He smiled, and it reached his mouth and stopped there. "I'm told you're a woman who notices things, Madam Ashgrave. The landlady says you hear everything on this street."

"The landlady says a great many things."

"She does. It's why I'm paying her too." He said it lightly, and it landed heavily, and Quentin understood that this was the shape of him: a man who told you the truth wrapped in enough courtesy that you couldn't take offense, only fear.

"I notice damp," she said. "I notice when the herring run comes early. If I notice anything else, Master Corvane, I'll be sure to mention it to the watch."

"To me," he corrected gently. "Directly to me. You'll find I'm easier to talk to than the alternative."

He wished her goodnight then, courteously, and closed the door, and Quentin stood a long time on the landing listening to the small sounds of a stranger making himself at home in her house: the creak of floorboards, the scrape of a chair, silence. Above all, silence. No muttered prayers, no scratching of pen on paper, no whispering of names into a recording-crystal such as senior inquisitors were rumored to carry.

Just a man, alone, being quiet.

She hated how much that unsettled her.

* * *

Over the following week they developed a ritual neither of them named. He came down near dawn when she lit the shop lamps; she left bread and strong tea on the landing table; he paid for both with exact coins, always exact, as if generosity were a debt he refused to incur. They spoke in the mornings, briefly, across the counter while she weighed out charms for fishermen who wouldn't meet his eyes.

Their conversations were fencing matches, though neither drew steel. He would ask about the city—who owned which warehouse, which priests had grown rich, where the night market set up its stalls—and she would answer in truths so complete they became useless, whole histories with no center. He would smile at that. Once, she caught him smiling at her specifically, a look she couldn't file anywhere, and it had frightened her more than any interrogation.

Because here was the thing she did not want to know, did not consent to knowing: she recognized the arrangement of his face. She wore one herself. Ten years of practice had taught her exactly how much warmth to put in a greeting, how much vagueness in an answer, how to be *pleasant* the way a wall is smooth—nothing for the world to grip. And watching Sebastian Corvane pour tea for a nervous dockworker, watching him perform the affable crown servant down to the last calibrated degree, she saw the same craft. The same exhaustion underneath it.

Two masks, nodding at each other across a shop counter.

"You're from the capital originally?" she asked him one gray morning, mostly to test the blade.

"Originally." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, precise as everything he did. "My family had an estate near Corvale. Hence the name—the crown grants surnames to families it finds useful, and takes them back when it doesn't." Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice. "The estate is gone now. The name remains. The crown is generous in strange ways."

"And you serve it anyway."

"I serve it *because*," he said, and did not finish the sentence, and drank his tea, and Quentin understood that she had just watched a man step to the edge of a truth and step back, and that he knew she'd seen.

That afternoon she caught herself doing something unforgivable: noticing that he hummed, very quietly, when he thought himself alone. An old song. A border ballad her mother had sung, from a country that no longer existed on any map the crown honored. She stood in the stairwell with a basket of rowan bark in her arms and felt dread and kinship twist together in her chest like two snakes in a sack, each swallowing the other's tail.

*He is the enemy,* she reminded herself. *He is the crowned eye. Every kindness is a hook.*

But hooks needed bait, and she could not stop wondering what he was baiting, or for whom—or whether, beneath the badge and the arranged face, there was simply another person keeping themselves alive one careful day at a time.

* * *

On the eighth night, she woke to lamplight moving under his door. Past midnight, past any honest hour. Quentin lay in the dark telling herself to sleep, and then rose anyway, barefoot on cold boards, and pressed her ear to the gap.

Paper rustling. Then his voice, low, speaking to no one in the room—dictating, perhaps, or praying, or rehearsing. She caught only fragments through the wood:

"...no trace of the gifted in the lower quarter... the apothecary's records burned clean... requesting guidance on the matter of the *sigil*..."

Sigil.

The word went through her like a splinter seeking the heart. There was only one kind of sigil the Inquisition cared about, and only one reason a man would report finding none while sitting awake past midnight, choosing his words like a surgeon choosing knives.

Quentin retreated to her own room and sat on the edge of her bed until the sky went gray, hands folded in her lap, feeling the old binding wake along her tongue like a scar remembering the knife. Somewhere below her, the inquisitor slept—or didn't—and somewhere beyond the city, in whatever archive held the crown's lists, her true name waited in ink.

And Sebastian Corvane, humming his dead country's songs, had just lied to his masters about a scorched oath-mark he had found and hidden.

She did not yet know why. She only knew that the fencing match had changed—that the blades were real now, and the next pass would draw blood from one of them, and she had eight days of shared bread and exact coins telling her it would not be the blood she'd expected.

Downstairs, faintly, she heard him begin to hum.
