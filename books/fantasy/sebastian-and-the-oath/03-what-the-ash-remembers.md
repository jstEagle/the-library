## 3. What the Ash Remembers

The apothecary on Tanner's Row had been dead for three days before anyone thought to send for him, and by then the ash had opinions of its own.

Sebastian Corvane stood in the doorway of what remained of Marlow & Daughters, boots crunching over char that had once been a threshold of good Vellmark oak. The fire had eaten from the inside out—no forced entry, no scorch trail leading back to the street. It had simply *begun*, somewhere near the back room where the tinctures lived, and it had begun hungry.

Smoke still hung in the rafters like a congregation reluctant to disperse. Beneath the usual stink of burnt pitch and ruined timber ran something sweeter, fainter: wormwood, he decided. And under that, something older. Something that smelled less like a shop and more like a chapel after candles.

He crouched and pressed two fingers into the ash at his feet. Still soft at the center. Cool at the edges, but not cold—not yet, not after three days. The fire remembered itself here. That was the phrase his instructors at the Inquisitorial College had used, back when he was nineteen and believed everything they said: *fire remembers*. Arson leaves fingerprints. Magic leaves confessions.

"Corvane." The watch-sergeant who'd met him at the street was a broad woman with a soot-smudged jaw and the flat patience of someone who had seen too many burned buildings to be impressed by this one. "You'll want the cellar."

"Was anything taken?"

"Nothing we can prove. Coin box is melted shut with the coin still in it. Whole stock of poppy resin upstairs, untouched by the looters." She shrugged. "Vellmark folk don't steal from burned houses. Old superstition. They say the smoke tells the neighbors who took what."

*Superstition.* Sebastian filed the word away with all the other words this city kept using around him, like a family speaking a language they pretended their guest didn't understand. He followed her down a staircase whose bottom steps had been licked black but not destroyed, into a cellar that should have been full of water and wasn't.

That was the second wrong thing.

Tanner's Row sat below the flood line. Every cellar on this street flooded twice a year; the watch complained about it every spring. But this cellar floor was dry as a sermon, and in the exact center, ringed by ash that had fallen and settled and been undisturbed by any drop of water, was the mark.

A circle. A sword laid vertical through it, point down. And threaded through the blade's crossguard, a line of script too fine to read at first glance—characters that weren't letters at all but oaths compressed into symbol, each stroke a promise made binding.

An oath-sigil. Swordspeaker's work.

Sebastian knelt beside it for a long time without touching it. The last recorded swordspeaker had died eleven years ago at the field called Hollowmere, along with the entire order, in a battle the histories now described as a *border incident* in the same flat tone one might use for a bridge collapse. He had been fourteen when the edicts came down. He had grown up in a kingdom that taught its children the oath-speakers were liars with dangerous mouths, that their gifts were chains dressed as virtues, that the burning of them had been regrettable hygiene rather than murder.

And yet here was a sigil scorched into a cellar floor, fresh enough that the ash hadn't finished settling into its grooves.

"Who lived above?" he asked.

"Marlow himself. Apothecary, forty years in the trade. His daughter ran the front counter." The sergeant hesitated. "Neither one's been seen since the fire. Watch assumed they died in it."

"Assumed."

"We didn't find remains, if that's your next question. But fires like this—" she gestured vaguely upward, at everything— "don't always leave much."

Fires like this. As though there were a category. Sebastian rose, brushed ash from his knee, and did the arithmetic he had been trained never to do aloud: a missing apothecary, a missing daughter, an oath-sigil dry-kept through a wet winter, and a fire that had started indoors without a door being opened.

"Seal the house," he said. "No one enters until I return."

"And when you report—"

"I haven't decided what I'm reporting," Sebastian said, and heard how it sounded, and left before the sergeant could decide what *she'd* heard.

* * *

His father had taught him cards on a winter much colder than this one, in a house that no longer belonged to the Corvanes.

It was one of his few unpoisoned memories: lamplight, a chipped deck, his father's big clumsy hands shuffling badly because they were surgeon's hands and had never learned sleight. *Watch the hands, not the eyes,* his father would say. *Eyes are honest. Hands are what people train.*

Then the scandal had come—the trial that was barely a trial, the accusations of malpractice against patients who had powerful relatives, the fines that became debts that became ruin—and the crown had appeared, as crowns do, offering rescue shaped exactly like a hook. Sebastian's father's name cleared, the family kept from the debtor's row, in exchange for a son. Fourteen years old, clever, pliable, *grateful*. The Inquisitorial College took boys like that the way the army takes orphans: permanently.

He had repaid them with twenty years of competence. Warrants served quietly. Gifted folk found and processed with a courtesy his superiors mistook for zealotry and which was, in truth, only shame wearing a uniform. He told himself the story every morning the way other men prayed: *I hunt them so that my family keeps its name. I am gentle because I cannot be just.*

But lately the story had developed seams.

Because in the last four months, his orders had changed texture. The old warrants named names—this hedge-witch, that charm-peddler, specific crimes, specific sentences. The new ones came from a level above his handler, sealed in grey wax, and they asked stranger things. *Map the gifted networks of the eastern coast. Identify concentrations. Note bloodlines.* Bloodlines. Since when did the crown care about the ancestry of a luck-charmer? You didn't need a pedigree to hang a smuggler of hexed trinkets.

And there was the matter of the requisitions. He had signed off, weeks ago, on a convoy manifest bound for Vellmark's citadel—iron wagons, chained freight, escort doubled. The cargo line read *ceremonial materials*, which meant nothing and could hold anything. He had learned since that the wagons went down into the citadel's undercroft and did not come out.

Arrests filled ledgers. This was something else. This was collection.

* * *

He walked back to Quentin Dravenmoor's house as the light went copper over the rooftops.

It was a habit now, and he distrusted habits he couldn't account for. Three nights a week they ate together at her table—she cooked plainly and well, and she listened the way soldiers listen, which is to say completely and without giving anything away. She was the most guarded person he had ever met, and he had met prisoners awaiting interrogation. Everything about her was composed, economical, arranged. Her hands never fidgeted. Her eyes went to exits the way his own did.

Once, passing her a plate, their fingers had brushed and he'd felt calluses in patterns no shopkeeper earns—a swordsman's calluses, ridged along the palm in the exact geography a blade teaches. She had withdrawn her hand smoothly, smiled her closed-door smile, and spoken of the weather.

He had said nothing. Professional habit. Or perhaps—he examined this thought the way he'd examine a coin suspected false—perhaps something else. Because there was a kinship in it he hadn't expected and didn't welcome: they were both performances walking upright. He performed the loyal inquisitor. She performed, whatever she actually was. Two masks across one table, each recognizing the lacquer in the other.

Tonight the house smelled of stew and lamp oil. She greeted him at the door with the same measured warmth, asked after his day with the same practiced mildness, and he answered with the same careful nothing.

"Fire on Tanner's Row," he said instead of *fine, thank you*. Watching her face the way his father had taught him to watch hands.

She ladled stew. "Terrible. Was anyone hurt?"

"The owners are missing."

"That happens," Quentin said mildly, "with fires." And her voice was level, and her hands were steady, and her eyes held his for exactly as long as politeness required and not a heartbeat more.

Everything correct. Everything trained.

He ate, and praised the food, and talked of rents and weather and the price of coal, and beneath the table his thumb traced the shape of the sigil he had memorized and burned out of his official notes: circle, sword, oaths threaded through steel.

Back in his rented room, he took out the grey-sealed orders and read them again by candlelight, all the way to the final paragraph he had skimmed past twice before because it made no sense:

*Upon identification of any remaining oath-bound voices, do not process locally. Report disposition directly to the Citadel. The realm has need of living instruments.*

Living instruments. Not arrests. Not pyres.

They wanted them alive, and they wanted them counted, and they wanted them delivered—and suddenly the dry cellar on Tanner's Row looked less like a hiding place than a specimen jar, and Vellmark less like a city than a net drawn slow and patient around something the crown had been missing for eleven years.

One oath-bound voice, the old records claimed, had survived Hollowmere. Unconfirmed. Presumed scattered. Presumed harmless.

Sebastian pinched out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to Quentin moving quietly downstairs—locking up, banked hearth, the small nightly liturgy of a person who had rehearsed safety until it looked like nature.

Tomorrow, protocol demanded, he would file his report on Tanner's Row.

He lay awake long past midnight, staring at the ceiling, doing the only arithmetic that had ever truly mattered to him: what he owed, and to whom, and whether a debt incurred at fourteen could still bind a man who finally understood what he'd been sold to collect.

Below him, the floorboards creaked once—her footsteps, pausing, he thought, just beneath his window. Listening for him, perhaps. Or listening for the dark.

He did not sleep. And in the morning, the report he wrote contained a sigil-shaped absence where the truth should have been, and both of them knew it, though neither had yet said a word.
