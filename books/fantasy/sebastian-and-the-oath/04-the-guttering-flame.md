## 4. The Guttering Flame

The letter came on a morning that smelled of rain and old fish, carried by a courier with salt-cracked hands who asked no questions and took no coin.

Quentin found it wedged beneath her shop door when she went to sweep the step—a habit now, three weeks into Sebastian Corvane's tenancy, though she told herself it was only tidiness. The envelope was plain brown paper, unsealed, unmarked. But her fingers knew before her mind did. The paper was Vellmark-made, cheap, the kind sold in bundles of fifty at Ferrow's stationer's—and across its face, in a hand she had not seen in eleven years, someone had drawn a single small mark: a sword, point-down, thrust through a flame.

Her order's sigil. Her mentor's shorthand for *danger*.

She stood on the step a long moment, the letter light as a moth in her palm, while the harbor bells counted out the hour behind her. Down the street, the baker was arguing with his apprentice about lye. A gull screamed over the fishmongers' stalls. The world went on being ordinary, and Quentin Dravenmoor let it, because ordinary was the armor she wore, and she had worn it so long she sometimes forgot there was anything underneath.

Inside, she bolted the door. Then, because the house held another pair of ears these days, she carried the letter up to her room—the small one under the eaves where the roof leaked in one corner and the light came slantwise through a window too narrow to climb through. She checked the floorboards for creaks out of habit, though Sebastian had left before dawn. He always left before dawn. Inquisitors kept inquisitor's hours.

She broke the seal that wasn't a seal and unfolded three pages of cramped, failing script.

*Little Q.*

Only one person had ever called her that. Maerin Solde, last master of the Oathbound, the woman who had taught her to hold silence like a blade held flat against the tongue. Dead two years now—or so Quentin had believed. The letters were dated across eighteen months. Someone had gathered them. Someone had walked them north, city by city, keeping them safe until they could reach her. The courier's face surfaced in her memory: salt-cracked hands, eyes that had flicked once to her face and away again. One of Maerin's, then. Or one of what remained of them.

*If you are reading this, I am ash, or near enough. Do not grieve long; grief is loud, and you were always my loudest student in the ways that mattered least.*

Quentin's throat tightened. She sat down on the narrow bed, the mattress complaining beneath her, and made herself read.

*They have found it, Little Q. The last flame. Under the citadel at Carrowmoor—no. They have moved it. I traced the wagons myself before my lungs gave out: iron-banded, warded cold, going south along the coast road toward Vellmark. Toward you, though they do not know it is toward you. They know only that their ritual wants an oath-bound voice, and oath-bound voices are scarce as honest tax collectors now. You are the last. You must believe me when I say they will find you, whether you hide or run.*

*Here is what I learned in the dying of my days. The crown does not hunt the gifted out of fear. Fear is the mask. They hunt us because our oaths are the lock, and dragon-fire is the key. Speak an oath into flame and it does not bend reality—it binds it. Permanently. Every mind within the working's reach, bent to the speaker's will. And if the speaker is the crown—*

*You see it. Of course you see it. You always saw the shape of a thing faster than anyone I taught.*

*A ritual of bound obedience. One voice above every throat in the realm. No more rebellion, no more dissent, no more thought that the throne has not approved. They call it peace, Little Q. They will make everyone call it peace.*

The pages trembled. Not from any wind; the room was still. Quentin realized the trembling was her own hands, and pressed them flat against her knees, and breathed the way Maerin had taught her—in through the nose, four counts, hold, out—until the shaking stilled.

*I could not stop it. I am sorry. An old woman's fire is not what it was, and my oaths were spent long ago keeping fools like you alive. But you—you have your gift whole, buried deep where even I could not see it. That is why I never called you home. That is why I let you think me dead. A hidden blade is worth ten drawn ones.*

*Now hear the hard part. Your vow of silence protects you, yes—but it also makes you exactly what they need. A swordspeaker who cannot speak is a swordspeaker they can use. The ritual requires an oath spoken willingly, or coerced past willingness—it matters little to the flame which. If they take you, they do not need your consent. They need only your voice, and pain is very persuasive.*

*So you have a choice, and I will not insult you by pretending it is a kind one. Run far enough and fast enough, perhaps you die free in some border town, nameless, and the flame burns without you until they find another way. Or stay, and stand, and spend yourself as we all swore we would—the oath we took was never silence, child. Silence was only the sheath.*

*The oath was this: that power answers to conscience, or it serves cruelty. Remember what the words were for.*

*Burn this after reading. Trust no badge. And Little Q—if a man in a grey coat ever sleeps under your roof, count the knives he carries and pray they stay pointed outward.*

The signature was a single down-stroke of the pen, the way Maerin signed everything, as if time itself had grown too precious for flourish.

Quentin read the letter twice more. Then she did not burn it. She folded it small and hid it inside the hollow heel of her left boot, because burning felt like killing him twice, and she was not yet ready to be that obedient—even to the dead.

* * *

She went through the day the way a drowning woman goes through the motion of swimming: technically correct, entirely hollow.

She sold a fever-charm to the cooper's wife—harmless, warm, a whisper of comfort stitched into copper wire, nothing the pyres would notice. She haggled with a wool merchant over dyed thread and won, as always, by yielding twice and standing firm once. She smiled at Mrs. Aldery from the chandlery and listened to a full account of the Aldery cat's digestion. All the while, the letter sat against her ribs like a swallowed coal.

*Every mind in the realm.* She turned the phrase over and over, looking for the flaw in it, the exaggeration, the dying woman's drama. Maerin Solde had never been dramatic in her life. She had once described a plague as "an inconvenience," and a war as "a disagreement poorly managed." If she wrote *every mind*, then she meant every mind. The baker arguing about lye. Mrs. Aldery and her cat. The dockworker whose luck-charm Quentin had mended last month, saving him from a rope's end and a cold sea. All of them, bent like wheat. All of them, smiling, obedient, empty—and calling it peace.

And the wagons were coming south. Toward Vellmark. Toward her.

By late afternoon she had built the case for running so carefully, so logically, that it almost convinced her. She had money hidden—three years of careful savings sewn into a quilt. She had a face forgettable enough to travel under. She had done it before; she had been running since she was nineteen years old, since the night the order burned and she had walked out of the smoke wearing a dead girl's name. She knew the roads. She knew how to be no one.

It was the work of an hour to pack. It took less than that to unpack, and she could not afterward have said why, except that her hands stopped halfway through folding her spare shift and simply refused.

Because—she admitted it finally, sitting on the floor of her room amid the neat, cowardly stacks—because of the man downstairs.

Not sentiment. She was careful to tell herself it was not sentiment. But Sebastian Corvane had slept under her roof for three weeks, and in three weeks he had arrested no one, reported nothing she could detect, and looked at the world with eyes that grew more tired and more questioning each day. He was a leash, Maerin would have said. Trust no badge. But leashes were attached to collars, and collars were attached to necks, and necks could turn. She had watched his turn beginning, slow as a season, and she hated herself for hoping it would continue.

If she ran, they would follow the trail she'd leave whether she willed it or not—straight through Vellmark, straight past the charm-shop, straight to whatever remained in this city that the crown wanted. And if the ritual needed her voice, then running did not save the realm. Running only decided *where* they caught her, and how many people stood nearby when the screaming started.

Terror sat in her chest like a stone dropped in still water, and the ripples spread outward from it: fear of pain, fear of fire, fear of being used—her voice pulled out of her like thread from a spool, her oath turned into a chain for every mind she had ever tried to protect. She was afraid the way she had been afraid at nineteen, and that frightened her most of all, because at nineteen she had still believed someone older and braver would come.

No one was coming. Maerin was ash. The order was smoke. The list of people willing to stand between the realm and the throne had, apparently, one name left on it, and the name was hers.

*Silence was only the sheath.*

She laughed—one short, ugly sound with no humor in it—and put her head back against the wall. Eleven years she had told herself the vow was virtue. Caution, discipline, wisdom. All those comfortable words for the same simple truth: she had been afraid, and she had called the fear an oath.

But the oath—the real one, the one spoken at nineteen with Maerin's hand heavy on her shoulder—had never said *hide*. It had said *power answers to conscience*. And her conscience, it turned out, was a stubborn, inconvenient thing that had waited eleven years in the dark and now stood up and refused to sit back down.

Evening came. She heard Sebastian's boots on the stair, his key in the lock, the particular care he took to close the front door quietly—as though quietness were a courtesy he owed the world and the world had never thanked him for it. She listened to him move about below, lighting the lamp, setting his boots by the mat, and she thought: *he counts his knives. I've seen him do it. Three, always three, and he checks them like a man checking whether his faith is still sharp.*

Downstairs, something shifted. A chair scraped. And then, through the floorboards, low and almost lost, she heard him speaking—not to her, not to himself, but in the clipped cadence of report. Words rose up between the boards like bubbles through dark water: *"…no confirmation… subject remains unremarkable… recommend continued observation…"*

A pause. Longer. Then, quieter, in a different voice entirely, the voice of a tired man rather than an instrument:

*"Understood. Until the wagons arrive."*

The wagons. Here, in his mouth. Coming here, confirmed in his mouth, and he had known—had perhaps known since before he ever knocked on her door and asked, so politely, for a room.

Quentin sat very still in the gathering dark, her heart beating slow and enormous, like a bell tolling underwater. Above the fear, above the fury, something else was rising in her now—small and bright and terrible, a coal refusing the ash. Not courage, not yet. Closer to anger's first cousin, the one that thinks clearly.

They were coming for her. Very well.

Then they would find that the last swordspeaker had not finished speaking.

She rose, took up the lamp, and went down the stairs to learn what her lodger knew—and what he was willing to tell.
