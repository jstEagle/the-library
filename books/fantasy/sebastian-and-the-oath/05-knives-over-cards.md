## 5. Knives Over Cards

The night market of Vellmark woke only after the sun had given up on the city, when the lantern-sellers came out like moths in reverse, lighting their wares one by one until Saltrow Lane glowed like a vein of amber beneath the smoke-stained sky. Quentin moved through it as she always did—hood up, hands empty, eyes down. A woman buying nothing. A woman who was no one.

She had come for salt and lamp oil and to hear what the harbor was saying. The harbor said plenty, if you knew how to listen. Tonight it said that the inquisitor's men had been seen measuring doorways. That a family on Tanner's Row had been taken in their sleep. That the pyres at the garrison were being fed twice a day now, and the wind carried the smell of them all the way to the fish stalls, where no one mentioned it and everyone breathed through their mouths.

Quentin bought her oil from a woman with flour-white knuckles and did not let her fingers linger on the coins. Ten years of practice. Ten years of keeping her gift folded up small behind her teeth, a sword wrapped in wool and buried so deep even she sometimes forgot its weight.

Then she heard the child laugh, and everything changed.

It came from the mouth of Cooper's Alley—a bright, careless sound, obscene in its innocence—and Quentin turned despite herself. A girl of perhaps six stood at the edge of the lantern-light, chasing a paper charm someone had sold her, a cheap thing with a painted sun on it that was supposed to bring good dreams. The girl's mother was haggling three stalls away. And coming down the alley, boots striking cobbles in the rhythm Quentin had learned to dread, were four men in the grey-and-crimson of the crown.

Not Sebastian. She knew his tread now; she hated that she knew it. These were his subordinates, and they were not here for oil or salt.

"Stop there, little one." The nearest soldier smiled the way men smile when they want to be seen as gentle. "That charm you've got. Who gave it to you?"

The girl held the paper sun against her chest. "Mama."

"Mama, is it." He crouched, and his hand went to the girl's wrist—not cruelly, but with the terrible casualness of a man handling evidence. "We'll just have a look at your mama, then."

Quentin's body made the decision before her mind could object. Later she would try to reconstruct those three seconds and fail. She remembered the lantern-light bending. She remembered the word rising in her throat—not spoken, never spoken, but *shaped*, given breath without voice, an oath whispered into the seam between moments: *forget this street.*

The world hiccuped.

It was a small working, smaller than any she'd attempted since Vellmark, and it cost her like a knife between the ribs. The soldiers' heads turned as one, their attention sliding off the girl like rain off oiled leather, snagging instead on a drunk two stalls down who had chosen that moment to sing something bawdy about a duchess. They laughed. They forgot. The girl's mother swept her up without ever knowing her daughter had stood one heartbeat from a pyre.

And Sebastian Corvane, standing at the alley's far end with a warrant in his pocket and the smell of ash still on his coat from the day's burnings, watched the whole thing happen.

He didn't move. He didn't call out. His face did something complicated and then did nothing at all, which was worse, and Quentin understood with cold clarity that she had just handed him the knife he'd been sent to find.

* * *

Sebastian had known, of course. He had known for weeks, in the way one knows a house is on fire before admitting it—the smell of it in every room, the heat behind every door. The way she never touched anyone. The way charms turned up in her shop that were too good, too clean, too *kind* for a city this frightened. The way she watched soldiers the way other people watched weather.

Knowing was survivable. Seeing was another matter.

He stood in the crowd's edge and felt the moment split him cleanly in two. On one side: ten years of service, a debt the crown held over his family like a thumb on a scale, the faces of his superiors, the neat arithmetic of duty. On the other side: a paper sun clutched in a small fist, and a woman whose magic had been, by every measure he could see, nothing but mercy.

*Forget this street.* He hadn't heard the oath—he'd felt it, a pressure behind his ears, a wrongness in the light—and he understood that she could have done anything in that moment. Could have bent the soldiers' minds like wire. Instead she'd spent her secret to make four armed men look elsewhere.

"Corvane." Sergeant Hallow, red-faced from the ale stall, clapped his shoulder. "You see that? Woman back there selling luck-charms to children. We ought to have a word."

"A word," Sebastian agreed, and heard his own voice come out level, bored, official. "But not tonight. The edict targets practitioners, not mothers buying trinkets. You want to drag a six-year-old in front of the magistrate and explain why we burned her mother over a painted sun?" He let the pause stretch. "The Lord Inquisitor General wants results, Hallow. Not noise."

Hallow grunted, unconvinced but unwilling to argue rank, and wandered off toward the ale.

Sebastian remained. Across the market, Quentin had gone very still among the lanterns, a hare deciding whether stillness might save it. Their eyes met across forty feet of smoke and lamplight, and neither of them looked away, because looking away would be an admission, and they had both just agreed, silently, to admit nothing.

He walked toward her anyway. God help him, he walked toward her.

* * *

They did not speak of it on the walk home. That was the first rule of the new country they'd entered without either signing its borders: they walked side by side through streets that smelled of tar and woodsmoke, and they talked about the price of coal, and the leak in the kitchen roof, and whether Mrs. Aldery's cat had gotten into the flour again, and every ordinary word was a brick laid carefully over a grave.

Only when the shop door closed behind them, when the bolt slid home and the candle was lit and the world reduced itself to one room, did Quentin finally turn to him.

"You saw."

"I saw."

"I could say it was nothing. A trick of the light."

"You could," Sebastian said. He set his hat on the peg with exaggerated care, as if his hands needed employment. "I could say I didn't understand what I saw. We could both say a great many things." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw how tired he was—not the tiredness of a long day but of a long war conducted entirely inside himself. "I won't ask you to explain. I'm asking you to tell me one thing, and I'll take your answer on faith. Did you hurt them? Any of them? Ever?"

"No." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "My order bound us to protection. Only protection. An oath spoken to harm is an oath that eats the speaker. We were swordspeakers, Sebastian. Do you know what that means? It means our words were weapons, and so we swore never to draw them." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "Ten years I've kept my mouth shut around you. Around everyone. Do you know what it costs to hold a word in your teeth for a decade?"

"No," he said quietly. "But I know what it costs to hold silence of another kind. My family's name, my commission, my sister's marriage portion—all of it sits in a ledger in the Lord Inquisitor General's office. I do what I'm told, and the debt stays paid. I stopped asking what I was told *for* some years ago." He sat down heavily at the kitchen table, and for the first time since he'd rented her spare room, he looked less like a crown inquisitor than like a man. "Tonight I asked again."

"And?"

"And I don't like the answer." He rubbed his eyes. "The apothecary fire last month. The arrests on Tanner's Row. They're not hunting the gifted, Quentin—they're *collecting*. There are lists. Sigils copied from burned workshops and sent south in sealed cases. Whatever the crown is building, it needs more than prisoners." He looked up at her. "It needs something it doesn't have yet. And I think—I think whatever it is, it has to do with people like you."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to shield it.

"Why are you telling me this?" Quentin whispered. "You could walk out that door and report everything. What you saw tonight. What I am. You'd be decorated."

"Yes," Sebastian said simply. "I could."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. If I told you I'd already decided, I'd be lying, and you'd know it, and we'd have built this whole—" he gestured vaguely at the room, at the strange domestic shape their lives had taken, "—on sand. I don't know what I am anymore. But I know I didn't stop Hallow tonight for the crown. And I know you didn't spend ten years of silence to save one child for strategy." He leaned forward. "So here is where we stand, you and I. Each of us holding a knife. Each of us choosing, for one more night, not to use it."

Quentin sat down across from him. The table between them was small enough that their hands nearly touched, and neither moved closer, and neither moved away.

"My mentor used to say," she offered at last, "that trust isn't the absence of betrayal. It's just betrayal that hasn't found its price yet."

"He sounds like he'd have liked me," Sebastian said dryly.

"He'd have arrested you on sight."

"There it is. Common ground at last."

Despite everything—a raid, a secret spent, a future cracking open like river ice—she laughed. It startled her, the sound of it, rusty and real. And she saw it land on him too, that flicker of warmth crossing his guarded face before discipline smoothed it away.

They played cards after that. Neither suggested it; the deck was simply there, as it had been most nights, and the ritual of it was its own language. Quentin lost the first hand badly and suspected him of cheating and suspected herself of not caring. Somewhere past midnight, with the candle burning low and rain starting against the shutters, she caught him watching her across the cards with an expression she couldn't name—something between apology and warning.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing." He gathered the cards, shuffled, dealt. "Just thinking that I've raided forty houses looking for what was sleeping down the hall from me the whole time."

"And now that you've found it?"

His jaw worked. The rain thickened. When he answered, he didn't look at her, and that was how she knew he meant it.

"Now I'm wondering what happens," he said, "when the crown comes for what it's been collecting. Because Quentin—it's going to come. Soon. And I've seen the orders. Whatever they're building in the citadel undercroft, they need an oath-bound voice to finish it." He set down the deck. "There are maybe three such voices left in the world. And I'm beginning to fear the reason you survived when your order died is that someone wanted you alive."

The rain filled the silence. Outside, far off toward the citadel, a bell tolled once—the hour, or perhaps a warning—and in the space between its notes, Quentin felt ten years of hiding collapse around her like a house whose walls had finally been measured.
