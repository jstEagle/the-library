## 7. Where Loyalties Are Weighed

The citadel of Vellmark had been built by masons who believed in fear as an architectural principle. Its walls rose sheer and black from the cliff above the harbor, its windows narrow as knife-slits, its gates crowned with iron spikes that had never once been decorative. Quentin had spent ten years training her eyes to slide past it, the way one slides past a wound. Tonight there was no sliding past anything.

She crouched beside Sebastian in the shadow of a drainage arch at the citadel's base, where the tide breathed against old stone and the smell was salt and rot and something else beneath—something faintly sweet, like honey left too long over coals. She knew that smell. Every swordspeaker knew it.

*Dragon-fire.*

Her stomach turned over. "It's real," she whispered. "I kept telling myself the message was a dying man's fever dream."

Sebastian didn't answer at once. He had his warrant-case open on his knee, the royal seal catching what little light leaked through the arch, and he was studying the guard rotation he'd memorized three nights ago with the flat attention he gave everything—cards, knives, her lies over dinner. But when he spoke, his voice had lost its inquisitor's polish.

"The undercroft entrance is through the old well-house. Two guards until midnight, then one changeover, four minutes unwatched." He paused. "Quentin. If this goes wrong, you run. Not argue. Run."

"If this goes wrong," she said, "we'll both be ash before I finish arguing."

Something almost like a smile crossed his face and died. Then he closed the case, tucked the warrant away—the warrant he should have used on her weeks ago—and nodded toward the well-house.

They moved together through the dark like two people who had been doing it for years, which frightened her more than the guards did. When had she started trusting the rhythm of him? When had trust become another thing she couldn't afford?

The well-house door gave to Sebastian's key—his rank still opened doors here, though how much longer it would, neither of them said. Inside, the descent began. A spiral stair worn hollow by centuries of feet, lit every dozen steps by a lantern burning a flame that was *wrong*: too steady, too gold, humming faintly, like a struck bell no one could hear.

Quentin stopped at the third landing, cold flooding through her despite the warmth rising from below.

"Those aren't oil lamps."

"No."

"They're burning *her*. Whatever's down there—they're feeding it into the lights of Vellmark." Her voice came out thin. "We've been walking under it all these years. The whole city's been lit by a dying dragon and nobody knew."

"Nobody wanted to know," Sebastian said quietly. "That's not the same thing, but it isn't better."

* * *

The undercroft opened around them like the inside of a throat.

It was vast—a cavernous vault older than the citadel above it, pillars of raw rock holding up a ceiling lost in darkness. And at its center, caged in a lattice of black iron etched so densely with sigils that the metal seemed more writing than substance, burned the last living flame of the dragons.

It was small. That was the horror of it. Quentin had grown up on stories of fire that filled valleys, wings that blotted out moons, and what lay before her was a knot of light scarcely larger than a hearth-blaze, guttering, dimming, flaring weakly as if calling for help in a language only the dead remembered. The heat it threw off was immense and desperate, pressing against her skin like a hand begging to be held.

Around the cage, in concentric rings on the floor, ran channels of carved stone, dry now but built to carry something. Quentin looked at them and understood without wanting to: blood, or oath-light, or both, drawn outward from the fire to a central dais where a single chair waited—iron, high-backed, shaped like a throne's poor relation.

"An altar," she breathed. "They built an altar around a living creature."

"They've been starving it slowly," Sebastian said. His voice had gone rough. He stood closer to the cage than she did, close enough that the failing light painted his face gold and terrible. "Look at the sigils. They're not containment work. Containment keeps power *in*. This is a tap. They've been drawing it out drop by drop—for what? Lanterns?"

"For practice," said a voice from the dark between two pillars. "For patience. For the great day they've been promised is coming."

* * *

He stepped into the firelight slowly, as if his body had forgotten how bodies worked, each movement an argument with itself. He was gaunt. His hair had gone white at thirty-some years of age, or been made white, and his robes were keeper's grey, clean, official—but his eyes were the worst. Quentin had seen drowned men look less empty.

Except. Except that under the emptiness, something flickered when he saw her face. Something like recognition. Something like grief.

"You're supposed to be dead," Quentin whispered. "Maerin Vosk. You taught me my first binding. You held my hands over the oath-candle and told me—" Her throat closed. "They burned you at Ashford. I *heard* it. Everyone heard it."

"I heard it too." Maerin Vosk smiled, and the smile was the cruelest thing in the room. "From inside the pyre that wasn't. They needed the order to believe we could be killed. So they let the realm believe it. All of us. One by one, the swordspeakers died beautifully in public while the ones worth keeping were carried down here instead." He gestured at the cage with a hand that trembled. "I was worth keeping. Aren't you proud?"

"Maerin—"

"*Don't.*" The word cracked out of him with such force that the guttering flame surged against its cage, and for one heartbeat the whole undercroft blazed gold, shadows leaping, and Quentin saw the truth written in that flare: he was bound, threaded through with obedience-sigils finer than any she'd ever woven, a puppet whose strings had grown into his flesh. "Don't call me teacher. Teachers don't do what I've done. Ten years, Quentin. Ten years I have fed this creature its own death one measured spoonful at a time, because if I refused, they would simply bring someone who would waste it faster. That is the arithmetic they leave you. That is how they weigh your loyalty—never against your honor. Against your *usefulness*."

Sebastian's hand had gone to his belt, to nothing; he'd left his blade topside. His voice was very controlled. "The ritual. When does it happen. What does it need."

Maerin laughed, and it hurt to hear, because underneath the brokenness it was still her teacher's laugh, dry and exact. "The crown inquisitor asks. How the mighty curriculum has changed." He turned, dragging his eyes back to Quentin, and what lived in them now was worse than emptiness. It was pity.

"It needs three things, girl. It needs the fire—I have kept it alive for them, barely, because a dead flame fuels nothing. It needs the channels bled full on the night of the conjunction, four days hence. And it needs a voice." He raised one finger, pointing at her chest, at the place behind her sternum where her binding sat like a swallowed stone. "An oath-bound voice. A true swordspeaker, bound in silence, standing at that dais, speaking the words the crown writes for her. Her gift becomes the loom. Her oath becomes the thread. Every mind in the realm, bound in obedience, forever—and the voice that wove it silenced into the bargain. Ash. That's the price they pay. That's the price *you* pay."

The cavern was very quiet except for the fire's failing breath.

"That's impossible," Quentin said. "There are no other swordspeakers. If I'm the last—"

"You are the last," Maerin agreed softly. "Which is why you're standing here. Which is why you were never found. Think, child. Ten years in a city crawling with inquisitors, selling charms out of a shop with your name on the lease. Ten years of luck that no honest woman deserves. Did you truly believe you were *hiding*?"

The floor tilted. Or she did. She felt Sebastian step toward her and could not feel his hand when it reached her arm.

"Ashford burned eleven of us," she whispered. "I watched the lists. My name wasn't on them because I'd already fled. Because I was careful. Because I—"

"Because the woman who drew up those lists was paid to leave one name off," Maerin said. "Because the crown has known where you were since the week you arrived. Because every charm you sold, every coin you saved, every friend you made—you built it all inside a snare they baited the day you were born to the gift. They didn't hunt the swordspeakers, Quentin. They harvested us. And you—" his voice dropped to almost tenderness, which was unbearable— "you are the harvest festival. They let you live the way a farmer lets the best ewe live. Everything you called survival was husbandry."

*No.* The word screamed through her and did not reach her mouth. Ten years. The fear, the loneliness, the exhausting nightly theater of being nobody—all of it reorganized itself in her memory, every narrow escape becoming a leash slipped deliberately loose, every quiet year becoming a fattening. Even the edict, even the hunt—even *him*, maybe, the thought arrived like ice water—

She looked at Sebastian. He had gone pale in the firelight, and she saw him arrive at the same thought at the same moment, saw it land in his eyes: *the room, the rent, the friendship—was even that assigned?*

"No," he said aloud, to her, not to Maerin, fierce and low. "Whatever this is—whatever they planned with me—that part was mine. Finding you was mine. This—" his hand tightened on her arm— "is mine. Don't let them have that too."

"Sentimental," Maerin said. "Good. You'll need it." He straightened as much as his ruined body allowed, and the obedience-sigils along his throat glowed faintly, and Quentin realized with a lurch that he was fighting them just to speak this freely, spending something irreplaceable with every sentence. "Listen, because I don't know how many more words they'll let me buy. Four days. The conjunction. They will come for you themselves if you don't walk in willingly—they have never needed you to be willing, only present. And hear the rest: a bound voice can weave the binding, yes. But an oath is a two-edged instrument, always. It was the first thing I taught you. *What binds can also cut.*"

His eyes held hers, and in them, deep beneath the ruin, her teacher looked out at her one last time—not pity now, but something urgent, something almost hopeful.

"There is a way to break the ritual from inside the dais. There always was. They know it. It's why they chose a bound speaker rather than a free one—it's why they *bound* the order's voices in the first place, all those centuries ago. But the way costs everything the speaker is. Understand me. Not her life. Her *self*." His voice frayed. "I would tell you not to do it. I am not permitted to tell you not to do it. Isn't that funny. After ten years, that is the joke they've left me."

Above them, faint through leagues of stone, a bell began to toll the midnight change. Maerin's head snapped toward the stair, and his expression smoothed instantly into keeper's blankness, the puppet settling back onto its strings.

"Go," he mouthed. "*Four days.*"

And then, as they backed toward the dark of the pillars, he spoke once more in his ordinary ruined voice, pitched to carry, the words of a faithful servant reporting to unseen ears:

"She came. She saw the flame. She wept, and asked nothing, and went away again. As predicted."

Quentin walked up the spiral stair with Sebastian's hand steady between her shoulder blades, and the wrong gold light hummed at their backs, and she counted the steps and did not weep, because some part of her had understood, finally and completely, that even her tears had been budgeted for.
