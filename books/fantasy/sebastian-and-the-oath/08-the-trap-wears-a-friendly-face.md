## 8. The Trap Wears a Friendly Face

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a courier's satchel that smelled of tallow and iron. Sebastian knew the seal before he broke it—the crowned eye of the Inquisition, pressed deep enough into black wax to leave a bruise in the paper. He had carried a hundred such letters in his years of service. He had never once dreaded opening one.

He opened it on the landing outside his rented room, because some part of him, even now, did not want Quentin to see his hands shake.

*Come alone. Come tonight. The citadel remembers its debts.*

No signature. None needed. Only one person in Vellmark wrote to him like a creditor.

* * *

Handler Vess received him in a room without windows, high in the citadel's eastern spire, where the stones drank the last light and gave nothing back. She was small, gray, and neat as a ledger, seated behind a table bare except for a single candle and a shallow dish of water in which something silver turned slowly, though there was no wind.

"Corvane." She said his name the way a surgeon says the location of a tumor. "Sit."

He sat. The chair was cold through his coat.

"You've been busy," she said. "The apothecary fire. The night market. The girl who sells luck-charms and lives above you." She let each item fall like a coin onto stone. "You filed no reports. You burned your field notes—I watched the ash myself, from the street. Very thorough. Very sentimental."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That has always been your tragedy, Sebastian. You believe what you're told right up until the moment believing becomes inconvenient." She turned the dish a quarter-circle with one finger. The silver thing beneath the water shuddered. "Do you know why you were sent to Vellmark?"

"To hunt the gifted. Under the edict—"

"You were sent," Vess said, "because we already knew about her."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"Quentin Dravenmoor," she continued, tasting the name. "Last swordspeaker. We have known for six years. Do you understand what that means? Every charm she sold, every whisper of power she smothered—we catalogued it. We let her live because a bound voice is worthless until it chooses to speak. And a woman hiding for ten years does not choose to speak for a stranger's coin or a stranger's threat." Her eyes lifted, mild and terrible. "She speaks for someone she trusts."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly beneath Sebastian's chair.

"So we sent you. Not to find her. To *become* found by her. Your discretion, your loneliness, your tired eyes—you were chosen for all of it. The debt your family owes made you pliable; your reputation made you credible. You were never the hunter, Corvane. You were the leash."

Sebastian heard his own breathing, slow and wrong, as if it belonged to someone else. Ten days of fencing politeness over shared bread. The way she'd laughed once, badly, at his joke about the harbor gulls—and how he'd thought, absurdly, *she is lonely the way I am lonely.* All of it weighed, measured, designed. He had been an instrument fitted to a lock.

"The ritual requires an oath-bound voice," Vess said. "Yours to deliver. Tomorrow night, the undercroft. Bring her willing if you can. Willing voices bind deeper." She slid a folded warrant across the table. "And Corvane—your sister's family remains in Crownhaven. They are comfortable. They will remain comfortable exactly as long as you remain useful."

There it was. The blade he had known was coming, held not to his throat but to someone else's.

He looked at the warrant. His own name in the crown's hand. Below it, a space for a second name, left deliberately blank.

"And if I refuse?"

Vess smiled with genuine warmth, which was the worst thing in the room. "Then we take her anyway, and your sister's children learn what their uncle's pride cost them. There is no version of tomorrow in which you are innocent, Sebastian. There is only the version where you are useful." She rose. "You were always going to break. We simply decided which way."

* * *

He walked home through streets gone blue with evening, past shuttered stalls and gutters running with the day's meltwater, and the city had never looked so much like a thing already lost. A lamplighter worked his way down Cordwainer Row, and Sebastian stopped to watch him—a man doing honest work with fire, unafraid of it, and Sebastian envied him with a violence that frightened him.

In his room, Quentin had left bread on the common table, wrapped in cloth, with a note in her careful, disguised hand: *Market had honey loaves. You eat like a man apologizing to food.* Small. Human. The kind of kindness that could not be performed on command, which meant everything they had built between them was real even if its beginning was a lie—and somehow that was worse.

He sat with the warrant a long time.

He thought of his father's face the day the scandal broke, and the crown's cold mercy afterward: *we will forget, if you serve.* Twenty years of service bought from one moment of fear. He thought of his sister's children, warm in Crownhaven, and hated the crown for making them hostages, and hated himself more for the traitor arithmetic already running in his head—*two children against one realm*—as if souls could be counted like coins.

But that was the old arithmetic. The crown's arithmetic. And he had seen the dragon-fire caged in the dark, dying and immense, and he had seen the keeper's ruined throat, and he understood at last what Vess's masters wanted: not order. Obedience. Every mind in the realm bent gently toward the throne, forever, and no one ever unhappy enough to know it.

His family would be safe inside that obedience. Safe as furniture.

Sebastian struck a match.

The warrant caught quickly, eager, the crowned eye blistering and curling into black lace. He held it until the flame kissed his fingers, then dropped the last corner into the dish where Quentin sometimes soaked her paintbrushes, and watched the ink dissolve into gray ghosts.

"There," he said quietly, to the room, to the crown, to the boy he had been. "There is my answer."

* * *

He knocked on her door past midnight, and when she opened it—hair loose, a knife already palmed, because of course she'd heard him hesitate on the stairs—he said, before courage could fail:

"They sent me for you. From the beginning. I was the trap, Quentin. The friendship was the bait, and I didn't know, and knowing doesn't clean it." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "Tomorrow night they'll come whether I deliver you or not. Your mentor's warning was true. The ritual needs your voice, and they've spent six years arranging the world so you'd walk into it holding someone's hand."

She stared at him. He watched ten years of vigilance decide, in real time, whether to kill him or believe him.

"They have my sister," he went on. "They'll use her regardless. I've burned my warrant. As of tonight I'm nothing—no office, no protection, no name worth having. I am asking you to run, and I am telling you they will not let us run, and I am—" he exhaled, ragged, "—I am done being their instrument. Whatever you decide, I stand where you stand."

The knife lowered, slowly, though her knuckles stayed white around it.

"You could still lie well enough to save yourself," she said.

"Yes. But then I'd have to live in the world they're building, and I've seen its foundations." He met her eyes. "I'd rather burn in this one."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the citadel's bells began to toll—not the hour, not an alarm, but the deep ceremonial rhythm he knew from investitures and executions alike. Tomorrow had arrived early. Torches were moving along the ramparts like a lit fuse burning toward them both.

Quentin looked past him at the glow, and when she spoke, her voice was steady in the way of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried too long.

"Then we don't run," she said. "We go to the undercroft. Tonight. If they want an oath-bound voice—" something old and dangerous woke in her face, something he had only glimpsed in the night market, "—they should have been more careful what they wished for."

The bells tolled again, closer now, and Sebastian understood there was no road back to the man he had been. He picked up his sword, and followed her down into the dark.
