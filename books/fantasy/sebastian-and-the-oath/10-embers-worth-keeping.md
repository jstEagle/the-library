## 10. Embers Worth Keeping

The first frost of the season came on a Tuesday, and Quentin woke to find it had written its name across her window in silver script—ferns and feathers and long looping rivers of ice that would be gone by noon. She lay still beneath the wool blanket and watched the light come through it, fractured into small rainbows that crawled across the floorboards as the sun climbed.

Once, she could have spoken a word and kept the frost forever. Once, she could have sworn it into permanence, bound it with an oath until the world itself forgot how to melt it.

Now she simply got up, wrapped her shawl around her shoulders, and let the frost do what frost does.

It was strange, how little she missed it. Stranger still, how much.

* * *

The shop opened at the turn of the season—not the charm seller's stall she'd kept for ten years in the shadow of the fish market, but a real shop, with a real door that rang a real bell, on Tallow Street where the lamplighters passed twice a night. She'd painted the sign herself: **QUENTIN'S GOODS — CANDLES, INK, SMALL MERCIES**. The last part had been Sebastian's suggestion, offered deadpan over supper, and she'd laughed hard enough to spill her wine before realizing he meant it.

Small mercies sold better than anything else on the shelf. A tin of salve for chapped hands. A packet of willow bark for fever. Little folded papers bearing nothing but kind words, written in her own plain hand, for people who needed to hear them and had no one to say them. No charms. No luck woven into the stitching, no blessings whispered over the wax. Just things, honestly made, honestly priced.

Her customers came anyway. That was the miracle she was still learning to hold without flinching.

They came because word had traveled, the way word does, that the woman on Tallow Street was the one who had stood in the citadel square with fire raining from the sky and told the truth so loudly it broke a ritual older than the crown. They came because the edicts had softened—or rather, because the men who wrote them had learned what burned at the heart of their own machine, and found they no longer had the stomach for it. The pyres were cold now. The inquisitors' black coats were fewer each month, and those who remained walked softer, watched more than they seized.

Magic was still feared. Quentin understood that fear would outlive her; fear always did. But it was feared the way storms are feared now, not the way plagues are. Something you respect. Something you might even stand outside to watch pass.

And in the alleys—the small lights had begun again.

She saw them on her walk home from the market, when she took the long way through Cooper's Row just to look. A candle in a window that guttered blue when someone lied nearby. A child's chalk sigil on a doorstep, drawn wrong but drawn with love, that made the milk sour a half-day slower. An old man on a stoop whose breath fogged into shapes on cold mornings—a bird, once, unmistakably a bird—and his neighbors pretended not to see, which in Vellmark was as good as a blessing.

No one reported them. That was new. That was everything.

* * *

Sebastian swept her step every morning.

He had done it since the second week after the trial—or whatever the crown's embarrassed, hurried proceedings deserved to be called, the ones where his name had been struck from the rolls and his warrant burned twice, once by his own hand and once officially, for the record. He had done it the morning they'd reopened the shop, and every morning since, and when Quentin had finally asked him why, he'd leaned the broom against the doorframe and considered the question with the same grave attention he'd once given evidence.

"Because I'm good at it," he'd said. "Because it's honest work. And because—" and here he'd paused, and she'd watched him decide, deliberately, to say the true thing instead of the deflection, a habit he was still building the way one builds any muscle, "—because I spent eleven years learning every way there is to lie to a room full of people, and I find I like starting my days with something that can't be faked. Dirt is dirt. You sweep it or you don't."

So he swept. Every morning, while the frost still held and the gulls argued over the fish market's leavings, the disgraced crown inquisitor Sebastian Corvane stood on the step of Quentin's Goods with a birch-twig broom and cleared away the night's grit, and the neighborhood had long since stopped finding it remarkable. Mrs. Aldery from the dyer's brought him coffee. The lamplighter's boy asked him questions about the citadel and he answered them plainly, all except the ones that hurt, and even some of those.

His family wrote to him now. That had taken longer. His sister's first letter had been four lines and cold as a summons; the latest one ran six pages and ended with a sketch of his niece, missing two teeth, holding a sign that said COME FOR WINTERFAIR. He kept it pinned above the shop's back-room desk, where he'd taken to doing the accounts, because Quentin was hopeless with numbers and he refused to let her pretend otherwise.

"You balance a ledger the way you used to balance a blade," she'd told him once.

"I never balanced a blade," he'd said. "I swung it and hoped. At least the ledger tells me when I'm wrong."

* * *

On the evening the frost came, they closed early. There was no reason to stay open; the cold had emptied Tallow Street by mid-afternoon, and Quentin had spent the last hour doing nothing but watching the light move through the jars of ink and candle-grease, gilding everything it touched.

Sebastian built the fire in the back room while she cut bread and sharp cheese and the last of the apple preserves from the autumn fair. They ate at the scarred table by the window, and neither of them spoke for a while, because they had long ago stopped needing to fill the quiet. It had taken months. In the beginning, silence between them had been a thing with edges—full of everything unsaid, the confession neither spoke, the raid on the night market, the moment he'd stepped between her and his brothers-in-arms and known, absolutely, that there was no road back. Silence then had been a debt.

Now it was just silence. Warm as bread.

"The lights are spreading," Sebastian said at last, turning his cup slowly on the table. "Harrowgate sent word. And down the coast, in Saltmere. Small things. A hedge-witch prescribing nettle tea and calling it what it is. A ferryman whose boat never capsizes." He glanced up. "People are less afraid of each other than they were."

"People are always less afraid of each other than their rulers need them to be," Quentin said. "It takes the rulers a while to notice."

"They'll notice."

"Some of them already have." She said it lightly, but the weight of it settled into the room all the same, familiar as an old coat. There would always be another edict waiting somewhere in a drawer. Another hand reaching for fire because fire was easier than understanding. She had broken one oath and one empire's favorite toy, and the world had not been remade—it had only been tilted, a degree or two, toward mercy. Degrees mattered. She had to believe degrees mattered, or the loss of everything she'd been meant nothing at all.

But that wasn't quite true either, and she was done lying to herself in comfortable ways. The loss would have meant something regardless. She was learning that too.

* * *

After supper, they did what they did most evenings now: nothing ambitious. Sebastian read aloud from a history of the Oath Wars he'd bought off a peddler, correcting its errors under his breath with increasing irritation until Quentin laughed and took the book away. She mended a shirt cuff. The fire ticked. Outside, the frost deepened, and somewhere down Cooper's Row a window glowed faintly, stubbornly blue, and no one shouted, and no one came with chains.

Later, when the house had gone quiet around its banked coals, Quentin stood alone at the window and looked east.

The horizon beyond the rooftops of Vellmark shimmered.

It was subtle—you had to know where to look, and when, and she suspected only she and perhaps a handful of others in all the world still saw it. A glimmer along the dark line where land met sky, gold shot through with green, breathing almost, slow as a sleeping thing's breath. The dragon-fire. Free. The last living flame of the old world, loosed from its cage of iron and oath, gone wherever fire goes when it is finally allowed to go.

Some nights she thought she could feel it, the way you feel sun through a closed window. Not power—that was gone, gone as thoroughly as her childhood, and she did not mourn it the way she'd expected to. Just warmth. Just the knowledge that something vast and ancient and unowned was alive out there in the dark, answering to no throne, bound by no voice, including hers.

Especially hers.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass. Behind her, Sebastian turned a page. On Tallow Street, the lamplighter made his rounds, and in Cooper's Row a child was drawing a sigil wrong and with love, and the frost on the window began, very slowly, to melt—as frost should, as all things should that are not chained against their nature.

Quentin Ravensworth, the last swordspeaker, the first oathbreaker, the woman who sold candles and ink and small mercies, watched the fire breathe on the edge of the world and felt, at last, entirely ordinary.

It was the finest thing she had ever earned.

Tomorrow there would be bread to buy and ledgers to argue over and a customer, probably, who wanted to talk about the glimmer on the horizon and whether it meant anything. Tomorrow Sebastian would sweep the step, and she would bring him coffee, and neither of them would say what the ritual of it meant, because they had both learned that the truest oaths are the ones you never speak aloud.

But tonight—

Tonight, far to the east, the dragon-fire flared once, bright as a struck bell, and Quentin smiled at it like an old friend seen across a crowded room, and went to bed.
