## 1. Charms for a Starless Night

The fish came in gray that morning, and the luck-knots went out the same color.

Hazel Voss had learned to read a harbor the way other men read weather. Brindle Harbor spoke in smells—brine and tar and the sweet rot of nets left too long folded—and this morning it was saying something he didn't like. The tide ran wrong, sluggish as old honey, and the gulls sat on the pilings with their feathers puffed and their heads tucked, refusing to fish. Even the water looked tired.

He set out his tray anyway. Three years of market mornings had taught him that routine was a kind of armor: lay the cloth square, arrange the charms in rows of five, keep your hands busy so your thoughts couldn't find you. A luck-knot braided from harbor-grass and red thread. A salt-whistle that made dogs sit and children laugh. A fever-charm pressed flat as a coin, good for three days against the damp-coughs that came off the water in autumn. Small things. Legal things—or legal enough, in the way that a thing is legal when no one important has bothered to look at it.

The charms worked. That was the strange part, the part he'd stopped asking questions about years ago. Magic was outlawed—had been outlawed since before Hazel could walk, since the Edict of Sealing closed the borders and turned every hedge-witch and oath-singer into either a criminal or a corpse. And yet his little knots held. His whistles sang. He'd told himself once, in the early years, that it was because the magic in them was so small the wards couldn't see it. A mouse slipping under a door built for wolves.

He knew better now. Or suspected better. There were nights, working thread by candlelight, when he could feel something feeding his work—a warmth in the fibers, faint as breath on a winter window. Something leaking. Something that wanted to be used.

He never let himself think past that. Thinking past that led to questions, and questions led to the sword sleeping in the bottom of his sea-chest, and the sword led to the oath, and the oath led back to a night three years ago when he had laid it down and sworn—out loud, in words that still lived somewhere in his chest like a swallowed coal—that he was done.

"Master Voss." The voice came small and determined from below the edge of his table. "I've got four coppers and half a dried herring."

Hazel looked down at Tansy Corr, the fisherman's daughter, nine years old and all elbows, her hair tied back with net-twine. She held out her coins in a cupped palm, solemn as a tithe-collector.

"Four coppers gets you a whistle," Hazel said. "The herring buys you nothing. I don't deal in fish."

"It's a *good* herring."

"It's a *dried* herring."

"My da says the luck-knots are working better lately," she said, ignoring him with the ease of long practice. "He says the boats that carry yours come home when the others don't. He wants two knots. For the *Maren's Pride* and the little skiff."

Hazel's hands paused over the tray. Two knots. Old Corr was careful with his coin; he didn't buy double unless he was frightened, and fishermen only got frightened about one thing.

"The water's been strange," Tansy said, filling the silence because children always do. "Da says it's like the sea's holding its breath. And last night the fog came in wrong. It came in *square*."

"Square," Hazel repeated.

"Like it had corners." She said it without drama, the way she'd report a dead crab on the sand. "Ma made us sleep in the front room."

Hazel took the four coppers. He braided the second knot while she watched—harbor-grass, red thread, and beneath it all that faint borrowed warmth—and when he handed both over he kept his voice easy, market-easy, the voice of a man who sold trinkets and nothing else.

"Tell your da to keep close to shore a few days. Weather's turning."

"Is it bad weather?"

"It's weather," Hazel said. "Go on, then."

She went, clutching her knots, and Hazel watched her pick her way down the quay toward the boats, and he did not believe a single word he'd just said.

* * *

He spent the afternoon pretending otherwise, which was its own kind of ritual. He mended a whistle. He re-sorted his stock. He ate bread and hard cheese on the seawall with his boots dangling over water that moved too slowly, and he watched the sky.

That was what finally settled it—not the square fog, not the dead tide, but the sky. Brindle Harbor sat far enough north that the stars came down thick in autumn, a spill of silver across the black, and the fishermen steered by them without thinking, the way you steer by anything you've trusted your whole life. Hazel had spent three years learning those stars again, naming them with harbor-names instead of the old names, because the old names belonged to a life he'd buried.

Tonight there were fewer of them.

Not clouded—clouds have edges, clouds drift. These were simply *gone*, patches of sky where starlight should have been, smooth and dark as spilled ink. As he watched, another winked out. Then another, farther east, toward the border no one crossed and no one spoke of.

Hazel sat very still on the seawall and felt the dread arrive, quiet and total, like cold water finding the seam in a boot.

The stars weren't falling. They were being *put out*. One by one, in order, like lamps along a road being snuffed by someone walking away from him.

He knew what lay east. Everyone knew, the way everyone knows the shape of a scar they've stopped looking at. The border wards—the great sealing, the reason magic died and the Salt Court rose and an entire country turned its face inward—ran along the eastern marches, anchored at gates older than any kingdom. If the wards were failing—

But they didn't fail. That was the whole point of them. That was the price paid for them. Whatever else happened, whatever kings rose and fell, the wards held. That was the one fixed point the world still had.

Another star went out.

Hazel stood up, brushed crumbs from his coat, and walked back through the market with measured steps, nodding to neighbors, calling a greeting to the chandler, buying a twist of tobacco he didn't want because a man who changes his routine is a man who knows something. He climbed the stairs to his room above the sailmaker's shop. He lit the lamp. He set the kettle on.

And then he opened the sea-chest.

The sword lay at the bottom under three winters of wool shirts, and it looked exactly as it had the day he'd laid it there: plain blade, worn grip, no ornament worth stealing. An oath-sword wasn't a weapon, not really. It was a contract with an edge. It woke only for its bearer, and only when the oath it carried was threatened—and for three years it had been the most patient piece of steel in the world.

Hazel reached in and touched the grip.

The flare went through him like a struck bell.

Light poured up the blade—not the clean gold of forge-light but a deep, urgent amber, dragon-colored, the color of a fire he hadn't seen since before the sealing—and the sound it made filled the little room, a low ringing hum that rattled the lamp glass and set his teeth buzzing. The sword was awake. After three years of silence, of deliberate, cultivated sleep, it was awake and *shouting*, and the message needed no translation:

*The oath is breached. The oath is breached. The oath is breached.*

Below him, in the street, a dog began to howl. Then another. Somewhere a shutter banged, and a woman's voice rose in complaint, and then cut off, because everyone in Brindle Harbor heard it at the same moment.

It came from the east, rolling across the dark country like weather: a sound like breaking glass—but vast, cathedral-vast, the sound of a window the size of the sky giving way. It went on and on, longer than any real glass could shatter, harmonics folding into harmonics, and underneath it Hazel heard—or imagined he heard, though his bones insisted otherwise—a second sound, thinner, almost lost beneath the crash. A keening. Like something enormous exhaling after years of held breath.

Then silence, total, pressing, the kind of silence that has weight.

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight. The kettle, forgotten, began to sing.

Hazel stood in the middle of his small room with a burning sword in his hand and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes too late to be useful, that everything was already different. Not would be. *Was.* The quiet life—the market mornings, the luck-knots, the careful smallness he'd built plank by plank over three years—it hadn't ended tonight. It had ended the moment the first star went out, and he'd sat on a seawall eating cheese and watching it happen, telling himself it was weather.

Down in the harbor, the *Maren's Pride* was putting out despite the hour, old Corr's lantern bobbing at her stern, running for deep water on a night when every instinct said come ashore. Hazel thought of Tansy's two knots, clutched in a child's fist, and of the warmth in the braid—that leaked, borrowed, stolen warmth—and of where it must have come from, and of what it meant that the source of it had just shattered like glass on the far side of the world.

His charms were going to stop working. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe within the hour. Every knot, every whistle, every fever-coin in his tray was drawn from something that no longer existed, and when it guttered out, so did every ward, every blessing, every small protection between the people of this coast and whatever came through the hole in the east.

The sword flared again, insistent, and swung gently in his grip until its point faced east. Toward the broken gate. Toward whatever was coming through it—or whatever had been trapped behind it and was now, finally, free.

"No," Hazel told it, the way you talk to a dog that wants a walk in a storm. "No. I'm done. I laid it down. I *swore* it."

The light held steady, patient and pitiless, and outside the window the last stars over the eastern hills went out all together, like a door closing.

Hazel set the sword on the table, still glowing, still humming, and packed his sea-chest with hands that almost didn't shake. Wool shirts. Charms—all of them, even the ones half-finished. The luck-knots he'd promised no one. Bread. The last of the tobacco.

At the door he stopped, looking back at the small warm room he'd built out of the wreckage of a life, and let himself mourn it for exactly as long as it took the kettle to finish singing.

Then he blew out the lamp, took up the sword, and went down the stairs into a starless night, east, toward the sound of breaking glass—because somewhere out there, at a collapsed gatehouse on the edge of a dying country, someone would have to hold the door.

He just didn't know yet that it would be a boy.
