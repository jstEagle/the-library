## 3. A Country That No Longer Exists

The decree was nailed to the tollhouse door with four iron nails, and someone had already stolen the fifth.

Sebastian read it twice, because the first time he'd been too busy noticing that his hands were shaking to take in the words. *By order of the Regency Council in exile, the realm of Vael is hereby dissolved. All offices, oaths, and obligations are void as of this date. Citizens are advised to conduct themselves accordingly.*

Conduct themselves accordingly. He turned the phrase over like a river stone, looking for the smooth side. He was twelve years old and he had held a door for two days without sleeping, and now some council in exile said his obligation was void. As if it had been a debt. As if you could just—stop.

"Void," Hazel said behind him, in the flat voice he used when he didn't want a thing to be true. "They voided the whole country."

"They can't void the door," Sebastian said. It came out more stubborn than he meant it to. The gatehouse was half a mile back down the road, still leaning where the wards had torn it open, and he kept catching himself listening for it, the way you listen for a dog you've left tied somewhere.

Hazel didn't argue. That was one of the things Sebastian had decided he liked about him, out of the very short list of things he knew about him: Hazel Voss argued with the world, but not with children. He just looked at the decree for a long moment, and then at the road ahead of them—the Salt Road, wide and pale and utterly empty of anyone collecting anything—and said, "Well. I suppose we're not citizens anymore."

"What are we, then?"

"Pedestrians." Hazel shouldered his pack. His sword rode across his back wrapped in oilcloth, and even through the cloth Sebastian could see the faint pulse of light under it, slow as breathing. It hadn't stopped since the night the stars went out. "Come on. If we're nobody, we'd better be fast nobodies."

* * *

They fell in with Maren Quill on the third morning, at a crossroads shrine that had been picked clean of offerings.

Sebastian saw her before Hazel did—a woman sitting on the shrine's low wall with her boots off, rubbing her feet, and beside her a leather satchel so official-looking it might have been a small animal. She wore the grey-and-white of the tithe service, or she had; the salt-caduceus pin was still on her collar but the collar itself had been torn, deliberately, in a neat line, like she'd started to rip it off and lost her nerve halfway.

She watched them approach with the expression of someone doing arithmetic.

"You're the swordspeaker," she said when they were close enough. Not a question. "Voss. They had your likeness posted in every collection house south of the Weep. *Charm-monger. Unlicensed. Oath-forfeit.*" She said it the way other people recited poetry. Then she looked at Sebastian, and something in her face went carefully blank. "And this is?"

"The door," Sebastian said.

"He means ward-keeper's apprentice," Hazel said.

"I mean what I mean."

Maren Quill laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her—and pulled her boots back on. "Right. Well. I'm Maren Quill, senior salt-tithe collector, route eleven, which is to say I'm nothing, because as of four days ago there is no route eleven, no tithe service, no—" she gestured broadly at the empty sky, the dead stars, the whole pale horizon, "—country. So." She stood, and squared her satchel on her hip with a motion so practiced it looked like breathing. "You two heading toward the Hearth Road?"

"Why?" Hazel asked.

"Because everyone with anywhere to be is heading toward the Hearth Road, and roads are safer with numbers. Also," and here her voice did something complicated, went light over something heavy, "I have food, and you look like men who've been eating charm-bought luck instead of bread."

Sebastian's stomach made a decision for all of them. It growled loud enough that a crow lifted off the shrine roof.

Hazel closed his eyes briefly, as if praying for patience from gods who had recently resigned. "We'll walk with you as far as the ford," he said, in the tone of a man negotiating terms he has already lost.

"As far as the ford," Maren agreed, and smiled, and Sebastian noticed that neither of them moved to set a pace that would separate them.

* * *

The trouble with pretending, Sebastian discovered, was that it required everyone to keep pretending at the same time, and none of them were any good at it.

At the ford, Maren shared her bread and hard cheese and dried plums, portioning them out with a tithe-collector's exactness—three equal shares, no more, no less—and when Sebastian finished his first and stared hopefully at hers, she sighed and gave him a quarter of hers without being asked, muttering about growing boys as if it were an imposition rather than the thing she'd clearly been waiting to do since the crossroads.

At the ford, too, Hazel mended the strap of her satchel with a small charm, quick fingers and a word under his breath, and Maren watched him do it with an expression Sebastian couldn't name—hunger and grief wearing each other's clothes.

"That's illegal," she said.

"It's a strap."

"It's a *leak-charm*. Skims fire off the ward-lines. I confiscated forty of those last year." She took the satchel back and tested the strap, twice, though it was obviously perfect. "I reported every one. Names, dates, quantities. There's a ledger."

"There's always a ledger," Hazel said.

"Yes," said Maren Quill, and for a moment she sounded tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking. "There always is."

Sebastian walked between them after that, and found that he liked the shape of it: an adult on either side, their shadows overlapping his in the late light, their voices trading dry observations over his head like a rope bridge he could stand on. Hazel knew the old names of rivers. Maren knew which villages had paid their tithes in full and which had shorted the collectors and by how much, and told these stories like gossip, scandalous and fond. Between them they knew the whole country, and the country was gone, and they talked about it anyway, the way you talk about someone at a funeral because stopping would be worse.

He began, without quite deciding to, to orbit them. When the path forked he waited to see which way they'd go before committing. When Hazel scouted ahead, Sebastian drifted after Maren; when Maren stopped to check her maps, he drifted back to Hazel. He told himself it was tactics. Stay near the people with food and charms and a sword that glowed. Any sensible orphan would do the same.

But at dusk, when they made camp in the shell of a waystation whose hearth had gone cold—stone dead, no ember, no warmth, the fire-starved the way everything was fire-starved now—it was Maren who built the little ring of stones without thinking, collector's habit, a hearth-shape for a fire they had nothing to light. And it was Hazel who sat beside Sebastian on the broken step and, without comment, put his own cloak around the boy's shoulders when the wind came up, and kept talking to Maren about the ford as if his arm weren't doing anything at all.

Sebastian sat very still inside the cloak and let himself want it. Just for a minute. Let himself imagine this was a thing that belonged to him: a fire-ring, a woman with maps, a man with a glowing sword, a night, a tomorrow that included the same people.

"We're not staying together," Hazel said to Maren, across the cold hearth. "Past the ford. I want that understood."

"Obviously," Maren said. "I have route obligations."

"You don't have a route."

"I have *habits*, Voss, and my habits say I walk route eleven until someone tells me otherwise, and the only people who could tell me otherwise work for a country that doesn't exist, so." She spread her hands. "Route eleven."

"The boy needs proper ward-keeper training," Hazel said, "not a tax collector's shadow."

"And you need someone watching your back who knows which villages will sell you to the Court and which will feed you. Not a smuggler's shadow."

Sebastian looked between them, at the two adults solemnly agreeing to abandon each other while sharing a blanket's worth of fireless camp, and felt something in his chest crack open—not painfully.
