## 4. What the Salt Remembers

The inn at Drossen's Ferry had no name, or if it had once, the salt had eaten it off the sign. Maren Quill chose it because it was the kind of place where no one asked questions of a woman with a collector's satchel, and because the fire in its hearth was real wood, crackling and stupid and honest, burning for nothing but warmth.

She sat with her back to the wall and her ledgers open across three tables pushed together, because that was how much room they took. Sebastian watched from the bench nearest the fire, chin on his fists, eyes going from page to page like a boy reading a story written in numbers. Hazel stood apart, arms crossed, the way he'd been standing since she'd said *sit down, both of you, there's something you need to see*.

"You were a tithe collector," Hazel said. Not a question. He'd known since the ferry crossing, when she'd paid the tollman out of habit and then stared at her own hand as though it belonged to someone dead.

"I was the best tithe collector in the Western Reaches." Maren turned a page. The paper was good stock, court-stock, watermarked with a crest that wasn't any crest anyone had ever seen on a banner or a coin—a hearth inside a circle of salt. "Eleven years. I never missed a collection day. I never wrote a house off the rolls until I'd knocked on their door four times. I knew every farmstead between here and the Graywater by the sound of its gate."

"And what exactly were you collecting?"

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it on the road, in the dark, while the two of them slept—Hazel with his hand near that absurd little sword he pretended wasn't a sword, the boy curled against the cart wheel like a cat. She had rehearsed it and every version came out sounding like confession, so she had decided to stop pretending it was anything else.

"Salt," she said. "Officially. The Tithe of Preservation, it was called. Every household owed one measure per season, weighed against the household's hearth-stone. You've seen the scales."

"Everyone's seen the scales," Hazel said. "You weigh your salt, you pay your due, you get your stamp. My mother grumbled about it every autumn of my childhood."

"Your mother," Maren said quietly, "was paying for something she was told kept her safe. And she was. Kept safe, I mean. That part was true." She pulled the top ledger toward herself and turned it to face him. "Look at the columns. Not the amounts—the destinations."

He looked. So did Sebastian, sliding off the bench to stand at Hazel's elbow, close enough that Maren could smell woodsmoke and river-water on him. The boy read faster than either of them expected; she caught his lips moving through the route-codes, the waystation marks, the tally of measures received and forwarded.

"These aren't granaries," Sebastian said. "I know the granary codes. My master made me memorize them. These go—" His finger stopped on the page. "These don't go anywhere. There's no place called Hearth-Circle Nine."

"No," Maren said. "There isn't. There never was, on any map I could buy or beg or steal. Eleven years, and I delivered my collections to sealed wagons at sealed depots, and the men who took them wore no livery, and the receipts bore that mark." She tapped the watermark. "A hearth in a ring of salt. I thought it was an accounting office. I thought it was the King's Own Purveyance, some dusty bureau where clerks stacked barrels and died of boredom."

"But it wasn't," Hazel said.

"It was a court." Her voice stayed level. That was the trick of it, the thing she'd learned in eleven years of knocking on doors: keep your voice level and the world will believe nothing is wrong. "Not the King's court. Something older, underneath it. They called themselves the Salt Court, and they had been taking a measure from every hearth in the country since before anyone now living was born, and no one—not the collectors, not the payers, not the kings—knew what the salt was for."

Sebastian had gone very still. "Ward-keepers know," he said. "We know some of it. My master—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "My master used to say the borders weren't walls. He said they were a mouth. And mouths have to be fed."

Maren met the boy's eyes. Twelve years old, and he already understood more than she had at thirty, because he'd been raised behind the door instead of outside it.

"That's the half of it," she said. "Here's the whole of it, as far as I've pieced it together, and I'll thank you not to interrupt, because if I stop I may not start again." She drew a breath. "There is a fire under this country. An old one. Dragon-fire, the first fire, the one all hearths are descended from—it's why a flame lit from your hearth will light another and stay itself, why charms work at all, why wards hold. Everything burns because something beneath us has burned since the beginning. And it is dying. It has been dying for a long time. And the Salt Court found it, and caged it, and fed it."

"With salt," Hazel said.

"With salt. With eleven measures a year from every hearth in the realm, carried by collectors like me along routes like these"—she spread her hands over the ledgers—"to a fire we were never allowed to see. Every grain we collected went into that furnace. Every stamp I pressed, every scale I balanced, every widow I pressed for the last measure she could spare—I was feeding a captive god so the Court could keep it leashed and dole out its warmth on their terms. The borders didn't fall because the magic ran out. The borders fell because the Court got greedy, or careless, or both, and drew too hard on a fire that hasn't enough left to give."

* * *

The fire in the inn's hearth popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard groaned. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a tide against glass.

Hazel hadn't moved. But Maren saw the change in him, the way a stillness settled into his shoulders that was different from before—heavier, older. When he finally spoke, his voice was strange.

"The charms," he said. "My charms. The luck-knots, the ember-charms, the little things I sold at market for copper. They were outlawed. Everything small was outlawed—the Court licensed magic, hoarded it, made anything a common person could work into contraband. And yet mine worked. For years. Better than they should have, honestly. A luck-knot shouldn't turn aside a fever. A hearth-blessing shouldn't keep a fisherman's boat afloat in the Graymaw squall." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "I always thought I was just good. Talented. The last oath-bound swordspeaker, skimming power off the top of whatever the licensed channels let leak."

"You were," Maren said. "Skimming. That's exactly the word." She pulled a map from the bottom of the satchel—one she'd drawn herself, in the last three days, from memory, because her memory was the only honest record she had left. It showed the Western Reaches in fine ink lines, and threaded through them, in red, the collection routes. And around the routes, in a different color, a faint haze she'd shaded with the side of her pencil. "The Court's system leaked. Caged fire doesn't burn clean. Every wagon, every depot, every transfer bled a little warmth into the ground around it. Like a rich man dropping coins in the street and never noticing." She looked up at him. "You built your life in the gutters of their machine, Hazel Voss. Your charms worked because you lived close enough to the routes to catch the runoff. The villages where your charms held strongest—" she touched three places on the map, "—here, here, and here—are all within a mile of a red line."

He stared at the map for a long time. Then he sat down, slowly, on the bench across from her, as though his legs had made the decision without consulting him.

"So even my rebellion was theirs," he said. "Even the little good I did was scraps from their table."

"Yes," Maren said, because lying to him would have been a kindness, and she had spent eleven years being kind in exactly that way, and look where it had gotten everyone. "And so was mine. And so—" she hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, "—so was yours, Sebastian."

The boy flinched. Hazel's head came up sharply. "Careful."

"He deserves the truth more than either of us does." Maren turned to face Sebastian fully. "Ward-keepers draw their stones from the border-wards. And the border-wards were fed from the same source. The Court keyed the wards themselves, boy. Your master's order, the gates, the posts, the whole sacred duty of holding the door—they weren't keeping the country safe from outside. They were keeping the cage locked from inside. Every ward-stone you ever carried was a link in the chain."

Sebastian didn't cry. Maren had expected him to, had braced for it, and the fact that he didn't was somehow worse. He just looked at his own hands, turning them over, as though checking whether they'd changed.

"My master died holding the door," he said finally. His voice was very small and very flat. "Was that for the cage too?"

"I don't know," Maren said. "Maybe. Maybe he believed in it. People can serve a lie sincerely—that's what makes lies strong. But listen to me." She leaned forward. "Serving a thing without knowing isn't the same as choosing it. I didn't choose to starve widows. You didn't choose to lock a door on a dying god. We were tools. And the difference between a tool and a traitor is that a tool can be picked up and pointed somewhere else."

* * *

They worked through the night after that, because grief is a poor bedfellow and work keeps it at arm's length. Maren laid out her maps—all forty-one of them, every collection route in the Western Reaches—and began marking, in careful ink, every hearth that had gone cold in the last three years. There were more each season. The fire was failing faster now; she could see it in the columns, the way a physician sees death in a pulse.

Hazel sat across from her with his sword drawn across his knees, that short, plain blade that wasn't plain at all, watching the ink dry. Once, without looking up, he said: "The First Hearth. Is that where the fire is?"

"If the old stories are true, that's where it was born. Which means it's where the Court keeps it." She set down her pen. "It's also suicide. The Court's knights are still sworn, still armed, and now that the wards are down they'll be hunting anything that can re-key the bindings."

"Re-key them with what?"

Maren glanced at Sebastian, asleep at last, curled in the corner of the bench with his head on his folded arms, one hand still loosely closed around the empty loop where his last ward-stone had hung. She lowered her voice anyway.

"With blood that's bound to the wards," she said. "Keeper's blood. Old blood, going back generations. The Court will want that boy, Hazel. Whatever else happens, they will want him, and they will be polite about it right up until they aren't."

Hazel looked at the sleeping child for a long moment. Then he sheathed the sword, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet in a way she was beginning to understand meant danger.

"Then we'd better reach the fire before they reach us," he said. "How far to the First Hearth?"

Maren unrolled the oldest map in her satchel—the one she'd stolen from a depot clerk three nights ago, the one with the red lines converging like threads into a single point deep in the Salt Wastes, a point marked with that watermark she now knew by heart: a hearth inside a ring of salt.

"Nine days," she said, "if the roads hold. And Hazel—" She paused, because the next thing was the thing she'd been circling all night, the thing the ledgers had finally forced her to say aloud. "There's a notation in the newest ledger. Dated the night the wards fell. It says the Court has begun *drawing down the reserve*. All of it. Every measure ever collected, poured into the binding at once."

"To save the fire?"

"To save the cage." She met his eyes. "If they succeed, the fire lives and stays theirs forever. If they fail—" she looked at the cold hearth across the room, at the wood burning its honest, ordinary, borrowed heat, "—it goes out everywhere. All at once. Every hearth in the country. Every charm. Every ward. Everything."

Outside, the starless dark waited. Inside, the maps lay spread between them like the veins of a body, all running red toward one distant, beating point.

Nine days.
