## 5. The Guttering Road

The old tithe-road ran north like a scar that had never quite healed, and Hazel walked it the way a man walks through his own memory—carefully, and with his eyes down.

Three days out of the drowned gatehouse, the country had begun to show them what it looked like when no one was pretending anymore. Tollbars stood open and rotted. Wayshrines to saints nobody could name had been stripped of their salt-offerings, their little basins scoured white and empty as teeth. And everywhere, on every mile-marker and milestone and leaning post, someone had painted over the crown-and-crescent of the realm with a single word in dripping tar: **DISSOLVED**.

"Does it feel different?" Sebastian asked. He'd been walking backwards for a quarter mile, watching the road unspool behind them, the way children do when they haven't yet learned that the world can sneak up from the front.

"Does what feel different?"

"The country. Maren says a country's just a promise everybody makes at once. She says now nobody's making it." He hopped a rut without looking. "I keep trying to feel the difference and I can't."

Hazel considered the boy's boots, patched twice, the ward-keeper's badge he still wore though its silver had gone dull as pewter. "That's because promises were already thin out here," he said. "The center of things got fed first. The edges have been starving for years. You just couldn't see it until the lights went out."

Maren, walking ahead with her ledger-satchel hitched high on her hip, snorted. "Listen to the philosopher. Three days ago he was charging two coppers for a luck-knot."

"Two coppers," Hazel said, "was a fair price for a knot that didn't work very well."

"It worked," Sebastian said immediately, and then went pink, because they all remembered whose hands had tied it.

They made nine miles by midday, following Maren's route-marks—the small chiseled tithe-glyphs cut into stones every league, each one shaped like a little hearth with a door. Hazel had passed these marks all his life and thought them surveyor's signs, boundary stones, boring civic furniture. Now, knowing what Maren knew, he saw them differently: a trail of mouths. Every glyph had once drunk its sip of salt and warmth and sent it north, north, north, to a court that officially did not exist, feeding a fire that officially was not captive.

"You're doing the face again," Maren said without turning around.

"What face?"

"The one where you look at my route-stones like they owe you an apology."

"They do," Hazel said. "So do mine."

She let that sit. That was the thing about Maren Quill she was still learning to be: she could hold a silence like a scale, weighing both sides of it, and not tip it until she was ready.

The first village came on them in the middle of the afternoon—a scatter of turf-roofed houses around a well, nameless on any map Hazel owned, which was most maps. They knew something was wrong before they reached the first house, because there were no smells. A village at that hour should have smelled of bread or dung or smoke or fish; this one smelled of nothing at all, of swept air, of a room after the fire has been carried out of it.

The wards had failed here. Not collapsed like the border wards—that great glass-shattering ruin they'd all heard and felt—but guttered, starved down to nothing, the way a lamp dies when the oil runs low. Hazel felt it as an absence, a smooth cold place in the world where there should have been texture.

A woman met them at the well with a baby on her hip and three more children peering from behind her skirts. Her eyes went straight past Maren and fixed on Hazel's satchel with the naked, terrible hope of someone who has stopped hoping and then been shown otherwise.

"You're a charmsman," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a diagnosis.

"I do small work," Hazel said. "Knots. Thresholds. Nothing grand."

"Our hearth-ward died last month," she said. "My husband walks the perimeter every night with an iron bar like we're back before the Settlement. The baby—" her voice did something complicated and came out flat— "the baby cries at things that aren't there. Things that come up from the salt-marsh when the light goes. Wraiths don't cross a live threshold. Ours is dead."

Hazel knelt and opened his satchel.

Here was the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the gatehouse, laid out in cord and bone-beads and wax-sealed twists of ashwood. He counted without touching: eleven charms left. Eleven small skimmings of leaked dragon-fire, hoarded the way a miser hoards, each one a candle-end of a conflagration locked away beneath the Salt Court. Eleven nights of protection he could buy, against a road that stretched ahead of them through a country full of dead thresholds.

He took out the strongest one—a hearth-ward keyed to a family line, good for a season if the season was kind—and held it out.

The woman stared at it. "What do you cost?"

"Nothing."

"Nobody does anything for nothing. Not even now. Especially not now."

"He's famous for it," Sebastian said solemnly. "It's a whole condition."

Hazel pressed the charm into her hand and closed her fingers over it. "It wants your blood-line to key it. One drop on the wax, think of everyone under your roof, living or coming. It'll hold a year. After that—" he hesitated, then lied gently, "after that you send to me and I'll renew it. Voss. Ask along the river; they know the name."

The woman looked at him the way people look at a door opening onto a room they'd sealed shut. Then she was crying without any noise, and pressing bread and hard cheese into his arms, insisting, refusing thanks while demanding it, and Hazel understood that this too was payment, and that she would be insulted if he refused, so he accepted.

They were half a mile gone when Maren said, quietly, "Ten."

"I can count."

"Can you? Because from where I'm walking it looks like a man spending his own life one coin at a time and calling it generosity." She stopped in the road and faced him, and her collector's eyes were hard and bright. "I kept ledgers, Hazel. I know what depletion looks like. I've written it down often enough. What happens when you reach zero? Do the wraiths eat you, or does the oath-sword do something worse first?"

"I don't know," Hazel said honestly. "Nobody's ever spent one all the way down. We were always taught to hoard. Hoarding was the whole religion." He shifted the satchel higher on his shoulder. "Maybe that's why it feels so much like relief."

Maren looked at him a long moment. Whatever she saw made her jaw tighten, not in anger but in the effort of swallowing whatever she'd meant to say next. She turned and walked on. But that evening, when they camped in a ruined waystation, Hazel woke once to find her sitting up with her ledger open, adding columns by moonlight—and the column wasn't salt or tithe. It was names. Every village, every family, every hearth that had gone cold on her routes, entered in a neat, merciless hand, as if she intended someday to present the bill.

* * *

They struck the pattern of it over the next four days, the way you learn the rhythm of a sickness.

A hamlet where the well-ward had failed and the water soured—Hazel gave a cleansing twist, nine remaining. A crossroads shrine where travelers huddled against something that whistled in the dark and wore voices—Hazel gave a night-knot, eight. A ferryman whose blessing-charm had rotted in his fist mid-river, who now refused to cross at all and sat drinking beside his beached boat—seven, and the man wept into his beard and poled them over for free anyway, sobbing apologies for the state of the rope.

Each time, Hazel performed the same small liturgy: kneel, open the satchel, choose, press the charm into stranger's hands, lie about renewal, accept bread. Each time, he felt the reserves thin another degree—not painfully, exactly, but the way a room dims when a shutter swings closed. His dormant oath-sword, that traitorous flare of light at the gatehouse, stayed quiet now, and he was grateful, and afraid of how grateful.

Sebastian watched all of it with a stillness that should have warned them.

On the sixth day they climbed out of the marsh country into chalk hills, and found a village that had given up entirely—not dead, worse, resigned. Half the houses stood empty, doors open to weather. In the one street, a girl of maybe eight sat on a step wrapped in three blankets in summer, shivering, her lips gray-blue, while her mother wrung a cloth over her forehead and a neighbor argued uselessly about sending for a physician in a country with no roads worth the name and no physicians worth their fees.

"Fever-wrack," Maren said grimly. "Or something wearing its clothes. Salt-country sickness—it comes in behind the wards when they die, like damp through a cracked wall."

Hazel was already kneeling. Six charms. This called for something keyed inward, a mending-knot, and he had only two of those, and the road ahead ran through the worst of the salt-flats where—

He was reaching for the first mending-twist when he noticed Sebastian wasn't beside him.

He found the boy twenty feet away, crouched at the sick girl's other side. Sebastian's hand was closed tight around something at his neck, and his face wore the expression Hazel had last seen on him at the gatehouse: the terrible adult gravity of a child holding a door.

"Seb—" Hazel started, softly, warningly, not even sure what the warning was for.

But the boy had already lifted the cord over his head. On it hung a ward-stone: a knuckle-sized piece of cloudy quartz veined with faint silver light—the badge of his office, the last working stone from the collapsed gatehouse, the last live thing Sebastian owned from the entire life that had burned down around him. Ward-keepers' apprentices got one stone apiece, Maren had told them; it was how they learned to feel a boundary, holding it in their palm until the stone's hum became their heartbeat.

Sebastian pressed it into the girl's blanket-wrapped hands and folded her fingers over it the exact way Hazel folded strangers' fingers over charms. The stone's glow strengthened faintly, a candle behind fog, and the girl's shivering eased—not cured, nothing so kind, but eased, the fever meeting something in the room that refused to negotiate.

"That's your stone," Hazel said quietly, when they'd moved aside. "You'll need it. For the door-holding. For everything."

Sebastian shrugged with one shoulder, eyes still on the girl. "Ward-keeper's rule. First law. You don't keep the ward—you keep the people inside it. Master Ellery said it so many times I hated him." His voice caught on the name and steadied itself with visible effort. "If I'm going to be a keeper, I should start keeping."

Hazel looked at the boy—at the hollows under his eyes, the badge gone dull, the empty cord hanging against his collarbone—and felt something turn over in his chest with the slow weight of a keystone settling.

*What you give away is what you actually own.* His grandmother had said that once, over knots and cord, back when Hazel thought it was just old-woman nonsense to make charity taste better. He'd spent six days proving it true with his satchel, counting down from eleven toward zero like a man emptying himself onto a road. And here was a twelve-year-old who had understood it instantly, completely, with the one thing he couldn't replace, while Hazel still flinched at every subtraction.

"Maren," he said, "how far to the First Hearth?"

She consulted her ledger, her maps, her memory of routes she'd audited for a court she'd believed was a ministry. "Nine days. Ten, if the flats are flooded. And the last three days are through territory the tithe-records mark with a black line." She tapped the page. "Black line means 'do not collect.' Even the Court wouldn't send collectors there. Which tells you what the Court thought was living in it."

"And our supplies?"

"Bread enough for five days if we're honest, six if we're liars."

Hazel nodded slowly and did the new arithmetic—the one he'd been refusing, the one with no clever answer, where every charm spent on strangers was a night of their own journey left dark, and every night left dark might be the night something found them on the black-lined miles.

Six charms. Nine days. A dying fire at the end of it that needed all three of them alive.

Behind them the mother was kissing Sebastian's filthy hair and trying to give him her wedding ring, and he was pushing it back, laughing for the first time in days, telling her ward-keepers don't take payment, it's a whole condition.

Hazel closed his satchel and looked north, where the hills fell away toward a horizon already whitening with distant salt.

"We walk fast," he said, "and we pray the black line stays a line."

And far off, past the edge of hearing, past the edge of sense, something on those salt-flats heard the word *pray*, and laughed with a voice like wind through bones.
