## 6. Court of Cinders

Sebastian smelled the knights before he saw them—ozone and hot iron, the scent of wards freshly fed, a smell that belonged to gatehouses and not to open road. He had spent three years learning to read that smell from the inside of a door, and now it came walking toward him across the salt-flats like weather.

"Maren," he said. "Someone's coming. Someone *warded*."

They had made camp in the lee of a collapsed tithe-station, its roof caved in like a broken basket, its walls furred white with salt-bloom. Hazel was restringing a luck-knot by firelight, his fingers moving through the old patterns without looking, and Maren was hunched over her ledgers as always, cross-checking routes against the map she'd drawn from memory. Both of them went still at his voice. That was the thing Sebastian had learned about them in the weeks since the gatehouse: they listened to him. Nobody had ever listened to him before except to give orders.

Hazel rose without a word and put himself between the camp and the road. It wasn't dramatic. He just stood up, and suddenly there was a wall where there hadn't been one, and Sebastian felt something twist warm and painful under his ribs, because walls were supposed to be things you built for other people, not things other people became for you.

Six riders came out of the dark. Their armor was salt-white enameled steel, and their horses' hooves struck sparks off the crusted flats, and above each visor burned a small cold light—the color of dragon-fire kept in a jar. Knights of the Salt Court. Sebastian had seen their like only once, from the top of the gatehouse tower, riding out on some errand no ward-keeper's apprentice was told about.

The lead rider dismounted. When the visor lifted, the face beneath was ordinary—a tired woman with grey at her temples, the kind of face that belonged to someone's mother. That was somehow worse.

"Hazel Voss." Her voice carried flat across the flats. "Swordspeaker. Third Oath of the Line of Ember."

"That title died three years ago," Hazel said. His voice was easy, but Sebastian saw his hand rest near the hilt of the oath-sword, which hadn't left his back since the night the stars went out. "Along with everything else."

"The Court doesn't agree." The knight dismounted fully and walked forward until she stood close enough that Sebastian could see the salt-crust on her gauntlets, glittering like frost. "The Court has been patient while lesser matters settled themselves. But the wards are down, Voss. The realm is bleeding magic into the ground, and we are the only ones still holding a cup."

"Are you." Maren's voice came sharp from beside the fire. She didn't stand. "Funny. I used to carry the cup myself. I know exactly who it gets poured for."

The knight ignored her, which told Sebastian everything about how the Court ranked tithe-collectors. Her eyes stayed on Hazel. "You were sworn once. You can be sworn again. Full restoration. Rank, standing, the sword re-keyed to your hand instead of rotting on your back. All of it returned—"

Sebastian saw it then, the way you see lightning before the thunder: the way the knight's gaze slid sideways, past Hazel, past Maren, and found him sitting small by the fire. The way her expression didn't change at all, the way a ledger doesn't change when you write a number in it.

"—if you surrender the boy."

The words landed in the silence like stones into a well. Sebastian counted the seconds waiting for the splash—for Hazel to say something, anything—and the silence went on so long that he understood, with a child's terrible arithmetic, that some part of Hazel was actually considering it. Not the surrender. But the offer. The *shape* of it. A life handed back, whole, after three years of selling luck-knots to fishermen.

"He's a child," Hazel said finally, "not a key."

"His blood is a key." The knight said it the way Maren read figures from a ledger: precise, unashamed. "Ward-keeper's line, last of it, keyed to the border matrix since birth. One drop on the First Stone and every ward in the nation re-seats itself for a hundred years. No more wraiths. No more villages going dark. You've seen what's happening out here, Voss. You've been spending your charms on it. One boy buys all of it back."

"One boy," Maren repeated softly, and now she did stand, and Sebastian had never heard her voice sound like that, like a blade being drawn very slowly. "I collected tithes for eleven years. Do you know how many times I heard 'one hearth' sacrificed for 'the whole street'? One street for the town? I wrote the numbers down, madam. I wrote down every single one. And do you know what I learned?" She stepped forward into the firelight. "The whole is never whole. There's always another one. There's always a smaller number to feed into the bigger one, forever, until nothing's left but the counting."

"The Court thanks you for your service," the knight said, without a flicker of irony.

"Run," Hazel said.

It took Sebastian a heartbeat to realize the word was for him—that Hazel had turned his head slightly, just slightly, eyes never leaving the knight, and mouthed it again: *run*. And Sebastian's legs decided before his mind did, because three years at a gatehouse teaches you that when an adult says run, the adults have already lost the argument they're having.

He ran.

* * *

The salt-flats at night were a country of mirrors. Every crust pool held a scrap of starless sky, and Sebastian ran through a world doubled and inverted, his own reflection fleeing beneath him, the lights of the knights' visors blooming behind like a constellation fallen to earth. Behind him he heard voices rise—not combat, not yet, but the particular cadence of negotiation collapsing—and then a horn, long and low, and hoofbeats.

He knew these flats. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke: he'd memorized the maps in the gatehouse, every route the tithe-carts took, every sink and crust-bridge, because a ward-keeper's apprentice learns the doors of everywhere even if he owns none of them. So when the ground ahead shimmered wrong—when the mirror-surface gave way to a thin film over black brine—he swerved left without thinking, and behind him he heard a horse scream as its leg punched through the crust, and he didn't stop, didn't look, just ran with his lungs full of knives.

The burning started at the third mile.

He smelled it first—sulfur and something sweeter, like sugar left too long in a pan—and then the horizon to his right glowed, and he understood: the gas vents. Maren had warned them, drawing lines on her map with a burnt stick. *The flats breathe,* she'd said. *Old fire under the salt, leaking. Don't be out there when the wind shifts.* The Court's knights carried warded lanterns; Sebastian carried nothing but a dead ward-stone on a cord around his neck, the last gift of his master, drained the night the borders fell.

The wind shifted.

Fire ran along the flats in blue ribbons, following the gas seams, and the world became a maze of light and death, and Sebastian ran through it crying and didn't know he was crying until the tears froze salt-stiff on his cheeks. Because this was the part he'd known would come someday. Everyone left him. His master had gone to check the wards and never come back. His mother had gone to the tithe-house and never come back. And Hazel—Hazel had hesitated. Sebastian had counted the hesitation. Three heartbeats, maybe four, before he said *he's a child, not a key*, and those heartbeats were going to live in Sebastian forever, growing like salt-crystals, unless—

Unless.

A rider came around a burning seam ahead of him, cutting off the low ground, and the knight's visor-light swept across him and the voice called out, almost gentle: "Boy! Boy, you're not in trouble. We're not going to hurt you. We just need you to come home. Your master's people signed the protocols—you belong to the wards, and the wards belong to the Court—"

And Sebastian, twelve years old, lungs shredded, standing in a ring of blue fire with his dead master's stone hanging cold against his chest, heard the sentence for what it was. *You belong to the wards.* Not to anyone. To a function. To a door. He had held the door for three years alone because *someone has to*, and the Court had never once asked if the door-holder wanted feeding or sleep or to be a person, and now they wanted to pour him into the First Stone like brine into a tithe-barrel and call it a hundred years of safety.

"No," Sebastian said.

It came out small. He said it again, louder, and the fire seemed to lean toward the word. "No! I'm not a stone! I'm not a *door*!" And he turned and ran straight at the burning seam, because everyone knew the crust-bridges held over active vents—the heat kept the salt fused—and everyone knew it because his master had told him, and his master had died for less useful knowledge than this.

He crossed the seam with fire licking blue at his heels, and on the far side the ground rose toward a ridge of heaped slag, and he scrambled up it and looked back.

The knights were gathering at the seam's edge, unwilling to follow. And beyond them, further out on the flats, two figures were running—running *toward* him, through the burning country, one tall and one stocky, and the tall one's sword was drawn and flaring with a light Sebastian had only seen once before, the night the stars went out, and the stocky one was carrying a lantern and screaming his name in a voice cracked raw.

"*SEBASTIAN!*"

Maren's voice. Maren, who had argued with a knight rather than let him be traded. Hazel, who had hesitated four heartbeats and then drawn a legendary sword against six riders rather than finish the trade. They were coming for him. Through fire. Nobody had ever come for him through anything.

He sat down on the slag-ridge, quite suddenly, because his legs were done, and he watched them come, and he made a decision the way you make any real decision: without knowing you're making it, the way you don't decide to bleed.

When they reached him—Hazel dropping to his knees first, hands hovering over Sebastian's shoulders as if asking permission even now, even after everything—Sebastian spoke before either of them could.

"You hesitated," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Back at the fire. Four heartbeats. I counted."

Hazel went very still. In the blue glow of the distant seams, his face looked carved and old. "Yes," he said. "I did."

"Why?"

"Because the offer was my whole life back, and I'm a selfish man who's been dead for three years, and dead men grab at ropes." Hazel's jaw worked. "And then I remembered what my oath actually says. Who it's actually *for*. And I remembered that I've been choosing you since the gatehouse, kid, whether I admitted it or not. The hesitation was the last of the old life dying. That's all it was."

Sebastian searched his face for the lie and couldn't find it. Found, instead, something better: Maren wrapping her coat around him, smelling of ink and salt and ledgers, muttering, "*Four heartbeats*, honestly, men, I'd have thrown the lantern before she finished the sentence—" and laughing, or maybe crying, both of them doing it together.

"We should keep moving," Hazel said at last. "They'll regroup. And they'll send more next time—riders who don't negotiate."

But Sebastian didn't get up yet. He pulled the dead ward-stone from around his neck and looked at it—his master's last gift, drained and dark for weeks. In the firelight, deep in its core, something glimmered. Faint. Warm. The color of a hearth seen through a keyhole.

"Hazel," he whispered. "Look."

The stone was drinking the light of the burning flats. And somewhere far to the north, past the dark horizon, something vast and caged was guttering—and the stone, and the boy wearing it, could feel it pulling at them both like a tide.

"It knows where the fire is," Sebastian said. "My master's stone. It's pointing."

Hazel looked north. So did Maren. And the wind shifted again, carrying the smell of sulfur and old, old heat, and somewhere beneath the salt the First Hearth breathed.
