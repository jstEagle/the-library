## 7. The Oath-Bound Lie

They found the shrine by accident, or the shrine found them—Hazel could never afterward decide which.

It stood in the lee of a fallen waymarker on the old tithe-road, a squat stone hut with a roof of slate gone green as pondwater, its door hanging open like a slack jaw. Salt had crept up the walls in white tide-lines, marking years of storms the way rings mark a tree. Inside there was nothing but a broken bench, a cold lamp niche, and a sword-shaped hollow cut into the altar stone—the kind of hollow every oath-sword was forged to fit, back when forging them was legal.

Back when anything had been legal.

"We should keep moving," Maren said. She said it without conviction. Three days since the salt-flats, three days since Sebastian ran and Hazel chased and the knights' offer hung between all of them like smoke that wouldn't clear. The boy hadn't spoken more than ten words at a stretch since. He sat now on the shrine's threshold, knees drawn up, watching the road behind them with the fixed attention of someone who expected the horizon to open its mouth.

Hazel didn't answer her. He was looking at the hollow in the altar, and his hand—the one that still ached where the sword's hilt had burned it awake—was moving toward it before he'd decided anything at all.

"Hazel."

"The knights knew my name," he said. "Not just my name. My *line*. They quoted the Voss oath at me, word for word, and I never taught it to anyone. Never wrote it down. So either they've been listening through three years of silence, or—" His throat closed. He swallowed past it. "Or the words have always belonged to them, and I've been carrying their property around thinking it was mine."

He knelt at the altar. The hollow fit his palm the way a lock fits its key, which was the whole horror of it: it *fit*. Something in the stone woke under his touch, faint and warm as a banked coal, and light crawled up from the depths—not firelight, not starlight, but the pale mineral gleam of salt held against a flame.

Words rose with it. Carved so deep into the stone they'd been invisible until the stone chose to show them, worn smooth by centuries of hands that must have known exactly what they were reading.

Hazel read them.

And the world went very quiet, the way it does in the moment after breaking glass, when everyone is still deciding whether to scream.

*The bearer swears service to the Court of Salt, keeper of the First Hearth, in perpetuity, binding heir to heir. The bearer shall guard the tithe. The bearer shall return all strayed fire to the vault. The bearer shall hold no loyalty above the Court, neither kin nor country nor conscience.*

Neither kin nor country nor conscience.

He read it again. And again. As if repetition might change the grammar, might find some clause he'd missed, some merciful footnote. There was none. The oath his grandmother had whispered over him at seven years old, the oath he'd believed bound him to *the people*—to hearths and thresholds and the small frightened lights of ordinary homes—bound him instead to the thing that caged the fire. Every charm he'd ever spoken, every luck-knot and ward-stitch and warming-word, had been performed in service of the Court whether he willed it or not. He hadn't been a rebel skimming crumbs from their table.

He'd been their errant knight, three years absent without leave.

"Treason," he heard himself say. The word came out flat, dead, a coin bitten and found base. "That's what they meant. On the flats. When the Lord Tithe's man said *come home*. It wasn't an offer. It was a summons." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I thought I was deserting them. I was deserting *with them*. Everything I did—the charms, the teaching, the little fires I kept lit in people's kitchens—I did it under their seal. I'm not the last swordspeaker standing against the Salt Court, Maren. I'm the last sword *of* the Salt Court, rusting in a ditch."

Sebastian had come inside at some point. Hazel didn't know how long the boy had been standing there, listening. Long enough. The child's face had gone carefully blank in the way children's faces do when adults break in front of them—watchful, braced, already calculating how much of the wreckage would land on him.

"You're not going to leave," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't steady either. "You're not going to give me to them because the paper says you have to."

"Seb—"

"Because they said your oath. They said you *have* to." The boy's voice cracked on the last word, twelve years old and furious about it. "So do you? Do you have to?"

And Hazel opened his mouth to say *no, of course not, never*, and found that he couldn't. Not because the answer was yes. Because he no longer trusted himself to know the difference between the vow and the man making it. Three years of believing himself free, and the freedom had been a room with painted walls. What else was painted? What else in him belonged to them?

He put his head in his hands, kneeling at the altar like a penitent, and for a long moment the only sound was the wind working at the loose slate overhead.

* * *

Maren Quill had spent eleven years collecting other people's debts. She knew despair when she saw it—not the loud kind, the theatrical kind that wanted witnesses, but the quiet kind, the kind that sat down inside a person and started rearranging the furniture. She'd worn it herself for most of the walk from the flats, wearing it like a second coat she couldn't take off: every ledger line in her memory suddenly a confession, every hearth she'd measured and taxed and drained a small murder she'd signed her name to.

So she knew the look. And she knew something else too, something she'd learned the hard way, in the year after her own world dissolved: that the moment you most want to lie down is the exact moment someone has to stand over you and refuse to allow it.

She picked up the broken bench, tested its weight, and set it upright against the wall. Then she sat down across from Hazel, deliberately, the way you sit across from a debtor who thinks he has nothing left to pay with.

"Read it to me again," she said. "The last part."

"Maren—"

"*Read it.*"

He read it. *Neither kin nor country nor conscience.* His voice broke apart on the last word like ice on a thawing river.

"All right," Maren said. "Now tell me what it doesn't say."

He looked up at her. His eyes were red-rimmed and lost, a man reading his own death warrant and finding his name misspelled—no comfort in it, only confusion.

"It doesn't say you have to obey," she said. "Listen to me. Eleven years I worked the tithe routes. You know what I learned about contracts? About oaths, decrees, writs, all of it? They're words. Ink and breath. They have exactly as much power as people agree to give them—and the Court knows that better than anyone, because the entire Court is built on making people agree. That's what the wards were. That's what the borders were. A giant agreement nobody was allowed to read." She leaned forward. "Your grandmother swore those words over you. Fine. Words were sworn. But an oath isn't a chain, Hazel. It's a story you tell about yourself. And you've been telling a different story for three years. Which one do you think was true?"

"The written one," he said miserably. "Written ones always win. In court. In law."

"And I'm telling you law is just the Court's favorite story." Her voice hardened, and beneath the hardness, for the first time since he'd known her, he heard the thing underneath it—the guilt, the grief, the eleven years of complicity she carried like ballast. "I collected the tithe. Do you understand what that means? I walked into starving houses and took their last pinch of warmth and called it *revenue*. I told myself it was lawful. Lawful, lawful, lawful—it was the prayer I said over every theft. And then the wards fell and the law died overnight, and do you know what I discovered?" She laughed, short and bitter. "Nothing changed. The fire was still captive. The people were still cold. The crime had never needed the law's permission. The law was just the costume it wore."

"So what are you saying? That we're both criminals?"

"I'm saying criminal is their word for it." She reached out and took his wrist—the burned one, the one the hilt had marked—and turned his hand over, palm up, the way you'd read a ledger. "Look at this hand. What did it do? It stitched wards around a fisherman's daughter's window. It warmed a sick girl's room on the Guttering Road. It gave away everything it had to strangers who couldn't pay. If that's treason, then treason is the only honest work left in this country, and I'll sign my name to it twice."

Sebastian made a small sound. Both of them looked up. The boy had dragged his pack over and was digging through it, and he came out with the tithe-ledger—Maren's ledger, the one she'd carried out of her old life like an amputated limb, the one she hadn't let anyone touch since.

"You keep saying the records convict them," Sebastian said. "In your sleep, you say it. *The records will convict them.*" He set it on the bench between them, awkwardly formal, like a boy presenting evidence at a trial he'd rehearsed in his head. "Maybe that's the point. Maybe their words can hang them. You're always saying oaths are just words." He looked at Hazel, and his chin came up, and for a moment he looked so much like a small stubborn gatekeeper holding a door that Hazel's chest hurt. "So use theirs against them. You swore their oath? Fine. Read it *all the way*. Oaths end somewhere. Everything ends somewhere. Even doors."

Hazel stared at the ledger. At the boy. At Maren, who was watching him with the level patience of a woman who had talked a hundred debtors back from the edge of ruin and would talk down one more, even if it took all night.

Then he turned back to the altar, and did the thing he should have done at seven years old, at seventeen, at the moment the stars first went out: he read the oath to its end. Past the binding clauses, past the perpetuity, past the iron language of ownership—all the way down to the final lines, carved deepest of all, where the original drafters had grown careless or arrogant or simply certain no one would ever look.

*This bond shall hold while the First Hearth burns caged. Should the fire be spoken free by the bearer's own voice, the oath dissolves, and the bearer owes the Court nothing—neither service, nor fealty, nor remembrance.*

Silence.

Maren read it over his shoulder. He felt her go still. "Hazel," she said slowly. "Do you understand what this is? They didn't write you a leash. They wrote you a *fuse*. Some Voss, generations back, hid a way out in plain sight and hoped someone down the line would be brave enough to read to the bottom."

"Not brave enough," Hazel said. His voice came out strange, steadied, the despair burning off like morning fog. "Desperate enough. There's a difference, and I've finally got time to learn it."

He rose from the altar. The ache in his palm had changed character—no longer the burn of a brand, but the itch of a healing wound, something closing over.

Outside, the light was failing. Sebastian stood in the doorway, silhouetted against the last grey of the sky, and far off down the tithe-road—too far to be real, close enough to be believed—there was a glow on the horizon that was not dawn and not sunset. Pale gold. Flickering. The color of a fire seen through a very long corridor.

"They've stopped chasing us," Sebastian said quietly. "Why would they stop chasing us?"

Hazel looked at the glow, and understood, and felt the understanding settle over him like a mantle of coals.

"Because we're not running anymore," he said. "We're walking straight to them. To the First Hearth." He picked up his pack. "They know what I know now. They know the oath ends if the fire goes free. So they're not trying to catch us, Maren."

He stepped out onto the road, into the dark, toward the distant burning.

"They're preparing to meet us."
