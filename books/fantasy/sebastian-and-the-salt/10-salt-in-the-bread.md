## 10. Salt in the Bread

The school had been a tithe-house once, and it still smelled faintly of salt.

Maren had tried to scrub it out—boiled vinegar in every room, scraped the ledger-shelves down to bare wood, pried up floorboards that had soaked in brine for a generation—but the smell clung like memory. Hazel had stopped noticing it months ago. Now, standing at the front of what had been the counting-room, chalk dust on his sleeves and forty faces turned toward him across borrowed benches, he caught it again and found he didn't mind. Salt was honest, Maren said. It remembered what had been done with it. She wanted the smell there, she'd told him once, so no one ever mistook this place for something innocent.

"Again," Hazel said, and held up his hands, empty. "Watch the fingers, not the hands."

He worked the luck-knot slowly: thumb over forefinger, thread crossing twice, the little hitch of tension where the charm caught hold of nothing and made it into something. Forty pairs of fingers fumbled along with him. A baker's widow in the second row got it on the first try and laughed out loud, startled, as her knot glowed a soft butter-yellow and stayed lit. Beside her, a boy of maybe fifteen swore with great feeling when his unraveled for the fourth time.

"It's not strength," Hazel said. "It's not talent. It's attention. The fire doesn't care who you are. That was the whole lie—that it cared, that you had to be chosen, sworn, licensed, tithed." He let his own knot dim and opened his hand. "You were always allowed. Someone just built a door between you and the permission, and charged you to walk through."

In the back row, by the window, Sebastian sat with three children clustered around him, all of them younger than he was, none of them older than ten. They weren't learning knots. They were learning doors—or rather, the shape of them: how a ward felt from the inside, how to stand at a threshold and know whether the night beyond meant harm. Sebastian had drawn a gatehouse in chalk on the floorboards, complete with a portcullis rendered in careful perspective, and the smallest girl was walking an imaginary patrol around it while he narrated in a low, serious voice that Hazel could hear even across the room.

"—and if the ward hums low, that's tired, that's fine, you just feed it a little warmth. But if it goes *silent*—" Sebastian's voice dropped, and the children leaned in, rapt, "—if it goes silent, you don't wait. You don't check twice. You *run* and you get someone, because silence means someone's already through."

Hazel looked away before the boy could catch him watching. Some lessons Sebastian taught better than Hazel ever could, because Sebastian had learned them the hard way, alone, at twelve, holding a door that the whole world had decided wasn't worth holding.

The afternoon lesson wound down the way lessons did now—in fits and arguments and one small triumph after another. The baker's widow left with her luck-knot still glowing, tucked into her shawl like a coal. The swearing boy left with a scrap of chalk and a promise to practice until his fingers bled, which Hazel privately doubted but publicly encouraged. The tithe-house filled and emptied the way a kitchen fills and empties, people coming for what they needed and leaving with more than they'd asked for.

Maren appeared in the doorway of the old counting-room as the last students filed out, a ledger under her arm—the new kind, the kind she kept now, though she still wrote in the same merciless hand.

"The miller's wife says your knots are too slow," she reported. "She wants you to teach the fast version."

"The fast version is how you burn your fingers off."

"She said to tell you she's burned worse things for less reason."

Hazel smiled. "Tell her Thursday."

Maren leaned against the doorframe and looked out over the emptying benches, and Hazel watched her do the thing she always did at this hour: the accounting. Not of coin—there was hardly any coin anymore, and what there was moved without ledgers—but of the other thing. Who had come. Who hadn't. Which villages on her old collection routes had sent learners and which had sent nothing, which hearths had been relit and which were still cold, still waiting, still owed.

"Three more from Duncarrow," she said finally. "And a letter from the coast. The fisher-folk want a ward-keeper trained among them. Properly trained. They're asking for Sebastian."

"He's twelve."

"They're asking anyway." Maren's mouth twisted—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "I told them we'd discuss it when he's thirteen. I've learned you can put almost anything off a year if you say it like a tithe schedule."

From the back of the hall came a crash, then Sebastian's voice, urgent and mortified: "It's fine! It's fine, the chalk's fine, don't touch the portcullis—"

They both looked. The smallest girl had knocked over the chalk gatehouse and was near tears; Sebastian was already down on his knees rebuilding it, showing her how the walls went back together, how a broken ward was just a ward that hadn't been mended yet.

"There's a sentence," Hazel said quietly, "that would have gotten you hanged two years ago."

"A lot of sentences would have." Maren pushed off the doorframe. "Come on. Bread won't bake itself, and tonight of all nights I want it in the oven before dark."

* * *

Tonight of all nights.

Everyone knew why, though nobody said it plainly, because saying it plainly felt like tempting fate, and the country had spent enough years under sealed skies to be superstitious about open ones. But the almanacs agreed, and the fire-priests—freed, unchained, blinking in daylight like men released from a long illness—had done their calculations and confirmed it: tonight, for the first time since the wards fell, since the Court fell, since everything fell and everything began again, the stars would come back.

Not all at once. The dragon-fire flooding free through the land had burned bright and wild for months, and its light had washed out the sky the way a bonfire washes out a candle. But it was settling now, sinking into the soil, into the wells, into the luck-knots and door-wards and bread-ovens of a nation learning to own its own inheritance. And as it settled, the old lights were emerging behind it, one by one, like guests arriving at a feast they'd been locked out of.

Maren baked the way she did everything: precisely, furiously, and while lecturing. Hazel ground the grain because his hands were good for grinding and bad for sitting still. Sebastian was banished from the kitchen after the flour incident—which everyone had agreed never to describe aloud—and instead sat on the doorstep of the old tithe-house with the smallest girl from his class, teaching her ward-sense on the garden gate while the smell of baking bread rolled over them both.

"You're hovering," Maren said, without looking up from her dough.

"I'm supervising."

"You're hovering. Sit down. Knead. If you're going to be restless, be useful-restless."

He sat. He kneaded. The kitchen of the former tithe-house was warm and close and smelled of yeast and woodsmoke, and through the window Hazel could see the light going strange outside—that particular deepening, that particular clearing, as if the whole sky were being wiped clean by an enormous patient hand.

"Do you remember," Maren said suddenly, "the first night? On the road. When you wouldn't make a fire because you were afraid the charm-light would draw wraiths, and Sebastian cried because he was cold, and I told you both you were idiots?"

"I remember you telling us we were idiots."

"You were. Both of you. Freezing in a ditch out of pride and duty respectively." She folded the dough, punched it down, folded it again. "I'd collected tithes from that ditch's village, you know. Twelve years running. I knew exactly how much they'd paid and exactly how cold their hearths ran, and I sat there in my collector's coat thinking: I am the most hated woman in this province, and these two fools are the only people who've shared a fire with me in a decade." Her hands stilled. "I thought about going back, that first night. Handing myself over. Saying the whole thing was my fault and letting the Court sort me out."

"What stopped you?"

"Sebastian snored." She said it flatly, but her eyes were bright. "This terrible little whistle, like a kettle deciding whether to commit. And I thought: no. Whatever I owe, I don't owe it to them. I owe it forward. There's a difference, and it took me thirty years and one collapsing border to learn it."

The bread went into the oven. The light outside went on deepening, and deepening, and then—

Sebastian's voice, from the doorstep, cracked straight down the middle:

"Hazel. *Hazel.* Come *look*."

* * *

They came out with flour on their hands and didn't care.

The sky over the eastern hills had begun to salt itself with light. One star first—a hard white point above the ridge, so sudden and so bright that the smallest girl shrieked—and then another, and another, and then dozens, hundreds, the whole slow avalanche of them pouring back into a sky that had been empty for longer than anyone living could remember. The dragon-fire's glow was fading beneath them, sinking gently into the earth, making room. Making way.

People came out of their houses all down the street. Doors opened along the whole valley road, and Hazel could see, far off, other villages doing the same, little constellations of lamplight answering the real ones overhead. Nobody cheered, at first. There was a silence like the inside of a held breath, a whole country looking up together.

Then somewhere a bell rang, and somewhere else a fiddle started, badly, and the night broke open into noise.

Sebastian stood in the middle of the road with his head tipped all the way back, and he was crying without any attempt to hide it, and Hazel put a hand on his shoulder and let him. The boy had kept a door for a world with no stars in it. He had earned this the way stone earns a river.

"First Hearth," Sebastian said thickly, when he could speak. "Do you think it can see them? From in there—I mean—from where it is now. In everything. Do you think it knows?"

"I think," Hazel said, "it's the reason they're back. So yes. I think it knows."

Maren came out with the bread wrapped in cloth, still steaming, and broke it right there in the road because there was no time for ceremony and no desire for any. Three pieces—one large, one large, one larger, because growing boys required tribute—and they ate standing up, under the returning sky, crumbs falling on the dirt of a street that belonged to no court and no crown and no ledger at all.

"To the salt," Maren said, raising her piece of bread, deadpan. "May it remember everything, and may we do better anyway."

"To the door," Sebastian said.

Hazel looked at his—his family, the word fit now, it had fit for months, he'd simply been waiting for the sky's permission—and raised his bread to the two of them, and to the fire in every hearth in every window down the valley, and to the sword-shaped absence at his hip that no longer ached.

"To staying," he said.

They ate. The stars came home. And far to the north, past the hills, past the drowned flats, a light answered the lights above it—steady, patient, unmistakably alive—and Sebastian saw it first, because Sebastian always watched the doors.

"That's not a star," he said quietly. "That's coming *from* the ground."

Hazel turned. Maren turned. The northern horizon pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Something else had survived the winter. Something was waking.
