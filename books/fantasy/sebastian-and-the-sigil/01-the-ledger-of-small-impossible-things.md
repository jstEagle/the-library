## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossible Things

The dead liked the smell of beeswax.

Jude Underhill had learned this over the years the way one learns anything about neighbors: by observation, by small accommodations, by never once letting on that he noticed them at all. He kept a candle burning on the corner of his workbench—not for light, because the guild cellar had plenty of that from the brass lamps bolted along the walls, and not for warmth, because the cellar was cool in the way cellars are cool regardless of season—but for the smell. Beeswax, apparently, was comforting. It reminded them of temples, or of funerals done properly, or of their mothers' kitchens; it was different for each of them, but it settled the murmuring down to something like a lullaby, and a settled murmuring meant Jude could carve.

He was carving now: a memorial token, three inches of riverbone worn smooth as an old coin. The client was a cooper's widow up on Chandler's Row who had paid him four coppers and a heel of bread—Jude never charged much for these, and sometimes nothing at all—and wanted her husband's name worked into the bone so she could hold it in her fist when the nights got long. *Tomas Merle*, Jude carved, the letters small and even, and beneath the name a little barrel with its staves showing, because Tomas had been proud of his barrels, and the widow had said, *put a barrel on it, he'd like that, vain old stick.*

Tomas himself had not said anything. The dead rarely spoke directly to Jude about themselves. They murmured. They drifted through the walls of the world like smoke through a keyhole, and what they said was mostly fragments—the tail end of arguments begun decades ago, half-remembered recipes, the names of dogs, the first line of songs they could never finish. Tonight, around Jude's bench, the murmuring was gentle. An old woman two streets away was repeating *the blue dress, the blue dress* in a loop as patient as a tide. A drowned man somewhere near the docks was counting. He'd been counting for years. Jude had never learned to what number, because the man always started over.

*Forty-one, forty-two,* said the drowned man, contentedly.

Jude blew bone dust off the token and held it up to the lamplight. Good work, even by his standards. His hands were better than he was, people said. They said it kindly, meaning he was quiet and steady and unremarkable in all ways except the ones at the end of his arms. Jude had spent thirty-one years making sure this was true, and if there was a craft to being unremarkable, then he had mastered it the way other men mastered swordplay or song.

Invisibility, Jude had decided long ago, was safety. Not comfort—he wasn't foolish enough to confuse the two—but safety, which lasts longer.

*You're brooding,* said no one, because the dead did not comment on his moods. That was one mercy among many. They didn't see him any more than the living truly did. He simply happened to be standing where they drifted.

He wrapped the finished token in flannel, tucked it into the drawer where completed commissions waited for delivery, and reached for the next piece of riverbone. Then the bell above the stairwell rang—the low, flat toll that meant the counting-house above was closing its ledgers, which meant Jude had perhaps twenty minutes before Bram Tull came down the steps with his lantern and his list and his voice like a door dragging across stone.

Time to close the night ledgers.

*

The Meridian Guild dealt in impossible things.

That was the joke the apprentices told each other, anyway, usually after their third cup of cheap wine: *we deal in impossible things, and the most impossible thing of all is that anyone pays us.* But it wasn't really a joke. The guild's warehouses held items that made sensible people nervous—a lantern that burned only in the dark (which is to say, went out in daylight), a mirror that showed rooms instead of faces, a jar of last year's rain that fell upward when opened. Most of it was smaller than legend suggested: charms of dubious potency, relics of dubious origin, curiosities priced high and explained away faster. The guild's genius was not in finding impossible things but in making them seem ordinary enough to sell.

And every impossible thing that crossed the guild's threshold passed, eventually, through a ledger. And every ledger passed, eventually, through Jude Underhill.

There were six of them for the night shift: tall books bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, their spines cracked into a geography of use. Jude carried them two at a time to his bench, set the beeswax candle closer, uncapped his ink, and began.

Item by item. Description, provenance, condition, price, disposition. His handwriting was famous within three floors of the guild—small, upright, perfectly legible, the handwriting of a man who had decided early that clarity was the safest form of self-expression. Bram Tull called it "the only tidy thing in the cellar," which was as close to affection as Bram ever came.

The first ledger held the evening's intake from the river barges: eleven crates of salted eels (ordinary), a consignment of dyewood (ordinary), and one crate marked FRAGILE — DO NOT SHAKE containing, per the manifest, "a wind-up bird, brass, sings one song, unknown origin." Jude noted it, priced it provisionally at twelve marks, and moved on.

The second ledger was where things got interesting.

Interesting was a relative term. Interesting meant the guild's specialists—men like Odry Fenwick, whose title was *Appraiser of Peculiarities* and whose actual talent was making up plausible histories for objects that had none—had flagged something for closer cataloging. Tonight there were three such flags, pasted into the margin on slips of yellow paper in Odry's looping hand.

Flag one: a comb carved from whale ivory that, per the seller's oath, "untangles grief." Odry had written beside it: *Probably just a nice comb. Test on someone sad. Low priority.*

Jude wrote the entry out cleanly. Comb, whale ivory, claimed property: emotional untangling. Provenance: unverifiable. Price pending assessment. He did not write what he knew, which was that when he'd brushed his thumb along the comb's teeth during intake, a woman's voice had sighed through his skull—*oh, that's better, oh, thank you*—with the bone-deep relief of someone setting down a weight carried too long. Whether the comb had untangled *her* grief or simply remembered doing so, Jude couldn't say. He had learned not to say. Saying such things led to questions. Questions led to doctors, priests, and worse: attention.

Flag two was a compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost rather than wherever north was. Odry's note: *Points at lost things. Currently pointing at itself? Investigate.*

Jude cataloged it. When his fingers rested on its case, faintly, distantly, he heard a child laughing in a garden that smelled of cut grass, and understood without knowing how that the compass was pointing at the owner's childhood. He wrote: *Compass, anomalous orientation. Note: appears to indicate objects or conditions of personal loss.* That much was defensible. Anyone could have guessed it from Odry's note.

The third flag was the trouble.

It sat alone in a shallow tray lined with wool: a key. Plain iron, older style, the bow shaped like a closed hand. About the length of a finger. Unremarkable in every way except—

Except that the moment Jude had lifted it from the tray at intake, four hours ago, the entire cellar had gone silent. Not quiet. Silent. Every murmur, every drifting fragment, every looping lament of the dead that ordinarily filled Jude's ears like weather—gone. As if the key had drawn a curtain across the world. And in the silence, clear as a struck bell, a single voice had spoken:

*Turn me.*

He had set it down immediately. The murmuring had flooded back like water returning to a drained pond, and Jude had stood there with his heart hammering, telling himself it was nothing, a trick of exhaustion, he'd been carving since dawn.

But keys did not speak. Keys did not silence the dead. And the yellow slip in Odry's looping hand read:

*Key, iron, function unknown. Seller claims it "opens doors that aren't doors." Won't elaborate. Won't take it back, either. Gave me a bad look and left. Recommend secure storage until assessed. Also recommend we don't touch it.*

Jude sat with his pen poised over the ledger and looked at the key in its woolen nest.

The dead were murmuring around him—*the blue dress, the blue dress; forty-three, forty-four; oh, that's better*—and the key lay silent among them like a held breath.

Here was the thing Jude knew, and had known since he was nine years old, and had told no living soul in twenty-two years: the dead were always talking. Not to him, exactly. Just near him, through him, the way weather passes through an open window. He had heard them at his mother's funeral standing very straight with his father's hand heavy on his shoulder, and he had heard them every day since, in every room, in every crowd. He had built a life on the assumption that this was a private madness to be managed, not a gift to be declared. Managed meant: never flinch, never look toward voices no one else hears, never write down what they say. Managed meant: be boring, be competent, be invisible.

So now Jude Underhill, model employee, did what he always did with the impossible: he made it ordinary on paper.

*Key, iron, antique. Bow formed as closed hand. Function: unverified. Seller's claims anecdotal. Recommend storage, Class C.* Class C meant a locked drawer in the deep vault, checked monthly, forgotten promptly. Perfectly ordinary procedure. No flag raised. No specialist summoned. No one touching it, including—especially—himself.

His pen scratched. Around him the dead murmured on, unaware that one small object in the room had, for one terrible moment, hushed them all.

"Underhill."

Jude did not jump. Twenty-two years of practice. He capped his ink in one smooth motion and turned with the bland, attentive face he wore like a second skin.

Bram Tull stood at the foot of the stairs with his lantern and his list. Bram was a wide man built like a filing cabinet, with side-whiskers that suggested strong opinions about hair oil and eyes that suggested strong opinions about everything else. He had supervised the cellar for nineteen years and had never once asked Jude a personal question, a quality Jude valued more than wages.

"The night ledgers," Bram said. "Done?"

"Just closing them now, sir."

"Tidy?"

"Always, sir."

Bram grunted, which in Bram's currency was praise, and shuffled forward to peer at the open page. His whiskers twitched as he read. "This key here. Class C?"

"Function unverified, sir. Seller's claims anecdotal. Seemed best stored quietly until Odry can assess."

"Seemed best," Bram repeated slowly. He looked at Jude. Jude looked back with the face of a man who had merely followed procedure. Inside, a small cold voice whispered: *he knows, he always knows—* and Jude told the voice, as he told all voices, to wait its turn.

"Hm." Bram tapped the page once with a thick finger. "Odry wants a look at it tomorrow. First thing."

Something slid sideways in Jude's chest. "Tomorrow? I'd marked it for the vault tonight, sir. Standard interval—"

"Odry says the seller's come back twice already asking after it. Says if the thing draws that kind of hunger, better to know why sooner than later." Bram shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "Not our problem what it does, Underhill. Our problem is writing down what it *is*. You've written what it is?"

"What it appears to be, sir."

"That'll do. Vault it, then, and mind you—" Bram paused, squinting at the tray across the bench, at the little iron key lying in its wool like something sleeping. "Mind you don't handle it more than needful. Things that get handled get attached. Ask anyone in acquisitions."

*If only you knew, sir,* Jude thought, and aloud said, "I'll use the tongs, sir."

Bram nodded, satisfied, and clumped back up the stairs. His lantern-light shrank, the door at the top thudded shut, and the cellar settled back into lamplight and murmuring.

*Forty-nine,* counted the drowned man, patiently. *Fifty.*

Jude looked at the key for a long time.

Then, because he was Jude Underhill—competent, tidy, invisible—he took up the tongs, lifted the key with the detached care of a man handling someone else's problem, and placed it into a Class C lockbox, and locked the lockbox, and signed the transfer slip in his famous handwriting.

*Key, iron, antique. Storage: vault, shelf nine. Function: unverified.*

The pen hovered. For one heartbeat—one indulgent, dangerous heartbeat—Jude let himself wonder what doors that weren't doors might look like, and what waited behind them, and whether one of those doors might open onto a version of his own head that finally, blessedly, went quiet.

Then he put the pen down, because wondering was how men like him got found out.

*

The walk home took eleven minutes if he took the bridge, fourteen if he took the long way along the canal, and tonight he took the long way, because the canal at night was where the dead were thickest and, strangely, that was where Jude felt least alone.

They gathered there like moths at a lamp: the drowned, mostly, and the old ones who'd lived in tenements along the water and never quite stopped pacing their former evenings. Their murmurs braided together over the black water. *The blue dress. Fifty-one, fifty-two. —and that's why I never forgave him, not once, not even—* A child's voice sang three notes of a skipping rhyme over and over, cheerful as a broken music box.

Jude walked among them with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, and if any living person had glanced at him they'd have seen only a thin, neat man in a carver's apron going home late, unremarkable, safe.

"You're brooding again," he said under his breath, to no one, because the dead couldn't hear him any more than the living truly saw him. That was the arrangement. That was the whole shape of his life: voices everywhere, and none of them his.

He was halfway across the second canal bridge when he felt it—that wrongness, that held-breath silence—rising ahead of him like cold off ice.

The murmuring stopped. All of it. Every voice, every fragment, every patient count. The night went utterly, horribly quiet, quieter than any night had been since he was nine years old, and Jude froze mid-stride on the bridge with his heart slamming against his ribs, because there was only one object in the world that did this, and he had locked it in a box on shelf nine of the guild vault, four miles away.

The silence pressed against his ears like deep water.

And then, out of it, a voice he did not know—a living voice, ragged and running out of breath—came stumbling up the towpath behind him, calling his name.

* * *

How does a stranger know your name? Jude would have years to turn that question over. That night, he only turned around.

A woman staggered out of the dark onto the bridge—an odd figure even by canal-side standards, in a courier's oilskin gone dark with blood, one hand pressed to her side and the other stretched out toward him like a swimmer reaching for a rope. Her boots left prints on the wet boards. Red prints.

"Underhill," she rasped again. "Jude Underhill. Bone-carver. Meridian Guild." She said it like a recitation, like words memorized in a hurry by someone who expected not to live long enough to learn them properly. "Thank the—thank *something*. You're real. The ledger-keeper. She said—the map said—"

"I think you've mistaken me for—"

She collapsed against the bridge rail, and thrust something into his hands, pressing it hard, folding his fingers over it with a strength that surprised him. Wax-sealed. A rolled map, heavy paper, the seal stamped with a mark he almost recognized—something spiraling, something like—

"Listen." Her face was very close to his, gray as tallow, her breath coming wet and short. "The covenant's failing. Do you understand? It's *failing*, and nobody remembers, and they killed—" A cough bent her double. When she straightened, her eyes were fierce and terrified and certain. "Take it. Don't let them have it. Don't let Vess have it. Go to the Spire. The Spire remembers."

"I don't—what Spire? Who are you?"

But the strength was going out of her fingers, sliding off his wrists like water. She sagged down against the rail, and the oilskin bloomed dark across her middle, and Jude knelt in the spreading warmth shouting for help at a canal where no living soul ever walked at midnight, and the woman looked up at him with an expression that was somehow not afraid at all—relieved, if anything, relieved to have gotten it *done*—and whispered:

"The Spire remembers."

Then she died, right there on the bridge, holding the edge of his sleeve.

And Jude—who had spent thirty-one years hearing the dead drift past like weather, who had built an entire life on never answering them, never looking at them, never once letting on—felt her presence linger. Felt it settle beside him on the cold boards, close as a companion, patient as the tide.

For the first time in his life, a ghost looked at him.

And waited for him to speak.
