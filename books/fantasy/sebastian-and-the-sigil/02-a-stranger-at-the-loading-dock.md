## 2. A Stranger at the Loading Dock

The closing bell at the Meridian Guild rang at six o'clock sharp, and Jude Underhill had learned, over nine years of service, to be halfway through locking the ledger room by the second chime. Punctuality was a kind of armor. A man who was always exactly where he was supposed to be, doing exactly what he was supposed to do, attracted no attention whatsoever—and in Jude's experience, attention was the one commodity the guild never traded fairly.

He was carrying the day's final ledger toward the counting office—a slim volume bound in gray calfskin, its spine stamped with the guild's triple-meridian seal—when the dead started up again.

They were always there, of course. The dead murmured around him like wind through wheat, a low constant rustle he'd spent half his life learning to ignore and the other half learning to pretend he couldn't hear. Most of them had nothing urgent to say. They repeated their last thoughts, circled their last regrets, hummed fragments of songs from towns that no longer appeared on any map. The dead were boring, mostly. That was the terrible secret nobody told you: dying didn't make you interesting. It just made you stuck.

*Cold,* said an old man who'd died on the guild steps some forty years ago, every evening at this hour, as reliably as the bell itself. *So cold, and my wife will worry.*

"She stopped worrying in 1187," Jude muttered under his breath, adjusting his grip on the ledger. "I've told you twice."

The old ghost didn't answer, because ghosts never did—not really. They weren't conversations; they were echoes with opinions. But it was a comfort, in its strange way, having someone so predictable. The dead of Meridian Hollow kept better schedules than the living did.

He was passing through the counting office, nodding to Tam Broadstairs—the junior clerk who was always the last to leave because he was always the first to arrive, a young man whose entire personality was arithmetic—when the loading bell clanged. Not the closing bell. The *yard* bell. The one that meant someone was at the outer gate, past hours, wanting in.

Tam looked up from his columns with the expression of a man whose tidy universe has been poked with a stick. "We're closed."

"The bell says otherwise." Jude set the ledger on the counter. He should have let Tam deal with it. That was the sensible thing, the invisible thing—junior clerks handled yard business, that was practically carved into the job description. But something in the way the bell had rung had snagged against something in his chest. It hadn't been pulled properly. It had been *struck*, the way you strike a bell when your hands are shaking too badly to pull a rope.

"I'll see to it," Jude heard himself say. "You finish the tallies."

Tam blinked at him, mildly scandalized. Senior ledgers-men did not see to the yard. But Jude was already moving, and Tam was already returning to his numbers with visible relief, and the moment passed unremarked—which was, Jude would think later, the last easy moment of his old life.

* * *

The guild yard was all shadow and lamplight at dusk, crates stacked like a wooden city, the smell of tallow and river mud and the particular brine of goods that had come through the Veilward docks. Jude crossed it quickly, keys already in hand, rehearsing the speech he gave to late arrivals: *Guild hours are posted. Return at dawn. No exceptions.* He'd given that speech perhaps two hundred times. It required no thought, no feeling, no self.

Then he reached the gate and saw the courier.

She was slumped against the ironwork, and for one merciful heartbeat Jude thought she was simply drunk—Meridian saw its share of drunk couriers, drunk sailors, drunk everything. Then the lamplight found her side and showed him the dark spread of it, soaking through her riding coat, glistening on the cobbles where she'd left a trail across half the yard like the world's most dreadful signature.

She was maybe thirty. Gray-eyed, hollow-cheeked, road-worn down to the bone in the way of people who had ridden too hard for too long. One boot was missing. Her hands were pressed flat against her wound as if she could hold herself together by will alone, and when she lifted her head and looked at Jude, she did not look confused or frightened or pleading.

She looked *relieved*, which was somehow worse.

"Underhill," she rasped.

Jude's blood went cold in a very specific way. Not the cold of fear—fear he knew, fear he could file away. This was colder. "I'm sorry, madam, but I don't—"

"You're Underhill." She laughed, wet and thin. "Bone-carver. Ledger-man. Third window, second floor. You keep your ink pot on the *left*, which drives old Hessel mad." Her voice was fraying at the edges like rope dragged over stone. "I've been looking for you for eleven days."

"Madam, you need a surgeon—" He stepped forward, hands out, already composing the story he'd tell Tam: unknown courier, arrived wounded, sent for the watch, none of it his affair—

"No surgeons." Her hand shot out and caught his wrist with a strength that astonished him, and then she pressed something into his palm, folding his fingers over it with her own bloody ones. "No time. They're behind me. Vess's people. Listen—listen—" She swayed, caught herself on the gate. "The covenant is failing. Do you understand me? The covenant. It's not a story. It was never a story."

Jude knew what she was holding against his palm before he looked down: a scroll case, wax-sealed, the wax stamped with a mark he didn't recognize—a circle broken by three descending lines, like a sigil falling down a staircase into darkness. The case was warm. Warmer than it should have been, warm as a held hand, and beneath the warmth he felt something else, faint as a heartbeat through a wall.

Something inside the case was *humming*.

"You take this to—" The courier's breath hitched. Her grip was loosening. "To the Spire. Before equinox. Find Oduya—Maren Oduya, canal-side, she'll hate it but she'll help. And Underhill—" Her eyes fixed on his, and in them he saw something that made the hair rise on his arms: recognition. Not of his face. Of something under his face. "You carry it too. I can see it. You've been *hiding* it—"

"I don't know what you mean," Jude said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him, because inside he was suddenly screaming. *Hiding what? Who are you? How do you know about my desk?*

"The Spire remembers," she whispered. Her eyes had gone past him now, unfocused, seeing the yard or something beyond it. "The Spire remembers, and so does the blood, and yours remembers, it *remembers*, oh—" And then, softer, almost wondering, as though she'd finally been given permission to stop running: "Oh. There you are."

"There who is?" Jude asked—but even as he said it, he heard her.

Not with his ears. With the other place, the place behind his sternum where the dead lived. Her voice was coming apart even as her body failed, fraying into echo, and yet within it he caught one clear thread, one last coherent thought, offered up the way the dying offer things—unwrapped, unhidden, true:

*I never told anyone my name. Clever girl. Keep it safe. Keep it safe until you need it.*

And beneath that, fading, fading: *my mother's garden, I never got back to my mother's garden—*

The courtyard went very quiet. Even the old man on the steps had stopped saying *cold.*

Jude knelt beside her on the cold cobbles, holding a humming scroll case in one bloody hand, and did the only thing he knew how to do with death, which was nothing. He had stood beside enough of it—heard enough of it seeping out of strangers, out of hospital rooms he'd walked past, out of the walls of old houses—to know that his presence changed nothing. He was a witness who could not testify. A door that opened onto nothing anyone wanted.

But this time, for the first time since he was nine years old, the silence after a death was not silent.

Because she stayed.

* * *

The dead moved on. That was the rule, the one mercy in the whole arrangement: whatever lingered in Jude's hearing, it was echo, residue, repetition. The actual person went wherever people went, and what remained was a recording played in an empty room. Nine years at the guild, thirty years alive, and Jude could count on one hand the times a voice had lasted more than a few days after death, and never—never—had one lasted with anything resembling *awareness*.

This one followed him across the yard.

He didn't realize it at first. He raised the alarm—shouted for Tam, sent for the watch, performed the whole theater of a shocked clerk discovering a corpse, and he was genuinely shocked, but the performance was real in a way that disturbed him more than the death had—and while Tam ran white-faced for the constable, Jude stood under the lamp with his hands folded neatly, invisibly, at his back, hiding the scroll case up his sleeve the way he'd hide a miscounted column.

*Third shelf,* said a voice directly beside his ear, warm and dry and amused. *You keep your spare gloves on the third shelf. Left side. You alphabetize your bones by donor name. That's either very respectful or very strange, carver.*

Jude did not jump. Jumping was attention. He turned instead, slowly, toward the empty air beside the lamp post, and said, in the flattest voice he owned: "It's respectful."

Tam, twenty feet away and sprinting, did not look back. Of course he didn't. Tam had heard nothing. Nobody ever heard anything.

The watch came. Two constables and a surgeon who pronounced the woman dead of a blade wound and noted, professionally, that she'd been dead some minutes before anyone found her. They asked Jude questions. He answered them in the voice he used for auditors—precise, bland, forgettable. Unknown to him. Arrived at the gate. Died at once. Tragic.

*You lied beautifully,* the voice observed, somewhere near his left shoulder. *Except your pulse. Your pulse is a disaster. I could hear it from the gate.*

*I don't know what you are,* Jude thought back, and immediately hated himself for thinking back, for answering, for engaging, because engaging was how it started, engaging was how a gift became a reputation and a reputation became a cage—or worse, a pyre. People drowned for less than what he was. In certain parishes they still burned.

*What I am,* said the voice, and there was something underneath the amusement now, something tired and sad and stubborn, *is late. Eleven days late. And dead, which is inconvenient. And stuck to you, apparently, which is stranger. I tried letting go, carver. I drifted off toward... wherever, and then you picked up the case, and here I am, anchored like a boat to a dock. You're the dock. Congratulations. You're everyone's dock.*

Jude walked home. He lived three streets east of the guild hall, above a chandler's shop, in rooms so deliberately unremarkable that visitors sometimes forgot his address while standing in it. He climbed his stairs. He lit his lamp. He sat down at his workbench among his neat rows of bone blanks and his labeled tools, and he took the scroll case out of his coat, and he set it on the wood, and he stared at it for a long time.

The humming had not stopped. If anything, in the quiet of his rooms, it was louder—not sound, exactly, but pressure, the way a storm announces itself through aches in old scars. The wax seal gleamed dully. Three lines descending into a dark circle.

And on his left wrist, beneath his sleeve, beneath the leather cuff he had worn every single day since he was nine years old, something answered the humming. A faint warmth. A faint itch. An old scar, smooth and pale, shaped like nothing—or so he had told himself for twenty-one years, shaped like *nothing*, just a birthmark scar, just one more thing to hide.

*Open it,* said the dead woman. She was sitting on the edge of his workbench now—he refused to look, refused to confirm, because confirming made it real and real things required decisions—but he could feel her there, weightless, patient, watching him with those gray eyes that had seen whatever lived under his skin. *Or don't. Starve in your little room with your alphabetized bones and your cuff buttoned up tight. But the map doesn't care about your comfort, Underhill, and neither does the thing hunting it. Vess's riders found me outside Corvane's Reach. They'll find the trail by morning. You have perhaps one night of being invisible left. Spend it wisely.*

"My name isn't Underhill to you," Jude said quietly. "You said 'carver.'"

*Everyone's a carver,* she said, *if you wait long enough. Dust carves canyons. Grief carves faces. Time carves names into stones.* A pause, and when she spoke again the humor had drained away entirely, leaving only the tired, stubborn truth beneath. *I watched you lie to that surgeon tonight. Good lies. Safe lies. You've built a whole life out of good safe lies, and it's a lovely little house, and the covenant is burning down around it. So. One question, bone-carver, and then I'll leave you to your lamp:*

She leaned close, and though she cast no breath upon his skin, Jude shivered as though a winter draft had touched him.

*When you were nine years old, and you heard her calling for you—you ran. I know. I've seen it in you, it's stitched through everything you are. So tell me, Jude Underhill:* *how long do you plan to keep running?*

The lamp flickered. Outside, far off toward the river, a church bell tolled the hour—one stroke past midnight, into the first morning of the rest of his carefully invisible life.

Jude sat very still for a long moment. Then, with hands that were not quite steady, he unclasped the leather cuff from his wrist, laid it on the workbench beside the humming scroll case, and reached for the wax seal.

The dead woman smiled—or seemed to—and settled in to watch.

*That's the spirit,* she said softly. *The Spire remembers. Let's find out what you do.*
