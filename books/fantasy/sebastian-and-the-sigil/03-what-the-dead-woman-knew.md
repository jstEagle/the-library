## 3. What the Dead Woman Knew

The dead woman's blood was still on the loading dock when Captain Vess came for him.

Jude had spent the hours since closing bell doing what he always did when the world went wrong: he worked. He'd scrubbed the stones himself, though that was the yard-boys' job, because he hadn't been able to bear the sight of it. He'd balanced the night ledgers to the copper. He'd sharpened his carving knives, oiled them, put them away. He had made himself into a picture of a man with nothing unusual in his life at all, and then Vess had walked through his door without knocking, and the picture had cracked straight down the middle.

"Underhill." She said his name like she was reading it off an inventory. "Captain Vess. Guild enforcement."

"I know who you are." Everyone knew who Vess was. She had a face like a closed ledger and a reputation like an open one—the kind of enforcer who never raised her voice because she never needed to. She stood in the doorway of his little counting room with her hands folded behind her back, taking in the shelves of ledgers, the bone shavings in their bin, the single candle he kept burning past midnight because the dark made the voices louder.

"You found the courier," she said.

"I heard something in the yard." That much was true. "I went out. There was a woman. She was hurt."

"Hurt how?"

"Badly." His mouth had gone dry. He reached for the ink pot just to have something to do with his hands. "I don't— I'm not a physiker, Captain. I called for help. By the time anyone came, she was gone."

"Gone." Vess let the word sit there between them like a stone dropped in still water. "And did she say anything? Before she was gone?"

Here it was. Jude had rehearsed this moment in his head perhaps forty times since the yard, and every rehearsal had gone differently, and none of them had prepared him for the way her eyes didn't blink.

"No," he said.

It came out too fast. It came out about half a second too fast, and half a second too high, and he watched—with the peculiar clarity of a man watching his own house burn—Captain Vess register all of it.

"No," she repeated.

"She was dying," Jude said. "People say things when they're dying. Mostly it's nothing. Names. Prayers. 'Tell my wife.'" He managed a small, apologetic shrug, the shrug of a man who dealt in numbers and small impossible objects and certainly not in wax-sealed maps currently hidden inside a hollowed-out ledger spine three feet from where she was standing. "There wasn't time for anything important."

"Mm." Vess crossed the room without hurrying. She looked at the ledgers. She looked at the bin of bone shavings. She looked, for one long moment, at the shelf where the hollowed ledger sat, and Jude's heart performed a maneuver he was fairly sure would have killed a weaker man.

"You've been with the guild nine years," she said.

"Eight. Nearly nine."

"Never late. Never sick. Never once flagged in an audit." She turned to face him. "Do you know what we call clerks like you, Underhill?"

He did not say *invisible*, though the word rose in his throat like bile. "Reliable?"

"We call them nothing," Vess said. "That's rather the point of them." She moved toward the door, then paused with her hand on the frame. "If the courier said anything—if she gave you anything—I'll know. Not tonight. Maybe not tomorrow. But paper leaves traces, Underhill. People leave traces. Even quiet ones." Her eyes flicked to the bone shavings, then back to his face. "*Especially* quiet ones."

Then she was gone, and Jude sat very still in his chair until the candle guttered, listening to the dead woman's voice saying, faintly and patiently, from somewhere near the window: *You lie like a man reading from a bad script.*

"I know," he whispered.

*It was terrible,* she agreed. *I've seen better lying from children caught stealing pies.*

"Thank you," he said bitterly, "for your constructive feedback."

*Anytime.*

---

He waited until the third bell past midnight, when even the night porters had gone to their bunks and the guild hall settled into the particular silence of a building full of sleeping ambition. Then he took down the hollowed ledger, pried out the map with hands that were steadier than he expected them to be, and carried it to the candle.

It was smaller unrolled than he'd imagined—a rectangle of good vellum, soft at the edges from handling, sealed originally under a blob of green wax stamped with a mark he didn't recognize. Someone had drawn on it in iron-gall ink: a rough sketch of streets he half knew, leading out of the city's south quarter toward the marshes and beyond, toward mountains drawn as a child might draw them, all jagged enthusiasm and no scale. And at the top, dominating everything, someone had drawn a spire.

Not a tower exactly. A spire like a needle, like a splinter driven up through the skin of the world, wrapped round and round with lines—dozens of them, hundreds, coiling from its base to its tip in patterns that hurt slightly to look at, the way bright snow hurts.

A sigil. He knew enough of sigilwork to know that's what it was, though no sigilwright alive drew like this anymore; Maren Oduya's disgraced work in the guild registry looked like a child's chalk scrawl beside it.

But none of that—not the spire, not the impossible linework, not the route marked in careful stages with dates beside them—none of that was what stopped Jude's breath.

What stopped his breath was the small mark at the map's lower corner. The cartographer's seal, maybe. A signature. A curling, interlocked design no bigger than his thumbnail, drawn in a single unbroken line.

Jude pushed back his sleeve.

The scar on his wrist—he'd had it since birth, his mother told him once, before she stopped talking about such things; a birthmark shaped wrong, the midwife had said, like a knot in rope—was the same design. Exactly. Every curl. Every intersection. As if someone had taken the mark off his skin and set it in ink, or taken the ink and pressed it into his skin before he was born.

The candle flame leaned sideways in a draft that wasn't there.

*Now you see it,* the dead woman said. She was closer now. He could feel her the way you feel someone standing at your shoulder in a crowd—not sound, not quite, but pressure, attention, the sense of being *regarded*. *Took you long enough. I pressed it into your hands and you hid it in a book. Very you.*

"What are you?" he asked the empty room, and hated how his voice shook.

*Dead,* she said, with dry patience. *We've covered this. The question is what* you *are, bone-carver. And I think—"*

The voice cut off. Not faded—cut, like a door slammed mid-sentence—and the pressure at his shoulder vanished so completely that Jude gasped at the absence of it.

For a moment the room was only a room. Then, from somewhere below—a floor down, two floors, the yard itself—he felt it again. Fainter. Moving. As if she were pacing the guild hall's corridors, learning its shape. As if she had decided, with the same stubbornness that had carried a dying courier across half the city, that she was not going anywhere.

Jude looked at the map. He looked at his wrist. He thought about Vess's parting words—*even quiet ones*—and about the word *covenant*, which he had copied into a ledger four years ago beside a confiscated box of sigil-paper and which had nagged at him ever since like a splinter under a nail.

He should burn it. That was the sensible thing. Feed it to the candle, sweep up the ash, go back to being nothing, because nothing was safe, nothing survived, nothing got visited in the night by captains with unblinking eyes.

His hands, entirely without consulting him, began rolling the map back up instead.

"Oh, stop it," he told them.

They did not stop.

---

He didn't sleep. Around the fourth bell he gave up pretending and sat by the window with the map across his knees, tracing the route with one finger. South quarter. Marshes. Gray Pass. The Spire. Dates beside each stage in a cramped, urgent hand—the last date circled twice: autumn equinox. Eleven days off.

*She planned it well,* the dead woman said. She had drifted back sometime in the dark hours, settling near him again like a cat returning to a warm chair. *Odile plans everything well. Planned. Tenses are difficult when you're me.*

"Odile," Jude repeated. The name arrived in his mouth and stayed there, warm and strange, like a coal passed hand to hand.

*I didn't tell you that yet,* she said, and there was something new in her voice—not the dry commentary, but something underneath it, surprised and a little raw. *Huh. You're not just hearing me. You're* reading *me. That's keeper-blood for you. My grandmother could do that. Said it was like having her pockets picked by polite thieves.*

"I'm not anything," Jude said automatically. "I keep ledgers."

*You keep ledgers,* Odile agreed, *and you talk to ghosts in your counting room at midnight, and you've got a covenant-seal growing out of your wrist like it owns the place. Keep telling yourself you're nothing, bone-carver. You're very convincing.* A pause. *You're not, though. For the record. You're the worst liar I've met in thirty years of courting dangerous parcels.*

"Why are you still here?" he asked. It came out harsher than he meant. "The dead don't—they move on. They fade. They don't follow clerks around making comments about their lying."

Silence, for a moment. When she spoke again, the dryness had dropped away entirely.

*Because I died carrying that map to someone who could use it,* she said, *and I don't know yet if I succeeded. Because the last time I trusted the guild, people I loved paid for it. And because—"*

Again the cut-off. Again the sudden absence. But this time, before it took her, he caught the shape of the last words, the way you catch a glimpse of a face in a passing coach:

*—because you're one of us, and I am not leaving you alone with this.*

The candle burned down. Outside, the city turned gray toward morning, and somewhere below, Captain Vess was almost certainly awake, and patient, and thinking about quiet men.

Jude sat in the window with a dead woman's map on his knees and a seal on his wrist that matched the drawing at its corner, and understood, with dread and—worse, far worse—with a small, treacherous spark of curiosity, that whatever he had been for eight years and eleven months of careful, invisible competence, he could not be that man anymore.

He could not unknow what he was.

Which left only one question worth asking, and it wasn't whether to run or hide or burn the map.

It was whose name appeared in the old ledgers beside the word *covenant*—because he was fairly sure he remembered, and he was almost certain it belonged to a drunk.

Maren Oduya. Canal-side tenements. Disgraced.

Jude looked at the gray light, at the map, at the place where a ghost's attention rested on his shoulder like a hand.

"Well," he said to no one, to everyone, to her. "I suppose you're coming along."

*Obviously,* said Odile.
