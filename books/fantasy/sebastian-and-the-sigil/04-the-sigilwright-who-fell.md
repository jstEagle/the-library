## 4. The Sigilwright Who Fell

The ledger entry had been waiting for him for three days, patient as a spider.

Jude found it on the second night after Odile's death, when he should have been sleeping and instead sat in his narrow room above the counting house with the map unrolled across his knees and a single candle burning low. He had copied out every name, every date, every notation from the courier's route—habit more than hope, the bone-carver's instinct to work a problem with his hands—and somewhere past midnight his pen had stopped over an old guild record he'd transcribed years ago and never thought about twice.

*Maren Oduya — sigilwright, third class. Discharged. See: covenant (suppressed).*

*See: covenant.*

Two words in a margin, written by some clerk's careful hand and then struck through so hard the nib had torn the paper. Someone at the Meridian Guild had wanted that word gone badly enough to damage the ledger doing it. And someone else—Jude himself, three years younger and blissfully ignorant—had dutifully copied the damaged page into the archive without ever asking why a sigilwright's discharge would reference something that officially did not exist.

He looked down at the map, at the sigil inked in faded violet at its heart, and then at his own wrist, where the same mark lived in his skin like a scar he'd never earned.

"Well," he said to the empty room, because talking to himself was safer than listening to what else might answer. "That's not ominous at all."

From the corner, where she had taken up a sort of residence the way a cat claims a sunbeam, Odile's ghost made a sound that was almost a laugh. She'd grown more solid since the loading dock—or perhaps Jude was simply getting better at seeing her, which felt like a far more dangerous development. Tonight he could make out the frayed hem of her courier's coat, the mud still dried on her boots, the way her head tilted when she was amused.

"You know her," Jude said. It wasn't quite a question. The dead were rarely subtle once you started listening; their wants leaked out of them like water through a cracked bowl.

Odile's expression went carefully blank, which for a dead woman was as good as a confession.

"You *know* her," Jude repeated, "and you didn't say anything."

She pointed—not at him, but past him, toward the window, toward the canal district beyond the rooftops. Then she pressed one translucent hand flat against her chest, over her heart, and mouthed something he couldn't yet catch. Her voice came to him only in fragments, like words heard through a wall: *...told her... sorry...*

"Wonderful," Jude muttered. "A ghost with unfinished business and a grudge-adjacent apology. That always ends well."

But he was already reaching for his coat.

* * *

The canal district smelled the way it always did in late summer: green water, tar, frying onions, and the particular sweetness of rot that everyone who lived there had long ago agreed not to notice. Jude had spent exactly one year of his apprenticeship in these tenements, copying invoices for a fish-oil merchant, and he had left with the firm conviction that he was a stairs-and-ledgers sort of person, not a balconies-over-open-sewage sort of person. Life, it appeared, disagreed.

Maren Oduya's building leaned over the Tallowmere Canal at an angle that suggested it had lost an argument with gravity sometime in the last decade and simply decided to live with the result. The stairwell smelled of lamp oil and old smoke. On the third floor, a door hung ajar, and through the gap Jude could hear singing—badly, cheerfully, and with great commitment to all the wrong notes.

He knocked. The singing stopped.

"Go away," said a voice like gravel soaked in honey. "I'm closed. I'm always closed. I'm professionally closed."

"Madam Oduya? My name is Jude Underhill. I keep ledgers for the Meridian Guild, and I—"

The door flew open.

The woman in the doorway was perhaps fifty, dark-skinned, silver-streaked hair escaping a bun that had clearly surrendered hours ago. She wore a man's shirt buttoned wrong and an expression of such concentrated hostility that Jude took an involuntary step backward. In her right hand she held a bottle by the neck, and in her left, impossibly fast, a small knife that had come from nowhere.

"Meridian Guild," she repeated. "You have some nerve, boy. You have some absolute, Olympian, gold-plated nerve, coming to my door wearing their pin."

Jude glanced down at the small brass badge on his lapel—the one he usually forgot he wore, the one that meant *harmless functionary, nothing to see here.* "It's just a job," he said. "I'm not—they don't tell me anything. I copy pages. I'm very good at copying pages and completely useless at everything else, if that helps."

"It doesn't." But the knife lowered an inch. Her eyes—bloodshot, clever, and currently operating at perhaps half their usual power—narrowed. "Why are you here?"

"I need a sigilwright."

"You need a priest. Or a bath. Possibly both." She started to close the door.

"The word 'covenant,'" Jude said quickly. "In your discharge papers. Someone tried to strike it out of the ledger, but they missed my copy."

The door stopped moving.

For a long moment Maren Oduya stood perfectly still, and Jude watched something happen behind her eyes—a war between the drunk woman and someone older and sharper, fought quickly and brutally beneath the surface. When she spoke again, the gravel-honey voice had gone quiet and level.

"How much have you had to drink tonight?"

"Nothing. I don't—drink makes the voices louder."

Her face did something complicated. "Voices."

"The dead," Jude said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it sounded mad even to him, and he'd spent thirty years being carefully, exhaustively sane about it. "Look, I know how it sounds. I've hidden it my whole life. I carve bones and I hear the people the bones belonged to, and I keep my head down, and until two nights ago that was working fine. And then a dying courier gave me a map with this on it—"

He pushed back his sleeve and held out his wrist.

Maren Oduya dropped the bottle.

It shattered on the floorboards, gin spreading in a slow amber tide, and she didn't even glance at it. She caught Jude's arm with both hands—her grip shockingly strong for a woman who could barely stand straight—and turned his wrist toward the gray light from the window, and Jude watched the drunkenness burn off her face like fog off morning water.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "That mark died with the keepers. I inscribed the memorial sigils myself. I *carried* them. There were nine, and I buried—" She stopped. Her thumb moved over the mark on his skin, gentle now, tracing lines that Jude had spent his whole life pretending were only a birthmark. "This isn't inked. This isn't scarred. This is *grown*. Who are you?"

"Jude Underhill. Bone-carver. Ledger clerk." He swallowed. "Orphan. My parents died when I was six. Nobody ever told me what they did."

Something shifted in her expression—the sharpness cracking along a fault line of something older and sorer. "Nobody told you," she said slowly, "because nobody who knew is alive. Except me." She let go of his wrist as though it had burned her, stepped back into the room, and stood amid the wreckage of her evening with her arms folded, looking at the broken bottle as if it owed her money. "Come in, then. Mind the glass. And if you're going to ruin my life, at least do it inside where the neighbors can't watch."

* * *

The room beyond the door was smaller than Jude's, and messier, and infinitely more interesting. Every surface bore evidence of a craft abandoned mid-thought: chalk diagrams half-erased on the walls, a workbench crowded with cold solder and jars of powdered metals, a stack of wooden practice boards each carved with sigils that seemed to squirm slightly when Jude wasn't looking directly at them. One entire corner was given over to bottles, arranged with more care than anything else in the room—which, Jude thought, said something sad and eloquent about priorities.

Odile drifted in behind him and settled near the window, watching Maren with an intensity that made the air feel thicker. Jude pretended not to notice. He was getting alarmingly good at pretending not to notice things.

"Maren Oduya," she said, dropping onto a stool and gesturing for him to take the chair opposite, which wobbled ominously. "Third-class sigilwright, formerly of the Meridian Guild, currently of nowhere in particular. And you're the clerk who copies things."

"I'm the clerk who copies things," Jude agreed. "Which is how I found you. Your discharge file references the covenant. Someone tried to erase it."

"Corvane's people." She said the name flatly, without explanation, and something in her tone told Jude it would be explained eventually, whether he wanted it or not. "Guildmaster Corvane has been striking the word from every record in the archive for twenty years. Slowly. Carefully. A page here, a ledger there. Do you know how much paperwork it takes to unmake a piece of history?" She laughed, short and bitter. "I do. I helped, at first. Before I understood what we were erasing."

"And what were we erasing?"

Maren looked at him for a long time. Then she got up, crossed to the workbench, and came back with a rag and a small tin of solvent, and began cleaning the gin off her hands with the focus of a woman buying herself time.

"The world has a floor," she said finally. "Did you know that? Underneath everything—the city, the marshes, the sea—there's another place. The Hollow. Not hell. Not heaven. Just... underneath. Where the dead go when they finish here, and where other things live that never started here at all. And the floor between us and them isn't stone. It's an agreement. A covenant, kept by bloodlines sworn to maintain it, renewed at the Threshold Spire every generation since anyone can remember." She set down the rag. "Nine keeper families. Nine marks. Yours is one of them."

"My parents," Jude said. His voice came out strange.

"Your parents were keepers, boy. Which means either you're the last of your line, or—" She stopped herself, and Jude watched her decide not to say whatever came next. "Where did you get the map?"

"A dying courier. She was attacked in the guild yard. She pressed it into my hands and said—" His throat tightened. "*The Spire remembers.*"

Maren went very still. "Describe her."

"Dark hair. Courier's leathers, gray and green. A scar through her left eyebrow. She—"

"*Odile Vane,*" Maren breathed, and beside the window, the ghost sat up straight. "Odile Vane was one of the nine. Keeper-blood, like you. She carried the renewal map because she was the fastest and the most careful, and because the others trusted her with their lives. Eleven years ago the covenant was due for renewal, and Odile rode out with the map, and she never arrived. And the Spire stayed silent, and the covenant began to fail, and the Guild covered it up because Corvane—" Her jaw worked. "Because Corvane arranged the whole thing. I found proof. I took it to the wrong person, and three days later my workshop burned, my certification burned, and my name went into a ledger next to a word that doesn't exist."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a barge horn moaned on the canal.

"She followed me home," Jude said at last. It sounded absurd. He said it anyway. "Her ghost. She's been following me since the yard. She's here right now—by the window. Gray coat, mud on her boots. She keeps trying to tell me something but I can't hear her properly. I hear the dead like weather—all of them, all the time, a kind of noise. But her..." He shook his head. "She comes through in pieces."

Maren stared at the space by the window. Jude realized, with a lurch, that she couldn't see Odile—that he was describing empty air to a stranger, and that this was precisely the moment most people would begin edging toward the door.

Instead, Maren Oduya picked up the gin bottle she'd apparently retrieved from somewhere during his explanation, looked at it, and set it down on the table between them, deliberately, like a card player folding a hand.

"Eleven years I've drunk myself stupid trying to forget that I failed those people," she said. "And now a clerk walks in wearing a keeper's mark with a dead keeper's map, telling me her ghost is standing in my parlor." She rubbed her face with both hands. "What does she look like? Is she angry?"

"No. Sad. Mostly sad. And—" Jude hesitated, then committed. "Just now, when I said your name to her outside, she put her hand over her heart and said something like *told her, sorry.*"

Maren's breath caught. For a moment the sigilwright looked ten years older and ten years more fragile, and then she squared her shoulders, and Jude recognized the gesture—it was the same one he made every morning in the counting house mirror, the small physical lie of *I am fine, I am functional, no one needs to know otherwise.*

"Odile Vane once talked me out of resigning," Maren said quietly. "Told me the Guild needed people inside it who asked questions. Ironic, given how that worked out for both of us." She looked up, and her eyes were clear now—clearer than they'd been since Jude walked in, maybe clearer than they'd been in eleven years. "Right. Here's what happens now. You're going to tell me everything, in order, starting with the loading dock. I'm going to sober up, which will be unpleasant for both of us. And then we're going to read that map properly, because if the covenant's been failing for eleven years, we may already be too late—but I refuse, I absolutely *refuse*, to find out while sitting in this room feeling sorry for myself."

"That sounds," Jude said, "like a plan that ends with enforcers kicking down doors."

"Oh, almost certainly." Maren was already clearing bottles off the workbench with sudden, ferocious energy. "Corvane will have flagged your name the moment Odile died in your vicinity. We have maybe a day, possibly less. So talk fast, carver. And start with the part where a guild clerk decides to trust a disgraced drunk with the most dangerous document in the realm, because I'd genuinely love to know what you were thinking."

Jude considered the question honestly. He thought about thirty years of silence, of keeping his head down, of invisibility as armor. He thought about a dying woman's fingers pressing wax into his palms. He thought about the mark on his wrist, warm now, warmer than it had ever been, as though it knew it had finally come home to someone who could read it.

"I wasn't thinking," he admitted. "That's rather the point. I've spent my whole life thinking, and it got me a small room and a ledger and nobody knowing my name. Two nights ago somebody chose *me*—out of everyone in that yard—to carry something that matters. And I'm tired of being safe."

Maren paused, a bottle in each hand, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't quite read—something between pity and recognition, the look of one exile taking the measure of another.

"Tired of being safe," she repeated. "Gods help us both, then." She set the bottles down and pulled a battered leather case from under the bed, opening it to reveal instruments of silver and black iron laid out in worn velvet. "First lesson, and it's free: that mark on your wrist isn't a birthmark, and it isn't just a sign. It's a *keyhole*, boy. And keys—" she nodded at the map, still rolled in his coat pocket, "—want to be used. The longer you carry it without turning it, the louder it gets. Has it been loud?"

Jude thought of the warmth spreading up his arm at night. Of the dreams of a white spire. Of the way the dead had begun drifting toward him in the streets lately, like moths finding a new lamp.

"Loud," he said.

"Wonderful." Maren selected a stylus and a stick of violet chalk, and her hands, which had trembled when she'd opened the door, were steady as bedrock now. "Then we'd best find out what it opens before someone else does. Now—start talking. From the beginning. And leave nothing out, because I promise you, whatever you think is too strange or too shameful to mention is exactly the part I need to hear."

So Jude talked. About the loading dock, and the blood on the cobblestones, and Captain Vess's questions. About the ledger entry and the struck-through word. About a lifetime of hearing the dead and pretending deafness, told aloud for the first time to a living person who didn't flinch, didn't laugh, and didn't reach for a knife.

And by the window, unnoticed by everyone except the one person in the room who could see her, Odile Vane listened to her own death described, and wept silent, luminous tears—and smiled.
