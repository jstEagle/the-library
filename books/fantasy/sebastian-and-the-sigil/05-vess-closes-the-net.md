## 5. Vess Closes the Net

Maren Oduya had survived three decades in the sigilwright's trade by following one rule: never let anyone see how much you know. It was the knowing that got you ruined, not the doing. The doing could be blamed on tools, on apprentices, on drink. But the knowing—that stayed with you like soot under the fingernails.

So when she heard boots on her stairs at an hour when no honest person climbed stairs, she did what any sensible woman would do. She blew out her lamp, swept the covenant diagram off the table and into the cold hearth ash, and put a bottle of cheap plum brandy in her hand as a prop.

The door came down anyway. Of course it did. Meridian Guild doors came down with a particular courtesy that made it worse—three measured knocks, then splinters.

"Sigilwright Oduya," said a voice she knew from bad years ago. "Captain Vess. We'd like a word about your visitor."

*My visitor.* Maren's mind went cold and quick. Jude Underhill had left through the back not twenty minutes past, map rolled inside his coat, walking with the careful gait of a man who had spent his whole life practicing invisibility. He should have been three streets away by now, swallowed by the evening markets.

Unless he wasn't.

"Visitor?" She took a long, theatrical pull of brandy and let it burn. "Captain, I'm a disgraced drunk in a canal-side tenement. My visitors are rats and regret."

Vess stepped over the broken door. She was younger than Maren remembered, or perhaps just better armored against showing it—a broad-shouldered woman in guild gray, her enforcer's badge polished to a shine that said *I follow orders and sleep well*. Behind her crowded four more of them, lanterns swinging, faces arranged into professional blankness.

"Your name appears in a ledger entry dated nine years ago," Vess said. "Beside the word *covenant*. You'll come with us and explain it."

"Nine years." Maren laughed, and it cost her something to make it sound wet and stupid. "Nine years I've been paying for that word. You know what I was inscribing back then? Wine charms. Love tokens. A commemorative plate for the Fishmongers' Ball. If someone wrote *covenant* next to my name, they were describing my bar tab."

One of the enforcers snorted. Vess didn't.

"We found bone dust in your hearth," Vess said, crouching by the fireplace. "Fresh. And this—" she lifted something from the ash with two fingers, and Maren's stomach dropped through the floorboards, "—is a corner of vellum that hasn't finished burning."

It was nothing. A scrap the size of a thumbnail, singed brown. But Vess held it up to the lantern light the way a jeweler holds a stone, and Maren understood with terrible clarity that the captain was not stupid. Stupid enforcers were manageable. Stupid enforcers could be fed lies like pigeons.

"You were burning papers tonight," Vess said. "Papers you'd kept hidden for years, until a bone-carver from the counting house brought you something worth hiding."

"I burn papers every night. It's how I stay warm. Poverty is very flammable."

"Search the workshop," Vess said quietly. "Take the tools. All of them."

That landed harder than Maren expected. Her tools—her gravers, her silver-point styluses, the little brass compass her mother had given her before the guild stripped Maren's own name from its rolls. Four enforcers moved through her small kingdom with the gentle violence of people who believed they were being thorough rather than cruel, wrapping chisels in cloth, lifting jars of pigment, pocketing her chalk like it might bite.

She should have felt rage. What she felt instead was a strange, floating grief, watching a stranger wrap her mother's compass in a rag as if it were evidence.

And then, through the window above the canal, she saw him.

Jude Underhill, pressed flat against the shadowed wall of the boathouse across the water, having clearly doubled back when he heard the raid—and now frozen there, caught between running and some fool impulse to help her. His face was a study in terror. His eyes found hers through the glass.

*Marens don't get rescued,* she thought. *Especially not by accountants.*

But he was moving—slipping along the boathouse, hauling open the old flood-door that every canal-side tenement pretended didn't exist because it led down into the bone-carvers' tunnels, which were supposed to be sealed, condemned, forgotten. He held the door open and looked at her with an expression that said, with the desperation of a man who had never once done anything reckless: *please don't make me do this alone.*

"Well," Maren announced loudly, to the room, to Vess, to the gods who presumably enjoyed this sort of thing, "if you're taking my tools, the least I can do is show you where I keep the good ink."

She walked toward the back wall. Toward the window. Toward the flood-door.

"Stop her," Vess said—not shouting, never shouting, just precise—and Maren dove through the gap as a lantern shattered somewhere behind her, and cold canal water soaked her to the knees, and Jude Underhill hauled her bodily into the dark.

* * *

The tunnels smelled the way Maren remembered: tallow, wet stone, and bone. Old bone, worked bone, the sweet dusty smell of a craft district that had once carved half the city's memorials underground before the guild decided underground work was unregulated work.

Jude lit a stub of candle with hands that shook only a little, which Maren considered admirable.

"You came back," she said.

"You looked—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "I heard them coming. From the street. And I thought, if they take you, then the map is just paper and I'm just a clerk and none of it ever gets understood."

"And instead?"

"Instead I'm fleeing through condemned tunnels with a woman I met four hours ago." He laughed—a small, cracked, astonished sound. "I've never fled anything in my life. I pay my taxes early."

"Congratulations. You're a criminal now. It suits you badly; we'll work on it."

They walked. The tunnel sloped down and branched, and Jude navigated with an ease that surprised her until he explained, sheepishly, that the Meridian Guild's night ledgers included maintenance records for every sealed structure in the city, and he had spent seven years reading them out of boredom.

"Boredom," Maren repeated.

"It's very quiet at night. The numbers are..." He hesitated, and she watched him decide how much truth to spend. "The numbers are calmer than people."

*Calmer than the dead*, is what he meant, though he didn't say it. She filed it away.

They had gone perhaps half a mile, wading a stretch where the ceiling dripped and the candle flame bent sideways, when the light behind them changed. Not lantern-glow—something colder, a pale wash bleeding around the corners of the passage, and beneath it a sound like chalk squeaking on slate, multiplied.

Maren knew that sound. Every disgraced sigilwright knows that sound. Guild tracing-chalk, laid down in patterns that made air itself reluctant to carry a fugitive's footsteps.

"They're tracking us with resonance lines," she whispered. "Standard practice. They draw a net across the tunnels and anything living that crosses it registers."

"Anything living," Jude repeated. His voice had gone odd.

"Yes, why—"

He set the candle down. "Stay here. Don't move. And whatever you see, don't scream, because I need to concentrate."

Before she could ask what in the name of all merciful gods he meant, Jude Underhill sat down cross-legged in the dripping dark, closed his eyes, and began to whisper to nobody.

Maren had seen sigils drawn, sung, burned, bled. She had never seen a man simply *talk to the empty air* with such desperate sincerity—and she had certainly never seen the empty air answer. The candle flame stood perfectly still. The drip of water seemed to hold its breath. And Jude's lips moved, rapid and low, like a clerk reconciling accounts, asking questions of someone only he could hear.

Then he opened his eyes. "There's a side passage forty paces back, behind a collapsed shelf. The resonance line doesn't cover it—the dead say the carvers dug it during the plague year and never told the guild. They also say," he added, standing, knees popping, "that we should hurry, because the enforcers have dogs, and the dogs are not conversationalists."

"The dead," Maren said carefully, "told you."

"The dead tell me everything. That's rather the whole tragedy of my life." He picked up the candle. "You drew covenant-work for nine years after your disgrace. I've been hearing ghosts since I was six. Between us, we're either the most qualified people alive or the least."

"Both," Maren said, and followed him into the plague-year passage.

* * *

They surfaced near midnight in the root cellar of a shuttered chandlery in the Tallow District, and it was there—sitting on flour barrels, sharing a heel of bread Jude had produced from some clerkish pocket—that Captain Vess found them.

Not with enforcers. Not with dogs or chalk-nets or broken doors. Alone, stepping out of the cellar stairwell with her lantern raised and her badge conspicuously dimmed, looking less like a hunter than like a woman who had walked a long way to say something she hated saying.

Jude was on his feet instantly, a boning knife from the chandlery stock in his hand, and Maren noted with interest that his grip was terrible but his resolve wasn't.

"Put it down, Underhill. If I wanted you taken, you'd be taken."

"That's what worries me," Jude said.

Vess set her lantern on a barrel. Up close, in honest light, she looked tired in a way that armor couldn't hide. "I'm going to tell you things I shouldn't. Then you're going to make a choice, and I'd rather you made it knowing." She looked at Maren. "You first, Oduya. Nine years ago, you submitted a design to the Registry. A renewal-sigil. Full covenant architecture. Do you remember what happened?"

"I remember being expelled for 'unlicensed speculation.' I remember my name struck from the rolls. I remember—" Maren stopped. "I never understood why. The design was sound. It was *old*. Older than the guild. I'd reconstructed it from fragments in the deep archive."

"You reconstructed it because the deep archive was supposed to be empty," Vess said. "Someone missed a box. And when your design crossed the Registry desk, it went upward. Past the examiners. Past the Council. To Guildmaster Corvane himself." She paused. "Corvane has known about the covenant for thirty years, Oduya. The guild has known. The suppression isn't ignorance—it's policy. Every record of the Threshold Spire, every keeper genealogy, every mention of the Hollow in guild holdings has been quietly bought, burned, or reclassified for three decades. Your expulsion wasn't punishment. It was silencing. They let you live because a disgraced sigilwright talking nonsense in taverns discredits the whole subject better than any decree."

Maren sat down slowly on a flour barrel. Thirty years. She had spent thirty years believing she had failed some private test, carried the shame like a stone in her chest, and the whole time she had been a piece of someone else's machinery. "That absolute bastard," she said, with feeling.

"There's more," Vess said, and turned to Jude, and her voice changed—softened in a way that made Maren wary. "Underhill. Your parents didn't die in the ferry accident. That's the official record. The actual record says they were keepers. Last of the bloodlines the guild hadn't yet accounted for. They came to the guild nineteen years ago asking for help renewing the covenant. Corvane received them personally." She let that sit. "No ferry was involved."

Jude had gone very still. The kind of still that Maren recognized from her own mirror, years ago—the stillness of a person holding something enormous and pretending it's a parcel.

"They drowned," Jude said finally. "I was there. I was six. I *heard*—"

He stopped. Whatever he had heard, he swallowed it, and Maren watched him do it, watched the old habit clamp down over his face like a shutter.

"You heard what happened to them," Vess said gently. "Yes. I imagine you did. Corvane needed the bloodline ended quietly, and the boy—well. A bone-carver's apprentice with a gift for hearing things was useful to the guild, once he was frightened enough to hide it. Think about it, Underhill. Who arranged your apprenticeship? Who paid your fees? You've spent your life inside the institution that killed your parents, keeping its books, grateful for its charity."

The silence in the cellar was total. Even the river, somewhere beyond the walls, seemed to hush.

Maren watched Jude's hands. They were steady—clerk's hands, ledger hands—but the knuckles had gone white, and when he spoke, his voice was the flattest she'd heard it, flat as a ruled line.

"The guild gave me a trade. A room. A life."

"The guild gave you a cage and called it a life," Maren said. "Believe me, I know the craftsmanship."

"Why are you telling us this?" Jude asked Vess. "Why warn us? Why not take the map?"

For the first time, Captain Vess looked away from them. She looked at her lantern, at the little flame doing its honest work, and Maren saw something flicker across the enforcer's face that she would later recognize as the beginning of doubt—the first hairline crack in a wall built of orders.

"Because I was told I was protecting this city," Vess said. "Thirty years of files, and tonight I read far enough to learn that what we've been protecting is a lie with a payroll. I can't undo what I am. But I can give you until dawn before I report this cellar." She straightened, armor settling back into place, though it fit differently now. "The equinox is coming. If the covenant fails, the Hollow doesn't just die—it comes *through*. And Corvane wants that. Understand that. He isn't preventing the catastrophe. He's scheduling it."

She picked up her lantern and started for the stairs, then paused.

"Underhill. Your mother carved bone too. Did you know that? There's a token in the evidence vault with her mark on it. I've looked at it for years without knowing why." She didn't turn around. "Dawn. Don't be here at dawn."

Her boots faded up the stairwell, and then it was just the two of them among the flour barrels, and the map between them on Jude's knee, glowing faintly at its sigil-marked heart like something patient.

"So," Maren said at last, because someone had to. "Your parents were keepers, my disgrace was a cover story, and the head of the most powerful trade house in the city is planning to murder a world on a deadline. I don't suppose you have any brandy?"

Jude shook his head. But he was looking at the map—at the sigil that matched the scar on his wrist—with an expression Maren had never seen on a ledger-keeper's face. It was grief, yes, and fury, and fear. But underneath all of it, unmistakable, was purpose.

"No," he said quietly. "But I know where we can go that Corvane's people won't think to look. Somewhere outside the walls. Somewhere... thin." He rolled the map closed and stood, and for the first time since she'd met him, Jude Underhill sounded less like a man hiding and more like a man beginning. "We leave before dawn. And Maren—we'll need supplies for the marshes."

"The marshes," Maren repeated. "Of course. The marshes. Why does the map always lead somewhere damp?"

But she was already gathering bread, and wicks, and the one graver the enforcers had missed, hidden for nine years inside a hollowed-out hymnal, and thinking: *the third bloodline is thought extinct, the archives said, but extinct things have a way of turning up in marshes.*

Above them, the city bells rang midnight. Eleven days to the equinox.

Somewhere in the dark of the tunnels behind them, faint as breath, Maren could almost believe she heard footsteps that belonged to no living thing—keeping pace, patient, following the bone-carver home.
