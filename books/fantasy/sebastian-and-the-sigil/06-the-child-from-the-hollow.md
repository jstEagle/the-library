## 6. The Child from the Hollow

The marshes outside Vellamere smelled like a promise the city had never kept.

Jude noticed that first, before the cold, before the ache in his feet. Three days underground in the bone-carvers' tunnels and one desperate night on the river barge had stripped him down to a person who could notice things like that—the smell of open air, green and rotting and alive all at once—and feel something dangerously close to gratitude.

"Gratitude," Maren muttered beside him, hauling her satchel higher on her shoulder as they picked their way along a duckboard path half-sunk in black water. "That's what I'm feeling. Gratitude. For the mud."

"You said you liked the tunnels," Jude said.

"I said I *survived* the tunnels. There's a difference, and it smells like this." She gestured at the reed beds stretching out ahead of them, silver-grey under a sky the color of old pewter. Dragonflies stitched lazy patterns over the water. Somewhere distant, a marsh bird made a sound like a rusty gate complaining about its life.

Jude didn't answer right away, because Odile was walking just off his left shoulder—she always walked there now, as if she'd claimed the spot—and she was humming. She'd been humming for an hour. He'd learned that Odile hummed when she was thinking, and thought when she was worried, and worried most of the time, because being dead apparently did not cure you of having opinions about other people's survival decisions.

*You should be further north,* Odile said, her voice arriving in his head like a hand resting gently on his shoulder. *The veil is thinner where the water runs backward.*

"It's not water running backward," Jude murmured.

"What?"

"Not you. Sorry." He cleared his throat. "I was—" Talking to a dead woman, he almost said, then realized there was no longer any point pretending otherwise to Maren. That was the strange new arithmetic of his life: he'd spent years hiding one secret, and now he had four companions, two of them fugitives and one of them deceased, and the hiding was over whether he liked it or not. It should have terrified him. Mostly it did. But underneath the terror was a small, stubborn thing that felt suspiciously like relief.

"The ghost says we should go north," he told Maren aloud. "Where the water runs backward."

Maren stopped walking. She looked at him, then at the empty space by his shoulder, then at him again, with the expression of a woman recalculating several debts at once.

"Your dead courier has navigation opinions."

"She guarded the map. She knows the routes." Jude shrugged. "And she hasn't been wrong yet."

"She's been dead for three days."

"That doesn't seem to slow her down much."

Maren snorted—a real laugh, quickly strangled—and started walking again. "Fine. North it is. If the water starts running backward and we all drown, I'm haunting *you*, Underhill, and I will be extremely tedious about it."

Odile laughed too, a bright sound only Jude could hear, and the three of them went north through the marshes while the sky went slowly the color of a bruise healing.

* *

They made camp that night on a hummock of high ground between two channels, in the lee of a leaning willow. Maren built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and cooked a mash of barley and dried plums that tasted like punishment but sat warmly in the stomach. Jude took first watch, which suited everyone: Maren needed sleep, and Jude needed quiet, or as close to quiet as his life ever came.

Because here was the thing nobody warned you about, traveling with ghosts. They talked. Not constantly—not cruelly—but the dead were everywhere in the world, thin as smoke, and out here beyond the city walls there were more of them than Jude had ever felt before. Drowned travelers under the marsh water. An old eel-fisher who still checked her traps out of habit, decades after her body stopped needing eels. A soldier from some war so old even Odile didn't recognize his uniform. They drifted past the camp like moths around a candle only Jude could see, and each of them wanted something—witness, mostly. Someone to say *yes, you were here; yes, it mattered.*

Jude sat with his back against the willow and listened, and for once he let himself listen without flinching.

*It was cold,* said the drowned traveler, who had been telling someone about the cold for sixty years. *Nobody ever asks about the cold.*

"I'm asking," Jude whispered. "Tell me about the cold."

The ghost brightened like a banked coal given air.

He was deep into the drowned man's story—he'd gotten as far as the ice, the terrible singing quality of lake ice underfoot—when he heard the breathing.

Small breathing. Fast. From the reeds twenty paces off, where the firelight gave up and the dark took over.

Jude went very still. The drowned man faded politely, the way the dead did when living danger arrived—they seemed to understand that the living required room. Odile appeared at his elbow instead, sharp-eyed, all business.

*Something's out there,* she said. *Small. Alive. Heart going like a rabbit's.*

"Maren," Jude breathed.

Maren was already awake—she slept like a knife balanced on its edge, three days of flight having taught her that much—and she had a bone-handled knife in her hand and a look on her face that said whoever was in those reeds had made a serious error. She moved to Jude's side without a sound.

"What is it?" she mouthed.

Jude shook his head. He didn't know. He closed his eyes and did the thing Odile had been teaching him, reaching out with the listening instead of waiting for it to crash over him—

—and recoiled.

There was something in the reeds, all right. But it wasn't a person, exactly. It registered to his gift the way a wrong note registers to a musician: alive, yes, but *keyed differently.* The dead sounded like bells struck underwater. This sounded like a bell made of some metal he'd never heard of, ringing in a key that didn't exist.

Hollow-born. The word arrived whole in his mind, unbidden, from some ledger entry or half-remembered guild rumor: *Hollow-born. Do not catalog. Report sightings to the registry directly.* He'd copied that instruction a dozen times over the years and never once asked what it meant. Invisibility had been safer than questions.

The reeds parted.

The child couldn't have been more than eight or nine, though it was hard to tell, because everything about the child was slightly *off* the way a reflection is off—skin a shade too pale, eyes catching the firelight like a cat's, hair the grey-white of birch bark. They wore clothes that might once have been fine and were now rags held together by mud and stubbornness. In one grubby fist they clutched a stone that glowed faintly, softly, the way coals glow long after the fire seems dead.

The child stared at Jude.

Then the child stared at the space just over Jude's shoulder, where Odile stood.

And the child smiled—a huge, gap-toothed, uncomplicated smile—and said, in a voice cracked from disuse:

"Oh! You've got a *ghost.* Is she yours? Can I hold her?"

Maren's knife did not lower. "Absolutely not," she said. "Whatever it is, it talks. Things that talk are trouble."

"I talk," Jude said mildly.

"Yes, and look how you've ruined my life."

The child ignored her entirely, which Jude suspected would become a signature move. They crept closer, low to the ground, moving with a strange liquid grace that put Jude in mind of a heron—or of nothing human at all—and stopped just at the edge of the firelight, close enough that he could see the mud dried in their eyelashes and the hollows of their cheeks. Close enough to see how badly they were shaking.

Not from fear. Jude knew fear on every kind of face; he'd studied faces his whole life so he wouldn't have to study people. This was the shaking that came after fear, when the thing you'd run from had finally stopped chasing and your body remembered it was tired.

"When did you last eat?" Jude asked.

The child considered this with great suspicion, as though it might be a trick question. "What's eating?"

Maren made a noise like a kettle giving up. "Oh, wonderful. Feral. You've collected a feral one."

"I didn't collect anything," Jude said, already digging in his pack for the barley mash. "It followed us."

"It followed your *ghost.*"

"That's what I said. Followed."

*Careful,* Odile said quietly. *Look at its hands.*

Jude looked. The child's fingers were smudged with charcoal and clay, and the tips were stained the deep brown-black of someone who worked bone or wood or stone. And there, on the inside of the small wrist, half-hidden by a ragged sleeve—a mark.

A sigil. Small, incomplete, but unmistakable. The same lines, the same geometry, that curled across Jude's own wrist like a scar that had decided to be beautiful.

His stomach dropped through the marsh, through the water, through the bones of the world.

*Odile,* he said. *Do you see—*

*I see.* Her voice had gone strange. *Keeper's blood. On a child. Out here alone.* A pause, and then, very softly: *That shouldn't be possible. Unless—*

"Unless the veils are failing," Jude finished aloud, before he could stop himself.

The child's head snapped up. "You know about the doors?" Their voice climbed, urgent, cracking. "Do you know the way home? I've been walking for—I counted nine sleeps but then I lost counting because sleeping is hard out here—are you door-keepers? Are you *my* door-keepers?" The glowing stone trembled in their grip. "Because mine broke. My door broke, and the light went all wrong, and Mama said *run toward the loud place,* except the loud place was everywhere, and then I was here and it's all *wrong* here, the colors are flat and the water doesn't sing—"

The words poured out of the child like water from a cracked jug, days and days of them, and halfway through the pouring the child's knees simply quit. Jude caught them before they hit the ground.

They weighed almost nothing. That was the detail that undid him, later, when he tried to think about it calmly: how light the child was, how their ribs pressed against his forearm like a bundle of dry reeds, how their fist stayed clamped around that dying-glowing stone even unconscious, even exhausted, as though letting go of it was the one thing their body refused to do.

"Underhill," Maren said. There was no mockery in it now. She crouched beside him, knife finally sheathed, and studied the sigil on the small wrist for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice was carefully level, the way voices go when the speaker is working very hard to keep them that way. "That's a keeper's mark."

"I know."

"A *child* with a keeper's mark, alone in the marshes, talking about broken doors."

"I know, Maren."

"The guild pays informants for exactly this kind of sighting. We could—"

"We're not reporting a child to the institution currently hunting us across half a country."

"I said *could.*" She exhaled through her nose. "I'm establishing that I considered the sensible option. For the record."

"Noted for the record."

The child stirred against his chest and mumbled something. Jude bent close.

"...name's Pip," the child whispered, mostly asleep already. "Don't tell the flat-color people."

* * *

Pip ate like a small wolf and slept like a stone and woke like a spring trap, and within a day Jude had learned three things about Hollow-born children.

First: they saw everything. Pip noticed Odile within seconds of waking and never once looked through her, never once glanced past her the way the living did. When Odile moved, Pip's eyes tracked her. When Odile spoke, Pip answered. It was, Jude realized with a jolt, the first time in Odile's death that anyone besides him had treated her as fully present—and watching the courier ghost sit cross-legged across the fire teaching a feral child a clapping game, both of them laughing at nothing, hurt somewhere in Jude's chest in a way he decided not to examine.

Second: they had no fear of the dead whatsoever. The drowned traveler drifted by the second evening, mid-story about the cold, and Pip waved cheerfully. "Hello, cold man!"

*Nobody's called me anything in sixty years,* the ghost said wonderingly, to no one, meaning Jude. *Cold man.*

Third, and most dangerous: they trusted Jude completely, instantly, and without any apparent awareness that trust was a thing that could be misplaced. By the second morning Pip had appointed themselves Jude's shadow, following him on his water-gathering trips, copying the way he held his hands, asking approximately four hundred questions per hour ("Why do you talk to the air? Is the air rude to you? Does Maren know she snores? What's a *ledger*? Why would anyone write numbers for fun?").

"Why do you follow me?" Jude finally asked, on the third morning, when Pip trailed him down to the channel's edge and squatted beside him in perfect imitation of his squatting posture.

Pip considered this with the gravity of a scholar. "You're the quietest loud thing here," they decided.

"That doesn't make sense."

"Neither does your world." Pip poked the water with a stick. Ripples spread. Pip frowned at them like they'd personally failed. "In the Hollow, water remembers which way it's been pushed. Here it just forgets. Everything here forgets." A pause. "Except you. You remember things that aren't even *here* anymore." Pip looked up at him, and those too-bright eyes were suddenly older than nine years had any right to be. "That's why the ghost likes you. That's why I like you. Rememberers are rare."

Something in Jude's throat went tight. He looked out over the marsh instead of at the child, at the flat pewter water and the reeds and the ordinary forgetting world, and heard himself say:

"Pip. About your door. Your home."

"Mm?"

"We're going to find a way to send you back. I want you to know that. Whatever it costs." He hadn't known he was going to say it until it was out. His whole life he had been a man who calculated exits—who kept his head down, kept his ledgers clean, kept invisible. Every instinct he owned was screaming at him that promising things to a Hollow-born child in a marsh while guild enforcers hunted them was the single least survivable act of his career.

But Pip's face did something complicated and shining, and Jude found that he didn't care about his instincts at all.

"You promise?" Pip said. "Really-promise, not grown-up-soft-promise?"

"Really-promise."

"Then you have to do the seal." Pip grabbed his hand, spat ceremoniously into their own palm—which Jude, after a moment's internal struggle, matched—and slapped their palms together. "Sealed," Pip declared. "Now if you break it, the marsh gets to keep one of your shoes. Forever."

"That seems proportionate."

"It's very serious magic."

Odile, watching from the bank, was laughing so hard she had to hold onto a reed that did not hold her back. *You've done it now, carver,* she managed. *Spit-oath. There's no escaping a spit-oath.*

"No," Jude agreed quietly, looking at the small muddy hand still wrapped around his. "I don't suppose there is."

* * *

They broke camp at noon, because Odile had gone quiet and grim at the tree line and finally spoken: *Someone's coming up the western channel. Living. Armed. Moving like hunters.* And because Maren, listening to Jude relay this, had simply said, "North, then. Faster."

But Pip balked at the edge of the reed bed, planting their bare feet in the mud like roots.

"The loud place," Pip said. "Before we go anywhere—you said you're rememberers. Real ones?" Their chin came up, and for the first time there was steel under the softness. "Then come see. Come see what's happening to my home. Through the thin place—it's close, half a sleep east. You can *see* through. See why my door broke." The glowing stone rose in their small fist, its ember-light pulsing like a heartbeat. "See why you have to hurry."

Jude exchanged a look with Maren. Behind him, Odile had stopped laughing entirely.

*Half a sleep east,* she repeated softly. *Jude. The Threshold Spire is east.*

Somewhere far away, under the marsh and the mud and the forgetting water, Jude felt the covenant shift another degree toward breaking—and understood, with terrible clarity, that Pip hadn't wandered through a broken door by accident.

The door had broken because everything was breaking.

And the child was leading them straight toward the proof.

"*East* it is," said Maren, and sighed, and shouldered her satchel.
