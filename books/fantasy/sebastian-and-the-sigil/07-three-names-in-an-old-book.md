## 7. Three Names in an Old Book

The smuggler's archive smelled of mildew, lamp oil, and forty years of men pretending they hadn't cried near it.

Maren Oduya knew the smell well. She'd spent six years frequenting establishments like this one—canal-side rooms where the tide did half the housekeeping and the other half was done by a woman named Bracka who charged by the candle and remembered every debt in her head because paper could be seized and heads, so far, had not been.

"Three coppers for the reading table," Bracka said now, not looking up from her knitting. "Five if you want the good lamp. Ten if you're going to sneeze on anything."

"I don't sneeze on things," Maren said with dignity.

"You sneezed on the Vashti folio in spring. I had to dry it over the stove for two days."

"That was *damp*," Maren said, "not a sneeze."

Behind her, Jude Underhill stood very still in the doorway with his collar up and his eyes doing that thing they did—flicking sideways at nothing, listening to some conversation no one else could hear. The child, Pip, was draped against his back like a small damp monkey, having refused all morning to walk on their own feet when riding was available.

Bracka's gaze slid past Maren to Jude, then to Pip, then to the empty space where Odile's ghost hovered, invisible to everyone but the two of them who could see her.

"No ghosts," Bracka said flatly.

"She's very quiet," Maren said.

"They're always quiet until they knock something over." Bracka knitted three more stitches with the air of a woman who had buried husbands, rivals, and at least one archivist. "Four coppers. Ghost tax."

Maren paid the ghost tax. It was, she reflected, still cheaper than the last archive she'd used, which had required her to sign a confession of prior sigilwork before touching anything—a confession that had then been sold to the Guild within the week. That was how Vess had found her workshop. That was why she was here, in a room where the books were catalogued by rumor instead of title, paying ghost tax, with eleven days on the clock and a dead courier's map unrolled across her knee.

*Eleven days.* She kept turning the number over like a stone with a sharp edge, hoping to find a smooth side somewhere.

* * *

The archive proper was three rooms deep, lit by lamps that guttered whenever the canal wind came through the floorboards. Shelves leaned against shelves in the manner of old friends holding each other upright. Half the volumes had no spines; the other half had spines that lied about their contents, which Bracka insisted was deliberate. "A book that tells you what it is gets confiscated," she'd said once. "A book that says it's a treatise on eel husbandry lives forever."

Maren had learned to read the archive the way she read sigils: by structure, by weight, by what was deliberately wrong.

"Covenant material," she told Jude, pulling him toward the back room. "Pre-Schism. It'll be shelved under agriculture or maritime law or something equally boring. Nobody hides exciting books anymore—they hide dull ones."

"And we find them how?"

"We find them by asking the right question of the wrong shelf." She ran her fingers along a row of water-stained ledgers. "The keepers weren't mystics, whatever the ballads say. They were administrators. Bureaucrats of the impossible. There will be forms. There are always forms."

Jude made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You say that like it's comforting."

"It is comforting. Magic that can't be filed can't be renewed. If the covenant is failing, there is paperwork somewhere that says how to fix it, and paperwork, Master Underhill, is the one language I am still fluent in." She said it lightly, but the truth underneath was heavier: she had spent six years being fluent in nothing else anyone wanted. A sigilwright who drew the wrong line once—for mercy, for love, it hardly mattered now—was a sigilwright who drew lines nowhere, ever again.

Pip slid off Jude's back and immediately began climbing a shelf like it was a cliff face.

"Pip—" Jude started.

"Let them," Maren said. "Children find things. It's the whole reason anyone brings children anywhere." She paused. "Also I cannot physically stop them and neither can you."

Pip, from the top of the shelf, made a noise of profound agreement and began pulling down books at random, sniffing each one.

It was Pip, naturally, who found it. Forty minutes into Maren's methodical search of the maritime law section, the child dropped into their midst with a thump and a volume hugged to their chest—a fat, spineless thing bound in gray leather gone soft as skin, its cover stamped with nothing at all except, if you tilted it against the lamplight, a faint geometric impression that Maren's hands recognized before her eyes did.

Her heart did something complicated.

"Where," she said, very calmly, "did you get this."

Pip pointed up. "Smelly shelf."

"The smelly shelf," Maren repeated reverently. "Of course. Preservation through mummification." She took the book with both hands, the way you take something that might be sleeping. "Jude. Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit down anyway. This is going to be worse than you think, and you'll want your knees."

He sat. He was learning. She noticed, with a small private warmth she didn't examine too closely, that he sat close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers, and that Pip wedged themselves into the gap between them without asking, as though the gap had been built for exactly that purpose.

She opened the book.

* * *

It was a keeper's manual. Not the romantic kind—the real kind. Margins full of corrections in four different hands. A page of supply requisitions for the Threshold Spire dated a hundred and sixty years back (tallow, chalk, silver wire, "one measure keeper's blood, voluntary"). And there, a third of the way in, a chapter headed in a careful clerk's script:

**ON THE RENEWAL OF THE COVENANT, AND THE THREE WHO MUST KNEEL.**

Maren read it twice before she let herself speak, because the first time her eyes had skated over the worst part, the way eyes do.

"All right," she said. "The good news."

"There's good news?" Jude said.

"There's *news*. The good news is that the rite exists, it's written down, and it doesn't require anything we can't carry. No relic. No sacrifice of innocents, despite what the ballads want. The covenant is reforged at the Spire by three people of keeper blood, standing at the threshold stone, each giving—" she checked the phrase again, wanting to be sure— "*that which they most hide.* Freely offered. Witnessed by the Spire itself."

"Giving it how?" Jude asked. "Given up forever?"

The manual was maddeningly vague on mechanics; the original author had apparently assumed readers would know, the way a recipe assumes you know what an egg is. "It doesn't say. Which means either it was obvious to keepers, or it's different for each person, or the author enjoyed watching future generations suffer. Possibly all three."

"And the bad news."

Maren turned the book toward him and put her finger under the line.

*Three bloodlines must kneel: the Hand, the Voice, and the Seal. Lacking any one, the rite fails, and the door closes for an age.*

"Three bloodlines," she said. "Not three people. Three *lines*. You're one—you already know that, the mark on your wrist is the Hand, the carver's mark, the ones who shaped the seals in bone and stone." She watched him touch his wrist without seeming to realize he was doing it. "I'm the second. The Voice. Sigilwrights were the covenant's speakers—we wrote the terms into the world. My family served it for nine generations before I—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Before I stopped serving it. The blood doesn't care whether I've disgraced myself. Blood is patient that way."

"So we need a third." Jude frowned. "Another keeper descendant. In eleven days."

"A Seal-blood," Maren said. "And here is the part where I tell you the bad news properly." She braced herself. "The Seal line was the smallest to begin with—keepers who held the covenant's anchor, who *were* the living seal between realms when the rite demanded it. There were never many. And after the Schism, when the Guild started making keeper knowledge disappear..." She tapped the page. "This book survived by smelling like eels. Most of its siblings burned. The Seal line was recorded as ended seventy years ago. Extinct. There are songs about it. Sad ones."

Silence, except for the lamp guttering and Pip chewing thoughtfully on a lock of their own hair.

"So the rite needs three," Jude said slowly, "and one of the three has been dead as a line for seventy years, and we have eleven days, and the man hunting us has more soldiers than this archive has books."

"When you say it like that it sounds bleak."

"How else would I say it?"

"You could say it like a puzzle," Maren said. "Extinct lines aren't extinct, Jude. They're *unrecorded*. People move. Names change. A keeper's granddaughter marries a cooper in a marsh town and nobody writes it down because nobody thinks it matters—until suddenly it's the only thing that matters." She was talking herself into it as much as him; she could hear it happening, hope assembling itself out of scrap, the way she used to build sigils. "Somewhere out there is someone with Seal blood who doesn't know what they carry. We just have to find them faster than the equinox."

"Faster than the equinox," Jude repeated. "And faster than Vess."

"Yes."

"And we have no idea where to look."

"I have three ideas and one of them is terrible."

"Which one is the terrible one?"

"The bone market," Maren said, and watched his face go carefully blank, the way it did when the dead said something to him he didn't want to repeat. "Later. First—"

First, the other thing. Because there was another thing, and she had been circling it since they'd opened the book, and it wasn't going to go away by being circled.

The ghost had drifted closer while they read. Maren felt her before she saw her clearly—that cold-at-the-edge-of-vision pressure, like standing near an open cellar door. Odile, the dead courier, hung above the table now, her translucent face bent over the open manual, and her expression was one Maren had never seen on a ghost in six years of seeing ghosts.

She looked like someone reading their own obituary and finding it inaccurate.

"She keeps pointing," Maren said quietly. "At the chapter heading. Jude—ask her. Whatever it is you do. Ask her why she followed us here."

Jude hesitated. Then he turned his chair, faced the empty air the way he sometimes did—apologizing with his whole body for the interruption he was about to make—and spoke softly, as if to someone standing just behind his shoulder.

"You knew this book," he said. It wasn't quite a question. "You knew it because—" His breath caught. "Because you were there. When it was written?"

The cold in the room shifted. Deepened. Concentrated.

And then, for the first time since the loading dock, since the blood and the wax seal and the whisper of *the Spire remembers*, the dead woman spoke in more than fragments—and she spoke clearly enough that even Maren, who had never heard a ghost plain in her life, caught the shape of it through Jude's stunned repetition:

"*My name is Odile Vane. Keeper of the Seal. Last of the third line.*"

The lamp went out.

In the dark, Pip giggled nervously. Jude's hand found Maren's sleeve and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

"She's saying—" His voice cracked and steadied. "She's saying the line isn't extinct. It's *her*. She never had children, she never passed it on, and the covenant counted her as the end of it—but death didn't release her. She died carrying the map. She died *on duty*." A pause, and then, hollower: "She says the blood doesn't care whether you've disgraced yourself. Or... whether you've *stopped*. She says it's patient that way."

Maren realized her mouth had gone dry. "Those were my words."

"She likes your words." A shaky almost-laugh. "She says she's been trying to tell us since the dock. She says—" Jude stopped. Started over, slower, as though handling something fragile. "She says the rite needs three bloods to kneel. It doesn't say anything about them needing to be alive."

The darkness felt different after that. Not emptier. Crowded.

"Well," Maren managed, when she trusted her voice. "That's either the answer to everything or the most dangerous loophole in covenant law, and knowing the keepers, it's both." She fumbled for the relighter, found it, coaxed the lamp back to life. Its glow returned to find them all leaning together over the gray book like conspirators—which, she supposed, they now irreversibly were.

Odile stood in the light they'd made, looking at her own name in the air between them, and Maren would have sworn on her disgraced sigilwright's oath that the ghost was crying, if ghosts could cry, and maybe they could, and maybe tears were just one more thing that didn't care whether you'd stopped.

"Eleven days," Maren said aloud, because someone had to say it, because deadlines were the only prayers she still believed in. "One carver. One fallen sigilwright. One dead keeper who refuses to be finished." She closed the manual gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "It's the worst-qualified covenant renewal team in history."

Pip raised their hand.

"Yes, Pip?"

"Can we get dinner first?"

Maren laughed—really laughed, the first time in longer than she wanted to count—and outside, far off across the dark city, a guild bell rang the hour, indifferent and counting.

They had ten days and twenty-three hours left. Dinner, she decided, was officially part of the plan.
