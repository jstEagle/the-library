## 8. Odile's Long Walk

The dead, in Jude's experience, did not take turns.

They arrived like weather—all of them at once, all of them forever. The drowned woman from the canal who hummed a lullaby in a language no one living spoke. The old carver whose hands had shaken too badly to finish his last commission and who now paced the workshop at night, muttering measurements. Children who had died before they learned enough words to say anything except their own names, over and over, small and lost.

Jude had spent thirty-one years building walls against that noise. He'd built them out of routine, out of ledgers, out of the careful fiction that if he never answered, he could pretend he wasn't hearing. He kept his jaw tight and his eyes down and his mind full of numbers, because numbers were quiet and orderly and belonged entirely to the living world.

But Odile did not behave like the others.

For three days she had walked beside him—not behind, not circling, but beside, the way a person walks with another person—and she said nothing at all. She simply waited. It was the most unnerving thing a ghost had ever done to him.

She looked as she must have in life: tall, broad-shouldered, her dark hair cropped short in the practical way of someone who traveled hard roads. Her courier's leathers were still torn where the knife had gone through, though the blood was only a shadow now, a stain remembered rather than real. Sometimes her boots made no sound on the cobbles. Sometimes, when she forgot herself, they did.

On the third evening, in Maren's borrowed room above the salt shop, while Pip slept curled in a chair with both thumbs in his mouth and Sef dozed by the cold hearth, Odile finally sat down across from him.

"Well," she said. "You're going to have to look at me eventually."

Jude's pen stopped mid-stroke. A drop of ink bloomed on the ledger page like a wound.

He looked up. The room was dim, lit by one guttering candle, and Odile sat in it the way living people couldn't—too still, too present, her outline sharp as a carving fresh off the knife. No other ghost had ever *addressed* him. They spoke near him, around him, through him. This was different. This was conversation.

"I'm looking," he said, and his voice came out rough from disuse.

"Took you long enough." She tilted her head. "Three days. I counted. Do you know what it's like to count your own funeral procession? Because I do, and I'll tell you—the eulogy was generous. The flowers were cheap."

A sound escaped Jude that took him a moment to identify as a laugh. It startled him so much he pressed his hand over his mouth.

"Oh, don't do that," Odile said, gentler now. "Don't swallow it. That's how they get you—how *you* get you. Everything goes down and nothing comes up until you're just a ledger with legs."

"That's—" He cleared his throat. "That is an unkind description."

"But accurate?"

He didn't answer, which was its own answer, and Odile nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something she'd suspected since the loading dock.

* * *

"You want to know how I died," she said, when the candle had burned lower. Not a question. An offering.

"You don't have to."

"No. But you need to hear it, and more than that, you need to hear it *properly*, and we'll get to what properly means." She flexed her ghost hands, watching the candlelight pass through them without warming anything. "I was a keeper's courier for nineteen years. Third generation. My mother carried maps before me, and her father before her. We didn't renew the covenant—we carried the knowledge of how, from the people who knew to the people who needed to know. Invisible work. You'd understand that, I think."

Jude's chest tightened. "I keep ledgers for impossible things. Nobody asks where they come from."

"Nobody ever thanks a bridge, either," Odile said. "But everyone walks across it."

Outside, the tide turned in the harbor, a long sighing sound. Pip stirred in his chair, mumbled something about doors, and slept on.

"There were four of us making the run from the Spire," Odile went on. "Four couriers, each carrying a copy of the map, each taking a different road so the guild couldn't silence all of us at once. We drew straws for the river road because it was the longest and the loneliest and the most likely to kill you." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I won. I was always lucky that way.

"And then at the ford at Marrowbridge, I heard the ambush coming—horses, steel, the guild's hounds—and instead of running toward my companions to warn them, I ran *away*. Into the reeds. With the map." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it trembled, the way ice trembles before it decides. "I told myself I was protecting the cargo. That the map mattered more than any of us. That was even true, probably. It's amazing, the truths you can hide behind when you don't want to look at what you actually did."

"The others?"

"Halda made it. Last I knew, years ago, she'd gone deep into the Gray Pass and out the far side. Brenna and Tomas didn't." She met his eyes. "I heard them die, Jude. From my hiding place in the reeds. And then I ran all night and got stabbed anyway, two days later, in a city full of people, and the map ended up in the hands of a bone-carver who flinches when ghosts talk to him."

The cruelty of it should have stung. Instead Jude felt something crack open—not painfully, or not only painfully. Recognition.

"You've been following me for three days," he said. "Why wait to tell me this?"

"Because guilt makes bad teachers and worse company, and I wanted to be neither when I finally spoke." She leaned forward. "I've been listening to you, these three days. To the way you hold yourself when the dead press close. You go rigid. You clamp down. You treat every voice like a wave hitting a seawall, and you wonder why you're exhausted all the time."

"I manage."

"You *endure*. There's a difference, and the difference is killing you by inches." Odile rose, drifting toward the window, and moonlight went through her shoulder like water through a doorway. "I spent nineteen years learning from keepers, Jude Underhill. Before the guild burned the schools, before Corvane started calling covenant-keepers smugglers and madmen. I know things. And I am going to teach you, whether you like it or not, because you are carrying my map and my failure and possibly the fate of two worlds, and I refuse to let all of that rest on a man who treats his own soul like a leaky roof he's too busy to fix."

* * *

The first lesson came that same night, whether Jude liked it or not.

It began badly, the way all his nights began. As the city settled into sleep, the dead woke—or rather, they grew louder, because the living noise that usually buried them thinned away. By midnight the room was a chorus: the humming drowned woman, the pacing carver, a new voice tonight, an old sailor somewhere below counting knots over and over. Forty voices. Sixty. Jude had never managed to count them; counting meant attending, and attending meant drowning.

He sat rigid on his cot, jaw clenched, hands flat on his knees, doing what he had done since childhood: enduring.

Odile appeared sitting cross-legged on the floor in front of him, insubstantial as breath.

"Stop fighting," she said.

"If I stop fighting, it takes me under."

"It takes you under because you fight *all of it at once*, like a man trying to bail the sea out of his boat with a teaspoon. Listen to me." She snapped her fingers—a sound like a distant door closing—and impossibly, for one heartbeat, everything else went quiet. "When I did that, did you hear the silence?"

"Yes."

"That silence isn't mine. It's yours. I just showed you where you keep it." She leaned closer, and her eyes were fierce and kind at once. "Every voice you hear is standing in a doorway. You don't have to let them all into the house. But you can't stand in front of forty doors at once either. So you learn to choose. One door. One voice. Deliberately."

"How?" His voice cracked on the word. Thirty-one years, and no one had ever asked him how—he'd only ever been asked to hide it, suppress it, be normal, be quiet, be invisible. "There are so many. They're all talking. They never stop."

"They never stop," Odile agreed, "but they're not all talking to *you*. Most of them are just... remembering out loud. Echoes. Weather. Here—close your eyes."

He did. The chorus surged immediately, pressing against the inside of his skull.

"Now, breathe. And pick one sound. Any one. The nearest one."

The nearest was the drowned woman's lullaby, faint and wavering. Jude seized onto it the way a drowning man seizes driftwood—

And the rest fell back.

Not silent. Never fully silent. But the other voices receded, the way a crowd steps back when two people begin to dance, and suddenly there was room. Suddenly there was *one* voice, clear and sad and singular, humming to a child long gone.

Jude's breath shuddered out of him.

"Oh," he whispered.

"Oh," Odile echoed, smiling. "Thirty-one years, and you thought the problem was the size of the sea. It was never the sea. It's that nobody taught you to swim."

* * *

They practiced every night after that.

It was harder than the first miracle suggested. Some nights the voices were many and loud and grief-stricken, and Jude's carefully built walls itched to slam back up, and Odile would sit with him in the dark saying, "One door, Jude. One door. You are allowed to close the others. Choosing is not cruelty." Some nights a voice would catch him—a dying man reliving his last morning, a mother calling a name into water—and he would lose his footing and drown a little, and come up gasping and ashamed.

"Don't apologize," Odile said, the fourth night, when he surfaced from a particularly brutal undertow of memory. "Grief is heavy. You're allowed to put it down between visits."

"But they're suffering."

"Some of them are. And some of them are just bored, and some of them are stubborn, and one of them"—she pointed at herself—"is avoiding her own unfinished business with impressive dedication. You cannot carry all of them. That was never your job. Your job is to *witness*. To listen to the ones who need listening, and to let the rest be echoes." She softened. "You've spent your whole life believing your gift is a curse you survived. Let me tell you what it actually is: you are the only ear in a world that has forgotten how to listen. That's not a curse, Jude. That's a lantern. It's heavy, yes. Lanterns are heavy. But they light things."

By the sixth night, he could hold three voices at once—one listening, two held gently at the threshold, acknowledged and waiting. By the seventh, he found he could ask a question of the dead and receive an answer, clean and specific, the way Maren consulted her sigil-books. He catalogued harbor deaths for practice, quietly, in a private ledger: dates, names where names could be recovered, last wishes where last wishes existed. When he showed the list to Odile she read it for a long time.

"What?" he asked, defensive.

"Nothing." Her ghost-eyes were bright. "It's just—you've turned your loneliness into a library. I've seen keepers train for twenty years and do less with the gift than you've done by accident, in hiding, ashamed." She handed the pages back. "Imagine what you'll do on purpose."

* * *

On the eighth night, Pip woke screaming.

Jude reached the boy's chair before Maren did, gathering him up, and Pip clung with both fists and stared past Jude's shoulder at something in the corner of the room, trembling.

"She's sad," Pip whispered. "The walking-lady. She's *so* sad."

Odile stood in the corner, very still.

"Pip sees true," she said quietly. "Hollow-born, seeing straight through to the marrow of things. Yes, little one. I'm sad. I've been sad for eleven years, and I've been telling myself it was about the ambush, and that's a lie I'm tired of keeping."

Pip squirmed down and padded over to her—walked right up to the ghost, unafraid, the way he was unafraid of everything that frightened adults—and patted the air where her knee would be.

"S'okay," he said. "Sad's okay here."

Something in Odile's face broke open then, gently, like ice going out in spring. She knelt—ghosts don't kneel, but she did—to be level with the child.

"When I died," she told Pip, though her eyes were on Jude, "I was afraid. Not of dying. Of arriving. Of meeting Brenna and Tomas on whatever shore waits for keepers, and having them ask why I didn't warn them." Her voice wavered. "I've been walking for eleven years because I couldn't bear to find out. Guarding the map was my penance. Delivering it was my absolution. And now it's delivered, and here I still am, and I think—" She laughed, wet and wondering. "I think I'm still here because being useful was easier than being forgiven."

"Then be forgiven," Jude said. The words came out of him raw and certain, from the place where his own oldest wound lived. "I ran from my mother's voice when she needed me, Odile. I've spent twenty years not forgiving myself for it. Don't do what I did. Whatever shore is waiting—it's waiting to hold you, not to judge you. I'd stake the covenant on it."

Silence. Even the background dead seemed to lean closer.

"Twenty years," Odile said softly. "Carrying that alone."

"Not anymore, apparently." Jude gestured weakly at the room—at Pip, at sleeping Sef, at Maren's door, at the ghost in the corner. "I seem to have acquired people."

"You have." Odile rose, and for the first time since the loading dock, her outline flickered—not fading, but *loosening*, like a knot beginning to untie. "Not yet," she added quickly, catching his alarm. "I have a bit more teaching left in me. And you have a Spire to reach, and a rite you don't understand yet, and eleven days—" she corrected herself, "—ten days now, and I intend to be unbearable right up until the end. But soon, Jude. Soon I'll try the shore. And when I go, I need you to promise me you'll keep listening. On purpose. Loudly. As if the whole world depended on it."

"Because it does?"

"Because it does," she agreed. Then her gaze sharpened, and the humor dropped away, and she pointed one translucent finger at the map case on his belt.

"And speaking of unbearable—that mark on your wrist has been itching since sundown. Don't think I haven't noticed you rubbing it. Keeper-blood answers the nearness of its purpose, and yours is answering *hard*." Her eyes narrowed. "Which means either we're closer to the Spire's call than I thought..."

From the street below, faint but unmistakable, came the tolling of the guild bell—struck once, twice, three times. Not the closing bell. Not any bell Jude knew.

Maren appeared in the doorway, already pulling on her coat, her face pale in the candlelight.

"That's the summons pattern," she said. "Old law. Three strikes. It hasn't sounded in sixty years." She swallowed. "The guild is calling all wardens to the wall gates. Something's coming through the veil—and Corvane wants every blade he has to meet it."
