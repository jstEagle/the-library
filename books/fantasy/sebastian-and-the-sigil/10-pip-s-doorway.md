## 10. Pip's Doorway

Pip knew the way home the way other people knew their own hands.

Not with thinking. With knowing. The knowing lived somewhere behind Pip's ribs, in the place where the Hollow had put a little of itself before spitting Pip out through the tear like a cherry stone flicked over a wall. The knowing said: *that way, past the crooked willow, down where the ground forgets to be solid.*

The problem was that the knowing had gone quiet lately. Quieter every day. Like someone turning down a lamp one notch at a time and hoping nobody noticed.

So when the tall one — Jude, with his sad hands and his crowd of invisible friends — crouched down and asked, very seriously, "Can you show us where you came through?" Pip did not hesitate.

"Yes," Pip said. Then, because Maren was watching with her eyebrows up like she expected a trick: "Probably."

"Probably," Maren repeated. She said things twice sometimes, like she was tasting them.

"The knowing is sleepy," Pip explained. This seemed reasonable and complete. Maren looked at Jude. Jude looked at Odile's ghost, who only Pip could see properly, and who made a face like a fish that had swallowed something it regretted.

"Well," Jude said. "Probably is more than we had yesterday."

Pip decided then and there that Jude was acceptable. Not good yet. Acceptable. Good required biscuits, and so far these people had produced only hard travel bread that tasted like a punishment for bread.

* * *

They walked for two days out of the marshes, following the sleepy knowing, and Pip learned things about the three of them that they probably didn't mean to teach.

Maren walked like she was arguing with the road. Every step was a small victory she refused to celebrate. She muttered sigil-words under her breath when she thought no one listened, and once, when a branch snapped, she threw up a ward so fast and so fierce that it singed the eyebrows off a moth. Pip liked her enormously. Maren was all sharp edges on the outside and, underneath, more sharp edges, but the edges were *warm*, like a knife left too near a fire.

Jude walked like he was trying to take up less space than a person legally could. He kept touching his wrist, where the scar-mark lived under his sleeve. And he kept stopping — just slightly, half a step — tilting his head as if hearing something far away. When he did that, Odile drifted closer to him, and her mouth moved, and Jude's face went careful.

Pip could hear Odile without any head-tilting at all. Ghosts were loud in the Hollow. Back home, everyone heard the dead; it was rude not to. Here, apparently, it was a whole tragedy. Humans were strange. They had good ears and used them for nothing.

On the second evening, they camped in the ruins of an old watchtower, and Pip finally asked the question that had been scratching at Pip since the marshes.

"Why does Jude pretend he can't hear her?"

The fire crackled. Maren froze mid-bite of bread. Jude went very still, the way rabbits go still, which never once fooled anybody.

Odile laughed — a sound like wind through a keyhole, bright and thin. "Oh, I *like* this child."

"I don't—" Jude started.

"You tilt your head," Pip said. "Every time. Like this." Pip demonstrated, tilting so far sideways that Pip nearly fell over, which got a startled huff of laughter out of Maren and a strangled noise from Jude. "And your eyes go to where she's standing even though you're pretending she isn't anywhere. You're bad at pretending."

"He's terrible at it," Odile agreed warmly. "I've watched him lie to guild clerks. It's like watching a cat impersonate a spoon."

Jude put his face in his hands. "There are three of you now," he said to his fingers. "Three of you critiquing me."

"Four," said Maren. "I was going to say something about the spoon thing."

Pip grinned, and something in Pip's chest unclenched a little — the thing that had been clenched since the tear spat Pip out. It was nice, being around people who were also bad at being what they were supposed to be. Back home everyone was exactly what they were, loudly, all the time. Here everyone hid inside themselves like hermit crabs. It must be exhausting. No wonder humans slept so much.

But the grin faded, because the knowing behind Pip's ribs chose that moment to speak again, and it did not sound sleepy anymore.

It sounded scared.

"Tomorrow," Pip said quietly. All the silliness drained out of Pip's voice, and Pip felt the others notice — felt them go quiet the way grown-ups do when a child stops being a child for a sentence or two. "Tomorrow we'll see it. The doorway. And then you'll understand why I can't stop crying at night even though I tell myself to stop."

Nobody said anything clever after that. Maren put an extra log on the fire. Jude sat close enough that his shoulder almost touched Pip's, which was how he hugged, Pip had figured out. Humans hugged with almosts.

* * *

The thin place announced itself before they saw it.

First the birds stopped. Not flew away — stopped, mid-song, like music cut with a knife. Then the light went strange, the way light looks underwater, everything green-gold and wobbling. Then Pip's teeth started to hum, deep in the jaw, the hum of standing too near something enormous and old.

"There," Pip said, and pointed.

The tear hung in the air between two leaning stones on a hillside that had probably been ordinary last year. It looked, from a distance, like heat-shimmer above a summer road. Up close it looked like a wound. The edges of it fluttered, opening and closing, opening and closing, like gills. Through it, if you squinted just right, you could see colors that didn't have names here — the deep violet-green of Hollow moss, the pale lantern-light of a village evening.

And through it, drifting out like smoke from a dying fire, came pieces.

Pip ran to the tear before anyone could shout, because one of the pieces was a door. A blue door with a copper knocker shaped like a frog. Mrs. Tallow's door. Pip would know that frog anywhere; Pip had polished its nose a hundred times for luck.

The door lay flat on the grass, real as anything, except that it was fading. Even as Pip watched, the blue went paler, the frog dimmer, until the wood was gray as ash and then thinner than ash, and then it was a shape of nothing lying on nothing, and then it wasn't even a shape.

"No," Pip said. "No, no, *no*—"

Pip grabbed at the place where the door had been and got two handfuls of ordinary hillside grass. Behind, footsteps — Maren shouting something about wards, Jude shouting Pip's name — but Pip wasn't listening, because through the tear Pip could see the village. Could see *home*. The lanterns along Lantern Row. The crooked chimneys. Old Brannic's house with its roof of growing shells.

Except the lanterns were guttering. Not blowing out — *unlighting*, the flame forgetting itself from the top down. And Brannic's roof — Brannic, who was four hundred years old and complained about it constantly — his shell-roof was going translucent, and through it Pip could see the empty rooms inside, and then Pip couldn't see the rooms either, and then—

"Pip." Jude's hand landed gently on Pip's shoulder. "Pip, look at me."

"Don't want to."

"I know." He crouched, bringing his face level with Pip's. His eyes were wet. Grown-ups always tried to hide that and always failed; their eyes went shiny like polished stones. "Look at me anyway. Just for a moment."

Pip looked. It was easier than looking at home coming apart.

"What's happening to it?" Maren asked from behind, and her voice was doing the thing where it stayed flat because going any other shape would cost too much. She had stepped up beside the tear and was holding her palm near it, reading it the way she read sigils. Her lips moved. Whatever she read made her take a step back. "Jude. The veil here isn't just thin. It's *dissolving*. The membrane between realms is — it's unwinding. Everything that belongs on the other side is losing its—its definition. Its *agreement* to exist."

"The covenant," Jude said slowly. Pip watched him touch his wrist again, the scar under the sleeve. "This is what happens when it fails."

"This is what happens when it fails *here*," Maren said. "One thin place. There will be hundreds. Thousands. The Hollow isn't being attacked, it's being *forgotten* — no, worse. It's being *unsigned*. Like a contract with the ink lifting off the page."

Through the tear, a figure appeared on Lantern Row. Small. Shimmering. Walking with a cane and complaining, even now, even at this distance Pip could feel the complaint buzzing through the veil like a trapped fly.

Old Brannic. Or what was left of him. His outline kept losing bits — an elbow, a hat, an opinion — and reassembling them wrong.

"*Brannic!*" Pip screamed through the tear, and the name tore out of Pip bigger than Pip meant it to, big enough to hurt the throat. "*BRANNIC, IT'S ME, IT'S PIP—*"

The old ghost — because that's what Brannic had been for two centuries, dead but refusing on principle to leave — turned his shimmering head. For one heartbeat his eyes found Pip's through the wound in the world, and they widened, and his mouth formed a word.

Then his left side forgot itself, and he flickered like a candle in a draft, and the tear shuddered, and the image was gone.

Pip sat down in the grass. Hard. The kind of sitting down that isn't a decision.

Small arms wrapped around Pip — Jude's, folding Pip in against a chest that smelled like wood shavings and ink and long walking. Pip hadn't asked for the hug. Pip didn't fight it either. Somewhere in the last few days Pip had quietly reclassified Jude from *tall stranger* to *mine*, the way you reclassify a stray cat that keeps showing up, and stray cats get held whether they've consented to the arrangement or not.

"I want to go home," Pip said into Jude's coat. It came out very small. Smaller than Pip had ever let words come out. "I know I said I wanted an adventure. I know I followed the pretty lights through the tear because Brannic said I'd fall in a bog and I wanted to prove him wrong. I *did* fall in a bog. But I want to go *home*, and home is *fading*, and nobody here even knew my world existed until I fell out of it, and that's not fair, that's not fair, that's not—"

"I know," Jude said.

"You don't! You have a whole world! Nobody's erasing your world!"

"No," Jude agreed quietly. "But I spent years pretending the loudest truest part of me didn't exist, because admitting it was too frightening. So I know a smaller version of what you know. Hiding hurts. Being erased hurts more." His hand moved on Pip's back, slow and steady. "Listen to me, Pip. We are going to fix this. Not maybe. Not try. *Fix.* There's a rite, and a Spire, and blood like mine, and a sigilwright who is currently pretending she isn't crying, Maren—"

"I have ash in my eye," Maren said, in a voice thick as porridge.

"—and eleven days, and we are going to walk up to that Spire and we are going to sign the world back into existence. Your village. Your frog-door. Your horrible old Brannic." Jude pulled back and held Pip by the shoulders, and his face was doing something new. Something fierce. Pip had seen him frightened, and sad, and carefully blank. This was none of those. This was a man deciding. "I promise you. On my mother's bones, I promise."

Pip sniffled hugely, which Pip found embarrassing and did anyway. "You promise on *bones*? That's the strongest kind of promise there is. Everyone knows that."

"I'm aware," Jude said gravely. "I'm a bone-carver. It's the only kind I make."

Behind them, Odile drifted to the tear and stood looking through it for a long time. Pip could hear her, faintly, the way you hear a song through a wall. She was humming something in Hollow-fashion, an old round that children sang at lantern-lighting, and Pip's chest cracked open all over again because that was *Lantern Row's* song, that was the tune Mrs. Tallow sang off-key every single evening.

"She knows it," Pip whispered. "How does your ghost know our song?"

Jude tilted his head — his honest tilt, the one he'd given up hiding. After a moment he said, softly, "She says she guarded keepers who traveled to the Hollow, long ago. She says..." He paused, listening. "She says the Hollow taught the living realm its first songs. That the debt runs deeper than anyone remembers. And she says—" his voice caught, just slightly, "—she says she's sorry it took her death to bring any of us back to pay it."

The tear gilled open and shut behind them. Open and shut. A wound trying to breathe.

Pip looked at it a long time. Then Pip wiped Pip's nose on Jude's sleeve (he flinched only a little; he was learning), stood up, and planted both feet in the grass like roots.

"Eleven days," Pip said. "Then we make the door swing both ways again. And then—" Pip pointed at the tear with the full dramatic force of a finger that had pointed at many things, mostly accusingly, "—Brannic owes me an apology about the bog."

Maren laughed — a real one, surprised out of her. Jude smiled the smile he didn't know he was doing. Even Odile's humming brightened a note.

But as they turned to make camp below the hill, out of the tear's sick light, Pip looked back one last time.

And through the fluttering wound, in the last visible scrap of Lantern Row, something glinted. Small. Blue. A door, standing whole again for just one heartbeat — Mrs. Tallow's door, frog-knocker and all — as if home had heard the promise and was holding on.

Then it was gone, and the dark where it had been seemed darker than dark should be.

Pip's ribs went cold, because the knowing woke fully at last, and it said, clear as a bell:

*Hurry.*
