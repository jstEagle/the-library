## 11. Blood in the Bone Market

The Bone Market smelled of tallow and old secrets.

Jude had known that before they ever reached it—every carver in Meridian knew it—but knowing a thing and standing in the middle of it were different animals entirely. The market lived in the sunken quarter beneath the Tallowbridge, where the city's foundations had settled crooked over some older, forgotten street, and the merchants had simply built their stalls on top of the ruins rather than dig them out. Lanterns hung from rib cages. Actual rib cages, iron-reinforced, strung with colored glass that threw red and amber light across counters of polished femur. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune in a minor key, and somewhere else a man was shouting prices for knucklebone dice guaranteed to remember every hand that had ever rolled them.

"Cheerful place," Maren muttered at his shoulder. She'd pulled her hood low to hide the guild sigilwork scarred into her left cheekbone—the mark of a sigilwright struck from the rolls. In this light it looked like just another scar. In daylight it looked like a sentence.

"It's honest, at least," Jude said. "Everything here is dead already. Nobody pretends otherwise."

"That's the most comforting thing you've said all week."

Pip, walking between them with Odile's ghost drifting alongside like a pale second shadow, tugged Jude's sleeve. "It smells like soup," Pip announced, with the gravity of a scholar delivering a thesis.

"It does not smell like soup."

"Bad soup."

Jude decided not to litigate the point. Three days out of the marshes, Pip still flinched at loud noises and hoarded food in both pockets, but the child had also learned to say *soup* and *cheerful* and *Maren*, which felt like progress worth protecting.

They moved with the crowd toward the central rows, past a stall selling whale-ivory combs carved with scenes of storms, past a woman with a voice like gravel offering "reliquary bones, blessed, semi-blessed, or frankly ambiguous," past a cage of songbirds whose skulls, the seller claimed, would whisper your name after death if you fed them honey in life. Jude kept his hands in his coat pockets and his eyes down. He had spent eleven years being invisible in the Meridian Guild's back offices; he knew how to be furniture. What he did not know—what made his stomach knot in an unfamiliar way—was how much he hated doing it now.

*You keep looking up,* Odile observed. Her voice came the way it always did, not through his ears but through the listening place behind them, clear as a bell rung underwater. *A man trying not to be seen looks down. You keep checking the crowd.*

"I'm checking for enforcers."

"You're checking for something you haven't admitted yet. I was a keeper for forty years, boy. I know what seeking looks like."

He didn't answer, because she was right, and because ahead of them the third row opened into the market's heart: the Hall of Provenance, where the expensive things were kept.

* * *

The archive in the smuggler's cellar had given them one clean fact wrapped in three days of frustration: the third bloodline, the line of the Warden-of-Bones, was not extinct. It had been *acquired*. The ledger entry, in a clerk's cramped hand, read: *One (1) relic, living, provenance Warden line — held, Hall of Provenance, Bone Market — inquiries to Collector Vane.*

*A living relic,* Maren had repeated, when they'd pried the page loose from its binding. "They wrote him into a ledger like a shipment of dye."

"They write everything into ledgers," Jude had said quietly. "I know. I used to be the one writing."

Now, standing under the Hall's arched entrance—a jawbone of some prehistoric beast, mounted and gilded, forming a gate you walked through between its teeth—Jude felt the old shame rise hot in his throat. He had catalogued impossible things for years without once asking where they came from or who they'd been taken from. A key that unlocked memories, entered in his own neat hand: *one key, brass, effect as described by consignor.* He hadn't asked whose memories. He hadn't wanted to know. Invisibility, he'd told himself, was safety.

It had also been cowardice wearing competence's coat.

"Right," Maren said, all business. "The Hall keeps collectors' hours. Private viewings only, unless you've got credentials." She glanced sideways at him. "Which is why I'm going to stand here looking disreputable while you do the thing you're famously good at."

"The thing I'm famously good at is hiding."

"No. The thing you're famously good at is bone." She nodded toward the gatekeeper's podium, where a broad woman in collector's livery was turning away a pair of nervous-looking buyers. "Vane doesn't sell relics to people. He sells them to *peers*. Craftsmen. Connoisseurs. You walk in there with your hands showing and talk shop, and that door opens. It's the one language money respects more than money."

"And if he asks me to prove it?"

"Then prove it. You've been proving it your whole life. You just never let anyone watch."

Pip squeezed his fingers once—quick, fierce, gone—and stepped back into the shadow of Maren's cloak. Odile drifted closer, close enough that the lantern light bent faintly around where she stood, a heat-shimmer in the shape of a woman.

*I'll be listening,* she said. *For once, so will everyone.*

Jude took a breath, pulled his hands from his pockets, and walked between the teeth of the gate.

* * *

Collector Vane received him in a gallery that made Jude's skin crawl in ways he couldn't immediately name.

It wasn't the bones themselves. Bones were honest; he'd said so and meant it. It was the *arrangement*. Every piece sat under glass or behind velvet rope, labeled in gold leaf, lit from below like theater. A saint's fingerbone in a reliquary of blackened silver. A dragon's tooth the size of a forearm, provenance disputed. A wall of memorial tokens—hundreds of them, each one someone's grief, someone's name, hung in ordered rows like stamps in an album. Jude's eye, trained by years of ledgers, caught the mislabeled ones immediately. Two tokens swapped. One attributed to the wrong century. Small errors no one here would ever notice, because no one here cared what the bones *meant*. Only what they cost.

"You're staring at my Wall of Voices," said a voice like oiled hinges.

Vane was a small man made mostly of rings. His fingers glittered as he gestured; his smile glittered worse. "Most visitors find it moving. The bereaved especially. Grief, properly displayed, appreciates."

"Does it," Jude said.

"Oh, immensely. Now—" The rings rearranged themselves into a gesture of welcome. "Grellis says you claim carver's credentials. Unaffiliated, she says. No guild marks." His eyes, small and quick as a rat's, went to Jude's hands. "In this day and age, a craftsman without a guild is either a criminal or a romantic. Which are you?"

"A carver," Jude said, and turned his palms up.

He'd spent the morning preparing for exactly this, and even so, his heart hammered as Vane leaned in. The calluses were real—eleven years of them, knife-callus on the right thumb, burnish-callus on the palm, the deep groove in the middle finger where the burin rode. But calluses could be faked. What couldn't was what Vane found when he lifted one of Jude's hands and turned it in the lamplight with the proprietary air of a man appraising livestock.

"Hm," said Vane. "Memorial work. Long-form. You carve names for the dead."

"When I'm paid to."

"And when you're not?"

Jude met his eyes. "When I'm not, I carve them anyway. Somebody has to."

Something flickered behind the rat-bright gaze—not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but *recognition*, the look of a collector who has spotted an uncut stone in a riverbed. "Yes," Vane murmured. "Yes, I think you'll do. Come. Since you appreciate provenance, I have something you should see. Something I show to almost no one."

*Careful,* Odile breathed, at the edge of hearing.

*Too late,* Jude thought, and followed.

* * *

The case stood alone in the gallery's inner room, and it was the largest thing there: a cabinet of smoked glass and iron, tall as two men, lit from within by lamps turned low and gold. Velvet inside. A chair inside. And in the chair—

An old man.

He sat very straight, the way people sit when slumping has been punished out of them. His skin was brown and fine-lined as old paper, his hair white and braided close to the skull in a style Jude had seen only in the oldest guild portraits. His hands lay open on his knees, palms up, and even from across the room Jude could see the calluses. Carver's calluses. *His* calluses, mirrored.

A brass plaque on the glass read:

**LIVING RELIC — LAST OF THE WARDEN LINE**
**AGE UNKNOWN. DOES NOT SPEAK. VIEWING BY APPOINTMENT.**

"He's real," Maren said softly. She and Pip had slipped in behind a pair of departing buyers, and now she stood frozen at the case's edge, her hood fallen back, all pretense abandoned. "Jude. He's *real*. They put a man in a box and sold tickets."

"He isn't the last of anything," Jude heard himself say. His voice came out strange—flat, quiet, the voice he used for reading ledger totals aloud. "Look at his hands. Look at the braids. That's Warden work, yes, but the Warden line had daughters, Maren. The archive said *thought extinct*, not *proven extinct*. They didn't find the last one. They found the last one who *let himself be found*."

The old man's eyes opened.

They were pale, nearly colorless, and they went first to Maren—at the sigilwright's scar, Jude realized, at the mark of her fallen order—and then to Pip, and then, slowly, with an expression that cracked something open in Jude's chest, to Jude's own upturned hands. To the calluses. To the groove of the burin.

The old man's mouth moved. No sound. But his fingers moved too, lifting from his knees, and began to trace shapes against the velvet—small, precise shapes, over and over.

Jude knew those shapes. He'd made them ten thousand times.

*Token-script. The carver's shorthand. He's spelling.*

"What's he saying?" Pip whispered. "What's he saying, Jude?"

Jude's throat was tight. "'Name,'" he translated. "He's carving 'name' in the air. He wants—" And then he understood, and the understanding arrived like cold water. "He wants to know if we can read it. He's been in there for gods know how long, signing into the dark, and he wants to know if anyone can *read*."

He raised his own hand and answered in token-script, quick and sure: *I read. I hear. We came for you.*

The old man went utterly still.

Then, slowly, tears cutting lines through the dust-fine powder on his face—he'd been arranged, Jude realized. Powdered. Presented. Like everything else in this room—he signed one word back.

*Finally.*

* * *

What happened next, Jude would remember afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a storm: in pieces, out of order, all of it louder than sense.

He remembered Vane's voice behind them, silk over steel: "I don't believe we concluded our appointment." He remembered the soft click of the gallery door locking, and Grellis the gatekeeper filling it like a wall. He remembered Maren stepping in front of Pip, one hand already sketching the opening strokes of a warding sigil in the air. He remembered Odile, invisible to everyone but him, saying with terrible calm: *They will not let him leave, Jude. Men like this never do. Decide who you are.*

And he remembered looking at the case—at Sef, though he didn't yet know the name, at the old man's palms still open, still waiting, still *signing finally finally finally* into the smoked glass—and feeling eleven years of careful, competent invisibility burn off him like morning fog.

Because here was the thing. Here was the whole rotten thing of it.

Jude had spent his life among people who catalogued grief and called it commerce. He'd written the entries. He'd balanced the books. He'd told himself that seeing and doing were different trades, that a ledger-clerk's silence was neutrality, that invisibility was safety. And all the while, somewhere in a glass box, a man who shared his craft and his blood had been *displayed*. Priced. Lit from below.

There was no neutral seat in this room. There never had been.

"Open the case," Jude said.

Vane's eyebrows rose, delighted. "I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me." Jude turned to face him fully, and was distantly surprised to find his hands weren't shaking. "The Warden line isn't extinct, Vane—it's *inventory*, and you know it, and so does whoever sold him to you. That makes you not a collector but a jailer with good lighting. So open the case, return his effects, and I'll consider whether the Guild hears about your provenance problems from me or from someone less polite."

"Provenance problems." Vane laughed, a dry rustle. "Mr.—?"

"Underhill."

"Mr. Underhill. Do you know what I pay for anonymity? For *my* anonymity, and for the discretion of my clients? More than you'll earn in a lifetime of carving names for peasants." He snapped his ringed fingers, and Grellis began to move. "But since you've raised the subject of politeness—Grellis, escort our romantic to the street. Firmly."

Maren's sigil flared blue-white. Pip grabbed Jude's coat. Odile said, sharp as a blade: *Left!*

And Jude—quiet, careful, invisible-for-eleven-years Jude—did not go left, did not run, did not reach for stealth or sense or any of the hundred survival instincts that had kept him alive this long.

Instead he picked up the nearest object—a dragon's tooth, provenance disputed, price upon request—and brought it down on the case's lock with all the righteous, terrified, long-overdue force of a man setting down a weight he'd carried since childhood.

Glass shattered. Alarms—actual bells, hidden in the jawbone arch—began to scream across the Bone Market.

"*Run*," Maren shouted, and then, incredulous, half-laughing despite everything: "*Jude!*"

Sef was already out of the broken case, moving faster than a man his age had any right to, one hand gripping Jude's sleeve like an anchor. Behind them Vane was shrieking about ruin and restitution and *names, I will have all your names*—and Jude, running between the teeth of the gate with a fugitive sigilwright, a Hollow-born child, a ghost, and a legend at his heels, realized he was smiling.

Let them have his name. Let them write it in every ledger in the city.

For the first time in his life, Jude Underhill intended to be *found*.
