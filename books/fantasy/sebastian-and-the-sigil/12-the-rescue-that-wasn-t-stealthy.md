## 12. The Rescue That Wasn't Stealthy

Maren Oduya had planned heists before. Good ones. Elegant ones. In her prime, she had lifted a sigil-etched reliquary out of the Vault of Saint Aldery with nothing but a forged writ, a borrowed coat, and a smile she'd practiced in a spoon. The plan for Sef had been elegant too: Jude would carve his way into the collector's favor with a demonstration piece, Maren would slip a quieting sigil onto the display case's ward-lock, Pip would crawl through the ventilation gap no adult could fit through, and by morning bell they'd be three districts away with an old man who had never once known they existed.

The plan lasted exactly as long as it took Pip to see the case.

They were in the back gallery of Colvess the Younger's private bone market—a place that smelled of tallow and old money—standing before a glass-and-brass cabinet lit by a single lamp, and inside it sat Sef like a saint in a shrine nobody had asked for. He was small and brown and folded up like a letter someone had given up reading. His wrists bore the keeper's mark, faded to the color of tea left too long. Around him, arranged on velvet like treasures, were his own carvings: tiny spires, tiny doors, tiny people with their hands raised as if waving goodbye.

Pip pressed both palms flat against the glass.

"He's *sad*," Pip said, in the carrying whisper of a child who has never once managed a real whisper. "Maren. Maren, he's sad *in there*."

"I know," Maren murmured, working the ward-lock pick under her sleeve. "That's why we're here. Quietly."

"He's crying."

"He is not—" Maren glanced. The old man's face was dry, but something in it was worse than crying: the particular stillness of someone who stopped expecting anything a very long time ago.

"Is that the man from the book?" Pip asked. "The third blood?"

"Yes."

"Then why are we being quiet?"

Because, Maren wanted to say, because the gallery had two guards, a ward-hound sleeping by the door, and a collector upstairs who paid the guild for protection; because quiet was the whole architecture of the plan; because she had built her entire second life on the principle that elegance meant never being seen at all.

But Pip had both hands on the glass now, and Pip's eyes had gone that strange Hollow silver, and Maren remembered—too late, always too late—that children from beneath the veil did not entirely understand the rules of this side of things.

"Pip," she said carefully, "step away from the—"

Pip breathed on the glass.

Not blew. *Breathed*, the way you breathe on a cold window to draw a picture, except what bloomed across the pane was not fog but frost-sigils, curling and spreading, and the ward-lock under Maren's pick went from warm to screaming-cold to simply, comprehensively dead.

Every lamp in the gallery guttered.

Somewhere upstairs, a bell began to ring.

"*Well*," said Maren, with the calm of a woman watching eleven days of planning evaporate, "there goes stealth."

* * *

To her credit—and she would want this noted, wherever notes were kept—she adapted fast.

"Jude!" she hissed across the dark gallery. "Change of approach!"

Jude materialized out of the shadows near the service stair, which was unnerving, because she hadn't seen him move. He'd gotten good at that lately, moving like a man who'd spent years being invisible and had finally decided to use it professionally.

"What happened?"

"The child happened. The child is a natural disaster with opinions." She wrenched at the case's brass frame. "Help me get this open before the guards—"

The guards arrived first. Of course they did. Two of them, lanterns high, cudgels drawn, faces set in the expressions of men who expected a simple burglary and were about to have a very educational evening.

"Hold there! In the guild's name—"

"It's not the guild's name," Maren snapped, still pulling at the case. "It's a collector's name. Get your rhetoric right if you're going to threaten people."

"Maren," Jude said quietly.

"What?"

"Down."

She dropped. A guard's cudgel whistled over her head and cracked against the display case instead, shattering the already-dead glass into a glittering rain. Which meant, she realized as she rolled upright among the shards, that the case was open. Technically. By force. Through no virtue of her elegant plan whatsoever.

Sef looked up at them from the wreckage of his velvet throne, and his eyes—old, dark, unhurried—went first to Jude's wrist, where the keeper's scar showed pale in the lamplight.

Then he smiled. It transformed his whole face, twenty years falling off it at once, and Maren thought: *oh no, he's one of those, one of the ones who's kind.*

The second guard grabbed for Jude. Jude ducked—not gracefully, he'd never been a fighter—but the guard's hand closed on empty air anyway, because between one heartbeat and the next the man went white as milk and staggered backward, clawing at his own ears.

"What did you—" the guard gasped. "What's *in this room*—"

"Nothing," said Jude, in a voice Maren had learned to recognize: gentle, apologetic, and absolutely lying. "Nothing at all. You should probably leave. All of you. There's a great deal of nothing in here and it's very upset."

The first guard fled. The second followed on the grounds that whatever wasn't in the room was clearly worse than the cudgel he was dropping on his way out.

The ward-hound, wisely, did not get involved. It put its tail over its nose and pretended to be furniture.

* * *

Getting Sef out was its own comedy. He was light—too light, Maren thought, anger flaring fresh every time she registered how light—and slow, and insisted, in gestures, on stopping twice: once to take his carvings off the ruined velvet, gathering them into his shirt with the care of a man collecting children; and once at the market's back gate, where he laid one palm flat against the doorpost and left there, glowing faintly for a heartbeat, a mark Maren recognized and had hoped never to see outside of forbidden books.

A waymark. A keeper's waymark, keyed to the Spire road.

"You know where we're going," she said.

He nodded.

"And you've been there."

He nodded again, and touched his own throat, and shook his head—the whole story of his muteness in two gestures, though whether the silence was born or made, Maren couldn't yet tell.

Then the bells started properly, all of them, every tower in the district, and the night turned into a footrace.

They went through the tannery row with its reeking vats, over the drying-fences while a tanner's dog sang behind them, down the bone-carvers' stair that Jude knew from his guild years—narrow, greasy, lit by nothing—and out along the canal towpath where Maren's lungs filed a formal complaint. Pip rode on Sef's shoulders, chattering the entire way, telling him things in a mixture of words and Hollow-sign that somehow the old man seemed to follow perfectly, nodding along, occasionally patting the child's ankle as if to say *yes, I know, terrible, isn't it.*

Jude ran beside Maren, breathing hard.

"They'll report to Vess," he said. "The description. Four of us, one old, one small. She'll have the map of every route out of the district within the hour."

"I know."

"She'll know we're headed for the Spire."

"She already knew, Jude. She's known since the tunnels." Maren vaulted a mooring post. "What she didn't know—what *Corvane* didn't know—was whether we could actually do it. Now they do. We've traded our cover for certainty, and honestly? Certainty was getting expensive anyway."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not a comforting woman. I'm a sigilwright. Comfort is for priests and bakers."

Behind them, the market district lit up with lanterns, a spreading stain of orange against the dark, and Maren understood with perfect clarity that the Meridian Guild's quiet era of pretending the covenant didn't exist had just ended, loudly, in a shattered display case. Whatever came next would come fast.

* * *

They holed up before dawn in a smuggler's boathouse Maren had used twice in another life, all rot and rope-smell and one candle stub. Sef sat down on an overturned skiff, Pip folded into the crook of his arm like the last puzzle piece finding its place, and for a moment nobody spoke. The adrenaline drained out of the room and left everyone wrung out and slightly hysterical.

"Well," Maren said at last. "We're burned, hunted, and four days from a mountain pass with the entire guild between us and it. But we have the third bloodline. So on balance I'd call it a triumph."

"We broke a lot of glass," Pip offered solemnly.

"We did. We broke a truly educational amount of glass."

Jude crouched in front of Sef, slow and careful, the way he approached everything that mattered. "Can you tell us your name?" he asked. "Or—can you tell us anything? About the keepers? About the rite?"

Sef regarded him for a long moment. Then he held up one finger: *wait.* He dug into his shirt and brought out the carvings, laying them on the skiff's hull between them, sorting through the little bone pieces with fingers that trembled only slightly.

He picked up a carved spire. Set it down in front of Jude.

Picked up a tiny figure with raised hands. Placed it beside the spire.

Then a third piece: a small, broken archway, cracked clean through.

"Sef," Maren breathed. "You carve in tokens. Full token-script."

Jude had gone very still. "My mother used token-script," he said softly. "For messages too dangerous to write. I thought—I thought it was just her. Just our family."

Sef looked at him then, really looked, and something passed between the two carvers that Maren, standing outside it, could only witness: the recognition of craft, of lineage, of a language shared by exactly the sort of people the world kept trying to erase. Sef reached out and turned Jude's wrist over gently, thumb brushing the scar, and made a low sound in his chest—not speech, but agreement. Greeting. *Kin.*

Then he got to work, quick now, arranging pieces on the hull: the spire, the figure, the broken arch. Then he added a fourth carving Maren hadn't seen him make—one he must have cut in the dark of his case, over years, waiting—a tiny boat with a hole in its hull.

"A warning," Jude translated slowly, his voice changing as the grammar settled. "'Three go to the Spire. One falls.' Or—" he frowned, rearranging, "—no. Not *falls*. 'One is given.'" He looked up. "Sef, what does that mean? Who's given?"

Sef closed the old man's hands over the little boat and tucked it away, and his face was patient and sorrowful and utterly unhelpful, and Maren wanted to shake him and hug him in equal measure.

"He'll show us when it matters," she said grimly. "Token-carvers are like that. My grandmother was the same. They never give you the whole sentence until you've earned the punctuation."

Outside, the sky was going gray at the edges. Somewhere across the city, Captain Vess was reading reports and drawing lines on maps, and every line pointed east, toward the Gray Pass, toward the Spire, toward four days that were now three.

Jude sat down on the skiff beside Sef, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and began—quietly, wordlessly—to sort the remaining carvings into rows, learning the old man's vocabulary piece by piece. Sef watched him with an expression Maren could only call *delight*, the delight of a master finding one last student when he'd believed the school long dead.

Pip fell asleep against Sef's ribs mid-sentence, one small fist still curled around a tiny carved door.

And Maren stood watch at the boathouse door with the candle guttering, and found, to her considerable irritation, that her eyes were stinging. Not fear. She knew fear intimately; this wasn't it. This was the other thing, the thing she'd sworn off after her disgrace, the thing that got you hurt:

These ridiculous people. A bone-carver who heard ghosts, a feral child from under the world, a voiceless old relic, and herself, a drunk with a criminal's hands and a scholar's memory. Allies, she'd called them. Assets. A fragile alliance, she'd written it in her own head, careful not to use any warmer word.

But somewhere between the shattered glass and the sleeping child and the two carvers bent together over their silent language, *allies* had quietly died, and something else had taken its seat without asking permission.

Family, then. Fine. She'd been exiled from one family already; apparently she was constitutionally incapable of learning.

She heard footsteps behind her—Jude, moving soft, coming to stand at her shoulder.

"You should sleep," he said. "I'll take the watch."

"In a moment." She nodded east, where the gray was strengthening. "They'll have riders on the Pass road by noon."

"I know."

"And Corvane won't send constables next time. He'll send the quiet ones."

"I know that too."

Maren sighed and handed him the candle stub. "Then let's get our family moving before the world catches up."

Behind them, in the dark of the boathouse, Sef was already awake—had perhaps never slept—and was pressing one last carved token into Pip's sleeping hand: a tiny spire, whole and unbroken, its point aimed east.
