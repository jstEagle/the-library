## 13. What Jude Buried

The fire was Sef's idea.

He had made it clear in his wordless way—tapping a carved token against Jude's knee, then miming cupped hands and striking flint with two stones—while Maren argued that smoke would draw guild hunters like flies to a jam jar. But they were three days out from the city, deep in the hill country where the roads went to ribbons and the pines swallowed sound, and even Maren had to admit that cold was its own kind of beacon. A shivering man cannot walk quietly. So they built the fire small, in a hollow ringed by granite, and Sef fed it one stick at a time as though each were a promise he intended to keep.

Pip fell on the food like a small wolf. The child had eaten two of Maren's travel biscuits, pronounced them "rocks that lied about being food," and then eaten a third anyway. Now Pip sat cross-legged by the flames, turning a pinecone over and over, watching it the way other children watched beetles—with the patient hunger of someone cataloguing a world.

Maren was cleaning her sigil-needles. She did this every night whether they needed cleaning or not, laying them out on a square of oiled cloth in order of fineness, and every night she checked the order twice. Jude had come to understand it was not fussiness. It was how she prayed.

And Odile stood at the edge of the firelight, half in, half out, her outline flickering like heat off summer stone. She had been quieter these last days. Watching him. Jude knew what was coming. Ghosts always circled before they spoke; the dead, like surgeons, liked to mark their incisions first.

"You're going to ask me," he said, without looking up from the token he was carving—a spire, small enough to sit in a palm, for no reason except that his hands needed work when his chest felt tight.

"Ask you what?" Odile said, though her tone said *you know exactly what*.

"About my mother."

Maren's needles stopped moving. Even Pip looked up.

"I wasn't going to ask," Odile said gently. "I was going to wait. There's a difference."

"There isn't, with you. You just wait louder."

That startled a laugh out of Maren—one short, rusty bark, as if the laugh had been stored somewhere unheated for years. Sef's mouth twitched. He could not speak; the collectors who had caged him had taken his voice long ago, some ritual or cruelty Jude didn't yet have the stomach to hear about. But he communicated plenty through his tokens, and just now he set down a little bone disc carved with an open eye, and tapped it twice, and looked at Jude with an expression of terrible kindness.

*We see you,* the token said. *You may as well say it.*

Jude turned the half-carved spire over in his fingers. The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which struck him suddenly as the funniest thing in the world, because he had spent thirty years doing the same.

"All right," he said. "All right."

* * *

"I was nine," he began, and found his voice came out steadier than he expected, the way a rope holds better once you've committed your weight to it. "My mother was a laundress in the Tannery Quarter. Worked herself gray by forty. And I'd been hearing things since I was six—the dead, murmuring. Under doors. In drains. In the walls of the washhouse where the steam never quite cleared." He shrugged. "I thought everyone heard them. I thought it was like weather. Then I mentioned it at supper one night and my mother went white as bleached linen and told me never, ever to say it again. Not to anyone. Not even to her."

"Because of the guild," Maren said quietly. It wasn't a question. Everyone in the trade knew what the Meridian Guild did with people who heard too much. They didn't burn them anymore—that was untidy, and drew questions. They simply made them disappear into contracts, into cellars, into ledgers.

"Because of the guild," Jude agreed. "So I learned to be quiet. I learned to hum when they got loud. I learned which streets to avoid—the ones near old battlefields, the ones with the plague pits under the cobbles. I got very good at it. Being quiet." He smiled, and hated the smile, and kept it anyway because it was armor. "I was so good at it that I convinced myself it was a skill instead of a wound."

Pip had stopped fidgeting with the pinecone. The child's eyes were enormous in the firelight, and there was nothing afraid in them—only attention, total and unblinking, the kind of listening most people never manage in a whole life.

"The night she died," Jude said, "I was eleven. Fever came through the Tannery Quarter in autumn. She took to her bed on a Tuesday. By Thursday she was talking nonsense, and by Friday—" His thumb found the groove of the carving and pressed there, hard. "By Friday night I was sleeping in the loft above the washhouse because I couldn't stand the sound of her breathing change. And then, past midnight, I heard her."

The fire cracked a knot. No one moved.

"Not her body calling for water. Not the fever voice. *Her.* Clear as a bell rung inside my skull. She said—" and here, thirty years later, the words still arrived whole, still wore her voice like a borrowed coat, "—she said, *Jude-love, come hold my hand. I don't want to go alone.*"

Odile drifted closer. The firelight passed through her and came out the far side dimmed, as if she were a window with something behind it.

"And I ran," Jude said. "Not away. Don't mistake me—I didn't leave the building. I just... buried myself. Pulled the blanket over my head and hummed until my ears rang and I made myself so quiet, so *deeply* quiet, that I couldn't hear anything at all. Not her. Not the dead. Nothing. I lay there in the dark humming like a mad little monk until morning, when the neighbor woman found her." He looked at the fire. "She died reaching for a hand that wasn't there. Because her son was hiding under a blanket, humming."

The silence afterward was not empty. It was full—the particular fullness of a room where everyone is holding space for one person, the way water holds a dropped stone.

"I've muted it ever since," he went on, because now that the dam had cracked he might as well let the whole flood through. "Every day. Every hour. It takes effort—you can't imagine. Like holding a door shut against a tide. That's why I'm tired all the time. That's why I'm *gray* all the time. I have spent my entire life paying rent on a silence I built the night my mother called for me and I pretended to be asleep." He laughed, short and awful. "And then a courier dies in my arms and hands me a map, and a ghost follows me home, and a sigilwright tells me I'm the blood of covenant-keepers, and the whole time I keep thinking: *they've chosen the wrong man. They've handed the covenant to a coward who wouldn't hold his own mother's hand.*"

"Stop," Odile said.

It was not harsh. But it cut through everything else like a needle through cloth, and Jude stopped.

"Come here," she said. "Into the light. All the way."

"I don't need—"

"You've hidden at the edge of every room you've ever stood in, Jude Underhill. I've watched you do it for weeks. Come into the light."

His legs felt foreign, but they carried him. He sat on the flat rock beside Sef, close enough to the fire that its warmth pressed against his face like a palm.

"Now listen to me," Odile said, "because I died guarding a map, and I have had a very long time to think about dying, and about what we owe the people who call for us." Her form steadied, sharpened, until for a moment she looked almost alive—young, even, with wind-chapped cheeks and a courier's lean hardness. "You were *eleven*. Do you understand what you're doing? You're putting a grown man's guilt on a child's shoulders and making him carry it for thirty years. An eleven-year-old is not equipped to watch death. An eleven-year-old is barely equipped to watch *dinner*."

"But I heard her—"

"You heard her, and it terrified you, and you did what terrified children do. You hid." Odile knelt—or made the shape of kneeling, beside him, her face level with his. "Do you know what I did the night the enforcers caught me? I had the map. I had a fast horse. I had a head start. And I froze. Stood in an alley like a post while they closed in, because I was thinking about a promise I'd broken to my fellow keepers years before, and the guilt locked my knees. If I'd run, the map might have reached the Spire a season sooner. People might already be saved." She shook her head slowly. "Guilt freezes everyone, Jude. The living and the dead alike. It is not a moral failing. It is a wound. And wounds are not confessions—they're injuries, and injuries want tending, not punishment."

"You don't owe your mother silence," Maren said. She had resumed cleaning her needles, but her hands had gone slow, almost ceremonial. "You owe her the truth, which is this: you loved her, you were a child, and you have punished yourself so thoroughly and for so long that frankly it's become a bit self-indulgent."

"Maren," Sef rapped, scandalized—or rather he tapped a token carved with a jagged line, which in Sef's vocabulary meant roughly *that was blunt even for you.*

"What? It's true." Maren set down her needles and looked at Jude squarely. "I drank for eleven years because I mis-scribed a sigil and a bridge ward failed and four people drowned. Eleven years. You know what finally sobered me? Realizing the drinking wasn't grief. Grief had left years before. What stayed was the *performance* of grief—the comfortable, familiar misery of it. Misery can be a hiding place same as any blanket." She nodded at him. "Yours just happens to be made of humming."

Jude wanted to be angry. He searched for the anger and found, instead, something worse: recognition. He huffed out a breath that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been so close to the other thing.

"You're all terrible," he said. "This is supposed to be a confession. There's supposed to be solemnity."

"We're solemn," Pip announced. "Aren't we, Sef?"

Sef tapped a token carved with a straight, grave line. Then, after a moment, another one: a crooked little figure with its arms open wide.

Pip scrambled up, crossed the fire-ring, and climbed onto the rock beside Jude with the absolute entitlement of the very young and the very small. "Sef says ghosts forgive easier than people do," Pip translated, "on account of they've seen how everything ends and it's mostly just people being scared." The child settled against Jude's side, bony shoulder fitting under his arm as though the space had been measured for it. "Also Sef says your humming was probably terrible."

"It was," Jude admitted. His throat had gone tight. "I only ever knew the one song. My mother used to sing it over the washboards. I hummed it wrong for thirty years."

"Sing it right, then," Pip said, with the simple ferocity of a command from a small general.

So he did. Quietly, roughened, more spoken than sung—the wash-song, the one about the river taking the dirt and leaving the cloth, the one every laundress in the Tannery Quarter sang and no one remembered learning. His voice cracked in the middle and he kept going. Odile closed her eyes. Maren's hands went still over her needles. Sef bowed his white head, and his fingers moved silently across his tokens, tracing the melody he couldn't voice.

When it was done, Pip yawned enormously, a yawn that seemed to involve the child's entire skeleton, and burrowed deeper against Jude's ribs.

"That was better," Pip mumbled. "Still not as good as Mama's singing. But better." A pause. "I don't remember my mama's singing. But I know it was better."

Jude's arm curled around the child without asking his permission. "Then when we get you home," he said, "we'll find someone who remembers it, and they'll teach you, and you can carry it back into the Hollow yourself."

"Mm," said Pip, already sinking. "You smell like bone dust and campfire. It's a good smell. Don't move."

Within a handful of breaths the child was asleep, one fist knotted in Jude's shirt, breathing in the slow deep rhythm of someone who trusted the ground beneath them and the person above them. Jude sat very still, hardly daring to shift, feeling the small weight of it—the actual, physical weight of being needed—and understood with sudden clarity that this was what his mother had wanted that night. Not ceremony. Not absolution. Just this: a hand held in the dark, a small warm weight against her side, proof she wasn't going alone.

"I'm sorry I couldn't give it to you," he whispered, to her, to the dark, to whatever part of her had gone wherever mothers go.

"She knows," Odile said softly. "They always know. It's the living who take so long to learn it."

* * *

Later, when Maren had taken first watch and Sef slept sitting upright with his tokens arrayed on his knees like a chessboard mid-game, Odile remained by the fire. Jude hadn't moved—Pip's weight had long since gone from burden to anchor—but sleep wouldn't come, and Odile seemed content to keep him company in the waking hours, which was, he was learning, one of the great underrated services of the dead.

"Can I ask you something?" he said. "Now that we're confessing."

"Confession's cheap," Odile said. "Ask away."

"When you taught me to listen deliberately—to sort the voices, to choose what I hear instead of drowning—" He hesitated. "You never said what happens when the covenant is reforged. To you. Keepers who lend their death-memory to the rite. What does it cost?"

The firelight moved through her. For a long moment she said nothing, and Jude braced himself for deflection, for a ghost's evasions.

But Odile had never once lied to him.

"It costs everything I have left," she said simply. "A keeper's death-memory is the keystone. Without it, the sigil has no weight. With it..." She spread her hands, translucent palms up. "I finish. Whatever unfinished thing has kept me walking gets finished, and I go where the dead go when they're done. Which, for the record, is nowhere bad. I've listened to enough crossings to know. It's quiet, and it's warm, and nobody's running." She smiled at whatever his face was doing. "Don't make that expression. I've been dead for months, Jude. I've been *tired* for longer. Finishing isn't a punishment. It's a door closing gently behind you after a very long walk."

"You could have not told me."

"You could have not hummed," she said. "We're both done hiding, aren't we?"

He looked at her—at the courier who had died so a map could live, who had walked beside a stranger for weeks asking nothing, teaching everything—and found his eyes stinging.

"Eleven days," he said. "Maren says the equinox is eleven days out. The Spire's eight hard days from here, if the weather holds and the hunters don't find us."

"Then we'd best sleep when we can and walk when we can't," Odile said. But as Jude finally settled back against the rock, careful of the sleeping child, her voice followed him down into the dark, quiet as ash settling:

"One more thing, carver. Your mother's song—the river one. The dead in the city used to hum it too, you know. Laundresses who crossed over, decades back. It travels." A pause, and when she spoke again there was something in it he had never heard from her—something almost shy. "It's how I found you, that night at the loading dock. Before the map. Before any of it. I heard a man humming a dead woman's song wrong, and I thought: *there. That one listens, even if he doesn't know it.*"

Jude lay awake a long while after that, Pip's heartbeat tapping against his ribs like a second, smaller pulse, and stared at the stars through the pine branches, and did not hum, and did not need to.

Eight days to the Spire. And somewhere behind them, unseen in the dark, Captain Vess was already climbing.
