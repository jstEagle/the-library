## 14. Vess's True Master

Captain Sera Vess had been summoned to the top of the Meridian Guild tower eleven times in her career. She knew the count because she counted things when she was nervous—stairs, breaths, the number of times her boot heel clicked on marble. Eleven summons in fourteen years of service, and every one of them had ended with her feeling smaller than she'd started.

This would be the twelfth.

She climbed the last flight of stairs slowly, giving herself time to arrange her face into the expression that had earned her three commendations: calm, competent, faintly bored. A woman who had seen everything and been impressed by nothing. It was a good face. It had never once told the truth about how tired she was.

The doors to Guildmaster Corvane's study were older than the rest of the tower—black oak, iron-banded, carved with the guild's founding sigil. Vess had walked past them a thousand times without really looking at the sigil. Tonight, waiting for the porter to announce her, she looked.

It was a circle broken by a line. She'd always assumed it was decorative. Some merchant's idea of mysticism, the way rich men bought maps of places they'd never go.

The porter knocked. The doors opened. Corvane's voice said, "Send her in," before the porter could speak, which meant he'd been watching for her, which meant this was not a routine report.

"Captain." Corvane stood at his window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the city lights come on below. He was a spare man, silver at the temples, dressed the way all guildmasters dressed—in gray so fine it looked like weather. "You've had a difficult week."

"Yes, sir."

"The courier. Odile Renne. Dead in your guild yard." He turned then, and his face was kind in the way that expensive faces are kind. "And the map gone. And a bone-carver who has suddenly become very interesting."

Vess kept her spine straight. "Underhill claims he doesn't have it. I searched his rooms myself. Nothing."

"And you believe him?"

"I believe he's a bad liar," Vess said. "Which is worse, in some ways. Bad liars do unpredictable things."

Corvane smiled. It reached his eyes, which unsettled her more than if it hadn't. "Sit down, Captain. Have a drink. You look like a woman who's been sleeping in her uniform."

She hadn't been. She'd been sleeping in a chair beside her office door with a loaded crossbow across her knees, but there was no version of this conversation where admitting that helped her.

He poured two glasses of amber liquor from a decanter that probably cost more than her annual salary and handed her one. Then he sat across from her, close enough that she could smell the cedar oil in his hair, and said, pleasantly:

"Tell me what you know about the Hollow."

* * *

Vess had been with the guild since she was nineteen. She'd joined for the same reason most people joined—for the pension, the boots, the sense that the world had edges and someone was paid to stand on them. In fourteen years she'd broken up smuggling rings, recovered stolen relics, escorted cartographers through territories that ate cartographers. She'd arrested a man who sold bottled thunder. She'd once spent six weeks tracking a ledger-thief through the Salt Fens and come back with leech scars she still itched in damp weather.

In all that time, the Hollow had been a word. A place on old maps marked with hatching, like a hazard. Children's stories—a world under the world, where the dead went when they weren't done being people. Guild policy on the Hollow was one sentence long, and Vess had read it a hundred times in the training manuals: *The Meridian Guild does not acknowledge the existence of the Hollow.*

That was the whole policy. Not denial as strategy. Denial as architecture.

So when Corvane asked her what she knew, she said, "Nothing, sir. Officially."

"Officially," he repeated, tasting the word. "Good. That's the correct answer. Now let me tell you what you know unofficially, and we'll see how good a listener you are."

He rose and went to a cabinet against the far wall—one she'd never noticed, which bothered her, because noticing things was her profession—and drew out a scroll sealed with black wax. He did not offer it to her. He simply set it on the desk between them, unrolled it flat, and weighted the corners with his inkwell, a paperweight shaped like a bull, and her untouched glass.

It was a map. But not like any map she'd seen. The city was there—their city, the streets she'd patrolled for half her life—but drawn beneath it, in lines that seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at them, was another city. Older. Denser. Built downward instead of upward, or perhaps built first and the other city built on top of it like a lid.

"The covenant," Corvane said, "is an agreement. Made four hundred years ago between the founders of this guild and the keepers of the Hollow. The living realm gets stability. The Hollow gets... continued existence. Two worlds, sharing a wall, each agreeing not to lean on it." He tapped the map. "The wall is failing. Has been failing for decades. And the guild's official position—" he smiled again, that expensive smile, "—has always been that there is no wall."

"You're saying the stories are true."

"I'm saying," Corvane said gently, "that you've spent your career protecting this city from threats it couldn't see, and the largest of them has been sitting underneath it the entire time, held in place by a handful of dying bloodlines and a piece of paper nobody's read aloud in a century. The keepers are nearly extinct. The last of them are running around my city right now with a map they stole from a dead woman, planning to perform a rite at the Threshold Spire that would bind us to that place for another four hundred years."

Vess looked at the map. The lower city seemed to breathe. "And you don't want them to succeed."

"I want you to understand what success would mean." Corvane came around the desk and stood beside her, looking down at the map with something she might have mistaken for grief, if she hadn't known better. "Four hundred more years of feeding it. Do you know what the covenant costs us, Captain? Every year, a little more. Magic bleeds out of our world into theirs. Our sigilwrights' craft thins. Our healers' arts fade. We've been paying rent on our own ground since before anyone alive can remember, to a kingdom of ghosts who contribute nothing."

"They're people," Vess heard herself say.

Corvane looked at her with mild surprise, as if a chair had spoken. "They're *dead*, Captain. They had their turn. The Hollow is a hospice that refuses to close." He straightened. "When the covenant fails—and it will fail, with or without interference, because everything fails—the veil will thin and then tear, and what leaks through will be manageable. Contained. And what remains in the Hollow will be ours to harvest. Every art we've lost. Every craft. Four hundred years of stolen magic, returned with interest."

"Harvest," Vess said. The word tasted like the fens.

"A strong word. I prefer 'recovery.'" He picked up his own glass and drank, unhurried. "Here is what happens now. Underhill and his companions will make for the Spire—they have no other play, and my sources confirm they've acquired the third bloodline, which I confess I didn't expect. Old Sef. The collector will be compensated." He said it the way a man says *the gardener will be notified*. "You will take your company up the Gray Pass and intercept them before the equinox. No killing unless necessary. I want the map intact and the keepers alive. Dead keepers can't be questioned, and there may yet be uses for keeper blood that don't require their cooperation."

Vess sat very still. Somewhere below them, the evening bells rang the closing hour, and she thought absurdly of Jude Underhill at his ledgers, of the small careful man she'd interrogated who lied so badly she'd almost felt sorry for him.

"Sir," she said. "When I took this assignment, I was told I was protecting the city from smugglers moving contraband across the veil. Relics. Dangerous artifacts."

"You were."

"You didn't tell me I'd be hunting a child."

Corvane paused. "A child?"

"There's a Hollow-born child with them. Eight years old, maybe nine. My scouts have seen her." Vess kept her voice level, professional, the voice of a woman reporting weather. "Feral. Can't get home. If the covenant falls, sir—if the Hollow dies—what happens to her?"

"She goes home," Corvane said, "or she ceases to be a problem. Either way, Captain, she is one child, and you are describing to me the fate of an entire city." He set down his glass. "I chose you for this because you don't flinch. Because in fourteen years you have done every ugly thing this guild has required of you and done it cleanly. Don't develop a conscience in the final week. It's unbecoming, and it's late."

* * *

Vess walked home the long way, which was a thing she did when she needed to think and would never admit to needing anything.

Her lodgings were two rooms above a chandler's shop in Tanners' Row. Modest, by choice—she'd been offered better quarters years ago and declined, because a captain who lived well attracted questions, and questions were the one thing she'd always avoided. She had built her whole life the way Underhill had built his, though she'd never have admitted the resemblance: small, quiet, invisible. Safe.

Tonight she climbed her stairs and did not light a lamp. She sat in the dark by the window with her boots off and her sword across her knees, and she counted.

Twelve summons. Fourteen years. One sentence of policy: *the guild does not acknowledge the existence of the Hollow.* She'd read that sentence as a girl of nineteen and thought it meant *there's nothing there.* She'd read it ten thousand times since and never once considered that it might mean *there is something there, and we are lying.*

She thought about the map. The lower city, breathing. She thought about harvest. She thought about the word *recovery,* and how a man could spend forty years choosing gentler words for uglier things until the gentle words wore grooves in him deep enough to live in.

She thought about the child.

Pip, the scouts called her—not a name, probably, just what the marsh folk shouted when she stole their fish. Eight years old. Vess had a niece that age. Her sister Marta's girl, Juniper, who lost teeth at an alarming rate and narrated her own dreams at breakfast in exhaustive detail. If Juniper fell through a hole in the world, Vess thought, and someone with a crossbow was sent to bring her back—

No. That wasn't the assignment. The assignment was intercept. Detain. Question. *No killing unless necessary.* Corvane had been precise about that, and precision was its own kind of reassurance, and she hated herself a little for how much the reassurance worked.

But then—why send twelve armed enforcers to detain a bone-carver, a drunk, a ghost, and a child? Why the Gray Pass? Why the urgency?

Because the Spire was real. Because the rite was real. Because four hundred years of quiet theft was about to be audited, and Corvane intended to rob the auditor.

Vess got up and lit the lamp after all. She dug through the bottom drawer of her desk—the one with the false back she'd installed herself, because even invisible women kept secrets—and pulled out the folder she'd assembled on Jude Underhill. Thin folder. Bone-carver, night clerk, no debts, no vices, no family. A man who catalogued impossible things and touched none of them. She'd written *no threat* in the margin of his file and meant it.

Then the second page. The interview transcript. Her own notes, in her own hand: *Subject lies poorly. Subject's left wrist scarred—birthmark or burn, subject evasive. Subject flinched twice at sounds I could not hear.*

At sounds I could not hear.

She'd written that at two in the morning after the interrogation and told herself it was fatigue. Now, sitting in her lamplight with Corvane's amber still burning sour in her throat, she let herself consider the other explanation. That the room had not been empty. That the dead courier had been standing there, saying something, and only Underhill had heard it.

The keepers heard the dead. Maren Oduya's old research said so—research the guild had confiscated, research Vess had filed herself, years ago, in a case she'd been told involved forgery. Everything she'd ever touched had been *something else.* Every clean job, every ugly thing done neatly—it had all been mortar in Corvane's wall, and she'd mixed it gladly, because the alternative was asking questions, and asking questions was how careers ended.

Or how they began again, said a small voice in her head that sounded annoyingly like her sister.

Vess closed the folder. She sat for a long time, counting—fourteen years, twelve summons, one child—and then she did the thing she had trained herself never to do.

She made a list of questions.

*What does the covenant actually cost us? Who audits Corvane's numbers? What happened to the last captain who asked? What happened to Odile Renne's body? Why does a guildmaster keep a four-hundred-year-old map in a locked cabinet like a man keeping proof?*

And beneath all of them, the question that mattered, the one she wrote last and underlined twice:

*If the Hollow dies, what exactly comes through the tear—and why won't he say?*

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. In all his elegant talk of harvest and recovery, of manageable leaks and contained failures, Corvane had never once described what was actually *in* the Hollow besides dead people and stolen magic. He'd talked around it the way men talk around a debt. And Sera Vess had spent fourteen years learning that whatever a powerful man won't name is the exact thing that will eventually bite you.

Outside, the city settled into night. Somewhere north, past the rooftops and the walls and the gray beginning of the mountains, four fugitives were climbing toward a spire with eleven days on the clock. Twelve armed enforcers would ride at dawn. Her orders were signed. Her company was loyal. Her career was a clean, straight road, and all she had to do was walk it.

Vess looked at her list of questions for a long moment.

Then she folded it small, tucked it into the breast pocket of her uniform—over the heart, where she kept the things she wasn't ready to admit to—and began, quietly, to pack a second bag.

Just in case, she told herself, of absolutely nothing in particular.

The lamp guttered. In the dark, her list of questions stayed folded over her heart, and it weighed, she thought, rather more than paper should.
