## 15. The Ascent Begins

Four days.

The number sat in Jude's chest like a swallowed stone as he stood at the mouth of the Gray Pass and looked up at what remained of the world. The pass did not so much climb into the mountains as claw its way between them—a throat of gray shale and older gray stone, patched with dirty snow that had given up trying to be white sometime last winter. Somewhere above, past the switchbacks and the wind-scoured saddles and whatever else the mountain intended to be rude about, the Threshold Spire waited.

Somewhere behind them, so did Vess's hunters.

"Cheerful place," Maren said, coming up beside him with her pack straps creaking and her expression suggesting she had personally reviewed every route in the world and chosen this one out of spite for herself specifically. "Reminds me of my last workshop review."

"Your workshop burned down," Jude said.

"See? Nostalgic already."

Pip scrambled up a boulder with the boneless confidence of a child who had never once considered falling, crouched at the top like a gargoyle, and peered down at them. "It smells wrong," Pip announced. "Not bad-wrong. Just wrong. Like when bread pretends to be cake."

"That is the single most useful reconnaissance report I have ever received," Maren said gravely, "and I once worked with a man who could smell lies."

"He was lying about that," Sef signed from his seat on a flat stone, his carved tokens clicking softly against each other in the pouch at his belt. The old man's hands moved with the economy of someone who had spent decades saying things without words: *Bread-cake. Good omen.*

Jude still wasn't entirely fluent in Sef's language of signs and tokens, but he was learning fast, mostly because Sef refused to let him not learn. There was something about being taught by a mute old man who had spent years in a collector's glass case—displayed, labeled, *owned*—that made complaining about the curriculum feel obscene.

"We move before the light goes," Jude said, and heard, with a small internal start, that he had said it the way you say a thing when people will actually do it. "Odile says there's a shelter cave two miles up. She remembers it."

He felt rather than saw Odile settle at his shoulder—that particular cold-and-present sensation he'd stopped flinching from somewhere around day six of her haunting him. *I remember more than caves,* she said dryly, in the voice only he could hear, which sounded to everyone else like a shift in the wind if they noticed anything at all. *I remember dying on this path. Do try to do better than I did.*

"You died buying time for others," Jude murmured under his breath. "That's a hard act to follow."

Maren glanced at him. "Talking to your ghost again?"

"She has opinions."

"All ghosts do. It's the living who've run out." She shouldered her pack. "Lead on, Underhill. You're the one with the map in your blood."

That was the trouble, really. He was the one with the map in his blood. The sigil on his wrist had grown warmer every mile northward, and by the time they'd made the foothills it had begun to itch in a way that felt less like skin and more like memory—the mark remembering where it came from faster than he did. Four days to the equinox. Three, if the weather turned. And behind them, gaining or not gaining—he couldn't tell anymore—guild hunters who wanted the map silenced, the keepers scattered, and the Hollow quietly allowed to die.

*They're back,* Odile said suddenly, all the dryness gone from her voice. *Jude. Look back. Now.*

He turned.

Far below, at the pass mouth they'd occupied an hour ago, four figures had appeared. Even at that distance he knew the walk of them—the economical stride of guild enforcers, men and women paid to make problems stop existing. Sunlight glinted off one of them: the dull silver of a suppression chain, the kind the guild used to collar rogue sigilwrights' work.

"They've picked up our trail," Maren said, following his gaze. Her voice stayed level, but Jude had spent enough weeks with her now to hear the wire underneath it. "How?"

"The sigil," Sef signed, then tapped his own wrist. *It shines. To those taught to see.*

Jude looked down at his arm. He couldn't see it shining. But then, he'd spent thirty years not seeing most of what was true about himself; one more item on the list.

"Then we don't stop until dark," he said. "And after dark, we don't stop either."

* * *

The Gray Pass earned its name honestly. Everything in it was gray—the stone, the snow, the lichen, even the light, which arrived filtered through cloud layers like an apology. By midday they were climbing switchbacks cut by hands long dead, and Jude found he could feel those hands too, faintly, the way he felt all old places: a murmur of labor and cold and small stubborn hope, the masons who'd cut these steps humming work songs that had been dust for centuries.

He listened to them while he climbed. It helped. Odile had been teaching him—not drowning in the dead but *listening deliberately*, choosing whose voices to hold. It was like learning to stand in a river instead of being swept by it. He still went under sometimes. But he surfaced faster now.

"Tell me about the rite again," he said, because Maren had asked him to ask her questions when the silence got too heavy, and because his own courage was a finite resource he was rationing carefully.

Maren didn't break stride. "Three keeper-bloods at the Spire's heart. Blood freely given, not taken. Each keeper offers the thing they most hide." She ticked them off on gloved fingers. "Sef hid his voice—gave it away long before the collectors ever took him, in a way. His tokens carry it now. Odile—" she paused, respectful, "—hers is already given, isn't it? A death remembered. That's why she can lend it."

"And mine," Jude said, and didn't finish, because finishing meant admitting aloud that the thing he most hid was his own silence—thirty years of holding his gift shut like a fist around a screaming bird—and that giving it up meant opening that hand forever.

"You'll manage," Maren said. "You're stubborn in a quiet way. Worst kind."

"I learned from watching the guild."

"Don't." But there was no edge in it. She'd forgiven him—or was in the process of forgiving him—for the years he'd spent keeping their ledgers blind. Forgiveness, Jude had learned, wasn't a door that opened. It was a stair you kept climbing.

Pip, meanwhile, had appointed herself scout, which meant running twenty paces ahead, tasting the snow (bad idea, done twice anyway), and reporting findings of variable utility. "There's a hole here!" she called back at one point. "A big hole! With darkness in it!"

"That would be the shelter cave," Jude called back. "Well spotted."

"It smells like old fire and somebody sad!"

"...Also well spotted."

The cave, when they reached it, did indeed smell of old fire, and—if you knew how to listen—of somebody sad. A shepherd, Jude gathered, from a hundred and some years ago, who'd sheltered here through a storm that killed his flock and very nearly him, and who'd left a cairn at the back wall as thanks to whatever gods bothered with shepherds. Jude stood in the entrance a moment longer than necessary, letting the dead man's grief wash through him and out, the way Odile had taught him: acknowledge, witness, release. *Everyone needs a witness eventually,* she'd said. *Even the ones nobody remembers. Especially those.*

"What is it?" Pip asked, tugging his sleeve. She could see ghosts plain as daylight, and she was looking at the empty corner where the shepherd lingered.

"He lost his sheep," Jude said. "Long time ago."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she walked over to the corner and waved cheerfully at the empty air. "Hello! Your sheep are probably fine. Sheep are good at finding grass."

Jude felt something in the corner ease—a loosening, like a knot finally slipping—and the shepherd's presence thinned toward peace. Pip grinned at him over her shoulder, gap-toothed and filthy and radiant.

*She does in ten seconds what takes you an hour,* Odile observed.

"She cheats," Jude said. "Children cheat. They haven't learned that ghosts are supposed to be frightening yet."

Sef had gotten a fire going with the economy of long practice, and they ate hard bread and harder cheese and drank snowmelt, and for one hour the Gray Pass almost resembled a place people could live. Pip fell asleep against Maren's side mid-sentence, which Maren pretended not to notice and rearranged her whole sitting posture to accommodate.

Jude took first watch at the cave mouth. Below, the switchbacks unspooled into dusk. He watched them the way you watch a wound.

They came at moonrise.

*Jude.* Odile's voice, sharp as a blade laid flat against his neck. *Four of them. Halfway up the third switchback. They've brought a lantern-sigil—they'll see heat in the dark.*

He woke the others without raising his voice, which surprised him; he'd assumed panic would come out of him loud. Instead there was a strange cold clarity, the same feeling he'd had at the loading dock the night Odile died in his arms. Fear, transmuted. Not gone—never gone—but changed into something with edges you could grip.

"Back of the cave," he breathed. "There's another opening—I heard it earlier, air moving. We go through and climb. If we're above them on the scree fields by dawn, they can't flank us."

Maren was already rolling the bedroll. Sef snuffed the fire with a handful of ash. Pip woke instantly, silent—too silent, the silence of a child who had learned that waking loudly could cost everything—and Jude's chest ached at it even as he admired it.

The rear passage was exactly as narrow as his optimism had feared. They went through sideways, single file, Sef's tokens clicking softly, Pip's hand locked in Jude's. Behind them, lantern-light began to strobe against the front chamber walls.

Then the mountain decided to speak.

* * *

He heard it before anyone else—of course he did. The mountain had dead in it too, centuries of them, miners and climbers and one entire caravan that had trusted a shortcut, and their voices rose all at once in a chorus of *move, move, MOVE*, the way the dead always spoke loudest about the thing that had killed them.

"Run," Jude said. "Now. All of you. Don't ask."

They ran.

The passage spat them onto a ledge above the scree fields—a vast gray apron of loose stone fanning down the mountainside, glittering under the moon. Above them, the slope reared up in shelves of rock and packed snow. The Spire was invisible beyond the ridge, but Jude's wrist burned like a held brand. Close. Close.

The sound started low. Not an avalanche yet—just the mountain clearing its throat, a deep grinding groan somewhere in the heights, snowpack shifting on ice.

"Oh," Maren said, in the tone of a professional recognizing another professional's worst work. "Oh no."

"GO!" Jude shoved Pip forward. "Across the scree—get below the fan line—"

They ran out onto the loose stone, and the mountain roared.

It came off the upper slopes as a white-gray flood, a thousand tons of snow and shattered rock pouring down the face of the world. Jude had one image of it—moonlit, terrible, almost beautiful—and then the world became noise and cold and the absolute conviction that he was going to die hearing the dead scream, which was somehow worse than the dying.

Something struck him—not the main mass, but the tongue of it, the trailing edge. He was lifted, rolled, buried to the waist, then the shoulder, then—

Then it stopped.

Silence rang like a struck bell. Snowdust hung in the air, fine as flour, silvering the moonlight. Jude coughed, dug, fought his way free to the chest, and lay gasping on the scree with his ears full of ringing.

"Pip?" His voice cracked. "MAREN. SEF—"

Nothing. Just the settling whisper of the slope, and the dust, and the moon.

He clawed upright. The scree field had changed shape entirely—the fan of stone had slumped and slid, carrying everything downslope, and where the four of them had been climbing together there was now a raw new scar of displaced rock cutting diagonally across the mountainside like a wound. On his side of it: Jude. Alone.

On the far side, somewhere upslope and east, hidden behind the fresh rampart of debris: his family.

"Maren!" he screamed, and heard his own voice bounce off the rocks and come back sounding small. "PIPPIN! SEF!"

Dust drifted. The mountain said nothing.

*They live,* Odile said quietly at his shoulder. He hadn't even felt her arrive. *I can't promise more than that. But they live, Jude. I would know if they didn't. Dead leaves... marks. Trust me on this one.*

"How can you possibly—"

"Because I've been dead for months and I know exactly what it feels like when new deaths happen nearby. It's like a bell being struck." Her voice gentled. "No bell struck tonight. Only the mountain's. They're alive, buried or battered or both—but alive."

Jude pressed his forehead against the frozen scree and shook. Not from cold. From the specific vertigo of having, for one black instant, been certain he'd lost them—and discovering that certainty had been wrong, and that the relief hurt worse than the fear.

Then he made himself think. Because that was the job now. Apparently the job was his.

* * *

He found them at dawn. Or rather—Pip found him, which amounted to the same thing.

He'd spent the night doing the hardest listening of his life: not for the dead, but for the living, using everything Odile had drilled into him. The scree field was thick with trapped echoes, old deaths layered like sediment, and threading through all of it, three bright threads of *not-dead*—breath and heartbeat and stubbornness. He followed them upslope along the scar line, shouting at intervals, digging where the threads converged.

A small hand shot out of the snowbank and grabbed his boot.

"You're LATE," said Pip, hauling herself free like a furious mole, hair crusted white, eyes blazing. "We've been digging FOREVER. Maren says swear words now. Real ones. She taught me two."

"Only two?" Maren's head emerged from a hollow behind her, gray-faced, one arm cradled against her ribs, alive and glaring. Sef rose slowly beside her, dusted white as a monument, and immediately checked his token pouch with trembling fingers. All present. Jude saw his shoulders drop half an inch.

"We thought—" Maren stopped. Started again. "When it hit, we thought you'd gone under."

"So did I." Jude sat down heavily in the snow, quite unable to stand any longer, and Pip climbed into his lap without asking, which was how he knew she'd been frightened too. "I'm sorry. I shouted the warning late. I—"

"You saved us," Maren said flatly. "Whatever you heard, however early—it was early enough. Don't you dare apologize for nearly being crushed to death, Underhill, it sets a terrible precedent."

Sef signed slowly, stiff-fingered with cold: *The path is gone. The scar cuts across the whole route. Days to go around. We have three.*

Jude looked up at the mountain. The avalanche had erased the trail completely—no, not erased. *Rewritten.* The slide had exposed something beneath the old path, high on the face: a seam of darker stone, vertical, running up toward the ridge. Not natural. Cut. Ancient stairs, hidden for centuries under snow and scree, revealed by the mountain's tantrum like a page torn open.

His wrist blazed.

"There," he said. "The mountain didn't bury us. It showed us the way. Keeper-road. Straight up."

Maren squinted at it. "Straight up a cliff face, you mean."

"The Spire remembers," Jude said, and heard Odile laugh softly behind him—surprised, pleased—as though he'd finally quoted her correctly. "So does the mountain, apparently. We go up. Together."

Pip raised one grimy fist into the air. "UPWARD," she declared, with total conviction, and Sef smiled for the first time since the avalanche, and Maren sighed the sigh of a woman assembling her fourth impossible alliance in one lifetime.

They broke camp in the snow. And Jude, shouldering his pack, understood at last that no one had ever handed him leadership—he'd simply picked it up somewhere on the road, the way you pick up a tool, and discovered it fit his hand.

Two days to the equinox. One hidden stair. Four hunters somewhere below.

Above them, at the top of the newly opened seam, something ancient caught the morning light and gleamed: the first step of the keeper-road, waiting.

It had been waiting a long time for Jude Underhill.
