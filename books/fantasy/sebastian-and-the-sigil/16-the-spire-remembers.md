## 16. The Spire Remembers

Maren Oduya had spent twenty years telling anyone who would listen—and a great many who would not—that sigilwork was dead craft, that the old rites were superstition dressed in geometry, that the Spire was a ruin and the covenant a bedtime story for anxious archivists.

She had said it so often, and with such conviction, that she had almost believed herself. That, she reflected now, was the true genius of disgrace: it let you build a whole new person out of denial and cheap wine, and the new person never had to look up.

She looked up now.

The Threshold Spire's interior rose around them like the inside of a bell that had been ringing since before the world learned to listen. The walls were not stone, exactly—or they were stone, but stone that remembered being something else. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, pooling in the grooves of carved lines that spiraled upward beyond sight. And the lines moved. Slowly, patiently, the way frost climbs a windowpane, the sigils on the walls shifted and re-formed, writing and rewriting themselves in an alphabet Maren knew from her studies and had never once seen alive.

"Oh," she said. It was not a very scholarly word. She said it again anyway. "Oh."

"Is it supposed to do that?" Pip asked, hanging off her hand and pointing at a wall where a sigil the size of a barn door was calmly untying itself and re-knotting into something older.

"No," Maren said. "Yes. I don't know. Nothing this old is *supposed* to do anything. It's supposed to be dead." She fumbled at her satchel for chalk out of pure habit, then stopped, because you did not chalk over a living wall any more than you'd tattoo your own heartbeat. "It's not dead. Jude. Jude, it's not dead."

Jude wasn't listening. He had stopped three paces inside the door, and he was staring at the nearest wall with an expression Maren recognized because she'd worn it herself once, years ago, standing in front of her examiners as they struck her name from the rolls: the face of someone watching their entire understanding of the world get quietly, politely demolished.

The wall was showing them people.

Not reflections. Not shadows. Figures stood in the stone the way figures stand behind frosted glass—soft-edged, luminous, moving. A woman with braided hair pressed her palm to a sigil while two others knelt at her feet. Somewhere above them, another figure raised arms toward a light that no longer existed. They were faint, and slow, and utterly unmistakably *alive*, in the way that memory is alive.

"It's every one of them," Odile said softly. The ghost drifted past Maren's shoulder—Maren still hadn't gotten used to not flinching at that—and her voice carried the hushed weight of someone coming home to find the furniture rearranged but the hearth still lit. "Every keeper who ever renewed the covenant. The Spire keeps them. I told you it remembers. I didn't—" Her voice caught, which Maren would have thought impossible for someone already dead. "I didn't know it kept us like *this*."

Sef stood very still, his carved tokens cupped in both hands, and turned slowly in place, reading the walls the way other men read scripture. After a moment he lifted one token—a small bone disc scratched with a spiral—and held it up toward the light. The nearest wall-figure, an old man with a crook-backed stoop, seemed to incline its head.

"He says the Spire taught the first carvers," Maren translated, though Sef hadn't said anything she could translate. Some things you just understood, standing in a room like this. "Bone, stone—it's all the same grammar. Hold a shape long enough and it starts holding you back."

"Well, that's either beautiful or deeply alarming," Maren said, "and in my professional experience it's always both."

Pip tugged her sleeve. "Maren. There's a lady looking at Jude."

---

There was. One of the wall-figures had come closer—not walking, exactly; the stone simply arranged itself so that she was nearer, the way dreams move you without bothering with the trouble of steps. She was small and dark-haired, with laugh lines cut deep into the stone of her face, and she was looking at Jude Underhill with an expression that needed no translation in any alphabet.

Jude's breath went out of him all at once, like a man punched by something gentle.

"Mum?" he said.

The word fell into the vast quiet of the Spire and rang there.

Maren's stomach dropped. She had known—of course she had known, since Vess's taunts in the tunnels, since the ledger entries in her own disgraced handwriting—that Jude's parents had been keepers. Knowing a thing in your head and watching it walk out of a wall toward the boy who'd been carrying its absence for thirty years were different animals entirely.

The figure raised a hand. Not touching—there was a finger's width of air between her palm and Jude's cheek, and the light between them bent like heat-haze—but close enough that Maren saw Jude lean into it anyway, eyes shut, jaw working.

"She can't hear me," Jude whispered. His voice had gone young. "Odile—can she—"

"The Spire holds what they gave," Odile said gently. "Their memories. Their selves, in the way a song holds the singer. She isn't *here*, Jude. But she was here, and what she left is true."

The echo-mother spoke then, and her voice came from everywhere—from the walls, from the floor, from the marrow of Maren's own teeth. It was warm and slightly exasperated, the voice of a woman who had spent a lifetime saying *come inside, you'll catch your death* to people who never listened.

"*Little wren. If you're hearing this, then the covenant has fallen thin, and you've come home the hard way. We hoped you'd never need to.*"

"Mum, I—" Jude's hand rose uselessly toward the stone. "I didn't know. I didn't know any of it. Why didn't you *tell* me—"

"*Because knowing is a weight, and you were so small, and we wanted you to have one ordinary life in this family if the gods allowed it.*" The echo laughed, low and rueful. "*They didn't allow it. They never do. Listen, little wren. The rite needs three bloods, and bloods are only the door. What opens it is a giving. Every keeper who stands at the heart must give up the thing they most hide—the thing they've built their whole self around protecting. Your father gave his anger. I gave my fear of the dark, which sounds small until you learn what lives in it.*"

Beside the mother, a second figure had emerged from the stone—a tall man with Jude's exact shoulders and a smile that sat crooked, the same crooked way Jude's did when he was pretending not to be amused. He didn't speak. He simply looked at his son for a long, long moment, and then he touched two fingers to his own heart and then to the space where Jude's was, and the gesture was so plainly, unbearably *fatherly* that Maren had to examine the ceiling very intently for several seconds.

Pip was crying openly and without embarrassment, the way children cry, and had taken hold of Jude's coat hem with both fists.

"What did you give, Mum?" Jude asked. His voice cracked down the middle. "What did you most hide?"

The echo-mother smiled, and it was the saddest and kindest thing Maren had seen in twenty years of sad and kind things.

"*You, little wren. I hid you. From the guild, from the Hollow, from your own blood—I hid you so well I hid you from yourself. And the covenant took that hiding as my offering, and it held for a generation. But hiding was never meant to be forever. Nothing true is.*" Her stone-hand hovered again over his cheek. "*Give up yours. Whatever it is. You'll know it when the Spire asks. It always asks kindly, but it does not ask twice.*"

The figures began to recede—not vanishing, but settling back into the stone like silt into riverbed, patient, permanent. Jude lunged forward half a step.

"Wait—Mum—Dad—"

"*We were never gone, love,*" the walls said together, mother's warmth and father's silence braided into one voice. "*We were waiting. Now stop gawping and go save the world. You get that from me, the stubbornness. The carving, unfortunately, is all him.*"

And then they were stone again, and the Spire hummed, and Jude Underhill stood in the middle of the greatest archive of souls in two worlds and cried like a boy, and nobody—including Maren, who had a reputation to maintain—thought anything less of him for it.

* * *

They made camp in a side chamber that some long-dead keeper had clearly used for the same purpose: there were scorch marks from a cookfire generations cold, alcoves worn smooth by sleeping bodies, and—Maren discovered with a whoop that echoed embarrassingly—a sealed jar of preserved plums that were somehow, impossibly, still good. The Spire provided. The Spire, apparently, had opinions about feeding people properly before asking them to sacrifice things.

"Eat," Maren ordered, distributing plums. "All of you. Rites of world-saving run on empty stomachs about as well as anything else, which is to say badly and with complaining."

Pip ate four plums and fell asleep mid-bite into the fifth, curled against Sef's side like a kitten against a warm wall. Sef arranged his tokens in a slow spiral around the sleeping child—Maren watched, and realized it was a warding pattern, an old one, the kind that hadn't been carved in earnest since before her grandmother's grandmother. He was guarding the girl the only way he could.

Jude sat apart, turning his wrist over and over, looking at the birthmark-scar that had started everything.

"You heard them," Maren said, sitting down beside him with the gracelessness of a woman whose knees had opinions about stone floors. "Really heard. Not the drowning-noise. Them."

"They sounded happy," Jude said. "Underneath everything. Isn't that strange? Dead thirty years, dissolved into a wall, and underneath it all they sounded like people who'd made peace."

"That's what giving does, I think." Maren rolled a plum pit between her fingers. "My teachers called the rite a transaction. Payment. All that ledger-language, sacrifice as accounting." She snorted. "But look at them. That wasn't a payment. That was a *handing-over*. You put down the thing you've been clutching so hard it's grown into your palm, and it turns out the covenant doesn't want your suffering. It wants your honesty. Suffering's just usually what honesty costs first."

Jude was quiet a while. Firelight from their small cookfire moved on his face, and above, the Spire's own light moved too, and the two lights got along fine.

"I know what mine is," he said finally. "What I most hide. I've known since the tunnels, maybe since the dock. I just haven't said it out loud, because saying it out loud means it's real, and real things can be taken."

"Want to practice on me?" Maren offered. "I'm excellent at keeping secrets. It's the one skill they couldn't strip from me when they struck my name. Well. That and making truly spectacular mistakes."

That earned her the ghost of the crooked smile. Then it faded, and he looked at the fire.

"When I was nine," Jude said, "my mother sent me to the market at dusk. I was halfway home when I heard her. Not calling—she wasn't calling. I heard her the way I hear everyone. Inside. She was singing, and then she was afraid, and then—" His throat worked. "Then she was gone, and I ran the other way. I ran because I thought if I could get far enough away, the hearing would stop. It didn't. It never stops. So I built a wall in my head and I put the dead on the far side of it, and I muted them, all of them, for thirty years. Including her. Including—" his voice dropped to almost nothing— "including whoever else might have needed witnessing, all those years. All those voices, begging to be heard, and I pretended I was deaf because deafness felt safe."

Maren said nothing. There was nothing clever to say, and she was wise enough, at last, to know when wisdom meant shutting up.

"And tomorrow," Jude went on, "the Spire's going to ask me to give that up. The muting. The wall. To hear *everything*, forever, without flinching. Every dying breath in two worlds, every grief that wants a witness." He laughed, hollow. "My parents hid me to keep me safe, and the price of saving everything is becoming the one thing they hid me from."

"No," Maren said.

He looked at her.

"No," she said again, firmer. "Listen to me, Jude Underhill, because I failed my rite and I've had twenty years to autopsy why, so I know this better than anyone breathing. They didn't hide you *from* yourself. They hid you *for* later. For this. For the version of you that walked through the Gray Pass and pulled a child out of the marshes and broke an old man out of a collector's cage and refused Captain Vess to her face." She jabbed the plum pit at him for emphasis. "The mute boy couldn't have done the rite. He'd have given the Spire his silence and meant it as surrender. You'll give it and mean it as *opening*. Same gift. Different giver. That's the whole difference, and it's enormous, and your parents would be proud as hell, and if you argue with me I will throw this pit at you and my aim is legendary."

Jude blinked. Then, helplessly, he laughed—a real one, cracked and wet and human—and Maren counted it among her finer victories.

Across the chamber, Odile drifted near the walls, reading the keeper-figures with an expression Maren couldn't name until she could: recognition. Somewhere in that stone was a younger Odile, whole and alive, pressing her palm to a sigil beside friends she'd failed. Tomorrow the ghost would lend her death-memory to the rite. Tonight she was visiting her own grave, and finding flowers on it.

Sef caught Maren's eye across the fire and lifted a token: a small bone square, scratched with a single line rising through a circle. Dawn. The rite. Readiness.

"Yes," Maren said aloud, though he hadn't asked. "Tomorrow. Gods help us, tomorrow."

* * *

The Spire woke them before dawn—which is to say, the light in the walls changed from deep blue to pale gold, and a sound moved through the structure like a great inhale, and none of them could have slept through it if they'd tried.

They climbed.

The heart of the Spire was a circular chamber at the top of a stair that had not existed yesterday—Maren was fairly sure it hadn't; she'd have remembered stairs that materialized overnight, even in her current state of philosophical flexibility. The chamber was bare except for a floor mosaic: three interlocking circles, worn shallow by centuries of feet, each circle ringed with script.

Three circles. Three keepers.

Maren knelt at the first and unpacked her chalks with hands that shook only a little, which she considered a triumph. This part she knew. This part was hers—the geometry, the anchoring lines, the great sigil that would bind blood and memory and offering into one holding shape. Twenty years of disgrace, and it came back to her hands like it had never left, and she laughed out loud at the feel of it.

"Maren?" Jude said.

"It's still in there," she said, grinning like a maniac, chalk flying. "Twenty years of drink and disgrace and it's still *in there*. Right. Blood next. Everyone knows the drill—"

The Spire interrupted her.

The light in the walls gathered—all of it, from every level below, streaming up through the stone like sap rising—and condensed in front of Jude into a single figure. Not his mother. Not his father. Something older, faceless, made of every keeper at once, a chorus given one body and one purpose.

It extended a hand toward Jude, palm open. Asking.

And the voice that filled the chamber was kind, and vast, and did not ask twice:

"*Keeper-blood. What do you most hide?*"

Jude closed his eyes. Maren saw him reach for the wall inside himself—the wall he'd built at nine years old, mortar and stone and thirty years of silence—and she saw him, instead of cowering behind it, lay his hands flat against it the way you'd lay hands on an old friend's shoulders.

And far below them, faint but unmistakable, carried up the stair that hadn't existed yesterday, came the sound of many boots, and the ring of steel, and a horn blown in the guild's three sharp notes.

Corvane had found them.

The Spire's voice did not change pace. It did not hurry. It simply waited, palm open, while the world broke against its doors—

and Jude Underhill opened his mouth to answer.
