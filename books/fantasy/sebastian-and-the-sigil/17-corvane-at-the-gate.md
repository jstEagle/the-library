## 17. Corvane at the Gate

The Spire woke us before the horns did.

I felt it through the floor first—a low hum in the stone, like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop ringing. Then the walls began to whisper. Not the dead, exactly. The Spire's memories, the keeper-echoes Maren said lived in every inch of it, all of them turning toward the same thing at once: the gate.

"They're here," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt.

Maren was already on her feet, chalk stub in hand, sigil-dust on her cheek where she'd fallen asleep mid-inscription. "Of course they're here. Corvane's been two days behind us the whole climb. He didn't want to catch us tired." She looked around the antechamber—at Sef sitting cross-legged by the cold hearth with his tokens laid out like a soldier's kit, at Pip curled in a corner with Odile's faint shape keeping watch over them both—and something in her face went hard and bright. "He wanted to catch us *committed*."

"Is there a difference?" Pip asked sleepily, then sat bolt upright as the first horn sounded, far below, thin as a needle pushed through the mountain's ear.

"Yes," Maren said. "Committed means we can't run."

*You could still run,* Odile said, only for me. Her voice had grown clearer over the weeks, or perhaps I'd simply learned to listen without flinching. *There are back ways. Keeper ways.*

"Is that what you did?" I asked her quietly. "At the end? Run?"

Her silence had a shape to it—old guilt, worn smooth as river stone. *No,* she admitted. *And look how well that ended for me.*

"So we stay," I said aloud.

"So we stay," Maren agreed, and began chalking the great sigil onto the Spire's heart-floor with hands that shook only a little.

* * *

They came up the stair in numbers I hadn't let myself imagine. Through the arrow-slit windows I watched torchlight crawl the switchbacks of the Gray Pass like a lit fuse burning toward us, and I counted, and then I stopped counting because counting wasn't helping anything.

Vess came first. Alone, under a parley banner, which told me everything about how much Corvane trusted even his own captain.

She stopped ten paces from me in the outer court, snow gathering on her shoulders, and looked older than she had in the guild yard a lifetime ago. Or maybe I just saw her more clearly now. That was the trouble with learning to listen—you started seeing everything else too.

"Underhill." She glanced past me at the Spire doors. "How many inside?"

"Enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting."

Something almost like respect crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Corvane's given orders," she said. "For when he takes this place. You understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand you're telling me instead of doing it."

"I'm giving you until dawn to come down the mountain. All of you. The child especially." Her jaw tightened. "Whatever you think the covenant is, whatever Oduya's filled your head with—the guild has held this city safe for two hundred years. Corvane says the Hollow is dying anyway. He says we can save *this* side if we stop pretending the other one matters."

"And you believe him?"

"I want to," Vess said simply. "That's worse, isn't it."

It was, but I didn't say so. Instead I heard myself ask, "Captain. When you were told to hunt a bone-carver with a map—did anyone ever tell you *why*? Did anyone ever tell you about keepers? About the veil?"

"No." The word came out flat and bitter. "No one tells me why. That's rather the point of me."

Behind her, down the pass, the torch-fuse burned closer. Somewhere below, a war-horn answered another war-horn, and the sound made the Spire's stones shiver.

"Dawn," Vess repeated. "Please, Underhill. Be gone by dawn."

She turned and walked back into the dark, and I stood in the falling snow feeling the strangest thing: that I had just been warned by someone who thought she was my enemy, and that she had done it because no one had ever told her why either.

*She'll turn,* Odile said beside me, so softly I felt it more than heard it. *Or she won't. Either way, Jude—she bought you hours. Spend them.*

* * *

We spent them badly, the way you spend any last hours—with words that don't fit and silences that do.

Sef worked. That was the thing about Sef; he never wasted a moment on fear that could be spent making something. His fingers moved over bone tokens with a speed that shamed mine, carving the seal-marks Maren needed for the rite, each one small enough to cup in a palm, each one singing faintly when he finished it. I sat across from him and carved too, because it was the only language we shared, and because my hands needed something to hold besides dread.

Halfway through the night he set down his knife and pressed a token into my hand.

It was new. Not one of the seal-marks. A little figure—a man, hunched, hands over his ears. And around him, cut fine as frost, a ring of small open mouths. Listening mouths. Speaking mouths.

"I know," I said stupidly. "That's me. That's always been me."

Sef tapped the token. Then he turned it over. On the back, in his careful alphabet of cuts: **NOT A CURSE. A DOOR.**

"You've been saying that since the marshes," I told him. My throat hurt. "You're very repetitive for a man who doesn't talk."

He smiled—he had a wonderful smile, crooked and warm, the smile of someone who had decided decades ago that muteness was the world's loss, not his—and went back to carving.

Pip fell asleep against my shoulder somewhere past midnight, the way Pip always did, with the absolute trust of a child who had decided I was furniture worth sleeping on. Odile stood watch at the window, and once, looking down at the torches swarming the pass, she said:

*I guarded a map for eleven years. Do you know what I was actually guarding?*

"The covenant."

*A letter. One letter, from one keeper to another, saying: it is failing, come home. Eleven years I carried it because I couldn't face the people I'd have to say it to.* She was quiet a moment. *Don't carry things past their time, Jude. Whatever happens tonight—say the true thing while your mouth still works.*

"I will," I promised, and meant it, and would spend the rest of my life being glad I did.

* * *

Dawn came gray and early, and it brought Corvane.

Not the man himself—not yet—but his voice, amplified by some guild-thing of brass and binding, rolling up the mountainside like weather:

**KEEPERS OF THE SPIRE. OPEN THE GATES AND WALK OUT, AND NO HARM COMES TO YOU. HOLD THEM, AND NOTHING THAT STANDS WITHIN THESE WALLS AT SUNSET WILL BE REMEMBERED BY ANYONE.**

"He means it," Maren said grimly, chalk dust to her elbows now, the great sigil half-born on the floor beneath us. "Memory-binding. It's old guild craft. He could make this whole mountain forget it was ever climbed."

"Can he finish the rite without us?"

"No. But he doesn't need to finish it. He needs only to stop *us* from finishing it. The Hollow dies slowly either way. He's patient. Dead worlds don't fight back."

The gates boomed. Once. Twice. On the third blow, iron shrieked and gave, and the sound of boots in the entry hall was like rain beginning.

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do—all of it clear and none of it orderly.

Maren drove us back toward the heart-chamber, shouting the order of the rite. I carried Pip, who had woken and gone eerily quiet, arms locked around my neck. Sef gathered his tokens into their cloth with the calm of a man packing for a journey. And Vess—

Vess was suddenly *there*, in the doorway between us and the flood of enforcers, sword drawn, facing the wrong way.

"Captain?" The lead enforcer stopped, confused. I knew him vaguely—Harrow, from the night ledgers, a decent man who watered the office plants. "Captain, Guildmaster's orders. The keepers are to be—"

"I know what the orders are, Harrow." Her voice carried down the corridor, level and terrible. "I've known for three days what the orders are. I read them. All of them. Including the ones about the child."

Silence, except for boots still coming up the stairs below.

"Captain—"

"*Stand down*, Harrow." And then, when he didn't move: "Two hundred years the guild kept this city safe. Two hundred years, and somewhere in there it became a thing that sells other worlds for parts and calls it prudence. I signed those orders because I trusted the hand above mine. Well." She lifted her sword a fraction higher. "I've met the hand. Stand down, or stand against me. Those are the choices."

Harrow looked at her for a long moment. Then—gods bless the decent men who water office plants—he stepped aside, dropped his blade, and put his hands up, and three of the six behind him did the same.

The other three did not.

The fighting was short and ugly and I will not dress it up. Vess fought like someone settling accounts, and for a hundred heartbeats she held the corridor the way a dam holds a river—alone, magnificent, buying us the minutes Maren needed. I saw her take the first wound high on the arm and keep fighting. I saw Harrow drag one of the turncoats down. I saw the second wound, low, deep, and I saw Vess go to one knee and *keep holding the line*, roaring at us over her shoulder:

"THE RITE, UNDERHILL! GO! Don't you dare watch—GO!"

We went. Of course we went. And I hated every step of it, and I have never in my life been prouder of anyone.

* * *

The heart-chamber was round and vast and full of light that had no source, the keeper-memories swirling in the walls like snow in a globe. The great sigil waited on the floor, complete at last, its channels empty.

"Three bloods," Maren breathed. "Three seals. Sef first—the eldest line anchors the foundation. Then me. Then—" she looked at me, and couldn't finish, and didn't need to.

Sef walked to the sigil's edge and knelt, and I understood—one heartbeat before anyone should have understood—that he had understood for days.

"Sef." My voice cracked. "Sef, wait—"

He smiled at me again, that crooked warm smile, and pressed the cloth bundle of tokens into my hands. All of them. Every seal-mark, every message, every small impossible thing he'd carved in our weeks together—including the little hunched figure with the ring of listening mouths.

On the top token, freshly cut, the ink of it still sharp: **FINISH IT.**

"Sef, there has to be another way—"

He shook his head, gentle, certain, and touched his own chest, and then the sigil, and then—this undid me entirely—he reached up and touched my cheek, once, the way you'd bless a grandson.

Then he stepped into the light.

The first seal took him the way dawn takes a candle—no violence, just a softening, a becoming-part-of-something-larger. The sigil drank his blood and blazed white-gold, and where he had knelt the stone remembered him: I felt it, through the floor, through my gift, through everything I was—*a carver, a keeper, a man sold as a relic who died free and chose it*. His voice, wordless all his life, filled the chamber in a language older than words, and every mouth-mouthed token in my hands grew warm as living skin.

**FINISH IT**, the tokens said, in his cuts, in his silence, in the roar of the waking covenant.

So I knelt at the second channel with Maren, and gave her my wrist, and did not look away.

* * *

Below us, the corridor fighting had gone quiet in the worst way. Above us, the ceiling began to crack, because Corvane did not send a second wave—he sent the guild's oldest weapon, a binding-engine meant to unmake wards, and the Spire groaned like a ship in a storm.

"One seal," Maren gasped, chalk flying, her own blood already opening along her palm for the second channel. "One holds for minutes. Two holds for hours. Three—"

"Three holds forever. I know."

"Then hear me, Jude Underhill." She grabbed my collar, chalk-white, blood-streaked, grinning like a woman at her own funeral. "When it asks you for the hidden thing—and it *will* ask—give it. Don't bargain. Don't flinch. Everything you buried, everything you muted, every voice you ever ran from—" her eyes flicked to the wall where my parents' echo lived in stone, and softened, "—especially those. That's the price. That's also the point."

The doors burst inward.

And standing in the ruin of them, brass-and-binding engine smoking behind him, robes immaculate amid the ash, came Guildmaster Corvane himself—old, unhurried, wearing the patient smile of a man who had already grieved for the world and gotten over it.

"Well," he said pleasantly, to Maren, to me, to the blazing half-made seal. "Let's see what you're willing to pay."

The tokens in my hands burned. Somewhere below, faint but stubborn, Vess's voice was still shouting orders to men who had finally, at last, been told why.

I closed my hands around Sef's carvings, and stepped forward.
