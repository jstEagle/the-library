## 20. Ledgers, Rewritten

The Meridian Guild had always smelled of money and secrets. Maren Oduya noticed, on the morning she walked back through its doors for the first time in eleven years, that it now also smelled of lye soap and scorched paper—the particular perfume of an institution scrubbing itself clean.

She stood in the entry hall with her hands jammed in her coat pockets, feeling like a cat that had been thrown out a window and was now being invited back to sit on the cushion. The marble under her boots was the same marble that had gleamed beneath her when the guildmasters stripped her sigilwright's license and escorted her out by the elbows. She'd spat on it, as she recalled. There had been no one to see but a junior clerk, who'd looked scandalized and then, briefly, envious.

The junior clerk—now considerably less junior, with gray at his temples and a chain of office that suggested he'd done rather well for himself—was the one waiting for her.

"Maren Oduya," said Aldous Fenwick, and bowed. Actually bowed. "The council thanks you for coming."

"The council," Maren said, "can thank me by not calling me 'the disgraced sigilwright' in its minutes anymore. I've read them. I get a very dramatic adjective every time."

"Would you prefer 'the celebrated'?"

"I'd prefer 'the correct,' but I've learned to live without it."

Fenwick smiled the smile of a man who had spent three weeks discovering exactly how rotten the floorboards of his beloved institution were, and had decided that gallows humor was cheaper than weeping. "Walk with me. There are things you should see before the council makes its offer."

* * *

They walked the long gallery first, where the guild's history hung in portraits: founders, guildmasters, benefactors. Workmen were already at the far end with ladders and crowbars.

Guildmaster Corvane's portrait came down while they watched. It did not go gracefully. The frame caught on a nail, swung, and nearly brained a workman, who swore with impressive creativity for someone employed by a trade house.

"He sat for that two years ago," Fenwick said. "Paid for it himself, out of petty cash he'd earmarked for 'covenant suppression.' We found the ledger entries." He rubbed his eyes. "Do you know what the worst part is, Oduya? It isn't that he betrayed the covenant. Betrayal, at least, is interesting. It's the bookkeeping. Thirty years of entries. *Item: one memory-key, provenance obscured. Item: one veil-thread, sold north. Item: one silence, purchased from a dying courier.* He wrote it all down. He thought he was being *thorough*."

Maren thought of Jude Underhill, who kept ledgers too, and who had once told her, in the marsh dark, that numbers were the only voices that never lied to him. "Some men write everything down because they're proud," she said. "And some write it down because they can't bear to hold it in their heads alone."

"And which was Corvane?"

"Proud," Maren said. "Definitely proud. A guilty man burns his papers."

They passed the council chamber, doors open, where six people sat around a table arguing about the registry of impossible things. Maren heard fragments—*provenance requirements*, *independent audit*, *what do we do with the memory-keys, we can't just hand those back*—and felt something she hadn't felt inside these walls since she was twenty-three and idealistic: the sense that the argument mattered.

Then Fenwick took her down the stairs, past the vaults, to a room she had never been shown even in her glory days.

It was long and low-lit, lined with shelves, and every shelf held things that should not have existed. A lantern that burned cold. A jar of last words, stoppered and labeled in a careful hand. A door that opened onto nothing, currently padlocked, with a sign reading DO NOT KNOCK. And in a glass case at the center, under a fresh card in Fenwick's handwriting—*recovered; return pending*—a map marked with a sigil Maren knew better than her own signature, because she'd spent eleven years dreaming about how badly she'd failed to inscribe it correctly.

"The covenant archive," Fenwick said quietly. "Corvane kept it. Everything the guild confiscated from the keepers, over thirty years. He called it the Deep Registry. Only three people knew it existed." He looked at her sideways. "Four, now."

Maren stood in front of the map for a long moment. Through the glass she could see the ink had faded along the Spire road, as if the map itself had been grieving.

"You're going to ask me something," she said. "Ask it upstairs. I don't make life decisions next to a jar of last words. It's bad luck, and I've had enough bad luck to fill a second jar."

* * *

The council made its offer after lunch, which Maren considered poor planning on their part, because she'd had two glasses of the good wine and her filter was famously the first casualty.

"We would like you to oversee the new Registry of Impossible Things," Fenwick said. "Full authority. Independent reporting line. Your license restored, retroactively, with a formal apology entered into the record."

"Read the apology aloud," Maren said. "In the hall. Where everyone spat on my name."

A councilwoman near the end of the table winced. Fenwick didn't blink. "Aloud. In the hall. I'll do it myself."

"And the adjective?"

"'The correct,'" Fenwick said solemnly, "has been proposed."

Maren leaned back and looked around the table. Six faces. Two of them she recognized as having voted, years ago, to strip her license—one of those had been the loudest vote, she remembered, a man named Harrow who had since grown jowls and apparently a conscience, because he couldn't meet her eyes. Good. Let him squirm. Squirming was the tax institutions paid for their sins, and the Meridian Guild owed back taxes.

She thought about saying no. It would have been easy. Easy was her native language; she'd spoken it fluently for eleven years, from a canal-side tenement, drinking herself into a philosophical stupor and telling anyone who'd listen—which was nobody—that the guild was beyond saving.

But then she thought of Pip, asking with terrible eight-year-old directness why the big-house people let the Hollow get thin. *Did they not look?* Pip had asked. And Maren had had no answer except the true one: they looked away, because looking was expensive, and looking away was cheap.

Institutions changed when individuals refused to look away. That wasn't a philosophy she'd have put on a sampler. But she'd watched a bone-carver refuse to look away from a dying courier, and a captain refuse to look away from her own orders, and an old mute man press carved tokens into a stranger's hands rather than die unspent—and she had run out of excuses for her own comfortable blindness.

"One condition," Maren said.

"Name it."

"Jude Underhill gets the workshop above the guild hall. Rent-free. Permanently. And the registry answers to him as much as to me—he hears things none of us can, and I'm not running an archive of impossible things without someone who can interview the previous owners."

Fenwick blinked. "The dead can own things?"

"The dead can *testify*," Maren said. "You have no idea how much easier your audits are about to become."

* * *

She went to see Vess that afternoon, because there were debts between them that neither of them knew how to name.

The hospital wing of the guild house had been built for wealthy merchants, all tall windows and hushed corridors, and Captain Vess looked profoundly wrong in it—a woman built for rain and rooftops, propped up in a bed with white sheets, her sword arm splinted and her pride visibly intact, which the healers said was the more serious injury.

"They gave you a private room," Maren said, taking the chair by the window.

"They gave everyone a private room. I threatened to arrest the ward nurse." Vess's voice was rougher than Maren remembered. There was a bandage above her ear and a bruise going yellow-green down her jaw, souvenirs of the gate at the Spire, of the moment she'd turned her blade against the man who'd given it to her. "Is this a social call?"

"I wanted to see what a conscience looks like after it's been run over by a guildmaster."

"It looks like this," Vess said. "It hurts to laugh. It hurts to breathe. It lies awake at night doing arithmetic." She turned her head toward the window. "I arrested forty-one people over nine years, Oduya. Keepers, sympathizers, a woman who sold veil-threads to feed her children. I told myself I was protecting the city. Corvane told me I was protecting the city. And the whole time I was the lock on his cellar door."

"Yes," Maren said, because Vess deserved honesty more than comfort, and comfort had never once worked on either of them anyway.

Vess almost smiled. "You haven't changed."

"You have. Slightly. Don't ruin it."

Silence. Down in the courtyard, a cartload of confiscated goods was being wheeled toward the registry—toward *her* registry, she supposed, gods help them all—and Maren watched a clerk check items off a list with the earnest diligence of a man who had no idea half his inventory could whisper.

"They're reassigning me," Vess said finally. "Quietly. Border survey, up past Gray Pass. Nobody will say the word 'demotion.' They'll say 'recuperative posting.'" Her jaw tightened. "I turned on Corvane. I took a blade meant for Underhill. And they're sending me to count fence posts."

"What did you expect? A parade?"

"I expected—" Vess stopped. Started again, quieter. "I don't know what I expected. Something proportionate."

Maren studied her for a moment. Then she leaned forward. "Let me tell you something about proportion, Captain, from a woman who spent eleven years being punished for a mistake and learned precisely nothing from the punishment. Institutions don't know how to say thank you or sorry. They only know how to file things. So they filed you somewhere quiet until they figure out what you are now." She stood, and put a small object on the bedside table: a carved bone token, a spire, crude but unmistakable—Jude's work. "Sef's craft, mostly. Jude finished it. They asked me to bring it to you. Apparently the dead speak well of you lately, and the living are catching up slowly. Border survey is a start. Fence posts need guarding too."

Vess picked up the token and turned it in her fingers, and for just a moment her composure cracked like river ice—something moving underneath, vast and cold and thawing.

"Tell Underhill," she said, "that the ledger's balanced. Not forgiven. Balanced. There's a difference."

"He knows," Maren said. "He keeps books. It's rather his whole thing."

* * *

The formal apology happened three days later, in the great hall, and Maren attended in her best coat and her worst mood, which was traditional.

Fenwick read the statement aloud, every word, including the parts where the guild admitted it had suppressed knowledge of the covenant, destroyed keeper records, and stripped the license of a sigilwright who had been, in the guild's own newly discovered archives, *correct*. When he reached the phrase "the correct sigilwright Maren Oduya," somebody in the back of the hall snorted, and Maren chose, magnanimously, to pretend she hadn't heard it.

Afterward, a line formed. Not of well-wishers—of petitioners. Word traveled fast in a city where a ghost had recently testified in a public inquiry (the stenographer had resigned; her replacement wore earplugs and drank tea steadily and was, Maren thought, showing real promise). People came with objects they'd inherited and feared. A woman with a ring that whispered her grandmother's recipes. A man carrying a locked box that hummed. A child holding a wooden bird that flapped at dusk and said, in a voice nobody recognized, *almost, almost.*

Maren catalogued them all. Name, nature, provenance, disposition. She wrote in her own hand, in a ledger she bought herself, with a cover she liked, because if she was going to be institutionalized she was going to do it comfortably.

On the fourth day, Jude came down from his workshop with Pip in tow, and Maren watched the registry change weather.

Pip went straight to the humming box, put both hands on it, and announced, "It's lonely. It wants to be opened on a hillside at moonrise by someone named Brannic." The man with the box went white, then gray, then sat down heavily on a stool and wept, because Brannic had been his brother, forty years drowned, and no living soul knew the name.

"That one goes in the ledger twice," Maren said, writing. "Once for the box. Once for the boy. The boy's rarer."

Jude drifted along the shelves, trailing that air he had now—not invisibility, its opposite, a kind of gentle gravity—and stopped at the glass case where the map lay. Odile's map. Odile's ghost had faded a week after the rite, smiling, her long walk finally ended, but the map remained, its ink darkening day by day as if the Hollow were breathing color back into it from a great distance.

"Return pending," Jude read. "Pending what?"

"Pending us deciding what a city owes a world it spent thirty years robbing," Maren said. "The council's drafting reparations. It's going slowly. Councils move like glaciers, and glaciers at least have the decency to be silent about it."

Jude smiled faintly. Then he tilted his head—the listening tilt, the one Maren had learned meant the room had acquired an audience—and said softly, "There's a woman by the door. Dead. She says she has something for the registry. She says—" he paused, and his expression shifted into something Maren couldn't quite read, wonder braided with wariness, "—she says she died holding a key, and the key still works, and it doesn't open any door anyone's ever built."

Maren set down her pen.

"Ledger," she said, "new page."

And from the doorway, faint as breath on winter glass, came the sound of a key turning in a lock that had no door—yet.
