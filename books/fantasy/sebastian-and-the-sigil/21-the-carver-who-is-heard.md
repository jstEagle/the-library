## 21. The Carver Who Is Heard

The sign took three tries.

Jude carved it himself, of course—oak rather than bone, because Maren had pointed out that a man who worked in bone hanging a bone sign over his door might attract the wrong sort of clientele, and the right sort of clientele was precisely what he was trying to cultivate. The first attempt read JUDE UNDERHILL, CARVER, which was accurate and felt like a lie all the same. The second read THE UNDERHILL WORKSHOP, which sounded like a place where shoes were repaired by someone named Underhill who had never heard a ghost in his life.

The third—and final, because Pip's door had been closed for four months now and Jude had promised himself he would stop hiding behind smallness—read:

**JUDE UNDERHILL — HE WILL LISTEN**

Maren had laughed until she had to sit down on the guild hall steps when she saw it. "That's not a trade," she'd said, wiping her eyes. "That's a confession."

"It's both," Jude had said. "Everything worth doing is both."

Now the sign hung above the narrow stair that climbed the eastern wall of the rebuilt Meridian Guild hall, and every morning when Jude unlocked the little workshop at the top of it, he paused on the landing to look at those four words and feel something he was still learning to name without flinching.

Pride came close. But pride had always been Maren's word, loud and unembarrassed. What Jude felt was quieter than that. It was closer to the feeling of setting down a ledger he'd been carrying so long his arms had forgotten their own weight.

*He will listen.*

Not *he hears voices*, whispered like an accusation. Not *the bone-carver who talks to himself*, as the apprentices had said behind their hands for years. Just the plain truth, hung out in daylight where anyone could read it.

It had taken him thirty-one years and one collapsing covenant to write four honest words about himself. He tried not to be bitter about the arithmetic. Most days he succeeded.

* * *

The morning's first client arrived before the tea finished steeping, which was becoming traditional.

Widow Anselm climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, carrying a cloth-wrapped bundle the way people carried things they could not yet set down. She was seventy if she was a day, with a face like a folded map and eyes that had done a great deal of looking-at-things-bravely and very little looking-away.

"Master Underhill." She settled into the chair across from his workbench, breathless from the climb but refusing to show it. "They told me at the desk downstairs you might—well. That you do a particular kind of work."

"I carve memorials," Jude said. "Tokens, plaques, keepsakes. Bone, antler, sometimes river-ivory when it can be had honestly."

"Yes." She smoothed her skirt. "But I'm told—" She stopped, started again, the way people did when they were approaching the true request sideways, afraid to spook it. "I'm told you *listen*. While you work. To the ones who've gone."

Jude set down his tea. This part never got easier—not the hearing, but the moment when a living person said the quiet thing out loud and waited to find out whether they'd been foolish.

"Who would you like me to listen for?" he asked gently.

"My husband. Tomas. Eleven years gone this autumn." Her hands tightened on the bundle. "And before you say anything, Master Underhill, I know how it sounds. My daughter says grief makes women credulous. But my sister's boy had a token carved by you last spring—a memorial for his father—and afterward he told me, red-faced and staring at his boots, that the carver had known things. Things about the day his father died. Things nobody living knew."

"The dead talk," Jude said simply. "Most people can't hear them, and that's probably a mercy. But some of them have been waiting a long time for someone who can. It's not magic, mostly. It's more like..." He searched for the words Odile had given him, back when she was teaching him to listen deliberately instead of drowning. "It's more like being the only person in a city who can read, and discovering everyone else has letters they've never been able to open."

Widow Anselm's chin trembled. "Eleven years," she said again. "I've had eleven years of questions."

"Then let's begin," Jude said, and unwrapped the bundle she'd brought: a femur, old and clean, polished by decades of handling. "Tell me about Tomas while I work. Start anywhere. The dead don't mind where you start."

She talked. He carved. And beneath her voice—beneath the story of a ferryman who'd loved card games and undercooked onions and one specific woman for fifty-three years—Jude let his attention sink into the bone the way he'd once sunk it into ledgers, line by line, patient as water.

Tomas Anselm was there, of course. They usually were, in the bones their families kept. Not trapped—that was the misconception, and Jude corrected it kindly whenever it came up. A soul moved on; what remained in the bone was more like an echo with opinions. A recording that could, if you listened properly, answer back.

*Ask her,* Tomas Anselm's echo said, amused and fond, the moment Jude touched the carving knife to the bone. *Ask her about the blue coat. She thinks I never knew.*

Jude smiled and kept working. "He wants to know," he said, "if you ever wondered whether he noticed the blue coat."

Widow Anselm made a sound like a laugh and a sob shaking hands. "The blue coat. I sold my mother's silver for that coat, and he never once said a word about it, and I spent forty years wondering if he thought me wasteful."

*I married a woman who sold silver for a coat because it made her happy,* the echo said. *You think I'd call that waste? Tell her the coat outlived three umbrellas and one hat, and I counted myself well traded.*

By the time the token was finished—a small spire-and-river motif, because Tomas had ferried the Graywater for forty years and the Spire had watched him do it—the widow was laughing through tears, and Jude had learned that Tomas's last thought had been irritation at dying before finishing a card game, and that his last feeling had been gratitude so large it embarrassed him even in death.

"He was there the whole time?" she asked at the door, holding the token like something warm.

"In the bone, yes. And elsewhere too, I expect. Places I can't follow." Jude hesitated, then added, because honesty was the whole point of the sign: "He isn't suffering, Widow Anselm. Whatever else is uncertain about death, I can tell you that much with confidence. The angry ones move on fast. It's only the loving ones who linger near the people they love."

She pressed his hand with both of hers. "Eleven years," she said a third time, but the weight had changed. It wasn't a stone anymore. It was a keepsake.

After she'd gone, Jude sat for a moment in the sun coming through the workshop window, listening to the ordinary sounds of the guild hall below—carts, haggling, someone's apprentice being scolded for miscounting crates—and let the quiet settle into him like a good meal.

There had been a time when silence was the thing he wanted most in the world. When he'd have traded his hands for an hour of true quiet, no murmuring, no voices leaking out of every carved thing he touched.

Now the voices were work. Companionship, even. The dead needed witnessing, Odile had taught him, the way the living needed bread: not constantly, not desperately, but regularly, or something in them thinned. And someone had to be the baker.

He'd stopped minding that the someone was him.

* * *

Odile appeared at midday, as she did most days now, fading up out of the corner by the window where the light came in slantwise. Four months since the rite, and she was thinner than a candle flame in a draft—but she was still there, and every day she was there felt, Jude had privately admitted, like borrowed time he intended to spend gratefully.

"You're smiling at your tea," she observed. "Either it's very good tea or Widow Anselm went well."

"She's going to send half the widowhood of Meridian up those stairs within the month. Tomas was apparently the gregarious type."

"Dead men make excellent references." Odile drifted along the workbench, inspecting the day's tokens with the critical eye of a woman who had guarded a sigil-marked map across half a kingdom and taken an arrow for it. "Your linework's improved. Your spires used to lean like drunkards."

"They still lean. Now it's intentional."

"Mm." She settled—or arranged herself in the shape of settling—on the windowsill, translucent hands folded. For a while she just watched the street below, and Jude carved, and the two silences sat together comfortably, the way they had learned to.

Then she said, "Vess came by yesterday. While you were at the market."

Jude's knife paused. "Did she now."

"To check the roofline, she claimed. Captain Vess, checking rooflines." Odile's smile was dry as old paper. "She stood across the street for a quarter hour looking up at your sign. Then she went inside and bought an apple from the fruit stall where she could see the door. She ate the whole apple, Jude. Slowly. Watching."

"That's either surveillance or the saddest lunch break in guild history."

"With Vess it's usually both." Odile turned from the window. The afternoon light went through her like she was made of it. "She's different since the Spire. Since Corvane. Did you know she requested reassignment? They gave her the archives. The *archives*, Jude. The woman who hunted us across three provinces is now cataloguing forty years of suppressed covenant correspondence and writing reports about it."

"Good," Jude said. "Let her sit with it. Sitting with things is underrated. I did it for thirty years and look how well-adjusted I am."

"You hid in a basement counting impossible keys."

"And now I have a sign. Growth takes many forms."

Odile laughed—that real laugh, rare and bright, the one that made her flicker like a struck match—and then, as it faded, something else crossed her face. Something Jude had been watching gather at the edges of her for weeks now, the way you watch weather come in over hills.

She was lighter. Not ghost-light, which she'd always been. *Lighter.* As though whatever held her here was loosening its grip thread by thread, and she was letting it.

"Odile," he said quietly. "How much longer?"

She didn't pretend to misunderstand. She never had. It was one of the thousand things he'd loved about her—the dead courier who'd refused to move on, who'd walked beside him from a loading dock to the top of the world, who'd taught him that his curse was a craft and his silence was the wound underneath it.

"Not long," she said. "A season, maybe less. The dead stay for unfinished business, Jude, and mine has been finished for months. Every memorial you carve, every question you answer, every widow who walks down your stairs lighter than she came up—that's the business, being finished. Bit by bit." She looked at her hands, which were more window than woman today. "I stayed because I failed my fellow keepers. Because I died running instead of standing. And then I stood, at the end—with you, at the Spire. And then I stayed because you needed teaching. And you don't. You really don't anymore. Your listening is better than mine ever was."

"Your listening started a war's worth of trouble and saved the world. Mine answers questions about coats."

"Coats matter." Her voice gentled. "That's the whole lesson, Jude. That's everything I had to teach. The small things are the covenant. A map is just a coat for a promise. A spire is just a coat for a door. And a ghost is just a coat for love that hasn't found its shelf yet."

Jude looked down at his hands—carver's hands, scarred, steady—and found his throat tight. "I keep thinking I should argue. Find some reason for you to stay. There must be unfinished business somewhere. You never told me what happened to your brother's ring, or—"

"Jude."

"—or the lighthouse keeper you mentioned once, in the pass, you never finished that story—"

"*Jude.*" She was smiling. It broke his heart and mended it in the same stroke. "Do you know what I hear, when I stand in your workshop? Nothing. Do you understand? For the first time since I died, I hear *nothing pulling at me*. No debt, no guilt, no arrow-wound screaming its one note. Just the sound of you working, and the street below, and the tea." She shook her head, wonder-struck even now. "I spent eleven years as a ghost thinking peace was a place I'd failed to reach. It turns out it's a workshop above a guild hall, and it smells like bone dust and bad tea, and it has a ridiculous sign."

"*He will listen* is not ridiculous."

"It's a little ridiculous. Keep it forever."

He laughed, wet-eyed, and she drifted over and put her hand near his shoulder—the closest she could come to touching, a warmth like sunlight through glass—and they stayed that way while the afternoon aged.

"When you go," he said finally, "will I be able to—after? Will you be somewhere I can reach?"

"No. And that's right, Jude. That's how it should be. You'll grieve, and it'll hurt, and then one morning you'll carve something and realize the silence where I used to be doesn't ache anymore—it just *is*, the way a healed bone is. Stronger at the break." She tilted her head. "Besides. You'll be busy. Word travels, in the city and... elsewhere. The dead talk to each other, you know. We compare notes. There's already a queue forming on the other side, carver. Souls who heard about the man at the Spire and want to know if the stories are true."

"What stories?"

"That the covenant holds because one small frightened man stopped hiding." She smiled. "I may have spread a few of those myself."

* * *

Autumn deepened. The queue, as Odile promised, formed on both sides of the veil.

Living clients came first: the ferry-guild wanting a memorial for six boatmen lost in the spring floods; a young sigilwright—one of Maren's new students, earnest and ink-stained—who wanted a token for a grandmother she'd never met; even, one gray Tuesday, Captain Vess herself, standing awkwardly in the doorway with her cap in her hands, asking in a voice stripped of all command whether he could listen for a soldier who'd died under her orders years ago, whose name she'd never spoken aloud since.

Jude carved for her until dark. Neither of them mentioned the apple.

And the dead came too—not lingering echoes in bones, but passing souls, drawn to the workshop like moths to a lamp that actually worked. They came with small weights: a message undelivered, an apology unsaid, a name unspoken. Jude learned to hold workshop hours for them the way he held hours for the living. Tuesdays and Fridays, after the tea went cold. Maren teased him that he was running the afterlife's most bureaucratic post office. He took it as a compliment. Bureaucracy, done kindly, was just care with good recordkeeping, and nobody knew that better than a ledger-keeper.

Sef's tokens hung above the workbench on a leather cord—the old mute carver's last works, each one a sentence in a language of bone that only Jude could fully read. *We were never alone. We were never wrong. We were enough.* On hard days, Jude touched them the way other men touched talismans, and they worked better than talismans, because they'd been paid for in full.

Pip's empty chair sat by the window. Nobody ever sat in it. Maren had threatened to, once, jokingly, and had received a look from Jude that had made her raise both hands and retreat, and neither of them had joked about it since. The door between worlds was closed—but it was closed, Odile reminded him, the way a door is closed when someone has just stepped through it and means to come back. Doors like that aren't shut. They're waiting.

Some evenings, working late, Jude would catch himself talking aloud to the empty room—to Odile's fading presence, to Sef's tokens, to the memory of a child who'd seen his ghosts plainly and hadn't been afraid—and he no longer felt foolish doing it. The dead had taught him that speaking into apparent emptiness was not madness. Half the time, it turned out, the emptiness was listening.

On the first frost of the season, Odile came at dusk, and Jude knew—before she said a word, before she even fully gathered herself out of the corner—he knew the way you know the last page of a book is coming, by the thinning of the pages under your thumb.

"It's tonight," she said.

The knife stopped. Outside, the first snow of the year was starting, fat and slow, catching the lamplight from the guild hall below.

"Tonight," he repeated. His voice came out sanded flat.

"You've been ready for weeks. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise." She drifted to the window, to the chair that belonged to Pip, and looked out at the falling snow with an expression of such simple contentment that it steadied him more than any speech could have. "I watched the city burn once, from a rooftop, the night I took the map. I thought that would be the last beautiful terrible thing I saw. Instead I got a rebellion, a child, a rescue that was not remotely stealthy, a confession around a fire, and a sign that says *he will listen*." She turned. "Jude Underhill. You were the best unfinished business I ever had."

"Odile—"

"Listen to me. Last lesson." Her form was going soft at the edges, like charcoal in rain, and her voice was warm as a hearth. "You spent your whole life believing invisibility was safety. Look at you now. Known by name. Sought out. *Heard.* By the living and the dead alike. And the world didn't end—it got mended. Remember that, the next time fear tells you to hide. Fear lies. It lied to you for thirty years, and you survived it anyway, and then you stopped believing it, and look what grew."

She raised a hand—nearly solid, nearly warm, the closest she'd ever come—and Jude raised his to meet it, palm to almost-palm, sunlight through glass against sunlight through glass.

"Thank you," he said. "For the map. For staying. For teaching me that hearing isn't drowning."

"Thank you," she said, "for answering. All those years, all those voices, and you never once stopped wanting to answer. That was the keeper in you, Jude. It was always there. We just had to dig down to it." Her smile was the last thing to fade, and it faded slowly, generously, like a lamp being carried away down a long road rather than snuffed. "Mind the shop. Mind the tea. And mind the door—"

The snow swirled where she had been.

"—doors like ours," the air seemed to finish, faint as breath on a window, "have a way of opening."

Then she was gone, truly gone, and the workshop was silent in the way a home is silent—full, not empty.

Jude stood a long time, hand still raised, snow falling past the window. He did not weep, quite. Or he did, a little, the way you water something you've just planted.

Then he wiped his eyes, banked the fire, and picked up his knife—because there was a token to carve, the hardest one yet, for a ghost who no longer needed it but deserved it anyway. A spire, and a road, and a figure walking it without hurry, because she had finally arrived.

And as he set the first cut into the bone, three floors below, the guild hall bell rang the closing hour—and beneath it, clear and small and utterly impossible, from the direction of the stair—

Jude heard a knock.

A small knock. Child-sized. Patient. Familiar as his own heartbeat.

He was smiling before he reached the door.
