## 2. What the Dead Keep

The bell above the Gilderscale's door had a crack in it, and so it rang wrong—a flat, wounded note that Orsen had long ago stopped hearing as anything but the sound of business. They set down the bone button they'd been polishing, squared the ledger on the counter, and prepared to be nobody.

This was the trick of it, the whole art of surviving with a gift like theirs: be furniture. Be the clerk behind the counter, ink-stained and unremarkable, a person the eye slides off like rain off waxed canvas. The Gilderscale dealt in impossible things—compasses that pointed at what you'd lost rather than north, mirrors that showed you at your best age, keys cut for doors that hadn't been built yet—and impossible things drew impossible clients, and impossible clients needed someone to write it all down without flinching.

Orsen flinched constantly. They had simply gotten very good at doing it on the inside.

The client was a man in his fifties, dressed in the good-but-not-too-good wool of a successful chandler, holding his hat in both hands as though it might bolt. His eyes were red-rimmed in the way of a man who had cried in private and intended to keep it that way in public.

"Master Whitlock?" He read the name from the little brass plate on the counter, mispronouncing it slightly. Orsen let him. "They said—I was told you handle... appraisals. Of sentimental items."

"That depends on the item," Orsen said. Their voice came out flat and professional, exactly as rehearsed. "And what you want it for."

He set the hat down and drew something from an inner pocket wrapped in a handkerchief. Even before he unwrapped it, Orsen felt it—the way you feel a draft from a door left open three rooms away. A thin thread of presence, tugging at the edge of their awareness like a fisherman testing a line.

*No*, Orsen thought, to the object, to themselves, to the universe generally. *Not today.*

But he unwrapped the handkerchief anyway, and there it was: a locket. Silver, oval, old enough that the engraving of ivy around its border had worn soft as breath on glass. The kind of thing that had been held so often it had forgotten how to be anything but held.

It began speaking before it even touched the counter.

"—tell her the blue dress, tell her I finished it, tell her it's in the cedar chest under the winter cloaks, she'll know which one, she'll know—"

A woman's voice. Warm, hurried, the voice of someone who has a great deal to say and very little time to say it. It poured out of the locket the way water finds cracks, and only Orsen could hear it, and Orsen kept their face perfectly still while their stomach turned over.

"Chandler Vess," the man said. "Tomas Vess. This belonged to my wife. Maren." He said the name carefully, like setting down something fragile. "She passed in the spring fever. Three years past."

"I'm sorry for your loss," Orsen said, because that was the script, and scripts were load-bearing.

"The fever took a lot of people that year." He swallowed. "What I want is simple. I want to know—" His voice caught, steadied. "I want to know if she's still in there. If any part of her is. The peddler who sold it to me secondhand said these things sometimes... hold a person. Not the soul, nothing wicked, just—an echo. An impression. And if there's even a scrap of her in this locket, I'd pay anything to hear it."

The locket, meanwhile, had not stopped talking.

"—and tell her about the money in the flour tin, don't let him spend it on drink, not that he would, he's a good man, he's such a good man, tell her I never once regretted marrying a man who smells of tallow—"

Orsen looked at the locket. The locket, in the way of objects, did not look back, but it pressed against them anyway, urgent and bright, a candle flame of a dead woman still trying to finish a sentence three years after her mouth had stopped working.

Here was the rule. Here was the rule Orsen had carved into themselves deeper than any guild mark: *never answer the dead.*

Not because answering was hard. That was the horror of it. Answering was the easiest thing in the world. You simply spoke, and they heard, and then—then everything changed. Then they knew you could hear them, and they never stopped asking. Orsen had learned that lesson at nine years old, in a room that smelled of camphor and grief, and they had paid for the lesson in ways they did not permit themselves to think about during daylight hours.

So: the rule. Never answer. Never confirm. Never let a client see the moment the object speaks to you, because clients talk, and talk travels, and the word *gifted* was already dangerous in certain quarters of the city, whispered in certain rooms, priced in certain ledgers.

"It's a fine piece," Orsen said aloud, turning the locket with careful, impersonal fingers. "Silver, low-grade alloy in the hinge—see the discoloration? Engraving done by hand, local work, maybe thirty years old. Sentimental value high, market value modest. If you're looking to sell, the Gilderscale can offer—"

"Sell?" Chandler Vess looked at Orsen as though they'd suggested selling one of his children. "I don't want to sell it. I want to know if she's *in* it."

Inside the locket, the woman said: "*Please.* Please, whoever's listening. Please. I've been talking for three years."

Orsen set the locket down on the counter between them, gently, the way you'd set down something sleeping.

"Master Vess," they said, "I'm going to tell you something true, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide anything." They folded their hands on the ledger, ink-stained and steady. "Objects hold impressions. That's real—it's documented in our own catalogues, it's why some items trade at ten times their material worth. A well-loved thing keeps the shape of the love. But it isn't her. It's the memory of her, pressed into silver the way a flower presses into a book. Flat. Silent. Beautiful, perhaps, but it won't speak, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you cruelty at a markup."

The lie came out smooth as poured wax. Three years of practice, ten years, fifteen—the lies had gotten so polished Orsen sometimes mistook them for truth, which was, they suspected, the entire point of polishing.

Vess stared at the locket. His jaw worked. "The peddler swore—"

"Peddlers swear to make sales."

"And there's no one—" His voice dropped, went ragged. "There's no one who could... ask? If a scrap of her lingered. Just to know if she went easy. Just to know if she knew we loved her at the end, because I wasn't— I was at the shop when the fever took its turn, I wasn't *there*, and I have thought every day for three years about whether she died wondering—"

"*Tell her,*" the woman in the locket begged, and her voice cracked like river ice. "*Tell her I knew. Tell her I knew every day. Tell my daughter—my daughter, please, she'll be grown now, she'll be taller than me, tell her the blue dress is hers, tell her I finished the hem the night before, tell her—*"

Orsen counted the floorboards. One, two, three, four. An old habit. Counting gave the guilt somewhere to stand besides your chest.

"There are frauds," Orsen said quietly. "Men with cold reading tricks and paid confederates. There are desperate people who believe what they need to believe. And there is nothing in that locket but silver and memory. I'm sorry. Take it home. Talk to your daughter yourself—that's the living answer, and it's the only one worth having."

For a long moment Vess didn't move. Then he nodded, small and broken, and gathered up the handkerchief, and the locket with it. As his fingers closed around the silver, the woman's voice spiked—one last flare, like a fire collapsing—

"*Don't let him leave, don't let him—oh, oh, please, I can feel him letting go, please, please—*"

—and then the shop door shut behind him, and the bell rang its flat wounded note, and the silence where she'd been was somehow louder than she had ever been.

* * *

Orsen sat alone at the counter for a long time.

The afternoon light moved across the floor in a slow amber bar, catching dust motes, catching the edges of the shelves where the Gilderscale's inventory waited in patient rows: a lantern that burned regrets instead of oil; a pair of gloves that remembered every handshake; a music box that played the song you'd danced to at your wedding whether or not you'd ever had one. Impossible things. Kept things. Each one a small sealed room with something inside it.

Orsen had spent eleven years learning to walk through those rooms without opening the doors.

It had started as self-preservation and hardened into doctrine. Master Corvane Hale himself had taught it, early on, in the gentlest possible terms. *You have an ear for provenance, Whitlock. A rare gift for tracing an object's history. But hear me:* Hale had leaned close, smelling of pipe smoke and good paper, *the history is the merchandise. The voices are not. A client pays for what an object remembers, never for what it says. Blur that line and you'll ruin the guild's reputation—and worse, you'll ruin yourself. The dead are endless, child. Answer one and you've answered them all, forever.*

And Hale had been right. Hale was almost always right, which was why Orsen trusted him more than they had ever trusted anyone living, and why the trust sat in Orsen's chest like a stone they tried not to look at directly.

Because here was the thing Orsen knew, and carried, and would carry to whatever grave eventually received them:

The woman in the locket had been real. Not flesh, not soul, perhaps—but real. Three years of talking into silver with no assurance anyone heard. She had known she was dying. She had used her last strength to press a message into an object, the way a drowning sailor presses a message into a bottle, and a grieving man had carried that bottle into the one shop in the city where the message could actually be read, and the clerk had told him it was empty.

*Guilt dressed up as discipline*, Orsen thought bitterly. That was the phrase for it. They'd invented rules and called them professionalism. They'd built a fortress of procedure around a wound and called the fortress a career.

They opened the ledger and made the entry, because the ledger always got made, because the ledger was the one conversation Orsen never refused to have:

*Item 4471. Silver locket, ivy border, c. 30 yrs. Client: T. Vess, chandler. Appraisal only. No sale. Impression: strong. Disposition: returned to family.*

Then, after a pause, in smaller script, squeezed against the margin where no auditor would look:

*Blue dress. Cedar chest. Under the winter cloaks. Hem finished.*

If the daughter ever came asking—if Vess ever worked up the courage to bring the locket back, or if gossip carried the girl to the Gilderscale on her own—well. Orsen couldn't answer the dead. But nothing in the rulebook said they couldn't leave notes for the living.

It was a coward's compromise. Orsen knew that too. It was a way of paying the toll without crossing the bridge, of feeding the guilt just enough to keep it quiet without ever actually looking at it. Eleven years of margin-notes. Eleven years of messages in bottles, thrown backward through time, most of them never collected.

Outside, the light was failing toward evening. Somewhere in the workshop, the bone button from that morning sat on the bench where Orsen had left it, and its dead owner was probably still complaining about the cold, patient as frost, willing to wait forever for a reply that would never come.

Orsen capped the ink, closed the ledger, and told themselves—as they told themselves every night—that tomorrow would be ordinary, that the bell would ring, that they would be furniture, that the rule would hold.

They almost believed it.

Which was when Guildmaster Hale appeared in the doorway of his office, pipe unlit, expression pleasant, and said, in the warm voice he saved for matters of quiet importance:

"Whitlock. A word. Nothing troubling—just a client inquiry I thought you'd want to handle personally. Someone asking after a locket."
