## 3. The Guildmaster's Kindness

The summons came the way all of Corvane Hale's summons did: a folded slip of cream paper, sealed with green wax, delivered by a boy who never met Orsen's eyes and never waited for an answer.

*Come up when the floor is quiet. — C.H.*

Orsen read it twice, then set it beneath the ledger's brass weight and went back to cataloguing. That was the trick of Hale's notes—they were never orders, only invitations shaped like them, and the correct response was to appear as though you had arrived at his door by your own unhurried decision. Orsen had learned that in their first year, when they were nineteen and still flinched at every voice that rose out of the inventory. Hale had smiled then too, warm as a hearth, and said, *You'll do well here, Whitlock. You understand that nothing in this building is only what it seems.*

By late afternoon the showroom floor had emptied of clients. Orsen climbed the narrow stair behind the tapestry of the drowned merchant—Hale liked the tapestry; he said it reminded clients that the Gilderscale dealt in things with histories—and knocked once on the office door.

"Enter, enter." Hale's voice carried the particular brightness he saved for people he was about to be generous to.

The office smelled of cedar oil and old money. Maps of trade routes hung framed on the walls, their ink faded to the color of weak tea, and every surface held some impossible object arranged with studied casualness: a compass that pointed at whatever you wanted most, its needle trembling; a bell jar containing what appeared to be a small storm, sulking; a chess set whose pieces shifted position when no one watched, mid-game against itself for eleven years now. Orsen knew each item's provenance by heart. They had written half the entries themselves.

Hale stood at the window with his back half-turned, hands clasped behind him, watching the lamplighters work their way down Gilderscale Row. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, silver threading his beard, dressed in the deep green of guild rank with a chain of office that caught the light and threw little coins of gold across the ceiling. When he turned, his face did the thing it always did—the warmth arriving like weather, settling over everything.

"There she is. Or *he*. Forgive me, I never remember which you prefer this season."

"Either," Orsen said. "Both. It doesn't matter."

"It matters to you, so it matters to me." He gestured to the chair across from his desk, and poured two glasses of plum wine from the decanter that lived there—another performance, but a well-rehearsed one. The wine was genuinely good. That was the difficulty with Corvane Hale: almost none of it was false, exactly. It was simply all of it aimed.

"To discretion," Hale said, raising his glass.

They drank. Orsen waited. In seven years they had learned that Hale always spoke first about the weather or the wine or the price of river-tar, circled twice, and landed where he had intended to land from the beginning. There was no hurrying him. Hurrying made him pleasant; pleasant made him dangerous.

"The Aldermoor woman," Hale said at last, settling into his own chair with a sigh. "The locket, Tuesday last. Tell me about her."

Orsen kept their face still. "She brought a silver locket for appraisal. Hairwork inside, provincial make, maybe forty years old. I valued it at three crowns. She took it back unsold."

"And?"

"And nothing. She left."

Hale smiled and swirled his wine. "You see, this is why I sleep soundly, Whitlock. Another appraiser would have chattered. Would have told me she wept over it, asked strange questions, wanted to know if objects could be *listened to*—" he said the word lightly, mockingly, a man handling a superstition he didn't believe in, "—and I'd have had to decide whether she was a curiosity or a problem. But you bring me nothing. Clean ledgers. Quiet rooms. You have a gift for making the Gilderscale feel safe."

*I have a gift,* Orsen thought, *for making myself feel safe.* But they said, "That's the job."

"That's more than the job." Hale leaned forward, and the lamplight moved on his chain. "I've been thinking about your future. Seven years you've kept the impossible-ledgers without a single error I've found—and I look, Whitlock, you know I look. You know which items hum and which ones merely sit there, and you've never once let that knowledge leak into a valuation. Do you know how rare that is? I had a reader once, before your time. Marla. Wonderful with the haunted pieces. And within a year she was telling clients their grandmothers' last words for coin, and within two she was telling *me* what my own desk thought of me, and after her I had to—" he waved a hand, breezy, "—well. She moved on. To the coast. Suddenly."

The fire crackled. Somewhere below, the shop's front bell chimed and fell silent.

"I'm not Marla," Orsen said.

"No. No, you're not. Marla wanted to be interesting. You want to be *left alone*, which is far more useful quality in a keeper of secrets." Hale set down his glass. "So. From the first of Frostmonth, I'm raising your commission. A full share-point. And I want you taking the sensitive consultations directly—no more routing through Pell at the front desk. Clients who come asking after the strange stock, the bound pieces, the things we keep in the cellar drawers. You'll handle those personally."

It was, on its face, a promotion. It was trust, offered openly, with wine. Orsen felt the gratitude rise in them, genuine and warm, and hated how easily it came—seven years of being fed and sheltered and praised by this man, and the body still responded to his approval like a plant to sun.

"Thank you," Orsen said. "Why the change?"

"Why not?" Hale laughed. Then, because he was honest in the way a knife is honest, he added: "Because lately certain clients ask questions I'd rather be answered carefully. And because I'd rather know who asks them."

He opened a drawer—not the locked one, the ordinary one—and drew out a slim book bound in grey leather. Not one of Orsen's ledgers. One Orsen had never seen.

"You keep our records beautifully," Hale said, "but a guildmaster keeps other records. Nothing sinister. A note of who comes asking about the listening-work. Who lingers near the cellar stairs. Who pays in old coin, or asks whether we buy *cursed* stock outright, no questions." He turned pages with one thick finger, and Orsen saw names, dates, small neat annotations in Hale's hand. "The Aldermoor woman is here, you'll notice. Third visit this year. Always with something that belonged to a dead relative. Always asking, in her roundabout way, whether the dead can be reached."

"She's grieving," Orsen said. "Half the city grieves."

"Of course. Of course they do." Hale closed the book gently, the way one closes a sleeping child's door. "And grief makes people careless, and carelessness, these days, makes people *visible*. There are men in this city, Whitlock, who collect names like mine collects objects. I keep this ledger so that when such men come asking—and they will—I can tell them truthfully that the Gilderscale has never trafficked in the gifted. We appraise objects. Full stop. What a client does with her sorrow in the privacy of our showroom is between her and her dead." His eyes were kind and absolutely clear. "But I need to know who they are. You understand. Forewarned is forearmed, and I am forearming on behalf of everyone under this roof—including you."

*Including you.* The words sat in Orsen's chest like a swallowed stone.

There it was, then, laid out plain if one looked: the kindness and the cage built from the same timber. Every client Orsen ever served would pass through Hale's grey book. Every question asked in a lowered voice would become an entry, a date, an annotation. And Orsen's own discretion—the silence they had spent years perfecting, the refusal to answer the dead, the careful blankness—was not merely appreciated. It was *harvested*. Their invisibility was an asset on someone else's balance sheet.

And yet. And yet the wine was good, and the share-point was real, and the alternative to Corvane Hale's shelter was the street, where Orsen had been before, at fourteen, hungry and hearing voices in every fence rail and door latch until they thought they'd go mad in broad daylight. Hale had found them haggling over a bone comb that wouldn't stop whispering its dead owner's name, and had bought it—bought *them*, though he'd have said rescued—with no questions and a hot meal. Whatever else was true, that meal had been real.

"Understood," Orsen said.

"I knew you'd see it." Hale beamed, refilling both glasses unasked. "One more thing, and then I'll release you to your buttons and your bones." He tapped the grey book once. "A client is coming Friday. Name of Doss. He'll ask about a mirror. Don't value it—just listen to what he asks, and afterward, come tell me. Word for word, if you can manage it."

"What kind of mirror?"

"The kind that shows rooms it shouldn't." Hale said it lightly, but his thumb pressed flat against the grey leather cover, holding it shut, and Orsen noticed—for the first time all evening—that the guildmaster's hands were not entirely steady. "He claims it showed him a chamber in the royal spire. A chamber that officially doesn't exist. Now, most likely he's a fraud or a drunk, and we'll send him off with tea and sympathy. But if he isn't—" Hale smiled, and for just a moment the warmth thinned enough to show the machinery underneath, all counterweights and intent, "—then the Gilderscale may soon hold an object worth more than everything on this row combined. And I should very much like to know it before anyone else does."

* * *

Orsen walked home through streets going blue with dusk, past shuttered stalls and the first stubborn lamps, and tried to sort the evening into its proper piles: gratitude here, unease there, and the two of them bleeding into each other at the edges like wet ink.

Hale had saved them. Hale used them. Both statements were simply true, the way a lock and its key are both iron.

What troubled Orsen most, turning it over on the long walk, was not the grey book itself. Spies kept books; cities ran on them. What troubled Orsen was the shape of Hale's care—how precisely it fit around them, how it had always fit, from that first hot meal onward. *You want to be left alone, which is far more useful.* As if Orsen's whole self had been appraised, valued, and entered into a ledger long ago, in a hand even neater than their own.

At the workshop door, Orsen paused. Through the window, the night's inventory waited on its shelves: the singing kettle, the mourning ring, the bone button from the old man who complained of cold. Tomorrow there would be valuations. Friday there would be Doss and his impossible mirror, and afterward Orsen would climb the stairs and repeat every word to a man who wrote down the sorrows of strangers in grey leather.

Inside, in the dark, something on the third shelf began—very softly—to hum.

Not the kettle. Not the ring.

Something Orsen had never catalogued at all.
