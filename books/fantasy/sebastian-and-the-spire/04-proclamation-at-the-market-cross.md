## 4. Proclamation at the Market Cross

The morning the heralds came, Orsen Whitlock was haggling over tallow.

"Four coppers a pound," said the chandler, Marta Ostrey, her hands planted on her hips like fence posts. "You think I'm robbing you?"

"I think you're robbing the pig the fat came from." Orsen held up the block, turning it toward the light. It was good tallow—pale, clean, no rancid edge—but they had done this dance every market day for six years, and both of them knew the choreography by heart. Three coppers. A sigh. A grudging nod.

Marta leaned closer across her stall and dropped her voice. "And how's your mother's candle burning? The one I gave you for the grave?"

Orsen kept their face pleasant. "It burns."

It burned, in fact, in the corner of the workshop where Orsen had never once lit it, because the dead woman who had owned it—the chandler's actual mother, whose memory was pressed into the wax like a flower in a book—would not stop humming a lullaby, and Orsen could not bear lullabies before dawn.

That was the whole shape of Orsen's life, if you looked at it plainly. A shop on Tanners' Row with a crooked sign. Ledgers full of impossible things, kept in a hand so small and neat it might have been printed. And everywhere, always, the murmur of the touched-dead: complaints in the grain of old chairs, grief soaked into wedding rings, the long slow boredom of things buried in attics. Orsen had learned to walk through the world the way a man walks through shallow water—one ear tuned to the current beneath, face betraying nothing.

Three coppers changed hands. Marta wrapped the tallow in brown paper and was just pressing it across the counter when the bells began.

Not the chapel bells. Not the alarm bells on the seawall, which had a ragged, panicked rhythm everyone knew from the flood year. These were the palace bells—deep, bronze, unhurried—and they rang in a pattern Orsen had heard exactly twice in their lifetime. Once for the king's accession. Once for the king's funeral, seven years ago, though the city had been told it was a fever and the body had never been shown.

The second time, the coffin had weighed almost nothing. Orsen knew because they had carved the nameplate, and the wood had told them so—told them, in the dry whisper of seasoned oak, that whatever lay inside was lighter than any king had a right to be.

Orsen had put that thought away like a knife in a drawer, and had not opened the drawer since.

Now the bells rang again, and the whole market began to turn toward the square like grass bending under wind.

* * *

The Market Cross stood at the heart of Ashfall's lower town, a squat granite pillar older than the guildhalls around it, its carvings worn to suggestion. On ordinary days it was where fishwives argued and children played knucklebones against its base. Today it wore a scaffold of fresh pine, and on that scaffold stood three figures in the black-and-gold of the crown, and behind them, mounted, a dozen soldiers with the new pale livery that nobody in the market could quite place—not the old royal blue, not anything Orsen recognized from the ledgers of regalia the Gilderscale had brokered over the years.

New uniforms meant new orders. New orders meant new money. New money meant somebody at court was afraid, and fear, as Guildmaster Hale liked to say over his evening wine, was the most reliable commodity in any kingdom.

The crowd gathered the way crowds do—loosely at first, out of curiosity, then densely, drawn forward by some animal instinct that danger is better witnessed in company. Orsen stayed at the edge, near the shuttered front of the pie-shop, tallow parcel tucked under one arm, and let the bodies close between themself and the scaffold. Old habit. Invisibility was a craft like any other; it wanted practice.

The middle herald unrolled a scroll. He was young, with a voice trained to carry, and he read the way men read when they are glad the words are not about them.

"By decree of His Majesty, the King, Protector of the Harvests, Keeper of the Long Peace—" the titles went on a while; there were eleven of them now, and Orsen noticed two new ones since the last public reading, *Warden of Winter* among them, which was strange, because winter had always kept itself—"—let it be known throughout the realm and all its holdings:

"That there exist among Our subjects persons possessed of unlawful faculties. Persons who traffic with the dead. Who read the hearts of objects. Who steal what is not theirs to know. Such arts are abominations before crown and chapel alike, and shall be rooted out for the safety of the faithful.

"Therefore: all such persons shall present themselves at the Hall of Registry within thirty days of this reading, to be named, examined, and entered upon the rolls. Those who register shall be granted the King's protection and lawful license to labor under supervision.

"Those who do not—" the herald paused, and here his training failed him slightly; his voice thinned, "—shall be taken. Taken means taken. Their names struck. Their goods forfeit to the Crown. Their houses sealed.

"And be it further known: any free subject who shelters, conceals, or aids an unregistered gifted person shall share their sentence entire.

"So decreed. So written. So witnessed."

He rolled up the scroll. Somewhere in the crowd a baby started crying, thin and furious, and was quickly hushed.

The silence that followed was worse than any sound Orsen had ever heard, and Orsen had spent their life listening to sounds other people couldn't hear. It was a silence made of hundreds of people each privately doing arithmetic—counting neighbors, counting themselves, weighing thirty days against the rest of their lives. You could feel it move through the square like cold water through cloth.

Then someone near the front said, too loudly, "Good. About time," and the silence broke into a hundred separate conversations, all of them quieter than conversations should be.

* * *

Orsen did not run. Running was for the guilty, and the first rule of invisibility was to be boring. They walked. They bought nothing else. They nodded to Marta, who did not quite nod back, and passed the doorway of Cobbler Fenwick, whose awning they had sheltered under during ten years of rains, and Fenwick was looking at the space just left of Orsen's shoulder, which is what people look at when they are deciding whether they ever really knew you.

At the mouth of Tanners' Row, old Ivet the rag-collector caught Orsen's sleeve. Her fingers smelled of dye and she was missing two teeth and she had known Orsen since before the shop, since the foundling years, and she said, very low:

"You'd tell me, wouldn't you? If it were you. If they came for you."

"I'm a bone-carver, Ivet," Orsen said. "I make buttons."

"Aye." She searched their face with eyes gone milk-pale but still shrewd. "Buttons." She let go. But she watched until Orsen turned the corner, and the watching had a weight to it, like a hand resting flat between the shoulder blades.

In the workshop, Orsen set down the tallow, sat on the stool, and did not carve anything for a long while.

Thirty days.

They thought of the ledger in the locked drawer downstairs at the Gilderscale hall—Ledger Nine, the quiet one, the one with entries in Orsen's smallest hand: the midwife on Bell Lane who could soothe fevers with a touch of salt; the ferryman who always, always knew which crossing would hold; the widow Doss whose knitting needles remembered every prayer she'd ever murmured into wool. Eleven names. Eleven clients who trusted the guild's discretion because the guild had spent forty years selling discretion like it sold silverware, and because Orsen had spent those same forty years making sure the ledger told the truth to no one.

Eleven names, and now the crown wanted them on different paper.

Orsen got up, took a blank button-blank of cow bone, and set the graver to it, because hands needed work even when the mind refused to come along. Shave, curl, shave. The familiar resistance of the material. The little white curls falling into the apron lap.

And beneath it all, faint as a fly against a window in another room, the bone began to murmur.

The cow had died four years ago in a slaughterhouse off Butcher's Bridge. Its residue lived in the bone the way warmth lives in a stone after sunset—not a soul, exactly, not words, more like the impression of having been warm and then not. Usually Orsen could work through it. It was the hum of a distant road.

Today it sounded like screaming.

Orsen stopped. Set down the graver. Listened properly, opening that inner door they usually kept cracked only wide enough for whispers—

—and heard the street.

Not the living street. The *kept* street. Every object within reach of Orsen's awareness had woken at once, like birds before an earthquake. The iron hinge on the door groaned with the voice of the smith who had forged it eighty years gone: *they came in the night, they came in the night, they came.* The floorboards muttered. The dead woman in the unlit candle hummed her lullaby faster, off-key, frightened. Even the tallow parcel on the counter had something in it—a pig's dumb contentment curdled into dread, though pigs had no reason to understand proclamations.

But they understood. That was the horror of it. The dead had always been patient with Orsen, the way weather is patient, the way seasons are. Now they were afraid, and their fear came up through everything Orsen touched like frost coming up through a boot sole.

*They will take us,* whispered the hinge. *They took us before.*

"They've never taken objects," Orsen said aloud, to an empty room, and hated the shake in their own voice. "It's people. It's people they want."

The candle's lullaby broke off entirely.

In the silence that followed, Orsen became aware of a second silence underneath it—the silence of the drawer where the ledger of small impossible things sat upstairs in the shop, eleven names thick, and of the larger ledger below the Gilderscale, and of the question that had been circling all morning like a gull over a drowning man:

*Who does the crown think keeps those ledgers?*

Because the decree had used a particular word. *Persons who traffic with the dead.* That wasn't chapel language; the chapel said *necromancy*, said *abomination*. This was guild language. Ledger language. Somebody had written that decree who knew exactly how the trade worked—who knew that somewhere in Ashfall there was a book with names in it, and that books could be surrendered.

Orsen thought of Guildmaster Corvane Hale's warm laugh, his wine-dark study, the way he'd clapped Orsen's shoulder last month and said, *You're the best discreet pair of hands I've ever employed, child. The guild is lucky. I am lucky.* And how, saying it, his eyes had gone briefly to the ledger shelf, and lingered, the way a man's eyes linger on a strongbox.

No, Orsen told themselves. No. Hale had pulled them out of the foundling gutter. Hale had fed them, taught them letters, taught them the trade, given them a life with walls and a lock on the door. Whatever the crown wanted, Hale would stand between it and his people. That was what he *did*. That was the whole architecture of him.

Wasn't it?

Outside, the light had begun to fail early—or seemed to. Autumn was leaning hard on the city this year; everyone said so. Orsen lit a lamp, and the flame burned steady and yellow and ordinary, and Orsen stared at it a long time, memorizing the color.

* * *

Near midnight, unable to sleep, Orsen went downstairs to the workshop to do the thing they never did: answer back.

Not to a client's locket. Not to a commissioned ring. Just—to talk. Because the house felt wrong, because every joist and nail had been muttering since sundown, and because a person can only be shouted at by the furniture for so many hours before courtesy breaks down.

"All right," Orsen said, standing in the middle of the dark shop, arms crossed like a scolding parent. "I hear you. What is it? What do you all know?"

The answer rose from everywhere at once.

From the bone blanks in their tray. From the whalebone stay in the corset waiting for collection. From the antler-handled knife, the ivory chess knight, the little carved saint on the sill that a client had never returned for. Dozens of dead residues, none of them souls enough to reason, all of them feeling enough to panic, and they spoke together in a chorus without language, a pressure rather than a sound, and for the first time in twenty years Orsen could not keep their feet.

They sat down hard on the stool. The room spun with voices. Under the chorus, one thread cut through, clearer than the rest—the old door hinge, the smith's voice, eighty years drowned and still stubborn:

*They came for us in the year of the gray harvest. They wrote our names in a book. Then they came with the book.*

A book.

Orsen's mouth went dry. "What book?" they whispered. "Whose book? Where is it?"

But the dead did not answer questions. The dead had never answered questions; that had always been the mercy of the gift, that it gave feelings and fragments and never testimony. The chorus simply swelled, and swelled, and outside the window the first flakes of the season's first snow came down through the lamplight—and did not melt on the sill.

Orsen watched them sit there, unmelted, wrong, and understood with perfect clarity that the thirty days had already begun to count, and that the floor of their ordinary life had not merely dropped—it had opened onto something much deeper down, something with a book in it, and a bell-pattern that rang for kings who were already dead.

Tomorrow, they would go to the Gilderscale. Tomorrow, they would ask Corvane Hale, face to face, what the guild intended to do with its ledgers.

Tonight, alone in the roaring quiet, Orsen Whitlock picked up the graver, and began, with hands that would not stop shaking, to carve.
