## 5. Voices Rising

The proclamation had been nailed to the market cross at noon, and by dusk it was everywhere else too—copied in a clerk's careful hand and pasted to the guildhall doors, the temple steps, the shuttered windows of the moneylenders on Candle Row. *By order of His Majesty: all persons possessed of unnatural gifts shall present themselves for registration within fourteen days.* Fourteen days. The ink was still wet when someone had scratched through *His Majesty* and written something else beneath it, small and furious, before the watch could sand it away.

Orsen read it twice on the walk home and touched nothing on the way. That was the trick of it, the discipline of it—hands in pockets, eyes down, walking the long way around the tanner's yard where the fence posts were old enough to have opinions. They had opinions tonight. Orsen could feel them even without touching, like heat coming off a stove two rooms away, like the pressure change before a storm. The whole city felt that way. The whole city felt *awake*.

The workshop above the Gilderscale's counting room was cold when Orsen let themselves in. They did not light the lamp at first. They stood in the doorway with their hand on the latch and listened to the ordinary silence—the settling of beams, the tick of the iron stove cooling, the river muttering under the street. Ordinary silence was a currency Orsen had spent thirty years learning to earn. Every quiet hour was bought with care: gloves in public, no lingering touches, never resting a palm on a doorframe longer than courtesy required.

They lit the lamp.

The button on the workbench said, *It's cold.*

It always said that. It was the first thing Orsen had ever heard—a bone button, carved from a shin-bone some apothecary had sold to a scrimshander, and the man it had been complained about the cold for eleven years without pause. Orsen had learned to sort his voice into the background the way you learn to sleep beside a busy road. Tonight, though, the button's voice came with company.

*Cold,* said the whalebone stay of a corset hanging on the wall hook, waiting to be mended for Mistress Aldery. *Cold, cold, they left me in the ground so long, cold.*

*Cold,* agreed the boxwood comb in the drawer, its owner a girl who had died of fever at nine and who only ever spoke in questions. *Is it cold? Is it cold where you are? Is it—*

*Hush,* said the antler-handled knife.

Orsen set the lamp down very carefully, the way you set down a full cup when the table has begun to shake.

This happened sometimes. Not often. Twice a year, perhaps, on nights when the wind came off the fens carrying something older than weather, or after an earthquake far away that no one in Varrowmarch even felt but the dead, who felt everything. On those nights the objects woke together, a congregation, and Orsen would bank the stove, wrap their hands in wool, and sit up until dawn doing sums in their head until the chorus thinned back into murmurs.

But this was not fen-wind. This was not any earthquake.

*Did you hear?* The comb again, urgent, its little voice like a nail drawn across silk. *Did you hear what they said? Did you hear about the list?*

*I heard,* said the corset stay, and there was something in its tone Orsen had never heard from an object before. Fear was common enough—fear was practically the dead's native tongue. But this was fear with a direction. Fear pointed somewhere. *They're making a list. My granddaughter's name is on a list.*

*Your granddaughter is forty years in her grave,* said the knife, not unkindly. Knife-owners were usually soldiers, and soldier-voices had a way of organizing a room.

*That doesn't stop it being true.*

Orsen took off their coat. Hung it on the peg. Kept their hands moving, because moving hands were steady hands, and steady hands were the whole architecture of their life. They crossed to the bench and began laying out tomorrow's commissions in order: the alderman's cane to be re-tipped, the locket chain to be soldered, the set of dice for the Gilderscale's own gaming room, which were made of something Orsen preferred not to identify and which never, ever spoke, which was its own kind of answer.

The moment their bare fingers closed around the cane, the alderman's grandfather said, *Boy. Boy, listen to me.*

"I'm working," Orsen said, out loud, because talking to them out loud kept the habit of silence intact—if anyone ever heard, it would sound like a carver's muttering, the harmless soliloquy of a tradesman alone.

*Listen.* The old man's voice was thin and dry as pith. *They came to our village in my day, too. The king's men, with their papers and their tongs. You know what we called it?*

"I don't want to know."

*The Counting. They counted us. And then they took the ones they'd counted.* A pause, the particular pause of a dead man choosing his words with the patience of the buried. *Counting comes before taking, boy. It always has.*

Orsen put the cane down. Wiped their hands on their apron as though the voice were grease.

The drawer with the comb was shut, but shut had never meant silent. *Is it cold? Is it cold in the cells? My mama said the cells under the spire are warm, she said they keep them warm on purpose, why would they keep them warm—*

"Because warmth keeps bone from cracking," Orsen said quietly. "For the readings. Bone-dust reads better warm." They knew this the way they knew most terrible things: from the guild's own ledgers, in Hale's neat hand, in the margins, in the columns marked *crown requisitions*. Orsen had been keeping those ledgers for six years and had told themselves every year that bookkeeping was not complicity, that ink was not action, that a man who wrote down what others did was a witness and not a hand.

Tonight, with the dead murmuring in the walls, the lie sat in Orsen's chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

By the second bell past midnight, the workshop was a riot.

It began with the shelf of unclaimed goods—the Gilderscale's overflow, items whose owners had died indebted, waiting out the statutory year before the guild could lawfully melt or sell them. Forty objects. Forty voices. On ordinary nights they were a low tide, a wash of half-words Orsen could wade through with practice. Tonight the tide came in all at once, and it did not murmur. It *argued*.

*—told him not to sign it, I told him, I was in his pocket when he signed it, I felt his hand shaking—*

*—the registry, the registry, ask about the registry—*

*—my daughter thinks I abandoned her, tell her, someone tell her I went back for her, I went BACK—*

*—they're burning the lists in Harrowgate, my cousin saw, they burn the lists and take the people anyway—*

*—quiet, quiet, he can hear us, look at him, he can HEAR us—*

That last one stopped Orsen mid-step, one boot half off, balanced absurdly on one foot in the middle of their own floor while forty dead things discussed them in the third person.

*He can hear us,* the voice repeated—it belonged to a silver christening cup, its owner a priest dead sixty years—and there was no mockery in it. There was something worse. There was *hope*, naked and awful, the hope of the drowned toward a boat. *You can hear us. Please. Please, we've been so quiet, we've been so good, please—*

"No," Orsen said. Their voice cracked on it. "No, I don't—I don't do that. I keep accounts. I write down what things are and what they're owed and that's all. That's *all*."

*What am I owed?* the cup asked instantly, terribly. *Tell me what I'm owed. No one will tell me what I'm owed.*

Orsen got the other boot off. Sat down hard on the stool. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until the dark behind them bloomed orange, and the voices did not stop, because of course they didn't; the voices had never once stopped for anything, not prayer, not whiskey, not the three days in their nineteenth year when Orsen had lain face-down on this same floor and begged every object in the room for silence and received instead the patient, puzzled compassion of the dead, who could not understand why hearing them was a wound. To them, Orsen knew, it was like shouting into a well and having the well finally, blessedly, shout back.

The rule was simple. The rule had kept them alive for thirty years: *never answer the dead.* Answer one, and word traveled—and the dead's word traveled in ways living gossip could not follow, through touch and wood and water, through the slow underground telegraph of everything people had ever held. Answer one, and soon every locket in Varrowmarch would find its way to the bone-carver's bench, and every desperate widow after it, and then the wrong widow, and then the watch, and then—

And now the crown had made it official. *Registration.* A polite word with a cell underneath it.

*They'll come here,* said the knife, and its soldier-calm cut through the din like a whistle through a flock. *All of you, be quiet. QUIET.* And astonishingly, the room obeyed—not silent, never silent, but lowered, the way men lower their voices when the sergeant starts speaking. *Boy. Carver. Whatever you are. Listen to me. I was taken in the Counting of my day. Do you know how they found us?*

Orsen lowered their hands. "How?"

*They didn't find us. We were given up.* The blade hummed faintly against its leather sheath, a sound like a held breath. *Neighbors. Priests. Mothers. A gift is a heavy thing to carry alone, and frightened people set down their burdens wherever they can. Someone will give you up, carver. It's already decided; it just hasn't happened yet. The only question is whether it's someone who loves you.*

"Hale won't," Orsen said, and heard how it sounded—even to their own ears, a boy insisting the wolf was a dog.

The dead, with the terrible tact of the dead, said nothing at all.

* * *

Toward the fourth bell, the noise changed character, and that was the worst part of the night.

The arguing died down. The pleading wore itself out, plea by plea, the way rain wears itself out, and what settled over the workshop was quieter and infinitely heavier: the sound of forty dead things listening. Listening to the street below. Listening to the river. Listening—to Orsen's utter horror—to each other, in that vast subterranean conversation the living were never meant to know existed, trading what they knew of the hunt like villagers trading news of a fire over the next hill.

*Three taken in Dunmark Low.*

*Five. My brother-in-law's neighbor's son was fifth.*

*They read the bones of everyone in the jail. Everyone. Whether registered or no.*

*My keeper's wife says the spire's been humming. Says the dogs won't go near the square.*

*The king hasn't shown his face since spring.*

*The king hasn't shown his FACE in seven years, if you ask me, and I was a face-man forty years, I know faces—*

*No one asked you.*

*No one asks anyone anything anymore, that's rather the problem—*

Orsen sat wrapped in a blanket with their gloved hands folded, watching the lamp burn down, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the dead were afraid *for themselves*. Not of death—they had that behind them. Of this. Of being counted, catalogued, read for dust and sorted into columns. The dead had endured everything the living could do and had made their peace with it, and now the living had invented something new, and the peace was broken, and the whole invisible nation of the held and handled things was stirring like a hive before a storm.

And every stirred thing remembered, dimly, distantly, that there was one person in Varrowmarch who could hear them.

*Carver,* said the priest's cup, gently, into the lull. *We know you're awake. We can always tell.*

"I'm asleep," Orsen said.

*You've been asleep for thirty years,* said the corset stay, and there was no cruelty in it, only the blunt mercy of the dead, who had no use for kindness that wasn't true. *We've watched you. All these years, we've watched you walk through us with your hands in your pockets like a man walking past a beggar he's decided not to see. And we understood. We did. Being seen is dangerous. But carver—the danger's come to your door regardless. It's nailing papers to crosses with your name in its pocket. So the question isn't anymore whether it's safe to hear us.*

The lamp guttered. Outside, snow had begun to fall—the first of the season, early, too early, fat flakes that seemed to fall more slowly than snow should, as though the sky were reluctant.

*The question,* the cup finished softly, *is what you'll do with the hearing, now that hiding inside it won't save you.*

Orsen looked at their hands in the gloves. A carver's hands, a ledger-keeper's hands, careful hands, invisible hands. For thirty years those hands had been a wall, and Orsen had believed the wall was a home.

Somewhere below, in the counting room, the guild's great safe clanked—Hale, working late again, later than he'd worked in years, since the proclamation. Since the lists. Orsen thought of the master's warm hand on their shoulder that afternoon, the praise, the *you're the only one I trust with the sensitive clients, Orsen*, and felt for the first time in six years of faithful service the faint, unforgivable chill of a question forming where trust had always lived:

*Which clients does he call sensitive? And who does he tell?*

The dead heard the thought arrive—Orsen was certain of it, the way one is certain of a change in the wind. The whole room of them went still, attentive, forty held breaths.

And then, cutting through the hush from the street three floors down, came a sound Orsen had never heard in this neighborhood at this hour: boots. Measured, unhurried, armored at the heel, stopping below the workshop window. A scabbard's tip tapped stone, once, twice—a fencer's habit, a soldier's restlessness.

The dead recognized it before Orsen did. Orsen felt the recognition move through the room like light through water.

*A sword,* whispered the comb. *There's a sword down there. An old one. Oh, it's so sad, it's so sad, it remembers everything, it remembers every single one—*

The knock, when it came, was precise and brief. Three raps. Businesslike. The knock of someone who expected the door to open and had a plan for if it didn't.

Orsen rose, heart hammering, forty dead voices holding their breath behind them, and went down the stairs into the dark to answer it.
