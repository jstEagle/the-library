## 6. The Knight Who Lost Her Name

The bell above the Gilderscale's side door rang wrong.

Orsen had spent six years learning the grammar of that bell—the fat double-toll of the wool merchants, the hesitant half-ring of widows selling their husbands' effects, the bright triple chime of children dared inside on a wager. This was none of those. This was a single strike, hard enough to rattle the hinge, and Orsen knew before looking up that whoever stood in the doorway had rung it the way you knock on the door of an enemy.

Rain came in with her. It beaded on a travel cloak the color of wet slate and pooled around boots that had been good once, before the road ate them. She was tall—not merely tall but built upward, like something meant to be seen from below—and she carried herself with the particular straightness of people who had been taught posture by men with sticks. Under the cloak's shadow: a face all angles and old sunburn, a jaw set like a door barred from inside, and eyes the gray of a blade left out overnight.

She did not remove her hood. That was the first thing Orsen noticed. The second was what she carried wrapped in oilcloth across her back—a long shape, sword-length, held not slung but gripped, the way you carry a thing you do not trust to stay quiet.

"Guild takes odd items," she said. Her voice was low, scraped at the edges, the voice of someone who had spent months talking only to herself. "That's what the sign says."

"The sign says we take *impossible* items," Orsen said. "Odd is for pawnshops."

Something moved behind her eyes—amusement, maybe, or its corpse. "Then I'm in the right place." She unwound the oilcloth in three precise motions, no wasted gesture, and laid the sword on the counter between them.

Orsen had handled ten thousand objects in this room. They spoke to Orsen the way strangers speak in queues—small complaints, small griefs, the muttered residue of lives. A spoon remembers soup. A wedding ring remembers a hand going thin. Most of it was weather. Most of it was nothing.

The moment Orsen's fingers brushed the scabbard, the room went silent.

Not empty-silent. *Held*-silent. The way a forest goes still when something large moves through it. Every voice in every shelf and drawer drew itself down to a whisper, the way crowds hush when a coffin passes. And from the sword itself came a sound Orsen had never heard from any object: a kind of attention. It was listening back.

"Name?" Orsen asked, because the ledger demanded one, and because the ledger was a wall Orsen hid behind the way other people hid behind fortifications.

There was a long pause. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere upstairs, Hale's pen scratched through the ledger of ordinary things, steady as a heartbeat.

"Voss," the woman said finally. "Sera Voss."

The name landed strangely. Not false—but worn, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many pockets. Orsen wrote it down and felt the ink dry crooked.

"And the item?"

"My sword."

"It has a name of its own, I expect. Swords usually do."

Her hand twitched toward the hilt—an old reflex, the flinch of a woman who had heard that question before from men who wanted more than an answer. "It did."

"What happened to it?"

"You tell me." Sera Voss leaned forward, and for the first time she looked directly at Orsen—really looked, past the counter, past the apron stained with bone dust, into whatever it was she thought she saw there. "You're the one they say can read them."

The words dropped into Orsen's stomach like stones into a well. *They say.* There were always whispers about the Gilderscale; that was the guild's armor, mystery instead of explanation. But whispers didn't usually arrive wearing rain and carrying swords that made the dead go quiet out of respect.

"I appraise provenance," Orsen said. "Maker's marks. Alloy. Craftsmanship. If you're looking for fortune-telling, the tanners' row has—"

"Read it." Not a request. Barely even a demand. Something rawer than either. "Please."

And there it was—that word, *please*, dragged up from somewhere deep, costing her visibly, the way pulling a splinter costs. Orsen had spent six years perfecting the art of refusal. Never answer the dead. Never get involved. The rule was load-bearing; pull it out and everything came down. Orsen opened the mouth that said no.

What came out instead was: "Take it out of the scabbard. Halfway. I need to touch the steel."

* * *

Sera watched the shopkeeper's hands and understood, in the way she understood terrain before a battle, that she had made a mistake or found an answer, and could not yet tell which.

The hands were wrong for a clerk. Too scarred across the knuckles, nicked at the fingertips—the hands of someone who carved, who worked material all day, who touched things constantly. Everything about Whitlock was modest, forgettable, arranged for invisibility: the plain apron, the spectacles, the careful dullness of the voice. But the eyes behind the spectacles were doing something Sera had only ever seen in two places before. In mirrors, during the war, when she looked at herself after a bad order. And in the faces of animals when the fire came through the wood.

Someone watching themselves very carefully to make sure nothing true showed through.

The blade cleared the scabbard halfway. Even now—even disgraced, even sold—it came out clean and eager, that soft whisper of oiled steel that had been the last sound for eleven men. Twelve. She'd stopped counting at twelve because thirteen would have made it real.

Whitlock touched the flat of the blade with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was tame.

Sera saw it happen. She would deny later that she believed it while it was happening, but she saw it: the change in the shopkeeper's face. The dullness fell away like a mask knocked loose, and underneath was something naked and drowning. Whitlock's breath caught. Their pupils blew wide. Their lips parted around a word that never quite formed.

The sword remembered.

Sera had known that for years, in the superstitious way soldiers know things—in the way the blade seemed heavier some mornings, in the way she dreamed of faces she couldn't place until she woke screaming names. Men she'd killed lived somewhere in the steel. She had told herself it was guilt. Guilt was survivable. Guilt was just your own mind gnawing its cage.

But watching Whitlock's face, seeing eleven—twelve—lives flicker across it like reflections on moving water, she understood the truth was worse. Guilt was hers. The memories were the sword's. She hadn't been imagining anything. She had been *carrying them*, all this time, in her hand.

"Stop," she heard herself say, and her voice came out cracked. "Put it down."

Whitlock snatched their fingers back as if burned—or as if released. They sat down abruptly on the stool behind the counter, hard enough that it skidded. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Rain filled the silence.

"They're loud," Whitlock said at last. It wasn't an appraisal. It was almost an apology.

"How many?"

"Eleven." Whitlock removed the spectacles and pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "No. Twelve. One of them isn't angry. He's—" A pause, and something crossed their face that Sera recognized with a sick lurch: pity. "He says he stepped in front of your blade on purpose. He wants you to know it wasn't your fault."

The floor tilted. Sera put her hand flat on the counter to steady herself, and hated it, hated the weakness of the gesture, hated that her body had betrayed her in front of a stranger. Tam. Corporal Tammerly, who had walked into her sword at Redford Bridge with his arms open and his captain's orders burning in his pocket—orders to kill the very people his company had been sent to protect.

"That's a trick," she said. "Some parlor cold-reading. You saw my face and gave me what I needed to hear."

"Yes," Whitlock agreed mildly, replacing the spectacles. "I could have done that. It would have been faster." They slid the ledger aside and looked at her with those careful, drowned eyes. "Why did you really come here, Ser Voss? Because I don't think you came to sell a sword. I think you came to find out if you're damned."

The rain kept falling. Upstairs, the pen scratched on, oblivious.

"And am I?" she asked. "Damned?"

Whitlock was quiet for so long she thought they wouldn't answer. When they did, their voice was very soft, and it was the voice of someone speaking from experience, not doctrine.

"The dead don't keep score. That's a thing the living invented, so we'd have something to hold over each other. The dead just... remain. What's in the steel isn't judgment, knight. It's witness." They pushed the sword gently back toward her. "Witness isn't the same as guilt. I take it you've learned the difference the hard way."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," Whitlock said. "You don't. Yet."

* * *

She should have taken the sword and left.

That was the sensible move, and Sera Voss had survived four years of disgrace by being sensible—by keeping her head down in dockside boarding houses, by drinking less than she wanted and noticing more than anyone liked. The shopkeeper was gifted. Openly, catastrophically gifted, the kind of gift the new proclamation called *unregistered arcane sensitivity*, the kind that got people taken to the spire in canvas hoods. Sera had read the decree nailed to the market cross along with everyone else, had watched the crowd's warmth curdle into arithmetic as neighbors counted neighbors. She knew what the reward was for reporting one.

She also knew what Marr's inquisitors paid informants, because she'd served under officers who took that coin.

Instead she heard herself saying: "How long have you been able to do that?"

The question hung between them like smoke. Whitlock's whole body changed—shoulders drawing in, chin tucking, the entire architecture of the person rearranging itself around denial. Sera had interrogated prisoners. She knew a man bracing against a blow.

"Do what?" Whitlock said.

"Don't." Sera kept her voice level. She sat down uninvited on the client's bench, which put them eye to eye, and she watched the shopkeeper realize they were trapped by courtesy in their own shop. "I watched your face change when you touched that blade. I've watched men die and I've watched men lie and those are different faces, and yours was neither. You heard them. The dead in the steel." She leaned forward. "So here's how this goes. Either you tell me I'm mad, and I take my sword and walk out, and I never think about this shop again. Or you stop insulting both of us."

For a moment—just a moment—something furious and terrified flashed behind the spectacles, and Sera thought: *there you are.*

Then it was gone, banked, buried, and Whitlock said quietly, "Since I was nine. Since my mother pressed my hand to my grandmother's ring and I heard her ask who was minding the stove." A bitter almost-laugh. "I thought everyone heard it. I said so. At dinner. With guests."

"And?"

"And within the year my parents were answering questions from the crown's assessors." Whitlock's hands folded neatly on the ledger, white-knuckled neatness. "I was sent to live with an uncle in another city. The family that remained... dispersed. That's the polite word." The gray eyes lifted. "You know what happens to families of the registered. So no—I'm not registered. No one knows what I am except Hale, and Hale knows because he found me starving in his cellar at fifteen and decided I was useful. Now you. Congratulations. You're the third person alive who knows."

"Why tell me? You don't know me."

"I know you came here instead of to an inquisitor." Whitlock tilted their head. "A woman sells a sword she clearly loves, to a guild of impossible things, in the week the crown starts hunting people exactly like me. That's not commerce, Ser Voss. That's a woman trying to bury evidence. You want the dead out of the steel because you're afraid of what they'll say if you're ever searched." A pause. "Or afraid of what they'll say if you're never searched. If you carry them forever."

Sera stood up. The bench scraped. Her hand had gone to her hip where the sword used to hang, and found nothing, and the absence was a wound reopening.

"You presume a great deal, clerk."

"Yes," Whitlock said simply. "It's the one dishonest thing about me. I pretend to be humble, and then I do that." They looked at her, and there was something in the look that Sera hadn't seen directed at her since before Redford Bridge—not fear, not contempt, not the careful blankness of civilians around a disgraced knight, but *recognition*. The weary, wary recognition of one hunted animal smelling another. "You recognized what I am the moment you walked in. Don't insult both of us twice."

The rain intensified, drumming on the roof, running in bright ropes down the window glass. Through it, the street outside was dim and empty—but Sera had been a soldier too long to believe empty meant safe. Empty streets these days meant eyes behind shutters.

"If I report you," she said slowly, "they'd pay me."

"They'd pay you."

"And I'd sleep fine. I've slept fine after worse." Her jaw tightened. "Probably."

"So why haven't you walked out yet?"

Because, Sera thought, because for three minutes in this dusty shop a stranger had put their fingers on her sins and come back with Tam's face and Tam's forgiveness, and whether it was sorcery or madness or truth it was more absolution than the Orders had given her in four years of penance. Because the sword on the counter was the last honest thing she owned. Because this tired, invisible little clerk was the first person since her cashiering who looked at her like a person rather than a cautionary tale.

Because she was so tired of being alone with it.

"Fix it," she said instead.

Whitlock blinked. "Fix what?"

"The blade. You said the dead just remain. Fine. Then help me—" She stopped, swallowed pride like a stone. "I need to know if I betrayed him. My brother. There's a ring. His ring. I've carried it for two years and I can't—every time I try to remember his last letter, I remember my court-martial instead, and I can't tell anymore what actually happened and what I've decided happened." Her voice had gone rough. She hated that most of all. "You hear the dead. Hear him."

The shopkeeper went very still. Sera watched the refusal assemble itself behind those eyes—the same wall she'd hit at the start, the practiced no, the discipline of a person who had survived by never getting involved. She watched Whitlock open their mouth to say it.

And she watched the wall fail.

"Not today," Whitlock said hoarsely. "Bring the ring tomorrow. Early, before the shop opens. And Ser Voss—if anyone follows you here, if anyone sees you come—"

"Then we were never in this room together," Sera finished. "I remember how this works. I used to be on the other side of it."

She took up her sword, rewrapped it in oilcloth with those three precise motions, and slung it across her back where it belonged. At the door she paused, hand on the bell, and looked back at the strange clerk among the shelves of murmuring, impossible things.

"What's your name? Your real one. Ledger names are for ledgers."

A hesitation. Then, so quietly she nearly missed it beneath the rain:

"Orsen. Orsen Whitlock."

"Sera Voss," she said, though they both knew she'd already given it. "Tomorrow, then."

The bell rang behind her, one hard toll, and the street took her back.

* * *

Alone in the shop, Orsen did not write the entry.

The ledger lay open, the pen uncapped, the line for *Voss, S.—one (1) longsword, maker's mark of the Third Company armories* waiting to be made permanent, to become fact, to flow upward into Hale's summaries and Hale's summaries into—who knew where, lately. Who knew where anything flowed, now, with the heralds' ink barely dry on the market cross.

Orsen looked at the space where the entry would go and heard, faintly, from the sword's lingering impression on the air, the twelfth soldier saying again: *it wasn't her fault, it wasn't her fault,* patient as rain.

Twelve men in a blade. A brother in a ring. And tomorrow, Orsen had promised to listen—to reach into a grieving woman's deepest wound and pull out the truth, the one thing Orsen had sworn six years ago never, ever to do.

Never answer the dead. Never get involved.

Orsen dipped the pen, hesitated, and wrote: *No sale. Item withdrawn by owner.*

Then, in the margin, in the tiny private shorthand only Orsen could read, three words that felt like stepping off a cliff: *she knows me.*

Upstairs, Hale's pen stopped scratching.

Footsteps crossed the floor above. Slow. Unhurried. Stopping, Orsen realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the weather, directly over the spot where the counter stood—as though the guildmaster were reading, through the boards, exactly what had just been written below.

The footsteps moved on. The pen resumed.

But Orsen sat in the lamplight a long while, listening to the dead murmur in the walls, wondering which of them had already told Hale everything.
