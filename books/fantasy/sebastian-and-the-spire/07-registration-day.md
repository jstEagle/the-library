## 7. Registration Day

The summons came at dawn, nailed to the Gilderscale's door with a spike of black iron that hummed when Orsen touched it.

*By order of the Crown, all persons possessed of unnatural gifts shall present themselves for registration within seven days. Those who comply shall be catalogued and released to their lawful trades. Those who conceal themselves shall be taken.*

*Taken.* Not punished. Not tried. Taken, the way one takes a tooth, or a coin from a dead man's eye.

Orsen read it three times while the market woke around them, and the iron spike whispered against their palm—a thin, metallic voice like a knife being sharpened very slowly. It had been forged in a place where someone had died. Everything was. That was the joke the world played on bone-carvers: there was no such thing as an object without a history, and no history without somebody in it.

"Reading it over and over won't change the words," said Tam, the guild's door-warden, hauling the spike out with a grunt. "Though I'll grant it's a fine piece of ironwork. Shame about the message."

"The words are the point," Orsen said.

They went inside together, and Orsen felt the eyes of the market follow them—not hostile yet, not quite, but *counting*. The baker's boy looked away too quickly. Old Marta at the flower stall pulled her shawl closer as Orsen passed, though the morning was mild. Seven days ago these people had nodded to Orsen Whitlock, the quiet carver who kept the Gilderscale's ledgers and never caused trouble. Now they were doing arithmetic. *Who among us is worth reporting? What does the crown pay?*

The proclamation hadn't just announced a hunt. It had made hunters of everyone.

* * *

The Gilderscale's counting room smelled of tallow and old vellum, and by midmorning it held every officer of the guild, arranged around the long table like pieces on a board no one wanted to play.

Guildmaster Corvane Hale stood at the head, hands folded behind his back, reading the proclamation aloud even though everyone had read it already. He had a way of making bad news sound like weather—unpleasant, inevitable, best discussed calmly over tea.

"—and so," he concluded, "the crown requires a full accounting. Every item in our inventory that carries a *residue*—every piece the registry might classify as gifted-work—must be declared. And more delicately: any client, any supplier, any associate of this house who may fall under the decree." He set the paper down. "The Gilderscale has survived four kings by being useful and invisible. We will survive this one the same way."

"And if invisibility fails?" asked Mistress Ondrey, who handled the gem trade. Her rings clicked against the table as she drummed her fingers.

"Then we give them what they ask for and nothing more. Names we must give; we give them accurately. But hear me—" Hale's voice dropped into the register he used for oaths, warm and grave at once, "—this house does not sell its people. We surrender objects. Objects have no mothers."

There were nods. Someone murmured approval. Orsen sat at the far end with the master ledger open before them, quill poised, recording the meeting as they always did, and thought: *He means it. He believes what he's saying.*

That was the worst part. Hale wasn't lying. Not yet.

* * *

The list came to Orsen that afternoon, because everything came to Orsen eventually. Three names, compiled by the guild's scriveners from years of transaction records, flagged for review before submission to the crown's commissioners.

Orsen read the first name and stopped breathing.

**Maren Ostwyck — lacemaker. Commissioned: mourning veil, human-hair thread, 4 yards. Buyer unknown.**

Maren, who came every month with her baskets and her chatter, whose veils were woven so finely that the grief in them could be *felt* through gloves—which was, of course, exactly why the Gilderscale sold them at triple price to buyers who wanted to wear someone else's sorrow like perfume. Maren, who had once pressed a honey-cake on Orsen and said, *You're too thin, carver, grief is heavy work, you should eat.*

The second name: **Jorin Fell — locksmith. Commissioned: lock mechanism, keyed to bloodline.**

Jorin, who built locks that recognized the hands meant to open them and bit the hands that weren't. Jorin, who'd taught Orsen to pick his own locks as a joke, laughing until he wheezed. Gifted, absolutely. Dead to rights under the new decree.

The third name made Orsen's stomach turn over slowly, like something settling in deep water.

**Ivet Sarel — apothecary. Supplier: bone meal, rendered, provenance unrecorded.**

Ivet, who ground her powders from bones she never asked questions about, and whose remedies worked because the dead in them *wanted* to help. Ivet, who had treated Orsen's fever two winters ago and refused payment, saying only, *You'll owe me a kindness someday. I collect debts slow.*

Three names. Three people who had been kind to Orsen in the small, careless ways that kindness happens between people who don't know they're building a web.

Orsen looked up. The counting room was empty except for the ledger, the lamp, and the long shadows of the shelves where the guild's impossible inventory slept in their wrappings, murmuring to each other in voices only Orsen could hear.

*I can't,* they thought. *If I alter the ledger, I'm not hiding myself anymore. I'm spending the guild. If Marr comes—and Marr will come, the crown doesn't send polite letters twice—I'll have made us liars. He'll take the guild apart looking for the seam in the record, and he'll find me, and he'll find Hale, and—*

And then, underneath the panic, quieter, in a voice that sounded almost like Maren's:

*And if you don't, they'll come for her with the honey-cakes still warm in her basket.*

Orsen sat for a long time. The lamp burned down a quarter inch. Somewhere in the wrapped inventory, a music box that had belonged to a drowned girl began, very softly, to play—the dead sensing fear the way dogs smell storms.

"All right," Orsen said aloud, to no one, to the girl in the box, to themselves. "All right."

They opened the master ledger to the current year and began to work.

* * *

Forgery, it turned out, was a craft like any other, and Orsen had been trained in crafts since childhood. The trick was not to erase—it was to *recontextualize*. A gifted commission hidden outright left a hole, and holes screamed. A commission *relabeled* left a smooth surface.

Maren's mourning veil became **ceremonial textile, horsehair blend, theatrical use**—theatrical goods were taxed differently, tracked loosely, and no commissioner wanted to wade through costume inventories. Orsen adjusted the ink of the original entry with a solution of oxgall and lemon, waited for it to lift, rewrote, then aged the new line with a breath of smoke and a pass of sandpaper worn smooth from years of handling. The page looked a hundred years boring.

Jorin's bloodlock became **mechanism, novelty, spring-driven, court jester's prop**—absurd enough to be true, dull enough to be skipped. Orsen added a fake secondary entry in a different hand, a scrivener's hand, dated wrong by design: clerks made mistakes, and commissioners expected to find them. Give the hunter one small wrong thing and they'd stop digging for the large right one.

Ivet was hardest. Bone meal was bone meal; there was no relabeling it. So Orsen didn't touch Ivet's entries at all. Instead they went back eleven years, to a fire in the Tannery Quarter that had destroyed half the guild's early records, and inserted a single line into the loss-inventory: **rendered stock, unprovenanced, destroyed in Quarter fire, quantity unrecoverable.** Let the crown chase ash.

It took four hours. When it was done, Orsen sat back and found their hands steady and their heart going like a trapped bird, and both sensations at once, strangely, were *good*. This was the feeling they'd spent a lifetime avoiding, they realized—the vertigo of standing on an edge and choosing to lean. It was terrible. It was the most alive they had felt since childhood.

"You've smudged your sleeve."

Orsen didn't jump. They'd heard him coming ten minutes ago—the particular creak of Hale's left boot, the whisper of the ring of keys he carried out of habit though he trusted no lock but his own hands.

Hale stood in the doorway of the counting room, holding a candle, watching. His face was unreadable in the way of a man who has practiced unreadability for forty years.

"Guildmaster." Orsen rose. "I was just—"

"I can see what you were just doing." Hale came into the room slowly, set the candle on the table, and leaned down over the ledger. He read Maren's false line. Then Jorin's. Then he turned back eleven years, unhurried, and found the fire entry, and something flickered across his face—surprise, maybe, or its cousin, respect.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. In the wrapped shelves, the drowned girl's music box had gone silent, listening.

"Do you know," Hale said finally, straightening, "what the penalty is for falsifying guild records?"

"Expulsion," Orsen said. "For members. For officers, referral to the magistrates."

"For anyone, during a crown audit, it's whatever Inquisitor Marr decides it is." Hale picked up the candle again. He did not look angry. He did not look kind. He looked like a man weighing something on scales too fine to see. "Marr tested our ledgers last month, you know. Before the proclamation. Bone-dust readings against our entries. He found three discrepancies."

Orsen's mouth went dry. "And you—"

"I told him our scriveners were sloppy." Hale smiled faintly. "He didn't believe me. He believed the ledgers, which is better. Ledgers are honest things; men aren't. It's why I trust them and why I employ you." He moved toward the door, then paused, his back to Orsen. "The theatrical goods exemption was clever. The jester's prop was cleverer. The fire entry—" he shook his head slowly, "—that was the work of someone who understands that the best lie is buried under a truth nobody wants to excavate."

"Guildmaster, I can explain—"

"No need." Hale's voice was mild as milk. "I watched you do it. I've been standing in the doorway since the horsehair."

The floor tilted. Orsen gripped the table's edge. "Then you know I've—"

"I know that tomorrow morning, three names go to the commissioners, and none of them will be Maren Ostwyck, Jorin Fell, or Ivet Sarel." Hale turned his head just enough that Orsen caught his profile in the candlelight—the long nose, the hooded eye, the face that had sheltered Orsen for nine years and never once asked why the carver flinched at certain antiques. "I know that this house does not sell its people. I believe I said that today, in front of witnesses."

"Yes," Orsen said carefully. "You did."

"Mm." Hale opened the door. Cold air moved through the counting room, guttering the candle, stirring the wrapped inventory into a low, restless murmur. "One thing, Whitlock."

"Guildmaster?"

"When Marr returns—and he will return—he will put his hands on this ledger, and he will read it the way you read bones. If he finds what you've done, he won't punish you first. He'll punish everyone you've ever been kind to, to see which name makes you flinch." Hale stepped into the corridor, and his voice drifted back, calm and terrible: "So decide now, before he comes, whether you're the sort of person who can watch that happen without flinching. Because I've met that sort of person. They're rare. And they frighten me."

His boots faded down the corridor. The candle steadied.

Orsen stood alone in the counting room with the forged ledger and the hammering of their own heart, and understood, at last, what had just happened. Hale had seen everything and said nothing. Which meant either the guildmaster was protecting them—

—or he was simply waiting to see how much Orsen was worth, and to whom.

In the shelves, the drowned girl's music box began to play again, soft and sweet and hopeless, and Orsen blew out the candle and walked home through the dark streets with the taste of ink on their fingers and a single thought repeating like a pulse:

*I chose. Gods help me, I chose—and now everything I love is on the same side of the ledger as I am.*

Two streets from the guildhouse, Orsen passed a wall where fresh bills had gone up in the lamplight. Not proclamations this time. Portraits. Sketched faces above printed names, rewards beneath.

The first face was Maren's.

The second was a knight in broken armor, sword reversed in mourning grip, and beneath it, in the crown's neat print: **SERANA VOSS. DESERTER. LAST SEEN AT THE GILDERSCALE QUARTER GATES. INFORMATION SOUGHT.**

Orsen stared at the poster for a long moment. They had never seen the woman in their life.

But the paper itself—cheap pulp, handled by a dozen printers' boys—was *screaming*. Fear soaked through it, and under the fear, something else: a memory caught in the fibers, a flash of a courtyard, a woman kneeling in the rain with her sword laid at someone's feet, and a voice saying, cold and clear: *You should have let him die, Voss. Now you'll carry him forever.*

Orsen pressed their palm flat against the poster, heart pounding, and for the first time in nine careful years, answered the dead.

"Who is he?" they whispered. "Whose death are you carrying, knight?"

The paper had no more to give. But the question stayed, hooking into Orsen like a fisherman's barb, as somewhere across the dark city a bell began to toll the hour—and somewhere above it, barely visible through the cloud-scud, the royal spire stood black against the sky, humming its hunger-song to no one.

Yet.
