## 9. Sera's Bargain

Sera Voss had learned, in the years since the order came down and she refused it, that shame was a kind of armor. It sat heavier than plate, it never needed oiling, and no smith in the city could beat a dent out of it. She wore it into the Gilderscale district three days after selling nothing at the bone-carver's shop—because the sword was still hers, wrapped in oilcloth across her back, and because she had spent those three days drinking cheap wine in a dockside room and telling herself she would not go back.

She went back.

The shop looked different in late afternoon light. The shutters stood open to catch the last of the sun, and through them Sera could see Orsen Whitlock bent over the workbench, a small knife moving with the patience of someone who had made ten thousand small motions and expected to make ten thousand more. A lamp burned on the bench even though daylight still touched the street. Sera understood why. People who lived with ghosts kept lights burning against the hour when the light outside went thin and gray and the dead felt closer.

She had known people like that. One person. One brother.

The bell above the door had been muffled with a strip of leather—a kindness to the neighbors or to the dead, she couldn't say—but Orsen's head came up before the door finished swinging, the way it had the first time, as if the carver heard her coming from somewhere deeper than ears.

"You again," Orsen said. Not unkindly. Wary, though. Wary the way a deer is wary of another deer that smells wrong.

"Me again." Sera set two coppers on the counter, an old soldier's habit: pay for your entrance even where none is asked. "I didn't come about the sword."

"No," Orsen said. "You came about the sword, just not to sell it."

That stopped her for a half-second. "How do you know?"

"Because you haven't taken it off your back once since you walked in. People selling things put them down. They want their hands free to count money." Orsen set down the knife. The little bone button under the blade caught the lamplight—white, unfinished, ordinary. "Also, you keep touching the hilt like you're checking whether it's still there. That's not how people hold things they're giving away. That's how they hold things they can't decide to bury."

Sera laughed, one short bark with no humor in it, and pulled out the stool across the workbench without being invited. Her knees cracked when she sat. Thirty-one years old and she creaked like rigging. "They told me you were discreet."

"They told you right."

"They also told me—" she lowered her voice, though the shop was empty, though the street beyond the shutters held only a fishwife and a boy hauling ice, "—they told me you could hear what's in things. What's *left* in things."

Orsen's face did something complicated and closed, a door shutting inside a door. "People tell stories about this shop."

"I don't pay for stories." Sera unwound the strap across her chest and laid the sword on the counter between them, oilcloth whispering. "I pay for answers. If you can give them."

For a long moment Orsen only looked at the wrapped blade, and Sera watched the carver's hands—the hands of a craftsperson who knew exactly what every object in reach wanted—and saw them go very still. Stillness, in her experience, was what fear looked like on quiet people.

"I don't do readings," Orsen said quietly. "I carve buttons. I keep ledgers. I balance accounts. That's all."

"That's a lie," Sera said, "and we both know it's a lie, because when I brought that sword in here three days ago you went gray around the mouth and you asked me—not where I got it, not what I wanted for it—you asked me how many. You asked me *how many men*. Nobody asks that question unless they already know the answer is written somewhere they can read it."

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft. Outside, the fishwife called her prices in a voice worn smooth by repetition.

Orsen reached up and pulled the shutter closed. Then the second one. The shop went amber and close, the smell of bone dust and lamp oil and something older underneath, something like cold stone and dried flowers. A workshop became a confessional in the space of two breaths.

"If I do this," Orsen said, "you will tell me exactly why. Not the polite version. Not the version you'd give a magistrate. The true one. Because what's in that blade isn't quiet, Ser Voss—I could hear it from the moment you crossed my threshold the first time, and it has been getting louder every day you carry it around the city like a wound you're showing off. Things that loud are loud for a reason. Dead men don't shout for fun. They shout because something's unresolved."

Sera's throat worked. She had rehearsed this. In the dockside room, bottle in hand, she had rehearsed a clean version: cashiered knight, hard luck, wants closure, will pay well. Clean versions were what you gave people so they could refuse you without hating themselves.

What came out instead was the truth.

* * *

"My name was Ser Sera Voss of the Ninth Lance, Order of the Pale Standard," she said. "Past tense on all of it. My brother Tam was a clerk in the Office of Registries—crown service, third tier, the kind of post where you copy lists until your eyes go soft. He got me my first commission, actually. Pulled strings. Our father died owing money to everyone in the Salt Quarter, and Tam wrote letters, and a knight-captain who owed him a favor signed the papers, and that was that. Voss children in crown colors, both of us. My mother cried for a week. Happy crying. You forget there's such a thing."

Orsen said nothing. Just listened, hands folded on the bench, and Sera thought: *this is what they must be good at. Listening. God help them.*

"Two years ago, winter like this one, the Ninth Lance got posted to palace escort duty. Honor rotation. We stood at doors and froze and felt important. And then one night—an order came down. Verbal. No seal, no signature, delivered by a man whose name I never learned, wearing livery I *did*: the spire's own gray-and-gold. The order was simple. A prisoner transfer was coming through the lower gallery at the third bell. Twelve guards. Our job was to be elsewhere. Anywhere else. To see nothing, record nothing, and if anyone ever asked, to have been at supper."

"Where were the prisoner and the escort going?" Orsen asked.

"Nowhere. That's what I couldn't stop chewing on afterward. Transfers go *somewhere*. Cells, courts, the block. This one went from a room to a corridor to nothing. And the man delivering the order—he wasn't nervous. Nervous I've seen a thousand times. He was *rehearsed*. Like a priest saying liturgy. 'Be elsewhere. See nothing. Record nothing.' Over and over, same words, same cadence. You don't rehearse orders unless the orders have been given many times before."

"What did you do?"

"I asked for the order in writing." Sera smiled, and it hurt her face. "Gods. I actually said it. 'I'll need that countersigned, sir.' You'd think I'd spat on the altar. The room went cold in a way rooms don't go cold. And the man looked at me—not angry, that's the thing, not angry at all—looked at me the way you look at a horse that's learned to open gates. Interested. Making a note. And he said, 'Then the Ninth Lance stands elsewhere tonight regardless, and Ser Voss may stand wherever she likes,' and turned and left."

"And the others went."

"The others went. Eleven of them, good soldiers, my soldiers, some of them boys I'd trained myself—they took the hint the way soldiers take hints, which is fast and without questions, because questions end careers and sometimes end more than careers. I stood my post. Alone. Third bell came. I heard boots in the lower gallery below me—eight sets, maybe nine, and between them a sound I still hear when I can't sleep. Not footsteps. A *dragging*. Something heavy being moved that didn't want to be moved. And then—"

She stopped. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight now, unnaturally straight, like a soldier at attention.

"—and then silence, and then the boots came back up, and there was no dragging anymore, and in the morning I reported it. Wrote it up proper. Chain of command, sealed dispatch, the whole ritual. I was proud of myself. I thought I'd done the one brave small thing available to me."

"What happened?"

"Three weeks later I was cashiered. Conduct unbecoming. Dereliction. False report—they produced two witnesses from my own lance who swore the gallery had been empty all night and I'd invented the whole affair. My commission was struck. My name came off the rolls. They let me keep my sword because taking it would have meant acknowledging I'd ever had it, and the whole point of the proceeding was that nothing had happened. Tam fought it. Wrote letters for months, pulled every string he had left, spent our mother's funeral money on advocates. Last letter I got from him was eight months ago. After that, nothing. His lodging house says he moved out. The Office of Registries says he transferred. Nobody transfers *where*, nobody says *when*, and his landlady gave me a box of his things she'd been paid to hold."

Sera's hands found the oilcloth and unwrapped it before she could make herself stop. Lamplight ran along the blade, along the fuller, along the nicks near the guard where steel had met steel and bone both.

"This was his. Our grandfather's, then his—he carried it as a cadet, ceremonial, but it was *his*, and when he disappeared I went to his lodgings and found the box and the sword was in it, wrapped, oiled, ready. Ready like someone planned to come back for it. And I've carried it for four months, and I'm not a sensitive, I'm not gifted, I'm nothing but a disgraced woman with bad dreams, but I swear to you on whatever's left of my honor that some nights the blade is warm. Some nights I wake up and the hilt is warm as a hand, and there's a word sitting on the edge of my hearing that I almost catch, and it sounds like my name."

The silence after that had weight. Orsen hadn't moved. But something in the room had changed—or rather, Sera realized, something in the room had stopped pretending. The air felt crowded. Attentive. Every object on the shelves, every carved blank and finished button, every tool hung in its place, seemed suddenly to be leaning in.

"He's in there," Sera said. Her voice came out rough. "Isn't he. Whatever you people hear—it's him. It's been him the whole time, hasn't it? Four months of carrying him around like a relic and I couldn't even—"

"Don't," Orsen said sharply. "Don't apologize to me. And don't talk to it yet. Please." The carver's voice cracked on the last word, and one hand rose halfway toward the sword and stopped, hovering, trembling slightly, like a hand over a flame. "If he's lodged in the steel the way I think he is, he's been waiting a long time, and the first minutes matter. Once I open that door I can't fully close it again. So before I touch it I need to know what you want from this. Truly. Because there are two kinds of questions people bring to the dead, and I'll answer either, but I won't answer blind."

Sera stared at her brother's sword. At the nicks on the guard. At the dark stain in the grip-leather that she'd always told herself was oil.

"I need to know if I betrayed him," she said.

It came out flat and terrible, the way truths do when they've been carried too long.

"Everyone thinks betrayal is a grand thing. Treason, knives in backs. It's not. Betrayal is arithmetic. It's counting what you'll lose against what's right and picking the smaller loss. I counted, that night in the palace. Eleven soldiers against one stranger being dragged to nowhere. My career against my oath. And I picked—I told myself I picked the oath, I stood my post, I *reported* it, didn't I, didn't I do the right thing—except reporting it cost me nothing but my name, and my name was already bought and paid for by a brother who pulled strings to get it. And while I was standing my noble post, doing my one brave small thing, whatever they dragged down that gallery happened *anyway*. It happened because eleven men went to supper, and because one man with a rehearsed voice counted on the fact that the ones who wouldn't go would be too few and too honest to matter. And Tam spent everything he had fighting it and then Tam vanished, and I have spent four months wondering—" her breath shuddered, "—wondering if they took him *because of me*. Because I filed that report. Because my name was on it, and his name was mine, and somebody in the spire read 'Voss' twice and decided to tidy the ledger."

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat on the counter to still them.

"So that's the question. Not whether he's at peace, not whether he forgives me—that's the coward's version, asking the dead to do the comforting. I need to know if I did it. If my one brave small thing is what killed my brother. Can you tell me that?"

Orsen Whitlock looked at her for a long time. And Sera, who had spent her life reading faces across parade grounds and courtrooms and the frozen gallery of a palace at night, saw something she had not expected: recognition. The carver recognized the shape of the question. Carried it too, maybe. Had a version of it locked in some drawer of their own.

"Yes," Orsen said softly. "If he's there, and if he'll speak, I can ask him. Whether he'll answer—whether he *can*—that's between him and the steel. But I'll ask. On one condition."

"Name it."

"When this is done—if he tells you what happened to him, and it's bad, and it points anywhere near the palace—you don't go marching to the Gray Gate demanding audience. You come back here. You sit on that stool. Because whatever killed your brother has been killing people with rehearsed voices and tidy ledgers for longer than you've been alive, and it eats brave women who charge at it alone for breakfast." A pause. "I know what I'm asking. Soldiers hate being managed. But I'd rather manage you than bury you."

Something loosened in Sera's chest that she hadn't known was clenched. It was absurd—being given orders by a bone-carver half her size behind a button-shop counter—but beneath the absurdity was the plain animal fact that this was the first person in four months who had believed her without needing convincing.

"Agreed," she said. "Two debts, then. Your condition for my answer. Protection for a reading. Fair trade across the table."

"It's not protection I'll need from you," Orsen murmured, reaching at last for the sword, fingers stopping a hair's breadth from the grip. "Though ask me again in a week. Things are getting strange in this city, Ser Voss. The heralds are reading proclamations about the gifted, and my clients have started flinching at shadows, and yesterday a portrait frame I was restoring sang to me in a voice that had no business knowing songs." The carver shook their head, dismissing it—or trying to. "But yes. Two debts. Now hold still, and don't speak, whatever you hear. And if I tell you to take it back—to take the sword and walk out and never come back—you do it without arguing. Swear it."

"I swear."

Orsen's hand closed around the grip.

The lamp went out. Not guttered—went out, snuffed like a pinched wick, and the amber dark of the shop filled at once with a sound Sera had no name for: not a voice, not yet, but a pressure, a crowd-noise, a distant roar like the sea heard through a wall. Frost feathered across the counter under her palms. Somewhere in the black, Orsen breathed in sharp and held it, and held it, and held it—

—and then, from everywhere and nowhere, thin and clear and unmistakable, a young man's voice said:

"*Sera? Sera, is that you? Oh gods—oh gods, you have to listen, there isn't much of me left—*"

"Sera," Orsen whispered in the dark, and the carver's voice was shaking. "Whatever he's about to say—brace yourself. He keeps repeating one word, over and over, and it's *registry*. He's saying *the registry, Sera, they took me into the registry*—"

And beneath it, faint but rising, from the direction of the market square, came the far-off bray of heralds' horns.
