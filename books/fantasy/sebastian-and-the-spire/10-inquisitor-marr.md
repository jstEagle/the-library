## 10. Inquisitor Marr

The summons came at dawn, folded into the beak of a brass crow that no courier carried and no hand had delivered. It sat on the guild's doorstep when I opened the shutters, wings crossed like a sleeping bird, its little metal skull warm as a living thing. I did not touch it with bare skin. I had learned that much about the crown in seven years of careful living: never touch what it sends you.

*By order of His Majesty's Inquisition,* the paper said, in ink so black it seemed to drink the lamplight, *the Gilderscale will receive Inquisitor Marr at the ninth hour. All ledgers will be made available. All persons present.*

*All ledgers.* I read those three words four times, and each time they weighed more.

I burned the summons in the workshop stove before anyone else could see it, then stood watching the paper curl, listening to the ash settle among the coals. The dead in my workshop were quiet that morning. That was the worst part. The bone buttons on their card, the whale-ivory comb, the little carved saint missing one hand—all of them silent, all of them holding their breath, if the dead can be said to hold anything.

They knew who was coming too.

* * *

Corvane Hale spent the morning making the Gilderscale beautiful, which was how I knew he was afraid. He had the apprentices polish the display cases until the glass sang. He moved the rarer pieces—the singing shell from the Salt Coast, the mirror that showed rooms instead of faces—down into the bone-cellars, and he did it himself, without asking me to carry anything, which he had never done in nine years. Guildmasters do not carry. Guildmasters gesture, and things are carried for them.

"You'll keep the ledger open," he told me, straightening his collar in the dark glass of a case. "Page by page as she asks. No flourishes, no commentary. You are a pen, Orsen. Pens don't think."

"And if she asks something a pen couldn't know?"

Hale smiled at me in the glass. It was his good smile, the one with warmth in it, the one that had made me trust him when I was nineteen and starving and hiding what I was behind a counter in a fish market. "Then you'll be a very clever pen." He turned. "She'll test you. That's what she does. She tests everyone. Give her nothing to find."

"Nothing to find," I repeated. It sounded like a prayer. It sounded like an epitaph.

At half past eight, the bells of the Registry began to ring—not the hour, but the pattern reserved for royal presence, six strokes and a pause, six strokes and a pause—and every apprentice in the building found somewhere else to be. Hale stood in the great room with his hands folded. I stood at the ledger desk with my pen uncapped and my heart going like a trapped bird against my ribs.

Inquisitor Marr arrived alone.

That was the first terrible thing. I had expected soldiers, a cordon, the machinery of fear made visible. Instead there was only a woman in a plain grey coat stepping through our door as though she owned the hinges, shaking rain—there had been no rain—from her shoulders. She was perhaps fifty, narrow as a blade in its sheath, with grey hair pinned back so severely it looked painted on. Her face was unremarkable. That was the second terrible thing. Faces like hers were everywhere; you passed them in markets without a second glance. She could have been a clerk. A widow. Anyone.

Around her left wrist, half-hidden by her cuff, she wore a bracelet of small white beads.

Bone beads. Dozens of them. And even across the width of the great room, even through the wool of her sleeve, I could hear them.

Not voices, exactly. Not yet. More like the memory of voices—a murmur, a crowd heard through a wall, dozens upon dozens of the dead pressed together into little polished spheres and worn like jewelry. My gift had never reached across a room before. It reached now, or the beads reached for it, or something in the arithmetic of that morning simply broke. I gripped the edge of the desk and kept my eyes on the ledger and thought: *a pen. You are a pen.*

"Guildmaster Hale." Her voice was dry and pleasant, like pages turning. "Thank you for the accommodation."

"The crown's business is always welcome at the Gilderscale." Hale bowed exactly the right depth. I had watched him bow to magistrates and smugglers and once, memorably, to a man selling a jar containing somebody's last breath, and it was always the same bow: respectful, warm, and giving away precisely nothing.

"I imagine it is." Marr removed her gloves finger by finger. "I'll want the trade ledgers first. Seven years back, please. Then the acquisition records. Then—" she glanced around the great room with mild, terrible curiosity, "—whatever else you keep."

"We keep only what the charter requires."

"Mm," said Marr, in the tone of a woman filing something away.

* * *

For two hours I was a pen.

She read the way I had never seen anyone read—backwards. She started with the current season and worked into the past, entry by entry, and after every tenth entry or so she would stop, produce a small leather roll from her coat, and open it over the page. Inside the roll lay a fine sieve of silver mesh and a vial of powder the color of old milk.

Bone dust. Everyone in the trade knew of it; almost no one had seen it used. Ground from the bones of the gifted dead—executed, rendered, processed—it held a residue of their gift the way a bell holds the ringing. Passed over writing touched by a gifted hand, the dust would... I didn't know what it would do. Nobody outside the Inquisition knew. That was rather the point.

She sifted it over entries I had written myself. Over the sale of a haunted music box to a merchant in Lowbridge. Over the acquisition of a ring that whispered. Over the button I had carved in chapter one of my own quiet life, logged honestly because honesty about small things was the best camouflage I knew.

The dust did nothing. It lay on the page like flour, and she tapped it away, and my heart climbed down off the ceiling by a single rung.

Then she came to March. To the entries I had forged.

I had done it well—I want to say that plainly, because whatever else happened that day, I did it well. Three clients whose gifts I had known about for years: the midwife in Tanner's Row who could soothe any pain with a touch, the ferryman who found drowned things, the old bookbinder whose hands remembered every book they'd ever held. When the proclamation came, I had altered their records, backdated sales, invented buyers in cities far enough away to be safe. I had matched my own ink aging by leaving the pages open near the stove. I had practiced Hale's countersignature until I dreamed in it.

Marr sifted dust over the midwife's entry. The powder lay flat.

Over the ferryman's. Flat.

Over the bookbinder's—

She paused. She did not tap this batch away. She bent close, close enough that her breath stirred the powder, and I watched one grain of it, one single grain near the margin, tremble. Just tremble. As if something under the page had knocked politely.

My whole body went cold and then hot and then cold again, and somewhere beneath my ribs the dead in my workshop began, very faintly, to sing. They do that when I am frightened—they had done it since I was a child, a low harmonic hum like wet glass rubbed at the rim—and I clamped down on it, on everything, and stared at the ledger as though numbers were the only language I had ever learned.

"Interesting," said Marr.

"Is it?" Hale asked pleasantly, from across the room, where he had been doing nothing with tremendous skill for two hours.

"The humidity in your archive." She straightened. "It varies. These older pages have absorbed damp unevenly. It makes the dust behave unpredictably." She tapped the grain away. It fell still. "You should invest in better shelving."

"I shall have Orsen attend to it at once."

Her eyes came to me for the first time.

I had prepared for this. In the sleepless nights since the proclamation, I had prepared for it the way a drowning man prepares for water. I gave her the face I gave difficult clients: attentive, slightly tired, entirely empty. The face of a man whose greatest ambition was a new set of shelves.

"You keep the ledgers," she said.

"Nine years, madam."

"Your hand is very consistent." She turned a page backward, then another, comparing. "Most clerks drift. Slants change. Pressure changes. Yours is the same in year one as it is today." She looked up. "Either you are extraordinarily disciplined, or you practice."

"My father was a forger of fair copies," I said, and the lie came out smooth as river stone, because it was nearly true—my father had been a scrivener, and I had loved him, and both facts were now dead weight I was spending to buy my life. "He believed a hand should be legible enough to outlive you."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not suspicion—suspicion I could have named and fought. This was older and worse. Recognition, maybe. Or appetite.

"A man after my own craft," she said softly. "We are both in the business of making things last past their owners, Master Whitlock."

"Yes, madam."

"Do you hear them?"

The question dropped into the room like a coin into a well, and I listened to it fall and fall and never hit bottom. Across the room, Hale went very still by the window. Stillness has a sound, if you've lived with it long enough, and his stillness said: *careful, careful, careful.*

"Hear them?" I let my brow crease. "The ledgers, madam?"

"The dead." She said it the way another woman might say *the weather*. "In the things they leave behind. The trade calls it resonance. Bone-dust readings detect it in objects. Some claim it exists in the living—that certain persons, touching a thing once owned, receive..." she chose the word with visible care, "...correspondence."

I laughed.

It was the riskiest thing I did all day, riskier than any forgery, and I did not decide to do it; some deeper animal in me simply took the reins, because I understood in that instant that a flinch would kill me and a stammer might, but laughter—laughter was the one response no guilty man rehearses. I laughed the way a clerk laughs at a merchant's joke, brief and deferential and just a shade too loud.

"Beg pardon, madam. It's only—nine years of inventory, and the strangest item I ever logged was a jar of breath." I shook my head. "If my quill starts corresponding, I'll be sure to write back."

Silence. The length of it was measurable in heartbeats. Four. Five. Six. In the beads at her wrist, the murmuring dead seemed to lean closer, dozens of them, listening through their little round prisons, and I thought with a clarity that felt like falling: *she wears them so they can listen for you. She doesn't need proof. She carries witnesses.*

"Inquisitors are often accused of humorlessness," Marr said at last, and she smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "You'd be surprised how much of this work is knowing when a laugh is real." She closed the ledger. "Your books are in order, Guildmaster. Tidier than most. Almost suspiciously tidy—but then, I'm told the Gilderscale prides itself on discretion."

"Discretion is our stock in trade," Hale said smoothly.

"Mm." She drew her gloves back on, finger by finger, unhurried. At the door she stopped, half-turned, and regarded me with the mild expression of a woman remembering an errand. "Master Whitlock. One more thing. A formality."

"Madam?"

"When the registry opened, every soul in this quarter signed the attendance rolls. Every soul but three, whose marks appear in the parish book as deceased—one a midwife of Tanner's Row, one a ferryman, one a bookbinder." She adjusted her cuff over the beads. "Curious profession, yours. So many of your clients die just when they're needed most alive."

"They were elderly, madam. The fever took two of them in autumn. The third—" I shrugged, helpless, honest, hollow. "People die. I only write it down."

"Yes," said Marr. "You do. You write everything down. That's what I like about you." She stepped over the threshold into the grey morning, and then, without turning around, she added:

"We'll see each other again, Master Whitlock. I have a feeling about your hand."

* * *

The door closed. The bells rang the half-hour, ordinary and indifferent. Somewhere upstairs an apprentice resumed sweeping, and the city went on being a city, and none of it meant anything, because I was standing at the ledger desk with my pen still uncapped and a bead of sweat tracking down between my shoulder blades, and I could not remember how to move.

Hale crossed the room. For a moment I thought he would put a hand on my shoulder—he had done it twice in nine years, both times when I truly needed it—but he stopped short, and instead he picked up the ledger and riffled the pages, checking, the way a man checks his purse in a bad neighborhood.

"She suspected the bookbinder's entry," he said quietly. "The grain moved. I saw it."

"The humidity," I said. "She said herself—"

"She said what served her." He closed the book. His knuckles were white around the cover, and I realized, with a strange floating sensation, that Corvane Hale was afraid. Not for me. For the ledger. For the guild. Perhaps for himself. "Orsen. Listen to me. She will come back. Women like Marr always come back, and next time she won't bring courtesy, she'll bring a warrant and a wagon. Whatever you've hidden in these pages—" his eyes flicked up, sharp as a nail, "—and we both know you've hidden things—you have days, not weeks, to make them disappear properly."

"Properly," I repeated.

"Unwritten. Unremembered. Gone." He set the ledger down and, this time, did rest his hand on my shoulder, briefly, heavily. "You lied to an inquisitor today and lived. Do you understand what that cost? Not the danger. The *lie*. Men who lie well once learn to believe they always can." He squeezed, once, and let go. "Don't become what she thinks you are."

He walked out. I stood alone in the great room with the polished cases throwing pale light across the floor, and slowly, joint by joint, I came back into my body. My hands were steady. That surprised me. Inside, nothing was steady; inside, I was still standing in that sixth heartbeat of silence, deciding whether to live.

I locked the great room, went down to the workshop, and sat among my quiet dead. The buttons. The comb. The one-handed saint. They hummed their low frightened chord, and for the first time in my life I hummed back—an answer, a small treason against my own oldest rule—and the humming steadied us both.

Three clients erased. Days, not weeks. And beneath it all, turning over and over like a coin that wouldn't land: *we'll see each other again. I have a feeling about your hand.*

I pulled a fresh sheet toward me, dipped my pen, and began to unwrite three lives.

And that was when I heard it—faint, from the direction of the street, threading under the door like smoke: not Marr's voice, not any living voice, but the beads at her wrist, the packed and polished dead, all of them whispering the same two words over and over as she walked away through the city, words I would spend every night afterward trying not to hear:

*Found you. Found you. Found you.*
