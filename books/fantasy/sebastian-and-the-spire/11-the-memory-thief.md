## 11. The Memory-Thief

The thing about stealing memories was that you had to be very quiet about wanting them.

Wanting left fingerprints. Wanting made your hands sweat, and sweat made the objects cling to you like cats to a warm coat, and then every trinket in the room started whispering its little history at you whether you'd asked or not. Wren Quill had learned this at nine years old, crouched behind a pawnbroker's counter with their palms pressed flat against a silver locket, so hungry for the woman inside it that she'd poured herself out unasked—sixty years of Sunday dinners and a dead husband's snoring and the particular ache of a daughter who never wrote—and Wren had carried all of it home in their skull like water in cupped hands, spilling a little with every step.

They'd spilled other things since. That was the trade. You took a memory from an object, you gave one up to make room. The ledger always balanced. Wren had stopped keeping track of what they'd paid around the time they realized they couldn't remember their mother's face—only the fact of having forgotten it, which was somehow worse, a hole shaped exactly like grief with nothing inside it.

So they were very careful, now, to want nothing.

Which was why the bone-carver was such a problem.

* * *

Wren had heard of Orsen Whitlock the way you hear about weather in another country—a rumor, a shape on the horizon. *The Gilderscale keeps a carver who hears the dead.* Every thief in the Undercity had heard it, and every thief dismissed it, because a person who could actually do that would either be dead or rich, and this Whitlock was neither. Just a name attached to neat ledger entries and immaculate provenance papers. Half the guild's impossible inventory passed through those hands and came out certified clean.

Then, three weeks ago, Wren had lifted a candlestick off a Gilderscale courier—not for the silver, which was plated, but because the candlestick had been *screaming*. Wren felt loud objects from across a room; it was how they sorted the valuable cursed goods from the merely decorative. This one had been shrieking like a kettle, and when Wren finally got their gloves on it in the safety of their own rooms, expecting the usual flood—

Silence.

Not empty silence. *Taken* silence. The memories had been read out of it so thoroughly, so gently, that what remained was a smoothness Wren had only ever felt in one place before: their own mirror, after the bad year, after they'd scraped themselves hollow trying to forget the fire.

Someone had drunk this object dry without leaving a drop spilled. Someone with control Wren hadn't known was possible.

Wren had sat on the floor holding the mute candlestick for a long time, feeling something they hadn't felt since childhood, and it took them until dawn to name it.

Envy.

* * *

Finding Orsen Whitlock was easy. Finding them *alone* was the work.

Wren watched the Gilderscale for six days. They watched the bone-carver's routine the way they'd case any mark: out before the market bells, back after dark, a life measured out in ledgers and lamp-oil. They watched Guildmaster Hale too, because you always watched the people who owned the building, and there was something in Hale's warmth that set Wren's teeth on edge—the particular generosity of a man counting his money while he shook your hand. On the fifth day Wren saw Hale pause outside the workshop door, look in at Orsen bent over their carving bench, and smile in a way that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

On the sixth day, Inquisitor Marr came to the Gilderscale, and Wren decided the window was closing.

They caught Orsen at the lamplighter's hour, in the crooked lane behind the chandler's where the carver bought tallow by the block. Not an ambush—Wren didn't do ambushes, ambushes were for amateurs and inquisitors—but a materialization, stepping out of a doorway with their hood back and their hands visible and their most disarming expression in place.

"Bone-carver," Wren said. "You dropped something."

Orsen Whitlock turned. Up close they were younger than their reputation and tireder than their face let on, ink-stained fingers, eyes that did a quick professional sweep of Wren's hands—gloved, always gloved—and then their face, and then dismissed them as a beggar. Good instincts. Wrong conclusion.

"I haven't dropped anything."

"You will." Wren held up the candlestick. "This. About three weeks ago. Courier with a limp, gray cloak, took the wrong turning on Sallow Street." They tossed it underhand, an easy catch, and watched Orsen's hands close around it and watched the exact moment the silence of it registered—that flicker, that wince, the way you flinch touching a scar you'd forgotten you gave someone. "Interesting," Wren said softly. "Most people feel nothing when they hold it. You felt what's missing."

"I don't know what you're talking about." Flat. Too flat. A lie with good posture.

"Sure you don't." Wren rocked back on their heels. "Here's what I know anyway. Somebody emptied this piece. Clean work. Surgical. I've been taking memories out of things since I was nine years old and I have never once seen a removal this tidy. Whoever did it didn't just steal the dead woman's evenings and her husband's snoring. They took her *carefully*. Like you'd carry a full cup across a crowded room." They tilted their head. "That's not a skill you learn for fun, bone-carver. That's a skill you learn because the noise is killing you."

Something moved behind Orsen's eyes—fear, yes, but underneath the fear, worse, a terrible recognition. The look of a drowning man hearing someone describe the water.

"The market cross," Wren went on, gentler now, because cruelty was a tool and this wasn't the job for it. "The proclamation. Register or be taken. And here you are, walking home through dark lanes with your hood up, buying tallow from a chandler who doesn't know your name. You're already hiding. I'm just the first one who found you."

"If you're an inquisitor—"

"Do I look like an inquisitor?" Wren spread their arms. Thin, wiry, dressed in clothes chosen specifically to be forgotten two seconds after being seen. "Inquisitors wear authority. I wear anonymity. It's better armor." They stepped closer, and lowered their voice to the register they used for secrets. "I'm going to tell you what I am, and you're going to decide whether to run. I'm Wren Quill. I take memories out of objects. Rings, locks of hair, murder weapons, wedding gifts—I've pulled forty years out of a teacup. And unlike you, I can make it *stop*, bone-carver. Whatever's screaming in your workshop at night. Whatever the dead keep saying to you when you touch their leavings. I can teach you how to make them quiet."

The lane was silent except for a cat knocking over a bottle two roofs away.

"You can't," Orsen said at last, and their voice cracked on it, and Wren understood suddenly that this proud, careful, invisible person had wanted exactly this for years and had never once let themselves want it out loud. "It doesn't work like that. They're not in the objects. They're—" A breath. "They're everywhere I go. I am the object."

Oh, thought Wren. Oh, you poor magnificent disaster.

"No," Wren said. "You're not. But we're going to need tea and a locked door to explain why you're wrong, and you're going to say no twice more before you say yes, so let's skip ahead. Ask me the price. Everyone asks eventually."

* * *

The locked door turned out to be a rented room above a dye-shop, walls stained purple, air thick with mordant and old wool. Wren lit three candles, put the kettle on a spirit-lamp, and laid out their tools on a cloth like a surgeon: cotton gloves, a jeweler's loupe, a sliver of riverstone worn smooth, a small brass box with no keyhole.

Orsen sat across the table with their own hands wrapped around a mug they weren't drinking, watching everything, cataloguing everything. Wren could practically see the ledger forming behind their eyes. *Item: gloves. Item: stone. Item: thief.*

"The voices live in residue," Wren said. "Everything a person touches hardens around them, eventually, like amber. Most of it wears thin and blows away. But some moments press so hard—death, mostly, and love, which is the same pressure applied differently—they cure permanent. You hear them because you touch the amber and the moment wakes up. Yes?"

"That's... a crude version."

"All versions are crude. Here's mine." Wren drew off one glove, flexed bare fingers in the candlelight. "I don't wake the moment. I *lift* it. Peel it out of the amber whole. The object goes quiet forever—it's not suppressed, it's emptied—and the memory comes away intact, portable, storable." They tapped the brass box. "I've got four hundred and eleven of these in there. A miller's last sunrise. A soldier's mother singing. A child's entire sixth year. I can hand them to you like coins and you could hold a stranger's joy in your palm."

"Why would anyone want that?"

"Because," Wren said, and here was the part where the fascination lived, the part they showed almost no one, "memories are the only currency that appreciates. Gold rots in value the moment a new vein opens. A true memory only gets rarer. There are collectors in this city who would trade a house for ten minutes of a dead queen's coronation, felt from the inside. And there are poorer uses too—blackmail, testimony, mercy. I sold a widow her husband's last words once. Charged her nothing. Some jobs you do for the ledger, some you do against it."

Orsen was staring at the brass box with an expression Wren recognized intimately, because it was their own expression, twenty years old, in a pawnshop, hungry. "And the cost. You said everyone asks the price."

"They do." Wren folded their bare hand closed. "Here it is. To lift a memory out of an object, I need somewhere to put my end of the exchange. The ledger balances. Always. So when I pull a dead woman's evening out of a candlestick, I pay with a memory of my own—pushed into the object, curing into the amber, gone from me. That's the craft. That's the whole craft. Everything I've ever taken, I've paid for out of my own skull." They smiled, and knew it didn't reach. "I don't remember my mother's face. I don't remember why I ran from the fire on Tanner's Row, only that I ran. I traded my ninth birthday for a hanged man's confession and I'm sure it was worth it, whatever it was."

The kettle began to sing. Neither of them moved toward it.

"So when you ask me to teach you silence," Wren said, "understand what you're asking. I can't give you a technique. There's no breathing exercise, no charm, no discipline. What I can do is *practice on you*. Take the loudest hauntings out of your workshop one by one, peel them clean, and show you, in your own hands, what emptiness feels like—so you learn to recognize the texture of a taken thing, and then, maybe, in a year, in five, you learn to do it yourself. Your gift won't die. But it can get *quieter*. Manageable. Livable." They paused. "And each one I take from your things, I pay for. Out of you."

Orsen went very still. "Out of *me*."

"You'd be the object, bone-carver. For the duration of the working, you lend yourself to it. I take a haunting out of your workshop, and the balance comes due against your own memory. One per lifting. Chosen blind."

"Chosen—"

"Blind." Wren kept their voice level, kind, immovable. "I don't pick. If I picked, I'd flinch, and if I flinch mid-lifting, the memory tears, and torn memories rot, and rotted ones come back *wrong*—you don't want to meet a wrong memory, trust me. So the rule is: I reach into you, you don't tell me what I'm reaching for, and I take the first thing my hand closes on. Could be a morning. Could be a face. Could be nothing you'll miss." They met Orsen's eyes. "Could be something you'll spend the rest of your life missing without knowing what it was. That's the honest shape of it. That's the whole price, written down plain."

For a long moment the only sound was the kettle, and the dye-shop's vats gurgling below like slow digestion.

"You've done this to yourself," Orsen said quietly. "All those holes in you. You chose them."

"Chose is a strong word. Necessitated." Wren poured the tea at last, because their hands needed a job. "Here's what nobody tells you about self-erasure, bone-carver. It doesn't hurt when it happens. That's the horror of it. It feels like setting down a heavy bag you forgot you were carrying. Lightness. Relief. You only find out what you lost later, when you reach for it and find smooth wall. I've built a whole self out of smooth wall." They slid the brass box an inch toward the table's center, between them, gleaming. "So understand that when I offer you this, I'm not offering you a bargain. I'm offering you the beginning of becoming me. Fascinating work. Terrible pension."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you go home to your workshop and tonight the dead scream at you again, and next week Marr's bone-dust readers come back to the Gilderscale, and you lie again, and someday you'll lie one degree less perfectly, and they'll take you somewhere with wards on the walls." Wren sipped their tea. "I'm not threatening you. I'm reading the same ledger you are. We're both arithmetic right now."

Orsen looked down at their ink-stained hands. Wren watched them do it—watched the exact moment the calculation finished, the moment discipline lost to exhaustion, the moment a person who had spent their whole life refusing the dead finally considered paying anything, *anything*, for a night of quiet.

"One," Orsen said. "One lifting. One payment. And I choose which haunting you take from the workshop."

"The haunting, yes. The payment stays blind. Those are the terms."

"And you swear—you swear on whatever you have left to swear on—that you'll tell me afterward what you took. Even if I can't remember it. I want to know the shape of my own hole."

Wren opened their mouth to say *of course*, because that was the merciful answer, the answer that closed the deal.

What came out instead, because Wren Quill had survived twenty years in this trade by never lying about the mechanics: "No."

Orsen's head came up sharply.

"No," Wren repeated, and hated themselves even as the words kept coming, steady, professional, damning. "Because telling you poisons the lifting. The moment you know what's missing, your mind worries the hole like a tongue worrying a broken tooth, and worried holes *grow*. I've seen it. A client of mine learned she'd given up something about her sister, just the category, nothing more, and within a month she'd mourned the sister entirely—grieved her out of existence, filled the hole with invented loss until the real shape was unrecoverable. Knowledge is corrosive to absence." They set down the cup. "You'll pay blind and stay blind. That's the price. Final."

The silence stretched. Below them, the dye vats muttered. Somewhere across the city, a bell marked the hour, and Wren thought: *he's going to walk out, and I'll have talked myself out of the most interesting skull I've ever seen, because I was honest, honesty, the great occupational disease—*

"Show me," Orsen said.

Wren blinked. "Show you what?"

"How it works. Tonight. Now. On something small." Orsen reached into their coat and set something on the cloth between the candles: a bone button, carved with a running hare, worn nearly smooth. "This belonged to a man named Tam. He complains about the cold. Constantly. Twenty years I've heard him complain about the cold." Their voice was steady but their hands, Wren noticed, were not. "Take him out. Show me the silence. And pay for it however the ledger demands."

Wren stared at the button, and at the carver, and felt the old hunger rise in them like floodwater—because this was the thing, this was the *whole* thing, this was why they'd spent six days in cold lanes watching a stranger buy tallow. Not the silence. Not even the craft. It was this: someone willing. Someone who understood the weight and chose to set it down anyway. Wren had been alone in this trade since a pawnshop counter and a silver locket, and here across the table was the first person alive who might actually comprehend what the ledger balanced *against*.

"Tam," Wren murmured, and picked up the button bare-handed.

Cold came off it immediately, the dead man's complaint rising like breath in winter—*cold, cold, whose fool idea, cold*—and beneath it, fainter, the warm ghost of the hands that had carved him. Wren glanced up. "Last chance to choose blindness differently."

"Just do it."

Wren closed their eyes. Reached—not with the hand, with the *other* thing, the thing they'd never had a word for—and found the button's amber, and found, beside it, the vast warm architecture of Orsen Whitlock's remembered life, corridors and rooms and doors, and extended their hand into it, blind, as promised, and felt their fingers close around something.

It was large. Larger than a morning. It had weight like a body.

Wren hesitated—one heartbeat, two—and Orsen said, "Don't flinch," in a voice like a closing ledger, and Wren did not flinch.

They pulled.

The button went silent in their palm, Tam's complaint gone as if it had never been, and something else crossed the space between them, unseen, unhoused, and settled into the bone like a seed into furrow. Wren opened their eyes. Orsen sat exactly where they'd been sitting, cup halfway raised, face unchanged.

"Well?" Orsen asked. "Did it work? Is he quiet?"

"He's quiet." Wren set the button down. Their own hands, they noticed distantly, were shaking, and they could not have said why—only that somewhere behind their ribs a door had swung open onto a room they were suddenly sure, absolutely sure, they had never entered before, though the dust on the floor said otherwise.

"Then it's done," Orsen said slowly, looking at their own hands, flexing them, searching them for the loss. "I can't even tell what's gone. Is it always like that?"

"It's always like that," Wren said. "That's the lightness I told you about. Enjoy it while it lasts."

But they were already thinking about the weight of what they'd taken. Already turning it over in the dark of themselves like a coin whose face wouldn't resolve. Because it *was* heavy, heavier than a birthday, heavier than a face, and it hadn't felt like a memory at all, really.

It had felt like a person.

* * *

Orsen left near midnight, hood up, steps quick in the wet lane. Wren stood at the window and watched them go, and told themselves the shaking was just the working's aftermath, just the toll collecting itself, just the ordinary cost of the craft.

Then they went to the brass box, and opened it, and counted.

Four hundred and twelve.

The newest one sat at the top of the tray, and it was warm, and when Wren touched it—carefully, the way you touch a sleeping animal—it didn't show them a scene. Memories showed scenes. Sunrises, kitchens, fires, faces.

This one showed a pair of hands. Small hands, a child's hands, gripping another hand—an adult's, ink-stained at the fingertips—and the whole memory was nothing but grip and warmth and the ferocious certainty, pouring off it like heat off a stove, of *don't let go, whatever happens, don't let go*.

Wren sat back hard on the stool.

They had taken a lot of things in twenty years. Mornings, marriages, murders, the taste of a specific plum. They had never once taken something that felt like it was still *holding on*.

Outside, the snow that wasn't supposed to fall until winter began, very softly, to come down over the city—and somewhere in the Gilderscale district, a bone-carver slept dreamlessly for the first time in years, lighter by one memory, and neither of them yet knew whose hand that memory had been holding, or why, when Wren tried to set it down among the others, it refused to let go of theirs.
