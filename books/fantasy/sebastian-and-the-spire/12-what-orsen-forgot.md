## 12. What Orsen Forgot

Wren Quill kept their workshop above a dye-house in Tanner's Row, where the air smelled of vinegar and old blood and the river's slow rot. Orsen climbed the outside stairs with their collar up and their heart going like a trapped bird, and told themselves—again—that this was a transaction like any other. A service rendered. A price agreed. The Gilderscale traded in impossible things every day; this was only one more line in the ledger.

The dead in their pockets disagreed. The bone button murmured its owner's fear of the dark. The locket—still unreturned, still waiting for a goodbye Orsen would not give—hummed its small grief against their ribs. Everything they carried had an opinion about where they were going, and none of them thought it was wise.

"Come in before you think better of it," said a voice through the door, and then the door opened on its own, which was the first thing about Wren Quill that was not entirely human.

* * *

The room inside was smaller than it should have been. Orsen felt the wrongness immediately—the way the walls sat at angles that didn't quite resolve, the way the single window showed morning light when the street below had been grey with late afternoon. Objects crowded every surface: rings and thimbles, a child's wooden horse, a soldier's medal gone green with age, a wedding veil folded into a box too small to hold it. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

Each one silent.

Orsen stopped dead on the threshold. In forty years of touching other people's keepsakes, they had never once stood in a room where nothing spoke.

"You feel it," Wren said. They were younger than Orsen expected—or older; their face did something complicated around the eyes, as though several ages were taking turns wearing it. "That's what I do. I don't destroy the voices. I take them out. All of them. Every memory a thing ever soaked up, lifted clean." They smiled, and the smile had a thief's economy to it—nothing wasted, nothing given free. "Quiet by the pound."

"How?"

"Trade secret." Wren swept an arm at the shelves. "Every one of these was screaming once. Now they're just things. People pay me to make haunted objects into honest ones. Heirlooms you can hold without weeping. Wedding rings that remember nothing but gold." Their gaze sharpened. "And sometimes people pay me for the opposite. To take something *out of them*. That's what you want, isn't it? You've got the look. The look of someone who hasn't slept in a week because everything they touch won't shut up."

Since the proclamation, since the night the whole workshop had risen into chorus, Orsen hadn't slept more than an hour at a stretch. The dead sensed the hunt coming. They were afraid, and their fear came through Orsen's hands like water through a cracked wall. They'd taken to wearing gloves lined with wool and wax, and even that only muffled it. Last night a spoon had sobbed into the small hours.

"I want silence," Orsen said. "Not forever. Just—" They heard how it sounded and hated it. "Just enough to think."

"Everyone wants enough to think." Wren pulled out a stool, gestured to another. "Sit. Let's talk price."

Orsen sat. The stool, mercifully, remembered nothing.

"I can't pay much," Orsen began. "Coin, I mean. The guild pays in discretion more than silver—"

"I don't want coin." Wren turned one of the silent objects over in their fingers—a brass button, twin in shape to the bone one in Orsen's pocket—and set it down. "I want a memory. Yours."

The room went very still. Even the light through the wrong window seemed to pause.

"That's not possible," Orsen said. "Memories aren't objects. They're not—"

"They're exactly objects. The oldest kind. Everything that happens to you soaks into you the way blood soaks into bone. I've been lifting memories out of living heads since I was nine years old." Wren said it flatly, without pride, and something under the flatness flinched. "It doesn't hurt. Not physically. But I'll be honest with you, because you strike me as someone who prices honesty: what's left behind is a hole. Shaped like whatever I took. You'll know something's missing. You'll feel the edges of it every time your mind goes looking. And you will never know what it was, because I'll have it, and I keep what I take."

"Why would anyone agree to that?"

"People agree to it to forget a dead child's face. To forget a betrayal. To forget what they did in a war." Wren's eyes flicked to the shelves—to all those quieted things—and away again. "You want to forget the voices. Fine. But here's my price, and it's not negotiable: I'll take one memory from you, and *I choose which one.* Blind. You don't get to pick. If you picked, you'd pick something you already regret, and regret is cheap. What I take has to cost."

"That's robbery."

"That's the trade." Wren leaned back. "You walked up my stairs because you're drowning. I'm offering you air. The price is air-shaped too."

Orsen thought of the spoon sobbing. Of the locket's woman saying *tell her, tell her, tell her* in a voice like smoke. Of the night every object in the workshop had spoken at once, hundreds of the dead crying out that they were afraid, and Orsen lying awake in the middle of it knowing that if the inquisitors came, every voice in that room would point at them like a compass needle.

They thought of Hale's hand on their shoulder. *You're the best of us, Whitlock. Discreet.*

"Say I agree," Orsen said slowly. "How do I know you'll take just one? How do I know you won't strip me hollow and leave me a shell who can't carve, can't read, can't remember why they climbed the stairs?"

Wren looked at them for a long moment. Then they laughed—not cruelly, but with a tired edge, like a blade drawn along a strop.

"You don't. Same way I don't know you won't run to the inquisitors the moment you're out my door and tell them there's a memory-thief on Tanner's Row." They stood, crossed to a cabinet, and drew from it a small object: a tuning fork, or something shaped like one, made of a metal Orsen didn't recognize—dark, faintly warm-looking, like iron that remembered being a star. "This is called a quiescent. I'm the only one who makes them. It finds the seam in a person where a memory sits, and it lifts. One pull, one memory. It can't do more than one—it burns out after. So you have my structural guarantee, if not my moral one." They turned it over. "Besides. Stripping you hollow would be a waste. Do you know what you are, bone-carver? Do you know what your gift actually is?"

Orsen's mouth went dry. "I hear the dead in the things they touched."

"That's what it looks like from inside your skull." Wren set the quiescent down between them on the table, and Orsen felt it the way you feel a held breath. "From out here, it looks like this: you are a door that never learned to close. Everything that ever happened near you, you keep. Every grief, every secret, every last word. Most people's minds shed. Yours *keeps*. Which means—" and here Wren smiled again, and this time the smile was almost gentle, which was somehow worse, "—you are the richest house I have ever been invited to rob."

* * *

Orsen should have left. They knew that even as they stayed. There is a particular vertigo in recognizing your own desperation from the outside—watching yourself do the foolish thing with perfect clarity, hands steady, mind cataloguing every reason to stop, body refusing to move.

"What if you take something I need?" they asked. "Something that keeps me alive. How to carve. How to lie to inquisitors. Whether I locked my door."

"Skills sit deeper than memories. I can't reach them—I take the *events*, not the abilities. You might forget your mother's face and still carve her likeness perfectly, and never know why your hands know it." Wren paused. "Though I'll say this: whatever I take, you'll grieve it. That's the part nobody believes until it happens. You'll stand in the hole and mourn something you can't name, and everyone around you will think you've gone soft in the head, weeping over nothing. Nothing," Wren repeated softly, "being the exact weight of what's missing."

"And you do this to yourself too?" Orsen asked. "To bear the quiet?"

The question landed somewhere Wren hadn't armored. For a moment the shifting ages in their face all aligned into one age, and it was young—younger than Orsen, younger than Sera Voss, a child's age wearing a thief's coat.

"I don't take from myself," Wren said. "I take from everyone else. That's the whole trouble with me." They straightened. "Last chance, Whitlock. Walk out, keep your voices, keep your whole self. Or sit still and let me make you quieter, and poorer, and freer. Both bills come due eventually. Choose which one you'd rather pay first."

Outside, far off, a herald's bell rang the second hour. Somewhere across the city, criers were reading the decree again: *all gifted persons must present themselves for registration.* Somewhere in the Gilderscale's ledgers, in Orsen's own careful hand, three names existed that shouldn't—three clients Orsen had hidden, a forgery Hale had watched them make without a word. The noose was drawing in. Silence wasn't a luxury anymore. It was armor.

"Do it," Orsen said.

* * *

Wren had them sit with both hands flat on the table, palms up, like a patient presenting for bleeding. The quiescent hummed when Wren lifted it—a note below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone. Orsen's pocketed dead stirred: the button whimpering, the locket keening low, and Orsen realized the voices could feel it too, could feel the thing that emptied things, and they were afraid of it the way the living were afraid of the inquisitors.

"Hold still," Wren murmured. "This is the part where most people ask me to stop. Don't. If you ask, I have to stop, and then you'll have paid half a price for nothing, and I'll keep the toll anyway. Thief's rules."

"Charming profession."

"We aim to please." Wren touched the fork's tine to the center of Orsen's forehead.

Cold. Not the cold of ice or iron but the cold of a *gap*—the cold of a doorway opening onto winter. Orsen felt the quiescent go down through them like a probe through wax, past thought, past the surface layers where recent days sat in a messy pile: the proclamation, Marr's visit, the forged ledger lines, Sera Voss's sword remembering its dead men. Past all of that, down to older strata. Down to childhood.

*No,* Orsen thought, with sudden animal clarity. *Not that. Not down there. Take the recent—take the bad night, take the spoon, take—*

"There it is," Wren whispered, and their voice had changed—hushed, awed, a little sick. "Oh. Oh, that's a deep one. That's—" They stopped themselves. Professional discipline. "Brace."

The pull was not painful. That was the horror of it. It felt like a sneeze that never quite arrived, like a word on the tip of the tongue, like standing in a room and knowing someone had just left it. Something rose up through Orsen's chest and throat and out through the place where the tine touched, and for one half-second Orsen *saw* it—saw the shape of what was leaving, glimpsed it the way you glimpse your own face in a dark window:

Warmth. A kitchen. Someone laughing—

Gone.

The quiescent chimed once, sweetly, and went dark in Wren's hand. Burnt out. Spent.

Orsen sat very still.

"Well?" they said. Their own voice sounded distant, like it was coming through a wall. "What did you take?"

"Thief's rules," Wren said quietly. "You know I can't tell you."

"No. I mean—" Orsen reached inward, the way you pat your pockets for keys, and found the hole.

It was enormous. That was the first thing. Not a missing fact, not a misplaced afternoon—an absence with *mass*, sitting behind the sternum, cold-edged, person-shaped. Orsen's mind went looking for it automatically, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth, and slid off the edges every time. There had been someone. The hole was shaped like someone. Shaped like years of someone—shaped like a whole weather system of someone, laughter and warmth and a kitchen, a kitchen with—what, a window? A blue door? There had been a name. Names were the first thing to go; Orsen could feel the name-shaped notch in the hole's crown like a keyway.

"Who was it?" Orsen's voice cracked on the second word. "Wren. *Who was it?*"

"A person," Wren said. They had set the dead quiescent down and were looking at Orsen with an expression Orsen couldn't read—guilt trying to pass itself off as neutrality and failing. "That's all I'll say. A person you loved. That's what it took. I didn't choose it, understand—the fork chooses, it goes for the deepest root, and yours—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again, flatter. "Yours went deep, bone-carver. Deeper than most. Whatever I took, it was holding up a lot of other things. You may find some rooms in your head rattling now."

Orsen stood up. Their legs worked, which seemed like a bureaucratic oversight. They walked to the window that showed the wrong hour and pressed their forehead against the cool glass, and the grief arrived.

It came the way floodwater comes—no warning, total, finding every low place. They wept for someone whose face they could not summon, whose name sat in a keyway their fingers kept reaching for and finding smooth. They wept the way you weep at a stranger's funeral, except worse, because the stranger was the dearest person they had ever known and the proof of it was the sheer size of the mourning. Forty years of love, it felt like. Decades of Sundays. An entire life lived alongside someone, and all of it scooped out and carried off by a thief in a dye-house loft, leaving only the grief—which, Orsen understood dimly through the roaring, had been too heavy to lift. Love leaves. Its weight stays.

"I'm sorry," Wren said behind them. "For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth anything right now—I am sorry."

Orsen wiped their face with the heel of their hand. Turned. The room looked different now, or they looked different in it—smaller, lighter, the way a house feels after a death, when a whole presence has been carried out the front door and the walls haven't learned it yet.

"The voices," Orsen said. It took them a moment to check, and when they did, they nearly staggered. Quiet. Real quiet. The bone button in their pocket lay mute. The locket was just a locket, cold metal and a curl of hair. The dead had gone out of Orsen's hands like a tide, and in their place was the hole, vast and aching and *clean*.

"It worked," Orsen said stupidly.

"It works." Wren was wrapping the burnt-out quiescent in cloth, not meeting their eyes. "Give it a day. The quiet holds. The hole holds too. They come as a set."

Orsen fumbled coin onto the table anyway—habit, guild-trained, settling accounts—and Wren pushed it back.

"Told you. Memory was the price. We're square." At the door, as Orsen's hand touched the latch, Wren spoke again, quickly, like someone paying a debt in installments: "Whitlock. The thing I took. It wasn't random. The fork goes for the deepest root, and yours went for a *person tied to power*. I've done this two hundred times. I have never once seen it go that deep for anything that wasn't—" They cut themselves off, jaw tight. "Forget it. Forget I said anything. You're good at forgetting now."

But Orsen had already caught it, the way you catch a scent on the wind of a fire miles off: *a person tied to power.* The words snagged on the keyway-notch in the hole and wouldn't come loose. Someone Orsen had loved. Someone connected to the palace, to the crown, to the machinery that was now hunting the gifted through the streets. Loved so deeply that the loss of them had been holding up half the architecture of Orsen's inner life—and lost *how*? When? Why couldn't Orsen even remember the losing?

Because, said a cold small voice at the bottom of the hole, maybe you didn't lose them. Maybe they were *taken*. Maybe everyone assumed you'd forgotten on your own, the way people do, the way grief does—and maybe the truth was stranger and crueller than that, and the proof of it was currently wrapped in cloth in a thief's cabinet on Tanner's Row.

"Wren." Orsen turned in the doorway. The stairwell light fell across them both, and for a moment the memory-thief looked, for all their shifting faces, like exactly what they'd admitted to being underneath: a nine-year-old who had learned early that everything loved could be lifted away. "If I come back. If I scrape together another price. Can a memory be given back?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the wrong-window light shifted.

"No one's ever asked me that before," they said finally. "Everyone wants to forget. Nobody wants to remember." They looked down at their hands. "I don't know. I'd have to try. Ask me again someday, if the hole gets too loud."

* * *

Orsen walked home through streets going blue with dusk, and the silence followed them like a faithful dog.

It should have been relief. It was relief, partly—the first true quiet in weeks, the dead hushed, their hands their own again. They bought bread from a baker and the bread said nothing. They passed the market cross where the decree had been nailed and the notice board said nothing. They touched the guildhouse door, worn smooth by ten thousand hands, and it said nothing, nothing, nothing.

And beneath the relief, filling the space the voices had vacated, the hole sang its one note. *Someone. Someone. You had someone.* They caught themselves constructing them out of scraps—a laugh with no face attached, a kitchen with no walls, the ghost-warmth of a hand in theirs, smaller than theirs, a child's hand? A sister's? The word *sister* surfaced and sank, surfaced and sank, like something drowning just beyond reach, and each time it sank the hole ached afresh, and Orsen walked the last mile home with tears freezing on their cheeks and no idea, none, whom they were mourning.

At the Gilderscale, the night clerk nodded them through. The corridors smelled of tallow and old paper. Orsen let themselves into their workroom, lit one lamp, and stood among the shelved commissions—the frames, the reliquaries, the commissioned restoration piece from the palace that waited under its dust-cloth like a sleeping animal.

Tomorrow, they told themselves. Tomorrow they would work. Tomorrow they would be grateful for the quiet, and stop grieving a nothing, and be the discreet, competent, empty-handed person the guild had raised.

They pulled off their gloves. And there on the workbench, where they'd left it days ago, sat the ledger—Orsen's ledger, the true one, the private one where they recorded every object's history in a code of their own devising, dates and owners and deaths. Bookkeeping as devotion. The only congregation they'd ever had.

Orsen opened it to enter the evening's transaction—*one memory, taken, price unknown*—and their pen hovered over the page.

Because there, in their own handwriting, in entries dated years apart, were references they had never noticed before. Small ones. A margin note beside a commission from six years ago: *paid in advance — see L.* Beside an older entry, faded: *L. says the registry lists are incomplete. L. worries.* And once, five years back, in a shakier hand: *L. has not written. Ask Hale if the palace post is reliable. H. says yes. H. always says yes.*

*L.*

The letters meant nothing. The keyway in the hole fit them perfectly.

Orsen sat down slowly in the lamplight, heart hammering, and stared at the page—at the trail of a person they had loved, walking backward through their own records toward the palace, toward the registry, toward Hale's assurances—and understood, with the terrible clarity of the newly hollowed, that the answers were not on Tanner's Row at all.

They were downstairs. In the guildmaster's office. Where Corvane Hale kept his ledgers.

All of them.
