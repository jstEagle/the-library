## 15. The Undercity of Kept Things

The way down began behind a tannery that had burned in Orsen's grandfather's time and never been rebuilt, because the ground beneath it refused to hold mortar. Sera lifted a rotted hatch with one hand as if it were made of paper, revealing stairs that descended into a dark the color of steeped tea.

"Stay close," she said. "And whatever speaks to you down here—don't answer."

Orsen almost laughed. *Don't answer the dead.* The rule they'd built their whole life on, recited back to them by a woman who'd sold her sword to buy their escape.

"I know the rule," they said.

"You don't." Sera went first, her boots finding each step without hesitation, though the stair was slick with something Orsen chose not to identify. "Down here the rule isn't discipline. It's mercy. Yours or theirs. Sometimes you can't tell which until it's too late."

The voices started three flights down.

Not the workshop murmur Orsen had lived with all their life—the drowsy complaints of buttons and chair legs, the small grievances of things that had been touched gently and often. This was different. This was a crowd.

*—they took my hands, they took my hands, I only wanted to hold—*

*—the fire, tell them about the fire, the fire was not an accident—*

*—I counted, I counted every one, four hundred and eleven, someone has to have counted—*

Orsen stopped on the stair and pressed a palm against the wall, out of habit, out of need—and immediately regretted it. The stone screamed. Not words, exactly; more like a chord struck from every death the wall had ever witnessed, compressed into a single note of such density that Orsen's teeth ached and their vision whited at the edges.

They snatched their hand back. Sera turned, took in their face, and did something unexpected: she reached up and pulled Orsen's hood lower over their ears.

"There's wool in the lining," she said. "Muffled mine, when—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "It helps. A little. Keep your hands to yourself and walk."

"The walls remember everything down here," Orsen managed.

"The walls are the least of it." Sera resumed descending. "Wait until you meet the shelves."

* * *

The Undercity was older than the city above it. That much became clear within minutes: the architecture down here didn't match any style Orsen knew from the ledgers—not the crown's granite, not the guild districts' brick, not even the rough fieldstone of the oldest temples. These were walls fitted without mortar, stones shaped by hands that had understood something about load-bearing that modern masons had forgotten, or perhaps never known. And everywhere, everywhere, there were doors.

Doors in walls that led nowhere. Doors set into floors. Doors carved into ceilings, hanging open like mouths. A city beneath the city that had been built around the act of keeping things shut.

"What is this place?" Orsen whispered.

"Officially? Nothing. The crown's maps show solid rock." Sera ducked under a lintel carved with symbols worn nearly smooth. "Unofficially, it's where the impossible goes to be stored. Every guild has its overflow. Cursed goods seized in trade disputes. Heirlooms too dangerous to inherit. Things people pay to have disappear." She glanced back, and in the lamplight her scarred face looked almost gentle. "You kept the Gilderscale's ledgers for six years, Whitlock. You think every entry ended with 'disposed of' because it actually was?"

Orsen thought of the ledger columns marked with the little symbol Hale used—a circle with a slash—that Orsen had always translated as *written off*. Hundreds of entries. Thousands.

"They're all down here."

"Somewhere. It's a big place. Nobody's mapped all of it." Sera paused at a junction where five tunnels met, each mouth breathing a different temperature of air. "Which is why we're going to see the people who do the mapping."

She chose the second tunnel from the left, and Orsen noticed—for the first time—that someone had chalked a mark on the wall beside it. A circle with a slash.

Hale's symbol. Or the Undercity's, first, and Hale had borrowed it.

The thought sat in Orsen's chest like a swallowed stone as they walked.

* * *

The dead got louder as they went deeper, but also—strangely—more organized. In the upper tunnels the voices had overlapped and tangled, a riot of private griefs. Here they had structure. Orsen heard it before they understood it: a rhythm underlying the noise, like a drumbeat under a mob. Voices taking turns. Voices repeating one another in what could only be called unison.

*—four hundred and eleven—*

*—four hundred and eleven—*

*—she was pushed, she was pushed, ask the well, the well knows—*

"Are they..." Orsen couldn't finish the question.

"Organizing?" Sera said it plainly, the way she said everything. "Yes. Been happening for years, from what the smugglers say. The dead down here aren't scattered the way they are up top. Too many of them, too close together, lodged in goods that were collected *because* they held ghosts. They talk to each other through the shelving. Through the floor. They've worked out signals." Her jaw tightened. "Some of them have worked out demands."

"Demands to whom?"

"To whoever will listen." Sera stopped walking. Ahead, the tunnel opened into light—not torchlight, but something cooler, a pale glow like moonlight through fog, though no moon had ever reached this far underground. "That's us, apparently. Tonight."

* * *

The warehouse hall was vast beyond reason—a cavern so wide that its far wall was a suggestion, a darkness with shelves in it, rising out of sight. And the shelves. Orsen had imagined wooden racks, crates, dust. Instead the whole cavern was terraced with stone galleries, and on every gallery stood objects. Thousands of them. Mirrors draped in black cloth. Chairs facing corners. Dolls in iron cages. A row of wedding dresses, each one hanging perfectly straight, each one swaying slightly, though the air was still.

And the glow came from the objects themselves. Faint, cold, pulsing slowly—in and out, in and out, like the breathing of some enormous sleeping animal made of everything humanity had ever wanted to forget.

Orsen stood at the threshold and let the sound of it wash over them, and for the first time since childhood they didn't try to sort the voices into manageable streams. They just listened.

It was a library. It was a parliament. It was a wound the size of a cathedral, and every voice in it was awake.

"This is what the crown is hunting," Orsen said quietly. "This is what registration is for. Not people like me. *This.*"

Sera said nothing, which was answer enough.

A shape detached itself from between two shelving units—a woman, or mostly a woman, wrapped in layered coats the color of wet slate, a lantern hanging from a pole over her shoulder. Two more figures flanked her, and Orsen's gift flared warnings off both of them: knives that remembered throats, a coat that remembered running.

"You brought a reader into the Deep Stacks," the woman said. Her voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone who had priced many things and been surprised by none of them. "Without clearing it with the council."

"She cleared it with me," Sera said. "That used to count for something, Maret."

"That was before you got cashiered and started dragging strangers past our marks." But Maret's eyes—small, quick, unblinking—never left Orsen. "Well, reader? What do you hear?"

Everything. Orsen heard everything: the mirrors whispering to each other across forty yards of dark, comparing reflections; the dolls counting in tiny voices, counting and counting; the wedding dresses humming a song that no living person had written, harmonizing across their row like sisters. And underneath it all, the drumbeat rhythm, the organized pulse of thousands of dead things keeping time together.

"They're holding a meeting," Orsen said.

Maret's lantern dipped slightly. For the first time, the smuggler looked uncertain. "Say that again."

"The dead. They're not just... lingering. They've formed—" Orsen searched for the word and found it absurd and true at once. "Factions. There's a group near the east gallery that keeps repeating a number. Four hundred and eleven. Over and over, in rotation, like sentries passing a watch-word. Another cluster by the dresses—they're singing. Coordinating. And there's a third group that's gone quiet entirely, which frightens me more than the loud ones, because quiet means they've decided something."

Silence, in the hall, except for the dead.

Then, from deep in the eastern gallery, faint and patient and terribly clear, the chorus rose again:

*Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and eleven.*

Maret crossed herself with an old gesture, half-mocking, half-earnest. "Six years I've walked these stacks," she said. "Six years I've heard the whispers get louder. I told myself it was the wind in the vents." She looked at Sera. "Your reader hears factions."

"My reader hears a lot of things," Sera said. "Including, I'm told, the truth about why the king hasn't aged in seven years. Which is why the inquisitors want them, and why we need your council's help getting them somewhere the inquisitors can't follow."

Maret was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed—short, hard, without humor. "The council won't like it. The council doesn't like anything that smells of the crown, and you smell of the crown, Voss, no matter how far you've fallen." She raised the lantern. "But the dead like your reader. Look."

Orsen looked. And felt their breath catch.

Along the nearest gallery, the pale glow was changing. The objects nearest to them—draped mirrors, caged dolls, the swaying dresses—were brightening, their slow pulse quickening, leaning toward Orsen like flowers toward a window. Not reaching. Not grasping. Just... orienting. As if, after years in the dark, something had finally come down among them that could hear.

*The reader,* the shelves murmured, in a thousand voices braided into one. *The reader has come.*

"It's never done that," Maret said softly. "In six years, it's never done that."

Orsen's skin crawled—but underneath the crawling was something else, something warm and terrible and familiar: the feeling of being needed. Of being, for once, not hidden but sought.

"They want something," Orsen said. "All of them. It's not random. There's a message being passed along the galleries, hand to hand, shelf to shelf, from the deepest stacks toward us. It'll take a while to arrive. But it's coming."

"Can you wait for it?" Sera asked.

Orsen looked out at the breathing dark, at the thousands of small cold lights turning toward them like a congregation, and felt awe and claustrophobia sit down together in their chest and refuse to leave.

"Yes," they said. "But Sera—I don't think it's a message for me."

"Who else would it be for?"

Orsen listened harder, following the relay backward through the dark, deeper and deeper, toward the oldest part of the stacks where even the smugglers' lamps had never been. And at the end of the chain, at the very bottom of the buried world, one voice waited—one voice unlike the others, older than the others, wearing the others like a coat.

A regal voice. A patient voice. A voice that had been rehearsing a single sentence for seven years.

"It's for the king," Orsen said. "There's a ghost down there asking for the king. And I think—I think he's about to tell us who killed him."
