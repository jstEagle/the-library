## 17. Testimony of Bones

Aldric did not walk so much as pour himself along the Undercity's corridors, a grey light moving through grey dark, and Orsen followed because there was nothing else in the world to do.

They had left the ghost-council chamber behind them—the drowned chapel where the dead had voted, where Aldric had knelt on stone that remembered his coronation—and now the passages narrowed, descending in spirals that predated the city above. The walls here were not brick but packed earth shot through with roots, and every root carried its own faint murmur, the way old rope carries the memory of hands. Orsen kept their palms pressed flat against their coat to keep from touching anything. After the council, after three hundred voices speaking at once in something like harmony, their gift felt scraped raw, an open wound dressed in skin.

"You're afraid," Aldric said. His voice was strange—layered, as if two men spoke with one throat, one long dead and one only recently.

"I'm always afraid."

"No." The shade paused at a junction where three tunnels met, and the darkness bent around him as if it, too, were paying respects. "You're afraid *now*, of what comes next. That is different. Fear of a thing is a kind of preparation."

Orsen almost laughed. "The dead give advice now?"

"The dead give testimony," Aldric said. "Advice is what the living do with it."

He turned down the leftmost passage, and Orsen followed, and behind them, at a respectful distance that was also a guard's distance, came Sera Voss with her sword drawn and Wren Quill with their hands full of other people's yesterdays.

* * *

The tunnel floor changed underfoot. Earth gave way to flagstone—old flagstone, each slab carved with a name worn nearly smooth. Orsen crouched despite themselves and ran a finger along one: *MAREN, WHO KEPT THE LAMPS.* Another: *TULL OF THE NINTH GATE.* Not graves. Memorials. Someone had carried these stones down into the dark, one by one, and laid them in a road.

"Who built this?"

"I did," Aldric said quietly. "In the first year. Before I understood what I was."

Sera's boots stopped behind Orsen. "Your Majesty," she said—it was the first time she'd used the title, and her voice cracked around it like ice on a pond—"where are you taking us?"

"To my bones," said the king.

No one spoke. Somewhere far above, muffled through forty feet of earth and the whole weight of the city, a bell rang the hour, and even that sounded like a held breath.

"They never found me," Aldric went on. "Seven years, and the realm has mourned a king who is not dead, and served a king who is not alive, and no one thought to ask why the body in the throne room never rots. Because they hid me well. Because *I* hid myself well." A pause, and when he spoke again the layered voice thinned until only the younger man remained, the one who had once been merely mortal. "I was afraid they would find me. Now I am afraid they won't need to."

Wren shifted. In the dark their eyes caught the light strangely, the way a magpie's do. "Bones remember," they said. It wasn't a question; it was a professional assessment. "Everything remembers. But bone—" They tapped their own wrist, where the skin was thin. "Bone is honest. Flesh lies about how it died. Bone just holds the door open."

"Then hold the door," Aldric said, and walked on.

* * *

The place he brought them to had been a cistern once. Orsen knew cisterns; the Gilderscale's cellars had been built over three of them, and Hale had taught Orsen to read the water-stains like ledger lines. This one had gone dry centuries ago. Its walls rose in a perfect circle, ribbed with arches, and in the center stood a low table of black stone—and on the table, arranged with terrible care, lay a man.

Not a skeleton. Bones, yes, but clothed still in the rotted honor of royal raiment, a breastplate gone to lace-rust, a cloak that had been crimson once and was now the color of dried blood, which is perhaps the same color. The skull rested on a folded standard, the way a living man rests his head on a pillow someone loved him enough to provide. Around the table, set into the floor in a ring, were seven candlesticks of white iron, unlit for seven years.

"Who—" Sera began, and stopped, because the answer was obvious. He had. He had crawled here, or been carried, or walked as a dying man walks when he refuses to die where his murderers can count it, and he had arranged himself with dignity because dignity was the last thing a king could carry unassisted.

"I had help," Aldric said, following Orsen's gaze to the candlesticks. "A lamplighter. Maren. You passed her stone." Something moved across the shade's face that was not quite a smile. "She asked no questions. She brought candles she never lit, because I told her the day would come when they should be lit, and she believed me, which was either faith or folly, and she is dead now, and I have never learned which."

Orsen stood at the edge of the black table and could not move closer.

This was the thing about the gift that no one understood, that Orsen had spent thirty years making sure no one would understand: it was not hearing voices. Hearing would be bearable. Hearing was weather, was noise, was the complaint of a button about the cold. But touch—touch was a door, and the dead did not knock. They simply opened it from the other side and pulled you through.

And this was a king. This was seven years of murder, folded and waiting.

"Orsen," Sera said softly. She had come up beside them, close enough that her shoulder nearly touched theirs, and her sword hand hung loose at her side—not threatening, ready. Ready for what, neither of them could say. "You don't have to."

"Yes," said Orsen. "I do. That's rather the whole shape of me."

Wren made a small sound—almost a laugh, almost grief. "There it is," they murmured. "The ledger-keeper's creed. Everything must be entered."

Orsen pulled off both gloves.

Their bare hands looked absurdly ordinary over the black stone, pale and scarred at the knuckles from thirty years of carving. They thought, absurdly, of the locket woman, the daughter they'd never said goodbye to. They thought of rule one, carved into their own discipline like a name into flagstone: *never answer the dead.*

Then they touched the bones of King Aldric the Second, called the Just, murdered in the seventh year of his reign.

* * *

The door opened from the other side, and it pulled.

Cold first—that was the mercy of it, cold before everything, because the body had been cold for hours by then and the cold was the last honest sensation of the man whose eyes Orsen now saw through. Cold flagstone under his back. Cold air in a corridor that smelled of tallow and lilies, the spire's upper floors, the king's private stair, and Orsen knew it instantly, horribly, because they had delivered a commissioned frame to those very corridors two months ago and complained inwardly about the climb.

*He is coming,* someone had whispered. *He is coming, and you will kneel, and you will be glad to.*

Aldric had not knelt.

That was the thing the histories would never record, Orsen realized, drowning in another man's final hour: the histories would say the king died in his bed, or vanished, or abdicated mysteriously—but the truth was that Aldric the Just had heard footsteps on his private stair at midnight, had known exactly what they meant, and had gotten out of bed anyway. Had put on the breastplate instead of calling for guards, because the guards had already stopped answering, because the palace had already turned, because the rot had gone all the way down to the foundations and the only person in the spire who didn't know was the man being murdered.

The door opened without being unlocked.

Three figures. Their faces were wrong—not masked but *smoothed*, as if someone had taken faces and pressed them flat like dough, and where features should have been there was only a suggestion, a courtesy, the idea of a face. Behind them, filling the doorway and then the room and then, somehow, the whole interior of Orsen's borrowed skull, stood a presence that did not have a shape so much as an appetite.

It hummed.

That was the worst of it, the detail Orsen would carry back into their own body like a splinter: it *hummed*. A hunger-song, low and patient, the sound a starving thing makes when it has learned that food will always eventually come to it. Orsen had heard that song before. Two weeks ago, from a portrait frame in a merchant's parlor, from a painted king whose smile belonged to something older wearing his shape.

*You are the harvest,* the humming said, and it was not words, it was the meaning underneath words, pressed directly into the meat of the mind. *You were planted for this. Your father signed. Your grandfather signed. Every crown of this line has fed the winter so the spring could come, and you have been generous, little king, you have been very generous—*

"I know what I am," Aldric said aloud, in his own voice, in his own throat, and Orsen felt the words form around them like armor. "I am the king. And a king is a door, and doors open onto what is real, and *you are not real*. You are a debt. And debts end."

The smoothed faces tilted. The hunger-song changed key—amusement, or the thing that wore amusement.

*Then let us keep you,* it said. *We keep everything. Nothing in this city is wasted. You will sit. You will watch. You will feel every warm thing your people burn to stay alive pass through you on its way to our keeping, and you will learn what generosity costs, and you will have seven years to reconsider your answer.*

Hands took him. Hands that were cold in a way flesh cannot be cold, cold like the space between stars, and they bore him down—not to a tomb, never to a tomb, tombs are for the finished—and the last thing Aldric the Just saw with living eyes was the ceiling of his own private stair, and the last thing he thought was not a prayer but a piece of statecraft, a king to the marrow:

*Maren. The candles. Someone will come. Someone always comes, if you leave a light for them—*

* * *

Orsen came back to themselves on their knees on the dry cistern floor, both hands still locked around the dead king's femur, gasping as though they'd been held underwater.

Someone was holding their shoulders. Sera. Her face was very close, and very pale, and there was a wet track down one cheek that she would deny to her grave. "Orsen. Orsen. Come back. Whatever you saw, it's done, it's past—"

"It isn't past." Orsen's voice came out ruined, scraped down to the grain. "That's the point. That's the entire point. It's *happening*. Every day. Every hour. There's a man on that throne feeling every fire in the city go out, feeling every warm meal, every—every child's fever breaking, every hearth—he feels it all get *taken*, and he's been sitting there taking it for seven years because he couldn't die properly, because the binding won't let him finish—"

They let go of the bone. Their hands shook. On the black table, the skull seemed no different than it had a moment ago, and that was somehow unbearable—that so much testimony could live in a thing so quiet.

Wren crouched at the table's edge, studying the seven unlit candlesticks with an expression Orsen had learned to read in the Undercity: appraisal. "The ring in the floor," they said. "White iron. Seven points. That's not decoration. That's a ward-anchor. Someone bound him *here* too—not just on the throne. A second knot in the rope." They glanced up, and for once there was no thief's slyness in their face, only a grim craftsman's respect for bad work. "Whoever built this wanted insurance. If the throne-binding ever loosened, the bones would hold him anyway."

"Then we cut both knots," Sera said.

"It's not rope," Wren said. "It's a pact. Pacts don't cut. They get *testified against*." They looked at Orsen, and something passed between them—the memory-thief and the bone-hearer, two people who dealt in what others kept hidden. "You heard it. All of it. The original terms."

Orsen closed their eyes. It was all still there, seared in: the hunger-song, the smoothed faces, the voice that had spoken of planting and harvest and crowns that fed the winter. *Every crown of this line has fed the winter so the spring could come.* A trade. A founding bargain, struck generations ago by men who had probably told themselves it was mercy—a famine-spirit bound and fed in exchange for the city's survival, and somewhere along the years the feeding had grown, and the spirit had grown, and the kings had become crops.

"A pact," Orsen repeated. Their voice steadied as they spoke, word by word, the way a ledger steadies when the entries finally balance. "Struck by the founders. Sealed with a king's consent—or the fiction of it. Fed by the warmth of the city. Enforced by two bindings, throne and bones." They opened their eyes. "Every pact has terms. Every term can be read aloud. And every reading needs a witness who can hear both sides."

The cistern was silent except for the drip of some far-off moisture counting seconds.

Aldric had drifted closer while Orsen spoke, and now the shade stood at the foot of his own remains, looking down at the rusted breastplate with an expression no living face could have managed—grief and distance braided together, mourning oneself from the far side of the grave-line.

"Seven years," he said. "Do you know what I have wanted, all this time? Not vengeance. Not rescue, even. I am past rescue; I think I was past it the night Maren carried me down here." The layered voice was steady, but Orsen, who had just lived inside it, could feel the fault-line running beneath every word. "I have wanted to be *heard*. To have the account read out. Kings get chronicles; I got a disappearance. My murder is an entry no one has made, and an unmade entry is a wound that stays open." He raised his eyes to Orsen, and they were the eyes from the stairway, the ones that had thought of candles and witnesses even as the cold came in. "You heard it. All of it. Will you enter it?"

Orsen looked at the bones. At the man who had refused to kneel. At the seven candlesticks waiting in their ring like an argument nobody had finished.

"I'm a ledger-keeper," they said. "It's what I'm for."

Something in the shade's posture shifted—settled, the way a burden settles when it is finally shared. "Then hear the rest," Aldric said. "Because what you took from the bone was my death. You have not yet taken my *reign*. And in my reign is the name of the one who wrote the pact down—who drew up the terms, who keeps the original still, hidden, in the spire, in a vault that opens only to a king's living blood." A pause, and the fault-line showed through, plain as a crack in glaze. "Orsen Whitlock. The registry did not take only strangers. When they purged the gifted twenty years ago, the lists were drawn by the man who serves the Crown now. And one of the names on his list—"

Above them, muffled through the earth, a bell began to ring. Not the hour. Not any hour Orsen knew. It rang fast and wrong, alarm-shaped, and Sera was on her feet with her sword out before the second peal, and from the tunnels leading back toward the smugglers' warrens came a sound Orsen had hoped never to hear again so soon:

Boots. Many. And beneath them, thin as wire, threaded through the stone itself—

a hunger-song, humming.
