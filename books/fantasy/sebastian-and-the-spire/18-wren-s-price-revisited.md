## 18. Wren's Price Revisited

Wren Quill had stolen from kings, bishops, and one very memorable archmage whose memories tasted of ozone and regret, and in all those years they had never once lost sleep over a job.

They were losing sleep now.

It was the third night since the taking, and the Undercity warehouse where Sera Voss had stashed them smelled of tallow and old rope, and Orsen Whitlock slept badly two rooms away, and Wren lay on a pallet that smelled of someone else's sweat and stared at the ceiling beams as if the answer to their problem might be written up there in the wood grain.

The problem had a shape. That was the worst part. Wren knew exactly what shape it was—a person-shaped hole, worn smooth at the edges by seven years of not-remembering—and they knew its weight, because they were carrying it. Stolen memories didn't vanish when you took them. They sat in you like swallowed stones. You could feel them turning over whenever you breathed too deep.

And this particular stone had a name inside it.

*Say it,* Wren told themselves, the way they'd told themselves every night for three nights. *Just say the name. Walk in there and say it and be done.*

They hadn't said it yet. Saying it would make it real, and real things demanded things of you, and Wren Quill had built an entire life on never letting anything demand anything.

*You're a coward,* said the stone.

*Yes,* said Wren. *That's the trade. Cowardice is the whole business model.*

* * *

They found Orsen in the common room at dawn, or what passed for dawn down here—Undercity light came through grates and gutters, gray-green and apologetic, like the sun sending its regrets. Orsen sat at a scarred table with a cup of something hot going cold between their hands, staring at nothing with the particular blankness of a person listening to silence.

That was another thing Wren had noticed about bone-carvers. Even when the voices weren't talking, Orsen listened like they might start. Like a man who'd lived beside a river all his life and couldn't stop waiting for the sound of water.

"You're up early," Orsen said, without looking around. "Or late. I can't tell down here."

"Both." Wren dropped onto the bench across from them, deliberately loud, deliberately casual. Casual was armor. They'd learned that young. "Your knight snores like a collapsing building."

"She's not my knight."

"No?" Wren raised an eyebrow. "She carried you through three flooded tunnels last week because you twisted your ankle on a stair. She checks your door twice a night. She pretends she doesn't, but she walks like a soldier on rounds, and soldiers only do rounds for people they've decided to protect."

Orsen's ears went pink. It was, Wren reflected, deeply unfair that the most powerful listener in the city could still be flustered by gossip. "She's repaying a debt," Orsen muttered.

"Everyone here is repaying a debt. That's what this place is. A warehouse for debts." Wren wrapped their hands around their own cup—not to drink, just to have something to hold. The warmth bit at fingers gone cold. Here it was, then. The moment. They'd rehearsed eleven openings on the walk over and every single one had evaporated. "I need to talk to you about the job."

Orsen went very still. Not afraid-still. Listener-still. As if Wren had become an object worth hearing.

"The memory I took," Wren said. "From you."

"I don't remember it."

"That's rather the point."

"I know." Orsen's voice stayed level, but their thumb moved against the cup's rim, a small repetitive motion, worry made visible. "I know there's a hole. I feel it sometimes. Like a tooth that isn't there anymore—the tongue keeps going back to check." They looked up, and their eyes were steady and tired and far too kind for what Wren was about to say to them. "Is something wrong? Did the taking hurt me?"

*Did it hurt you.* Wren almost laughed. Almost wept. Both would have been catastrophic, so they did neither, and reached instead for the professional tone, the thief's cadence, the one that turned everything into inventory.

"Here's how it works," they said. "When I take a memory, I take all of it. Not a summary. Not an impression. All of it. Every sense, every word, every name." They watched Orsen's face carefully. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Understanding arrived slowly, then all at once. Wren watched it land—the widening of the eyes, the hand that drifted up toward the temple, toward the hole behind it.

"You know who it is," Orsen whispered. "The person in the hole. You know their name."

"I know everything about them." Wren kept their voice flat, clinical, because if they let any feeling into it they were going to come apart right here on this bench. "I know how they laughed. I know what their room smelled like. I know the exact words they said to you the last time you saw them, and I know—" and here the flatness cracked, just slightly, and Wren hated themselves for it, "—I know why you forgot them. I know who took them away from you. And it wasn't me."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the fire to pop twice and for somewhere above them, in the smuggler-warrens, a child to start crying and be hushed.

Orsen's hands had stopped moving. Their face had gone through shock and out the other side into something harder to read—a kind of terrible patience, the expression of someone bracing for a blow they'd already decided to accept.

"Tell me," they said.

"No."

Orsen blinked. "No?"

"Not like this." Wren stood up, because sitting felt suddenly unbearable, and paced to the window-grate where the green-gray light fell across their shoulders like a judgment. "Not across a table with bad tea. Because once I say the name, you'll want to go to them immediately—I know you, bone-carver, you're the most impulsive careful person I've ever met—and I need you to hear the rest first. What it costs. What I did. All of it." They turned. "So I'm going to tell you a story instead. And then I'll give you the choice. Fair?"

"Fair," Orsen said slowly. "Though I notice you're making yourself the storyteller. Controlling the frame."

"Occupational habit." Wren sat back down, closer this time, elbows on the table. Close enough that they could see the fine scars on Orsen's knuckles, the marks of a life spent working small and precise. "Once upon a time there was a child in the Spire Quarter. Clever, quick fingers, no family worth mentioning, no prospects whatsoever. And one day a man came through the market wearing guild colors, and he saw the child lift a purse off a merchant without the merchant ever knowing, and instead of dragging the child to the watch, he crouched down and said: *you have a gift. Would you like to be paid properly for it?*"

Orsen's breath caught. "Hale."

"Hale." The name came out of Wren like a splinter coming out of skin—relief and pain in the same motion. "He trained me. Fed me. Gave me a bed in the Gilderscale cellars and called me his little magpie. And the work he gave me—" Wren's jaw tightened. "You think your gift is a burden, Orsen. Hearing the dead. Carrying their grief around. Mine is worse, because mine takes. Everything I've ever touched that mattered, I've emptied. I have a head full of other people's childhoods and none of my own, because I sold mine piece by piece before I was old enough to know what pieces cost."

"You sold your own memories?"

"To eat. To stay in Hale's good graces. He liked us hollow—hollow tools hold an edge." Wren smiled, and knew it was the wrong kind of smile, the kind that showed teeth and nothing else. "But that's not the confession. The confession is this: Hale didn't just use me for thieving. He used me for cleaning. When a client of the Gilderscale remembered something inconvenient—saw something, heard something, knew something about where the guild's goods really came from—Hale sent me. And I went. I was twelve the first time. There was a cooper's wife who'd noticed that the guild's 'recovered heirlooms' still had blood in their hinges. I took the whole afternoon from her. She woke up thinking she'd fallen asleep over her mending, and she never wondered about anything again, and I walked home with her confusion in my chest like a coal, and Hale gave me a honey cake."

Orsen said nothing. Their face had gone white, but they didn't look away, and Wren found, absurdly, that it was the not-looking-away that undid them more than any condemnation could have.

"I'm telling you this," Wren said, quieter now, "so you understand that when I took your memory, I told myself it was different. A fair trade—you asked for silence, I named a price, you paid blind. That's the rule. Blind payment. No exceptions, because exceptions are how thieves get caught." They dragged a breath in. "But I knew. Before I even finished taking it, I knew what I'd taken. Because I recognized the shape of it. Palace work. Registry work. The kind of thing Hale sent me to clean for years, except this time the cleaning was for free, because you paid me, and I let myself believe that made it clean."

"What's in it, Wren?" Orsen's voice was barely above a whisper. "What did you take?"

"A person." Wren held their gaze. "Someone you loved. Someone the crown took from you so thoroughly that they scrubbed the loving along with the living. And someone connected—" the word came out bitter, "—to the spire itself. Connected in a way that changes everything we're planning. In a way that means when we climb that tower, you're not walking into a stranger's house."

* * *

Orsen stood up so fast the bench scraped and the cold tea slopped over.

"They're alive." It wasn't a question. It was a detonation. "You said *took*, not *killed*. They're alive, in the spire, right now—"

"Sit down."

"Don't tell me to sit *down*—" But they sat. Or half-sat, hovering, hands pressed flat to the table as if holding it down would hold themselves down. "Seven years. There's been a hole in me shaped like a person for seven years and I thought—I assumed grief. Dead parents, dead friends, the usual grave-dirt. You're telling me the crown reached into my head and cut someone out of it like a—like a tumor—"

"Like a debt," Wren said. "The registry doesn't kill gifted children, Orsen. Worse. It uses them. And it makes sure nobody comes looking." They watched Orsen absorb that, watched the listener in them reach instinctively for the voices of the dead—as if the dead could confirm it, as if some ghost in the walls might corroborate—and saw the moment Orsen realized the cruelest part: the dead couldn't help. This crime had left no corpse. Only a living person, walking around inside the enemy's tower, loved by someone who'd been surgically prevented from knowing it.

"Why are you telling me this?" Orsen asked finally. The fury had banked down to something steadier and more dangerous. "You could have kept it. Sold it back to me later. Held it over me. That's what a thief does."

"Yes."

"So why?"

And there it was. The question Wren had spent three nights avoiding, because the honest answer was humiliating, and the dishonest answers were worse.

"Because I looked at you," Wren said. "After the taking. You were standing in the doorway of your workshop with this—this *gap* in you, and you smiled at me and thanked me, thanked *me*, and asked if I was all right after the procedure. And I thought: I have done this to dozens of people and never once watched them afterward. Hale never let us watch. We took and we left and we spent the coin and we didn't think about the holes we left behind." Their voice roughened. "You're the first hole I ever had to look at. And it turns out I can't carry it. I've carried kings' sins and bishops' shames and none of it kept me up at night, but your empty chair keeps me up at night, and I hate it, and the only way to put it down is to give it back."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. Then, gently—which was somehow the worst thing they could have done:

"Wren. What was their name? Your—before Hale. Did you have anyone?"

The question hit sideways, exactly where Wren wasn't armored. "Don't."

"You gave me back my person. Tell me about yours."

"There isn't one. That's the point. That's what I'm telling you." Wren laughed, short and graceless. "I stole my own childhood before anyone else could take it. First rule of the trade: never leave anything valuable lying around. So no. No name. No hole. Just a lot of other people's memories rattling around in me like coins in a stolen purse." They pushed back from the table. "Which brings us to terms."

"Terms?"

"I'm offering to return it. The memory. All of it—the name, the face, the last conversation, the reason you forgot. Everything." Wren held up a hand before Orsen could speak. "Before you say yes, understand what returning costs. Taking a memory out is delicate. Putting one back is surgery. It will hurt—it will hurt *a lot*—and for maybe an hour afterward you'll live the whole of it again, compressed, every year of absence crashing in at once. People have broken under less. And there's no guarantee you'll be functional afterward. Sera needs you functional. The plan needs you functional."

"And if I say yes anyway?"

"Then I do it tonight, here, with her watching the door, and we hope the Undercity's ghosts don't get too curious about the screaming."

Orsen looked at their hands. Turned them over, palms up, as if reading something written there—or asking something of whatever dead skin remembered. When they spoke again, their voice had changed. Steadier. The voice, Wren realized, of someone who had already decided and was simply being courteous enough to let the thief finish the performance.

"You said the name is connected to the spire. Connected how?"

Wren hesitated. This was the last gate, and beyond it lay everything.

"Orsen—"

"*How?*"

Wren exhaled. Reluctant tenderness, they thought. That's what this is. Tenderness you wish you didn't feel, handed over anyway, because keeping it has started to cost more than giving it away.

"The registry lists," they said quietly. "The ones Hale kept copies of, back when I was his magpie. Children taken in, assigned to palace service. There was a girl on those lists with your surname. Whitlock. Lys Whitlock. Age nine. Taken the same season your memory goes dark." They met Orsen's eyes. "She works in the spire, Orsen. She's been there for seven years. And she's alive."

The sound Orsen made wasn't a word. It was smaller than a word—the noise a lock makes when a key turns after years of rust, the noise of something long shut beginning, painfully, to open.

Then they straightened, wiped their face with the heel of one hand, and said, in a voice like carved bone:

"Tonight. Do it tonight."

* * *

By full dark, the common room had been cleared, the door barred, and Sera Voss stationed against it with her arms crossed and her sword within reach, watching Wren with the flat assessment of a woman deciding whether to trust a knife she'd been handed.

"This hurts her," Sera said. Not a warning. An accounting. "If it breaks her, I break you."

"Noted," Wren said. "Sit her down. Hold her shoulders if she asks. Don't if she doesn't."

Orsen sat. The candle between them threw both their shadows huge against the wall, two shapes leaning toward each other like figures on a playing card. Wren flexed their fingers, felt for the stolen stone behind their own eyes, and found it waiting—warm now, eager, seven years of a sister's laughter pressing to get out.

"One more thing," Wren said, and their voice betrayed them at last, going soft in a way they hadn't permitted it all day. "When it comes back—when you remember her—there will be a moment where you hate me. Properly hate me, for carrying her name while you couldn't. Let that moment happen. Don't fight it. It passes faster that way."

Orsen nodded. Closed their eyes.

Wren reached out and touched their temples, and began, gently, to give back what they had taken—

—and in the space between one heartbeat and the next, felt the memory stir and whisper its name like a key sliding home:

*Lys.*
