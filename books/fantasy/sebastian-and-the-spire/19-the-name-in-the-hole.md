## 19. The Name in the Hole

The memory came back the way water comes back to a dried riverbed—not all at once, but seeping, finding old channels cut deep and long forgotten.

Orsen woke in the Undercity with a name already half-formed on their lips.

They had been dreaming of a garden. That was all. A small garden behind a narrow house, walled with grey stone, where someone was teaching them to press seeds into cold earth with one finger. *One push, no more. Seeds know how deep they want to go.* The hands in the dream were small and brown and quick, and they wore a ring of braided copper on the second finger, and when Orsen tried to see the face above those hands, the dream folded them gently backward like a page being turned—

—and they woke with dirt under their fingernails that wasn't there, and a word caught in their throat like a fishbone.

*Lyssie.*

They sat up in the dark of the smugglers' storeroom, among crates of sealed mirrors and salted clocks, and said it aloud just to hear whether it would break.

"Lys."

It didn't break. It rang.

For eleven days—no, they could count now; the memory gave them the counting—eleven days since Wren Quill had plucked a memory from their head and left a hole shaped like a person, Orsen had been grieving something they couldn't name. They had wept at nothing. They had set out two cups of tea without knowing why and then stood over them, shaking, unable to explain to Sera why they couldn't bring themselves to pour one back. They had carved a bone button in the shape of a garden wall and not known whose garden they were remembering.

Now the shape filled in. Now the hole had edges, and a voice, and copper rings on quick brown fingers, and a laugh that started in the belly and climbed.

Lys Whitlock. Their sister. Two years older, four fingers' width shorter, braver in the way of people who have never once doubted they'd be loved back.

And taken. Taken by the crown's registry, eight years ago, in the first sweep—the quiet one, before the proclamation, before the heralds and the market cross and the hunt. The registry had come to Greywall Lane at dawn with soft boots and softer voices, and Lys had put her hand on Orsen's shoulder and said, *Don't do anything stupid, little ledger-keeper,* and walked out between the soldiers like she was going to market, like it was nothing, like she'd come home by supper.

She hadn't come home by supper.

Orsen had grieved her. Of course they had. But grief, over years, settles into sediment, and the sediment had been scraped away with everything else, and what remained was this: a person-shaped absence, a garden without a gardener, an ache with no story attached.

The story attached itself now, all of it, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Mother crying into her apron and Father saying *they'll feed her, at least, they'll keep her safe, gifted children are kept safe*—the lie families told themselves so they could sleep. The letters that stopped after three. The registry clerk's face, smooth as a closed ledger. The way Orsen had stopped carving anything shaped like gardens because every garden made the ache sing.

*Oh,* Orsen thought, lying still in the dark, hands pressed flat against their own ribs as if to hold the new-old thing inside. *Oh, that's what I lost.*

Not a memory. A person. A living person, who might be—

Wren had said it, that night in the thief's den above the tannery row, turning the little lead token between ink-stained fingers: *What I took from you holds the name of someone you loved. Someone connected to the palace.* They had offered to give it back, awkwardly, guiltily, the way Wren did everything that involved other people's hearts. And Orsen had been about to answer when Aldric's summons had come, and then Marr's hounds had come after that, and there had been no time for anything but running.

But Wren had slipped it back anyway. Orsen realized that now—or most of it. Sometime in the chaos of the running battle, in the smoke and the screaming and Sera bleeding out her strength to hold the stair, Wren had pressed the memory home like a hand pressing a wound closed. Perhaps all of it. Perhaps only enough.

It didn't matter. It was here now. Lys was here now.

Alive.

*Connected to the palace.*

Orsen lay in the dark and let the hope rise, and it rose like floodwater, warm and terrible, because hope this size is never gentle. Alive. Serving in the spire. Eight years in the spire. Eight years of the Hollow Crown's household, its offices, its registries, its blue-burning fires. Eight years of whatever the crown does to the people it takes.

The dead in the storeroom had gone quiet, sensing the shift in the living thing among them. Even the salted clocks held their ticking. In the Undercity, silence meant attention.

Orsen got up, lit no lamp—they hardly needed one anymore; the objects around them glimmered faintly with the lives lodged in them, a constellation of small ghosts—and found paper.

* * *

Sera found them at dawn, or what passed for dawn down here—a grey seepage through the ventilation shafts, the Undercity's idea of morning. She came in armor-less, moving carefully on the leg she'd nearly lost holding the line against Marr's men, and she stopped in the doorway when she saw Orsen sitting amid a scatter of papers, writing and crossing out and writing again.

"You've been up all night," she said. Not a question. Sera Voss had spent twenty years reading battlefields and had transferred the skill wholesale to faces.

"I remembered my sister."

Sera went very still. Then she crossed the room, lowered herself onto a crate with the care of a woman whose body had become a ledger of debts, and waited. It was one of the things Orsen was learning to love about her—that she waited. That she understood some doors open only from the inside.

"Her name is Lys." Saying it aloud again, in daylight, to another living person—it rang even truer. "She was taken in the first registry sweep. Before the proclamations, before any of this. I forgot her because—" Orsen's voice snagged. They pressed on. "Because Wren took the memory of her as payment. For teaching me to quiet the voices. And I've been walking around for weeks with a hole in me shaped like my sister and calling it tiredness."

"And now?"

"Now I remember everything. The garden. The way she braided copper wire into rings because our mother wouldn't buy silver. How she used to stand guard outside my workshop door when I was listening to something awful, and tell clients I was at prayer." A laugh broke out of Orsen, wet and cracked. "She called it 'the whispering.' She'd say, *my sibling talks to furniture,* and she'd say it proud."

Sera's mouth did something complicated—sympathy trying to be a smile. "Where is she now?"

"The spire." Orsen looked down at the papers. Pages of them. Without quite deciding to, they had spent the whole night reconstructing Lys from memory the way a carver reconstructs a figure from a block: her height, her laugh, the scar on her chin from falling off the well-wall, the copper rings, the last letter—which Orsen now read aloud, haltingly, because they had memorized it as a child and the words had come back with the rest:

*Sparrow—work is strange but good. They feed us honey with breakfast. I'm learning cipher. Don't worry about the whispers here, the walls are too thick for anything to get through. That's a mercy, isn't it?*

"That's a mercy, isn't it?" Orsen repeated. "I was nine. I thought she was happy. Thick walls, no whispers—I thought the spire was *quiet* for her." They looked up. "But it's not quiet, Sera. Nothing in that building is quiet. I've heard the stones themselves. If the walls are thick enough to seal out the dead, then they're thick enough to seal something *in*."

Sera was silent for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the flat steadiness she saved for worst-case assessments. "You're thinking she's alive because no one told you otherwise."

"I'm thinking she was alive three years ago, because that's the last census entry I can find in Hale's ledgers—L. Whitlock, spire household, domestic cipher corps. Three years ago she was carrying messages for the crown." Orsen stood, and the papers whispered off their knees. "And I'm thinking about what happens to gifted children raised inside the Hollow Crown's household. What they're *for*."

The word hung there between them, unspoken, but both of them heard it: *vessel*. The famine-spirit needed vessels. Kept things. The entity at the summit had offered Orsen a place among them—*you would be warm forever, little listener*—and Orsen had understood, in that frozen moment, that the offer had been made many times before, to many listeners, and accepted by some.

"She might be a clerk," Sera said. "People live ordinary lives inside fortresses."

"She might be a lamp," Orsen said. "Being fed on slowly, so the crown stays warm." Their hands had begun to shake. "Eight years, Sera. She was sixteen."

Sera reached out—hesitated, the way she always did before touching, as if asking permission from some internal court—and took Orsen's hand. Her grip was calloused and careful. "Then we get her out."

"We're climbing a tower full of binding-wrought horrors to unbind a famine-spirit while an inquisitor hunts us and winter seals the city." Orsen laughed, and it came out wrong. "Adding a rescue seems greedy."

"Good," Sera said. "Greed keeps soldiers alive. Despair gets them killed." She squeezed once and let go. "We'll need Wren."

* * *

Wren received the news the way Wren received everything: sideways, with deflection deployed like a blade.

"So the memory worked." They were perched on a barrel of cursed candlesticks, knees drawn up, looking anywhere but at Orsen. "You remember the sister. Good. Efficient. No refunds."

"Wren."

"I'm just saying, most clients cry or thank me, and you've done neither, which is frankly insulting given the craftsmanship involved—"

"*Wren.*"

The thief stopped. Looked. And Orsen watched the deflection fail, watched something raw surface in that sharp face—the guilt Wren had carried since the tannery den, since they'd admitted, in a voice smaller than Orsen had ever heard them use, that the memory they'd chosen blind had turned out to hold a person. That they'd known. That they'd carried the knowledge of Orsen's stolen sister for weeks, alone, because returning stolen goods was against every law Wren Quill lived by except the ones that mattered.

"I pressed it back during the fight," Wren said quietly. "Most of it. I was afraid—if I asked first, you'd say no, and then I'd have to live with knowing what I took, and I already—" They stopped. Started over. "There was someone I lost too. When I was young. That's why I take memories. So no one else has to carry theirs alone in the dark, unlooked-at." A bitter twist of the mouth. "It doesn't work. In case you were wondering. The trade doesn't work."

"It worked," Orsen said. "Wren. Listen to me. You gave my sister back to me. Whatever laws you broke doing it—" they stepped closer, took the thin wrists in their hands, felt the tremor there, "—I will carve your name in gratitude into every beam of my shop when we rebuild it."

"Don't," Wren said, and their voice cracked clean through. "Don't rebuild anything yet. Don't promise beams. Just—" They pulled free, scrubbed at their eyes with a sleeve, recomposed themselves with visible effort. "Just tell me what you need. You called me down here for a reason that isn't sentiment. You always have a reason. It's why you're terrifying."

Orsen spread the papers on the crate-top: the reconstruction, the last letter, the census line from Hale's hidden ledger. And beneath them, a fresh page, written in the small precise hand of a bone-carver who had spent years recording impossible things: a plan.

"She's in the spire's lower offices. Domestic cipher corps—third sub-level, if the architecture in my head serves. When we go up, we go through the undercrofts, and the undercrofts connect to the records wing. I can reach her before the ascent. Before the floors that demand memories. Because if I climb those floors carrying her—" Orsen's voice tightened, "—carrying *hope*, real hope, a living person who might be freed—the spire will take it. That's what those floors *do*. They eat truths. I won't feed my sister to a staircase."

Wren studied the plan. Sera studied Wren. And Orsen watched the two of them—the disgraced knight and the memory-thief, the two people the world had discarded, who had chosen to be discarded *together*, with Orsen, in the dark under the city—and felt the thing that the dead had never given them settle into place at last. Not belonging compelled. Belonging chosen.

"The cipher corps," Wren said slowly. "Messages in code. Which means she has access to routing tables. Which means—" the sharp face sharpened further, "—she may know things about the crown's communications that even Marr doesn't. If she's still herself. If she's not a lamp."

"If she's complicit," Sera said, blunt as a hammer, "we'll deal with that. People survive captivity by serving. I served for six years after the order I should have refused. It doesn't make them lost."

"No," Orsen said softly. "It makes them waiting."

They gathered supplies in silence after that—Wren palming lockpicks and three stolen keys that hummed with the memories of doors they'd opened; Sera wrapping her bad leg and taking up her sword, which muttered its roll-call of dead men as she belted it, and which she silenced with a touch, gently, the way you'd hush a frightened animal. The Undercity's smuggler-council had voted at midnight to lend them passage through the cistern ways to the spire's foundations; the dead had voted, by Aldric's kneeling, to lend them their strength. Everything was arranged. Everything was ready.

Everything except the thing Orsen could not arrange: the eight years. The honey at breakfast. The cipher lessons. The girl who had walked out between soldiers saying *don't do anything stupid, little ledger-keeper.*

As they made to leave, Orsen paused at the storeroom door and took out the bone button they'd carved—the garden wall, the one their hands had made before their mind knew why. Small, white, worn smooth at the edges where a thumb had worried it these past weeks without knowing whom it mourned.

They closed their fist around it.

"Hold on, Lyssie," they whispered. "Your sparrow is coming. And I'm bringing the whole graveyard."

Above them, far above, through fathoms of stone and earth, the spire stood against a sky going the color of old ash. The snow that didn't melt was falling thicker now. The fires burned bluer.

And somewhere in the third sub-level of the records wing, behind walls too thick for whispers, a woman with copper rings on her fingers was learning a new cipher—and did not yet know that the walls had finally failed her, that a whisper had gotten through after all, carrying her name.
