## 20. Winter Begins

The first snow fell on a Tuesday, and it did not melt.

Orsen noticed it from the smuggler-council's antechamber, where a slitted window let in a knife of grey light. The flakes came down fat and unhurried, settling on the cobbles of the Undercity's upper rim—cobbles that still held warmth from the tannery vents below—and they simply stayed. Lay there like coins on a dead man's eyes. By noon the layer was a finger deep and had not gone to slush. By dusk it was two fingers deep, and it had begun, very softly, to glow.

Not light, exactly. Not the honest white of snow under lamplight. A pale blue shimmer, as if each flake carried a coal of frozen fire in its belly.

"The cold's coming down off the spire," said Brannick, the council's one-eyed quartermaster, watching through the slit beside Orsen. He spat. "Snow in autumn. My grandmother would've called that an omen."

"What would she call it now?" Orsen asked.

Brannick considered. "A bill come due."

The dead felt it first, of course. They always did. All through the Undercity's warehoused dark—the shelves of cursed mirrors, the crates of wedding rings pried from drowned hands, the racks of children's shoes that whispered their owners' names when the wind moved wrong—the voices had changed pitch. For years they had been a murmur, a complaint, a low tide of grievance. Now they were a hush. A held breath. Orsen walked the long aisle between the shelved dead things and heard nothing but attention, thousands of small attentions all turned the same direction: up. Toward the spire. Toward whatever was being fed up there.

One voice broke the hush—an old woman lodged in a cracked hand-mirror, her face swimming in the silver whenever Orsen passed. *"It's starting again,"* she said. *"The last time the fires went blue, I was alive. I was nine. We ate the leather from our boots."*

Orsen stopped. The rule was never answer the dead. But the rule had been dying for weeks, one exception at a time.

"When?" Orsen asked quietly. "When were the fires blue?"

*"The Long Dark,"* the woman said, and her reflection shivered. *"Ask your king. If you can find what's left of him."*

* * *

In the Lamplighters' Row, three streets above the guild quarter, Tomas Beck climbed his ladder at four in the afternoon because the sky had already gone the color of a bruise.

He'd been lighting lamps for nineteen years. He knew the city's moods the way a wife knows a husband's breathing—when it was going to rain by the smell of the gutters, when there'd be trouble by how fast the taverns filled. And he knew, absolutely, that something was wrong with the light.

His taper caught the first lamp's wick, and the flame came up blue.

Not the blue of bad oil—he'd checked the reservoir twice, then a third time, kneeling on his ladder with his hands shaking. Every lamp on the Row burned the same impossible color, a cold cerulean that threw no heat. He held his palm beneath the glass and felt nothing. Worse than nothing: he felt the flame *drawing*, faintly, like a straw dipped in his wrist. Pulling warmth out of him the way a leech pulls blood.

Below him, the street had stopped moving. A fishwife stood with her basket on her hip, staring up. Two clerks from the counting-houses had halted mid-stride. An old man had taken off his hat, which was either prayer or surrender.

"Blue fire," the fishwife said. "My mother told me about blue fire." She didn't finish the sentence. Nobody asked her to.

By evening the crown criers were out, and their proclamation was short enough to memorize in one hearing: *By order of His Majesty, curfew is set at sundown. Hearth-fires are to be banked after the ninth bell. Hoarding of fuel is treason.* No explanation. No mention of the snow that wouldn't melt or the days that kept shrinking—not naturally, not the way autumn days shrink, but wrongly, like a candle burning down both ends. On Monday the sun had risen at half past six. By Friday it rose at seven and set before four, and the almanac-sellers had stopped pretending their tables meant anything.

Tomas lit forty lamps that night, every one of them blue, and walked home through snow that crunched without giving under his boots, and lay awake listening to his own house tick and settle around a hearth that seemed, somehow, colder when lit than when dark.

* * *

Sera Voss found the bread line outside Saint Verity's stretching past the fountain, which had frozen solid overnight—frozen from the bottom up, which old Mother Cass swore was against God's own ordering of things.

She hadn't come for bread. She'd come to count.

That was the discipline Sera had left the knighthood with, the one habit that survived the disgrace: count everything. Doors, guards, exits, weapons, minutes. A soldier who stops counting is already dead. So she counted the line—two hundred and eleven souls by the time she reached the corner—and she counted the granary doors across the square, barred with new crown-locks, and she counted the proclamations nailed over the older proclamations, layers of them like scabs:

*REGISTER GIFTED PERSONS.*
*REPORT UNLICENSED FIRE.*
*THE KING WATCHES OVER YOU.*

And beneath those, newer, fresher paste, the ink barely dry:

*BY DECREE OF HIS MAJESTY: THE HOUR OF KEEPING IS AT HAND. ALL LOYAL SUBJECTS WILL OBSERVE THE LONG DARK WITH CHEERFUL HEARTS.*

"Cheerful hearts," muttered the man ahead of her in line, a cooper with forearms like hams. "Cheerful. It's the third week of autumn and my cellar's froze. What are they keeping us *for*?"

"Keep your voice down," his wife hissed.

"Why? Who's listening? The snow?" He laughed, ugly and frightened. "Maybe the snow's a spy. Maybe the king's got ears in every flake."

Sera moved on before the conversation could become a crowd and the crowd could become a gallows-rehearsal. She had seen this before—not the blue fire, no one living had seen that—but the shape of it. Fear ripening into obedience. She'd watched a garrison captain use it once, starving a border town into docility before the tax wagons came. This was that, scaled to a kingdom. Someone wanted the city afraid and cold and grateful for any warmth it was allowed.

At the fountain she paused. The ice was strange—blue-veined, like the flames, like the snow—and pressed inside it, near the base where the water had frozen last, was a pigeon. Whole. Perfectly preserved. Wings half-spread, caught mid-liftoff, its eye visible through the clear ice and startlingly bright, as if it had frozen between one heartbeat and the next and might, if the ice ever melted, simply continue.

Sera looked at it a long moment. Then she did what soldiers do with omens. She noted it, filed it, and kept walking.

She was three streets away when she admitted to herself where she was walking: toward the Undercity. Toward Orsen Whitlock, who heard the dead, who had gone quiet and grey-eyed since the night they'd pulled him from the burning guildhall, who spent hours alone among the shelved dead things listening to whatever the dead were saying about the weather.

If anyone in this city knew what winter was coming to take, it would be the bone-carver.

* * *

Wren Quill sat cross-legged on the roof of the Saltcellar with their coat pulled tight and their hoard spread out around them on the frost-slick tiles: forty-one memories, stolen over a lifetime, each one sealed in its little vessel—a thimble, a locket-glass, a bird's egg blown hollow and waxed shut. They were checking them the way a miser checks coin. Counting warmth.

Because that was the thing about memories nobody told you: they were warm. Hold one to your skin and it gave back a ghost of the feeling it held—a summer afternoon, a mother's kitchen, a first kiss. Wren had survived more than one hungry winter by holding a stolen July against their chest until sleep came.

Tonight, every vessel was cold.

Wren tested them one by one, increasingly frantic, thumbing wax seals, pressing glass to the hollow of the throat. The July in the eggshell: cold. The wedding in the thimble: cold. Even the memory Wren thought of as *the furnace*—a baker's recollection of leaning into a brick oven's mouth at dawn, the single warmest thing Wren had ever stolen—even that had gone tepid, like milk left out.

"It's eating the past," Wren said aloud, to no one, because talking to no one was a habit of thieves and liars and people who lived on roofs. "Whatever's up there, it's not just taking the heat. It's taking the *keeping*. The warmth things hold."

They looked east, toward the spire. It stood against the bruised sky like a needle through fabric, and for the first time Wren could see—actually see, with thief's eyes trained to notice what glowed with value—that the tower had an aura. A slow pulse of pale blue rising through it floor by floor, like a swallow, like a throat working something down.

Something up there was swallowing the city's warmth. And it was getting faster.

Wren gathered the vessels, wrapped them, and thought—with a sour twist of guilt they weren't used to—of the memory they'd taken from Orsen. The hole-shaped one. It was still in the hoard, still sealed, and it was the coldest of all. Cold as a grave. Whatever name slept inside it, the famine-spirit's hunger had gotten its teeth into that too, and Wren found themselves wondering, for the first time, whether some things shouldn't be kept in stock.

Whether some debts should be paid back before the collector comes for everyone.

* * *

In the spire's lower offices, Lys Whitlock copied names.

That was her work now, and she was good at it, and she tried not to think about what the names were *for*. Registry ledgers, mostly—gifted persons catalogued, assessed, assigned. Some marked with a small black circle. She had learned early not to ask about the circles. Asking questions in the spire was like tapping the walls of a cell: it only reminded everyone, including yourself, that you were inside one.

Her office was on the fourth floor, and it had a window, and lately the window showed her snow falling upward.

Only at certain hours, only in thin threads, only if you watched long enough—but yes. Upward. Little spirals of it lifting off the rooftops below and streaming toward the spire's crown like smoke drawn into a chimney. The other clerks didn't speak of it. The other clerks spoke of almost nothing anymore. The offices had gone quiet in a way Lys recognized from somewhere deep and unhealed, some memory with a hole torn in the middle of it—a silence like the one that had followed the day men in grey coats came to her family's door.

*You're safe here,* the supervisors always said. *The Crown protects its own.* And she believed it, mostly, the way you believe a rope will hold when it's the only thing between you and the drop. But tonight, copying names by blue lamplight, she wrote a name and her pen stopped.

Whitlock, O.

An assessment slip, routed to her desk for filing. Gifted category: object-auditory. Status: unregistered. Disposition pending.

She read it three times. The ink swam. Somewhere behind the hole in her memory, something knocked—once, twice—and went silent.

Lys looked at the black circle stamped beside the name, and then at the disposition line, where a supervisor's neat hand had written: *Retain for binding season.*

She filed the slip. Her hands were perfectly steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. Outside the window, the snow fell upward, and the spire swallowed, and swallowed, and swallowed.

* * *

Inquisitor Marr stood at the top of the spire's ninth floor, in the room that wasn't on any plan, and waited to be acknowledged.

The king's body sat enthroned at the chamber's heart—or the thing that wore the king's body did, though Marr had stopped thinking of it as a body months ago. It was more like a glove these days, a desiccated puppet of yellowed skin and ceremonial silk, and the thing inside it was *leaning forward* now, insofar as a thing without muscles could lean. Its hunger had a posture. Marr could feel it the way you feel a storm's pressure in an old scar.

"The city burns blue," Marr reported. "Curfew holds. Fuel rationing begins tomorrow. The registry lists four hundred and six gifted for the binding season—more than enough vessels, if the harvest requires—"

*"More,"* said the Hollow Crown.

Marr waited. One learned to wait, in this room. The silence had texture; it tasted of iron and old snow.

*"The thawing-thief,"* the thing said at last, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. *"Someone in your city keeps warmth against me. Small hoards. Stolen summers. Find them. And there is a listener among the dead things below—one who hears what should be silent. The bones of the true king still sing to it. Bones must be ash before the Long Dark closes, or the testimony will be heard."*

"You believe the ghost intends to testify," Marr said carefully.

*"I know it. I was there when it was silenced the first time."* The puppet's head tilted, and in the shadow of the crown, something that was not an eye reflected the blue light. *"Winter is my oldest instrument, inquisitor. I have ended kingdoms with less than a cold snap. But the listener is a loose thread. Loose threads unravel bindings."*

Marr bowed. At the door, the voice followed, soft as frost forming:

*"And bring me the carver, Marr. Alive. I want to know what a gift sounds like when it is broken slowly."*

* * *

That same hour, in the deepest vault of the Undercity, Aldric stood among the kept things and listened to the city breathe.

He had been dead seven years, and he had learned the dead's geography: they pooled where grief was thick, and the Undercity was an ocean of it. Tonight the pool was stirring. Ghosts drifted up from their objects in numbers he hadn't seen since the purge—the mirror-woman, the drowned bride still wringing seawater from a veil no one else could see, the child-shoes' small chorus walking in a ring. They were all facing the same way. Up.

"They're feeding it early," Aldric said to the mirror-woman, who had appointed herself, without consultation, his herald. "The Long Dark isn't due for another month. It's accelerating."

*"It's afraid,"* the mirror-woman said. *"Dead things know fear, majesty. Something down here frightens it. Something it can't digest."*

Aldric looked at his hands—translucent, regal, useless—and understood. A listener. A bone-carver who had touched his remains and carried away the truth of his murder like a splinter under the skin. The famine-spirit had ruled seven years on the strength of one silence, and now the silence had a crack in it, and cracks, in the dead's country, were doors.

Above them, muffled through thirty feet of stone and earth, the city rang the ninth bell. Curfew. The blue glow of ten thousand forbidden flames seeped down through the ground like light through water, and the snow that would not melt went on falling, and falling, and falling.

"Send word to the carver," Aldric said. "Tell him winter has begun—and tell him we don't have a month. Tell him we have days."

The mirror-woman inclined her silvered head. And far above, in a workshop full of listening dead, Orsen Whitlock woke from a shallow sleep with the taste of iron in their mouth and every object in the room whispering the same word at once, over and over, patient as snowfall:

*Hurry. Hurry. Hurry.*
