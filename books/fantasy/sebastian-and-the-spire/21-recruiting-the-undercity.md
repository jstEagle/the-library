## 21. Recruiting the Undercity

The Undercity did not have doors so much as suggestions of doors—archways that appeared only when you had already decided to enter them, corridors that folded back on themselves like a hand closing into a fist. Sera led, Wren drifted behind me like a rumor, and Aldric walked where no one else could see, his presence a coldness at my left shoulder that I had learned, in the weeks since the bone-cellars, to read as companionship rather than dread.

"You'll want to let me do the talking," Sera said for the third time.

"No," I said, also for the third time. "You'll want to let me do the talking. You'll want to stand behind me looking like a drawn blade."

"That's a threat assessment, not a look."

"It's both." I adjusted the strap of my satchel—the ledger inside it was wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord I'd tied myself, three knots, each one checked twice. "They're smugglers, Sera. They deal in impossible things every day of their lives. What they don't deal in is strangers asking for favors. If you front this, they'll see a knight and start counting how much her armor would fetch. If I front it, they'll see what I actually am."

"And what's that?"

I thought about it as we passed beneath an archway hung with dozens of small brass bells that made no sound at all—which meant, I suspected, that their sound had been sold.

"A broker," I said. "Someone who understands that everything has a price, including silence. Especially silence."

Behind us, Wren snorted. "Oh, they're going to *love* you."

* * *

The council met in a chamber that had once been a cistern, if the waterline stains on the walls were anything to judge by—dark tide-marks climbing thirty feet up the curved stone, the ghost-geometry of a drowned century. Now the floor was dry, swept clean, and ringed by seven mismatched chairs arranged on a dais of stacked shipping crates: a chair of black oak, a chair of whalebone, a chair that was less a chair than a throne assembled from ship's fittings, and others whose materials I catalogued out of habit before I could stop myself.

I felt them before I saw them clearly. The dead clung to this place like barnacles to a hull. Every object on the dais hummed with old ownership, old argument, old grief. The whalebone chair remembered a sea hunt and the long drowning after. The oak remembered a hanging. Even the crates beneath them were dense with voices—not screaming, not here; the Undercity dead had learned something like discipline—but murmuring, the way a crowd murmurs when it is waiting to see what happens next.

Seven figures sat in those chairs, and none of them matched their furniture. They were ordinary-looking, deliberately so: a woman with flour still dusted on her sleeves, a man built like a cooper, a thin person whose age I genuinely could not determine, and four more who might have been shopkeepers or cutpurses or both. Only one detail broke the pattern: each of them wore, somewhere visible, a single object that could not exist. A brooch with a moving picture trapped in its amber. A coin stamped with tomorrow's date. A key made of smoke that hadn't yet dissipated.

Credentials. Badges of office. Proof, worn openly, that they trafficked in things the crown said were impossible.

"Whitlock," said the woman with flour on her sleeves, and my stomach dropped through the floor, because she knew my name.

Beside me, Sera's hand went to her sword hilt. On my other side, Wren went very still in the way that preceded running.

"I'm told," the woman continued, "by three separate sources who do not know about each other, that the Gilderscale's ledger-keeper has gone missing, that the guildmaster has burned half his own cellar to cover a warrant, and that a bone-carver named Orsen Whitlock is asking questions in places where questions get people floated down the Gray Canal in a barrel." She leaned forward. "So. Which of those is the lie?"

"The barrel," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "It was the sewer outfall. And I'd thank you not to repeat the canal version; it makes Marr sound more competent than he is."

A pause. Then the flour-woman laughed—a short, sharp bark—and some of the tension in the room went with it, draining away like water finding a crack.

"Vessa Marchetti," she said. "Harbormaster of nothing, mistress of everything that comes through the Undercity wet or dry. You have ten minutes, bone-carver. The last person who asked this council for men and maps got four."

"What happened to them?"

"He asked for five."

I took that as my cue to be efficient. I unwrapped the ledger.

* * *

Here is what I had learned about power in twenty years of keeping accounts for other people's sins: it is mostly bookkeeping.

Hale had understood this. That was the thing I could not stop seeing now, in every memory of him—his warmth, his praise, the way he'd called me *the quiet engine of this house*—it had all been bookkeeping. He had kept two ledgers the way a man keeps two fires, and I had warmed myself at both without looking too closely at what fed them. Every kindness had been an entry. Every client he'd praised my discretion about had been a name already sold, or priced, or marked for sale. When I finally found the second ledger, hidden behind the false back of his own writing desk—behind *my* desk, technically; he'd moved me into his old office years ago, and I understood now that this too had been an entry—it had not even surprised me. It had simply completed a sum I'd been refusing to carry.

The survivors' council in the Undercity wanted Hale's head. I had come to trade them mine for their hands.

"This is the true ledger," I said, laying it open on a crate between the dais and the floor. "Notarized dates, client names, prices paid by crown agents, and initials matching Inquisitor Marr's own correspondence cipher—I've verified it against three letters we recovered from the guild's strongroom. Fourteen years of sales. Forty-one names." I turned pages slowly, letting them see. "Three of those names are sitting in this room."

The thin figure in the ship-fittings throne went rigid. The cooper-built man looked at the flour-woman, Vessa, and Vessa looked at the ceiling with the studied calm of someone deciding whether to be furious now or later.

"Your people sold themselves to the crown to survive," I continued. "Or thought they were buying protection. Hale took their coin and then sold their names anyway. Some are registered. Some are already taken." I closed the ledger. "You want justice. So do I. But justice costs, and I am not here to sell you revenge. I'm here to sell you something better."

"Which is?" Vessa asked.

"An end to the registry itself. Not Hale—Hale is a symptom. The registry, the hunts, the whole rotten apparatus—it exists to feed the spire. To feed what sits in the spire wearing the king's face." I let that land. Around the circle of chairs, the ordinary faces went carefully blank in the way of people who have heard rumors and never dared say them aloud. "I have testimony. From the true king. Aldric Vane, murdered seven years ago by the thing that wears him now. His bones are hidden, his spirit is intact, and when the crown comes off that throne—if it comes off—every debt the crown ever contracted becomes void. Including the contracts your people signed to buy their safety. Including the trade charters the crown granted the Gilderscale and revoked from everyone else."

Silence. The dead in the furniture had gone quiet too, listening through the wood and bone and canvas as though the room itself had leaned in.

"You're proposing," said the thin figure slowly, "a new charter."

"I'm proposing the *old* one. Before the founders bound their famine-spirit into a king and started taxing winter. Trade rights under a free crown, formalized after liberation, witnessed—" I tapped the ledger, "—in writing, by me, who keeps books that hold. In exchange: men, maps, and silence. No word of our movements reaches any ear, living or otherwise, until the work is done."

"And if you fail?"

"Then the registry takes everyone eventually, and the charter question resolves itself."

More silence. Vessa stood, walked down from the dais, and picked up the ledger herself. She read the way I imagined she read cargo manifests—fast, ruthless, missing nothing. Her lips moved over the initials column. She stopped at a page near the middle, went pale under the flour dust, and closed the book with a snap that echoed off the cistern walls.

"Jorin Hale," she said quietly, "sold my brother's name to a crown agent eleven years ago. For forty crowns and a waiver on a customs seizure." She looked up at me. "He's been in the palace registers since. We assumed he was dead. This says *servant, spire household, third floor.*"

My chest tightened around a cold knot. Third floor. Spire household. I thought of Lys, and said nothing, because saying anything would cost more than I had.

"Third floor is real," I said instead. "People taken for the registry aren't all killed. Some are kept. I can't promise you'll find your brother alive. But I can promise the truth will be written down, and it will be *his* truth, not whatever story the crown filed."

Vessa set the ledger down. She looked at her fellow councilors, and something passed between them—not words, but the shorthand of people who have bargained together for decades.

"Maps," she said. "We have maps of the spire's undercrofts older than the current dynasty. Men—we have thirty who owe the council debts they'd rather pay with steel than coin. Silence—" her mouth twisted, "—silence we have in industrial quantities; it's the primary export down here. But I want one thing more, bone-carver, and it isn't negotiable."

"Name it."

"When this is done—win or lose—you testify. Publicly. In daylight. At the market cross where they read the proclamation, with the whole city watching. Everything in that ledger. Everything about Hale. Everything about what's in the spire." She jabbed a finger at me. "No quiet corrections filed in a guild archive. No tidy little housekeeping of history. The city hears it all from *your mouth*, because you're the one who kept the books, and bookkeepers don't get to hide behind their ledgers when the audit comes."

Every instinct I had, twenty years of careful invisibility, of answering the dead only in whispers, of being no one—every instinct screamed that this was the price that actually mattered. Not the fighting. Not the climbing. Standing in the light with my voice raised, knowing that everything I said would mark me forever.

I found, to my genuine surprise, that I wanted it.

"Yes," I said. "That was always part of the price. I just hadn't told myself yet."

Vessa studied me for a long moment, and then she smiled, and it transformed her from a baker into something considerably more dangerous. "Good. Then welcome to the supply chain, Whitlock." She turned to the council. "Votes?"

Five hands rose. The sixth belonged to the cooper-built man, who kept his arms crossed and said, "I want it noted that we're staking the Undercity on a bone-carver, a disgraced knight, and a thief."

"Two notes," Vessa corrected. "Wren Quill counts double."

"Flatterer," Wren said sweetly, and the tension in the room cracked again, and the vote became six of seven, and that was that.

* * *

The logistics consumed the rest of the day and most of the night. Maps unrolled across crate-tops: the spire's foundations rendered in faded ink by a surveyor two dynasties dead, annotated in three later hands, showing service tunnels, a drowned stair, and a sealed door marked only with a symbol—a circle broken by a vertical line—that made Aldric go very still beside me.

"That's the founder's seal," he said quietly. "Only I should have known it. Whoever drew this map was in the binding chamber."

"Then whoever drew it is either dead or complicit," Sera said.

"Both are possible," Aldric said. "Both are likely."

Thirty volunteers presented themselves in ones and twos through the evening: a woman with a crossbow and burn scars, twin brothers who spoke only to each other, a former guild porter who spat when he heard Hale's name and volunteered twice by accident. Each of them looked at me when they swore their silence—not at Sera, not at Wren, at *me*—and each time it landed somewhere in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. They trusted me. These people who had survived the Undercity by trusting no one, who measured strangers in seconds before deciding which pocket to pick, had looked at a bone-carver with ink-stained fingers and decided to bet their lives.

I kept waiting for the fear to arrive, the vertigo, the sense of the floor dropping out. It didn't. Instead there was something else, warm and strange and almost embarrassing in its simplicity: it felt *good*. Being trusted. Being counted on. All those years I had thought belonging was something the dead gave me—their voices, their company, their terrible intimacy—and here were the living, handing it over freely, asking nothing I couldn't give.

"You're smiling," Wren observed, materializing at my elbow while I marked routes on the surveyor's map. "Stop it. You look trustworthy."

"I am trustworthy."

"Worse. Listen—" Wren lowered their voice, and for once there was no performance in it. "The third floor. The spire household. Your sister."

I froze. Of course they knew; they'd been there when the memory returned, when Lys's name filled the hole they'd carved in me. "What about her?"

"If we're going in through the undercrofts, the third floor is above us, not in our path. You'll want it in your path. I'm telling you now so you can decide *before* the moment, because in the moment you won't decide well." Wren's eyes held mine. "I steal memories, Whitlock. I know what people do when a hole in them gets close to being filled. They stop thinking."

"I'm not going to abandon the mission for—"

"No. You're going to improvise something noble and half-planned and nearly die, and Sera will have to save you, and we'll lose time we may not have. Unless you plan for her now. Decide now: do we route past the third floor or not?"

I looked down at the map. My pen hovered over the service tunnel, the drowned stair, the sealed door with the founder's seal. Somewhere above it all, on a floor I couldn't see, Lys was filing papers for a king who wasn't a king, believing the Crown protected her.

"Not yet," I said finally. "One thing at a time. The gate first. Then her."

"That," said Wren, "is the first sensible sentence I've heard from you all week," and drifted off to steal something, probably on principle.

* * *

Near dawn, when the council had retired and the cistern chamber held only me, Sera, and the murmuring dead, I sat alone with the true ledger and read Hale's entries again. Not for information—for understanding. Fourteen years of betrayals, each one dated, each one priced. And there, near the beginning, in his younger handwriting, an entry I must have seen a hundred times without ever reading:

*Received: two names, out-of-guild, referred by parish register, St. Ellowen's. Sold to crown agent (M.). Consideration waived — favor owed.*

Two names. Out-of-guild. A parish register. St. Ellowen's was the church in the river district where I'd grown up. Where Lys and I had been baptized. Where our parents—

Hale had sold my parents. Decades ago. Before he ever sheltered me. Before he ever praised my discretion. The orphan the guild raised had been raised on the proceeds of the orphan's own family, and Hale had known, all along, exactly whose bones I carved and whose voices I heard, and he had kept me close the way a man keeps a tool that once cut him.

The ledger blurred. I pressed my palms flat against the page until the paper's dead whispered up through my skin—clerks and couriers and one poor soul who had died mid-signature—and I breathed until the shaking stopped.

Sera appeared at my shoulder. She didn't ask what I'd found. She'd read enough of my silences by now to know when reading them was cruelty.

"Tomorrow," she said instead, "we move on the undercrofts. Get some sleep."

"There's something I have to add to the bargain first," I said. "With Vessa. Testimony against Hale isn't enough anymore. I need the council's people to help me find two graves. Names redacted from every parish record in the river district. St. Ellowen's."

Sera was quiet for a moment. "Your parents."

"My parents." I closed the ledger, gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping. "When the crown falls, I'm going to write their true entry myself. Date, cause, and the name of the man who sold them. And then—" I stood, and the dead in the cistern walls stirred, and I realized my voice had gone hard enough that even the living furniture was paying attention, "—then we climb."

Above us, muffled through thirty feet of stone and earth, the city's bells began to toll the hour—and stopped halfway through the third stroke, as if a hand had closed around them.

In the sudden silence, every ghost in the Undercity screamed at once.
