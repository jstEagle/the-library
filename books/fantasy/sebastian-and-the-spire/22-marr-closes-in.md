## 22. Marr Closes In

Sera Voss had held a shield wall at Cael Ford with a broken collarbone and a fever, and she had not been afraid the way she was afraid now.

Fear at Cael Ford had been clean—blade to blade, breath to breath, the simple arithmetic of who was still standing when the horns blew. This was different. This fear crept. It came in through the smugglers' tunnels on soft feet, wearing a courteous face, asking questions of people who answered because they did not know yet that courtesy from the crown was a knife being drawn slowly.

She stood at the mouth of the Lantern Stair with a cup of bitter chicory she wasn't drinking and watched Orsen Whitlock sleep.

They slept badly these days—curled on a pallet of sacking in the corner of the warehouse the smuggler-council had lent them, one hand always near the wrapped bundle of Aldric's bones as if even unconsciousness could not quite let go of them. The ghost king kept his own watch, or something like it: a pale pressure in the lamplight, a shape like a man seen through frosted glass. He never slept. Sera suspected he no longer remembered how.

"You stare at them like a hawk over a henhouse," Wren Quill murmured, materializing out of the shadow of the stairwell the way they always did, soundless as spilled ink. They were turning a copper coin over their knuckles—an old habit, Sera had learned, a way of keeping their hands busy so they didn't steal things by accident. "The dead don't need guarding."

"The bones do." Sera set down the chicory. "And the person attached to them."

"Mm." Wren's eyes—dark, quick, always slightly amused, always slightly sad—flicked to Orsen and away again. Something moved behind their face. Sera had noticed it before, in the weeks since the Undercity: the way Wren looked at Orsen sometimes like a person looking at a locked door they'd once picked and couldn't stop thinking about. The memory they'd taken still sat in Wren's keeping. Sera didn't know its shape. She only knew that Wren carried it carefully, the way you carry someone else's child.

"Anything from the council?" she asked.

"Vess says the toll-keepers on the Salt Road report crown men buying passage. Not soldiers." Wren caught the coin flat against their palm. "Bone-readers. Two of them, and a woman in grey who pays in new-minted silver and doesn't haggle."

Sera felt the cold arrive ahead of the news, the way it always did now. "Marr."

"Marr," Wren agreed softly. "Three days out, maybe two. She's not hurrying. She doesn't think she needs to."

*She's right,* Sera thought, and hated her for it. Inquisitor Elara Marr did not hurry the way winter did not hurry. She had come to the Gilderscale once, in another life, and Sera had watched her test ledgers against bone-dust readings with the patience of a woman tuning an instrument—and Orsen had lied to her flawlessly and walked out with their heart hammering and their face smooth as river stone. Sera had fallen half in love with them somewhere between that lie and the smoke of the burning guildhall. She had not said so. There would be time, she told herself. There would be time after.

There was always an *after*, and it was always a lie.

"We move the bones tonight," she said. "Deep galleries, past the Drowned Chapel. Vess has a vault under the old tithe-house that even the smugglers don't map."

Wren raised an eyebrow. "Orsen won't like leaving the workshop quarter. The dead here are organizing, they said. They're listening for Marr too."

"Then they can listen from further underground." Sera stood, rolling her shoulders until the old scar tissue cracked. "I've run from superior forces before, Quill. You know what I learned?"

"That you don't run fast enough?"

"That you choose the ground before the ground chooses you." She looked up the dark throat of the Lantern Stair, toward the distant grey seam of the city above, where snow that refused to melt lay three fingers deep on the cobbles and the watchfires burned blue. "We pick our ground tonight."

* * *

They picked it wrong.

Later—much later, lying in a cot in the Drowned Chapel while a smuggler named Ivet sewed her side shut with waxed thread and hands that shook only a little—Sera would go over every decision like a woman counting coins and finding one short. The route through the Bonecutters' Walk. The timing, timed to the changing of the toll-watch. The choice to send Wren ahead to scout rather than keep them close. Any one of those decisions, made differently, and maybe the blood stays inside everyone where it belongs.

But Marr had not bought passage on the Salt Road. That had been a feint, paid for in new-minted silver, designed to be discovered. She had come down through the Well of Voices instead—the one entrance to the Undercity that the smuggler-council swore no living soul could use, because the ghosts there screamed anything human into madness within ten steps.

Marr had walked it without flinching. Sera would learn this afterward, from a toll-ghost who drifted into the chapel babbling about a woman in grey who had *listened* to the screaming the way other people listened to music, and had thanked the dead politely for their trouble.

The ambush came at the Narrows, where the Bonecutters' Walk squeezed between two walls of stacked ossuary brick and the ceiling dripped a mineral-rich water that glowed faintly green. Good ground, Sera had thought. A chokepoint. Six defenders could hold fifty.

Marr brought nine, and none of them were soldiers.

They came out of the walls—or rather, the walls stopped pretending they weren't full of them. Crown wardens in grey, faces slack, eyes lit from within with a cold blue light that Sera recognized with a lurch: the same blue as the city's burning watchfires. Puppeted. Dead or worse-than-dead, moving with the terrible economy of things that did not feel pain or hesitation or fear.

"Sera!" Orsen's voice, sharp with terror, from behind her. "They're *held*—the dead in them are screaming—they can't stop—"

"I know." She drew her sword.

It was not the sword she'd tried to sell in chapter and verse at the Gilderscale, all those weeks ago, when she'd been a disgraced knight with nothing but a name she couldn't use and a blade that remembered every man she'd killed. But it remembered anyway. Blades did. As the grey wardens came on, the steel woke against her palm with its familiar chorus—not voices, exactly, but weights, each life she'd taken pressing against her grip like hands on a rope. She had spent seven years trying to put that weight down. Now she picked it up deliberately, all of it, and found—to her surprise—that it balanced her.

"Hold the Narrows!" she shouted. "Nobody passes! Nobody!"

The first warden reached her and she killed it the way you kill a door—efficiently, without hatred. It went down and did not bleed, and beneath its slack face something flickered, something that might have been relief. That was the worst part. Every one of them, when her blade opened the binding holding it upright, wore for half a heartbeat the expression of a man set down from a long forced march.

She gave them that mercy six times. Then seven. The Narrows ran slick, though nothing bled properly, and behind her she heard Orsen shouting—not at the wardens but *to* the dead inside them, voice cracking, offering witness, offering names, doing whatever bone-speakers did to unmake the crown's puppets from within. One warden simply folded mid-swing, empty, its animating shade torn free by Orsen's words. Another turned on its fellows.

For one bright moment Sera thought they might hold.

Then the woman in grey stepped through the press of bodies, unhurried, and the temperature of the world dropped.

Inquisitor Elara Marr did not look like a monster. She looked like a schoolmistress approaching retirement: silver-streaked hair pinned neatly, spectacles on a chain, ink stains on her fingers. She surveyed the Narrows—the dead wardens, the glowing drip of the ceiling, Sera with her sword red with the wrong kind of ruin—with the mild disappointment of a woman finding her kitchen untidy.

"Serahilde Voss," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way certain voices do, as if the air itself were listening. "Cashiered for conscience. I reviewed your file. It's a good file. You were wasted on the border garrisons."

"Come closer," Sera said, "and waste me properly."

Something almost like a smile touched Marr's mouth. "You're guarding a box of old bones and a clerk who talks to them. Do you understand what you've thrown away? Your name, your honor, your life—for a *ledger-keeper*." She tilted her head. "Unless it isn't a ledger-keeper anymore. Unless it's something you've decided matters more than you do. That's not duty, Serahilde. That's just love with armor on. And love—" she sighed, genuinely regretful, "—is the easiest thing in the world to cut through."

"Then cut," Sera said, and raised her blade.

What happened next, she would remember in fragments for the rest of her life.

Marr did not fight. She *unmade*. She spoke a single word—a word in no language Sera knew, a word that tasted of frostbite and empty granaries—and the light went out of the ceiling-glow, and the dead in the walls around them began to scream in a register that bypassed the ears entirely. Sera's knees buckled. Her vision whited. Through the noise she heard Orsen scream back—not in pain, in *defiance*, words tumbling out, testimony, names, the dead answering—

And through the gap in the line that her own staggering body left open, two remaining wardens lunged past her, straight for the bundle of bones on Wren's back.

No.

The word arrived in Sera whole, complete, older than thought. She had failed a brother once—had followed an order she should have burned rather than obeyed, and learned only years later, reading his ring in Orsen's hands, that he had died believing she'd chosen the order over him. She had built her whole ruined life on the far side of that failure. She was not going to stand in a dripping tunnel and build another one.

She moved. Not gracefully—she was tired, bleeding already from a warden's knife along her ribs, forty-one years old with a bad knee—but the way a wall moves when it decides to fall on the right enemy.

She got between the wardens and the bones. She got her sword up. She got *in the way*, which was, when everything else was stripped away, the entire art of the shield, the whole secret of the trade she'd been born to and cast out of.

The first warden's blade took her through the meat of her left shoulder. The second drove a knife into her side below the arm, deep, deep, angling up, and the pain was white and total and clarifying, and Sera Voss did not fall. She hooked her sword around the first warden's neck and pulled, and the binding snapped like a lute string. She seized the second by the collar and headbutted it until the blue light guttered out of its eyes, and then she put her hand flat against its chest, over the frightened dead thing inside, and said, in the voice she'd used for twenty years on parade grounds:

"*Go home.*"

It went. Whatever held it—whatever Marr had wound into it—came apart against the plain authority of a knight's command, and the body dropped, and the shade rose out of it like steam off morning grass, and paused beside her, and she would have sworn on her lost name that it bowed.

Then her legs quit, and the floor came up, and the last thing she saw clearly was Orsen Whitlock's face above hers—smudged, terrified, lit by the green drip-light—saying her name over and over as if it were a spell, as if saying it enough times could keep her tied to the world.

"Don't you dare," she managed, and meant: *don't you dare blame yourself.* "Bones. Get the bones out. That's the— that's the mission—"

"Shut up," Orsen said, pressing both hands against her side, and their hands came away red, and their face did something terrible and controlled. "Wren! *Now!*"

The last thing Sera felt before the dark took her was not pain. It was Orsen's forehead pressed against hers, and the words breathed there, so quiet that perhaps Sera dreamed them:

"You don't get to leave. I finally have people. *Stay.*"

* * *

She woke to candle-smoke and chanting and the smell of the sea trapped in stone.

The Drowned Chapel was a smuggler hospital, a chapel, and a graveyard all at once—pews rotted to lace, an altar crusted with salt, tide-channels running through the nave where blind white eels swam in the glow of votive lamps. Someone had laid her on the altar, which struck her as either blasphemy or the highest available honor, and Ivet the smuggler-surgeon was sewing her side by lamplight while a circle of Undercity ghosts hovered at the edge of the light, watching the needlework with professional interest.

"Alive," Ivet said, without looking up. "Barely. The knife missed your liver by the width of a psalm. Your shoulder will work. Your pride is beyond saving."

"The bones?" Sera's voice came out as gravel.

"Gone deep with the ghost-king and the thief. The clerk wouldn't leave until you were stitched." Ivet tied off a knot, bit the thread. "They're sitting right there. Been sitting right there for six hours. We couldn't make them leave, and believe me, we tried."

Sera turned her head, carefully, past the fire in her shoulder.

Orsen sat on the chapel steps with their elbows on their knees and their face buried in their hands, and they had never looked smaller, or younger, or more breakable. The lamplight caught the bone-dust on their sleeves and the blood on their cuffs—her blood—and they were shaking, very slightly, the way a wire shakes after the bell stops.

"You're supposed to be resting," Sera rasped.

Orsen's head came up. Their eyes were wrecked. "You took a knife for a box of bones."

"Took a knife for *you*," Sera said. "The bones are just what you were carrying."

Silence, except for the eels sliding through their channels and the ghosts whispering among themselves like a jury deliberating. Orsen crossed the nave slowly, as if the floor might be rigged, and knelt beside the altar, and took Sera's unwounded hand in both of theirs. Their palms were warm. Living-warm. Sera had spent so long among the dead and the disgraced that she'd forgotten what living warmth felt like, and it undid her more thoroughly than Marr's word-of-frost had.

"When you fell," Orsen said quietly, "I heard every dead thing in this chapel hold its breath. Did you know that? Hundreds of them. They've been arguing for weeks about whether the living are worth helping. And when you went down getting between danger and us—" their voice cracked, steadied, "—the vote ended. Right there. They're with us now. All of them. Because of you."

"Fierce protectiveness," Sera managed. "It's a character flaw."

"It's the reason any of us are alive." Orsen lifted her hand and pressed it, briefly, against their cheek. Held it there. Looked at her with an expression that Sera had spent months telling herself she was imagining, and which was not imaginary at all. "When you're healed—when this is over—I have things to say to you. Important ones. So you are going to survive, Serahilde Voss. That's an order. I know you have a problem with orders, so consider it a request from someone whose ledger you're written into permanently."

Sera laughed, and it hurt, and she laughed anyway. "Yes, my lord clerk."

Above the altar, unseen by either of them, the pale shape of King Aldric watched, and smiled the sad smile of a man remembering what it was to want things, and turned to keep his vigil over the bones that would soon enough testify.

At the chapel door, Wren Quill slipped back in from the dark, rain-wet despite the frozen city above, and their usual flippancy was gone from their face.

"They're not regrouping," Wren reported softly. "Marr's pulling everything back to the spire. All of it. Wardens, readers, the registry clerks—everything." They swallowed. "And Orsen—there's something else. The toll-ghosts say the spire's doors opened tonight for the first time in seven years. Wide open. Like it's *inviting* us."

From the depths of the Undercity, faint through miles of stone, came a sound like a great bell made of ice, ringing once.

Winter had begun to sing.
