## 23. The Dead Vote

I had forgotten what it was to be afraid.

That is not quite true. I remembered fear the way a man remembers a language he spoke as a child—dimly, with the shapes of it but not the heat. In seven years of drifting through Kept Halls and cold corridors, through the long gray hours between one flicker of thought and the next, I had felt many things: sorrow like standing water, rage like a banked coal, the peculiar humiliation of being unseen by everyone who had once bowed to me. But fear—the animal, sweating, heart-hammering thing—I had set down at the moment of my death, and no one had handed it back.

Until the night the Undercity's dead convened to decide whether I was worth helping.

* * *

The chamber they chose was called the Tally Hall, though it had been a cistern once, before the city grew two stories taller than its own foundations and abandoned the lower rooms to the smugglers and their cargo. Wren Quill led us down to it by lantern-light that did nothing for me—I had not needed light since the spire took my breath—and Sera Voss came behind with her hand on her sword hilt out of habit rather than need, because the things watching us from the dark were past the reach of steel.

Orsen came last. They always seemed to come last now, as if walking at the rear of a funeral procession they hadn't agreed to join. The bone-carver's gift had grown louder every day we spent below the city; I could see it in the way they flinched from doorframes, from railings, from the smugglers' rings of keys. Everything down here was old and much-handled, and everything much-handled remembered.

"You don't have to do this," Orsen said to me, for the fourth time since dusk. Their voice echoed strangely off wet stone. "They're your people. If you asked them alone—"

"They are not my people." I said it gently, because it was the truest thing I knew. "That is rather the point."

Wren snorted. The memory-thief leaned against the cistern wall with their arms crossed, all sharp angles and sharper eyes, wearing the expression of someone about to watch a play they'd already read the ending of. "He's right," Wren said. "Kings don't have people. They have subjects. Different animal entirely. Subjects can be taxed."

"Wren," Sera said, in the tone of a woman who had spent three weeks learning when to cut that particular thread.

"What? It's relevant. Half these ghosts died building his walls, and the other half died in them. He's about to ask them for a favor." Wren's grin had no warmth in it, only edges. "I'd want to hear how he plans to square that."

I would have liked to say something cutting in return. I had been, in life, rather good at cutting things. But the truth was that Wren's barb found the soft place beneath my ribs—the place where a rib used to be—and lodged there, and I carried it down into the Tally Hall like a splinter of ice.

* * *

They came up out of the dark the way silt rises in disturbed water.

First the near ones: a dockhand whose skull had been crushed by a bale of misloaded cargo, still rubbing the back of his head as if the ache were fresh; a woman in the rotted finery of a merchant's wife, her ghost-self immaculate while her bones lay somewhere under the warehouse district; three children who had drowned in the old cistern itself, back when it held water, holding hands in a chain the way children do when they are told to hold hands. Then more, and more, rising through floors and foundations from every kept thing in the Undercity that had ever held a death inside it—a hundred, then hundreds, then a number I stopped counting because counting was a king's habit and this was not going to be a court.

They arranged themselves without arranging. That was the first thing that undid me. No herald called them. No protocol seated them by rank. They simply gathered, the way weather gathers, and looked at us—or through us—with the patient, terrible attention of people who have waited a very long time to be listened to.

At their center stood a figure I recognized, and recognizing him was like swallowing a stone.

Marek Thorne. In my reign he had been Master of the Granaries—a plain, honest, stubborn man who had come to me in the famine winter of my third year and told me, to my face, in front of my council, that my grain tariffs were killing the river districts. I had dismissed his argument. I had not dismissed *him*; I had promoted him, actually, which is the trick kings use instead of listening. Two years later he had died in the granary collapse that the crown's chroniclers blamed on rats and rot, and which I had privately suspected—then, and now—was neither.

"Your Majesty," Marek said. His voice filled the cistern without effort. Ghosts' voices carry the weight of everything unsaid. "You've come a long way to stand among us. Seven years, some might say. Others might say a lifetime."

"I've come to ask for help," I said.

"Yes." He turned—not dismissively, but deliberately, the way a man turns to face a debt he intends to name aloud. "We know why you've come. The carver's told half the Undercity, and the other half reads lips through walls." A ripple moved through the assembled dead—not laughter exactly, but the ghost of laughter, which is colder. "Before we vote on anything, there are matters to settle. Old accounts. You'll forgive us if we're thorough. We've had time to keep books."

Behind me I heard Orsen shift. The bone-carver had been hearing the dead's grievances for years, one whispering object at a time, and I wondered—not for the first time—what it cost them to hear a grievance spoken plainly, at volume, by a crowd.

"Marek," I said. "Whatever you need to say, say it. I will not defend myself tonight. I will only listen."

"That," he said, "is the first kingly thing you've done in our presence. Let's see if you can manage a second."

* * *

They spoke for a long time.

A woman named Ilse, who had laid stone on the eastern wall and fallen forty feet when the scaffolding failed, described the compensation paid to her family: eleven silver marks, less than the wall's masons spent on wine in a month. She did not raise her voice. She recited figures. She had been an accountant's daughter, she said, and numbers were how she mourned.

A fisherman named Doss spoke of the winter the crown requisitioned the fleet for war transport and his village starved within sight of full nets—because the nets were needed elsewhere, because the war mattered more, because a king's map has no room for small harbors.

An old man who had been a gate-warden spoke of executions. He had watched them all from his post, forty years of them, and he recited names—not the famous traitors, but the small ones, the poachers and debtors and one boy of fourteen hanged for stealing a loaf during the same famine winter Marek had tried to warn me about. The warden had written every name down in life, in a little book he kept under his bed, and he recited them now from memory, and the names fell into the cistern like stones into deep water, each one taking a long time to land.

Sera Voss stood through all of it with her jaw set and her fists closed, and I saw her glance once at Orsen, and once at me, and I understood that she was doing arithmetic of her own—that she had been the fist of a crown she was only now learning to see clearly, and that every name the warden spoke landed on her too.

Wren, for once, said nothing at all. The thief watched the dead with an expression I could not read, and I thought: *they steal memories from objects; they must be drowning right now, standing in a room where every grief is worn openly.* But Wren's face gave back nothing, which is its own kind of confession.

And through it all, Orsen Whitlock stood in the middle of the floor where the dead had placed them—placed, I noticed, exactly at the center, so that every ghost could see the living thing that had come to speak for the dead king—and listened. When Ilse finished her figures, Orsen nodded. When the warden finished his names, Orsen whispered each one back, softly, the way one repeats a prayer, and I realized the carver was writing them somewhere behind their eyes, keeping the ledger they had always kept, except now the entries were people.

It was nearly dawn when Marek raised his hand and the room stilled.

"The accounts are open," he said. "Not settled. Open. Now the question." He looked at me, and his ghost-face was as plain and stubborn as it had been across my council table seven years and one death ago. "The Hollow Crown sits on your throne, Aldric. It wears your face. It eats this city's warmth one winter at a time, and this winter it means to eat until nothing is left. The carver can unbind it—but only with your testimony, and only with strength enough to carry that testimony up nine floors of binding-wrought horror. We are being asked to lend that strength. Our strength. The dead's strength, given to the living, to fix a wrong the living committed against us in the first place." He spread his hands. "So we vote. But first, Your Majesty, we ask: why should we? Give us one reason that isn't about you."

* * *

I had prepared answers. Of course I had. I had been a king; preparation was the air I breathed. I had speeches sorted by audience and occasion, arguments filed like dispatches. Standing in that cistern, I reached for them and found them all to be ash in my mouth.

Because every answer I had was about the kingdom. About the city. About duty and order and the good of the many. And the dead had just spent the whole night showing me the ledger of what *the good of the many* had cost the few—the ones under the scaffolds, in the small harbors, at the end of ropes. My answers were the same coin that had bought their deaths.

So I did the only honest thing left.

I knelt.

My body was gone; kneeling should have meant nothing. But the dead understood gesture better than the living—they had nothing left *but* meaning—and when the shade of Aldric of the House of Veyra went down onto the wet stone of the Tally Hall, before the dockhand and the accountant's daughter and the warden with his book of names, the whole room went silent in a way that made the silence of my tomb seem loud.

"Aldric—" Sera started forward, and stopped, because even she could feel it: this was not a performance, and interrupting it would be a kind of vandalism.

"I cannot return your marks to your families," I said, still kneeling, speaking to the stone because I could not yet bear to speak to their faces. "I cannot unhang the boy or refill the harbor or catch you when the scaffold fell. Death does not negotiate, and neither, apparently, do I get to apologize my way out of a reign." I looked up then, at last, at Marek. "You asked for one reason that isn't about me. Here it is, and it is a poor reason, and it is the only one I have: I am asking you not to help *me*. I am asking you to help the carver—who never taxed you, who never ruled you, who has spent their whole life listening to the dead when no one else would—and the knight, who has already bled for this, and the thief, who will pay in ways none of us should wish to imagine. Help *them*. And if my testimony is useful to them, then let it be dragged along like luggage. I have no right to lead this. I am asking for the right to follow."

Silence. The long kind. The kind in which a man hears his own heartbeat, except I had none, and so I heard instead the drip of water in the dark and the vast collective attention of several hundred dead souls deciding what I was worth.

Then Ilse, the accountant's daughter, stepped forward.

"My father taught me that a ledger balances or it doesn't," she said. "There's no such thing as mostly balanced. But he also taught me that a debt acknowledged is a different thing from a debt denied. Even unpaid." She looked at me kneeling in the wet, and something in her stern ghost-face shifted by a degree. "Seven years, and no one from the palace ever said the word *debt*. Not once. Not in any direction." She turned to the assembly. "I say we lend them the strength. Not for him. For the carver. And let the king spend his afterlife working it off."

Murmuring moved through the dead like wind through wheat.

The warden with the book of names raised his hand. "On one condition." His voice cracked like old timber. "When the Crown falls—when the registry burns, and it *will* burn, I've seen the carver's face when they talk about it—every name in my book gets read aloud. Publicly. From the market cross. All four hundred and twelve. The city will know who we were."

Orsen spoke for the first time in hours. "Every name," they said quietly. "I'll carve them myself, if the living won't speak them. Bone holds words longer than paper."

Something passed through the assembly then that I can only call a turning—not unanimous, nothing among the dead is unanimous, we are too various and too stubborn—but a turning, the way a great ship comes about by degrees. The drowned children drifted closer to Orsen and stared up at them with the frank curiosity of the young. The merchant's wife began, softly, to tell the ghost beside her about her daughter, whom she had never gotten to say goodbye to, and I saw Orsen flinch and then deliberately lean toward her, listening, though answering the dead was the oldest rule the carver had broken only twice.

Marek raised his hand again. "All who lend their strength to the ascent, manifest."

And the cistern lit up.

Not with fire—with presence. Hundreds of shades brightening at once, the way a sky brightens before dawn, and the cold of the Undercity changed quality: no longer the passive cold of abandonment but a gathered cold, a chosen cold, an army's cold. I felt it settle over me and through me, and for the first time since my death I felt *heavy*, anchored, real—as if several hundred dead had decided to hold me together by sheer collective will.

"It won't last," Marek warned, as the light dimmed to a steady glow. "Strength lent is strength spent. Spend it well, spend it fast, and don't waste it on pride. The spire's bindings will fight you, and they fight dirty—they were built to make the dead betray the living." His eyes found mine. "Including dead kings."

"We leave tomorrow night," Sera said, stepping forward at last, and her voice had the ring it must have had when she commanded her company. "Wren has the undercroft keys. Orsen has the route. I have—" she touched her sword, and the blade hummed faintly with all the men she'd killed, "—enough."

"One more thing," Marek said, and his plain face grew plainer, graver. "The bindings on the lower floors are old. Founders' work. But the upper floors—" he hesitated, and around him the assembled dead stirred uneasily, a field of wheat in wind. "The upper floors have been changing. New work. Recent. There's a voice in the walls now that wasn't there before, and it knows names. It's been calling them. Calling down through the stone." He looked directly at Orsen. "Last night it called yours, carver. And it called another name with it. A name it said like it was *holding* it."

Orsen went very still. I watched the color leave their face in the lantern-light, and I heard Wren mutter a curse in a thieves' cant I almost remembered, and Sera's hand closed around her sword hilt hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

"What name?" Orsen asked. Their voice was small.

Marek's ghost-form flickered, and for a moment he looked genuinely sorry. "It said *Lys*," he said. "And then it said *come home*."

* * *

Above us, far above, snow that would not melt was beginning to fall on the city, and in the spire's high offices a girl who had once shared a childhood with Orsen Whitlock was lighting blue-flamed lamps and telling herself, as she did every night, that the Crown protected her.

She had one day left to believe it.
