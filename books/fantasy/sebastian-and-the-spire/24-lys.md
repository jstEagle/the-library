## 24. Lys

The registry office smelled of tallow and clean paper, and Lys Whitlock had come to love that smell the way a drowning woman loves the surface of the water.

She told herself that every morning, pinning her collar straight in the little brass mirror above her desk. *You are safe here.* The words had worn smooth as river stones from handling. *You are safe. You are useful. You are seen.*

The mirror showed a woman of twenty-six with ink-stained fingers and a face that had learned to be pleasant at nothing. Her hair was bound in the grey-and-white cord of the Registry's second clerks. There was a time—she could not say how long ago; the years before the spire had gone soft at their edges, like fruit left too long in a cellar—when she would have hated that cord. When she would have torn it off and laughed about it.

She did not remember laughing.

"Lys." Master of Records Dunmore stood in the doorway of the scriptorium, his shadow falling long across the desks of the other clerks. He was a soft man, Dunmore, with soft hands and a voice like wet wool, but he was kind in the way the spire permitted kindness: quietly, and never in front of witnesses. "There's a discrepancy in the third-district rolls. The winter census. I'd like your eyes on it."

"Of course, sir."

"You've a gift for discrepancies," he said, and smiled his wet-wool smile. "You always have."

*You always have.* She felt the small warm pride that sentence was designed to produce, and she let it fill her like broth. That was the trick of the spire, though she had never once said so aloud, not even to herself in the dark: it fed you exactly enough. A warm room. A title. The word *gift*, applied to her careful hands instead of to the thing in her blood they had taken away—or thought they had taken away—the day the wagons came.

She followed him down the corridor, past the tall windows where the city lay grey under a sky the color of old pewter. Snow still sat on the rooftops from three days ago. It did not melt. None of it melted anymore, and the fires burned blue at their hearts if you looked at them sideways, and the days came up out of the dark shorter than they should have been, and everyone in the registry spoke of these things in the same careful, pleasant nothing-voice, the way you speak of a limp or a scar. *Strange weather. Strange season. The Crown has it in hand.*

The Crown has it in hand. Lys believed that the way she believed in the floor beneath her feet.

The third-district rolls were spread on a table in the archive annex, a room with no windows and one lamp, and Dunmore left her alone with them, which was itself a kindness—a whole hour without anyone watching her hands.

It took her less than ten minutes to find what he wanted found. Three households marked *registered, gifted, remanded* whose follow-up entries were missing. Three families who had gone into the wagons and never come out the far side of any ledger she could find. She had found such gaps before. She always reported them. She did not ask what happened after *remanded*, because asking was the one thing the spire had ever punished faster than theft.

Her pen hovered over the correction line.

And then, faintly, from somewhere below the floor—from below the floors below that, from the deep stone throat of the spire's lower offices—she heard singing.

No. Not singing. Humming. A hunger-song, low and patient, the sound a thing makes when it is eating something very slowly and knows the meal will last all winter. The clerks said the pipes sang in cold weather. The clerks said a great many things.

Lys pressed her hand flat against the table until the humming faded, and made her corrections in her smallest, neatest hand, and did not think about wagons, and did not think about a girl with ink on her fingers who had once been someone's sister.

* * *

Orsen Whitlock came up through the coal chute at the hour when the registry's lamps were trimmed for the change of shift, when every clerk's eyes were on the door and no one's eyes were on the walls.

They had planned it in the Undercity with Sera pacing and Wren turning a stolen memory over in their fingers like a coin they weren't sure was real. *Lower offices,* Aldric had said, his voice like wind through an empty crown. *The registry keeps its living records there. If my sister-in-law's people kept lists of the taken, they will be shelved beside them.* And Sera had put her hand flat on the map and said, *If Lys is down there, you get her out. That's not negotiable. We don't leave family in the walls of this place.*

Family. The word had landed on Orsen like a stone dropped down a well, and they were still waiting to hear it hit bottom.

Now Orsen crouched in the coal store, grey with dust, wearing the borrowed livery of a lamp-oil factor, and listened to the building the way they had learned to listen to everything since the night every object in their workshop had screamed at once. The spire's lower floors were old stone, older than the palace above, and the stones remembered. They remembered boots and dragging feet and the particular weight of a body that has stopped resisting. Orsen breathed through it, the way Wren had taught them—*name it and set it down, carver, name it and set it down*—and waited for the shift-bell.

The bell rang. Boots receded. Orsen went up the service stair two steps at a time, quiet as a debt coming due.

The registry floor was a long room of desks under green-shaded lamps, empty now except for one figure at the far end, bent over a table of ledgers, her collar pinned straight, her hair bound in grey and white.

Orsen knew her instantly—not from the memory Wren had returned, which was still settling into them like a bone knitting, tender and wrong-shaped—but from somewhere older than memory. From the body. From nine years of a hole shaped exactly like that silhouette.

*Lys.*

For a moment Orsen could not move. All the careful architecture of the infiltration—the livery, the timing, Sera waiting at the coal gate with a knife and a plan B and a plan C—all of it dissolved, and what remained was a child standing in a doorway watching wagons, and a woman at a desk who had grown up into someone Orsen had never met.

Lys felt it before she saw it. Later she would tell herself she had heard the floorboard; really, she had felt it, the way you feel weather changing, the way she sometimes felt the humming rise. Someone was in the room. Someone was looking at her with a weight that had no business being in an empty registry at shift-change.

She turned.

The lamp-oil factor stood by the archive door with a hand raised halfway, as if to knock on air, and under the factor's cap and the coal dust and the nine years, the face was—

"No," Lys said. Out loud. To the empty room. "No, no—you're dead. You're both dead, they said—" And then the rest of it arrived, all at once, the way grief does: *Orsen. That's Orsen. That's my brother, that's my sister, that's the person I have spent nine years not thinking about, standing six feet away smelling of coal.*

"Lys." Orsen's voice cracked on the single syllable. "It's me. I know—I know how this looks, I don't have time, there are people waiting, but I couldn't come this close and not—"

"You can't be here." The words came out of her flat and fast, clerk's words, ledger words, and some distant part of Lys was horrified to hear them in her own voice. "This floor is restricted after the bell. Your livery isn't registered for the lower offices. If anyone—"

"Lys." Orsen crossed the room. Close now, close enough that she could see the dust in the creases of their eyes, the scar along their jaw she didn't remember giving them time to earn. "I've spent nine years not knowing your name. Do you understand that? Something was taken out of me and I only just got it back, and the first thing I did—the first thing—was come here. For you."

Something in Lys's chest turned over, slow and enormous, like ice shifting on a river.

"I'm fine," she said.

"You're not fine. You work in the building that eats people."

"The building doesn't eat people." She heard her own voice go pleasant. She heard herself do it, the tilt into the nothing-tone, and could not stop it. "That's sedition talk. That's the kind of talk that gets families—"

"Families what, Lys? Say it. Families what?"

"*Taken.* Remanded. Processed." The registry vocabulary poured out of her, smooth as ink, and underneath it something was screaming, some girl with ink-stained fingers who had once hidden under a table during the first wagon-day with her hands over her brother's ears. "It protects us. You don't understand, you've been out there, out there is where the gifted get hunted in the streets, out there is where Marr's readers burn bone-dust off every doorstep—but in here there's order. In here I'm *useful*. The Crown found me when we had nothing and it gave me a desk and a lamp and it never once—"

"It took our parents."

The room went very quiet. Even the pipes stopped humming. Or later, Lys would think the pipes had never stopped at all, and it was only her own blood in her ears.

"They were taken," she said carefully, "because unregistered gifts are dangerous. To themselves. To others. The Crown explained—it was very kind about it—they sat me down and explained, and Master Dunmore himself saw that I got the second-clerk posting even though I was nobody, even though I was a gutter-child from the third district, because the Crown *sees* people, Orsen, it sees them and it gives them a place—"

"It sold them." Orsen's voice was low and shaking. "Hale sold them to the crown decades ago and the crown fed them to something in the top of this tower, and I have touched their bones, Lys. I have *heard* them. Mother remembers your name. She says it. She's been saying it for nine years into whatever dark they put her in, and you're telling me about your desk."

Lys's hand went to her collar. Grey and white. Second clerk. She held it the way another woman might hold a crucifix.

"You don't know what it's like out there anymore," she whispered. "You think you do. You think you climbed up through a coal chute and you know this place. But I've read the files, Orsen. I file them. I know what happens in cities where the gifted aren't registered—whole streets, whole towns, burning each other's houses down over rumors. The Crown caught all of that in its hands. It caught *me*. Nine years ago I was a child in a house that smelled of bone-dust and hunger, and now I am warm, and I am safe, and my hands matter, and you want me to trade that for—for ghosts, for stories, for a sister who shows up in stolen livery talking about bones—"

"I'm your sibling, Lys."

"You're a discrepancy." The word was out before she could catch it, and she watched it land, watched Orsen flinch as if struck, and some part of her knelt down inside herself and began, quietly, to scream.

* * *

Here is what Lys had never told anyone, not Dunmore, not the confessional clerks, not the mirror: she remembered the wagons.

Everyone assumed the taken children forgot, that the registry's kindness included mercy of memory, and mostly it did—there was a tea, on the first night, sweet and heavy, and after it the years went soft. But Lys had pretended to drink hers. She had been eleven and stubborn and frightened of strange sweetness, and so she remembered the wagon, and the man with the reading-glasses who touched her wrist with bone-dust on his fingers and said *this one's strong, mark the roll twice*, and she remembered the last thing she saw of her family, which was Orsen's face in the window of the shop, getting smaller, getting smaller, getting smaller.

She had built nine years on top of that window. Desk, lamp, collar, corrections in her smallest hand. A life laid brick by brick over a hole, and she had called the bricks safety, and the Crown had let her call it that, because the Crown understood better than anyone alive that people will pay anything for a story in which they were saved.

And now the hole had walked in through the coal chute wearing a dead sibling's face, and it was saying: *the bricks are lies, the window was real, come back to the cold.*

"I can't," she said aloud. Her voice sounded very young. "I can't go with you. Whatever you're doing down here, whatever you and your knight and your thief are climbing toward—I have a posting. I have a pension accruing. I have—" Her breath hitched. "I have survived, Orsen. Do you know how hard that was? Do you know what it cost? And you'd have me throw it away on a feeling."

"Not a feeling." Orsen stepped closer, and Lys saw that they were crying, silently, the way they'd cried as a child, without noise, as if tears were also something that had to be kept invisible. "The truth. Mother's bones are in a vault under the east wing. Father's are with them. Aldric Vane—"

"Aldric Vane is *dead*." Sharp now, scandalized, clerk to the marrow. "Seven years. There was a state funeral. I filed the condolence registers myself."

"And I've shaken his ghost's hand." Orsen almost laughed, a broken sound. "He's up in the Undercity right now, Lys, waiting for us, and he remembers being murdered by the thing that wears his crown, and when this is done there will be no more wagons, no more registries, no more humming in the pipes. Come with me. Right now. Down the chute, out the coal gate, there's a woman named Sera who'd die before she let anything touch you, and a thief who'd steal your worst memory and sell it back to you at cost. Please. I didn't cross half this dying city to find out my sister files papers for the thing that ate our parents."

*I'm not your sister,* said the clerk. *Come with me,* said the girl under the table. Lys stood between them in her grey-and-white cord, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere above them, floors and floors up, something in the spire turned over in its long winter sleep.

And that was when she heard the boots.

Not the shift-change boots—she knew those, she could catalog those in her sleep. These were measured. Escorted. Two sets, descending the outer stair toward the archive annex, and one of them had the particular soft, deliberate tread of a man who had never once hurried in his life because the world hurried for him.

Master Dunmore. And with him—her stomach dropped through nine floors of stone—with him, by the cadence, someone from the upper offices. Someone with authority to be on this floor after the bell.

Later, Lys would replay this moment ten thousand times. The boots. The lamp. Orsen's dirty, tear-tracked, hopeful face. The chute thirty feet away, the coal gate beyond it, Sera with her knife and her plan C. Thirty feet. She could have said nothing. She could have coughed. She could have simply stood aside, silent, and let the moment pass the way she had let a thousand discrepancies pass unasked-about.

Instead, Lys Whitlock heard herself say, in a clear, carrying, second-clerk voice, pitched perfectly for the doorway:

"Master Dunmore? There's an intruder on the registry floor."

* * *

The next sixty seconds happened to Orsen rather than around them.

Boots filled the archway. A whistle blew somewhere above. Hands—Dunmore's soft hands and harder ones behind them—took Orsen's arms, and the lamp-oil livery was torn open, and someone said *bone-carver, it's the bone-carver, Marr's warrant covers this one* in a voice of genuine excitement, the excitement of men who have caught something rare.

Orsen did not fight. That was the worst of it, Lys thought, nailed to the floor behind her desk, her hand still raised from the betrayal as if the gesture had frozen there. Orsen did not fight. They only looked at her over the shoulders of the guards, and their eyes were not angry, which would have been bearable. Their eyes were full of a terrible, dawning understanding, the look of a bone-carver reading an object they had hoped would hold something kinder.

"Oh, Lys," they said. Just that. Two syllables, and nine years in them.

Then the guards turned them toward the stair, toward the upper offices, toward the inquisitor's rooms where the lamps burned late and the questions were courteous and awful, and Orsen Whitlock was taken out of Lys's sight the same way their parents had been taken out of a shop window—getting smaller, getting smaller.

The room emptied. The boots receded. The humming in the pipes resumed, patient, satisfied, mid-meal.

Lys stood alone among the green lamps with her hand still raised, and slowly, joint by joint, she lowered it. On the table before her lay the third-district rolls, open to her neat corrections. Three households. *Remanded.* No further entries.

She looked at her own handwriting for a long time.

Then she picked up her pen, dipped it, and wrote in the margin of the roll, in her smallest, steadiest hand, the first true entry she had made in nine years:

*Witness: L. Whitlock. Statement withheld.*

And downstairs, in a cell of quiet stone, her sibling was already being asked their first question—and answering it with a lie so flawless that even the bone-dust trembled.
