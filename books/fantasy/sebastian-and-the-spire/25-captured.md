## 25. Captured

They did not drag me.

That was the first cruelty, though I didn't understand it yet. Four guards in the spire's grey-and-gold, and not one of them put a hand on me. They simply formed a corridor in Lys's office doorway and waited, and the waiting had a shape to it, patient as a trap that has already sprung. Inquisitor Marr's people never hurried. Hurrying implied doubt.

"Walk," said the sergeant, not unkindly. That was the second cruelty. Kindness from the machinery of the hunt was worse than boots.

I walked. Down the stairs I had climbed an hour ago with my heart hammering out my sister's name, past clerks who did not look up from their ledgers—clerks who wrote everything down, I knew now, including the hour of my arrival and the color of my coat—and out through the spire's lower doors into a courtyard where a black carriage waited with no crest on its door, because everyone in Vharen knew whose carriage it was, and a crest would have been gilding.

The last thing I saw before they shut the door was a window on the third floor. Lys stood behind the glass. She did not wave. She did not turn away either. She just watched me go, her hand flat against the pane, the way you press a hand to a wound to keep something in—or keep something out.

I told myself she was holding the gate open in her mind. I told myself a great many things in that carriage.

* * *

Marr received me in a room with no instruments.

I had expected the bone-dust, the reading bowls, the little silver scoops they used at registration days to take a scraping from behind the ear. I had expected straps, maybe. Every gifted child in the guild's care grew up on stories of what happened below the Inquisition's offices, and every story had straps in it somewhere.

Instead: a fire, a table set for two, tea things in white porcelain, and Inquisitor Edran Marr standing at the window with his hands folded behind his back like a man admiring weather he'd personally ordered.

"You'll want to sit," he said, without turning. "You've had a long day. Finding your sister after so many years—I can only imagine."

"You can't," I said.

"No." He turned then, and his face was exactly as it had been in the Gilderscale's counting room: courteous, attentive, utterly without hurry. "I can't. That's rather the point of me. Sit, Master Whitlock. Or Orsen, if you prefer. I've read your file. There isn't much of it, which is itself remarkable. Twenty years in the Gilderscale and barely a rumor. Do you know how rare that is? Most of your kind leave traces. A healed wound here, a too-lucky merchant there. You left nothing. Like a man walking through snow and leaving no prints."

"I swept behind myself."

"Yes." He smiled, and sat, and poured the tea himself, which surprised me more than the straps would have. "Hale taught you that. Don't look shocked—he confessed a great deal under questioning. Not all of it useful to him. He spoke about you quite fondly, actually. *The best pair of hands the guild ever kept quiet.* His words."

"Hale sells people."

"Hale sold people who were going to be found anyway," Marr said mildly, "and fed his own family on the difference for twenty years. I don't say it to defend him. I say it because you should understand that everyone in this city makes the same bargain eventually. Some just get better terms than others." He pushed the cup across the table toward me. Chamomile. He'd asked someone, or guessed, or read it off some ledger of small preferences the Inquisition kept on everyone. "Drink. It isn't drugged. If I wanted you docile I'd have had them hit you harder at the arrest, and if I wanted you pliable I wouldn't be serving tea. I want you awake. This conversation requires an awake man."

I didn't touch the cup. He nodded as if I'd passed a test.

* * *

"Here is what happens next," Marr said, "in the version you're expecting. You refuse to register. I remand you to assessment. Assessment is a word we chose carefully—it sounds clinical, doesn't it, almost gentle—but what it means is that we take you apart layer by layer to catalogue what you are, and when we're finished, what's left of you does whatever work the Crown finds for the remainder. Some of the remainder sweeps floors. Some of the remainder stands in the registry hall wearing a placard. The dead you hear—" he watched me flinch, and noted the flinch, filed it away behind those grey eyes, "—the dead you hear will not be in attendance. We have rooms where nothing speaks. You'd hate them, I think. Or love them. I genuinely don't know which, and that uncertainty is one of the few pleasures this work still affords me."

"And the other version?"

"The other version is why I cleared my evening."

He rose, went to a cabinet against the wall, and came back with something wrapped in grey cloth. He laid it on the table between us with the care of a surgeon laying out tools, and unwrapped it, and my whole body went cold before my mind caught up to what I was seeing.

Bone. Carved bone—a fragment of a greater working, dense with script, the characters cut so fine they might have been etched by a hair. Even wrapped in wool and warded, even silent, I could feel it the way you feel a storm front coming: pressure behind the eyes, a taste like old pennies.

"Do you know what this is?" Marr asked.

"No."

"That's the first honest answer you've given me, so let me return the courtesy. Neither do I. Not fully. It's a binding-fragment, recovered from the upper floors of the spire during renovations seven years ago. It predates the current reign. It predates the last three reigns. Our scholars have spent years on it and produced nothing but headaches and two resignations." He turned it slowly, letting the firelight move across the script. "But our scholars can't *hear*. They read the marks and the marks mean nothing, because whoever cut them wasn't writing for eyes. They were writing for—"

"Listeners," I said.

"For listeners." He sat back. "There are perhaps four people alive in Vharen who could read this properly. Two are registered and work for us already, and between them they've managed to confirm that it's a binding and almost nothing else. One died last month—we think by his own hand; the note was very apologetic. And the fourth is sitting in my chair refusing tea."

My mouth had gone dry. "You want me to read it."

"I want you to do a great deal more than read it." He leaned forward, and for the first time the courtesy dropped away—not into cruelty, but into something worse: sincerity. "Master Whitlock. Orsen. I'm going to tell you what I believe, and you may decide for yourself whether I'm lying, since lying to power seems to be a specialty of yours. I believe the Crown is dying. Not the king—the Crown. The institution. Something is wrong at the top of this city and has been wrong for years, and the hunt for the gifted, the registries, the assessments—all of it—is triage. Crude, ugly triage, but triage. We are trying to find the people who can perceive what we cannot, because something in this city is being held together by bindings older than anyone living understands, and those bindings are failing."

The fragment sat between us, humming its silent hum.

"You felt it in the market," he said. "Don't bother denying it; the day the proclamation was read, three registered listeners in this building reported the same thing—the objects got louder. The dead got frightened. Whatever holds the winter back from this city, whatever keeps the harvests coming and the fires burning ordinary colors, is fraying. And when it goes, Orsen, it won't go quietly. It will go the way the north kingdoms went, eighty years ago. Have you read about Harrowmere?"

Everyone had read about Harrowmere. A city of forty thousand, and one spring morning the snow simply stopped melting, and the fires burned blue, and by midwinter there was no one left to write the chronicle.

"The Hollow Crown," I said, before I could stop myself.

Something moved behind Marr's eyes—not surprise. Satisfaction, maybe, or hunger. "Where did you hear that name?"

"A ghost told me."

He laughed—one short, genuine bark of a laugh, quickly mastered. "Of course it did. Of course. You collect testimony from the grave and you expect me to dismiss it as fever." He rubbed his jaw. "Yes. The Hollow Crown. The term appears in three documents, all pre-founding, all fragmentary. We believe it refers to the binding at the summit of the spire. The original pact. Whatever the founders traded to keep this valley livable, it lives up there, and it is *hungry*, and lately it has been eating faster than the seasons can feed it. Snow that doesn't melt. Blue flames. Days shortening past reason. You've seen the reports same as I have—or you've seen the sky, which is the same document written larger."

"And you want me to strengthen it."

"I want you to *read* it first. Understand it. Then yes—strengthen it, repair it, do whatever the reading tells us must be done. You would work at the summit, Master Whitlock. You would see the thing itself. No listener in two hundred years has been given that access." He spread his hands. "And in exchange—"

Here it came. I braced for it the way you brace for cold water.

"—registration. Real registration, not the placard-and-sweeping kind. Quarters in the spire. Your sister's debts, which I notice are considerable, dissolved. Her position secured—she's competent, they speak well of her, and she has suffered the misfortune of loving you, which counts against her in ways she doesn't deserve. Protection for the knight Voss and the thief Quill, insofar as protection from us means anything once they're in our custody—which, I'll be honest with you, is a coin flip. But a coin flip is better than a certainty, and the certainty otherwise is the assessment rooms." He paused. "And one thing more. The name of the fourth listener who died. He left papers. Coded. We believe he learned something about the binding that he found unbearable. If you climb, you will learn it too, and I would rather you learn it with a salary and a warm room than alone in the dark with nothing."

* * *

I looked at the tea. I looked at the bone.

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever quiet waits for me at the end of all this: for a moment—for one long, shameful, human moment—I wanted it.

Not the money. Not even Lys's safety, though God forgive me, that was part of it, that was the lever he'd chosen and chosen well. What I wanted was smaller and stranger. I wanted to stop running. I wanted a room with a fire in it and a task with edges, wanted to be *useful* instead of *hunted*, wanted the exhaustion of the last months to resolve into something with a schedule and a salary and a door that locked from the inside. Marr had understood something about me that I hadn't fully understood about myself: that a lifetime of hiding builds a person who dreams, secretly, shamefully, of being found—of being found by someone who says *ah, there you are, we've been looking for you, come inside.*

Every kindness I'd ever trusted had been a ledger entry. Hale's shelter, priced. The guild's warmth, invoiced. And here was the crown's own inquisitor pouring chamomile and saying *we need you*, and some starved animal in my chest lifted its head at the sound like it was a mother's voice.

"You're good at this," I said hoarsely.

"I'm sincere," Marr said. "It's worse. Anyone can be good at this. Sincerity is rarer, and it cuts deeper, and I won't apologize for it. Yes, I need you. Yes, the offer is real. And yes—if you say no, I will do everything I described, and I will sleep soundly after, because the alternative to using people like you is losing cities like this one." He tapped the bone fragment once, gently. "The founders made a bargain with a famine, Orsen. Someone has to hold up their end. I would vastly prefer it were a volunteer."

"And if the reading tells me the binding shouldn't be strengthened?" I watched him carefully. "If it tells me it should be *ended*?"

For the first time, Inquisitor Edran Marr hesitated.

It was a small hesitation—a breath's worth, a candle-flame flicker—but I had spent my life reading the pauses between words, the silences objects keep around the truths their owners couldn't say aloud, and I knew what I was seeing. He had an answer prepared for that question. He decided, in that flicker, not to use it.

"Then we will have a very different conversation," he said, "with different participants. Read first. Judge after. That's all I ask of anyone, and it's more than most of my profession asks of yours."

* * *

They took the bone away when I didn't answer. Of course they did. It had done its work; the wanting was planted, and wanting doesn't need the object to keep growing.

A guard escorted me down corridors I tried to memorize and failed at deliberately—left, right, down, a landing where the air changed temperature, a stretch where the stones under my feet went strangely, horribly mute. Dead stone. Warded stone. Stone that had been made deaf on purpose, the way you'd blind a horse to make it steady.

At the end of the mute stretch stood a door with no handle on the outside.

Marr walked beside me the whole way. At the door he stopped, and offered his hand, and I looked at it, and he withdrew it without offense.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your sister argued for you. Loudly. She was ordered out of the room twice." He studied my face, hunting for the effect of that, and I gave him nothing, because giving him nothing was the last thing I owned. "She believes in you, Orsen Whitlock. So do I, in my fashion. Think about which of us that should frighten more."

The door opened from within. Beyond it: a small stone room. A cot. A basin. A single candle, already lit, waiting for me as though the room had known I was coming—as though the room had been *expecting* me, the way everything in this city had been expecting me, patiently, for years.

No straps. No instruments. Just silence.

I stepped inside, and the door closed, and the silence closed over me like water—

—and it was wrong. Wrong in a way I didn't yet have words for, wrong the way a room is wrong when a clock you've heard tick for twenty years has stopped. I pressed my palm to the wall, out of habit, out of twenty years of habit, reaching for the low murmur of the dead that lived in everything humans had ever touched.

Nothing.

Not quiet. Quiet implies a hush, a held breath, a *listening*. This was absence. A hole in the world shaped exactly like my gift, and as I stood there with my hand flat against the deaf stone, understanding crept up through the floor of me like cold:

Marr hadn't given me a cell.

He'd given me a preview. A night alone with what surrender would cost, served up in advance, so that in the morning—when he came back with the tea and the bone and the smile—I would be that much closer to saying yes.

I lay down on the cot in the dark and the terrible nothing, and I held Lys's face in my mind, and Sera's blade-song, and Wren's crooked grin, and Aldric's patient grief, and I repeated them like a rosary, names against the void.

And somewhere far above me—at the summit of the spire, in the dark above the dark—something vast and starving turned over in its sleep, and dreamed of warmth, and I swear to God I felt it dream.
