## 27. Sera and Wren Break the Spire's Back Door

The spire's back door was not a door. That was the first thing Wren had to explain, crouched in the shadow of a tannery wall while sleet needled down through the blue-lit dark.

"It's a memory," they said. "A door that only exists if you remember it. The founders built it that way so no one could find it by looking."

Sera Voss shifted her weight against the cold stone and watched the undercrofts below—the sunken annexes where the crown's clerks came and went through a gate of black iron, guarded by four men with halberds and one man with a lantern that burned without color. "Then how do we remember it?"

Wren smiled, and in the blue light their face looked younger and older at once, the way a coin looks when it's been worn smooth. "I stole it. Years ago. From an architect who helped build the annexes and then regretted it so thoroughly that he drank himself into the canal." They tapped their own temple. "It's still in here. I just have to put it back somewhere it can do work."

"You're going to *give away* a stolen memory," Sera said. "You hoard them like a dragon. You told me yourself—you've never given one back for free."

"I'm not giving it back." Wren's smile thinned. "I'm spending it. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'll still have the hole afterward, I just won't have what fills it." They flexed their fingers, and Sera saw—she was learning to see it now—that they were afraid. Not of the guards. Of the emptying.

"Orsen needs us," she said.

"Orsen needs us," Wren agreed. "That's the terrible part. I liked being someone who needed nothing."

Below them, the colorless lantern swung once, twice, and stopped. The guard holding it turned his head slowly, like a man listening to water in a well.

* * *

SERA

She had walked these annexes before. That was the thing she hadn't told Orsen, hadn't told anyone: seven years ago, as a knight of the second rank with a clean name and a commander she trusted, Sera Voss had stood post in the undercroft corridor on the night the old king's body was carried down from the spire in a box too small for him.

She remembered the box. She remembered thinking, *that's wrong, that's too short*, and she remembered Commander Dain Roke putting a hand on her shoulder and saying, "You saw nothing, Voss. That's an order, and it's the last easy order you'll ever get."

She had obeyed. That was the rot in her, the thing she'd carried out of the service like shrapnel: not that she'd refused some monstrous command—that was the story she told, the cashiering, the honorable refusal—but that first, years earlier, she had obeyed a small one. She had seen nothing because she'd been ordered to, and her eyes had cooperated.

Tonight her eyes were going to cooperate with her instead.

"Positions," she breathed, and moved.

The plan was ugly and simple, which were the only plans that worked. Wren would make the door. Sera would make the noise. The Undercity smugglers—six of them, led by a scarred woman called Brack who smelled of pitch and grudges—would make the diversion at the iron gate, loud enough to pull the halberds, quick enough to leave before the spire's garrison woke properly. And then Sera and Wren would go through a door that did not exist, down into the binding-wrought dark, and open the way for Orsen and the dead king.

Simple. She said it to herself three times, the way you say a prayer you don't believe, and drew her sword.

The blade sang low in its sheath—not ringing, but humming, the way it always did now. Every man she'd killed lived in that steel, and lately they had stopped being accusations and started being company. *Left side first,* murmured a voice like wet gravel, a man she'd cut down at Marrow Ford eleven years ago. *There's a fourth one behind the cart.*

"I know," she whispered.

*You don't know. You never know until we tell you.* A pause, almost gentle. *But you listen now. That's new.*

Brack's diversion began with a scream—one of her people, a wiry boy, rolling out of the alley clutching his leg and howling about the blue snow, the blue snow, it's eating through my boot—and the guards at the iron gate did exactly what trained men do, which was converge on the obvious. The colorless lantern swung toward the noise. And in the two heartbeats of darkness its turning left against the tannery wall, Wren stood up, pressed both palms flat against blank stone, and *remembered*.

Sera felt it happen. She couldn't have said how—there was no light, no sound—but the air changed the way it changes when a struck bell is deciding whether to ring. Then the stone rippled, once, like the skin of a drum, and there was a door.

Not a grand one. A servant's door, oak gone grey, iron hinges furred with frost, set into the wall as if it had always been there and everyone had simply forgotten to use it. Which, Sera supposed, was true.

Wren swayed. Sera caught their elbow. Their eyes had gone glassy and strange, pupils blown wide, and when they spoke their voice came out flattened, like a page read aloud by someone who has forgotten what reading is for.

"There was a man," Wren said. "He had ink on his fingers and he was crying and he kept saying *it wasn't meant to hold a person, it was meant to hold a winter*. He showed me this door. He made me promise—" They blinked. The flatness cracked, and something like grief poured through the crack. "I don't remember what he made me promise. It's gone. I spent it."

"Wren—"

"Don't." They straightened, wiped their nose with the back of their hand, and managed something that was almost their usual crooked grin. "Door's open. Doors don't care if the opener is sad. Move."

They moved.

* * *

WREN

Inside, the annexes smelled of tallow and old paper and, underneath both, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a sealed room. Wren knew that smell. Memory had a smell, when it was being stored rather than lived—dry, mineral, patient—and the whole undercroft was steeped in it. The crown wasn't just keeping records down here. It was keeping *years*.

They descended a stair that spiraled the wrong way, built for someone descending who wanted to feel the wall on their right hand, and Wren counted doors. Nine landings. On each landing, a warded arch, and behind each arch, according to the architect's spent memory—which Wren could still feel the shape of, the way you feel the shape of a tooth after it's pulled—a vault of kept things. Confiscated gifts. Confiscated gifts' owners, sometimes. The registry didn't just take names.

At the third landing, the warding noticed them.

It came as a pressure, a slow tightening behind the eyes, and Wren recognized it with professional horror: a memory-lock, keyed to fear. It reached into the skull and started pulling—showing you your worst recollections, offering them up, demanding them as toll. Wren had picked locks like this from the outside a hundred times. Being inside one was different. Being inside one was drowning in your own attic.

*Sera,* they tried to say, and couldn't; the lock had taken their voice along with everything else, and what it left in exchange was—

—a kitchen. A woman stirring something. A child on a stool. *Mine*, thought Wren, *that's mine, give it back, that's the one I kept, the only one I kept—*

Steel rang. The pressure shattered.

Sera stood over them, sword bare, and the blade was blazing faintly with all its dead, every ghost in the steel lit at once like a window full of candles. She had struck the ward-arch itself, once, precisely, and the lock had recoiled from the blade the way a hand recoils from a stove.

"The sword remembers worse things than you do," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "It told me where to hit."

*We are useful after all,* said the gravel-voice, smugly.

Wren got up. Their legs shook. Somewhere in their head there was a new hole, small and round and aching, shaped like a kitchen. They catalogued it, sealed it, filed it with the others—the sister-shaped one, the sea-shaped one, the one that was just the word *home* repeated until it lost meaning—and looked at Sera.

"You'll want to hit every arch between here and the bottom," they rasped. "Don't. Only the locks that reach. The rest are alarms wearing locks as costumes."

"How can you tell?"

"Because I'm a thief," Wren said, "and thieves know the difference between a door and a mouth."

* * *

SERA

The fifth landing was guarded, and the guard was Dain Roke.

Sera knew before she saw him. She knew by the cadence of boots on stone, unhurried, heel-first, the walk of a man who had never in his life needed to hurry because the world arranged itself around him. Seven years since she'd heard it and her spine remembered it instantly, the way a dog remembers a whistle.

He stood at the foot of the next stair with six soldiers behind him, and the colorless lantern-light made his face look carved rather than grown. Older. Greyer at the temples. But the same. Of course the same. Men like Roke didn't wear out; they were finished objects from the start.

"Voss." He said her name the way he'd said it across a hundred muster-yards: mildly, with the smallest weight of disappointment, as if she were a report filed late. "The registry flagged you crossing the city two nights ago. I told them it was a mistake. I said, *Voss is many things, but she isn't stupid.*"

"Kind of you."

"It wasn't kindness. It was bookkeeping." He stepped forward, and his soldiers spread with drilled ease, cutting the stair. "Here's what happens now. You put down the sword. You come upstairs. Marr will offer you reinstatement—full rank, restored name, back-pay. All you have to do is tell her where the bone-carver is, and who else is in the tunnel with you, and what the ghost wants." His eyes flicked past her shoulder, dismissive, to where Wren crouched in the shadows. "And the little pickpocket can run. I don't waste paper on thieves."

Sera looked at him. She had imagined this moment for seven years—in the bad years she'd imagined it nightly, rehearsed speeches, imagined drawing steel, imagined justice arriving with trumpets. Now it was here and it was smaller than she'd imagined, and colder, and she found that all her rehearsed speeches had burned away and left only one sentence.

"You were there," she said. "The night they carried the king down. In the box that was too short."

Something moved behind Roke's eyes. It was very fast and very deep, like a fish under ice, and then it was gone, and his face was a parade ground again. "You saw nothing, Voss."

"That's what you told me. And I obeyed. That's the difference between us—I obeyed, and it hollowed me, and I've spent seven years climbing out of the hole. You gave the order, and it fed you." She lifted the sword. Its dead rose with it, a low chord of voices, and she let them speak through her because they were right and she was done pretending otherwise. "Marr will offer me my name back. Here's what I learned in the Undercity, Commander: a name you buy back with someone else's blood isn't yours. It's theirs, on loan, and the interest is everything you are."

Roke sighed. Genuinely, wearily sighed, and drew his own blade, and it was a beautiful thing, mirror-bright, and Sera understood suddenly that it was bright because it had never once been allowed to forget whose hand held it. No ghosts in that steel. Nothing kept. Nothing remembered.

"You were the best of them," he said, and it sounded almost true. "That's why this disappoints me."

"Come on, then," Sera said. "Disappoint yourself."

He came on.

Fighting Roke was fighting a lesson she'd memorized. Every opening he offered was a trap; every trap was an invitation to be somewhere she shouldn't. He fought the way he commanded—by making the other person's choices for them. Left feint, high line, the little half-drop of the shoulder that said *thrust coming* and lied. She had died a hundred times in drills against that lie.

But she had a hundred teachers now whispering in her wrist.

*Marrow Ford,* said the gravel voice. *He fights like the ford-man. Wait for the drop. It lies.*

*He leads with his right hip when he means it,* said another, a woman's voice, cool as well-water. *When he feints, the hip stays home.*

The hip stayed home. The thrust came anyway, beautiful and false, and Sera wasn't there. She turned inside it—inside his reach, where his length meant nothing—and put her shoulder into his chest and her boot behind his knee, and for one instant they were chest to chest, close enough to share breath, and she said, quietly, only for him:

"I'm not going to become you. That was the whole war, and I just won it."

Then she threw him down the stair.

He fell badly—seven years of desks had taken the fall-training out of him—and lay crumpled against the wall, winded, sword skittering. His soldiers hesitated, and in that hesitation Brack's people came boiling up the service stair behind them with cudgels and chains and the specific fury of people who had been invisible their whole lives, and the hesitation ended.

Sera stood over Roke. He looked up at her, blood in his mouth, and there it was again, the fish under ice: fear. Not of her sword. Of her answer.

"Kill me, then," he said. "Finish the story the way it's supposed to go."

"No." She knelt, took the colorless lantern from beside him—it had rolled free—and crushed it under her heel. Color flooded back into the world, warm and ordinary, torchlight yellow. "You live, and you remember tonight. That's the sentence. I'm told remembering is the worst thing this building does to people."

She left him there, groaning among his scattered soldiers, and ran down into the dark after Wren.

* * *

WREN

The bottom of the undercroft was a door of a different kind entirely: iron, banded, tall as two men, covered in script that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. The binding-writ. Wren had seen forged versions in collectors' cabinets, sold to idiots as decoration. The real thing made the forgery look like a child's drawing of fire.

"This is it," they said. "The back door to the spire's roots. Beyond this, the floors start taking payment." They turned. Sera stood in the doorway behind them, breathing hard, a cut on her cheekbone already freezing in the cold, and behind her, coming down the stairs with a lantern of honest flame, were Orsen Whitlock and a presence that made the torch bend.

The ghost-king did not walk so much as arrive continuously, like weather. Even dimmed, even worn thin by seven years of testimony, Aldric filled the stairwell the way a bell fills a tower. Orsen looked small beside him and exhausted and absolutely certain, and Wren—who had spent a lifetime being certain of nothing except the contents of other people's heads—felt something in their chest turn over.

"Well?" Orsen said. "Did it work?"

"We're standing in it, aren't we." Wren pressed their palm to the iron. The script writhed away from their skin, tasting them, and found—of course it found—the holes. All the holes. A life spent paying itself out. The lock hummed, considering, and Wren laughed, a small cracked sound. "It doesn't know what to charge me. I'm mostly absence. Thieves' privilege."

Then the door began to open, and the warmth of whatever waited above spilled down the stairs like water finding a crack, and Wren Quill, who had stolen ten thousand memories and kept none of their own, stepped through first—because someone had to, and because courage, they were beginning to understand, was not a thing you had.

It was a thing you did. Again. And then again.

Behind them, far above, a bell began to ring, and the whole spire woke like an eye.

The ninth floor was waiting, and it already knew their names.
