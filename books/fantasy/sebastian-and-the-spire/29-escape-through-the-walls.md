## 29. Escape Through the Walls

The cell they threw me into was not a cell at all. It was a throat.

I knew it the moment they dragged me through it—down stairs that had been climbed by ten thousand condemned feet, past iron doors whose hinges wept rust like old blood, into a corridor where the stones had been laid by masons who knew exactly what this place would become. The Spire of Vail had been built as a watchtower once, before the Hollow Crown, before the binding. Then someone had looked at its height and its cold and thought: *executions*. And the stones had learned.

They remembered everyone.

I felt them the way I'd felt every object I'd ever touched, but scaled up until it wasn't hearing anymore—it was drowning. Names without faces. Faces without names. A baker from the Ash Quarter who'd been taken for blessing bread with a charm his grandmother taught him. A girl of maybe fourteen, drowned in a rain barrel for making flowers bloom out of season. Three brothers hanged on the same morning for the crime of singing an old song about a river that no longer existed. The walls held them all, pressed flat and patient, and when my bare palm scraped the stone as the guards shoved me forward, they *screamed*—not in pain, in recognition.

*One of us. One of us. One of us.*

"Move," said the guard, and struck me between the shoulders with a gauntleted fist.

I moved. What else could I do? My wrists were bound behind me with wire that bit to the bone. Sera was somewhere above or below—I didn't know which; the last thing I'd seen clearly was her going down under three halberds in the undercroft while Wren pulled me toward the wall and the wall, traitorously, remembered how to be solid. Marr's people had taken Aldric's bones back into custody too, wrapped in lead-lined cloth that even now hummed against my awareness somewhere far overhead, a mute weight in a tower of noise.

And Lys. Lys had opened the inner gate and stayed behind to hold it, and I did not know if she was alive.

So yes. I moved. But as I moved, I listened.

That was the thing Marr had never understood, in all our courteous conversations across her ledger-lit table. She thought my gift was a tool—something you could aim, or better, something you could take away. She'd built the Cell of Quiet to prove it: a room warded so thoroughly that not even a whisper of the dead could reach inside. She'd put me there for two days once, early in my captivity, to soften me. And she'd been right about what silence does. It strips you. It shows you the shape of everything the voices were holding up.

But she'd made one mistake, and I had been turning it over in my mind ever since like a coin I couldn't stop biting.

She'd let me out.

She'd let me out because she needed me to read bindings, and the moment the ward-door opened, the dead came back—and they came back *angry*. Not at me. At her. At the crown. At seven years of a famine-spirit drinking the city's warmth through a dead king's mouth while heralds read proclamations about registration and harvest festivals went ahead in streets where the snow never melted. Every ghost in this spire had died under that crown's authority. Every single one. And now their murderer's jailers had locked me—the one living soul who could hear them—in a corridor built from their own compressed grief.

I stopped walking.

The guard hit me again, harder. "I said move."

"I'm listening," I said.

He laughed. I heard it the way you hear weather—distant, irrelevant. Because beneath his laugh, beneath the drip of water and the shuffle of his boots, the walls were speaking, and for the first time in my life I wasn't trying to quiet them. I was asking.

*I know,* I told them. *I know what they did to you. I've heard your names in the ledgers. Some of them I wrote myself, before I understood what the ledgers were for.*

The screaming faltered. Shifted. Became something else—attention, vast and terrible, like a forest going still around a hunter.

*You wrote?*

*Ledgers of small impossible things,* I said. *Objects the guild bought and sold. Your rings. Your buttons. Your mother's hair combs. I heard every one of you, and I kept your names in ink because no one else would.* My voice shook. I let it. There was no one here to see weakness except the dead, and the dead had never once mistaken my tears for anything but what they were. *I can't undo it. But I can open this door.*

*There is no door,* said the baker, in the flat tone of a man who has been dead thirty years and has counted every one. *We are the wall.*

*Then un-be the wall.*

Silence. Even the water seemed to hold itself mid-drip.

I knelt, awkward with bound hands, and pressed my forehead to the stone. It was cold the way the Cell of Quiet had been cold—but this cold was full, crowded, alive. "Listen," I said aloud, because some things need breath behind them. "Seven years ago a king was murdered in this tower and something else put on his shape. It eats warmth. It eats summers. This winter will not end while it sits on that throne. You were executed by its order—or by the order of the men who serve it. Either way—" I swallowed. "Either way, it owes you. And I am here to collect."

Nothing. Long enough that doubt began its familiar climb up my spine.

Then, softly, from somewhere deep in the masonry—a sound I had never heard from the dead before, though I would come to know it well in the days after.

Laughter.

*Oh,* said the girl with the rain barrel, and her voice was young and sharp as a fish knife. *Oh, the little ledger-keeper wants to make a withdrawal.*

*Can you pay?* asked the baker.

*What's the price?* I asked.

*Witnesses,* said a new voice—an old woman's, dry as paper. *We have been dying in this dark with no one to write it down. Seven years of the false king. Thirty years of Marr's master before him. A hundred years of executions, and the histories say the spire is empty.* The old woman's presence pressed close against my cheek, cold as a windowpane in frost. *Open the way, bone-carver, and we will walk these halls again. Let them see us. Let them *know* us. That is the price. That is the only price.*

"To witness," I whispered. Old word. Guild-word. The first law of my trade, the one Hale had taught me with such warmth: *a thing unwitnessed is a thing unforgiven.* He'd meant it as bookkeeping. The dead meant it as revolution.

"Yes," I said. "Yes. All of it. Every name."

The wall breathed.

I don't have a better word for it, though I've tried since, in quieter hours, to find one. The stones drew inward like lungs filling, and the mortar between them went grey and soft as ash, and then—where a hand's breadth of solid rock had been—there was a gap. An opening. A door that had never been carved, shaped like nothing, shaped like grief given permission.

Beyond it, the corridor continued. Torchlit. Guarded. Full of the sounds of a fortress preparing for war it didn't yet know had arrived.

And pouring out of the gap behind me, silent as smoke and bright as frost—

Them.

* * *

The first guard saw nothing, because the dead chose not to be seen—not yet. They flowed past him like cold air off a river, and I watched him shiver, frown, pull his cloak tighter, and keep walking his post. Patience, the girl-with-the-rain-barrel murmured beside me, and her voice was in my ear and in the stone and everywhere at once. *Patience, ledger-keeper. First the doors. Then the eyes.*

My hands were still bound. That was the absurdity of it—I stood in the middle of a ghost-army hundreds strong, and I couldn't so much as scratch my nose. So I did the next best thing. I walked.

The corridor bent left, following the curve of the spire's root, and I followed the bend because the old woman told me to. *Armory stair,* she said. *Two guards. Beyond it, the wardens' gallery, and beyond that—* she paused, savoring it, a connoisseur of the building's cruelties *—the record hall. Where they keep the execution rolls. Our names. Our crimes. Written in their hand.*

"And the cells?" I asked. "Sera Voss. The thief called Wren. Are they—"

*Below,* said the baker. *The wet cells. We can show you. We cannot open locks.*

"No," I said. "But I know someone who can." Wren had stolen half the spire's memories already—I'd watched them press a palm to a lock last month and wear the jailer's knowledge of it like a borrowed coat. If Wren was alive. If Wren was whole. The hole in my own memory ached where Wren's price had been paid, that person-shaped absence I still couldn't name, and I pushed the ache down where it lived and kept walking.

The armory stair guards never knew what happened. One moment they were two bored men complaining about the blue-flamed braziers; the next, every ghost within reach reached—and the temperature dropped so fast their breath froze mid-air, and their torches guttered, and something touched the back of the second man's neck with fingers that had been waiting thirty years to touch anything at all. He screamed. His companion dropped his pike and ran, which was wise, and the ghosts let him run, because terror travels faster than soldiers and we wanted it to arrive ahead of us.

I took a pike. Cut the wire on my wrists against its head, clumsy, bleeding, grinning like an idiot. My hands had never done anything so violent in their lives and they shook—but they shook with something that wasn't fear. I turned the feeling over, examining it like a client's heirloom, and identified it at last.

It was joy. Terrible, inappropriate, magnificent joy. For twenty years I had been a vessel, a listener, a keeper of other people's noise, apologizing to every object I touched. And now the gift was a weapon and the dead were a legion and I was not hiding in a workshop carving buttons. I was marching.

*You're smiling,* observed the rain-barrel girl, drifting at my shoulder.

"Don't tell anyone."

*Too late. We're telling everyone.*

* * *

The record hall was exactly what the old woman promised: long, cold, lined with shelves of ledgers bound in grey leather, each one a year, each page a name. I found the volume for the year of the false king's coronation and opened it with hands that remembered how to handle books even while trembling.

Aldric. King Aldric the Third. Died of a fever, said the entry, in the seventh month, attended by his physicians. Signed by the Lord Chamberlain.

"Liar," I said quietly.

*Every line,* agreed the old woman. *Now read them all. Aloud. That's the price, remember.*

So I did. I stood in the record hall of the Spire of Vail with a pike leaning against a shelf and blood drying on my wrists, and I read the execution rolls aloud, name by name, crime by crime, date by date. The baker from the Ash Quarter. The girl with the rain barrel—Maren, her name was Maren, and she'd made roses bloom in a winter courtyard to make her sick brother laugh. The three brothers and their forbidden river song. On and on, page after page, and with every name I spoke, the hall grew colder and brighter and more *crowded*, until the air itself was thick with the pressing, patient, furious presence of the wronged.

Somewhere in the middle of the third volume, boots thundered in the corridor outside. Voices. "The prisoner's loose—the record hall—Marr wants him alive—"

The dead turned toward the sound the way a field of wheat turns toward wind.

"Not yet," I said. "Wait for my word. We do this in order. Doors first. Then the cells. Then—" I looked up at the ceiling, and beyond it, in my mind's eye, the long climb to the throne room where a dead man wore a crown that ate the summer. "Then the eyes."

*And then?* asked Maren.

"Then they see us," I said. "All of them. Every soul they buried in these walls. And they understand what's climbing their stairs."

I closed the ledger. Picked up the pike. And walked out of the record hall at the head of the dead, through corridors that opened for us like throats learning to sing, while somewhere above us the false king felt the first cold finger of his own history touch the hem of his robe—

—and somewhere below, faint but unmistakable, carried up through stone that had finally decided to be on my side, I heard a sword ringing free of a scabbard, and a woman's voice shouting my name.

Sera was alive. And she was coming up.
