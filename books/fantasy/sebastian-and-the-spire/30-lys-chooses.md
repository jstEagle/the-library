## 30. Lys Chooses

The bells had stopped.

That was the first thing Lys noticed, in the way she noticed everything now—sideways, at a distance, the way you watch weather from behind glass. The spire's bells never stopped. They rang the hours, rang the shifts, rang the king's perpetual waking, a sound so constant that after seven years of service Lys had learned to stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. But tonight they had fallen silent mid-stroke, cut off like a throat, and the silence that rushed in behind them was worse than any ringing. It was full. It was crowded.

She stood at her desk in the Registry Annex on the fourth floor of the undercrofts, one hand flat on the ledger before her, and listened to the building breathe.

The lamps were burning blue again. They'd been blue for three days now—the color of winter deepening past all reason, the color the clerks didn't talk about because talking about it meant admitting what everyone already knew: that the days were shrinking faster than the calendar allowed, that the snow on the rooftops hadn't melted since it fell, that something at the top of the spire was drinking the city's warmth the way a drunk drinks the last bottle, without thought for tomorrow. Lys had filed the reports about the blue flames herself. She had written *anomalous combustion, under investigation* in her small, careful clerk's hand, forty-one times.

Under investigation. By whom? By her. She was the investigation. She was the careful hand and the small neat lie, and she had been both for so long that some nights she caught her reflection in the dark window and did not recognize the woman who looked back until she saw the registry pin at her collar.

A sound came up through the floor. Not footsteps. Something softer—a rush, like wind through wheat, like a crowd breathing. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, voices. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

Lys's pen stopped moving.

She knew what was happening below. Of course she knew; she had known since the moment the outer gate's ward-lamps had guttered out an hour ago, since the alarm that wasn't an alarm—the spire had no alarm for this, no protocol, nothing in any manual about the dead coming home through the walls. She had sat at her desk with her heart hammering and done nothing, which was its own kind of decision, and she had told herself it was prudence. Caution. Let the guards handle it. Let the inquisitors earn their silver.

But the guards were running now. She could hear them in the corridor outside, boots and shouting, and the shouts had gone strange—not orders anymore but prayers, men crying out to saints whose names they only half remembered, and underneath their voices came the other voices, the ones with no breath in them, saying things like *you stood there* and *you watched* and *we remember your face.*

Lys closed the ledger.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Seven years of service had taught her hands many kinds of steadiness—the steadiness of stamping deportation orders, the steadiness of telling a mother that yes, the crown had her daughter, no, visitation was not permitted, yes, the child was being *educated*. Her hands had learned to be steady the way a riverbed learns to hold water: by being worn into the shape of it.

But her heart was not steady, and it had not been steady since last night, when a figure in a bone-carver's apron had come through her office door with ash in their hair and their sister's eyes.

*Orsen.*

Even thinking the name felt like picking up a blade by the edge.

* * *

They had grown up in Tanners' Row, in a house that smelled of lye and lime, and Lys had been eight years old when she understood that her sibling could hear the dead. Not understood in the way of a secret shared—Orsen had never told her—but understood the way children understand anything: completely, and without knowing how they know. She had watched her sibling stand too long in the market, listening to a basket. She had seen Orsen flinch away from grandmother's ring as if it had bitten them. Once, when Lys was nine and feverish, Orsen had sat by her bed holding her wooden doll and wept, and when Lys asked why, Orsen said, "She says you're going to be fine," and Lys had believed it, because somehow the doll would know.

Their parents had known too. Their mother had been careful about it—careful the way poor people are careful about everything, folding the knowledge into the family like a garment too valuable to wear outdoors. *Never let them see. Never let them hear you answer.* And then the registry carts had come down Tanners' Row with their gray-cloaked readers and their bone-dust censers, and their mother had hidden Orsen in the lime cellar and lied with a steadiness Lys still marveled at, and the cart had rolled on.

And then, two years later, it had come back. And this time it had not rolled on.

Lys remembered the arrest the way you remember a wound—with terrible clarity and no context, every detail sharp and none of it making sense. The door breaking. Her mother's voice, high and wrong, saying words Lys couldn't parse: *not the girl, take me, I'll give you names, I know others—* Names. Her mother offering names. Lys had not understood then what her mother was doing, trading everything she had to buy one child's invisibility. She had only understood that her mother had put a hand on Lys's head and said, "Be small. Be ordinary. Be safe," and then the gray cloaks had taken her parents away, and Orsen had vanished the same night—run, everyone said, fled the city—and Lys had been left alone in a house that smelled of lye, being small, being ordinary, being safe.

Safe. She turned the word over now, at her desk, while the dead climbed her tower. What a thing to call it. What a long, slow poison to call safety.

Because the registry had not forgotten her. The registry kept lists, and her name was on them—*Whitlock, Lysandra, minor sibling of gifted subject*—and three years later a courteous man with a soft voice had appeared at her boardinghouse and offered her a position. Clerk, Registry Annex, Spire Service. Good wages. A pin for her collar. Protection, he'd said, and the word had landed in her like a hook in a fish's mouth, because protection from what? From whom? From the thing they'd done to her family, obviously. Protection was the fire and the shelter both. You burned, or you carried the torch.

She had taken the pin. She had told herself it was survival, and it was, and it was also surrender, and the two things lived in her together like tenants who never spoke. For seven years she had stamped and filed and written *under investigation*, and she had risen—junior clerk, senior clerk, keeper of the annex—and she had told herself every morning that she was keeping her head down, and every morning the head went down a little easier, and somewhere in those seven years the girl from Tanners' Row had become exactly what the crown had wanted: a functionary. A person shaped like a drawer, built to hold things and ask nothing.

And then last night, Orsen had walked through her door.

* * *

They had looked older. Of course they had; seven years. But it was more than age. Orsen had looked *carved*—the word came to Lys unbidden and would not leave, carved, as if the years had been a chisel and grief the mallet, cutting away everything soft and leaving something true and terrible. Ash in their hair. A knight's sword-calluses on one hand and a carver's grooves on the other. And those eyes—her sibling's eyes, the ones she'd known across a fevered bed, except now they held a city's worth of ghosts and were not afraid of them.

"You're alive," Lys had said, stupidly, because it was the only sentence her mouth could find.

"I need the inner gate," Orsen had said. No greeting. No reunion. Straight through her like a spear, and Lys had felt the old familiar shame rise up to meet it—shame, and its twin, relief. Someone had come for her. After everything, someone had come.

"The inner gate is sealed by inquisitorial writ," she had heard herself say, in her clerk's voice, her drawer-voice, and she had watched Orsen's face change—not with anger, worse, with recognition—and Orsen had said, quietly, "Lys. Look at me. Look at what you're saying."

And she had. She had looked at the blue lamps and the frozen snow beyond the window and the pin on her collar, and for one suspended moment she had seen the whole architecture of her life at once, every stamped order and every careful lie and every morning she'd told herself *survival*, and the sight of it had made her sick, actually sick, her hand going to her mouth—

—and then the corridor had filled with guards, and someone had shouted *intruder*, and Orsen had gone out the window rather than fight past her, rather than hurt her, and Lys had stood alone in her office with her sickness and her shame and the ghost of her sibling's eyes, and she had not raised the alarm.

Not raising the alarm. She had catalogued even that, even her first act of rebellion, in the language of the registry: *failure to report.* She was still filing herself. Gods, she was still filing herself.

Now the dead were in the corridors, and the bells were silent, and the choice that had been circling her for seven years had finally landed in front of her desk and refused to leave.

* * *

The door of the annex burst open at half past the hour that no longer existed.

It was not guards. Guards had stopped coming past her floor twenty minutes ago; the fighting had moved upward, toward the throne levels, and the sounds that drifted down now were not the sounds of men winning. The door opened on lamplight and smoke and a tall woman in half a suit of armor, blood on the gorget, a sword in her hand that seemed almost to be *singing*—and behind her, ducking through the frame, Wren Quill, the memory-thief, whom Lys had processed paperwork on twice and never once seen in the flesh, looking hollow-eyed and strangely light, like a house with the furniture sold.

And behind them, Orsen.

"Lys." Just her name. Orsen crossed the office in four strides and took her hands, and their palms were warm, warm, impossibly warm in the cold of the blue-lit room, and Lys found that her legs had stopped wanting to hold her. "The inner gate. It opens from your side. Your key-sign works."

"You're asking me to commit treason."

"I'm asking you to open a door." Orsen's voice was low and urgent and utterly without judgment, and that was the unbearable part—that after seven years of Lys's careful cowardice, her sibling offered her the truth of it plain: not *redeem yourself*, not *prove you're sorry*, just *open a door*. "What's above us is killing this city. Slowly, and then not slowly. Marr has Aldric's bones' location, Sera's wounded people are holding the stair, and every minute we stand here the binding at the top gets stronger. I can't pass the inner gate without you. So I'm asking. Not commanding. Asking."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I go find another way, and I will still love you, and I will grieve you, and I will not think less of you than I think of myself." Orsen said it simply, the way you state the weather, and Lys felt something crack in her chest along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"That's not—" Her voice broke. She pulled her hands free and turned away, pressing her knuckles to her mouth. "You don't get to make it easy. You don't get to *forgive* me, Orsen, you weren't here, you don't know what I—" She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and jagged. "I stamped the orders. Do you understand? When they swept the Lamplighters' Quarter, my office drew the lists. My *hand* wrote the addresses. There was a woman, Marisol Vey, a candlemaker, they said she read wax—she begged me at the counter, she knew my name from somewhere, she said *you're a clerk, you can lose a page, pages go missing all the time*—and I told her the registry does not misplace records. I told her that. In my careful voice. They took her the next morning and I wrote *processed* beside her name and I slept that night, Orsen. I *slept*."

The office was very quiet. Even the dead in the corridors, Lys realized, had gone still, listening—or perhaps the walls themselves were listening; the whole spire was full of the executed, Orsen had said once, the stones remember, and maybe Marisol Vey was in the walls right now, hearing this confession she had died to prompt.

Sera Voss shifted by the door but said nothing. Wren Quill looked at the floor with the expression of someone who knew exactly what it cost to look at what you've taken.

"Lys." Orsen waited until she turned. "I'm not forgiving you. Forgiveness isn't mine to give—it's hers, and the dead don't vote on our schedule." A ghost of a smile, terrible and tender. "I'm telling you the truth, because last time I offered you rescue and rescue is just another kind of lie. Rescue says: come away, be saved, be small somewhere else. Truth says: the door is there, and you are the only one who can open it, and whatever you choose will be yours forever. That's all I have. That's everything I have."

Lys looked at her sibling—at the carved face, the ash, the warmth still lingering in the air where their hands had held hers—and felt the drawer inside herself strain against its rails. Seven years of files. Forty-one reports of *anomalous combustion*. One woman named Marisol Vey, processed.

"What happens to me?" she asked. "After. If I open it."

"I don't know," Orsen said. "Honestly. Maybe nothing. Maybe Marr's people hold this floor and you hang. I won't pretend otherwise."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway."

Lys breathed out. It shuddered going in and came out steady, and she recognized the feeling with a strange, calm wonder: it was her hands learning a new steadiness. Not the riverbed kind. The other kind—the kind you choose, the kind that costs.

"The key-sign is in the warden's alcove," she said. "Behind my desk. The gate seals from this side—if I stay at the mechanism, it stays open. If I leave it, it drops and locks, and everything below you is shut out, and everything above you is shut in with you." She met Orsen's eyes. "So I have to stay. However this goes. I have to stay and hold it."

"No." Orsen said it fast and hard. "Come with us. Climb with us—"

"Somebody holds the gate." Lys heard her own voice and barely knew it—level, certain, a voice from before the registry, a Tanner's Row voice, her mother's stubbornness wearing her face. "You taught me that without ever saying it. Somebody keeps the ledger. Somebody stays at the post. I spent seven years holding the wrong door, Orsen. Let me hold the right one." She managed the smile. "Besides. If you bring back a freed king and a broken famine, someone respectable needs to be standing here with the paperwork in order. The council will want minutes."

For a moment Orsen's composure cracked clean through, and Lys saw the child from the lime cellar, the one who had wept over a doll, and the two of them gripped each other's forearms in the old way, the way you hold someone when hugging would break you both.

"If it goes wrong," Orsen said, "get to the cellars. Sera's people—"

"Go," Lys said. "Before I become sensible."

* * *

The inner gate was a slab of black iron banded with white bronze, older than the annex around it, older than the dynasty, covered in binding-script that hummed against Lys's teeth when she stood near it. She had walked past it every day for five years and never once touched it. Now she set her palm against the warden's plate and spoke her key-sign—name, number, the little oath she'd recited ten thousand times—and felt the words turn in her mouth like keys made of ice.

*In the crown's service, in the crown's keeping, in the crown's—*

"In my own keeping," she said instead, and pressed.

The script flared. Somewhere far above, something vast and hungry noticed her, and Lys felt its attention slide down the spire like a cold finger finding her spine—and she stood in it, and did not kneel, and the gate began to open.

Iron ground against stone. Cold air poured through, air from the upper levels, thick with the smell of old incense and older fear. Beyond the threshold the ascent waited—nine floors of binding-wrought horror between here and the summit, Orsen had said, each one demanding a toll.

Sera Voss went through first, sword singing. Then Wren, pausing to touch Lys's shoulder with fingers light as paper: "I stole memories once to survive. It doesn't make you empty. It makes you *hungry* for the true ones. Go find some." Then Orsen, turning at the threshold, and the look that passed between them needed no registry, no record, no stamp: it was written where no inquisitor could burn it.

"Hold the door," Orsen said.

"Go free the king," said Lys Whitlock, keeper of the annex, and pushed her sibling gently through, and turned back to the plate beneath her hand.

The gate stayed open because she stayed. That was the whole of the mechanism, and the whole of the vow. She could feel it working through her arm—the gate drawing on her, her steadiness feeding it, her choice becoming literally the thing that kept the way clear. Every minute she stood there was a minute the door remained. Every tremor of doubt would be a crack in it.

Above her, boots on stairs, and voices, and the sound of the dead rising.

Below her, in the dark of the lower corridors, she could hear them coming up to meet her—hundreds of them, the executed, the processed, the ones whose names she had stamped. They gathered at the foot of the gate like a congregation, and Lys braced herself for accusation, for the cold finger, for *you stood there, you watched.*

Instead, a voice like dry leaves spoke from the darkness, and it sounded almost gentle:

"We know your face, clerk. We watched you choose."

Lys straightened her spine, kept her hand flat on the plate, and answered the dead for the first time in her life.

"Then watch me hold it."

Far above, through nine floors of stone and horror, she heard the ascent begin—and heard, beneath the fighting, a sound she would remember for the rest of however long she had: the Hollow Crown, at the top of the world, beginning to scream.
