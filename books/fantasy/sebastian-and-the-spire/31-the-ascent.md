## 31. The Ascent

### ORSEN

The spire's door had no handle.

It opened anyway, when Lys's hand fell away from the inner gate behind us, and the dark inside breathed out cold that smelled of tallow and old stone and something sweeter underneath—something like a feast left too long on the table. I stood in the threshold with Sera at my shoulder and Wren at my back, and Aldric beside me where no one else could see him, and I understood that we were not entering a building.

We were being swallowed.

"Nine floors," Aldric said quietly. His voice was the voice of a man who had spent seven years learning to speak without lungs—patient, worn smooth, like river stone. "I climbed them once, alive. I do not remember all of them. That is not an accident."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the spire takes payment as you rise. A memory or a truth, on every floor. The founders built it that way so that no one could reach the Crown carrying anything they weren't willing to lose." He paused. "I was willing to lose everything. It took everything anyway."

Sera drew her sword—not the one she'd tried to sell me in another life, but the old blade from the Undercity armory, the one that remembered every man she'd killed. She'd stopped apologizing to it somewhere around the third cellar of the Gilderscale's burning. "Then we pay," she said. "We pay and we keep climbing."

Wren said nothing. They were turning their hands over and over, palms up, palms down, the way they did when they were counting something no one else could see—the hoard of stolen memories they carried, ten thousand lives folded small enough to fit inside a thief's skull. I caught their eye. They smiled the smile they'd learned from watching people who were pretending to be fine.

"First floor," Aldric said. "The Hall of Names."

* * *

### SERA

The first floor was a corridor lined with niches, and every niche held a candle, and every candle burned with a name written on its flame.

Sera read them as she passed. *Maren Ostwick. Tam Broadfoot. Ilse, called the Ferryman.* Hundreds of them, thousands, the whole long ledger of everyone the Hollow Crown had ever consumed to keep its winter warm—and each name flickered as she looked at it, leaning toward her like a plant toward light.

At the far end stood a brazier, empty, and above it words carved into the stone:

**GIVE WHAT THE DEAD CANNOT KEEP.**

"It wants a memory," Orsen said. Their voice had gone thin. Sera had learned, over these weeks, to hear the difference between Orsen frightened and Orsen deciding to be brave; this was the second kind, which was worse, because it meant they'd already chosen.

"Not yours." She stepped forward before anyone could argue. "Mine."

"Sera—" Wren started.

"You don't get to out-sacrifice me on floor one. Save it for later floors. There are eight more." She planted herself before the brazier. The names in the candles had gone quiet, all of them listening. "What do I give?"

Aldric drifted forward until he stood inside the brazier's iron basin, translucent as breath on glass. "It will choose," he said. "That is the cruelty of it. You offer everything and it takes what hurts most efficiently."

"Fine." Sera thought of what hurt most efficiently. The list was short. Her brother. Her name. The order she'd refused, and the order she hadn't.

She reached into the brazier.

Cold closed around her wrist like a manacle, and then—

—she was seventeen again, standing in her brother's room while he packed for the palace guard, laughing at how he'd folded his spare shirt wrong, teaching him to fold it right, and the memory played out warm and whole and *hers*, and then it simply wasn't hers anymore. It belonged to the candle. She could see it perfectly and feel nothing, the way you can look at a stranger's photograph of your own house.

The candle nearest her flared with a new name: *Devran Voss.*

"No," she heard herself say, very calmly, from very far away. "Not him. Take anything but—"

But the spire had already taken it, and the gate at the end of the hall swung open onto stairs, and Devran Voss was now a thing the spire kept, burning in a niche among ten thousand other kept things.

Orsen touched her elbow. She let them.

"He's still alive," she said. "I know he's still alive. I just can't remember loving him. I can only remember that I'm supposed to have."

"I know," Orsen said. And because they were who they were—they heard the dead, and the dead included, apparently, a seventeen-year-old boy in a candle—they added: "He says the folding lesson was the best day of his life. He says thank you for the shirt."

Sera laughed, or made a sound near enough to laughing, and climbed.

* * *

### WREN

The second floor was a library with no books, only shelves of small glass bells, and each bell held a sound: a mother's voice, a market morning, rain on a specific roof. Wren knew immediately what this floor wanted, because it was a floor after their own heart.

**GIVE WHAT WAS NEVER YOURS TO KEEP.**

"Oh, you're joking," Wren said aloud.

The bells chimed, softly, all together—a sound like recognition. Like family.

They had been stealing memories since they were six years old, since the night their own childhood was taken from them by a debt-collector with gentle hands, since they'd sworn that no one would ever be emptied again except by choice. Every memory in their head that wasn't theirs was a rescue. A refugee. They'd told themselves that for twenty years.

The floor waited. It was very good at waiting. The whole spire was.

"There's one," Wren said finally. "One I took that wasn't a rescue. I was fourteen. A man in the Saltmarket owed money to the wrong people and he sold me a memory to eat—sold it cheap because he needed coin more than he needed the year his daughter was born. I told myself I'd give it back someday." They swallowed. "I spent it instead. I lived inside someone else's love for a year because mine was gone, and it was the warmest year of my life, and I never gave it back."

They walked to the shelf, found the bell by touch—it hummed against their fingers, a girl's laughter, a kitchen, a father singing badly—and set it in the alcove at the room's center.

The bell rang once, and the memory left Wren the way water leaves a cup: completely, leaving only the shape of having held it.

They felt lighter. They felt robbed. Both things were true, and both things were theirs, and those were the only two things on this floor that still were.

"Come on," they said, hoarse, to the others. "Seven more. Pace yourselves."

* * *

### ALDRIC

The third floor was the throne room, or a copy of it, built at half scale, the way a child builds a copy of a thing that frightens them. On the little throne sat a little king made of wax, and the wax face was Aldric's face, and it was melting.

**GIVE WHAT YOU SWORE TO PROTECT.**

"I remember this floor," Aldric said, and his voice echoed strangely, because the room remembered him too. "This is where I came, the night before they killed me. I came to bargain with the Crown. I offered it my death in exchange for the city's winters. It accepted. It has been accepting ever since."

"Then it's already been paid," Sera said. "What does it want now?"

"The same thing. Again. Forever. That is what a binding is—a promise that never finishes being kept." He moved toward the wax figure, and the wax figure turned its melting head to watch him, and there was hunger in it, patient and vast. "There is a version of me that stayed here. Seven years ago, part of me chose to stop fighting—to let the binding hold, to let the city be safe and frozen and fed upon, because the alternative was risk. That part of me never left this room."

"And it wants that part back," Orsen said slowly. "It wants you to give up the piece of yourself that would have fought."

"Yes." Aldric knelt before the little throne, a king kneeling to a king, though only one of them was real. "And I am going to give it gladly, because that coward has sat here long enough."

He pressed his ghostly hands to the wax face.

For a moment he was everywhere in the room at once—in the walls, in the candles' names, in Sera's sword and Wren's emptied shelves—and then he came back to himself, smaller somehow, or perhaps just more exactly sized, and the wax king collapsed into a puddle that froze and cracked and blew away as dust.

"The rest of me," he said, rising, "is coming with you. All of it. Let's waste no more time."

* * *

### ORSEN

The fourth floor smelled of my mother's workshop.

That is not a figure of speech. Bone dust and linseed oil and the particular smoke of pine shavings—I stood in the doorway and my childhood hit me like a wave, and I understood, with the flat certainty of the doomed, whose floor this was.

**GIVE WHAT YOU HAVE BEEN CARRYING FOR SOMEONE ELSE.**

The room was furnished as hers had been: the carving bench, the rack of half-finished buttons, the window with the grey light. And in the corner, a shape wrapped in sacking, and I knew before I looked what was under the sacking, because I had carried it for eleven years.

Her last work. The commission she never delivered. The piece the guild confiscated when they took her, the one item in all the world I had never been able to bring myself to carve, or sell, or finish.

I unwrapped it with shaking hands. A bone box, lidless, unfinished, its interior hollowed and waiting. Inside it, faint as breath on glass, her voice still lodged in the grain: *Orsen. Finish it. Don't be afraid of finishing things.*

"She's been in there," I said. "All these years. Waiting for me to complete her work, and I couldn't, because completing it meant admitting she was gone."

The floor waited. Floors four through nine, I suspected, were all the same floor wearing different rooms.

So I sat at her bench, in a tower full of horrors, and I carved. My knife found the grain the way hers used to. I cut the hinge-grooves. I shaped the lid. I fitted it, and sanded it, and rubbed it with oil until the bone glowed like something alive, and when I set the finished box on the bench, her voice rose out of it one last time—not trapped anymore, just present, the way a song is present while it's sung.

*"Good lad,"* she said. *"Good child. Go up."*

And the box went quiet—not dead, but done—and I laid it down and left it, and for the first time in eleven years I did not take it with me.

Sera put her hand between my shoulders as we climbed. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Some weights you carry so long you forget they're weights, and then someone helps you set them down and you stagger from the lightness of it.

Four floors down. Five to go.

Above us, through the bones of the tower, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the temperature dropped, and our breath began to fog.

"Winter's faster than we are," Wren said.

"Then we climb faster than winter," Sera said.

* * *

### SERA

The fifth floor was a battlefield, small as a table, with tin soldiers arranged mid-charge, and Sera knew every one of them by name because she had given the order that killed them.

**GIVE THE TRUTH YOU TOLD NO ONE.**

She didn't hesitate this time. Hesitation was a luxury the spire taxed heavily.

"I refused an order," she said to the room, to the tin men, to whatever kept the ledgers of this place. "My commander ordered the palace guard to stand down during the succession—ordered us to let armed men walk past us into the royal apartments, and I refused, and I was cashiered for it, and everyone said I was mad or bought or both. But that's not the truth I've never told." Her jaw tightened. "The truth is that I almost obeyed. For three full seconds I almost obeyed, because he was my commander and obedience was the whole architecture of my life, and if Devran hadn't squeezed my arm—if my brother hadn't been standing next to me—I would have stepped aside with the rest of them. My honor isn't the story of the order I refused. It's the story of the three seconds I nearly didn't."

The tin soldiers, all of them, turned their faces toward her. Then, in unison, they saluted—not the palace salute, but the older one, the field salute, the one soldiers give each other across the lines of any army—and the battlefield dissolved into stairwell.

Aldric was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read. "Three seconds," he said. "You've carried three seconds for ten years."

"They were very long seconds."

"They usually are." He gestured upward. "Come. The sixth floor is mine again, and I would rather get it over with while I'm still feeling brave."

* * *

### WREN

The sixth floor was a cell, and in the cell was a child of about six years old, sitting with their knees drawn up, and the child looked up at Wren with Wren's own eyes.

**GIVE BACK WHAT WAS TAKEN FROM YOU — OR ADMIT IT IS GONE FOREVER.**

"Well," Wren said. "That's just rude."

The child didn't answer. Children made of memory rarely did. It just sat there being six years old, being the night before the debt-collector came, being the last evening Wren Quill had owned their own past—and the floor waited, patient as interest accruing.

"We could give it back," Orsen said gently. "Wren. Your hoard. You could pour it all into this—one childhood, restored, whole. The spire would accept it. It would cost you everything you've saved."

"Everything I've saved belongs to other people." Wren crouched in front of the child, this small arrested moment of themselves, and studied it the way they studied locks: professionally, tenderly, looking for the mechanism. "That's the truth, isn't it? That's what this floor wants. Not the memory. The admission." They exhaled. "Fine. Here it is. I am never getting my childhood back. It wasn't misplaced. It wasn't stolen by someone I can hunt down and rob in turn. It was *spent*—traded for bread, traded for survival, traded away by adults who had run out of everything else. And I built a whole self out of other people's memories because I couldn't bear the empty room where mine should be, and the empty room is still there, and it will always be there, and I am climbing this tower anyway."

The child stood up. Walked to them. Put one small hand against Wren's chest—not taking anything, just resting there, the way you'd rest your hand on a door you'd decided not to open—and then the child walked backward into the wall and was gone, and the wall opened onto stairs.

Wren stayed crouched a moment longer.

"For what it's worth," Sera said from above, "the empty room has excellent company in it. We're all carrying furniture we don't talk about."

"That," Wren said, standing, wiping their face with their sleeve in a motion too fast to be called crying, "is the single worst comfort anyone has ever offered me, and I'm keeping it forever."

* * *

### ALDRIC

The seventh floor was the nursery.

Aldric stopped on the top stair and could not make his feet—or the idea of feet, for he had none—carry him further.

His daughter's room. Reconstructed down to the crack in the shutter, the toy horse with the missing ear, the height-marks scratched into the doorframe: *Alianor, age 4. Alianor, age 5.* She had died of the grey fever two years before the Crown took him, and he had grieved her honestly, fully, in the way kings are rarely permitted to grieve—and yet here she was, or the shape of her, sitting on the little bed with her hands in her lap.

**GIVE THE GRIEF THAT HAS BECOME A WALL.**

"Papa," the shape said, in a voice assembled from his memory of her voice, which was not her voice, which was the cruelest thing the spire had yet done. "Papa, stay."

"No," Aldric said.

The shape tilted its head. Even now, even false, there was something of Alianor's fearless directness in it. "Why not?"

"Because you are a lock, my darling, and I am a key, and if I sit down on that bed I will turn, and everything I am will pour into this room and stay here, and the city will freeze, and your papa will have failed at the only thing left for him to fail at." He knelt—kings kneel so seldom that when they do, the world should stop and notice; he hoped, wherever she truly was, some part of her noticed. "I loved you. I love you. Those are different sentences and I have only recently learned to say both. And grief for you became a wall in me, a place the Crown could build against, and I am giving the wall away. Not the love. Never the love. The wall."

He reached out and, with great tenderness, unmade the shape of his daughter the way you blow out a candle—with a breath, with intention, with sorrow.

The nursery darkened. The doorframe's height-marks remained, scratched into wood that was already dissolving, and the last thing visible before the stairwell opened was *Alianor, age 5,* and then that too was stone again, anonymous, just marks.

Orsen was watching him with wet eyes. "How do you bear it?" they asked. "Climbing through your own life like this?"

"Bear it?" Aldric managed something that had once been his court smile, worn now to its honest frame. "Child, I have been dead seven years. This is the first time anyone has asked me to *live* through anything. I find I prefer it."

* * *

### ORSEN

The eighth floor was a registry office.

Rows of desks. Rows of clerks made of shadow, writing endlessly in ledgers, and every ledger was open to a page with a name on it, and I knew some of the names. Clients. Neighbors. People I had forged entries to protect, back when forging entries felt like defiance instead of delay.

**CONFESS WHAT YOU DID FOR LOVE THAT YOU WOULD CONDEMN IN ANOTHER.**

I stood in the aisle between the desks and thought about it, and the thinking didn't take long, because I had been rehearsing this confession for months without knowing it.

"When Marr came to the Gilderscale," I said, and my voice sounded very loud in all that scratching silence, "I lied flawlessly. I was proud of it. And lying to Marr wasn't the sin—the sin was that I enjoyed it. I liked being better than him. I liked the vertigo. Somewhere in me, the same part that keeps ledgers for a guild that trades in impossible objects—the part that loves a well-balanced column, that finds comfort in a system, any system, even a rotten one—that part looked at the crown's registry, at the neatness of it, at the *order* of it, and felt a kinship I have never said out loud." My hands were shaking. "If I had been born on the other side of the desk, I might have been a clerk here. I might have been good at it. That is my confession: the monster's machinery makes sense to me, and I have never once said so, because saying so felt like betrayal of everyone I've protected. But a lie of omission is still a column that doesn't balance."

The shadow-clerks stopped writing.

Every ledger in the room turned its own pages, rapidly, back to the beginning, to blank paper—and then the desks folded down into the floor like hands closing, and the stairway up appeared, and I was trembling so hard Sera had to steady me with both hands on my arms.

"You could have lied," she said. "Given it something smaller. It might not have known."

"It would have known. It always knows. That's the whole design." I laughed, shaky. "Besides. One floor left. I want to arrive at the top with nothing hidden. Whatever's up there, I want it to meet all of me."

Above us, the final staircase rose into blue firelight, and the cold coming down it was not the cold of stone.

It was the cold of a season with intentions.

* * *

### SERA

The ninth floor had no door, no inscription, no test that Sera could see—only a wide landing under a ceiling of frost-feathers, and a single mirror of black ice, taller than a man.

They all stopped in front of it. In the ice, four figures stood reflected: Orsen, hollow-eyed and upright; Wren, lighter by far than when they'd entered; Aldric, visible now even to living eyes, rendered in the mirror as a man of frost and fading gold; and Sera herself, sword drawn, gray at the temples, ten years older than the woman who'd walked into a bone-carver's shop to sell a haunted blade.

**THE LAST FLOOR ASKS NOTHING,** said letters that formed and melted in the ice. **IT ONLY SHOWS YOU WHO CLIMBED.**

"Is that it?" Wren asked suspiciously. "No toll?"

"The toll was the climb," Aldric said. "It always was. Nine payments, and the tenth is the summit itself." He raised a hand toward the ice, and the ice, courteously, showed them what lay above: a throne room drowned in blue light, and on the throne, a body that had been a king, and around the body, coiled and patient and vast, the thing that wore him.

Sera looked at her reflection a moment longer. The woman in the ice looked back, and there was no shame in either of them, and that, she thought, was the whole victory—small, private, paid for in candles and tin soldiers and a child-shaped absence.

"Right," she said, and rolled her shoulders, and pointed her old remembering sword at the stairs. "Last flight. Everyone keep their truths close and their memories closer. And Orsen—"

Orsen had gone rigid. Their eyes had fixed on the ice, on something in the reflection that wasn't in the room—no. Something that *was* in the room, above them, in the frost-feathered dark of the ceiling, where the ninth floor's payment had been waiting all along, patient, because the spire had saved the cruelest toll for the ones who thought they'd finished paying.

"Orsen," Sera said again. "What is it? What do you see?"

Orsen's voice came out very small, in a tower that had heard every truth they had.

"That's my mother's handwriting," they said. "On the ceiling. And it's counting down."
