## 32. The Floor of Orsen's Mother

The eighth floor was a kitchen.

That was the first cruelty of it. Orsen had expected horrors—had braced for them through seven floors of binding-wrought torment, each landing worse than the last: the gallery of screaming portraits, the corridor that breathed, the room where Sera's dead brother had asked her the question she'd spent ten years avoiding. Orsen had climbed all of it with their hands bleeding and their gift ringing like a struck bell, and at the top of the final stair, they had steeled themselves for something vast and terrible.

Instead: a kitchen. Small. Low-beamed. A kettle on the stove with steam feathering from its spout. Bread dough rising under a cloth on the windowsill, breathing slow as a sleeping animal. The light came in amber and low, the way light does in late autumn when it knows winter is coming and wants to apologize.

Orsen knew this kitchen.

They knew it before they understood knowing it—in their hands, which remembered the height of the table; in their knees, which remembered the cold flagstones; somewhere behind their sternum, in a place that had been hollow for twenty years and was now suddenly, horribly shaped like this exact room.

"No," Orsen said aloud. Their voice sounded wrong here, too loud, too adult. "No, I don't—"

The floor did not answer. The floors of the spire never answered; they only presented, patient as tax collectors, and waited for you to pay.

Behind them, the stairway they'd climbed had sealed itself into smooth grey stone. Ahead, the only door out stood at the far end of the kitchen, past the table, past the window, past the woman standing at the counter with her back to them, kneading bread.

Sera put a hand on Orsen's shoulder. She said nothing. There was nothing to say, and she was learning, floor by floor, when silence was the only honest thing left to give.

Wren stood near the sealed stair, arms wrapped around themselves. Whatever the famine-spirit had taken from them on the summit's threshold—or rather, whatever Wren had *given* it, emptying thirty years of hoarded memories into a starving god's mouth—had left them translucent in some way no one could name. They looked at the woman at the counter, then at Orsen, and their face did something complicated and grief-stricken, because Wren had stolen the memory of this woman once, blind, for a price, and had carried the guilt of it ever since without knowing what they'd taken.

"It's not real," Wren said quietly. It was not reassurance. It was a warning. "Nothing on these floors is real. It's binding-wrought memory. It can't touch you."

Orsen barely heard them. Because the woman turned around.

* * *

She was smaller than Orsen remembered. That was the first thing. In the hole-shaped absence she had occupied in Orsen's mind—the gap Wren's theft had carved, the wound shaped like a person—she had been enormous, monumental, a figure of pure significance without any features attached. Now she was just a woman: thin wrists, flour to her elbows, a smudge of it on her cheekbone, dark hair escaping its braid in wisps. Lines around her eyes that said she laughed often and slept little. Her sleeves were rolled up wrong, one higher than the other, the way she always—

The way she always rolled them. Orsen's hands curled at their sides.

"Ma," someone said, and it took Orsen a moment to realize the voice had come from beside the stove—from a child sitting on a stool with a bone button in one hand and a carving knife far too sharp for a child that age in the other.

The child was eight years old. The child had Orsen's face.

* * *

"You have to watch," Aldric said softly.

He stood apart from them, near the window, and the kitchen's amber light passed through him as if he were made of the same stuff. Since the summit, since his body had finally been laid down and freed, the true king had grown quieter, thinner, more translucent—as if being unbound meant, slowly, becoming less bound to anything at all. But his voice still had weight. It was the voice of a man who had testified before his own murder, who knew what witnessing cost.

"The floor will not let you pass until the memory completes itself," he said. "And it cannot complete itself while you fight it."

"I know how floors work," Orsen said. Their voice cracked in the middle of it.

"Do you?" Aldric's eyes—patient, ancient, kind—held theirs. "You've spent your whole life listening to other people's deaths, Orsen Whitlock. Every object you touched gave up its dead to you whether you willed it or no. You are the finest witness I have ever known." He nodded gently toward the woman at the counter. "But you have never once been permitted to finish watching your own."

* * *

Orsen tried.

Gods, they tried. They stood against the wall with Sera's shoulder solid and warm against theirs and watched their mother make bread on an ordinary evening in the last ordinary week of their childhood, and every instinct they owned screamed at them to intervene.

Because they knew what was coming. Not from memory—the memory was still a hole—but from the architecture of grief itself. From the shape of the absence. You did not carry a wound like this for twenty years without knowing where the knife had gone in.

It was evening. It was autumn. And there were going to be boots on the stairs.

The child-Orsen sat whittling, asking questions about everything and nothing—why the guildmaster's seal used two serpents instead of one, whether the moon got lonely—and Ma laughed at the questions and answered maybe half of them, and the kettle ticked as it cooled, and the dough rose, and Orsen stood against the wall with tears running silently down their face and their fingernails digging into their palms, counting seconds toward a horror they could feel approaching like weather.

"Don't," Sera murmured. "Orsen. Don't watch the door."

"I have to."

"You don't. Look at her instead. Just look at her."

So Orsen looked at her. At the flour on her cheek. At the way she tucked the cloth over the dough, corners folded neat as a bedsheet. At the moment she crossed to the child and ruffled their hair and said, "Show me that button when you're done, little wren," and the child beamed like they'd been handed a kingdom—

—and Orsen's heart tore cleanly in half, because *little wren*. Because Wren Quill had stolen this memory blind, decades later, off some object in a market stall—a button, probably, gods, almost certainly the very button—and had carried away the only person who had ever called Orsen that, leaving a hole and no name to grieve.

"I'm sorry," Wren whispered from across the room. They were crying too. "I didn't know. When I took it, I swear on everything, I didn't know what it was—"

"I know." Orsen's voice came out sanded flat. "I know what you didn't know. Watch."

* * *

The knock came at 743 beats of Orsen's heart. They counted. They couldn't help counting.

Not a knock, exactly—a percussion. Fist on wood, three times, official. And beneath it, the sound that had haunted the edges of Orsen's gift for twenty years without their knowing why: the creak of leather, the jingle of iron, the particular rustle of men who have done this many times and feel nothing.

Child-Orsen looked up. Ma went very still, both hands buried in dough, and Orsen watched her face do the thing he would spend the rest of his life trying to carve into bone and never quite manage: hope and dread occupying the same expression, perfectly, like two figures in one carving.

"Through the back," she said quietly to the child. "Now. Don't run till you're in the lane."

"But Ma—"

"*Now*, love."

And here was the thing the hole had hidden from Orsen for twenty years, the thing they had built their entire discipline upon—their rules, their refusals, their careful never-answer-the-dead—here was the thing:

The child obeyed. The child slipped out the back door into the darkening yard, button clutched in one fist, knife abandoned on the stool. The child ran. The child survived.

Which meant that everything Orsen had believed about that night—that they had hidden, that they had failed her, that some cowardice of theirs had left her alone—was false. Had always been false. Their mother had sent them away herself, with her floury hands and her steady voice, and had turned back to the door alone, on purpose, so that whatever came through it would find only her.

The front door opened without waiting.

* * *

There were four of them. Crown livery, but wrong—grey where it should have been blue, the crest stamped shallow, cheap, the regalia of men serving a king who was already dead and stuffed. Orsen knew the uniform now. They had seen it on Marr's escort, on the spire's guards, on twenty years of proclamations. The Hollow Crown's men. The famine's men.

Their sergeant held a paper. He read names from it the way a man reads a market list.

"Marisol Whitlock. Bone-carver. Registered gifted, tier two. By decree of His Majesty—" and here his voice performed a reverence that made Orsen's skin crawl, because they knew what wore the king upstairs—"you are remanded to the custody of the Spire Office for assessment."

"Assessment," Ma repeated. She wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried, and Orsen understood suddenly that the unhurriedness was armor—that she was making herself slow and calm and boring so they would not look past her, would not think to ask after family, would not search the house. "And what does assessment mean, sergeant?"

"Means the crown takes an interest in useful talents." He smiled with nothing in it. "You'll want your shawl. It gets cold up there."

Up there. Up in the spire. Where the floors kept things. Where binding-work ate memories and wore people smooth. Where Orsen's sister Lys would be taken five years later and remade into a woman who believed the tower protected her.

Where Orsen's mother had gone, and from which she had never come back—not because she died quickly, Orsen realized, ice-water through the veins, but because the spire did not waste material. The famine fed on warmth. On souls. On the slow heat of living people who had gifts worth keeping.

She might have been in there for years. She might have been in there *still*, in some sense, woven into the bindings Orsen had climbed through to reach this floor—this very floor, perhaps, wrought from the harvested warmth of a bone-carver who heard the dead.

"Oh," Orsen said. Very small. "Oh, ma."

On the floor-memory, Marisol Whitlock reached for her shawl. And paused.

She looked directly at the window—at the darkening yard beyond it, at the lane beyond that where a child was running with a button in his fist—and though she could not possibly have seen him, could not possibly have known that twenty years and seven floors away her grown child stood weeping against the wall, she smiled. Softly. The smile of a woman folding a corner of a sheet, tucking a cloth over rising dough, doing one last thing neatly before she goes.

"Keep my hands busy," she said—to the sergeant, absurdly, or to the room, or to no one—or perhaps, in the way of things said on thresholds, to everyone who would ever stand in this kitchen again, including a ghost of the future. "Whatever they do to me up there. Keep my hands busy. Promise?"

The sergeant blinked. "...Fine. Busy hands. Move along, mistress."

She untied her apron and hung it on the hook by the stove. Folded it once. Neat as a bedsheet.

Then she walked out the front door between four men in grey, and the kettle ticked, and the dough rose under its cloth, and the light went on being amber and forgiving, and the door swung shut behind her with the softest possible click.

* * *

Orsen did not move for a long time.

The kitchen stayed. The floor did not dissolve it—it let the room sit there, empty now, the apron on its hook, the child long fled, the bread rising for no one. Letting the silence after her go on. Letting the mourning, at last, reach the bottom of itself.

Twenty years Orsen had carried this night as an amputation—pain without anatomy, grief without a face. Twenty years of refusing the dead, keeping ledgers, staying invisible, all of it scaffolding erected around a hole they couldn't name. And now the hole filled in, and filling hurt worse than emptiness, the way a frozen hand hurts when blood returns—and underneath the hurt, impossibly, something else.

Her name. Marisol. Her laugh, half answers to silly questions. Her flour-smudged cheekbone. *Little wren.* Her sending them out the back door. Her hanging up her apron. Her asking a bored sergeant to keep her hands busy—because she had known. Gifted herself, registered herself, she had known exactly what the spire did to carvers who heard the dead. She had walked into it anyway so her child could run free, and her last request was that someone keep her hands busy.

Orsen stepped forward—off the wall, into the room—and touched the apron on its hook.

It was warm.

One heartbeat of warmth, of wool and flour and her, and then the kitchen exhaled. The amber light drained upward like water finding its level. The table, the stool, the abandoned knife, the risen bread—all of it softened, greyed, became stone. The walls folded inward without violence, the way a hand closes, and the sealed door at the room's far end unsealed itself and stood open onto a narrow stair climbing toward the ninth floor, toward the summit they had already breached once and must breach again in truth.

Aldric faded last. At the threshold he paused, translucent as breath, and inclined his head to Orsen with the full formality of a king honoring a witness.

"She testified well," he said. "As do you. Come. One floor remains, and the dark is coming down."

Orsen took their hand off the apron-hook—or off the stone where the hook had been—and found Sera's arm, and Wren's cold fingers, and turned once, briefly, to the emptying kitchen.

"Goodbye, Ma," they said. "I'll tell them everything. I promise. Your testimony is in my hands."

And the floor let them pass.
