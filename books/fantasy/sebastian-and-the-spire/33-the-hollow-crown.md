## 33. The Hollow Crown

The last door was not a door.

It stood at the top of the ninth stair, a frame of black iron set into nothing—no wall behind it, no wall before it, only air that tasted of frost and old bread. Beyond the frame the sky turned slowly, as if the summit were the eye of a wheel and everything Orsen had ever known hung from its rim.

Sera went first. Of course she did. Her sword was drawn, though what use a blade would be against whatever waited, Orsen could not say. The steel hummed faintly—the dead men in it had gone quiet somewhere around the seventh floor, quiet the way children go quiet when they hear something large moving in another room.

Wren came third, walking close enough to Orsen's shoulder that their sleeves brushed. They had been silent since the floor that took Aldric's coronation oath, silent since they gave up their own name's memory to pay the toll on the fifth landing. Their eyes had a scraped look, like parchment washed for reuse.

Aldric did not walk through the door at all. He drifted through the iron beside it, and paused there, half in, half out, his translucent shoulders bowed under a weight he had carried seven years.

"It will wear my face," he said. "I want you to know that before you see it. What you are about to meet is not me. It has never been me. But it knows my voice better than I do."

Orsen stepped through the doorless door.

* * *

The throne room at the top of the spire was small.

That was the first wrongness. Orsen had imagined a vaulted hall, banners, ranks of kneeling courtiers frozen mid-devotion. Instead there was a chamber no larger than the Gilderscale's counting room, its walls close and warm, its ceiling so low Sera had to duck. The stones sweated heat. Not warmth—heat, the animal kind, the kind that comes off a living body or a banked fire, and it pressed down on them like a hand on the back of the neck.

And there was the king.

He sat upon a throne of fused bone—not carved, Orsen realized, but grown, vertebrae knitted to femur knitted to skull, hundreds of skeletons melted together into one seat. The body in it had been a magnificent man once. Even now, desiccated, skin like tanned leather stretched over a king's broad frame, he held the posture of authority: chin level, hands resting open on the armrests, the way a ruler sits when he expects to be loved.

Upon his head sat the crown.

It was not gold. It was not anything. It was a shape, a suggestion of a circlet, visible only when Orsen looked slightly away from it—and when they looked directly at it, the crown looked back.

"Well," said the king's body, and opened its eyes.

The eyes were the worst part. They were alive. In a face gone to leather and dust, two wet brown eyes blinked, bright and moist and human, and the mouth beneath them smiled with living lips, and Orsen understood suddenly why the founders had bound this thing rather than destroyed it: because it was easier to feed than to fight.

"Three visitors," the thing said. Its voice was Aldric's voice, but slowed, deepened, the way a bell sounds when you strike it underwater. "And one ghost, hiding by the door. Come in. Sit. It has been so long since anyone climbed all nine floors to see me. The last ones were the founders themselves, and they were terrible guests. All business. No conversation at all."

"Sera," Sera said quietly, "stay between him and Wren." She had planted herself anyway. Her sword point did not waver, but Orsen saw her wrist trembling, and knew it was not fear. It was the sword. Every man she had killed was screaming inside that steel, and the thing on the throne was listening to them like a man listening to music.

"You brought the knight," the Hollow Crown said, tilting the dead king's head. "How interesting. She carries such a rich little instrument. Forty-one voices, is it? No—forty-two. Someone died recently. Someone who loved her." Its living eyes moved to Wren. "And a thief of memories, hollowed nearly to the rim. You gave away so much to climb here. I could give it all back, you know. Every stolen thing. I keep everything. That is my whole nature, little thief. I am the oldest ledger in this city."

Wren's jaw tightened. They said nothing. Their hand found Orsen's sleeve and gripped it.

"And you." The eyes settled on Orsen at last, and Orsen felt the gaze physically, a pressure behind the sternum, a cold finger tracing the place where the gift lived. "The bone-carver. The listener. Seven years I have felt you through the stone below us, reading your little objects, hearing your little dead. Do you know what you are, Orsen Whitlock?"

"A bone-carver," Orsen said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Guild-registered."

The dead king laughed. It was an awful sound, because it was such a warm sound—a real laugh, a father's laugh, Aldric's laugh—and it came out of a mouth that had not eaten in seven years.

"Registered," it repeated, savoring the word. "Yes. So are the sheep."

* * *

Orsen made themselves look at the throne properly. This was the discipline the guild had taught them, the one true inheritance Hale had given: look at the object until it stops being frightening and starts being legible. Everything tells you what it is if you listen long enough.

So they listened.

Beneath the entity's voice there was another sound, very old and very slow, like wind through a granary left empty too long. Hunger. Not the dramatic hunger of monsters in fireside stories—something patient and agricultural. A hunger that measured itself in seasons. A hunger that had learned, over centuries, to wait for winter.

"You're starving," Orsen said. "Right now. Even in this heat."

The smile flickered—just barely—and Orsen felt a small fierce satisfaction at having landed the blow.

"Every moment since the founders chained me here," the Hollow Crown said. "Do you know what I am? Truly? Your Aldric—" it gestured graciously toward the doorway where the ghost stood, and did not look at him, would not look at him, "—he calls me a spirit, a demon, a thing. The founders called me the Famine. Both are wrong. I am older than names for me. When your ancestors came to this valley, the winters here ate whole villages. Children died in December and were not found until the thaw, sitting upright in their beds, preserved like wax saints. I was that cold. I was the reason the founders built their city in the shape of a wheel—with the spire at the hub, and me at the center of the hub, bound in bone and bargain."

"And the bargain," Orsen said. "One harvest for one king. Aldric told us. The founders traded—"

"The founders traded *warmth*," the Crown said, and its voice rose with something that might have been passion in a creature less careful. "Not blood. Not souls—your ghost flatters himself. Warmth. Every hearth in this city burns a little hotter because I sit here hungry. Every child sleeps a degree warmer than the child of three hundred years ago froze. I drink the surplus of your comfort, a sip from every cup, and in exchange I hold back the true cold. The cold that remembers when this valley was a grave. That was the pact. That has always been the pact. And every king since has known it, and every king since has agreed, because it is a reasonable price and kings understand prices."

"Aldric didn't agree," Sera said. "You killed him."

The living eyes turned to her, wet and gentle.

"I *inherited* him," the Crown said softly. "Seven years ago, the binding weakened—it weakens each generation; the founders' craft decays as surely as flesh—and the pact required renewal. A new consent. Aldric came up these stairs himself, knighted and crowned and fully informed. He read the terms. He wept, I believe. And then he refused."

Silence, except for the low granary-wind sound beneath everything.

"He refused," the Crown repeated, and now the warmth drained from its voice, leaving something flat and vast underneath, like ice seen under black water. "He stood where you are standing, and he said: find another way. Let the city learn to keep itself warm. And I admired him for it—I have admired very few things in three hundred years—but admiration does not feed me. Winter was coming, as winter always comes. So I took the consent instead of receiving it. His body sits here. His warmth feeds me still. And the city above us has passed seven winters without losing a single soul to the true cold, and not one of them knows the price, and not one of them would pay it willingly, and that—" the mouth curved again, "—that is governance."

* * *

Orsen heard Aldric move in the doorway. Heard the ghost's ragged breath that ghosts did not need.

"You let them believe he was alive," Orsen said. "The court. The city. You wear him to council meetings."

"I wear him to *reign*. There is a difference. A kingdom needs a king the way a body needs a spine, and I have kept his spine straight for seven years while his heirs squabbled and his ministers thieved and his inquisitors burned the gifted who might have noticed the truth. Yes—" the Crown inclined its head, acknowledging, almost courteous, "—the registry is mine. Marr is mine, though she does not know it; she thinks she serves the crown, and in a sense she does. Every gifted person taken in this purge is brought within my reach eventually. Because gifted people are dangerous to me, bone-carver. People who can hear testimony in objects might someday hear testimony in a throne."

The words dropped into the hot room and lay there.

Orsen thought of Lys, somewhere below them in this tower, holding a gate, holding a lie, believing the Crown protected her. Thought of the cellars of the Gilderscale, burning. Thought of Hale's second ledger, neat columns of sold lives, and understood suddenly that Hale had never served the crown at all. Hale had served this. They all had. The whole apparatus of registration and hunt and ledger had been, all along, a feeding trough built by a starving god.

"You're afraid of us," Orsen said. "That's why the hunt. That's why Marr. We're the only ones who could ever catch you."

"I am *old*," the Crown said, "and old things respect competent threats. But fear is such a blunt word. Let me offer you precision instead."

It raised one desiccated hand from the armrest, and the gesture was pure Aldric—the open palm, the gesture of a king granting audience—and Orsen hated how much it hurt to see.

"You have spent your whole life among the kept things," the Crown said. "Buttons that complain of cold. Lockets full of goodbye. Blades crowded with the dead. Tell me honestly, carver—was there ever a night you did not envy them? They are held. They are housed. Nothing touches them without passing through hands that know their worth. And you—you have slept alone above a workshop full of voices, keeping other people's dead company because your own kind would not keep yours. I know what the guild was to you. A cage you mistook for a family. I know about your sister."

The room went very still.

"Do not," Orsen said.

"She is four floors below us," the Crown continued, mild as morning. "She believes in me, you know. She lights candles for the Crown's protection. She thinks the registry saved her from the streets. When this is over—if this ends the way I intend it to end—I will keep her safe. That is not a threat, carver. That is inventory management."

Sera shifted. Wren's grip on Orsen's sleeve had become painful.

"But here is my offer, and I ask you to weigh it truly, because I have watched you for seven years and I do not make this offer lightly." The dead king leaned forward on his throne of bones, and the crown-shape flickered above his head, and for one moment Orsen saw it plainly: not a circlet but a ring of teeth, or a ring of winter stars, or both. "When the founders bound me, they needed a witness—one person who could hear both sides of the pact, the hungry and the fed, and speak for each to the other. That witness died, and her gift passed down like an heirloom no one wanted: mother to daughter, skipping generations, surfacing in bone-carvers and midwives and madmen, always hunted, always alone. You are the strongest vessel of it in three hundred years. You climbed nine floors past the best bindings I have. Do you imagine that was skill? I let you climb. I have been waiting for you the way a larder waits."

"No," Sera said, stepping forward, and her voice cracked like a whip. "Whatever you're offering—"

"I am offering *belonging*," the Crown said, without looking at her. Its eyes never left Orsen. "Sit beside me. Learn the terms of the oldest contract in this valley. Be the witness again—not hunted, not registered, not sold by a guildmaster who weighed your life against his ledgers and found the ledgers heavier. Be *kept*, Orsen Whitlock. Warmth for warmth. Your sister under my protection. Your dead at peace within my walls, every one of them, gathered and housed and answered whenever you wish to speak to them. You will never be lonely again. That is the promise I made the founders' witness, and I keep my promises. Ask the city's winters. Three hundred years, and I have never once broken terms."

* * *

And here is the truth that Orsen carried out of that room and never entirely lost, however the story got told afterward: for three full breaths, they considered it.

Three breaths. Long enough to see it whole. A chair beside a throne, in a warm room, at the center of the wheel. Lys safe. The voices gathered and gentle instead of scattered and screaming. An end to running, to lying to inquisitors, to watching people they loved bleed for the crime of proximity. The dead had never given Orsen belonging—they had given Orsen work, endless work, the work of hearing what no one else would carry. And here was a thing older than grief offering what the dead never could: a household. A seat. A place at the fire, even if the fire was stolen.

The third breath ended because Wren spoke.

"You'd be a kept thing," Wren said. Their voice was thin, scraped, but steady—the voice of someone who had emptied themselves once already and knew exactly what remained. "You've spent your whole life freeing them. Buttons. Lockets. Swords. You told me—on the stairs, the fourth floor, after the toll—you told me the dead don't want to be kept. They want to be *heard*, and then let go. That's the whole difference. That's the entire difference, and you know it better than anyone alive."

Orsen looked at the throne.

At the leather face and the living eyes, at the beautiful corpse wearing patience like a mask over appetite. At the crown that was a ring of teeth.

"You keep everything," Orsen said quietly. "That's your nature, you said. Your whole nature. But nothing you keep is *yours*. The warmth you drink belongs to the hearths. The king you wear belongs to himself. Even your promises belong to the people who believed them." They stepped forward—Sera hissed a warning, and they stepped anyway, close enough to see the stitching of frost in the dead king's throat. "You're not a keeper. You're a hoarder. And I have spent my life learning the difference, because I used to be one myself. I kept the dead at arm's length and called it discipline. I kept clients' secrets and called it professionalism. I kept a hole in my own memory because looking at it hurt. Keeping isn't love. I learned that. You never will."

For the first time, the living eyes blinked twice, quickly—the only tell the ancient thing permitted itself.

"So you refuse," the Crown said. "As he refused." The dead king's head turned, slow as a drawbridge, toward the doorway where Aldric stood. "You understand what refusal costs. Last time, it cost a king. This time—"

"This time," said Orsen, "it costs you."

They reached into their coat and drew out the bundle they had carried up all nine floors: Aldric's remains, wrapped in the knight's cloak, light as kindling and heavy as testimony. They held it up between themselves and the throne.

"You wanted a witness who could hear both sides of the pact," Orsen said. "Fine. I'm the witness. And the testimony begins now."

Behind them, in the doorway, the ghost of the true king finally lifted his head, and began, in a voice worn smooth by seven years of practice, to speak.

The temperature in the room began to fall.

The Hollow Crown stopped smiling.
