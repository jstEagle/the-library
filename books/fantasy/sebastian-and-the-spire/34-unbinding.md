## 34. Unbinding

The summit of the spire was a room that had forgotten it was a room.

Orsen felt that first, before sight or smell—the absence of walls in the dead's sense of things. Every other chamber in the tower had been crowded, its stones thick with the memories of everyone who had ever stood within them, but this place was hollowed out like a gourd, scraped clean by centuries of one appetite. The air tasted of cold iron and old grain. Snow came through the broken crown of the roof and did not land; it hung in the upper dark, suspended, turning slowly, as if the room were chewing.

And on the throne at the center sat the king.

The body was seven years dead and dressed for a court that no longer existed—ermine gone grey as cobweb, the scepter fused to a fist that had not opened since the binding. Above the sunken face floated the Hollow Crown itself: a circlet of black iron worked into the shapes of wheat stalks, each stalk bent, each head of grain empty. It did not rest on the king's brow. It hovered a finger's width above it, humming.

*There he is,* Aldric said, from just behind Orsen's left shoulder. His voice was quieter here than anywhere else they had climbed through. Smaller. *My body. I had forgotten how tired it looks.*

"You look like a man who worked too many harvests," Orsen murmured.

*I was one.* A pause, and then something almost like laughter. *I used to complain about the weight of the crown at ceremonies. My father said every king does, and every king forgets to say so once he is dead. He was wrong about that, at least.*

Behind them, Sera held the stair door with her sword drawn and her back against the frame. Wren stood near the wall with their hands pressed flat against the stone, feeding the stolen memories back into the spire one by one—the keys that had unlocked nine floors now being spent like coins, buying them this last quiet minute. Wren looked thinner than Orsen had ever seen them, translucent around the eyes, as if some inner lamp were burning down toward the wick.

"It knows we're here," Sera said. Not a question.

"It has known since the undercrofts," Orsen said. "It's waiting."

*It is always waiting,* Aldric said. *That is what it does best.*

Orsen crossed the floor. Each step rang strangely, as though the stone beneath were a drumhead stretched over something vast. Ten feet from the throne, they stopped—and the dead king's body moved.

It was small, the movement. The head turned, slow as a door on rusted hinges, until the desiccated face found Orsen's. The eyes were open. They had been open for seven years, Orsen realized, and they were not the king's eyes anymore. Something looked out through them the way light looks out through a lantern—shaped by the vessel, belonging to neither.

"Bone-carver," the thing said. Its voice was the famine-voice Orsen had heard on every floor above the fifth: patient, dry, the sound of a field after the reaping. "You brought my witness."

"I brought *your* king," Orsen said.

"My keeper." The mouth barely moved; the voice came from everywhere, from the hanging snow, from the walls, from the empty wheat of the crown. "He agreed to be kept. Read your parchment if you doubt me. Everything in it was signed."

Orsen lifted the scroll they had carried since the tomb beneath the Undercity—Aldric's hidden remains wrapped beside it in oilcloth, riding against their chest like a second heart. The original binding. Founders' script in three hands, sealed with the sigils of the First Compact, older than the city's oldest street.

"I intend to," Orsen said. "Every word."

"Then know what you unmake." The suspended snow shivered. "Before your founders bound me, I took this city every winter anyway. Freely given, freely fed—I asked nothing they did not owe. They grew greedy for spring. So they built me a house and gave me a shepherd, and called the bargain mercy." The dead eyes glittered. "Your guild keeps ledgers, bone-carver. This is mine. Seven kings' worth of warmth, paid on time, every winter, without fail. Unbind me, and I will go back to taking what I am owed the old way. The city will learn what it forgot."

"That's a threat," Sera said from the door, blade steady.

"It is an *accounting*," the Hollow Crown said.

*Aldric.* Orsen reached back along the thread of the ghost's presence. *Are you ready?*

*I have been ready for seven years,* the true king said. *I have been ready since the night my brother's men held me down on this floor and poured me into the shape of a promise. But ready is not the same as able. Read, Whitlock. Give me the floor. I will testify.*

* * *

They began where all such work begins: with the reading aloud.

Orsen broke the founders' seal and spoke the first line of the Compact, and the summit changed.

Not visibly—or not only visibly. The hanging snow stopped mid-turn. The blue fires in the braziers along the walls leaned inward like grass in wind. And the room became, in the way of old magic, what it had always secretly been: a courtroom. Orsen felt the transformation settle over them like a mantle. The stones themselves remembered every word ever sworn in this place, and when the founding words were spoken in the founding cadence, the stones woke up to hear them again.

*"Let there be kept, at the crown of the works, a hunger housed—"*

"I object," said the Hollow Crown.

Its voice cracked across the summit like ice splitting on a pond. The suspended snow fell all at once—a soft, terrible snowfall inside the room—and where the flakes touched the floor they did not melt but sank, swallowed.

"This proceeding is irregular," the entity said. "The witness is dead. Dead men cannot testify."

*On the contrary,* Aldric said, stepping forward—out of his own corpse, out of the shadow of his own corpse, gathering himself into the shape he wore for Orsen: a broad man gone grey at the temples, wearing the ghost of a travel-stained cloak instead of ermine. He planted his feet on the floor of his own prison and looked up at the thing that wore his face's shape. *Dead men are the only ones who can. You made sure of it. Every living soul who knew the terms of our bargain, you saw buried or bought. You wrote a contract with no living witnesses and thought that made it eternal.*

"It made it *valid*," the Crown said.

"No," said Orsen, and read on. *"Housed, and fed, and answered—so long as the answer comes honest, from the mouth of the kept king, each solstice of each year—"*

The words pulled. Orsen felt it in their teeth, in the scarred pads of their fingers, in the gift that had been screaming and singing and guiding them since the market cross. The Compact was not merely text. It was a knot, tied through the spire's whole height, through the city's whole winter, through seven years of a stolen body—and every clause spoken aloud drew one loop of it tight enough to see. Orsen could *see* it now, the way they saw echoes in bone: a great tangle of golden-brown strands, wheat-colored, wound through the black iron of the crown and down, down, through ninety feet of stone into the roots of the city.

*"—answered honestly, lest the keeping lapse, and the housed return to its former taking—"*

"There," the Crown said swiftly. "You hear it. The lapse-clause. Cut me loose and I revert to my nature. Is that the mercy you climbed nine floors to deliver?"

"The clause cuts both ways," Orsen said. "You've never let him answer honestly. That's why you're afraid of tonight."

Silence, for the first time. The famine-spirit had nothing to say to that, because it was true, and even things older than cities flinch from truth spoken plainly in a room built to hold it.

*Solstice eve,* Aldric said quietly. *Tonight is solstice eve. In all seven years of my death, the thing in my throat has spoken my half of the rite for me. It has answered the Compact in my name, dishonestly, every winter—telling the founders' knot that the king consents, that the king is content, that the king is warm.* His ghost-hands curled into fists. *I have screamed behind those answers every year. Tonight you will ask the question it has always answered for me. And I will answer it myself.*

Orsen turned to the throne. To the body. To the hovering crown with its bent wheat.

"By the terms of the First Compact," they read, and their voice did not shake, though everything in them shook, "*the kept king shall affirm, of his own will and honest speech, his continuance in the keeping: Do you, Aldric, son of Maren, King of this city and its winters, consent to be kept?*"

The Hollow Crown moved.

It did not wait for the answer—it *became* the answer, lunging off the dead brow with a sound like a granary door slamming, pouring itself forward to fill the silence with the old lie, the rehearsed lie, the seven-year lie: *"I con—"*

"*I do not.*"

Aldric's voice went through the room like dawn through a shuttered hall.

He spoke from his whole self—from the bones wrapped in oilcloth against Orsen's chest, from the ghost standing in plain view, from seven years of testimony hoarded like seed corn against exactly this night. And the Compact *heard* him. The golden knot flared. Every strand of it lit from within, and the false answer died half-spoken, choked in the black iron, because the contract had been built to recognize one voice and one voice only, and the true king had finally, finally gotten a word in.

"I do not consent," Aldric said again, and now he was walking, circling the throne, addressing the room, addressing the knot, addressing the city below whose rooftops lay under snow that would not melt. "I never consented. Hear the record. On the longest night seven years past, I was brought to this floor by men I trusted—my captain among them, my treasurer among them, my own blood among them. I was told the harvests were failing and the famine below the city was waking, and that there was a remedy older than the remedy my grandfather used. I was told I would be asked."

He stopped before his own body. For a moment the ghost and the corpse regarded each other, king and remains, and Orsen heard—not with the ears—something pass between them, grief folding back into grief like hands clasping.

"They did not ask," Aldric said. "They held me down. They sang the founders' words backward, which is the way you tie rather than loose, and they poured me into the keeping like water into a cracked jar. And every year since, on this night, that *thing* has worn my voice and lied to the knot on my behalf. Seven years of forged consent. Seven years of a signature I never made." He turned to the Crown, and his voice dropped low, and it was worse than shouting. "You have been committing fraud with my mouth."

The Hollow Crown hung in the air between them, and Orsen watched something impossible happen: it dimmed.

Not much. A shade less certain. But the famine-spirit had ruled this room for a hundred years on the strength of an unbreakable pact, and the pact was coming apart strand by strand in front of it, and it knew it, and for one flickering instant it looked—Orsen would carry this image the rest of their life—*small*. A hungry thing in a borrowed shape, suddenly unsure whether the shape still fit.

"Liar," it said, without force.

"Hear the witness," Orsen said, and read the next clause, the clause the founders had written in fear and honesty together, the escape hatch they had carved into their own trap because they were not yet so proud as their descendants: *"Should the kept king's affirmation be withheld—if it be refused, or forged, or silenced—then the keeping lapses, and the housed shall stand unbound before the city it fed upon, to make account."*

"To make *account*," Sera repeated from the door, loud and clear, a knight's voice giving the legal formality its due weight. She sheathed her sword, deliberately, the way one does when the fighting is finished and only judgment remains. "Witnessed."

"Witnessed," said Wren, pushing off the wall, swaying, pale as paper, spending the last of their stolen hoard simply to stay upright and present. "Witnessed twice."

"Witnessed," said Aldric, "thrice—by the aggrieved party himself."

And Orsen, bone-carver, ledger-keeper, liar-to-inquisitors, said: "Witnessed four times, and entered in the record," and held up the founders' scroll so the knot itself could see its own handwriting.

The Compact unraveled.

There was no thunderclap. Orsen had expected thunder, had braced for the spire to shake apart around them. Instead the unbinding was quiet and total, like a knot slipping in wet rope, like a debt forgiven at last call. The golden strands loosened all through the summit, all through the tower, and Orsen heard—everywhere at once, faint and multiplying—the sound of the city exhaling. Blue fires guttered back to gold in ten thousand hearths below. Somewhere far beneath them, snow that had lain unmelted for a month sighed and began, at last, to give.

The Hollow Crown fell.

It dropped from the air like any other piece of iron and struck the stones with a small, foolish clang, rolling once, coming to rest against the foot of the throne. Bent wheat. Empty grain. Just metal now, Orsen thought—no, not quite. Even unbound, it hummed faintly, the way all kept things hum. But the hunger in the hum had nowhere to live anymore, and it sounded, for the first time in a century, lonely.

Which left the famine itself.

* * *

It came out of the king's body the way smoke leaves a snuffed candle—upward, unwilling, shaped for one more moment like the man it had worn. Then it unfolded. Orsen had seen it on the ninth floor, in the trial of truths; had heard it whisper through the walls; had felt its attention like frost forming. None of it prepared them for the sheer *size* of the thing unbound. It filled the summit the way winter fills a valley. It had no face, but it had want, and the want was a landscape.

It did not attack. That was the horror of it. It simply stood in the ruins of its contract and looked at the four of them—at Sera with her sheathed blade, at Wren swaying on their feet, at Aldric blazing in his testimony-shape, at Orsen with the scroll still raised—and it waited, because it had been bound for a hundred years and patience was the deepest groove in it.

"Well," it said, in a voice like wind through a harvested field. "Now what."

"Now you go," Aldric said.

"Where?" The famine spread its non-shoulders. "I am the winter under the world, little king. Your founders did not create me. They caught me. There is no 'away' for what I am. There is only fed and unfed." The temperature in the room began, gently, horribly, to fall. "Unbound, I take as I always took. Unless—" and here the want sharpened into something almost like cunning, almost like courtesy, the tone of a merchant who has lost the lawsuit and is already drafting the next bargain, "—unless someone prefers the new arrangement. A house without a captive king. A feeding without a fraud. One willing vessel, freely contracted, honestly affirmed—your own law, your own clauses. I will end the winter by morning. Ask your knight. Knights understand service."

Sera's hand went to her sword hilt. Orsen saw her jaw set, saw the old guilt move behind her eyes—the cashiered officer who had spent years believing she owed the world a body—and Orsen opened their mouth to stop her—

"—and ask your thief," the famine said, softer, faster, striking at the open wound. "One who knows what it is to be empty. Who has already spent everything. What is one memory more, when you are made of nothing else? I can fill you, thief. No one else will."

Wren looked at it. Looked a long time.

And smiled, thin and strange. "Tempting," they said. "But I've got a better offer."

*Wren—* Aldric started.

But Wren was already pulling something from inside their coat: not a weapon, not a key. A small, stoppered vial of smoked glass, and inside it a darkness that moved. Every memory they had not spent on the stairs. Every theft of a long, long career, hoarded against a rainy day that had finally come.

"You want to be fed?" Wren said. "Eat a hundred lifetimes. Eat everything I ever took. Weddings. Funerals. First snowfalls. A woman's seventieth birthday, whole." They pulled the stopper. "Freely given. Honestly affirmed. Witnessed by a king."

*No,* Aldric said, reaching for them. *Wren, that will hollow you—you'll be—*

"I know what I'll be," Wren said, and their eyes found Orsen's, and there was no fear in them at all, only the particular calm of someone settling a debt they'd carried too long. "I stole a name out of your head once, carver. Consider us square."

They drank the winter down.

* * *

The light went through the summit like a season changing. A hundred lifetimes burned in the space of ten heartbeats—Orsen felt them pass, felt the dead in them pass too, a river of other people's yesterdays pouring through the room and into the famine, which had never in its long existence been *given* anything. It staggered. It fed. It grew bright, then strange, then—quietly, terribly—full.

And full, for the first time, it slept.

What collapsed onto the summit floor was not a landscape of want. It was a figure: small, gaunt, androgynous, made of packed snow and dim light, curled like a child after a feast. Breathing, in whatever way such things breathe. Sated. Dormant. Perhaps, someday, capable of something other than hunger—but that was a problem for a wiser generation, and everyone in the room knew it.

Wren stood where they had stood. Wren's coat was the same. Wren's face was the same.

But when Orsen caught them as they swayed, the eyes that focused slowly on theirs were polite, and blank, and searching, like a guest trying to remember their host's name.

"Do I—" Wren said, frowning gently. "Do I know you?"

"You did," Orsen said. Their voice cracked clean through. "Come spring, we'll see if you do again."

Above the throne, in the sudden ordinary dark, the ghost of Aldric, son of Maren, stood alone at last—unbound, unkept, unforged, free for the first time in seven years.

Free, and fading. Orsen could see it already: the edges of him going soft, the way a voice goes soft when its owner is walking away down a long road. A freed spirit does not linger. A freed spirit *leaves*.

"Not yet," Aldric said, smiling, holding up a translucent hand as if to hold off the grief he could see arriving in Orsen's face. "Not yet, Whitlock. I've waited seven years to say goodbye properly. Grant me five minutes more, and a walk to the window. I want to see my city thawing."

Orsen wiped their face with the back of their wrist, nodded, and offered him their arm—bone-carver and bone-king, descending the tower steps together into the melting night, while behind them the Hollow Crown lay in the straw-light of the braziers, empty at last, and silent.
