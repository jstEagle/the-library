## 36. What the Famine Left

Wren Quill had stolen ten thousand memories in their life, and the one thing they had never learned to do was hold still.

But now, at the top of the world, with the spire groaning around them like a ship in a storm made of cold, Wren held very still indeed.

The Hollow Crown hung above the throne where a king should have been—or rather, the king *was* there, Aldric's desiccated body slumped in the great seat of black iron, and the Crown floated an inch above his brow, turning slowly, drinking. It was not gold anymore. It was not anything anymore. It was a shape the eye kept trying to refuse: a ring of winter, a circle of hunger, a hole wearing jewelry.

And it was starving.

Wren could feel it the way they felt everything—tasted it on the air like copper and old snow. The famine-spirit inside the Crown had fed on this city's warmth for seven years, sipping slow so no one noticed, and now that Aldric's testimony had cracked its binding open, it was spilling out into the chamber like water from a broken jug, all of it at once, desperate and enormous and *afraid*.

That was the thing no one ever understood about monsters, Wren thought. They were always afraid first.

"You unbound me," the Crown said. Its voice came from everywhere—from the walls, from the frost creeping across the windows, from the blue-burning torches that guttered as it spoke. "You took away my cage. Do you know what I was before the cage?"

Nobody answered. Sera Voss stood with her sword drawn, though her arm shook; she'd taken a blade through the shoulder two floors down and had refused, in her flat knight's way, to mention it. Orsen stood closer than anyone dared, hands empty, bone-dust still on their fingers from the reading. And Aldric—the true Aldric, the ghost, translucent as breath on glass—stood beside his own corpse and looked at the thing that had worn him for seven years with an expression Wren could not steal fast enough to keep.

"I was a harvest," the Crown said. "I was the reason your founders ate in the famine years. I was the bargain: a king for a kingdom, warmth for survival. I did not ask for the throne. I was *put* here." A pause, long as a held breath. "And now you have opened the door, and I am dying, and when I die, the cold comes back the way it was before. The way it was in the founders' time. Do you remember those winters? No. Of course not. No one survived to remember them properly."

The temperature dropped again. Wren watched frost race up Sera's sword blade, watched Orsen's breath come out white and ragged.

"So," the Crown said, and its voice went soft, almost gentle, the voice of something that had learned persuasion over centuries of being bargained with. "One last bargain. The last one. Winter ends tonight—one vessel, freely given, and I wear them instead of the city. One body to live in. One warm heart to sip from, gently, slowly, kindly. And outside these walls, spring comes on schedule. Snow melts. Children stop freezing in their beds." The Crown turned, slowly, until its terrible absence of a face seemed to regard each of them in turn. "Choose."

* * *

Later—and Wren would have years, or whatever they had instead of years, to turn this moment over—they would remember that Sera moved first.

Of course she did. That was Sera. She sheathed her sword with a scrape that sounded like a door closing, stepped forward out of the frost-light, and said, in the voice she used for orders she intended to be obeyed:

"Me."

"No," Orsen said.

"Orsen—"

"*No.*" Orsen's voice cracked on it, and Wren, who collected cracks in people the way other thieves collected coins, heard everything inside that crack: the sister lost to the registry, the mother on the arrest-floor, the guild burned, the gift spent, all of it stacked behind one word. "You don't get to do this. You held the line in the Undercity with a knife in your shoulder. You carried me through the cellars. You—" Orsen stopped, swallowed. "Sera. Please."

"That's exactly why," Sera said quietly. "Everything I was is gone anyway. My name. My order. My brother." Her hand went, without her seeming to notice, to the ring on its cord around her neck—the ring Wren had read for her, months ago, the night two debts crossed a table. "A body is just the last thing I own. Let it buy something."

"It's not buying," the Crown said, and its hunger-song crept back into its voice, sweet and low. "It's *keeping*. You would be kept, knight. Warm. Whole. Remembered forever by something that never forgets—"

"Don't listen to it," Wren said.

They hadn't meant to speak. But the words were out, and every face turned to them—Orsen's exhausted and questioning, Sera's hard, Aldric's sorrowful—and Wren stood there in their coat of forty stolen pockets and felt, for once in their life, completely unhidden.

Because here was the truth Wren Quill had been carrying since they were nine years old, since the day they'd reached into their mother's locket to take the memory of her singing and found they couldn't put it back: they knew what it was to be a vessel. They had been filling themselves with other people's lives for twenty years because their own had a hole in it shaped like a childhood. They knew exactly what the Crown was offering, dressed up in mercy.

It was offering to be full instead of loved.

"There's another way," Wren said.

"Wren." Orsen's voice was careful now, the way you're careful around a ledge. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I've been thinking about it since the Undercity, actually. Since the dead voted. Since Aldric knelt." Wren laughed—a short, strange sound, frost-smoke in the air. "Do you know what memories weigh? Really weigh? I do. I've carried ten thousand of them. Every one I ever stole, hoarded, kept, because I couldn't give them back and couldn't bear to throw them away. Weddings. First snows. Mothers singing. Soldiers' last mornings. A whole city's worth of *warmth*, all folded up in here." They tapped their temple. "You want a vessel, Crown? Here's a better one. Not one body. Ten thousand lives. All at once. A feast."

The Crown went very still.

Even the frost seemed to pause.

"A feast," it repeated, and the hunger in its voice was suddenly naked, suddenly vast, and Wren felt the floor of their own courage drop away beneath them—but they'd been falling for twenty years. They were good at it.

"You'll starve either way," Wren said. "One body sipped gently lasts you what—a decade? Two? Then you're back here, bargaining with some other poor soul on some other frozen throne. But this—" they spread their arms, coat pockets bulging with the weight of everything they'd ever taken, "—this is a banquet. Enough warmth to sleep for a century. Enough *lives* to dream about while you sleep. Isn't that better? Isn't that what you wanted, before they caged you? To be full?"

"You would be emptied," the Crown said. "Completely. There will be nothing left of Wren Quill."

"Yeah," Wren said. "There's a version of me that would call that a fair trade."

* * *

They didn't let Orsen argue. That was the only cruelty Wren allowed themselves, and it was a kindness really, if you squinted—because they did it fast, the way you pull a splinter, the way you cut a rope before it drags you over the side.

They stepped between Orsen and the Crown, and they caught Orsen's face in both hands, cold fingers against cold cheeks, and they said, quickly, before the thief's instinct to flee could win:

"The memory I took from you. The one with the hole shaped like a person. I gave it back—you know that, Lys is real, Lys is waiting downstairs holding a gate. But there's something else. Something I never told you I had." Their throat closed. They pried it open. "Your mother sang to you. Before the registry took her. I have it—I stole it off her hairbrush years ago, sold to the Gilderscale, before I knew whose it was. I was going to return it someday. I'm a coward, so I'm telling you instead of doing it. It's in the left inside pocket. Blue silk. Don't lose it."

Orsen's eyes went wide and wet. "Wren—"

"And tell Sera—" but Sera was already there, already gripping their arms, and words failed even a thief for once, so Wren just pressed their forehead to hers, knight and thief, hunter and hunted, and breathed.

"Idiot," Sera whispered.

"Obviously," Wren whispered back.

Then they turned to Aldric, who had drifted close, ghost-pale, and who bowed his head—the king bowing to the thief, which was, Wren thought, the single best thing that had ever happened to them, and they were going to forget it, and somehow that made it shine brighter.

"Hear them," Wren told him. "All of them. When I'm gone, every memory in me belongs to the Crown, but the *shape* of me—that's yours to testify. Tell the city who Wren Quill was. Lie a little. Make me taller."

"I will not need to lie," Aldric said gravely, and then, softer, with the ghost of a smile: "Perhaps only slightly."

Wren grinned. Then they walked to the throne.

* * *

The Crown descended to meet them.

Up close it wasn't terrible at all—it was *hungry*, which was worse, a gravity of wanting, and Wren felt it begin to drink before they'd even finished kneeling. Cold poured into them, and they poured back.

They opened every pocket.

Ten thousand memories went out of them like a river going over a falls. The wedding on the bridge at midsummer. The baker's daughter learning to whistle. The soldier's last morning, eggs and black bread and his comrade's laugh. A woman's hands teaching small hands to carve. Snow on a mountain no one alive remembered climbing. Song after song after song. Names. Names. So many names.

And underneath all of it, at the bottom of the hoard, the ones Wren had never shown anyone: a locket. A woman singing. A child reaching up. *Come back to me*, the memory said, in a mother's voice, and Wren gave that one too, gave it first and last and most, because if the famine-spirit was going to dream for a hundred years, let it dream of that. Let something in this frozen room learn what it was for.

The light in the chamber changed. The blue flames bent gold. Frost retreated along the walls like a tide going out, and the Crown—drinking, drinking, drowning in warmth at last—made a sound that no one present could describe afterward except Orsen, who wrote it down much later in a ledger of small impossible things: *it sounded like someone setting down a weight they had carried so long they had forgotten it was heavy.*

The last thing Wren Quill thought, with the final scrap of themselves, was not a thought at all but a feeling, warm and specific and aimed like an arrow:

*Tell them I paid my debts.*

Then the pockets were empty. Then the thief was empty. Then—

Then something remained. Not Wren, exactly. Not nothing, either. A hollowed figure knelt at the base of the throne, breathing, blinking, a person-shaped vessel with the firelight moving behind its eyes like weather seen through glass. It looked at its own hands. It looked at Orsen.

It smiled—slowly, uncertainly, the way a stranger smiles—and when it spoke, its voice was Wren's voice and ten thousand other voices underneath, layered like rings in old wood:

"...I know you," it said wonderingly. "I think—I think I know everyone. I know a wedding on a bridge. I know a song about carving. I know—" it pressed a hand to its chest, frowning, searching the vast new country inside itself, "—I know there was someone. Someone small and quick-fingered who owed a debt. Did he pay it? Does anyone know if he paid it?"

Orsen made a sound that was not a word.

Sera caught the empty figure as it swayed, held it up, held it the way you hold something you refuse to drop, and over its shoulder her eyes found Orsen's—fierce and wet and asking a question neither of them could answer yet.

And far below, faint through nine floors of stone, a bell began to ring.

Not an alarm. Not a death-knell.

The thaw-bell. The old bell in the Temple district that had not rung in living memory, rung only when the ice broke on the river in spring.

In the middle of winter, the city of Kept Things rang out the coming of the thaw—and somewhere beneath the spire, a gate held by a woman named Lys began, at last, to open.
