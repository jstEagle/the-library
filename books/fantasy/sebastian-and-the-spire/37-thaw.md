## 37. Thaw

The snow began to fail at dawn.

Not melt—fail. It had fallen for nine days without melting, blue-fanged in the gutters, warm to the touch the way a corpse is warm only once, and then on the ninth morning it simply stopped being winter's snow and became water's again, all at once, as if some vast hand had unclenched. The eaves of Keptmarket dripped in ten thousand silver threads. The canals cracked their ice like eggshell. And across the city, in doorways and attics and shrine-niches where people had huddled through the long dark with their fires burning that wrong hungry blue, ordinary orange flame guttered back up in hearths and lamps, and people looked at it and wept without knowing why.

The spire stood above it all, black against the brightening sky, missing its crown.

Where the Hollow Crown had been there was now nothing but broken masonry and morning light pouring through the gap, and for the first time in living memory the tower cast a shadow that ended when shadows should end.

* * *

Inquisitor Elara Marr woke on the spire's ninth-floor landing with her face pressed against cold stone and her hands still folded in the attitude of prayer, though she could not remember praying.

She sat up slowly. Her coat was scorched down one side. Her ledgers—the great chained case she had carried for eleven years, the registry itself, every name of every gifted soul in the province—lay scattered around her where the blast of unbinding had thrown them, pages fanned open like wounded birds.

She should have gathered them. That was procedure. That was eleven years of procedure.

Instead she sat among them and looked at her hands.

They were shaking. Not from fear. From the thing she had seen.

She had been on the stairs when Orsen Whitlock and the knight and the thief broke through. She had drawn her iron and gone up to kill them—she told herself it was duty, though by then even that story had worn thin—and instead of killing them she had stopped, three steps below the summit chamber, because the door had been torn open and what stood inside was not a king enthroned.

It was hunger wearing a king. It was famine given a body and a throne and seven years of a city's warmth poured down its throat, and it had turned its face toward Marr—toward her, the faithful servant, the one who had registered the gifted and burned the gifted and called it order—and it had smiled with the dead king's mouth and said, in a voice like frost forming:

*You kept them coming, little clerk. So many warm things. You were my best shepherd.*

She had not fired the iron. She had not moved at all. She had watched a bone-carver read an old binding aloud in a voice that shook, watched a murdered king testify in a voice that did not, and watched the whole rotten pact come apart thread by thread like a garment rotting off a corpse, and in the last moment before it ended the entity had looked at her again—not with hatred, which she could have borne, but with *gratitude*—and something in Elara Marr had finished dying.

Now she got up. Her knees cracked. Somewhere below, water was running in the walls, the whole spire thawing around her like a thing exhaling after holding its breath too long.

She knelt and began gathering the registry pages—but not to save them.

By midmorning she had carried them down to the spire's outer court, where the execution posts stood in their new puddles of meltwater, and she built a fire of broken binding-wood, the same pale timber the founders had used to cage the famine-spirit, and she fed the registry into it page by page, name by name, three thousand names of people who had only ever wanted to be left alone.

A guard captain found her there. "Inquisitor. What are you doing?"

"Burning state property," Marr said. She did not look up. The fire had gone orange. Ordinary. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen. "You may arrest me if you like. I'll make it easy."

The captain looked at the fire, at the ash lifting, at the spire with its crown torn out, and he made the calculation that half the crown's servants would make that day and the other half would take another month to reach, and he said, "I saw no one," and walked away.

Marr burned until the last page curled. Then she took off the inquisitor's chain of office, weighed it in her palm—it was heavier than it needed to be; everything about the crown's service was heavier than it needed to be—and set it on top of the ashes.

Then she walked out of the court, past the guards who pretended not to see her, out into a city that was dripping and steaming and alive, and she did not look back at the spire. She went looking, instead, for a guildhouse that had burned, because there was a ledger there she needed to answer for, and a bone-carver she owed an apology that would never be large enough.

Some debts you pay by walking toward them.

* * *

Sera Voss slept for two days.

She woke in a smuggler's cot in the Undercity with her shoulder bandaged, her sword—her father's sword, the blade that remembered every man she'd killed—lying on the floor beside the bed where someone had put it within reach of her hand, and she knew before she opened her eyes who had done that, because only one person alive understood that a knight sleeps badly without steel near her fingers.

Wren Quill sat in the corner, very still, watching her with eyes that were like windows in an empty house.

"You're awake," Wren said.

"Apparently." Sera pushed herself up; the wound pulled, hot and honest. Alive, then. "How bad?"

"You held the line at the Bonegate for—" Wren paused, counting, and Sera realized they were having to count. "For longer than anyone should have. Eleven of ours died. You killed six of Marr's wardens and broke your old commander's sword-arm, and then you sat down against a wall and wouldn't get up."

"And Orsen?"

"Goes to the guildhouse today." Wren's voice was flat in a way Sera hadn't heard before, flat the way ground is flat after something has been dug out of it. "To close the ledgers. To let the dead go."

Sera looked at them properly. During the ascent, floor by floor, the spire had taken its tolls—memories, truths, small pieces of each of them—and Wren had paid more than anyone, hoarded memories surrendered one by one, and then at the end, at the summit, when the starving entity had offered winter's end for a willing vessel—

When Wren had emptied themselves entirely.

"What do you remember?" Sera asked quietly.

Wren considered the question with the seriousness they gave to locks and prices. "I remember the climb. I remember the cold. I remember that I chose." A pause. "There are holes shaped like people. One of them is big. I think—I think it might be a child. I think it might be me." They looked up, and there was something raw under the flatness. "Orsen says the holes don't have to stay holes. Says people can be re-taught. Re-given. Says that's what belonging is—not the memory, the choosing again."

"Do you believe them?"

"I believe Orsen answered a button yesterday," Wren said. "Gently. Like it was a friend knocking." They almost smiled. It was a terrible, wonderful attempt. "Ask me again in spring."

Sera swung her legs off the cot, hissed at the shoulder, and reached for the sword. "Help me dress. If Orsen's walking into that ruin today, they're not walking alone."

"They'll say they don't need—"

"They will," Sera agreed, buckling the baldric one-handed, "and we'll ignore them. That's what belonging is."

This time Wren did smile.

* * *

Lys Whitlock came down the spire's service stair at noon, carrying a box.

She had stayed behind at the inner gate while the others climbed, holding it shut against the wardens with a bar and her body and a stubbornness that surprised no one who had known her before the registry took her. She had held it for four hours. When it was over she had walked out past the bodies and the meltwater and the guards who didn't know whether to salute her or arrest her, and no one had arrested her, because everyone in the lower offices had seen her hold that gate, and courage, even late courage, buys a strange kind of amnesty.

The box held her belongings from eight years of service. A hairbrush. A registry pin she threw into the first canal she crossed. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a bone button—carved by a child's hands, crude, lopsided, a swallow trying to be a bird—which she had kept hidden in her boot lining through every inspection, every oath, every year, because it was the only warm thing the crown had never managed to take.

Her brother's first carving. Made for her, when they were children, before the voices, before the hunt, before everything.

She stood on the canal bridge in the dripping light and held it in her closed fist, and she did not hear it whisper, because Lys had never been gifted. But she remembered the boy who carved it, and she knew where he lived now—or where he would be today—and she started walking.

It took her until evening to reach Keptmarket, and she stood outside the burned guildhouse for a long time before she could make her feet move, and inside, in the smoke-black workshop where it had all begun, Orsen Whitlock looked up from an open ledger, saw their sister standing in the doorway, and did not speak either.

The thaw ran loud in the streets between them. Neither of them heard it.

* * *

Hale heard it perfectly.

Guildmaster Corvane Hale sat in the survivors' council cell beneath the Undercity—a clean cell, a fair cell, which was more than he deserved and less than he expected—with his hands folded and his ears full of water-sound filtering down through forty feet of stone, and he listened to the city thawing above him the way a buried man listens to rain.

He had confessed everything. The second ledger. The sales. The decades of it, back to the beginning, back to two gifted parents whose names he had written in the crown's book for thirty silver and a promise of discretion. He had confessed because Marr's suspicion had stripped him down to the wood, and because—though he would never say this aloud, not to the council, not to Orsen, not to any god who might be listening—because some part of him had been waiting years for someone to catch him. The kindness had been real. That was the horror of it. He had genuinely loved them, every gifted soul he'd sheltered and then sold, the way a farmer loves a field.

Footsteps on the stair. The cell door opened.

Not the council. Not yet. Just a runner with wet boots and a message.

"The carver's closed the ledgers," the runner said. "All of them. Let the bound ones go, every ghost in the guildhouse, right at sundown. Whole street said the building sighed." The runner shifted. "Council sits on your judgment tomorrow. Thought you'd want to know about the ledgers tonight."

Hale nodded. "Thank you."

The runner hesitated at the door. "The carver sent a word. For you." An uncomfortable pause. "Said: *the ledgers are closed, Corvane. Yours isn't. Come honest.*"

Hale sat alone after that, listening to the water, and for the first time in forty years he prayed—not for mercy, which he had priced and purchased and understood, but for the spine to walk up those stairs tomorrow without excuses in his mouth.

Come honest. It was more than he'd offered anyone. It would have to be enough.

* * *

And in the ruined workshop of the Gilderscale, as the sun went down over a dripping, steaming, astonished city, Orsen Whitlock opened the last impossible-ledger and spoke the release.

It took hours. Every object that had ever been entered—a locket with a mother in it, a ring with a brother, a sword with its hundred men, buttons and combs and door-handles and wedding bands, the whole crowded archive of the touched and the tethered—each one heard its name read aloud and felt the old knot loosen, and one by one the dead went wherever the dead go when someone finally tells them they're allowed.

Orsen worked through the list steadily, voice going rough, and Sera stood watch at the door and Wren sat on a stool cataloguing the silences opening up in the room—they couldn't help it, the habit of inventory—and when the last entry was read, the workshop was just a workshop. Wood and dust and smoke-smell and ordinary quiet.

Orsen set down the pen.

"That's all of them," Sera said softly.

"Not quite." Orsen looked at the doorway, where Lys still stood, still silent, still holding her box, and something in Orsen's chest that had been a hole for twenty years and then a wound for seven days finally, finally began to close. "One more voice came back today. It just hasn't said anything yet."

Lys stepped inside. Set down the box. Held out the button—the swallow, the first carving, the one thing she had fought eight years to keep.

"I brought this," she said. Her voice broke on the second word and she went on anyway, because the gate she'd held taught her how. "I don't know if it remembers you. I don't know if you want it to. I betrayed you once and I held a door once and I don't know which one I am yet. But I'm here, and the snow's melting, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "I thought maybe you'd tell me what it says."

Orsen took the button.

And in the deep quiet of the ruined guildhouse, with the whole city thawing overhead and dawn still hours away and chapter upon chapter of reckoning waiting in the morning—the council, the confessions, the long slow work of a kingdom learning to live without its hunger—Orsen Whitlock listened to a scrap of bone carved by a child's clumsy hands, and smiled through tears, and answered.

"Well," Orsen said. "It says hello, Lys. It says hello."
