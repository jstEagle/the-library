## 38. Ledgers Closed

The Gilderscale had burned for three days before the winter broke, and what the fire hadn't taken, the thaw had drowned. Orsen stood in the doorway of what had been their life for eleven years and let the smell of it arrive—wet ash, charred oak, the sweetish rot of paper that had gone to pulp in the meltwater. Above them, where the roof had been, the sky hung open and pale as a fresh-scraped bone.

"You don't have to do this today," Sera said behind them. She'd insisted on coming, sword at her hip out of habit rather than need; there was no one left in Ashfall who would raise a hand against a bone-carver now. Not after the spire came down. Not after the snow stopped.

"I have to do it sometime," Orsen said. "And the dead have waited long enough."

They stepped through the doorway, over the blackened sill that still bore the guild's motto carved into it—*ALL THINGS HAVE A PRICE*—and into the ruin.

The great hall was unrecognizable and yet entirely familiar. The long counter where clients had laid their impossible objects was a ridge of charcoal. The shelves that had held the ledgers were splinters. But the shape of the room remained, the way a scar keeps the shape of the wound, and Orsen found their feet moving along paths worn by a decade of walking: past the appraisal alcove, around the spot where Hale's desk had stood, to the iron-banded door of the vault, which—because it was iron, because it was built to keep things *in*—had survived almost intact.

The voices began before they touched it.

Not screaming. That was the thing no one understood about the dead lodged in objects: they didn't scream, not usually. They *waited*. They pressed up against the inside of whatever held them like faces against frosted glass, and they waited for someone with ears to come near. Eleven years, Orsen had kept the rule. *Never answer the dead.* Never let them know you can hear. Because once you answer one, you cannot stop hearing all of them, and once you start hearing all of them, you belong to them more than to anything living.

But the rule had been made by a frightened apprentice trying to survive, and Orsen Whitlock was done surviving.

"Hello," they said aloud, to the vault door, to everything behind it. "I'm here to close your accounts."

* * *

The vault opened on the second pull, hinges shrieking rust. Inside, the air was cold and still as well-water, and the objects sat in their labeled niches exactly as they had on the day Marr's men set the fire above them—the fire had never reached down here, though smoke had seeped through the vents and killed nothing, because these things could not die twice.

Orsen lit no lamp. They didn't need one. The objects glowed faintly, each with the color of its grief: a wedding ring burning soft gold with the bride who'd died before her wedding day; a soldier's whistle, blue-white, holding the breath of the boy who'd blown it in a trench at Vellmark; a child's wooden horse, gray as morning fog, carrying something small and patient that had been waiting forty years for someone to say its name.

Orsen took down the master ledger first—or what remained of it. The book had been in the hall when the fire came, and only half of it survived, pages fused into a brittle black fan. It didn't matter. Orsen had spent years keeping these accounts. They knew every entry by heart, the way a priest knows psalms.

They set the ruined ledger on an overturned crate, took out their carving knife and a fresh sheet of vellum salvaged from Sera's saddlebag, and began.

"Entry one," they said, and their voice echoed strangely in the vault, and every object brightened a little, the way coals breathe when stirred. "Lot 114. Silver locket, portrait miniature, hairwork interior. Deposited by Master Corvane Hale on behalf of an unnamed client, spring, nine years past." They paused. The locket sat in its niche, gold light pulsing like a heartbeat. "Contents: one spirit, self-identified as Maren Ostrewe, deceased. Final disposition—" and here Orsen's hand did not shake, because they had decided it would not, "—released. With apology."

They picked up the locket. Warmth flooded their palm, and Maren Ostrewe's voice arrived like a woman leaning close across a kitchen table: *You came back. You came back after all this time.*

"I'm sorry I waited so long," Orsen said. "There was a rule. It was a bad rule."

*I know about rules,* the dead woman said, and there was no bitterness in it, which was somehow worse. *My daughter had rules too. Tell her—*

"I will," Orsen said. "I promise. That's part of why I'm here. Every account closed properly. Every message delivered. That's the whole ledger, from now until it's done."

They opened the locket. Nothing dramatic happened—no burst of light, no rushing wind. The warmth simply left the metal, the way heat leaves a stone at dusk, and the little portrait inside was suddenly just paint and bone-dust and hair, and the silence where Maren Ostrewe had been was a clean silence, an *empty* silence, and Orsen stood holding it and wept without quite knowing why, and let themselves weep, because that too was part of closing accounts.

Sera appeared in the vault doorway but said nothing. She had learned, over the hard weeks since the spire, when to stand watch and when to simply stand.

* * *

It took four days.

Orsen worked dawn to dark, and sometimes past dark, by candlelight that made the niches glow like a city seen from a hill. They went shelf by shelf, niche by niche, reading each entry aloud, naming each dead thing, opening each container or breaking each binding or—in the case of the objects whose spirits had grown attached to the object itself, who *chose* to stay—recording the choice formally, witnessed, so that no future carver would mistake a home for a prison.

Some of them went easily. The soldier's whistle blew itself out in a single long note that no one else heard, and was just brass afterward. Some fought. A mirror in the deep corner had held a vain merchant's ghost for sixty years, and he raged at Orsen, called them a thief, a murderer, demanded his face back—and Orsen sat with him for most of a day, listening, until the rage wore down to fear and the fear wore down to the truth underneath, which was that he was afraid of being nothing, and Orsen told him gently that nothing was not what waited, that going on was not the same as ending, and whether he believed it or not, he went.

One niche stopped Orsen cold.

It was small, low, near the floor—a deposit box rather than a display niche, the kind used for items the guild didn't want clients asking about. The label read: *W., O. — HOLDING FEE WAIVED — C.H.*

Inside was a bone button.

Orsen knew it instantly. Knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Carved from sheep knuckle, polished with beeswax, a spiral worked into the face—it was the first thing they had ever carved that spoke. They'd been nine years old. Their mother had held it up to the lamplight and laughed and said, *Listen to you, chattering away at it,* not knowing that the button was chattering back, that it carried the voice of the sheep it had come from, dim and placid, complaining about rain. It was the first proof that Orsen's strangeness was also a kind of company. It was the reason they had survived being what they were.

Hale had confiscated it their first week at the guild. *Distraction,* he'd said. *A carver who talks to his work makes sentimental cuts.* And Orsen, fifteen and grateful for a roof, had let it go.

Now they turned it in their fingers. The sheep-voice was still there, faint, placid, complaining about rain in a field that no longer existed.

"Hello, old friend," Orsen whispered.

*Rain,* said the button, contentedly. *Rain, rain, wet grass, good grass.*

"No," Orsen said softly. "You stay. You're not an account to be closed. You're—" They searched for the word and found it. "You're mine. You've always been mine."

They sewed it inside their coat, over the heart, where it murmured about weather for the rest of the day, and Orsen found the work went easier after that.

* * *

On the fifth day, the survivors' council came.

They arrived at midmorning, picking through the rubble of the market road: a dozen figures in mismatched clothes, Undercity smugglers beside Gilderscale clerks beside two of the spire's freed servants still wearing livery they no longer had any right to. Wren Quill walked among them with their hands in their pockets and their eyes hollowed and quiet—they were still mostly empty, the famine having drunk their hoarded memories, and they remembered the last year only in fragments, but they had asked to come, and Orsen was glad of it. At the council's center walked Dame Sera Voss, restored to her name by decree of a kingdom that barely existed yet, and beside her, at the head of the column, moved a figure no living eye could see but every dead thing in the ruin turned toward like flowers toward sun.

Aldric had stayed. Everyone had expected him to go on when the binding broke—he had finished his testimony, the crown was ash, the famine fed and sleeping in Wren's emptied memory-vaults until spring should wake it gently into the world—but the true king had looked at the ruined city, at the confused and grieving people picking through the thaw, and said, *Seven years I watched my kingdom be eaten. I will stay until it is planted again.* Ghosts, it turned out, could choose their unfinished business. His was a garden.

He drifted ahead of the council now, through the broken wall, and Orsen heard him draw breath he didn't need.

*So this is where they kept you,* Aldric said. *All those years. A counting-house for ghosts.*

"It paid the rent," Orsen said.

The council arranged itself in the ruined hall, on crates and rubble, and Hale was brought in.

They had kept him in the Undercity since the fall of the spire—not in chains, because chains meant nothing to a man that old and that tired, but under watch. He walked between two smugglers with his hands bound in front of him anyway, because he had asked for the bonds. *It will look better,* he'd said, *if it looks like I was brought.* Even now. Even now, calculating how the record would read.

Corvane Hale had been handsome once; Orsen could see the architecture of it under the ruin of him. He had lost weight in custody, and his fine clothes hung on him, and his eyes—those warm, appraising eyes that had made every frightened orphan feel chosen—moved across the ruined hall, the assembled survivors, and settled on Orsen with an expression that was almost gentle.

"Whitlock," he said. "You look well. Prison agrees with some people."

"Hale." Orsen stood behind the overturned crate where the ledgers lay. "This is a closing of accounts. Yours is one of them."

Hale's gaze flicked to the vellum, the knife, the neat columns of Orsen's hand. Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, maybe, of the craft of it. He had taught them double-entry himself, in the third year. *Every debt must appear twice,* he'd said. *Once as given, once as owed. That's the whole secret of honest business.*

"Then proceed," he said, and folded himself down onto a chunk of fallen beam like a man taking his seat at auction.

Orsen opened the second ledger—the true one, the hidden one, pulled from beneath the flagstone in Hale's office three days ago, its entries in Hale's own immaculate hand. Dates. Names. Prices. Thirty-one names over nineteen years, gifted clients marked with a small crown symbol, sold to the registry, sold onward to the spire. Orsen's parents' names were in it, dated twenty-six years back, in ink slightly older than the rest. The first entry. The founding transaction.

"Corvane Hale," Orsen read, voice steady, "you are charged by those present with the sale of thirty-three persons to the crown's registry, of whom twenty-nine are confirmed dead in the spire's service. You are further charged with the concealment and exploitation of bound spirits for profit, being one hundred and six lots recorded herein, now released or accounted for. You are further charged with the betrayal of—" and here Orsen paused, and chose precision over venom, "—of persons who trusted you, being named: Ostrewe, Maren. Voss, Tam. Whitlock, Edren. Whitlock, Maren—"

"My mother's name was Maren too," Orsen added, looking up. "You'll note I checked."

Hale's composure flickered, just once, like a lamp in wind.

"Do you deny these charges?" asked Grelda Moss, the smuggler-council's speaker, a broad grey-haired woman with a voice like a dock bell.

"No," Hale said.

The word dropped into the ruin and lay there. Several of the clerks looked away. One of the freed servants began quietly to cry.

"Then hear the judgment of this council," Grelda said. "We are not the crown. We have no dungeons and no headsman, and we are not minded to make ourselves into either. Hear it, then: Corvane Hale is stripped of name and guild. No one in Ashfall, above or below, will trade with him, house him, hire him, or bury him under their own stone. He will spend the rest of his life working off the debts in this ledger—under supervision, at labor chosen by this council, beginning with the graves. Twenty-nine graves, Hale. You will dig every one yourself, and carve every marker, and you will carve the true names, and you will learn them by heart, and if anyone ever asks you about any of those people, you will tell them the truth, all of it, for as long as you live."

Hale bowed his head. And then, because he was still Hale, he said, "May I make a request regarding the markers?"

"Speak."

"Slate, not limestone. Limestone weeps in the rain. These people have been wept over enough." He lifted his eyes to Orsen. "Your mother would have agreed. She had opinions about stone."

"Don't," Orsen said quietly. "Don't use her. That's the last thing you don't get to do."

And Hale—who had lied to inquisitors, sold kings' subjects, and worn kindness like a coat for fifty years—looked at the orphan he had raised as an investment and, for perhaps the first time in his life, simply accepted a loss. He nodded. "Understood," he said. "Whitlock. For what it is worth, which I grant is very little: you were the best thing this guild ever produced. Not the most profitable. The best."

"That," Orsen said, "is worth exactly what it cost me. Which was everything. Now—" they turned to the council, "—there remains one final entry."

They took up the pen. On the last page of the new ledger, beneath a hundred releases and dispositions, they wrote:

*Final entry. The Gilderscale Guild of Impossible Objects is hereby dissolved. All accounts closed. All debts recorded. All debts assigned. May every soul released find what it was owed, and may no one ever again make a trade of the dead.*

They signed it. Then, one by one, the council came forward and signed beneath—Grelda with a smuggler's mark, the clerks with careful hands, Wren Quill with a strange looping signature that was itself a stolen memory of someone else's name, Sera Voss with a knight's blunt strokes. Last of all, Aldric drifted to the page, and where a king might have pressed a seal, he laid one fingertip, and frost bloomed in the shape of a crown, and melted, leaving only clean damp paper.

*Well kept,* the dead king said to Orsen, in the voice of a man closing a long campaign. *Better than I kept mine.*

"Yours isn't closed yet," Orsen said. "Spring's coming. There's a garden."

*So there is.* He looked around the ruined hall a last time—at the empty niches, the silent objects, the place where a man had counted other people's souls like coin. *Come. Let's leave this place to its quiet. It has earned it.*

* * *

They walked out together into the late afternoon, the council dispersing toward the gates, Hale led away to begin his twenty-nine graves. At the threshold Orsen stopped, turned back, and looked a long moment at the shell of the Gilderscale—the scorched motto over the door, *ALL THINGS HAVE A PRICE*, half-eaten by fire so that it now read, if you squinted, *ALL THINGS HAVE A*—

"Well," Sera said, following their gaze. "That's about right."

Orsen smiled, touched the button sewn over their heart—*rain, good grass*—and pulled the door shut behind them for the last time.

They were halfway down the market road when Wren caught up, walking fast, face pale under the fading bruises of emptiness. "Orsen. Wait." They were breathing hard, though the news had clearly outrun them already. "Grelda's runners just came through from the coast road. There's a cart coming up from the south—crown colors, or what's left of them. Registry escort." Wren swallowed. "They're saying there's a woman in it. They're saying she asked for you by name. They're saying she calls herself Lys Whitlock, and she's carrying papers signed seven years ago, and she wants to know—" Wren's voice cracked, gentled, "—she wants to know if anyone remembers her sister."
