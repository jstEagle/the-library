## 39. Quiet, At Last

The shop smelled of cedar shavings and beeswax, and for the first time in Orsen Whitlock's life, that was all it smelled of.

They noticed it most mornings now—the absence. It arrived with them when they unlocked the door at dawn, sat down with them over tea, kept them company through the long hours of carving the way a well-behaved dog keeps a person company: present, warm, asking nothing. Silence used to be a wall Orsen pressed their ear against, straining for what moved on the other side. Now it was simply a room they lived in.

Most days.

Not every day. That was the honest accounting, and Orsen had spent enough years keeping ledgers to know that honesty was the only entry that never needed correcting. The gift hadn't died when the Hollow Crown came apart above the city like a knot slipping loose. It had faded—thinned the way a river thins in late summer, still running, but shallow enough to see the stones beneath. Some mornings Orsen could pick up a client's heirloom and hear nothing at all. Nothing, and then a kind of vertigo, and then the strange grief of a man who has lived beside a noisy neighbor for thirty years and finds the house next door empty.

Other mornings—

This morning, there was a button on the workbench that hadn't been there last night.

Orsen stood in the doorway with their coat half off and looked at it for a long moment before crossing the room. Small thing. Sheep bone, by the color of it, yellowed at the crown where a thumb had worried it for years. Four holes. A chip out of one edge, old and worn smooth, the kind of damage that happens decades before anyone thinks to mourn it.

They hadn't carved it. They were certain of that. And no client had come through the door since yesterday's closing bell, when old Mistress Anselm had collected her grandmother's thimble—silent now, or nearly—and paid with coins counted twice and a look of such naked relief that Orsen had thought about it half the night.

So: a button, on the bench, uninvited.

Orsen did not touch it. That was the first rule they'd ever learned, back when the voices were a flood and Hale's kindness was the only raft in sight, and though so much else had burned—the guildhouse, the ledgers, the man himself reduced to a gray figure awaiting judgment in the survivors' council hall—the rules had a way of outliving their reasons. You didn't touch an object you couldn't account for. Not because the dead were dangerous. Because *you* might be, if you answered without thinking.

But the gift was quiet this morning, and curiosity is a different animal from duty.

Orsen hung their coat, banked the stove, ground the day's first cup of tea, and worked for two hours within arm's reach of the button without touching it. They carved a set of chessmen for the school on Tanner's Row—Sera had bullied the headmistress into commissioning them, arguing that children who'd watched a spire torn down stone by stone deserved something to build with instead. The knife moved. The shavings curled. Outside, the street woke up in its ordinary way: the milk cart, the argument between the fishmonger and his brother-in-law, a child running past the window with a stick held out like a lance.

And underneath all of it, faint as breath on a cold pane, the button waited.

It wasn't calling to them. That was the remarkable part. In the old days—in the years before the hunt, before Marr, before everything—an object left on the bench would have clamored. The dead lodged in things had been desperate creatures, mostly, pressing their faces against the glass of their little prisons, mouthing words at anyone who happened to glance their way. Orsen had built a whole life out of not looking.

The button simply sat. And when Orsen finally set down the knife, wiped their hands, and reached for it—slowly, the way you reach toward a bird you don't want to startle—it said:

*Hello.*

Just that. A woman's voice, dry as autumn paper, unhurried. Not a scream. Not a plea. A greeting, offered the way you'd offer one across a fence to a neighbor whose name you knew but had never asked.

Orsen sat down.

The stool creaked under them. Sunlight had crawled halfway across the workbench, laying a bright bar across the button's four holes, and dust drifted through it in the slow golden way dust does when nothing in the world is in a hurry. Orsen turned the button once between finger and thumb. The voice inside it stirred, comfortable, like someone settling deeper into a chair by a fire.

*Cold hands,* the woman observed. *You want to mind the chill. My mother always said it. Cold hands, warm heart, she said, but she said it about everyone, so I never knew if it was true.*

"I'm sorry," Orsen said. Their own voice sounded strange in the quiet—rusty, almost, as if they'd been saving it up. "I don't know how you came to be here."

*Don't you?* A pause, and then something that was almost laughter, papery and fond. *Boy found me in the rubble. Up where the tower was. He plays at the edge of the garden now—they let the children in, since the thaw. He picked me up because I was shiny, and his gran told him to put it down, rubbish, she said, and he set me on a wall, and the wall remembered me, and the wall told the wind, and the wind—" She trailed off, the ghostly equivalent of a shrug. "Things talk more than they used to. Since the crown broke. Did you know that?"

"I'd heard," Orsen said carefully.

*You're the carver.* Not a question. *The one who listened. My sister's daughter lives on Coriander Street. She brought my buttons to your old shop once, years ago. Wanted to know if I was in them.* Another pause, longer this time. *You told her no.*

Orsen's hand went still around the button.

They remembered—or they remembered the shape of it, which after everything was sometimes the same thing. A young woman, rain-wet, holding out a small cloth bundle with the desperate care of someone carrying eggs. Buttons from a dead aunt's sewing tin. And Orsen, twenty-six years old and drowning in other people's dead, had looked at the bundle without touching it and said the sentence they'd said ten thousand times: *I'm sorry. There's nothing in them. I can't help you.*

A lie. Of course it had been a lie. The aunt had been in the buttons—Orsen had felt her the moment the bundle crossed the threshold, chattering away about hem lengths and a nephew who never wrote. But answering meant involvement, and involvement meant the guild noticing, and the guild noticing meant Hale's ledger, and Hale's ledger meant—

Well. Everyone knew now what Hale's ledger meant.

"You're right," Orsen said quietly. "I told her no. I shouldn't have."

*Oh, I'm not here to scold.* The voice was brisk, almost cheerful. *I'm dead, dear. Scolding's for the living; we haven't the energy. I'm here because the boy put me on the wall and the wall passed me along, and somewhere in the passing-along somebody said, take her to the carver, the carver answers now. Do you answer now?*

Orsen looked at the button turning slowly in their fingers. Bone, sheep's bone, cheap, mass-produced, the kind of thing a seamstress bought by the dozen and lost by the dozen. Forty years in a sewing tin. A niece who'd carried it through the rain hoping for a word. A boy on a wall. A wall that talked. A wind that carried.

"Yes," Orsen said. "I answer now."

*Good.* And the button sighed—not sadly, but with the relief of a traveler setting down a pack at last. *Then tell me if you'd be so kind: did my roses come back? The ones by the south wall. The frost took them the winter before I went, and I never got to see—*

"They'll come back," Orsen said. "The whole city's coming back. You should see Coriander Street. There's a gardener there—Wren—who's planting things nobody's planted in fifty years."

*Wren.* The name settled into the button like a seed into soil. *That's a thief's name, isn't it? I remember thieves' names. We used to trade them like recipes, in my day.*

"It was," Orsen said. "It's a gardener's name now."

* * *

Sera Voss came by at noon, as she did most days, ostensibly to inspect the door hinge she'd repaired last week and actually to eat lunch where someone else had cooked it. She filled the doorway the way she filled every doorway—broad, gray-templed, moving with the careful economy of a body that had been broken and reassembled and never quite trusted again. Her sword hung at her hip out of habit rather than need; these days the only thing she cut was bread.

"Shop looks good," she said, which was what she always said, and then, because she was Sera, she actually looked. At the new shelves. At the mended window with its diamond panes slightly wrong, salvaged from three different houses. At the sign outside—WHITLOCK, CARVER & RECKONER—which Wren had painted and which had caused a week of argument, because *reckoner* wasn't a real trade and Orsen had insisted it was the only honest word for what they did.

"Hinge's fine," Orsen said. "You checked it Tuesday."

"Hinges loosen." She dropped onto the customer's bench, which groaned. "Anything today?"

Orsen considered lying, considered the old reflex, and set the button gently on the bench between them instead.

"She wants to know about roses on Coriander Street. South wall. Someone should ask the gardener."

Sera looked at the button for a long moment. Two years ago—a year ago, even six months ago—she would have looked at it the way soldiers look at unfamiliar weapons: assessing threat. Now she just nodded, slow, and something in her face softened into the expression she wore only in this shop, the one that had no armor in it.

"The dead are quieter," she said. "But there's more of them talking. Does that make sense?"

"Perfect sense." Orsen turned the button over. "Before, they screamed because no one listened. Now some of them just... visit."

*A knight?* the button asked, interested. *She walks like one. My husband walked like that, after the war. Like the floor owed him money.*

"She says you walk like a soldier," Orsen relayed.

"I walk like a woman whose knees are sixty years old," Sera said, but she glanced down at the button anyway, and her hand twitched toward it—an old instinct, the instinct to draw steel at trouble—and stopped. "What's her name?"

"I didn't ask."

"Ask." Sera said it plainly, without judgment, the way she said most true things. "Names matter. Took me too long to learn that. Ask everyone's name. Even theirs. *Especially* theirs."

So Orsen asked, and the button told them: Maren Tull, seamstress, born in the reign of the old king's father, died abed at seventy-one with her family around her, which made her, as she herself pointed out with papery pride, *the luckiest woman in this whole city, and don't you forget it.* She had married a soldier named Dav who came home thin and strange and grew roses because he said flowers never lied to him. She had hemmed dresses for three generations of one family until her eyes went. She had one regret, and it was small, and it was the roses.

By the time Sera left—taking a message for Wren about the south wall, and a heel of bread, and, at the door, pausing to say *good afternoon, Mistress Tull* with grave courtesy that made the button go silent with surprise—Orsen was crying a little, and didn't entirely know why, and didn't entirely mind.

* * *

Wren came at dusk.

They came differently now than they used to. The memory-thief who had once emptied themselves into the mouth of a famine-spirit to buy a city's spring had come back from that bargain hollowed, and everyone could see it: the quickness gone from their hands, the hoard of stolen recollections burned away to ash and warmth, leaving behind a person who had to *learn* things the ordinary way, day by day, like everyone else. Sometimes Orsen caught them staring at their own reflection with the baffled patience of someone reading a stranger's diary. Sometimes Wren caught Orsen watching, and shrugged, and said, *Still better than being a pantry for a god.*

But they were learning. That was the thing. Wren Quill, who had spent a lifetime taking, was learning how to grow.

They came in through the front door like a civilized person, which was new, and set a clay pot on the workbench, which was newer.

"Roses," Wren said. "South wall, Coriander Street. First buds should show in three weeks. Tell your tenant."

Orsen lifted the pot. Inside it, wound among the roots, a button-sized whisper of gratitude rose up—Maren Tull, catching the scent of turned earth through whatever senses the dead keep, making a sound that was not quite a sob and not quite a song.

"She hears it," Orsen said. "Thank you."

"Mm." Wren perched on the bench, legs swinging, and studied the shop with those eyes that had once seen the inside of ten thousand minds. "You've changed the sign again."

"I added a line."

Orsen turned the hanging board around. Beneath WHITLOCK, CARVER & RECKONER, in fresh paint, smaller letters: *Inquiries answered. All inquiries.*

Wren read it twice. "That's a dangerous promise."

"It's an honest one." Orsen set Maren's button down in a shallow dish of soft cloth they'd prepared weeks ago, without quite admitting to themselves why. "I spent my whole life refusing to answer. Then I spent a year learning what it costs when the powerful refuse to answer—when a king won't say he's dead, when a guildmaster won't say what he sold, when a registry won't say where the taken ones went." They touched the dish's rim. "Silence isn't neutral, Wren. I used to think it was. It's just a debt that collects interest."

"And now you're paying it back one button at a time?" Wren's mouth curved. "There are thousands of them out there. Objects with tenants. Walls full of them, since the thaw. The Undercity ghosts alone—you could spend forty years just on the bridge arches."

"I know."

"That's not a retirement. That's a vocation."

"I know that too." Orsen poured tea—three cups, out of habit, though Wren was the only guest—and slid one across. "I'm not young. My hands ache when it rains. Some mornings I pick up a locket and hear nothing, and I grieve, which is absurd, grieving for the noise." They shook their head. "But the mornings it works—days like today—I wouldn't trade it. Not anymore. I used to think the gift was a burden the dead had hung on me. Something done *to* me." They looked at the button, resting in its cloth like an egg in a nest. "It was never that. It was an invitation. I just spent thirty years pretending I hadn't heard it."

*Maren* —the voice drifted up, drowsy now, contented, smelling roses in a pot in a shop that smelled of cedar—*tell the thief thank you. And tell the knight her boots are unlaced.*

Orsen relayed both messages. Wren laughed—really laughed, the first time in months—and Sera, returning for her forgotten gloves, knelt in the doorway to retie her boots while a dead seamstress critiqued her knots, and the lamp hissed, and the tea steamed, and outside the window the first star came up over the gap in the skyline where the spire had stood, a gap the city was only beginning to learn how to look at without flinching.

Later, when the shop was dark and locked, Orsen sat a while in the back room with the ledger open—not the impossible-ledger, closed forever, its final page bearing the seal of the survivors' council, but a plain book, new, blank except for one line entered that morning in careful hand:

*Maren Tull. Seamstress. Roses, south wall, Coriander Street. Answered.*

One entry. The first of thousands, maybe. Orsen blew out the lamp.

Through the wall, faint and friendly, the button murmured *goodnight*, and Orsen, smiling in the dark, answered.

Outside, far to the east, the first crocuses were pushing up through the ruin-garden soil, and a child was asking her mother why the tower was gone—and somewhere in the space between those two small wonders, spring was getting ready to tell the whole story over again, from the beginning.
