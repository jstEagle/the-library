## 40. Sebastian and the Spire

Spring came to the city the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once.

Orsen felt it first in the ground. They knelt in the dirt where the spire's shadow had fallen for three hundred years, a circle of earth that had never once known sunlight, and pressed their thumb into the soil. It was warm. Warm, and soft, and smelling of rain and rot turning into something sweeter. Beneath everything, faint as a pulse through a wrist, Orsen could feel it: nothing. No voices. No dead murmuring in the stones. Just earth being earth, patient and dumb and alive.

They had torn the spire down over the course of a winter that finally ended. It had taken four months, two hundred laborers, and an act of civic will no one quite knew how to explain to themselves. The council had argued about it first—the tower was a landmark, a wonder, the tallest thing human hands had ever raised—and then Marr had stood before them with the burned registry under one arm and said, quietly, *I have read what was done inside those walls, and I will not stand beneath them another day,* and the argument had ended. The stones came down cart by cart. Some were used to rebuild the guildhall's collapsed east wing. Some went into the harbor wall. A few—those from the upper floors, where the binding-wrought horrors had lived—were sunk in the deep channel beyond the bay, weighted with chain, because some things deserved neither reuse nor remembrance.

Now there was only a wide bare ring of foundation, pale as an old scar, and the city had decided, without anyone decreeing it, that the scar should be a garden.

* * *

"You're digging wrong," Sera said.

She stood at the edge of the plot with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hair tied back with a strip of red cloth that had once been a knight's favor and was now just a rag she liked. In her hands she carried a mattock like it was a sword she'd made peace with. The disgraced knight had become, over the winter, something else—not restored, never that; the order would not take her back and she had stopped asking—but *re-homed*, the way a blade is re-hilted. She still moved like a soldier. But she smiled now, sometimes, without checking who saw.

"I'm digging *traditionally*," Orsen said. "The way my mother taught me."

"Your mother taught you bone-carving."

"She taught me both. She said they were the same craft. You find the shape already living inside the material, and you set it free." Orsen turned the spade and lifted a clod of dark earth. "She'd have liked this garden."

Sera's expression did the thing it always did when Orsen spoke of their parents—a small tightening around the eyes, grief offered and refused in the same motion. "Then dig like her," she said, gentler, and drove the mattock into the ground beside them, and for a while they worked in the easy rhythm of two people who had held a line together and never needed to speak of it.

Wren arrived sometime past midmorning, drifting up the hill from the harbor road with a sack of seed packets clutched to their chest like contraband. They looked better than they had in winter. That wasn't saying much—in the weeks after the famine-spirit took their hoard, Wren had been a gray ghost of themselves, hollowed out, staring at their own empty hands as if trying to remember what they'd been for. But spring was doing its work. There was color in their face again. And though the stolen memories were gone—every memory they'd ever thieved, given away in a single burning act of generosity that still made Orsen's throat close to think about—they were building new ones. Slowly. Deliberately. Like a man planting an orchard he knew he'd never sit in the shade of.

"I brought marigolds," Wren announced. "And beans. And something called a moonflower, which the seed merchant swore blooms only at night, which seems like a personal failing on the flower's part."

"You hate gardens," Sera said.

"I hated *memories*. Gardens are new territory." Wren crouched at the plot's edge and studied the turned earth with the narrowed eyes of a thief casing a house. Then, quieter: "Is this where it stood? Exactly here?"

"This is where its heart was," Orsen said. "The summit room. We're standing in the footprint of the throne."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then they tore open a seed packet and began to plant, right there, in the exact spot where the Hollow Crown had fed on a city's warmth for seven years. Marigolds. Gold as small suns, planted over the grave of a hunger.

Orsen watched them do it and thought: *that is the bravest thing I have seen since the walls opened.* Which was saying something, because Orsen had seen Sera Voss refuse an order twice, walk back into a burning building for a stranger, and kneel to a ghost-king without flinching.

* * *

The child appeared in the early afternoon, the way children do—suddenly, and from an improbable direction.

She was perhaps seven, gap-toothed, wearing a man's coat cut down badly by someone who loved her, and she had been watching them work from behind the low wall for the better part of an hour. Orsen had noticed her of course. Noticing things was the last relic of the old gift: they still caught every small presence at the edge of sight, every held breath, every curiosity too shy to show itself. The gift had faded after the unbinding—most days, Orsen heard nothing at all from the objects they touched. But attention remained. Attention, it turned out, had never been magic. It had only been practice.

Finally the girl worked up her courage, crossed the bare foundation-ring with the solemn tread of someone entering a cathedral, and stopped at the edge of the turned earth.

"Are you planting a garden?"

"We are," Orsen said.

"Why here? There's nothing here."

"There's everything here," Wren said, not looking up. "It's just very small so far."

The girl frowned at them, unconvinced, and then pointed at the pale ring of stone all around, the scar of foundations, the absence rising up into the sky where the tallest thing in the world used to be. "My da says a bad king lived in the tower. He says the tower ate people." She lowered her voice, thrilled and frightened. "Did it eat *you*?"

Orsen set down the spade.

Sera went still. Wren looked up. And Orsen found themselves, absurdly, at the edge of something—the way you stand at the top of a stair in the dark, knowing the next step exists but unable to see it.

Because how did you answer that? How did you tell a child about the ledger of small impossible things, about lockets full of begging mothers, about a knight's sword heavy with the names of dead men, about a cell of perfect quiet and a sister holding a gate? How did you explain that the tower hadn't eaten them—that they had walked in carrying their own name, and walked out lighter by a sister and heavier by a kingdom?

There was a version of Orsen, two years ago, who would have said *it's a long story, little one,* and meant *never ask me again.*

But that Orsen had learned something in a warded cell where no dead could speak. Silence was not peace. Silence was just a story nobody told.

"It didn't eat me," Orsen said. "I climbed it."

The girl's eyes went enormous. "You *climbed* it?"

"With friends." Orsen nodded at Sera, who gave the child her best courtly bow, ruined slightly by the mud on her hands. "That's Sera. She fought the guards. And that's Wren, who can—" Orsen paused. "Who knows doors."

"Doors are important," Wren said gravely.

"And there was someone else," the girl said. "Everyone says there was a ghost. A *king* ghost."

Orsen looked out across the garden-to-be, at the marigold seeds sleeping in the dark of the throne-room's grave, and felt the old ache that had never quite healed and had, somewhere along the way, stopped wanting to. Aldric. Seven years a prisoner in his own body, and one spring morning walking out of it into whatever waits beyond the world, pausing only to press his cold hand to Orsen's shoulder and say, *Tell them. Whatever else you do with your life—tell them what happened here. Grief that isn't spoken becomes hunger.*

He had been right. He had been right about everything except how much it would cost to obey him.

"Yes," Orsen said. "There was a king."

The girl sat down cross-legged at the edge of the plot, coat pooling around her, waiting with the terrible patience of the young. Behind her, other shapes had begun to gather at the low wall—drawn, perhaps, by the sight of the famous bone-carver kneeling in the dirt, or simply by the oldest gravity in the world, which is the pull of a story about to begin.

Orsen wiped their hands on their knees. Picked up a handful of warm earth and let it crumble slowly, feeling it fall, feeling—just barely, just maybe, in the deep quiet where the voices used to live—nothing but the earth itself, and the spring, and the small living weight of everyone listening.

"There was a king," Orsen said again, and found they were smiling. "His name was Aldric, and he loved his city more than his own life, which was unfortunate, because there were people who intended to test exactly how much more..."

* * *

They stayed until the light went gold.

The girl—her name was Sebby, she declared, short for Sebastian, and she would answer to nothing else—asked forty questions and interrupted every answer with a better one. Other children crept closer. Then a fishwife. Then two apprentices from the rebuilt guildhall, who pretended they were passing by and stayed for an hour. By the time the shadows lengthened, half the neighborhood was sitting on the old foundation stones, and Orsen had gotten as far as the night the voices rose, and every object in a lonely workshop screaming warnings at once.

"—and I thought I was going mad," Orsen said. "But the dead weren't mad. They were *afraid*. They could feel what was coming, the way animals feel a flood. And that was the night I understood that being able to hear them was never the burden. The burden was pretending I couldn't."

Sebby's hand shot up. "What happened to the bad thing in the crown?"

"That," said Orsen, "is the last part of the story. And the last part needs everyone comfortable, because it took three people, one ghost, and a great many marigolds."

A ripple of laughter went through the listeners. Sera, leaning against the low wall with her arms crossed, caught Orsen's eye and raised one eyebrow—the old look, the *are you sure about this* look, the look she'd worn on the ninth floor of the spire with the binding-wrought horrors singing above them. Orsen answered it the same way they'd answered it then. With a small nod. *We go on.*

Wren had stopped planting. They sat in the middle of the future flowerbed, knees drawn up, listening with their whole hollowed-out self, and when Orsen glanced at them, Wren mouthed something no one else could see:

*Tell it true.*

So Orsen did.

They told it true all the way through the dusk: the false king humming his hunger-song in a gilded frame. The second ledger with its columns of sold names. The flight through the bone-cellars while the workshop burned, and how losing everything had felt, horribly, like being set down after years of carrying. The Undercity, and the ghosts who voted, and an old king kneeling in the dust to beg forgiveness of the least of his subjects. Lys at the inner gate—here Orsen's voice broke, just slightly, and Sera's hand found their shoulder and stayed there—and how forgiveness was a door and not a debt, and how Lys had held that door open with her whole body while they passed.

And then the summit. The desiccated king on the throne. The famine-spirit, ancient and starving, offering Orsen a place among the kept things—warmth without cost, belonging without risk, a shelf in the dark where nothing could ever hurt them again.

"What did you say?" Sebby whispered.

Orsen looked at the marigold bed. At Wren, who had paid for all of them out of the only treasury they had. At the sky going violet over the harbor, where the first star of evening was coming out over water that no longer froze in June.

"I said no," Orsen said. "I said belonging that costs other people their names isn't belonging. It's just a bigger cell." They paused. "And then I read the old binding aloud, word by word, while a dead king testified to what had been done to him. Because truth was the only blade that worked. It's the only blade that ever works."

Silence, then—the good kind, the kind Orsen had spent a lifetime fearing and had finally, finally made peace with. The silence of people holding a story gently, the way you hold something that has just finished hatching.

Sebby stood, brushed off her enormous coat, and surveyed the garden plot with the air of a general inspecting troops.

"When the flowers grow," she said, "will you tell it again? All of it? From the beginning?"

Orsen looked at Sera. Sera looked at Wren. Wren, who remembered none of their own childhood but every word of Orsen's story, grinned like a thief at a door left unlocked.

"Every spring," Orsen said. "As long as you keep coming to listen."

"Deal." Sebby spat in her palm and held it out, and Orsen, laughing, shook it, sealing the oldest kind of contract—the kind no ledger could hold.

The child ran off home through the blue dusk, coat flapping, shouting to the whole street that she knew the person who killed the tower. The crowd melted away by degrees, back to suppers and lamplit windows. And the three of them remained in the garden that did not exist yet, in the footprint of the throne, surrounded by seeds asleep in the dark.

Sera stretched until something in her back cracked. "You know," she said, "the order would have made a saint out of Aldric by now. Statues, feast days, the whole theater. Instead we've got marigolds and a seven-year-old with a spitting handshake."

"He'd have preferred it this way," Orsen said. "He hated statues. Said they were just stones wearing your face and telling your lies."

Wren patted the soil flat over the last seed, carefully, tenderly, the way you'd tuck in a child. "Do you think they'll grow?" they asked. "Here? On top of all that?"

Orsen knelt and laid their palm flat against the earth one last time. Warm. Alive. Quiet in the way a house is quiet when everyone in it is safe and sleeping.

"Things grow where the truth gets buried," they said. "That's practically all soil is."

They walked down the hill together as the lamps came on, three shadows among many, unhurried, unhunted, going home. And behind them the garden waited in the dark with its small buried suns, keeping the story the way the earth keeps everything—

patiently,

and ready

for spring.
