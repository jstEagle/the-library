## 2. What the Mountain Remembers

Wren did not sleep that night. He sat by the banked fire of the smugglers' camp with his knees drawn up and listened to the mules breathe, because the mules were easier to listen to than the voice.

*Wren Brightwater,* it had said, in the old woman's shrine, in a whisper like snow sliding off a pine bough. *I know your name. I have always known your name.*

He had done what he always did when the world pressed too close: he had said nothing, touched the nearest mule's nose — a gray gelding named Turnip who leaned into his palm like a great affectionate door — and walked away as fast as dignity allowed.

But the voice had followed him up the trail. Of course it had. Voices that lived in shrines did not need legs.

"You're not real," he told the fire, very quietly, so that Brannick the caravan master wouldn't hear him talking to himself again. Brannick already thought Wren was odd in the way of men who liked animals more than people, which was fine. Odd was safe. Odd was not *burned.*

*I am as real as the mountain,* the voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, patient as stone. *And I am dying, which is more than the mountain can say.*

"Good," Wren whispered. "Die somewhere else."

A pause. Then, to his horror, the voice laughed — a dry, rustling sound, like wind through autumn bracken, and underneath it something genuinely amused.

*Three hundred years of silence, and my first vessel tells me to die elsewhere. Sebastian wept when I found him. You tell me to take my business elsewhere.*

The name landed in Wren's chest like a dropped stone. Sebastian. The name from the ruined shrine above Thornfell, the one carved into the heart-stone where no honest pilgrim went anymore. His mother had told him the story once, hushed, before the fires took her: *the man who carried the god and hid him, and was unmade for hiding.*

"I'm not anybody's vessel," Wren said. "I'm nobody. That's the whole point of me."

*Nobody,* Vaelith repeated, and there was something almost tender in it now, which was worse than the amusement. *You speak to beasts and call yourself nobody. You have spent your whole life being quiet so loudly that even the gods heard you. Child, there is no such thing as a silence that complete. It is a shout.*

Turnip nudged Wren's shoulder with a nose like warm velvet, and Wren buried his fingers in the coarse mane and held on, because the ground felt less reliable than usual.

Above the camp, the stars wheeled over Thornfell, indifferent and ancient, and for the first time in his life Wren wondered if they were watching.

* *

They came down into the valley town of Marrow's Ford two days later, and Wren knew something was wrong before anyone told him, because the dogs were wrong.

Marrow's Ford kept six dogs between its three taverns, and all six were famous idlers who could be relied upon to sleep through anything short of an avalanche. Today they stood rigid at the ends of their ropes, hackles up, staring at the king's men dismounting in the square.

There were four of them, in ash-gray cloaks, and they wore no livery, which meant witch-hunters, because witch-hunters wore no livery the way crows wore no livery. One of them — a woman with a face like a closed ledger — was asking questions of the miller, and the miller's hands were shaking so badly his hat trembled on his head.

"...answered prayers," she was saying, in a voice pitched to carry. "An old woman. Gray shawl. Prayed at a wayside shrine in the high pass and received an answer. We are told she spoke of it freely." She smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "We should like to thank her personally."

Wren's blood went cold and then colder. Old woman. Gray shawl. The shrine.

*They will burn her,* Vaelith said, and her voice had lost all its dry humor. *As they burned the one who bore me before you. As they would burn you, little vessel, if they knew what slept behind your ribs.*

"Don't say things like that," Wren muttered, hauling a crate of tin off the cart with hands that wanted to shake. "Don't say *ribs.*"

Brannick clapped him on the shoulder. "Unload fast, lad, and keep your head down. King's men asking after miracles — bad business. Bad for trade, bad for everyone." He lowered his voice. "My grandmother used to say the old gods were only sleeping. I used to think she was soft in the head." He looked at the gray-cloaked strangers a moment too long, then away. "Get the cart to the stable. Back way."

It was the back way that saved him, or doomed him; he never decided which.

Because the back way passed the well, and at the well stood Sera Quill, arms full of laundry, arguing cheerfully with a goose.

"—and I'll thank you to keep your opinions about my washing to yourself, you feathered tyrant—"

*Sera.* The name arrived in Wren's mind with a warmth that had nothing to do with Vaelith. Sera Quill, who had shared stolen plums with him on this exact wall when they were nine. Who had cried at his mother's burning and been forbidden by her own father ever to speak to him again, and had spoken to him anyway, every market day, for fifteen years, about nothing — weather, geese, the price of wool — nothing, always nothing, because nothing was safe.

She looked up. Her whole face lit.

"Wren! Nobody told me the caravans were—" She stopped. Because Wren had gone white as flour, and because behind him, in the square, the ledger-faced woman had raised her voice:

"—and any person harboring knowledge of the answered prayer is guilty of the same heresy. Guilt runs downhill, friends. It always finds the lowest place."

Sera looked at Wren. Wren looked at Sera. And Sera Quill, who was not a fool and never had been, read his face the way she'd read it since childhood.

"You're leaving," she said. "Now. Without saying goodbye. Again."

"Sera—"

"It's the old woman, isn't it? Marta of the high pasture. They came asking yesterday and today they're *thanking* people." Her knuckles went white around the laundry. "Wren. What do you know?"

What he knew was that the voice in his skull had begun to murmur — *the low road, past the tannery, the hunters split their watch at dusk* — and that every second he stood here, Sera's name was being written into the same ledger that had his mother's name in it.

"I know you should go home," he said. "I know I'm sorry. I know—" His throat closed. Fifteen years of nothing, and he had maybe ten seconds left, and the truth came out sideways, cracked, half a confession: "I know the prayer was answered, Sera. And I know why. And I can't tell you, because telling you is how people like me get—"

"Like you," Sera repeated softly.

The goose honked. Somewhere in Wren's chest, the thing he had guarded since he was seven years old — the gift, the curse, the whispering kinship with everything furred and feathered that had put smoke where his mother should have been — strained toward the light.

And Sera, holding her laundry, looking at him across fifteen years of careful nothings, said: "Your mother didn't burn for nothing, did she. Oh, Wren." Not horror. Not fear. Grief, and fury, and something fierce underneath both. "Go. Go *now*. Take the tannery path — the hunters won't post it till dark, Doss is lazy. And Wren?" She grabbed his sleeve, pulled him close, and pressed something into his hand: a plum, slightly bruised, from her market basket. "Come back. Whatever you are. Come *back*."

He ran. Behind him he heard her turn to the goose and say, brightly, far too loudly, "Well! Nothing interesting happening here," and he nearly laughed, and nearly wept, and did both a little, running.

* *

The tannery path stank of lime and old hides, which was exactly why it was empty. Wren moved fast through the dusk, and Vaelith moved with him, a cold bright weight behind his eyes.

*She saw,* the god said. *She knows, and she chose you. Do you understand how rare that is, little vessel? In three hundred years, I have watched a hundred bearers of my memory hide, and be betrayed, and be burned. And this one handed you a plum.*

"She doesn't know what I am," Wren panted, climbing. "Nobody knows what I am. I don't know what I am."

*Then let me show you,* Vaelith said, and the world opened.

It was not pain, exactly. It was *more* — suddenly Wren could feel the mountain the way he felt Turnip's muzzle, vast and slow and dreaming, its roots tangled with older roots, and threaded through it all, faint as embers under ash, other presences. Sleeping. Waiting. Countless.

*We were many,* Vaelith whispered, wonder and sorrow braided together in her voice. *We are few now. But we are not none. Carry me to the heart-shrine of Thornfell, Wren Brightwater, and I will show you what you are. Or refuse me, and bury us all, and live small and safe and silent until you die of it.*

"That's not a choice," Wren gasped. "That's a knife with two handles."

*Yes,* said the dying god, gently. *Most true choices are.*

Behind him, far below, a horn sounded in Marrow's Ford — once, twice, the hunting-call rhythm the smugglers used to warn each other. They had found the tannery path empty and started guessing.

Ahead, the mountain rose black against the last light, and somewhere on its shoulders, a ruin waited with a name carved in it — a name that had been his mother's warning, and was now his destination.

Wren ate the plum as he climbed, down to the pit, and kept the pit in his pocket.

For luck. For Sera. For whatever came next.
