## 3. The Price of Carrying

Wren woke on the third morning above the snowline and could not remember his mother's face.

He knew he had one — a mother, a face, a fire he was not supposed to think about — but when he reached for her the way you reach for a name in a dark room, his hand closed on nothing. There was a shape where she should have been, warm-edged, and inside the shape a voice that was not hers.

*You are dreaming,* said Vaelith, gentle as snowfall. *Or I am. The distinction grows less useful.*

"Give it back," Wren whispered. His breath smoked in the hollow of the rocks where they'd sheltered. Sera slept ten feet away with her back to a boulder and her knife unsheathed in her fist, because she was Sera. "Whatever you took. Give it back."

*I take nothing.* A pause, the kind a liar leaves. *I borrow. Memory is food, little vessel. You are carrying a starving god through a country that burned our tables, and you are surprised to grow thinner?*

Wren sat up and pressed his palms against his eyes until colors bloomed. He inventoried himself the way he'd once inventoried a smuggler's cargo: mother — gone. The smell of the tannery at home — gone, though he knew there had been a tannery. The words to the lullaby about the fox and the miller's wife — gone, though the tune still lived in him like a house with its furniture removed.

What remained was startlingly intact: every pass, every switchback, every frozen waterfall between here and the Thornfell heart-shrine. Every trick for reading weather in a mule's ears. Every lie he'd ever told, polished and ready.

*You keep what keeps us alive,* Vaelith said, and for once she sounded tired rather than vast. *I am not cruel, Wren Brightwater. I am hungry. These are different sins.*

"Not to the person being eaten."

Outside, the wind carried something worse than cold. It carried dogs.

* * *

Sera counted the torches coming up the valley and did not like the arithmetic.

Nine hunters. Two dogs. And between them and the high col, three miles of open scree with nowhere to hide a man whose footprints glowed faintly gold when the god in him stirred — which, since dawn, was often. She'd woken twice in the night to see Wren sitting upright, eyes open and shining like coins at the bottom of a well, murmuring in a language that made her teeth ache and her childhood memories feel suddenly precious, the way things do when someone nearby is losing theirs.

She had known Wren Brightwater since they were six and he'd shown her a wounded kestrel and she'd sworn — on her grandmother's grave, which was serious business at six — never to tell. She had kept that oath through the burning years, through the witch-hunters' first visits, through every festival where the priests burned effigies of "beast-speakers and god-botherers" and Wren stood in the crowd with his face arranged into pleasant blankness. She was good at keeping oaths.

The problem was that the hunters had stopped asking only about Wren.

"They're offering amnesty," said Tam, the youngest of the village men who'd come up the mountain with her — ostensibly to help, actually to watch her, she'd realized somewhere around the second switchback. He wouldn't meet her eyes. "For anyone who assists them. Full amnesty, plus winter grain. The whole valley gets grain, Sera. Our stores flooded in the autumn melt. You know what February looks like if we don't—"

"I know what February looks like."

"And he's dying anyway." Tam's voice dropped, urgent, almost kind. "Marta saw him at the shrine before he ran. She says the god is eating him. Whatever he's carrying, it doesn't leave him alive. We'd be trading one life for forty families' winter. That's not betrayal. That's arithmetic."

Sera looked down the slope at the torches, then up at the rocks where Wren was crouched, feeding the last of their barley to a wild goat that had appeared out of nowhere and was regarding him with an expression of frank adoration. Even now, starving and hunted and half-erased, beasts loved him. It was the most honest thing about him.

"That's arithmetic," she agreed. "And I've always been terrible at sums."

Tam's hand went to his belt. Sera's went faster.

* * *

Wren heard the scuffle and came around the boulder in time to see Tam sprawled in the snow, disarmed and furious, and Sera standing over him with a look Wren recognized from childhood — the look she wore right before doing something magnificent and unforgivable.

"He offered me grain," she said.

"How much grain?"

"Enough for the whole valley."

Wren whistled low. "Tempting?"

"Sera," Tam snarled from the ground, "they'll burn your house. They'll burn your *name*. They'll plow your grandmother's grave for salt—"

"Then they'll find out what my grandmother put in that soil," Sera said pleasantly, "and I promise they'll regret it." She turned to Wren, and beneath the bravado her eyes were wet and furious and certain. "Well? Can you run?"

"I can run."

"Then we're past arithmetic." She hauled Tam's knife from the snow and threw it, deliberately, over the cliff edge. "Go."

They went.

* * *

The col nearly killed them anyway.

It was Vaelith who saved them, or damned them further — Wren honestly could no longer sort the categories. As the dogs closed the gap below and the wind turned traitor, carrying their scent upslope, the god surged forward through him and spoke to the mountain itself. Not to the goats, not to the ravens wheeling overhead, but to the old stone under the snow, the bones of Thornfell that remembered being worshipped.

*Move,* she said, in the voice that had once made glaciers kneel.

The cornice above the hunters groaned. Slid. Did not fall — Vaelith was precise, even starving — but came down in a white avalanche roar across the whole lower path, sealing it, burying the torchlight, swallowing the dogs' cries into deep and merciful powder.

Wren stood swaying at the top of the col, blood running from his nose, watching nine hunters' route become a wall of snow. Inside his skull, Vaelith blazed like a bonfire fed at last, and he felt her reach greedily toward him in her fullness — felt her fingers close around the memory of his father's laugh —

—and stop.

*No,* she said, to herself, in a voice like a door being barred from within. *Not that one. That one is his.*

"You can't hold out forever," Wren gasped. Snow, blood, laughter — hysterical, exhausted. "You just ate a mountain. I felt it. You're getting stronger, and I'm getting *less*, and at the end of this road there's a shrine where either I give you back or you finish the meal. Those are my options, aren't they? Sebastian found that out too."

Vaelith was silent so long he thought she might pretend not to hear.

*Sebastian hid,* she said finally. *He carried me into the dark and told no one, and I consumed him cell by cell, year by year, until there was no vessel left, only a hole shaped like a man, and then no hole either. I do not ask you to forgive me for it. I ask you to learn from it. There is a third road, Wren. It runs through the heart-shrine. It will cost more than your memories.*

"What could possibly cost more than that?"

*Everything else,* said the god. *Come. Dawn is close, and so are they.*

Below them, muffled by a hundred feet of fresh snowfall, something began to dig.

* * *

Sera caught up to them at the ridge where the heart-shrine's broken spire first showed against the stars — a black tooth of ancient masonry, older than the kingdom, older than the burning. Wren was walking strangely, she thought; too straight, too steady, like a puppet learning its strings. When he glanced back at her, his eyes were gold to the rim.

"Wren?" she said carefully.

"He's resting," said Wren's mouth, in a voice layered like two singers a breath apart. "I am driving. Do not be afraid, Sera of the valley. You kept your oath twenty years. I keep better ones than he does."

Sera's hand found her knife and did not draw it. "If you hurt him—"

"I am the least of what could hurt him." The gold flickered, and Wren surfaced behind it, gray-faced and shaking, gripping her arm like a drowning man. "Sera. Sera, listen. If I start forgetting *you* — if I look at you tomorrow and don't know your name — you have to tell me. Everything. The kestrel. The oath. All of it. Promise me."

"You'll remember," she said, and hated how thin it came out.

"Promise me."

Above them, against the stars, the spire of the heart-shrine seemed to lean closer, listening. And far below, patient as winter, the digging went on.
