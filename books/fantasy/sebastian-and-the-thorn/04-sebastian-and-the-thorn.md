## 4. Sebastian and the Thorn

The heart-shrine of Thornfell did not look like the seat of gods.

It looked like a place that had been very tired for a very long time. The walls had slumped into green shoulders of moss. The great doorway stood open to the sky because there was no longer any roof to argue with it, and ferns grew in neat rows along what had once been a processional floor, as if they'd been planted by someone with strong opinions about order. In the center of it all lay a broken stone basin, split down the middle like a dropped egg.

Wren stood in the doorway with Sera's hand on his arm and Vaelith's voice in his skull, and felt the strangest thing: he felt *welcome*.

"That's the basin," Vaelith said. Her voice had grown quieter over the last two days, which should have frightened him, but instead it sounded like an old woman settling into a chair by a fire she trusted would keep burning. "That is where I first woke. That is where I first learned to be afraid."

"Gods get afraid?"

"Gods get *erased*, Wren. It's much worse."

He crossed the fern-lined floor slowly. His boots left dark prints in the moss. Behind him, somewhere down the mountain, he could hear the witch-hunters' dogs — a thin, distant music, but getting nearer by the hour. Sera had counted eleven torches at last light. Eleven hunters, and half the village of Hollowmere behind them, dragged along by fear and the promise of amnesty.

"You said there was another," Wren said. He crouched beside the broken basin and set his palm on the cold stone. "Before me. You said his name once and then went quiet about it."

Vaelith was quiet again now. Long enough that he thought she might not answer.

Then the stone beneath his hand *warmed*, and the shrine filled with a voice that was not only in his head — it came up out of the floor, out of the walls, out of the ferns themselves, low and vast and grieving.

**SEBASTIAN.**

Sera made a small sound and gripped his shoulder. "Wren. The whole mountain just said a name."

"I know," Wren whispered. "I felt it in my teeth."

"He was the first," Vaelith said, and her voice returned to its usual size, tired and warm inside him. "The first vessel. A shepherd, actually. He found this shrine the way you found my roadside altar — by accident, with mud on his boots. I was dying then too, after the Burning Years, when your king's grandfathers put out our fires one by one. So I hid. Inside him. He kept me secret the way you kept your gift secret — beautifully, perfectly, for forty years."

"What happened to him?"

The silence that followed had a shape to it. Wren recognized it. It was the same silence his mother had worn, all those years ago, right before she told him how his father died.

"He hid too well," Vaelith said at last. "A god cannot live in a locked room forever, Wren. Memory needs to be *spoken* or it curdles. Sebastian never spoke. Not to his wife, not to his children, not to the sky. And so, little by little, I ate him. His favorite songs first. Then the smell of his mother's kitchen. Then his children's names." She paused. "At the end, he stood where you are standing, holding a basin full of rainwater, and he could not remember why he had come here. He could not remember his own face. There was nothing left of him but a hollow place shaped exactly like me."

Wren's stomach turned over. He thought of the last three days — the way his memory of his mother's laugh had gone soft at the edges, the way he'd caught himself reaching for the name of his childhood dog and finding only a hole. The erosion wasn't an accident. It was the *price*.

"And me?" he asked. "You brought me here to do the same thing?"

"No." The word came gentle and absolute. "I brought you here to offer you a choice Sebastian never got, because no one ever told him it existed. You can let me finish what I started — go quietly into you, and live, and watch you become a pleasant fog with my memories inside it. Or—"

"Or I speak," Wren said. "Out loud. To everyone."

"Or you speak," Vaelith agreed. "And it will hurt. And some of them will try to kill you for it. But a god spoken aloud belongs to no single skull. I would wake in the ground and the wind and the mules and the stubborn weeds of this shrine, and I would be a neighbor again, not a tenant. And you—" her voice caught, or did whatever a dying god does instead of catching, "—you would still be *you*. All of you. Even the parts you spent thirty years hiding."

* * *

They came at dusk.

Wren heard them before he saw them: dogs first, then boots, then the jingle of witch-irons, that particular chain-song that had haunted his nightmares since childhood. He walked out through the broken doorway and stood on the shrine's front step, and Sera stood at his shoulder, and the whole procession stopped in the trampled ferns below like water meeting a wall.

There were more than eleven. Word had traveled. Hunters in their grey coats, and behind them villagers — Hollowmere faces, faces from the valley, the old woman from the wayside shrine whose prayer had started everything, still clutching her bundle of offerings. Forty people, maybe fifty, torchlight shivering across all of them.

At their head stood the hunter-captain, a broad woman with a burned scar along her jaw and eyes like coins. Wren knew her type. He'd spent his whole life learning to recognize it from the inside of hiding places.

"Wren Brightwater," she called. "You're accused of the old crime. Beast-speaking. God-carrying. Step down and surrender, and your friend walks free."

"He always does that," Sera muttered. "Offers the friend. Like we're currency."

"We are currency," Wren said. "But not tonight." He raised his voice. "Captain! You want the god? She's in here." He tapped his own chest. "And I'll give her to you — but not the way you want. Not in irons. Not in a cage of my ribs, either, which is what you'd really prefer, because a hidden god can't argue with a king."

The captain's hand went to her sword. Half the hunters followed. The villagers didn't; the villagers leaned *forward*.

"There's another way," Wren said. "An older way. The way it worked before anyone decided the gods were contraband." He took a breath, and found, absurdly, that he wanted to laugh — thirty years of silence, and it ended with him giving a speech. "My mother taught me one rule above all others. She used to say, *what lives in you unspoken will eat you alive.* I thought she meant secrets. She meant everything."

He closed his eyes. He opened his mouth. And he *spoke*.

Not beast-speech this time, not the low murmur that calmed mules and coaxed secrets out of frightened animals. He spoke in the old cadence, the prayer-cadence, the one that lived in his bones beneath the gift like bedrock under soil — and he gave Vaelith away, syllable by syllable, memory by memory. The Burning Years. The shrines put to the torch. The names of every god who had gone quiet, spoken into the evening air where anyone could hear and no one could own them. He felt her pour out of him like water from the cracked basin, like breath from an open window, and with every word that left him, something came *back*: his mother's laugh, whole and sudden. The dog's name — Bramble, it was Bramble — arriving home.

When he finished, the mountain exhaled.

It wasn't dramatic, not at first. The ferns in the shrine rustled though there was no wind. The dogs sat down, all of them at once, and began making contented hound noises, to the visible fury of their handlers. The old spring beside the path, dry for a generation, made a sound like a throat clearing and began, tentatively, to trickle.

And in the air — everywhere, nowhere, in the ground and the stones and the mossy shoulders of the ruined walls — a voice, vast and unhurried and unmistakably *amused*, said:

"Well. Hello, neighbors. Sorry about the wait. My colleague under the eastern ridge snores, so if the avalanches start again, that's him, take it up with him directly."

Someone laughed. Then a dozen someones. The hunters looked at their captain; the captain looked at the sky, which had begun, gently, to snow — warm snow, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and honey — and did not draw her sword.

Sera squeezed Wren's arm hard enough to bruise. "You're crying," she said.

"I'm *unhidden*," Wren said. "It feels similar."

He stood on the step of the broken shrine, in the falling warm snow, in front of everyone, and for the first time in his life he belonged to exactly one person: himself. Around him the old gods were waking like neighbors leaning over garden fences, asking after the crops, complaining about the weather, and the world did not end.

It began.

Far off to the east, under the sleeping ridge, something enormous turned over in its sleep — and the mountain rumbled, once, like a snore.

"Oh no," said Vaelith, from everywhere. "I *told* him about the snoring."
