## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats always knew before I did.

I learned that in my first spring at Hollowmere, when the whole herd turned as one to face the western crags and stood there, ears pricked, staring at nothing anyone else could see. Old Marta had laughed and said they were watching for wolves. But there were no wolves in the valley. There hadn't been wolves, or wraiths, or wardens, or anything with a whisper of the old world in it, for thirty years. The valley did not permit such things.

That morning the goats were facing west again, all eleven of them, and the fog on the crags was the color of milk gone thin.

"Hesba," I called, and the lead doe turned her long face toward me with an expression I could only describe as apology. "Come on, then. Nothing's up there but rock."

She came. They all came, hooves clicking down the slate scree like rain on a roof, and I led them along the drover's path toward the lower pasture, and I did not look back at the crags, because looking was the first habit I had sworn off, and habits are how empires find you.

Hollowmere sat at the bottom of its valley like a coin dropped into a cupped hand. Forty-one roofs, a mill, a shrine with no god in it—just a smooth stone where people left bread for luck—and a river so clear you could count the trout from the bridge. It was, by every measure I allowed myself, a good place. The bread was dense and honest. The gossip was mild. Nobody asked where you'd come from, because asking where you'd come from was considered slightly rude, the way asking a woman her age is rude in some countries, or asking a soldier what he did in the war. In Hollowmere, forgetting wasn't failure. It was courtesy. It was civic duty, written nowhere and enforced everywhere: you did not ask about the time before, you did not hum songs with unfamiliar scales, you did not keep letters older than five years. The valley kept its own counsel, and it expected you to keep yours.

I had been there nine years. Long enough that my hands had softened around the sword-calluses and hardened around the milking pail. Long enough that children who'd been toddlers when I arrived now waved to me by name—Elodie, just Elodie, goat-girl Elodie of the upper pasture, no family name, because family names invited questions about families.

Long enough that I almost believed it myself, some mornings.

Almost.

* * *

There is a scar on my left forearm, from wrist to elbow, that looks like a line of script written in a language no one living reads. That is because it is exactly that—a swordspeaker's oath-scar, inked into the flesh not by any needle but by the words themselves, burned into the skin the moment they were spoken aloud and meant. Every member of my order wore one. Mine is the only one left in the world, as far as I know, and I have spent thirty years keeping it that way.

In Hollowmere I wore sleeves. Winter and summer, wool to the wrist, and if the summer was hot I worked at dawn and dusk and let people think I was delicate. Nine years is a long time to be delicate. Once, three summers ago, Marta's grandson fell into the millrace and I went in after him without thinking, sleeves and all, and pulled him out, and the whole village saw the scar while I carried him dripping up the bank. I told them it was a burn from a lamp when I was a child. They nodded. And then—the thing that saved me, the thing I still don't fully understand—they stopped seeing it. Not forgot it exactly. Let it go, the way water lets go of a shape. By the next market day, Marta was pressing barley loaves into my hands and saying what a blessing it was I'd been there, and nobody mentioned my arm at all, ever again, as though the valley itself had reached out and smoothed the memory flat.

That was when I understood that Hollowmere's forgetting was not merely a custom. It was a working. Something under this valley—warding it, wrapping it—did not want the past found here, and it bent the minds of everyone inside to help. Magic could not follow me into the valley. Neither, apparently, could memory, not truly, not sharply. The villagers thought themselves simply forgetful, contented, incurious folk. Perhaps they even were, once. Perhaps the warding had worn them smooth as river stones over thirty years, until curiosity itself eroded away.

And I lived among them, sleeveless in spirit, grateful every single day for their beautiful, manufactured dullness.

Because they were right not to ask. Because the empire that exterminated my order—the Swordspeakers of Veylmark, oath-bound, voice-bound, the last defenders of a country that officially no longer exists—had lists. Had inquisitors. Had a procedure for scars like mine, and the procedure ended in ash. A country can be erased from maps in an afternoon; erasing its people takes longer, and the empire is patient. Thirty years patient. So I tended goats. I spoke softly. I let them think me plain and shy and slightly lame in the winter, and I buried my voice's other use so deep that some mornings I woke genuinely unsure whether I had ever spoken to steel at all.

Some mornings. Not this one.

* * *

This one began with the tremor.

It came just past noon, while I was mending the fence by the birch stand, and it was not an earthquake—not properly. Earthquakes come from beneath, rolling and indifferent. This came from everywhere and nowhere, a shiver through the ground like the shiver through a rope when someone at the far end has plucked it. The birches rattled though there was no wind. The river went momentarily, horribly silent, every trout and current stilled, and in that silence I heard—or felt, or remembered—a sound I had not heard in thirty years:

A word. Spoken low, in the old cadence, the cadence of oath-taking. One word, half-heard, like a voice through a wall. *Veyl.*

Then the river rushed on, the birds resumed, and the valley was a valley again, green and dull and safe.

I stood with a fence-post in my hands and my heart slamming against my ribs like something caged, and I waited for the terror to arrive, the proper terror, the run-and-hide terror. It didn't come. What came instead was worse.

My scar warmed.

Not painfully. Warm as a hand laid gently on the forearm, warm as sunlight through wool, and along the length of it I felt a faint, insistent stirring, like a sleeper turning over. My oath. Dead words, dead magic, thirty years dormant—I had spoken them last at nineteen, kneeling in the sanctuary at Carrow Fell with forty voices around me, and I had never spoken them since, had starved them deliberately, had fled into a valley where such things could not live. I had assumed them dead. You do not assume things like that. You hope, desperately, and you mistake the hope for certainty.

The scar pulsed once more, and settled, and the warmth faded, and I was alone in the birch stand holding a fence-post like a spear.

"Goats," I said aloud, because saying something ordinary felt necessary. "Feed the goats."

But my hands shook through the whole evening milking, and Hesba watched me with those liquid amber eyes, and twice she looked west at the crags, and twice she looked back at me, and I understood that we were having the same conversation.

* * *

At the shrine that night I lit no candle, because candles draw eyes, but I touched the smooth stone the way you touch an old wound to check whether it still hurts. The stone was cold. It was always cold. The shrine had no god because Hollowmere had been founded—for what purpose I'd only ever guessed—by people who wanted no gods, or wanted no gods *finding* them, which amounts to the same architecture.

"You're failing," I whispered to the warding, to whatever vast and patient working held this valley shut like a fist. I don't know why I addressed it as a living thing. Thirty years of silence makes you talk to anything. "You've kept me safe. Keep holding."

The valley said nothing. Valleys are good at that.

Walking home, I passed the mill where lamplight spilled gold across the cobbles, and inside someone was singing—one of Marta's daughters, a harvest song with a scale that bent wrong, a scale from the old country, though the girl singing it had never heard of any old country and would have sworn the tune was her grandmother's invention. The valley let small survivals through. It couldn't catch everything. Songs lived in the body deeper than memory lived in the mind.

I stopped in the dark outside the mill and listened until the song ended, and I found that my eyes were wet, and I did not examine why. Examining was dangerous. Wanting was dangerous. For nine years I had made a religion of not-wanting: no past, no future, no questions, a life measured out in seasons and kids and barley loaves. Contentment enforced from without, and therefore—this was the lie I told myself—contentment nonetheless.

The tremor had cracked something in that lie. I could feel the edges of it now, the way you feel a hairline fracture in a cup: invisible, but suddenly you cannot stop feeling it. If the warding was failing—if the valley's great forgetting was thinning—then everything I had built my survival on was built on borrowed time, and somewhere beyond the crags the empire's patience was ending, and somewhere in my own arm a dead oath was warming like a banked fire, and I did not know which frightened me more.

I slept badly. In the grey hour before dawn I dreamed of Carrow Fell, of forty voices and the smell of cold iron and oil, and in the dream I was speaking the old words with my mouth open and no sound coming out, and the scar on my arm burned white, and someone was calling my true name across a distance that widened with every repetition.

I woke with dawn coming thin through the shutters, and my sleeve pushed up past the elbow, and I had no memory of pushing it.

The scar lay pale on my forearm in the early light. And beneath the skin, faint as a pulse in the wrist, it was moving—very slowly, very slightly, the way a map redraws itself, the way script rewrites.

I pulled the sleeve down. I fed the goats. I told myself it was nothing, the way you tell yourself things at the edge of a season that is about to change.

By midmorning, on the drover's path above the ford, the goats stopped again—all eleven of them at once—and faced downstream, toward the willows, and would not move.

And down at the ford, where the water ran shallow over the stones, there was a man on his knees in the shallows, and the water around him was blooming red.
