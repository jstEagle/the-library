## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The river at Hollowmere ran shallow and silver that morning, the kind of water that seemed to apologize for itself, sliding over its stones without a sound. Elodie Pemberton had crossed it a thousand times and never once thought of it as a border. It was just the ford—the place where the sheep track dipped between alders, where her skirts got wet to the knee every spring, where the village children dared each other to stand in the current until their toes went blue.

She was thinking about none of this when she saw him.

He was lying half in the water, half out, on the far gravel bar where the herons liked to fish. At first she took him for a drowned branch, a tangle of dark wood the flood had left behind. Then the branch moved—shuddered, actually, a long involuntary ripple from shoulder to hip—and she understood it was a man, and that he was dying.

Elodie did not run to him. That was the first thing worth noting, though she would not note it until much later, turning the morning over in her hands like a stone with two faces. She stood very still among the alders and let her eyes do what they had been trained to do thirty years ago by people who were now ash: read the ground, count the shadows, ask the landscape what had happened here.

The gravel told her plenty. He had come down the eastern bank at a stumbling run—there were the skids where his heels had failed him twice. He had fallen once already, gotten up, fallen again. And behind him, upstream, the water was wrong. Not muddy. Not disturbed. *Wrong*—a patch of current perhaps ten paces wide where the surface lay flat as poured glass while everything around it ruffled in the wind. The river flowed through that place and came out the other side unchanged, as if it had passed through a room no one was allowed to remember.

Her scar woke.

It sat along the inside of her left forearm, an old oath-mark, thin as a hair, the color of tarnished silver. For twenty years it had been nothing—a seam in the skin, a story she could not tell, a thing she hid under wool sleeves even in high summer because the women of Hollowmere noticed such things and asked questions, and questions were dangerous in a valley built on forgetting. Now it burned like a wire drawn fresh from a forge fire, and beneath the burn she felt something stir that she had spent two decades convincing herself was dead: her oath, coiled and patient, lifting its head.

*No*, she told it, the way you'd tell a dog. *Not here. Not now.*

But the flat water upstream said otherwise. Something had followed him to the edge of the valley, and whatever it was, the warding that kept magic out of Veylmark's last green hollow had let it come close enough to touch the river.

Elodie went down the bank with her staff first, testing stones, and knelt beside him in the shallows.

He was younger than she'd expected—thirty, maybe thirty-five, with the grey already claiming his temples and a cartographer's ink stains ghosting the nails of his right hand. His coat had been fine once. Now it was ruined past any cobbler's hope, soaked black from collar to hem, and the blood coming out of him was doing something blood should not do: it moved. It crawled back toward the wound in slow threads, as if being gathered, as if something on the other side of the world were reeling it in.

"Help me," he said, and then, absurdly, "I'm sorry," as if those were the same request.

"You're bleeding out," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "I can't stop that."

"I know." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; his whole body clenched around it. "I know. I've known since the pass. I just—" His hand shot out and closed on her wrist with a strength that had no business remaining in him, and his eyes found hers, and they were the eyes of a man reading a map he'd drawn himself. "You're her. You're one of them. The scar—I saw the scar."

Elodie pulled her sleeve down over her wrist. Useless. He'd already seen.

"There are no swordspeakers," she said. "There haven't been for thirty years. You're confused. You're dying."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly, as if she'd confirmed the weather. "Both true. Listen—" He tried to sit up, failed, tried again, and she found herself bracing his shoulders against her knee before she had decided to. The water ran cold around their legs. Upstream, the glass-flat patch of river had drifted closer. "My name is Sebastian Vael. Remember it. Write it somewhere. Because when I'm gone, this valley is going to eat my name, and you'll be the only one holding it, and that's a terrible weight, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry—"

"Stop talking," she said. "Save your breath."

"For what?" He smiled. It was a good smile, crooked and tired, the smile of a man who had made his peace somewhere on a mountain road and was only now catching up to it. "I have things to give you and very little time to give them. The thing behind me—it doesn't cross running water easily, but the ford slows the current, and it's patient, and I am *not going to be patient*, so."

His hand went into his coat and came out with oilcloth, wrapped tight and tied with waxed cord. He pressed it into her hands and folded her fingers over it, one by one, with the deliberate care of a man handling something holy or something cursed—he seemed unsure which.

"The Last Door," he whispered. "Out of Veylmark. There's a way, and it opens soon, and only for a season, and the key isn't a key. It's an oath. Your oath. Broken and remade. Everything you need is in there. Don't open it here. Don't open it where anyone can see. Burn it if you have to—it won't mind, it likes fire, it'll come back—but *read it first*. Promise me you'll read it."

"I don't promise strangers who bleed on me," Elodie said, but her hands had already closed around the packet, and her scar was singing now, a thin bright line of heat from wrist to elbow, and some traitorous part of her had already understood that nothing in her life would be small again after this morning.

"Fair," Sebastian said. "That's fair. Then take the other thing instead."

"What other—"

He caught her right hand—the sword hand, though she hadn't carried a sword in twenty years, though the closest thing to a blade in her cottage was a skinning knife—and turned it palm up. His own palm he pressed to hers, and his eyes rolled back, and he began to speak.

It was not a language she knew, and yet she understood every word, the way you understand a song heard once in childhood. The words were old. They were older than the empire, older than the fall, older than the burning of the sanctuaries. They were binding-words, oath-words, the grammar of a craft that had died with her sisters, and they went into her like cold water into cracked stone—filling her, finding all the empty places she'd carved out of herself to survive Hollowmere, settling there.

"*Voice to blade,*" he said, in plain imperial speech now, gasping between phrases. "*Blade to bearer. Bearer to door.* I bind what's left of me—not much, I warn you, mostly stubbornness—to the steel you swore on. Wherever it is. However far. You'll hear me. Gods forgive me, you'll hear me whether you want to or not."

"I never swore on anything," Elodie said. "I threw it in the river. This river. Twenty years ago."

Sebastian opened his eyes and looked at her with something like delight.

"Then it's still here, isn't it?" he said. "Rivers keep what we throw away. That's rather the point of them."

Upstream, the flat water stopped moving.

It simply arrived—no splash, no sound, the current going silent across ten paces of river as if a hand had been laid over it. In the middle of that silence stood a shape. Elodie's mind refused to hold it; her eyes slid off it the way they slid off the sun. She caught impressions only: a tallness. A patience. An absence wearing the suggestion of robes, of a hood, of a face that had been carefully, lovingly erased.

The valley's warding shuddered. She felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of old wounds—in the scar, which screamed now, a single clear note like a struck bell.

"It's here," Sebastian said. He didn't sound afraid. He sounded *vindicated*, which was somehow worse. "Good. Good—that means the borders are failing, that means the Door will open, that means—" He coughed, and the cough brought blood, and the blood crawled backward toward the wound and vanished. "Listen to me. Elodie. What's your name? No—don't tell me. Names are how they follow. Tell me later. When it's safe. When we're—"

He never finished the sentence. His body arched, and the gathering-blood reversed itself all at once, pouring out of him in a rush that stained the ford red from bank to bank, and whatever held him upright let go.

Elodie caught him. Of course she caught him; her arms knew the work before her mind consented to it. She lowered him onto the gravel and pressed her hands to the wound and felt, beneath her palms, the exact moment the crawling stopped—when the blood finally behaved like blood, warm and mortal and going nowhere, pooling in the stones.

"No," she heard herself say. "No, you don't get to—you don't get to put your voice in my hand and then *die*, that's not how oaths work, you absolute—"

But he was smiling again, faintly, looking up past her at the alders and the white sky between them.

"Read the map," he whispered. "Find the door. Carry us out. All of us. Everything we—" His breath hitched. Held. "Everything we forgot."

And then the valley closed over him like water.

* * *

She walked back into Hollowmere at midday with wet boots and a stranger's blood dried brown on her sleeves, and no one looked at her twice.

That was the first wrongness. The second came at Maren Tull's gate, where Maren was arguing with the smith about a broken hinge, and Elodie said, "There's a dead man at the ford," and both of them turned to look at her with mild, untroubled faces.

"At the ford?" Maren said. "What, a deer?"

"A man. Dying. I sat with him. He gave me—"

"Elodie love," the smith said gently, in the tone reserved for children and the grief-mad, "there's nothing at the ford. There never is. That's rather the point of it."

And they believed it. She watched them believe it, watched the certainty settle over their faces like frost, and understood with a cold, descending clarity that the valley had done exactly what Sebastian said it would. Hollowmere's magic did not merely keep enchantment out. It kept the world *smooth*. Anything too sharp—a wound, a wonder, a dying man with a name—got sanded away overnight, and everyone slept soundly, and no one asked why the river ran red at dawn.

Except her.

By evening she had asked four people, casually, cruelly, and received four blank kindnesses. By nightfall she had stopped asking. She sat alone at her table with the oilcloth packet unopened in front of her and her scar throbbing in time with her heartbeat, and she said his name aloud to the empty room, quietly, the way you set a lamp in a window.

"Sebastian Vael."

The name stayed. It was hers alone now, a grave with no marker, a debt with no creditor. Outside, the wind came down off the mountains carrying the smell of salt—salt, in a landlocked valley, thirty miles from any sea—and somewhere far upstream, the river ran flat and silent, and waited.

In her sword hand, faint as a pulse under skin, something answered when she spoke.

*I'm still here,* it seemed to say. *Read the map.*

She reached for the oilcloth, and her fingers were shaking, and she untied the cord.
