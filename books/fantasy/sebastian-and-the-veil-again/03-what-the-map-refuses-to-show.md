## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

I had been dead for three days before I understood what that meant.

Not dead the way I had imagined it in the cartographer's long nights, when the candle guttered and the ink froze in its well and I thought: *this is how it ends, alone at a desk, a country folded up inside my head.* No. Dead was this — a voice with no throat, pressed into cold steel like a seal into wax, watching through eyes that were not mine and could never be mine again.

Elodie Pemberton's eyes.

She was mending a fence when I first managed to speak to her, or trying to; her hands kept stopping over the wire, and I knew she could feel me the way one feels weather coming — a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the light. The goats watched her with their flat yellow patience. The valley held its breath around her, as it always did, as it always would, if it could.

"Elodie," I said, and my voice came out of her sword where it leaned against the fence post, thin as wind through a keyhole.

She did not scream. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of such things. She went very still, the way prey goes still, and then she picked up the sword and held it at arm's length and said, "You're supposed to be dead."

"I am."

"Then stop talking."

But her hands shook, and I heard in her voice the same thing I had heard at the ford, when she knelt in the shallows with my blood diluting pink around her knees: not fear. Grief. She had carried my name out of the water like something rescued, and names are heavy, even small ones. Even *Sebastian*, which is nothing, which is a sound the empire gave me because they took the name I was born with along with everything else.

"The map," I said. "You still have the map."

Her jaw tightened. "It's paper."

"It's more than paper. You know it's more than paper. You felt it when you took it from me." I pushed harder against the boundaries of the binding, and the blade hummed, and she flinched. "Look at it again. Please. I don't have much of anything left, Elodie, but I have this, and I need you to look."

* * *

She looked. Of course she looked. She waited until full dark, until Hollowmere's windows went amber and then black, until the only light was the little oil lamp she shielded with her body as though the flame itself might inform on her. She unrolled the map on her table and weighted the corners with a spoon, a whetstone, a river stone, a lump of salt.

And I saw it through her eyes, and I grieved all over again.

I had drawn it in the last year of Veylmark, when the country was already dying — when the salt was already creeping up the river valleys and the orchards stood bare and black as burnt matches. I had drawn it for people who would never use it, refugees who wanted to believe there was a way out that wasn't surrender. A door. There are always doors, in old countries; Veylmark had been old enough to have seven of them, and six had been sealed or swallowed or sold. But the seventh —

"You see the river?" I said. "Follow it past the drowned villages. Past Tollhouse Ford. Into the mountains where the maps go blank — where *my* maps go blank, and I made maps of everything." My voice scraped against the steel. "There's a pass there that isn't on any imperial chart, because no imperial surveyor ever came back from it. The Last Door of Veylmark. It opens once a year, when the season turns — one season only, one moon's turning, and then it shuts for another year. And this year—"

"This year is thirty years too late," Elodie said. Her voice was flat as slate. "Veylmark fell. You told me. Everyone told me. There's nothing left to walk out of."

"There's nothing left to walk *into*," I corrected gently, and hated myself for the gentleness. "The Door doesn't lead deeper into Veylmark, Elodie. It leads out. Out of the empire's reach entirely. Out of every reach. To lands the inquisitors can't chart because their instruments fail at the threshold. It's the only place in the world where a swordspeaker can live without hiding her scar under her sleeve."

The lamp flame bent. Outside, the wind moved down the valley, and beneath it — faint, but growing every day now, like a heartbeat heard through a wall — the tremor of the warding failing. She heard it too. I watched her hear it, watched her shoulders draw in as if against cold.

"And the key," she said. Not a question. She already knew. She had known since the ford, maybe since the tremor, maybe since childhood, since the stories her grandmother told in whispers about the order that had been and the oaths that had been broken. "Let me guess. The key is an oath."

"A reforged oath. Spoken by a living swordspeaker." I let the words sit between us. "Your order bound itself to the Doors, Elodie. That was the whole architecture of it. The Veil, the valley, the sanctuary seals — all of it hung on the swordspeakers' vows. When the empire killed your order, the Doors didn't die. They just stopped being openable. Except by someone who carries the oath. Someone who can speak the old words and mean them."

"I don't know the old words."

"They're written on the map."

"No," she said, "they aren't," and she turned the map over, and over again, and smoothed it flat with both palms as though she could press the truth out of it or press the truth into hiding. "It's rivers and passes and a door drawn in ink. There are no words on it at all."

"That's because you haven't bled on it yet."

* * *

She burned it the next morning.

I should say here that I understood. I want that understood, whoever reads this — if anyone reads this, if a voice pressed into steel counts as reading, if a dead man's testimony weighs anything in any court. She had spent her whole life learning that survival meant smallness. Hide the scar. Mumble when asked your trade. Let the valley's forgetting wash over you like a warm tide and be grateful for it, be grateful for the amnesia that passed for peace. Hollowmere did not merely tolerate forgetting; it practiced it, devoutly, the way other towns practice religion. And I had walked into that devotion bleeding, and died in it, and left her holding the one object in the world that could unravel everything she had built her life upon.

So yes. She burned it.

She built the fire herself, in the yard behind the goat shed, away from the house, feeding it dry broom so it caught hot and fast. She stood over it with the map in both hands for a long moment — long enough that I thought she wouldn't do it, long enough that hope did its stupid, stubborn work in whatever I had instead of a chest.

"You're a dead man's trick," she said to me, or to the map, or to the morning. "You're a dying stranger's fever dream. The valley protects me. The valley has protected me my whole life. I tend goats. I mend fences. Nobody in Hollowmere knows my name means anything, and that is worth more than some door in the mountains, and I will not — I will *not*—"

She fed the map to the flames.

It caught. Paper burns beautifully; that has always seemed to me a small cruelty of the world, that the things we write our lives on give themselves to fire so willingly. The corners curled. The ink darkened and shone. The rivers I had drawn with such care — the Aldwater, the Grey Sorrow, the hundred unnamed streams — glowed briefly like veins of ore and then went to ash.

Elodie stood there breathing hard, watching it burn, and for perhaps ten heartbeats she looked free.

Then she cried out.

I felt it happen before she did — felt the binding wake in the blade, felt the map's purpose assert itself the way a river asserts itself against a dam. Ink does not like to die. Especially not this ink. I had ground it myself, in the last year, from oak gall and iron salts and one other thing I have never told anyone, one thing I will tell her eventually, when she can bear it: a measure of the salt that killed the orchards. The empire's own poison, turned to preservation. Everything I drew with it stayed drawn.

The ash lifted off the fire. All of it, every fleck, rising against the morning breeze in a slow grey spiral, and Elodie backed away from it with her hand at her throat, and the ash came for her anyway, gentle as snowfall, settling on her forearms where her sleeves were rolled for work.

And drew.

I watched the lines take shape on her skin the way frost takes shape on glass — the Aldwater first, silver-dark, running from her wrist toward her elbow; then the mountain passes branching off it like winter branches; then, just below the crook of her arm, small and precise and unmistakable, the symbol I had used for the Last Door: a rectangle, and within it a vertical line, the way a doorway looks when it stands closed but not locked.

"No," Elodie said. "No, no—" She scrubbed at it with her sleeve, with water from the rain barrel, with pumice from the whetstone, hard enough to redden the skin, and the lines did not fade. They had not been laid *on* her. They had been laid *in*. "What did you do to me? What did you *do*?"

"What the map refuses to show on paper," I said quietly, "it shows on its bearer. I'm sorry. I designed it that way so it couldn't be confiscated. So it couldn't be lost. So that whoever carried it out of Veylmark carried it all the way." I paused, and the truth came out of me like a splinter. "And so that the empire couldn't simply take it from you. They'd have to take you."

She stood in the yard with her arms bare and my country written on them, and the morning was very bright and very cold, and somewhere far off — closer than yesterday, closer than the day before — the warding groaned like an old ship in a storm.

"The tremors," she said. Her voice had gone small. "They're getting worse. The valley's forgetting is getting thinner. I've felt it for weeks. The miller's wife said a word yesterday that nobody's said aloud in thirty years, and she didn't know where it came from, and everyone laughed, and I didn't laugh, because I knew exactly where it came from." She looked down at her arms. "If the warding fails—"

"The inquisitors come back," I said. "For the valley. For the village. And for the last swordspeaker, whose scar they will read like a warrant." I let the silence stretch, because there was no kind way to shorten it. "Staying invisible was always a loan, Elodie. Not a gift. And the lender has come to collect."

She sat down slowly on the chopping block, in the ash-scattered yard, and put her face in her inked hands, and I — a voice in a blade leaning against a shed — did the only thing left to me. I kept silent, and kept company.

It was nearly noon when she spoke again, and when she did, her voice had changed. The fury was still in it, banked like coals, but underneath it ran something colder and more durable. Dread, yes. But dread, properly tended, becomes resolve.

"The season," she said. "You said the Door opens one season. Which season? How long do I have?"

"The Door opens at autumn's turning," I said. "Sixty-one days."

She looked east, toward the mountains she had spent her life not looking at, and the map on her skin seemed to shift in the light, the passes darkening like roads filling with shadow.

"And Wren Ashvale rode into the valley this morning," she said. "I saw the horses from the ridge. Imperial grey. Six of them."

The hook of it went through me, dead as I was, like a spear through water.

"Inquisitors," I said.

"Yes," said Elodie Pemberton, last of the swordspeakers, standing in the yard of the only home she had ever known with a country inked into her arms and sixty days on the clock. "Looking for a stranger."
