## 4. The Inquisitor from Hollowmere

They came up the valley road on the ninth morning after the tremor, and Hollowmere knew them before it saw them.

The goats felt it first. Elodie was in the high pasture, mending a fence that did not need mending—her hands had needed the work more than the fence had—and when she looked down at the village, every animal on the hillside had gone still, ears pricked toward the pass like compass needles finding north. Then the church bell began to ring, not the slow toll for a funeral or the quick pattern for market day, but the flat, urgent repetition that meant *strangers on the road*, and Elodie's stomach dropped through the green earth of the valley as if it had been waiting nine days to do exactly that.

She counted six riders where the road bent out of the birch trees. Grey cloaks. The long coats of imperial cut, buttoned to the throat even in the valley's mild autumn, and on each breast a badge of black iron shaped like an open eye. Inquisitors. She had seen them only once before, as a girl, from behind her mother's skirts at a festival in Lowmark—the empire's hunters, its rememberers, its men and women who could look at a person and see what that person was trying never to be.

*They are looking for him,* said the voice at her hip.

Sebastian's voice. It lived in her blade now, bound there by his dying hand, and only she could hear it—a low, tired murmur like wind moving through an empty house. She had spent nine days learning not to answer aloud.

"I know," she murmured, turning away from the road, driving the goats downhill with a shepherd's easy boredom she did not feel. "Everyone in this valley is looking for him. That's the trouble with a dead man. He leaves such a large absence."

*They are not looking for a body, Elodie. They are looking for what he carried.*

Her hand went to her sleeve without thinking, where the map had burned itself into her skin three nights ago. She had held the parchment over the stove flame and watched it curl and blacken and come apart, and had felt, in the same instant, a heat crawl up her forearm like a climbing vine—and by morning the map lay inked beneath her skin in fine brown lines, rivers and passes and a door drawn in the crook of her elbow, complete with small annotations in Sebastian's cramped hand. *You cannot burn a thing that has already chosen you,* his voice had said, almost gently. She had not spoken to him for a day afterward.

Now she walked the goats down into Hollowmere, past the well where Marta Hale was drawing water, past the smithy where old Corvin hammered shoe-nails with the steady rhythm of a man determined to notice nothing, and understood, with a cold clarity, that the valley's great gift was already failing. For thirty years Hollowmere had forgotten things on purpose. Magic could not follow a person here; neither could memory, if a person worked at it. Her scar—the swordspeaker's brand, the oath-sigil seared into her left wrist when she was nineteen and foolish and proud of it—had faded year by year under the valley's gentle erasure, until some mornings she had to press her thumb against it to be sure it was still there.

But the tremor had cracked something. And now the eye-badged men were riding into the square, and every face in Hollowmere wore the expression Elodie knew best: the careful blankness of people rehearsing their own ignorance.

* * *

They set themselves up in the meetinghouse, which smelled of pitch and old grain, and the headman Tam Voss stood before them wringing his cap in his hands while the villagers filed past a table where a clerk recorded names in a ledger.

"The stranger," said the senior inquisitor, a woman with a voice like a file drawn across iron. She was called Serane; Elodie learned it by standing too near the open window of the meetinghouse, ostensibly shelling beans. "A man, dying or dead, who came over the western pass nine days ago. Someone carried him. Someone buried him, if he died. Someone remembers."

"No one came over the pass," Tam Voss said. "Nothing comes over the pass."

"Nothing comes over the pass," Serane agreed pleasantly. "And yet we are here. Curious, how often those two facts travel together." She turned a page. "We will ask each household. We will ask about wounds, about blood, about unusual kindnesses. Kindness is the tell, in these hidden places. Empires can forbid many things, but they cannot forbid people being kind, and so kindness becomes the crime we look for."

Elodie shelled beans and thought: *I washed his blood out of my skirt in the ford. I sat with him all night while he died. I dug his grave with my own hands above the tree line.* If kindness was the crime, she had committed a whole litany of it.

And then the second rider came through the meetinghouse door, and the beans nearly spilled from her lap.

He was taller than she remembered, and thinner, and he moved with the coiled economy of a man who had learned that stillness was safer than motion. But she would have known him anywhere, would have known him blind, by the way he paused in doorways as though asking permission of them. Wren Ashvale. Who had stolen apples with her from the orchard above the mill. Who had held her hand when the oath-brand was laid on her wrist and told her, laughing through his tears, that she looked like a saint and smelled like burnt hair. Who had left the valley at seventeen to take the empire's scholarship, and whom she had mourned quietly for years as one mourns the dead—not because they die, but because they stop writing.

He wore the grey coat now. The open-eye badge. An inquisitor's short sword at his hip, worn the way she wore hers: like something that had opinions about him.

Wren's gaze crossed the room the way a lantern beam crosses a barn—slowly, touching everything, resting on nothing. It passed over her. Passed back. Stopped.

For one heartbeat—one full turn of the world—Elodie watched recognition move through his face like frost through a windowpane. His eyes went to her hands, to the left wrist where her sleeve had ridden up as she worked, where the faded sigil showed pale as an old scar shows, which was exactly what it was supposed to look like and exactly what it wasn't.

Then he looked away, and asked the woman beside her whether she had seen anything strange on the roads, and his voice was perfectly level, and Elodie discovered she had stopped breathing somewhere in the last ten seconds and had to start again.

*That is Wren Ashvale,* said Sebastian's voice, and it was not a question. Ghosts, she was learning, perceived things strangely—they heard the shape of old love the way the living hear thunder.

"Yes," she breathed, barely sound at all.

*He knows your wrist.*

"He knows my whole life," she whispered into the bean basket. "That's worse."

* * *

They stayed four days. Four days of knocking doors, of ledger lines, of Serane's pleasant file-voice asking about blood and kindness. Four days of Elodie performing the valley's oldest art: forgetting in public. She smiled at Wren across the square with the polite blankness of a stranger, and hated herself for how easily she could do it, and hated the empire more for making blankness a survival skill.

On the fourth night, a stone clicked against her shutters in a pattern she hadn't heard since they were children. Two short, one long. *Come out. Careful.*

She went, blade wrapped in sacking on her back, heart hammering a rhythm older than fear.

He was waiting in the lane behind the tannery, hood up, alone. Up close the years fell off him and then settled back again, heavier. There was a new scar splitting one eyebrow, and shadows under his eyes that no academy taught you to carry.

"You should have gone over the pass the night the ground shook," he said, without greeting. "I told them the stranger died at the ford. Drowned. I put a nameless grave in my report, west slope, unmarked. They'll dig it tomorrow. It needs to hold a body, Elodie."

"You—" Her voice cracked. "You lied to the eye?"

"I falsified an imperial record." He said it flatly, like a man reading his own sentence. "Which is a hanging matter, so let us not waste time admiring it. Serane suspects the valley. She suspects everyone; that's her craft. What she does not yet suspect is you. Keep it that way for one more day and ride east before dawn."

"East." A laugh escaped her, jagged. "Wren, I don't even know what's east. I know a door that may not exist, and a dead man's map written on my arm, and—"

"I know what's east." Something moved behind his eyes, quick and pained. "I've read the sealed reports. Veylmark didn't fall, Elodie. It was *closed*. And the Door opens one season in thirty, and this is the season, and the Grand Inquisitor himself is coming to make sure it stays shut forever. You have perhaps two weeks' lead. Less, if Serane sends a bird tonight."

The words landed one by one, like stones dropped into a well. *The Grand Inquisitor.* The man whose name was spoken in the empire the way children speak of wolves.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Why any of it? You could report me right now. One word about my wrist and you're decorated."

He was quiet a long moment. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"When you took the oath-brand," he said finally, "you screamed, and I laughed, and you hit me with your unwounded hand, and we ate apples under the mill tree until dark. That's the last true memory I have that the empire didn't give me." His jaw tightened. "They've been giving me memories for eleven years, Elodie. Let me keep one of my own."

Then he pressed something into her palm—a folded paper, a route east through the salt country, marked in his own precise hand—and stepped back into the dark like a man stepping off a ledge.

"Before dawn," he said. "Ride hard. And Elodie—if they ever ask me, I will say I never found you. Make sure I'm telling the truth."

* * *

She left before the moon set, taking the drover's path above the village where the mist lay thick as wool. At the last bend she stopped and looked back at Hollowmere: the dark roofs, the banked fires, the little valley that had kept her safe by teaching her to forget. Somewhere below, a light moved in the meetinghouse—Serane, awake early, or Wren, feeding the fire that would cover her escape. She would never know which. Perhaps both.

*East,* said Sebastian's voice, warm at her hip like a coal. *Follow the water. The water remembers the way even when the maps forget.*

She touched her wrist once—sigil, scar, oath, whatever it was now—and turned her back on everything she had spent her whole life protecting by hiding.

Behind her, faint across the miles, she thought she heard the valley bell begin to ring.

Not the strangers'-coming ring. Not the funeral toll.

Something faster. Something like alarm.

She ran.
