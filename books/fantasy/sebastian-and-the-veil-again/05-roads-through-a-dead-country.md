## 5. Roads Through a Dead Country

I had drawn this road once, in ink and obedience, and I had never once walked it.

Now I walked it as a voice bound to a blade on a woman's hip, and the road did not remember me, which was fitting, because I had never remembered it either—not truly, not until it was too late to matter. A cartographer's curse: you know every country by its lines, and none of them by its heart.

Elodie walked ahead of me through the drowned village of Sarrowmere, her boots finding the submerged cobbles by feel, water to her ankles, grey-green and still as glass left too long in a window. The roofs of the houses broke the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. In one of them—numbered fourteen, though the numbers had been mine to assign, thirty years ago—a willow had rooted itself in what had been a kitchen and grown up through the thatch, its branches trailing in the flooded rooms where someone had once hung washing, fried fish, argued about the price of wool.

"You're quiet," she said. She said it without turning around. She was learning my habits, the way I went silent when something hurt.

"I'm remembering," I said. "It's a new discipline for me."

The water carried a smell of rot and iron and, beneath both, salt—the empire's signature, the taste of everything they had unmade. When Veylmark fell, they had not merely burned it. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be plowed under, and things grow from ash. No. They had salted the wells and opened the sluices and let the lowlands drink the sea, so that the country would drown in its own name. Then they had redrawn the maps. I had redrawn the maps. My hand, steady and fine-nosed, had turned nine hundred villages into *uninhabited marshland*, four cities into *quarantine zones*, a nation of songs and grievances and bread recipes into a blank space with a border running neatly through it.

Blank spaces are the most dangerous thing a map can hold. I knew that better than anyone alive, or nearly alive.

* * *

We made camp that night on the ridge above the orchards—or what had been orchards. The trees stood in rows like mourners at a funeral no one had dared to hold, their branches black and twisted, fruit long since fallen and rotted into the chalky soil. Salt-kill is a slow death; the trees had taken years to die, and in dying they had grown strange, barked over with white crustations like coral, reaching upward with fingers that seemed to beg.

Elodie built a small fire, shielded by stones, though there was no one for fifty miles to see it. Habit, she said. The habits of hiding outlive the hiding.

She ate hardtack and dried mutton and watched the dead trees, and I hovered at her shoulder in the way I had learned to, close enough to speak softly, far enough not to crowd whatever solitude she kept like a knife at her belt.

"My grandmother picked apples in those rows," she said at last.

"I know," I said. "Veylmark's western orchards were famous. Pippins the color of sunset. They shipped them as far as the capital." I stopped. I should have stopped sooner. But the knowledge poured out of me the way it always did, catalogued and precise, the way a man recites an inventory to keep from weeping over it.

"Shipped them," Elodie repeated. "You talk about my country like produce."

"It's how I was taught to talk about it."

She turned then, firelight on the scarred line of her jaw, and looked at me the way she had been looking at me for two days now—with the question held behind her teeth like a stone she was deciding whether to swallow or spit. That night she spat it.

"What were you, Sebastian? Before you were a bleeding man at a ford? Nobody runs from inquisitors carrying maps unless the maps matter. Nobody gets hunted across three provinces unless they *know* something." Her voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "So tell me what you were."

And I, who had rehearsed a dozen lies on the road—half-truths, softened truths, truths arranged in flattering light—found that all of them dissolved in the face of her patience. She waited. The fire cracked. A salt-dead branch fell somewhere down the slope with a sound like a bone breaking.

"I was the Cartographer Royal of the Imperial Survey," I said. "Third Office. Erasure Division."

* * *

She didn't move. I almost wished she would strike me; anger would have been easier to bear than the terrible stillness with which she received it.

"There were eleven of us," I went on, because once the dam breaks you may as well drown honestly. "The best draftsmen in the empire. We didn't carry swords—we carried pens, and we were told our work was mercy. That's the word they used. *Mercy.* A country that exists is a country that can rebel, that can be mourned, that can rise again. A country erased—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and awful. "A country erased is just weather. Just terrain. You cannot rally men to die for marshland, Elodie. We were very good. I was the best of them. I drew the borders of the new provinces over Veylmark's grave, and I adjusted the rivers, and I moved the mountain passes half a league east so that no road would lead where the sanctuaries had been, and when they asked me to falsify the old charts—the ones in the archives, the ones families kept hidden in floorboards—I did that too. I redrew a woman's grandfather's farm onto imperial land. I redrew her wedding church into a quarry. I did it beautifully. My linework was praised."

The fire had burned low. Elodie fed it a stick without looking away from me.

"The empire paid me in gold and in certainty," I said. "Certainty is the more addictive coin. Every morning I woke knowing exactly what the world was, because I had written it. And the people—" My voice failed there, briefly, and I was grateful ghosts cannot weep, or perhaps cursed. "The people were statistics. Displaced: forty thousand. Resettled: thirty-one thousand. Unaccounted: the rest. I signed the summary. I used my good pen."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you deserve to hate me with accurate information."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I admitted. "Here is the answer. Three years ago I found a chart in a provincial archive—a child's chart, drawn by hand, a girl who had copied her father's memory of Veylmark onto butcher paper. Rivers in blue thread. Little apples on the western orchards. And beside each village, in a child's careful letters, the names of the people who lived there. Nine hundred villages. Thousands of names. Names I had spent my career ensuring existed nowhere but in the heads of the aging and the hearts of the stubborn." I paused. "Her family was executed for possessing it. The chart was slated for burning. I stole it instead. I have been stealing it back ever since—one record, one name, one map at a time—running from the office I helped build, toward a door I only half believed in, carrying proof that Veylmark was real. That's what the inquisitors want burned. Not me. The *country*. Me they'd forgive. I'm useful."

Elodie was quiet a long time. The wind moved through the dead orchard, and the black branches creaked against each other like old men shifting in pews.

"I should leave you here," she said finally. "Tie your map to a stone and drop it in the floodwater and walk north alone."

"Yes."

"You helped kill my grandmother's apples."

"Yes."

"You helped erase—" Her breath caught, and for the first time I heard the fury underneath, banked but enormous, a forge-fire fed for thirty years by a woman who had been taught that feeling it was forbidden. "There were *songs*, Sebastian. My mother sang them to me in secret, whispering, with the shutters closed, and I never understood why until I was twelve and saw a man hanged in Hollowmere square for humming one. You took the songs off the maps. You took the *reason* for the songs off the maps. Do you know what it does to a child, to be handed a country in whispers? To inherit something everyone insists was never there?"

"No," I said. "But I know what it does to a man to insist nothing was ever there. It hollows him. He becomes very good at drawing coastlines and very bad at looking in mirrors."

She stared into the fire. I watched her contempt and her pity fight for possession of her face, and I did not flinch from either, because I had earned both, and because pity frightened me more. Contempt I could survive. Pity meant she had seen the shape of the punishment I had built for myself and was recognizing it as punishment.

"My order had a saying," she said at last. "*The oath remembers what the sword forgets.*" She looked at me sideways. "You're the reverse of a swordspeaker, do you know that? Everything you touched forgot. Everything."

"Yes," I said. "That's why I need you. Not the blade. The remembering."

* * *

We walked the next day along the raised causeway that had once been the King's Road of Veylmark—my road, my lines, my beautiful false geography—and past noon we came to a place where the salt gave way suddenly to green, a seam of living grass no wider than a stream, threading between the dead fields like a vein that had refused to clot.

Wildflowers grew there. Small, stubborn, blue as the ink I had once ruled the world with.

Elodie knelt and touched them, and said nothing, and I understood that this was the truest prayer either of us would offer in this country.

"They come back," she murmured. "Thirty years of salt, and they come back."

"Seeds travel," I said. "In birds' bellies. In mud on boots. In pockets." I hesitated, then gave her the last of my honesty for the day. "Exiles carried them. After the fall, refugees sewed seeds into their hems before they crossed the border. It was illegal—biological contraband, the Survey called it—but they did it anyway. Whole generations of gardeners planting Veylmark in foreign dirt, one stolen handful at a time."

Elodie rose. She brushed the earth from her knees with a strange gentleness, and when she spoke, her voice had changed—harder, aimed forward, down the causeway toward the mountains darkening on the horizon.

"Then we're doing the same thing," she said. "Walking seeds out of a dead country. Except ours are memories, and yours are confessions, and neither of us gets to set them down."

Ahead of us, where the green seam met the foothills, something caught the afternoon light—a roofline, a chimney smoke, the first sign of habitation since the ford. But it was not the smoke that made me go silent again, nor the roofline.

It was the banner hanging limp from a pole at the crossroads ahead. Grey cloth, unmarked, the flag of nowhere—which is to say, the flag the empire flew over places it had officially declared empty.

Someone was living in the empty place. Someone had lit a fire in the erased country.

"Elodie," I said quietly. "Smoke. And below it—do you hear that?"

She cocked her head. Faintly, on the wind, threaded through the smell of salt and coming rain, came something impossible: voices. Many voices. And underneath them, rising and falling, unmistakable even after thirty years of silence—

Singing.
