## 6. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

The rain found them an hour past dusk, and the tollhouse found them an hour after that.

It stood where the old salt road crossed a river that no longer had a name—Sebastian called it the Ammel, and said the name like a man laying flowers on a grave—a low stone building with a sagging roof and windows that glowed amber against the wet dark. Not the amber of firelight alone. Lamplight, many lamps, arranged with care. Light that someone had chosen to leave burning for travelers.

Elodie stopped at the edge of the drowned orchard that surrounded it and put out her arm to bar Sebastian's voice from going further, though he needed no barring; he had gone quiet the moment he saw the trees.

"Salt-killed," she said. The orchard was thirty years dead, every apple tree bleached white as bone, their branches twisted into shapes like hands raised in surrender or in blessing—she could not decide which. Rain dripped through them without leaves to soften its fall, so that the whole grove whispered.

"I mapped this orchard," Sebastian said quietly. "For the survey. Before." A pause, the way he paused when the word *before* had to carry everything it carried. "There was a family here who grew a cider so sour it made your eyes water. Children used to dare each other to drink it."

"Are there people in the tollhouse?"

"Yes." He said it simply, because lying to her had become something he no longer did, or no longer could. "Exiles. Veylmarki, most likely—they've kept to the old roads since the fall. Tollhouses were neutral ground once. Way stations. The empire never bothered burning them because they never bothered learning what happened inside them."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know. But you're soaked to the bone, your blade is rusting at the seam where my voice lives in it, and we have walked three days on bread that went hard before your grandmother was born." A ghost's laugh, dry as the orchard. "I am not going to be the one who talks you out of a warm fire, Elodie Pemberton."

She stood a moment longer in the rain, listening. Under the drum of water on stone she heard voices—not the sharp cadence of imperial garrison talk but something rounder, older, vowels that bent the way the valley people's vowels had bent before Hollowmere taught itself to flatten them. Her chest did something complicated and painful at the sound.

*You are a woman who hides a scar under a wool sleeve,* she told herself. *You are nobody. You tend goats.*

But her feet were already moving toward the light.

* * *

The door opened before she knocked.

A woman stood there, broad-shouldered and gray-haired, with flour to her elbows and eyes the color of strong tea. She looked at Elodie the way no one in Hollowmere had ever looked at anyone: fully, without the polite blindness that forgetting required.

"You're walking the salt road in this weather," the woman said. It was not a question, and it was not quite a welcome either. It was an accounting. "Alone?"

"Yes."

"No one is alone on the salt road." The woman stepped back. "Come in before the river decides otherwise. I'm Maren Ostrow. My mother was born in Carreth-am-Ammel, which is under forty feet of water nine miles east, and if that means nothing to you, come in anyway, because you look like it means something."

It meant something. Elodie crossed the threshold with rain running off her in ropes and stood dripping on flagstones worn smooth by two hundred years of travelers' boots, and the warmth hit her like a physical blow—the bread-smell, the lamp-oil, the low murmur of perhaps twenty people in the long room beyond, and under all of it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the smell of woodsmoke and old grief that seemed to live in the walls of every building Veylmark had ever raised.

Maren looked at the sword on Elodie's back for a long moment. At the wrapped hilt. At the way Elodie's hand rested near it, not threateningly, just *near*, the way a hand rests near a thing it has promised something to.

"We don't ask names here," Maren said. "Names get people killed. But if you're carrying what I think you're carrying, you should know the last swordspeaker came through eleven years ago, heading north, and none of us ever learned whether she reached wherever she was going."

"She didn't," Sebastian said, only to Elodie, only inside the steel. "I checked the records. They caught her at the border stones."

Elodie said nothing. She followed Maren into the light.

* * *

The long room held a life.

That was the thing that undid her, later, when she tried to explain it to herself—that it wasn't shelter merely, wasn't survival crouched in a ruin. There were children asleep on pallets by the hearth. There was a young man mending a fishing net with the particular patience of someone who intended to use it. There was an old man teaching two girls a song, clapping the rhythm wrong on purpose until they laughed and corrected him. On the walls hung things that had no business surviving thirty years of erasure: a woven banner in the green and white of the Ammel valley, a child's drawing of a city skyline Elodie recognized from Sebastian's maps as Vel Sarren, capital of a country that officially had never existed.

Food appeared in front of her without her asking. Barley soup with wild garlic, dark bread, a cup of cider so sour her eyes watered, and she laughed—actually laughed, a rusty sound—at the taste, and Maren saw it and smiled like a woman who had set a trap and caught exactly what she'd hoped for.

"Sour enough?" Maren asked.

"The children dared each other," Elodie said, and then heard herself say it, and went still.

"They still do." Maren sat down across from her, unhurried. "Recipes outlive countries. That's the whole trick of us, if you want to know. The empire can burn a library, redraw a map, make a language illegal in courts and schools. But they cannot follow a grandmother into a kitchen and stop her from teaching a girl how bread is supposed to taste." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "You're from a valley. South. Where the fog sits heavy and nobody asks questions."

Elodie's hand moved toward her sleeve. Stopped.

"I'm not going to pull it back," Maren said gently. "Whatever you're hiding under there, it's yours. I'm telling you what I see, which is a young woman built like a fence post and scarred like a soldier, eating soup in a house full of people who remember, and looking at our hearth the way a drowning woman looks at a rope." She leaned forward. "So I'll say the plain thing, because the plain thing deserves saying. Stay."

"Maren—"

"No, let me finish, because I've watched good people walk out that door into the empire's teeth and I am tired of being polite about it." Her tea-brown eyes didn't waver. "Stay. There's work here—there's always work. We're short of hands that know which end of a blade is which, and I won't pretend we don't need that, because the toll roads grow less safe every year. You'd have a bed. You'd have the winter. You'd have—" she gestured, taking in the sleeping children, the net-mender, the song, all of it, "—this. Whatever this is worth. And in spring, if you still want the road, the road will still be there."

"It won't," Sebastian said softly, in the steel against her spine. "Elodie. The Door opens one season. This season."

*I know,* she thought back, the way she had learned to think at him, wordless and half-formed, and felt him receive it anyway.

Because that was the terrible arithmetic of it. The soup was real. The hearth was real. The old man had begun the song again, and this time the girls got the clapping right, and the sound of it went through Elodie like a blade finding the gap in armor she hadn't known she was wearing. Thirty years ago these people had lost everything—country, names, graves—and they had rebuilt belonging out of barley soup and a banner and a song sung slightly wrong on purpose. It could be done. Belonging could be grown again from almost nothing.

And the Door wanted her to trade it. To give up the valley's protection forever, to stand in front of the Grand Inquisitor's vanguard with a reforged oath and a ghost in her sword, to pay for a country's memory with everything she had spent twenty years learning to survive without.

"Stay," Maren said again, quieter now. "You look like someone who has been alone a very long time."

"I have," Elodie said. It came out before she could stop it, honest as a wound.

"Then you know what I'm offering."

She did. That was the unbearable part. She sat in the lamplight with the sour cider warming in her blood and let herself want it—really want it, for ten whole minutes, the length of the song. She imagined winter here. She imagined learning the names Maren wouldn't ask for, giving hers freely. She imagined the scar staying hidden not because hiding was law but because no one here would ever need it shown. She imagined being a woman who tended something other than goats.

*Say yes,* said a voice in her that was not Sebastian's, that was entirely her own, tired and young and starving. *Let someone else open the Door. Let someone else remember.*

"There is no one else," Sebastian said, gentle, reading even that. "You know there isn't. I bound myself to a swordspeaker, Elodie. Not to a woman who wants a warm room. Though gods forgive me—" the ghost's voice cracked like old paper, "—I wish you could be both."

The song ended. The old man bowed to the girls' applause, and one of the sleeping children stirred and murmured a word in old Veylmarki, a word for *home* that no schoolbook had printed in thirty years, and Elodie closed her eyes.

"I'll stay the night," she said. "One night."

Maren studied her, and whatever she saw, she had the grace not to name it. "One night," she agreed. "The loft over the stable is dry. I'll bring blankets."

* * *

She woke to a hand over her mouth and Maren's face above hers, lit by a shuttered lantern, and the tea-brown eyes gone hard as river stones.

"Horses on the road," Maren breathed. "Six. Maybe eight. Imperial saddlery—I know the sound of it, everyone here does." Downstairs, barely audible beneath the rain, the tollhouse was moving: nets gathered, children lifted half-asleep from pallets, the banner coming off the wall with practiced speed, everyone folding themselves into the spaces the old building offered because they had done this before, more than once, and were good at it now, which was its own kind of grief.

"Where?" Elodie whispered.

"The root cellar behind the hearthstone. There's a false wall." Maren's grip tightened. "Whoever they're hunting—if it's you, understand what you cost us by being found. And if it isn't you—" her jaw worked, "—then hide anyway, and let us handle what comes through that door. We've survived worse than questions."

Boots on gravel outside. The jingle of tack. A fist on the door, courteous and terrible.

"In the name of the Empire, open. We seek two travelers. A woman with a sword, and—" a pause Elodie knew the voice making it, knew it the way you know a scar, "—and a man who is no longer living."

Wren.

Maren pulled her toward the hearthstone, and Elodie went, blade in hand, heart hammering a name she had not spoken aloud in fifteen years.
