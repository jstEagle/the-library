## 7. Ashvale's Choice

The commission arrived at dawn, and it smelled of ash.

Wren Ashvale stood in the grey light outside the Tollhouse of Small Mercies with the imperial seal warm against his palm, wax still soft from the courier's hurried fire. *By order of the Grand Inquisitor: the settlement known as the Tollhouse harbors remnant loyalists of the erased nation of Veylmark. Let none survive to carry its memory. Signed under the eye of the Throne.*

He had read such orders before. He had carried them out before—twice, in the border provinces, where the houses burned quickly and the screaming was brief because the empire's methods were efficient and the empire's mercy was a locked door. He had told himself, both times, that forgetting was a kindness. That a country dead thirty years was better off buried than mourned.

He read the order again now, and his hands would not stop shaking.

Behind him, the tollhouse slept. Smoke curled from its chimney in a thin, innocent thread. Somewhere inside, an old woman who called herself Maren was banking the coals for morning bread. Somewhere inside, children were curled together under wool blankets that smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke, children who had never seen Veylmark whole but who sang its songs in a language the empire had forbidden, because their grandparents had taught them the melodies first and the words after, and no edict could unteach a lullaby.

*Let none survive.*

Wren folded the commission along its crease. Then he unfolded it. Then he folded it again, smaller and smaller, until it was a hard little square he could have swallowed.

The valley wind came down off the pass and touched the back of his neck like a hand he almost remembered.

---

He had been nine years old when he first understood what Hollowmere asked of its children. Not obedience—Hollowmere's forgetting was gentler than that, a civic softness, a willingness not to look too long at anything. Elodie Pemberton had been the only child in the village who looked. She'd looked at the tremor-scarred hills, at the way travelers passed through without stopping, at the strange blankness in her neighbors' eyes when old names were spoken. She'd looked until looking got her scarred, and then she'd hidden the scar, and then she'd hidden herself, and Wren had loved her the whole time in the quiet, useless way that children love: entirely, wordlessly, and without ever once telling her.

Now he stood outside a house full of people who remembered everything, holding an order to kill them, and he understood at last that he had spent thirty years learning exactly the wrong lesson from the valley that raised him.

They had taught him not to look. The empire had taught him what happened when you did.

* * *

Maren found him on the porch as the sky went from grey to the color of old pewter. She was small and round and moved like someone who had survived by being unremarkable, and she looked at the paper in his fist and then at his face, and whatever she saw there made her go very still.

"They've come for us," she said. It wasn't a question.

"The vanguard is two days behind me," Wren said. "The Grand Inquisitor himself is behind them." He heard his own voice and barely recognized it—flat, precise, the voice they had trained into him at the Inquisitorial College, where boys from nowhere were given purpose and purpose was given teeth. "You have until tomorrow night. Maybe less."

Maren's hands folded into her apron. "And you? What does your order say you must do?"

Wren opened his fist. The little square of paper sat in his palm like a coal that hadn't finished burning.

"It says," he said, "*let none survive.*"

The old woman nodded slowly, the way one nods at weather. "And what will you do?"

That was the question, wasn't it. It had been the question since the ford, since he'd knelt beside the blood-darkened stones where Sebastian Corvane had died and seen, half-hidden in the trampled grass, the pale seam of an old scar on a woman's wrist—the swordspeaker's mark, the oath-line, the thing he'd been ordered to report within the hour and had instead covered with his own glove while his heart hammered like a traitor's drum.

He had told himself it was hesitation. Hesitation was forgivable. Hesitation was even useful; inquisitors who hesitated were watched, and watched men learned things.

But it hadn't been hesitation. It had been recognition. He had looked at that scar and seen a girl of eleven standing on the schoolhouse steps, refusing to say the loyalty verse, her chin up, her eyes furious and frightened and *alive*, and he had thought: *they will burn her for this,* and then he had thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *then let them burn me first.*

"I don't know," he said honestly.

Maren studied him for a long moment. Then she said, "Come inside. You look like a man who hasn't eaten since he stopped believing in himself."

* * *

He ate their bread. That was the beginning of it, though he didn't know it yet. He sat at their table among exiles who remembered a drowned country, and they fed him without asking his name, and a boy of maybe seven showed him a carved wooden boat—"my granda sailed the Saltmere ferry, before the water took it"—and Wren held the small toy in his hands and felt something crack open in his chest that thirty years of service had sealed shut.

These people were not traitors. They were *remains*. They were what the empire had failed to grind fine enough, and they had gathered here in a ruined tollhouse on a dead road and rebuilt, out of songs and seeds and stubbornness, everything the empire had decreed forgotten.

And he had come to burn it.

He left the table before the meal ended. Out in the yard, the night had come down thick, stars drowned in low cloud, and he stood in the dark with the commission in his hand and the college's teachings in his ears—*mercy is disorder, memory is sedition, the sword that spares becomes the sword that turns*—and he thought about Elodie, somewhere ahead of him on the road to the sanctuary, carrying a ghost's voice and a map written on her skin, walking toward the one door the empire feared above all things.

The Grand Inquisitor was coming for her. Not a vanguard this time, not a column of grey-cloaked functionaries with their neat warrants and neater fires. *Him.* The man whose name was spoken in the College only in whispers, if at all. The man who had designed the erasure of Veylmark, who had unmade a country so thoroughly that maps printed it as empty grassland, who believed—not cynically, but with the terrible sincerity of the true believer—that a people unwritten was a people saved from themselves.

Wren looked down at the commission.

Then he walked to the forge at the edge of the yard, where the exile smith banked his coals each night, and he found the embers still breathing orange under their ash, and he held the order over them and let go.

The paper caught slowly, reluctantly, the wax seal bubbling black, the Grand Inquisitor's signature curling into nothing. Wren watched every word of it die. *Let none survive.* Gone. The heat climbed his wrist and he did not pull back until the last fragment lifted off his palm as a fleck of light and vanished.

"There's no coming back from that," said the smith, from the doorway behind him. Not accusing. Just noting, the way one notes rain.

"No," Wren agreed. "There isn't."

"Then I'd move quick, son. And I'd take the north track. The dogs won't scent you past the birches."

* * *

He gave them until moonrise. When it came, thin and watery through the cloud, he knocked on doors and spoke low and fast: *the vanguard comes tomorrow, take the north track, scatter wide, don't regroup for a season.* Maren pressed a bundle into his hands—bread, dried apples, a flask—and gripped his forearm hard enough to bruise. "Tell her," the old woman said, and he knew she meant Elodie, knew the exiles had guessed who hunted whom across these hills, "tell her the Door remembers what we remember. Tell her we're coming."

"You can't come," Wren said. "It isn't safe—"

"Boy." Maren smiled, and for a moment she was not small at all. "We are the memory of a dead country. There has never been a safe place for us. There is only forward."

They scattered into the dark like sparks off a struck flint, and Wren stood alone in the empty yard of the Tollhouse of Small Mercies, listening to the silence where a community had been, and felt—for the first time in his adult life—clean.

Then he turned north, toward the sanctuary ruins, toward the woman he had been sent to capture, and began to run.

* * *

He caught her trail at dawn on the second day, where the road climbed out of the salt-flats and the air changed, grew older, grew *green*. Her tracks were careful—a swordspeaker's training, weight on the outer edges, pace measured—but Sebastian's voice must have been guiding her well, because she moved faster than any fugitive he'd ever tracked. Twice he found places where she'd doubled back to look at something: a fallen boundary stone carved with the old alphabet, a rowan tree grown through the ribs of a burnt barn. She was reading the country as she fled it. She was *learning* it.

He nearly called out to her there, on the ridge above the second ford, when he saw her small figure picking along the far bank. But the wind was wrong, and something else was wrong besides—a prickle at his nape, the trained animal sense that other hunters were near. The vanguard had moved faster than promised. Or the Grand Inquisitor had sent something ahead of his vanguard, the way a man sends his shadow ahead of his body.

So Wren did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than burning the commission, harder than betraying thirty years: he stayed hidden, and followed, and waited for ground where he could speak to her alone.

It came at dusk, in a hollow below the sanctuary road, where the ruins' broken towers finally showed against the sky like the stumps of burned teeth. She had stopped to rest—or the voice had made her stop—and he came down into the hollow with his hands open and empty and his sword still slung, and he said the only thing he had rehearsed for two days:

"Elodie. It's Wren. I'm not here to take you back."

She came up off the rock like a drawn blade, and even exhausted, even half-starved, she was the fastest thing he had ever seen—sword out, stance perfect, the oath-scar on her wrist faintly luminous in the dying light. Sebastian's voice was a cold shimmer in the air around her, and Wren felt it press against him like a hand testing a door.

"Wren Ashvale." Her voice was flat as slate. "Inquisitor Second Rank. Come to finish what the ford started?"

"Elodie—"

"Don't." The sword point didn't waver. "Don't say my name like you used to. You burned villages, Wren. I've heard what you've done. I've heard what you *are*."

Every word landed true, and he let them, because they were earned. "Yes," he said quietly. "All of it. I won't insult you by explaining it away." He sank slowly to one knee in the wet grass, deliberately, making himself small, making himself harmless. "I burned my commission two nights ago. I emptied the tollhouse before the vanguard arrived. There is no version of my life now that ends with me going home, because I don't have one." He looked up at her, and let her see all of it—the fracture, the ruin, the strange lightness underneath. "I'm here because the Grand Inquisitor is three days behind me. Maybe less. And Elodie—he isn't coming for the Door."

Her breath caught. Around her, the ghost-voice had gone very still, listening.

"He's coming for *you*. For the last swordspeaker. Because an oath reforged can't be unmade by burning a village or banning a song—it can only be unmade by killing the one who speaks it." Wren rose slowly to his feet, and behind him, far down the darkening valley, he saw it: a line of torches winding up the pass like a lit fuse, moving faster than any column had a right to move. "He'll be here by tomorrow night. So whatever you came to do at the sanctuary—"

The torches flared, all together, all at once, though the wind blew against them.

"—you have until then."
