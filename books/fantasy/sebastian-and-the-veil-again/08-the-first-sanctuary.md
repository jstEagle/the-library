## 8. The First Sanctuary

The mountain did not want us.

I felt it in the way the path narrowed and twisted back on itself, in the way the pines leaned close like gossips deciding whether to speak. Three days we had climbed since the tollhouse, since Wren's warning burned in my ears — *the Grand Inquisitor is coming himself* — and the higher we went, the more the world forgot to be gentle. Frost silvered the stones at dawn and did not melt by noon. The wind came down from the peaks carrying a smell like old iron and older snow.

Sebastian's voice rode the cold beside me, bound still to the blade I had not yet learned to stop carrying like a wound.

"You're quiet," he said.

"I'm counting," I said. "Steps. Breath. Everything I might lose."

"Elodie—"

"Don't." I said it softly, because the mountain was listening, but I said it all the same. "Not until we're there."

*There* revealed itself near dusk on the third day, when the trees gave up their argument and the valley opened above the treeline like a cupped hand. I stopped so suddenly that my boots skidded on scree, and for a long moment I could not breathe at all.

The sanctuary of the Swordspeakers sat in the bowl of the mountain exactly as it lived in my memory — which is to say, ruined. I had never seen it whole; no one alive had. My mother had described it to me in fragments, the way you describe a dream you're afraid of losing: *the training court with its seven pillars, the refectory roofed in blue slate, the hall where oaths were spoken.* Thirty years of weather had finished what the empire started. The seven pillars stood broken at different heights, like teeth in an old jaw. The blue slate had slid into the grass in glittering drifts. And the oath-hall —

The oath-hall still had its walls.

They rose out of the ruin pale as bone, windowless, doorless, waiting. Snow had drifted against them but would not lie upon them; it slid away in thin ribbons, as if the stone remembered being refused and refused in turn.

"It held," Sebastian whispered, and his voice cracked like ice underfoot. "After everything, it *held.*"

We climbed the last stretch in silence. At the threshold of the oath-hall I hesitated, one boot on the sill, and felt something pass over me — not resistance, not welcome. Recognition. The way a dog lifts its head when it hears a footstep it has waited thirty years to hear.

Inside, the floor was bare granite worn smooth in two long grooves, knee-width apart, where generations of kneeling swordspeakers had pressed their devotion into stone. Moonlight fell through the collapsed roof in a slanted column, catching motes of dust that turned, slowly, like things asleep.

I knelt in the grooves without deciding to. My knees found them the way water finds a channel.

"Now," Sebastian said gently. "Now I tell you the last of it, and then you'll hate me properly, and then — if you can — you'll listen anyway."

I stayed kneeling. "Say it."

"The Door isn't a place, Elodie. It's a promise. Veylmark's founders made it when the country was young — a way out, kept open once a generation, for anyone the land had failed. But a promise needs a keeper, and the keepers were the Swordspeakers. Not the empire's swordspeakers. Ours." He paused, and I heard him gathering courage the way a man gathers breath before deep water. "The Door opens to a reforged oath. Not the old words — those died with the order, and the empire made sure of it. New words. Spoken by a living swordspeaker who chooses what the sword protects rather than whom it kills. That's the key. That's the only key. And the price—"

"I know the price." My voice came out flat. "Wren told me. The valley's protection, forever. Everyone in Hollowmere left to the empire's mercy."

"No." Sebastian's voice went very quiet. "That's not the price. That's the *cost*, and they're different things. The price is this: the Door stays open only while the one who opened it keeps choosing. Every hour. Every day. For the rest of your life, you will stand at that threshold and choose protection over vengeance, memory over peace, strangers over yourself. The Door doesn't take something from you once, Elodie. It takes something from you *always.*"

The column of moonlight shifted. Somewhere far below, a bird cried out and was answered by nothing.

"And if I stop choosing?"

"Then it closes. On whoever is still passing through."

I looked down at my hands on the worn granite. The scar along my right forearm — the oath-scar, cut into me at fourteen by a woman three days from death, the last mark of a dead order — had been aching since the ford, since Sebastian bled out under my hands and put his voice into my blade. Now it burned like a coal wrapped in skin.

"They'll come tonight," I said. "The vanguard. Wren said the Grand Inquisitor follows within a day."

"Yes."

"Then I don't have time to hate you properly."

His laugh was small and broken and grateful. "No. You don't."

* * *

I spent the dark hours doing what my mother taught me before she died of the fever that took half of Hollowmere: cleaning the blade, mending the straps, sharpening what could be sharpened. Ritual work. Hand-work. The kind of work that keeps terror from climbing into your throat and nesting there.

But terror came anyway, in the hours before dawn, when the wind changed.

It came smelling of pitch and horse and sanctified oil, rolling up the mountain ahead of the torches, and I stood in the ruined training court among the seven broken pillars and watched lights bloom in the pass below like a fever breaking through skin. Twenty riders, maybe thirty. Lances with the empire's sunburst. Banners I remembered from the day the inquisitors came to Hollowmere's edge and turned back, thwarted, cursing the valley's forgetting.

They were not turning back now. There was nothing here to forget them.

"Elodie." Sebastian's voice, tight. "You don't have to face them here. The oath-hall — the old wards still have teeth. You could hold the threshold and speak the words behind stone."

"And let them tear the sanctuary apart around me while I fumble at words I've never said?" I rolled my shoulders. Drew the blade. His blade. Our blade. "My mother said the first sanctuary was built on a simple truth. That a swordspeaker meets the world standing up."

"That woman sounds like she'd have hated me."

"She'd have hated you *properly,*" I said, and stepped out onto the field of snow and broken slate.

They came up the switchbacks in good order, torches guttering in the wind, and at their head rode a man on a grey horse whose armor was white as the mountain and bore no ornament at all — which frightened me more than any banner would have. Men who decorate themselves are telling you who they want to be. Men who wear nothing are telling you they've already become it.

The Grand Inquisitor's vanguard halted forty paces out. A herald raised a horn but did not blow it; instead a voice carried up the slope, amplified by some engine of permitted sorcery, smooth as poured wax:

*"Elodie Pemberton. Last of the oath-bound. By decree of the Sunlit Throne, surrender the relic you carry and the ghost bound within it, and your death will be quick and your body returned to your valley."*

My valley. As if they hadn't spent thirty years pretending Veylmark had never existed. As if Hollowmere were anything but a wound they meant to reopen.

I walked forward alone, boots crunching frost, and stopped ten paces from the line of lances. Close enough to see faces under helmets. Young faces, mostly. Faces that had never seen a swordspeak, never heard an oath spoken true, never known there had ever been another way to hold a blade.

"I'm going to say some words now," I called out, and my voice surprised me — steady, almost conversational, though my heart was hammering like a fist on a locked door. "And when I finish, you should ride home."

The herald laughed. The white-armored man did not. He lifted one gauntlet, and the vanguard leveled its lances as one animal.

Terror rose in me then, whole and black, the way floodwater rises — I thought of Hollowmere's hearths, of the goats I'd never milk again, of Wren somewhere behind that wall of steel with his burned commission hidden against his chest, of Sebastian's voice that I would silence forever if I failed. My knees wanted the ground. My hands wanted to drop the sword.

Instead I knelt. Deliberately. In the snow, in front of all of them, the way my mothers and fathers of the order had knelt for four hundred years — not in surrender, but in *address*. You kneel to speak an oath. That is the whole of the tradition. You kneel because the words deserve a lowered head.

I laid the flat of the blade against my scarred forearm, and the scar sang.

"I am Elodie Pemberton," I said, and the mountain took my voice and gave it back larger. "Daughter of Hollowmere. Last speaker of the sword. I do not swear by whom my blade will kill. I swear by what it will protect."

The wind stopped. All of it, everywhere, at once — the whole mountain holding its breath.

"The door of my country stands shut, and I will open it. The people of my country stand scattered, and I will gather them. The memory of my country stands condemned, and I will carry it — through fire, through forgetting, through the empire's long night — into whatever waits beyond."

The scar split with light. Not blood — light, pouring along the old cut like dawn along a valley floor, and the blade in my hands woke with a sound I had no word for, a sound like a bell remembering itself.

"The sword protects," I said. "So help me keep the choosing."

Light broke upward from the oath-hall behind me, from the seven broken pillars, from every grooved stone where dead knees had prayed — and down in the vanguard, horses screamed and reared, and the white-armored man drove his spurs in with a snarl I heard even across the distance, even over the roaring of the sanctuary waking after thirty years of sleep.

"Elodie!" Sebastian's voice blazed in my skull, wild with something between terror and joy. "It's working — the Door is answering — but the reforging isn't finished! It needs the binding poured out — it needs *me* — and Elodie, once I go into the steel, I can't come back—"

Below me, the vanguard charged.

Above me, the mountain began, very softly, to sing.
