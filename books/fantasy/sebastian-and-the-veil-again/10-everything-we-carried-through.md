## 10. Everything We Carried Through

The land beyond the Door smelled of rain that had never fallen on Veylmark.

Elodie stood at the threshold long after the others had passed through, her hand pressed flat against the stone frame, feeling the last warmth of it drain away beneath her palm. The Door did not close with a slam or a whisper. It simply forgot itself—stone softening into hillside, hillside into meadow grass, until there was nothing left to mark where one country had ended and another begun. Only she would remember the shape of it. That was the price. That had always been the price.

Behind her, through the thinning veil, she could feel Hollowmere letting her go. Not all at once—the valley's forgetting came gently, like snow melting from a roof in spring. First it would take her name from the mouths of neighbors. Then her face from the memory of the baker's wife who had sold her bread every market day for eleven years. Then the goats, her goats, standing patient at their fence, would turn their heads toward an empty gate and feel no question about it. The warding was broken now; the valley could not protect what it could not remember, and so it chose, in its slow animal way, to forget the wound rather than tend it.

She let it happen. She stood in the new country's morning light and let it happen, and it hurt worse than any blade she had ever taken against her skin.

"Elodie." Wren's voice, behind her. Roughened by smoke and the pass, by everything he had burned to be here. "They're waiting."

"I know."

"The map's fading."

She looked down at her forearm, where Sebastian's ink had lived for the length of their journey—the drowned villages, the salt orchards, the winding road to the Last Door, all of it drawn in lines that glowed faintly when his voice moved through them. The Door was crossed now. The map had done its work. And still the lines held, stubborn as scar tissue, refusing to fade even though the ghost who had drawn them was gone into her blade and out of it, spent entirely in the reforging.

She was glad of that. She would carry him the way he had asked: not as a voice, but as a road.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a fold of hills above a river the exiles immediately began arguing about naming. The Tollhouse folk had come through in ones and twos over the final desperate days—Maren the weaver with her loom straps wrapped around her shoulders like a soldier's bandolier; old Tam and his granddaughter, carrying three hens and a jar of sourdough starter more precious to them than gold; the brothers Hollis, who had not spoken to each other in nine years and now would not leave each other's sight. Forty-one souls in all. Forty-one people who remembered Veylmark, which meant forty-one people the empire would have erased, and forty-one people Elodie had sworn—new oath, new words, new sword—to protect.

The seeds came out first, always the seeds. Maren spread them on a blanket by firelight: barley from the drowned terraces of Lowmarsh, apple pips from orchards the salt had killed, beans that had grown in Veylmark's soil for six hundred years before the empire ever drew its borders. They were dry and small and dead-looking, and when Tam's granddaughter asked if anything would grow in this foreign ground, nobody answered her directly. But everyone took turns holding the little cloth packets, passing them hand to hand around the fire like something being blessed.

"They'll grow," Elodie said finally, because someone had to say it, and because saying it was a kind of oath too. "Soil doesn't know whose it is. It only knows what you ask of it."

Maren smiled at her across the flames—a tired, cracked smile, but real. "You sound like your order's supposed to have sounded. Before."

"We never got the chance to find out." She turned her sword across her knees. The blade had changed in the reforging; the steel held a faint light now, like moonrise caught under the surface of water. "Maybe that's what we are now. The chance, late."

* * *

Wren found her at the edge of camp near midnight, the way he had always found her—in childhood, in Hollowmere, in the years when they'd been two children daring each other to touch the cold river. He sat down without asking, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below them the fire crackled. Somewhere a hen complained. The new country went on being unfamiliar around them, vast and green and utterly indifferent to their grief.

"The valley's forgotten me already," he said at last. "I felt it go. Like a door closing in a house I grew up in."

"Do you regret it? Burning the commission?"

He laughed, low and without much humor. "Every hour. And I'd do it again every hour. Both things are true." He turned to look at her, and in the starlight his face was older than thirty years should have made it. "That's what they never taught us in the inquisitor's academy, Elodie. That you can hold both. That holding both is the whole work of being a person."

"The empire teaches otherwise."

"The empire is a country that decided forgetting was easier than grieving." He said it flatly, without heat, and she heard in it the shape of his whole betrayal—not of her, never of her, but of everything he'd been made to serve. "We're going to be better than that. All of us. We're going to remember until remembering costs us something, and then remember anyway."

She reached over and took his hand. His fingers were blistered from the pass, from the rope, from the burning of his own name. She held them anyway.

"You could have gone back," she said. "You could have reported the exiles' route and been forgiven. Grand Inquisitor Vale spared you once already—he thinks you're hunting us."

"He thinks wrong." Wren squeezed her hand once, hard, and let go. "I'm not hunting anyone. I'm following. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters anymore."

* * *

Spring came to the new country before any of them trusted it.

The barley went into ground that had never known Veylmark's names for its seasons, and it came up green, and Tam wept over it openly and unashamed. The apple pips went into a nursery trench Maren guarded like a relic. The hens multiplied. Children who had been born in hiding learned to run in open fields without flinching at the sight of riders on distant roads—and there were riders sometimes, travelers from whatever kingdoms lay beyond, who looked at the settlement of forty-one strangers building houses in a river valley and saw only farmers, only refugees, only people.

Elodie taught them the songs.

That was the part she hadn't planned, the part that crept up on her in the second autumn, when she realized the children were singing harvest rounds in Old Veylmarki without knowing what the words meant, and she understood that this was how a country survived its own death. Not in maps. Not in borders. In songs passed mouth to mouth to mouths that didn't know they were keeping anything alive. She sang them the lullabies her grandmother had sung, the planting songs, the bitter funny ballads about tax collectors and foolish kings, and she watched the language take root in children who would never see the rivers it named.

Sebastian's map stayed inked on her skin, faded now to the color of old tea, the drowned villages rendered in lines only she could read. Some nights she traced it by candlelight, village by village, saying the names aloud to an empty room. Lowmarsh. Cael Ford. The Tollhouse road. The valley. Hollowmere. Saying them was the duty. Forgetting was the empire's weapon, and her whole body had become the counterargument.

* * *

On the third spring, a traveler came up the river road with dust on his boots and a sealed letter in his pack, and asked at the settlement gate for the woman called Pemberton.

Elodie met him in the doorway of the meeting hall, sword at her hip out of habit, and the traveler—a trader, by his look, weathered and unafraid—handed over the letter without ceremony. No seal she recognized. The paper was cheap. The handwriting inside was cramped and hurried and heartbreakingly familiar, though she had never seen it before in her life.

*If you are reading this, then the Door opened, and the valley forgot, and I am writing to a woman I cannot remember knowing.*

*I found pages. My own pages—I think they are mine. A journal in a hand like my hand, describing a ford, a stranger bleeding out, a map pressed into dying hands. I don't remember any of it. But the valley keeps things it forgets, buried under the chapel stones, and I dug until I found them.*

*The tremors have started again here. The warding is failing all over, they say. Whole valleys waking up. The empire is frightened, and frightened empires do what they always do.*

*Come home, whoever you are. Or send word. Someone in this village remembers you enough to grieve, and none of us know why.*

It was signed with a name she knew: the baker's wife. Marta. Who had sold her bread every market day for eleven years and now mourned a stranger she couldn't name.

Elodie sat down slowly on the meeting hall bench, the letter trembling in her hands, and outside she could hear the children singing the planting songs in a dead country's tongue, and the river running bright and unnamed, and Wren laughing at something in the yard—the sound of everything she had built, everything she had paid, everything she had carried through.

The valley was failing again. Which meant there were others like the Tollhouse folk, scattered and hunted and running out of places to hide. Others who needed a door.

She looked down at the faded map on her arm, at the ghost-lines of a country that existed now only in ink and song and the stubbornness of forty-one hearts.

Then she reached for fresh paper, and a pen, and began to draw.
