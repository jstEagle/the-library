## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There were three rules to surviving as the king's food-taster, and Zephyrine Delacroix had learned them all the hard way, which was the only way anyone learned anything in Thornfell.

Rule one: never eat with pleasure. A taster who enjoyed his meals was a taster who lingered, and a taster who lingered got curious, and curiosity in the palace of Virelay was a short road to a shallow grave behind the almonry. So Zephyrine ate like a man doing arithmetic. He chewed each mouthful exactly nine times—enough for the tongue to report, not enough for it to get attached—and he swallowed with the enthusiasm of a clerk signing someone else's name.

Rule two: never be interesting. For nine years he had cultivated an invisibility so complete that the under-stewards called him *the Grey Man* behind his back, and once, memorably, to his face by accident. He was of average height, average coloring, with a face like a door left slightly ajar—you could look at it and take away nothing. He had trained himself out of startle reflexes, out of humming, out of the habit of looking up when doors opened. The court forgot him between meals, which was precisely the point.

Rule three—the rule that mattered, the rule that had kept his heartbeat regular through two assassination attempts and one genuinely alarming cheese—was this: *know your poisons better than the poisoners do.*

He did. That was not pride speaking, or not only pride. It was simple bookkeeping. In nine years of service, Zephyrine had swallowed arsenic twice, nightshade once (bad week), distilled crow-bile four times, and a rotating cast of lesser villains: bitter almond smuggled into marchpane, foxglove steeped in a "medicinal" tisane, lead in the wine of a visiting duke who had made the mistake of insulting the king's hat. Each time, he had tasted it before it could reach the royal table, and each time he had survived, because Master Apothecary Hennet kept antidotes for everything that grew, crawled, or could be purchased south of the Veil-mountains.

The surviving was not heroism. It was simply that the king required a taster who could be poisoned *repeatedly*, and so the palace physicians had spent a small fortune making Zephyrine's blood into something like a fortress. Mithridatism, Hennet called it. *Building your walls stone by stone.* Zephyrine thought of it less grandly: they had fed him poison in small doses for nine years until his body stopped arguing. It was, he sometimes reflected in the grey hour before sleep, a strange way to be valuable—to be useful precisely because you could be hurt and keep standing.

Nobody had ever asked whether it *hurt*. It did. But that was rule zero, the unspoken one: nobody asks the fork about its feelings.

On the evening everything changed, the kitchen served quail.

* * *

He should have known from the quail. The kitchens were nervous that night—he'd seen it in the way the head cook, Dame Orla, kept wiping her hands on her apron though they were dry. A delegation had arrived from the eastern provinces, silver-tongued men with rings like knuckle-dusters, and the king wanted to impress them without seeming to want to impress them, which meant the menu had been revised four times since noon. Quail stuffed with morel and chestnut, glazed in honey and blackthorn. Ambitious. Ambitious food was where mistakes lived.

Zephyrine stood at the sideboard in his grey servant's coat while the under-cooks hovered, and he took up the small silver spoon that was both his tool and, in a sense, his entire identity.

First bite: the stuffing. Morels, chestnut, butter, a whisper of thyme. Clean. Second bite: the glaze. Honey, blackthorn, wine reduced with—

He stopped chewing.

There is no elegant way to describe what lightning tastes like, because Zephyrine was fairly sure no one had ever tasted it and lived to write about it, but he would have tried, later, if anyone had asked, which no one ever did. It was copper, but wrong-copper, copper turned inside out. It was the smell of a summer storm concentrated onto the tongue until the tongue itself seemed to hum. It was cold and hot at once, in the specific contradictory way of metal left out in sun. And underneath it, faint as a signature on a letter, was something green. Living. *Awake.*

His body knew what to do. His body had done this many times: the fortress-blood slammed its gates, his stomach clenched around the mouthful like a fist, and sweat broke cold along his hairline even as his face remained perfectly, professionally blank. Nine years of practice. You did not clutch your throat. You did not gasp. You set down the spoon, took a slow sip of water—not too slow, nothing to notice—and said, in the flat voice of a man reporting weather:

"The glaze."

Dame Orla went white. "The glaze? The honey?"

"The wine in the reduction." He kept his tone bored, which was the trick of it; panic in a taster caused more deaths than poison ever had. "Hennet will want the bottle. Don't let anyone touch the pan."

They moved. Kitchens, whatever their hierarchies, understood a command delivered calmly, and within moments there was shouting and running and a scullery boy dispatched at a dead sprint for the apothecary. Zephyrine remained at the sideboard, upright, grey, invisible, while inside him something entirely new was happening.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that made his hands, hidden at his sides, curl slowly inward.

Hennet arrived within the quarter hour, sniffed the reduction, went through his whole liturgy of reagents, and emerged frowning. "Not arsenic. Not any alkaloid I know. Some mineral contamination, perhaps—" He looked up at Zephyrine with the first genuine interest anyone had shown him in months. "What did it taste like?"

"Off," said Zephyrine. "Metallic."

"Hm." Hennet was already losing interest; a taster's palate was a tool, and tools were not consulted on their findings. "You'll take the broad-spectrum draught tonight, and again at dawn. Report any numbness, tremor, visions—"

"I know my symptoms," Zephyrine said mildly.

And he did. Numbness: no. Tremor: no. Visions: well.

Here was what he did not say. Here was what he carried back to his narrow room above the kennels—a room chosen for its remoteness, its anonymity, the fact that no one visited a kennel-adjacent taster unless the king was dying—here is what sat down on the edge of his cot with him and refused to leave:

The poison had not felt like poison. It had felt like *recognition*.

When the lightning-taste had hit his tongue, for one half of one heartbeat, the fortress of his blood had not slammed its gates. It had *opened* them. Every other toxin in nine years had met resistance, war, siege. This thing had walked in like it remembered the way. And in that half-heartbeat—before training clamped down, before he swallowed and killed the moment—Zephyrine had heard, clear as a bell rung inside his skull rather than beside it:

*"Rue."*

Just that. One word. His childhood name, the name his mother had used, the name that had died with her in the fever-year when he was eleven and that no living soul in Thornfell knew. Not spoken so much as *remembered at him*—warm, amused, familiar, the voice of someone greeting an old friend across a market square.

No one knew that name. No one *could* know that name.

Zephyrine sat on his cot and listened to the dogs settling below the floorboards and told himself, with the methodical calm that had gotten him through nine years of swallowing death on schedule, that poisons could cause hallucinations. That Hennet's list included visions. That the mind, starved of company, made voices the way a damp cellar made mold.

It was a reasonable explanation. It was almost convincing. It failed entirely on one point, and he kept circling back to it like a tongue returning to a broken tooth:

The voice had sounded *glad* to see him. Nobody in Thornfell had sounded glad to see Zephyrine Delacroix in nine years.

* * *

He did not sleep, which was normal enough after a poisoning; he counted the hours instead, the way he always did, marking his own pulse against the kennel-dogs' dreams. Toward the smallest hour of the morning, when the draught had settled and the trembling in his hands had finally stopped, he permitted himself the ritual.

Every taster kept a ledger. Officially, Hennet's ledger recorded doses and antidotes, symptoms and recoveries—dry lines for dry men. Unofficially, every taster Zephyrine had ever trained under had kept a second book, passed hand to hand, hidden in flour sacks and floorboards: a taster's true ledger, recording *tastes*. What the poison resembled. How it announced itself. Which dishes lied best. It was contraband knowledge, technically—it could hang you, teaching a poisoner to hide from your tongue—but it was also the only inheritance a taster ever got, since the job paid poorly and ended abruptly.

Zephyrine pried up the third floorboard—kennel-side, beneath where a dog named Bristle liked to sleep, because no thief in history had argued with Bristle—and drew out the oilskin-wrapped book. He dipped his pen. By candlelight, in the shorthand only tasters used, he wrote:

*Quail, honey-blackthorn glaze, wine reduction. Unknown agent. Copper, storm-smell, green underneath. Fortress opened rather than resisted. Heard: my mother's name for me. Duration: half a heartbeat. Cause unknown. Recommendation:*

He paused, pen hovering. What was the recommendation? *Avoid quail*? As if the poison had been in the bird. *Report fully to Hennet*? And say what—that the toxin had greeted him personally? They burned people for less in Thornfell, and lately they burned people for nothing at all; the old gods had been outlawed a generation ago, and the king's zealots still swept the villages for shrines, for charms, for anyone caught whispering to anything that listened. A taster who heard voices would not be cured. A taster who heard voices would be *examined*, and then disposed of, politely, in the way the palace disposed of everything inconvenient.

*Recommendation:* he wrote at last, *observe. Say nothing.*

Then, because his hand seemed to move without consulting him, he added one line more, small, at the bottom of the entry, in letters barely bigger than dust:

*It knew me.*

He sat back and looked at those three words for a long time. Candle-guttering. Dogs dreaming. Somewhere across the sleeping palace, the king breathed safely atop a meal that might have killed him, guarded by a Grey Man who had just discovered he was not, perhaps, guarding anything at all—or guarding something far larger than a table.

Zephyrine closed the ledger, wrapped it, hid it, and lay down.

Sleep, when it finally came, was shallow and strange, full of green light under water. And at the very bottom of the night, in the place where dreams stop being yours, he heard it again—not the word this time, not his name, but a voice like wind deciding to be kind, saying something he couldn't quite catch, patient as weather. As if it were quite content to wait. As if it had waited a very long time already, and knew, the way storms know, exactly which window he would leave open.

He woke at dawn with the taste of lightning still ghosting his tongue, and outside his narrow window, over the grey roofs of Virelay, the sky stood clear and cloudless and utterly innocent.

But far off to the east—so far it might have been imagination, might have been the last of the draught working through his blood—the horizon flickered. Once. Twice.

Like something, somewhere beyond the mountains, opening its eyes.
