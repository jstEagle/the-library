## 2. Prayers Answered

Brannoc was dying the way he did everything else: loudly, badly, and with a great deal of complaint about the quality of the accommodations.

"You call this a bed?" he rasped from the cot in the back room of the Drowned Lantern, where the smugglers' inn kept its sickroom, its storeroom, and its secrets all within arm's reach of one another. "I've slept on warmer flagstones. I've slept on *friendlier* flagstones."

"It's a bed," Zephyrine said, wringing out the cloth in the basin and laying it across his mentor's forehead. The fever had come on four days ago, after the crossing at Marrow Ford, and it did not behave like any fever Zephyrine knew. He knew fevers the way he knew poisons—intimately, catalogued by symptom and progression—and this one sat wrong. It burned cold at the edges. It made Brannoc's eyes go glassy and fixed on corners of the room as though something were standing there.

"It's a plank with ambitions," Brannoc said. Then, quieter, in the voice that always undid Zephyrine a little because it was so rarely used: "You didn't have to come."

"You'd have died alone."

"I'd have died *dramatically*. There's a difference. There's witnesses for dramatic dying." He coughed, a wet, terrible sound, and Zephyrine's hand hovered uselessly over him. Nine years of tasting the king's food had taught Zephyrine exactly how bodies failed, in meticulous detail, and none of that knowledge could be poured backward into a man to make him well. He knew the shape of every death except how to stop them.

"Drink this," he said instead, and held the cup of willowbark and honey to Brannoc's lips.

Brannoc drank, grimaced with theatrical offense, and said, "You've poisoned me. Finally. Took you long enough. I've been training you for it for twenty years."

"Twenty-two," Zephyrine said. "And if I were going to poison you, old man, it would taste better than that."

It was an old rhythm between them, worn smooth as a river stone. Brannoc had found Zephyrine at fourteen, half-starved on the docks of Greyharbor, and had fed him, taught him the roads and the ciphers and which customs men could be bribed with coin and which required flattery, and had never once asked about the boy's past beyond what was needed to keep him alive. In return Zephyrine had become the closest thing to a son the old smuggler would admit to, which he expressed chiefly through insults delivered with great affection and terrible timing.

Now the timing was running out. Zephyrine could see it in the gray of Brannoc's lips, in the way the fever climbed each evening higher than the last, no matter what the herb-wives brought. Three days, maybe four. He had begun doing the arithmetic without meaning to, the way he did with everything: how much willowbark remained, how far to the nearest physician worth the name, how many coins in his hidden purse against how many days.

"There's a thing," Brannoc said that evening, when the lamp was low and the inn below had gone quiet. His voice had gone strange—not delirious, but sly, the way it went when he was setting up a joke he thought was very funny indeed. "On the coast road. Past the salt marshes. There's an old shrine."

"The kind with the fines," Zephyrine said. Shrines were illegal. Had been for a generation—the Edict of Silence, they called it in the histories, though nobody called it anything anymore, because you didn't speak of gods the way you didn't spit into the wind. The roadside shrines had been smashed or buried or quietly converted into boundary markers and hitching posts. Praying to the old gods wasn't merely heresy; it was sedition, and the crown punished sedition with a thoroughness that had made the word *prayer* feel almost obscene.

"The kind with the fines," Brannoc agreed, grinning around the word like a man chewing a sweet. "Moss-grown. Half-fallen. Nobody's tended it in thirty years. You'll know it by the stone—it's got a bowl carved in the top, like a bird bath for very small, very serious birds."

"What about it?"

"I want you to pray there."

Zephyrine stared at him. "You want me to—"

"For me. A healing prayer. To—" Brannoc waved a vague hand at the ceiling, at thirty years of outlawed heaven. "Whichever one handles fevers. There used to be one. Sennet? Sorrel? Something with an S. They'll have a department for it."

"You're dying and you're spending your last lucid hours making jokes about divine bureaucracy."

"I'm spending my last hours however I like, boy, that's the entire privilege of dying." He caught Zephyrine's wrist. His grip was still strong; that was the cruelest part of it, that the hands were strong while everything behind them failed. "I'm not entirely joking."

Zephyrine went still. With Brannoc you learned to listen for the second layer under a joke, the cargo hidden beneath the manifest. "What do you mean?"

"I mean my mother prayed at that shrine when I was a lad, before the Edict, and I got better from a fever that should've folded me up like a letter. And I mean—" he paused, and for a moment the slyness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath looked almost like fear, "—I mean I've been seeing things in the fever, Zeph. Corners of rooms. Doors where there aren't doors. And I heard—I'd swear it on my own ship, and you know what that oath costs me—I heard someone say my name in a voice like water going under a bridge. And I don't spook easy. But I spooked."

"Fever dreams."

"Probably." He said it too quickly. "But humor me. It's a day's ride. The air will do you good. And if you pray and nothing happens, you can come back and tell me I'm a superstitious old wreck, and I'll laugh, and it'll be a good memory for you to keep. That's worth a day's ride, isn't it? Giving an old man's nonsense a fair trial?"

Zephyrine looked at him a long moment. Brannoc looked back with the particular stubbornness of a man who had talked his way past three separate blockades armed with nothing but confidence and a forged bill of lading.

"If anyone sees me," Zephyrine said, "I'll tell them I stopped to relieve myself against your shrine."

"That's the spirit. Irreverence is practically a prayer these days."

* * *

The road past the salt marshes was nobody's favorite road. It ran flat and white-skinned between the reeds, and the wind came off the water carrying a smell like old coins and rotten eggs, and the only sounds were the cries of birds that Zephyrine suspected of being personally offended by travelers. He rode with his hood up and his taster's face on—that careful blankness he wore in the palace, the face that noticed everything and revealed nothing. He had worn it so long now that it sometimes felt less like a mask than like his actual face, with whatever had been underneath filed away for safekeeping.

He found the shrine just past noon, where the road bent inland around a hill of gorse.

Brannoc had undersold it, which was unlike him. It was not half-fallen; it was mostly fallen, a huddle of mossy stones with one upright slab leaning at an angle of permanent apology. The carved bowl at its top was cracked down the middle and full of rainwater and one drowned beetle. Whatever god it had been raised to was illegible now, the features of the stone worn to a suggestion—a brow, perhaps, or a fold of robe. Around its base, someone had once planted white flowers; their descendants still came up every spring in a thin, defiant ring, though no one living had tended them.

Zephyrine dismounted. Stood there feeling foolish. Checked the road both ways with the reflexes of nine years of palace service, then checked it again, because praying was the sort of crime you couldn't talk your way out of.

"Well," he said to the stone. "Here I am. Committing treason on behalf of a dying smuggler who thinks fever is a postal service."

The stone said nothing. The beetle floated. Somewhere in the reeds a bird made its opinion known.

He should have turned around then. He was a practical man; practicality was his armor and his livelihood. But he thought of Brannoc's grip on his wrist, and the voice like water under a bridge, and—he would not examine why, not yet, not standing in the open—he thought of the poison that tasted of lightning, three weeks ago, and the voice that had said a name he hadn't heard since childhood. He had told himself it was the toxin. He had a whole shelf of explanations, neatly labeled, and he reached for one now and found his hand wouldn't close on it.

So Zephyrine Delacroix, food-taster to the crown of Thornfell, keeper of the king's most dangerous mouthfuls, knelt in the mud before a broken stone and did the thing that could cost him his tongue, his post, and quite possibly his head.

He prayed.

He didn't know how. There had been no temples in his childhood that admitted boys like him, and the words had been confiscated along with everything else. So he did it the way he did everything: plainly, and a little defensively.

"I don't know your name," he said. "I don't know if you're there. I don't know if this is even legal to say out loud, and I'd appreciate it if we could agree it wasn't. But there's a man named Brannoc Vane, and he's dying of a fever, and he is—in spite of everything, in spite of being a criminal and a liar and the single most irritating person I have ever met—the best man I know. He pulled me out of the harbor when I was fourteen and nobody else even looked. And I am asking—if asking works, if there's anyone to ask—you let him keep living. Not forever. I'm not unreasonable. Just longer than this. Long enough that his death is a story instead of a sentence cut off mid-word."

His voice cracked on the last of it, which embarrassed him so much he added, quickly, "Also I left the beetle in your bowl. I should have taken it out. Sorry."

Silence. Wind. The marsh ticking and settling around him like a house at night.

Nothing happened, obviously. Nothing happened the way it had not happened for thirty years, all across Thornfell, above every smashed altar and silenced bell. Zephyrine stood, brushed off his knees, and felt the specific foolishness of a grown man who has spoken aloud to weather.

He rode back to the Drowned Lantern telling himself the whole day had been for Brannoc's sake, a kindness purchased cheaply, and if some tightness in his chest refused to loosen, that was only the ride, only the wind, only the memory of kneeling in the mud and meaning it.

* * *

The fever broke in the night.

Zephyrine was asleep in the chair beside the cot when it happened, and woke to a silence that was wrong—wrong because the ragged, whistling labor of Brannoc's breathing had stopped, and for one black moment he thought he was too late. Then the old man snored. A tremendous, unlovely, miraculous snore, the snore of a man whose body had remembered how to simply breathe.

"Brannoc?"

"Mm." One eye opened. It was clear. Clear as spring water, clear as a sky after rain, with none of the glassy fixedness of the last five days. "What time is it? I'm starving. Is there pie?"

Zephyrine put the back of his hand to the old man's forehead. Cool. Dry. The fever that had laughed at willowbark and honey and every herb-wife in the district was simply—gone, the way a storm goes, leaving the air scrubbed and new.

"Your color's good," Zephyrine said faintly. "Your pulse is—" he took it, counted, frowned. "Annoyingly robust."

"Told you." Brannoc struggled up onto one elbow with the energy of a man twenty years younger. "Told you the shrine owed me. My mother paid in advance. Thirty years of interest." He grinned, and then the grin faltered, because he was looking at Zephyrine properly now, and whatever he saw made him go quiet in a way that was far more alarming than the fever had been. "Zeph. Your eyes."

"What about my eyes?"

"They're—" He groped for it. "There's light in them. Like a window at dusk when there's someone home. Sit down, boy."

"I'm sitting."

"Sit *down*."

Zephyrine sat. And it was then, in the lamplight, with Brannoc staring at him as though he'd grown a second head, that Zephyrine finally let himself hear the thing that had been humming at the edge of his attention since the marshes. Since the lightning-poison. A presence, faint as a smell of rain before the clouds arrive—not outside him but *alongside* him, attentive, patient, the way a dog waits at a door it knows will open.

"Something answered," Zephyrine said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. Tasters learned steadiness young; you swallowed the arsenic with a pleasant expression or you didn't keep the post. "At the shrine. I felt—afterward, riding back, I felt like I'd been listened to. I told myself it was nothing."

"And now?"

"Now it feels like it's waiting for me to finish talking."

Brannoc was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, the jokes were gone entirely, and what remained was the old smuggler at his most dangerous and most honest, the man who navigated reefs by reading water no one else could read.

"When I was a boy," he said, "before the Edict, my grandmother told me the gods never left Thornfell. Everyone says the Edict banished them, drove them out over the sea or into the ground or wherever. But she said that's not how it works. She said you can outlaw a god the same way you can outlaw a river—make it illegal all you like, the water doesn't care. All you can do is dam it. And she said—" he tapped the cot frame, once, hard, "—she said a dammed river doesn't stop. It *waits*. It gets heavy. And someday, somewhere, it finds the crack."

"The Edict was thirty years ago."

"Aye. And dams don't hold forever, do they." Brannoc looked at Zephyrine's hands—at the hands that had tasted nine years of poison, building immunity dose by dose, swallowing death in increments until his body became a fortress no toxin could storm. "Boy. What did you say the poison tasted like? The one that nearly took you, back in the autumn."

"Lightning," Zephyrine said. "Ozone and hot copper. No apothecary could identify it."

"And afterward you heard a voice."

"...Yes."

"Said what?"

Zephyrine hesitated. He had told no one. He had barely admitted it to himself, filing it under *toxin symptom, transient, resolved*. But Brannoc was looking at him with those clear new eyes, and lying to Brannoc had never once worked, not in twenty-two years.

"My name," he said. "Not my real name. My childhood name. The one my mother used. Nobody living knows it."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as though listening.

Brannoc exhaled slowly. "Then either you've been poisoned by something out of the oldest stories," he said, "or something out of the oldest stories has been waiting thirty years for a man with a tongue like yours—someone who swallows what would kill anyone else, someone who walks into the warden's own hall every day and drinks from the warden's own cup—and it just found its door."

Outside, far off across the marshes, thunder rolled. There were no clouds.

Zephyrine sat very still, awe and terror tangled in his chest like two hands clasped too tightly to tell apart, and understood that his life of careful invisibility had ended somewhere between a broken shrine and a bowl of rainwater—and that whatever had woken inside the silence of Thornfell knew his name, and was not finished speaking.

"We need to get you out of the capital," Brannoc said quietly. "Tonight, if we can manage it. Because if the gods are waking, boy, the first thing the wardens will do is find whoever they're waking *through*."
