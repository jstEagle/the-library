## 3. The Warden's Daughter

Isolde had been taught to read a room the way other girls were taught to read embroidery: patiently, thread by thread, until the pattern announced itself. So when she pushed open her father's study door at an hour no one was meant to disturb him and found him feeding papers into the fire one by one—slowly, almost tenderly, like a man burying a beloved pet—she read the room immediately.

Something was wrong. And something was *interesting*.

"Father," she said, because retreating would have required an explanation for retreating, and she hadn't got one that wasn't a lie. "You're up early."

The king did not startle. Her father never startled; it was one of the things the ballads praised about him, though Isolde suspected the balladeers had simply never watched him eat soup. He glanced over his shoulder with the mild expression of a man caught doing nothing worse than correspondence.

"Isolde. You're up earlier."

"I couldn't sleep." True enough. She'd slept badly all week, in the way one sleeps badly before a storm, or before an exam, or—in her case—before absolutely nothing she could name, which was the most irritating variety. "What are you burning?"

"Old maps." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, as though the world were a slightly dull banquet he was graciously attending. "Outdated. The surveyor's office produces new ones every decade; these are clutter."

He turned back to the fire before she could answer, which was itself an answer. Her father never ended a conversation first unless the conversation was winning.

She crossed the room anyway, ostensibly to warm her hands at the hearth, and looked down into the flames. Maps, yes—or what remained of them. She could see a coastline curling into ash, a mountain range going brown at its peaks. But they weren't surveyor's maps. Surveyor's maps were precise and dull, covered in elevation figures and the little stamped seal of the Office of Roads. These were older. The parchment was heavy, cream-colored, hand-inked. One fragment showed a river running through a valley labeled in a script she didn't recognize—not Old Thornish, not church Latin, something rounder, like handwriting that had decided letters were optional.

And there were places on it she had never heard of. That was the part that snagged in her like a fishhook. Isolde had been educated by the finest tutors money and royal anxiety could procure. She knew every province, every duchy, every market town with a population larger than a good dinner party. She had once won a bet with Corvin by naming all forty-one bridges of the capital in order of age. (Corvin had paid up sulkily and then claimed the bet had been about something else.)

These names meant nothing. *Vellimore.* *The Hushlands.* *Carrowmere.* They sounded like places from a storybook—which, she supposed, was exactly how her father wanted them to look as they burned.

"Clutter," she agreed pleasantly. "Terrible stuff, outdated geography."

Her father smiled at the fire. "Go back to bed, sweetheart. You'll want your rest for the Harvest audience tomorrow."

She went. She even made it look convincing—the small curtsy, the shuffle of slippers, the door drawn shut with a well-bred click. Then she stood in the corridor outside her father's study for the better part of an hour, listening to paper catch and burn, counting the sheets by sound. Forty-one. She counted forty-one pages, and then she thought of the bridges, and laughed once, quietly, at herself, and went to find the archivist.

* * *

Master Ellery Voss, Keeper of the Royal Archive, lived in the archive, in the sense that a barnacle lives on a ship. He was a narrow man of about sixty who had apparently decided sometime in his thirties that clothing was a formality best acknowledged rather than observed, and whose spectacles were perpetually smudged because cleaning them would require setting down whatever he was holding, and he was always holding something.

Isolde found him at dawn in the map room, asleep upright in a chair with an atlas open on his chest like armor.

"Master Voss."

One eye opened. It found her, assessed her rank, and closed again. "Your Highness. If this is about the request from the College of Heralds, the answer remains no. A coat of arms cannot be 'mostly dragon.'"

"It's not about the heralds." She pulled a stool over and sat, arranging herself the way she did for audiences: hands folded, spine straight, face pleasant and utterly immovable. She had learned from watching petitioners that stillness unnerved people far more than anger did. Anger gave them something to push against. Stillness just left them alone with you. "I have some questions about old maps."

"Mm." Both eyes open now. "Which maps?"

"The kind my father burns at five in the morning."

Silence. Somewhere in the stacks, a mouse moved with the confidence of a creature who had never once been catalogued. Master Voss took off his spectacles, polished them on a sleeve that had seen better centuries, and put them back on—a ritual Isolde recognized as the archivist's equivalent of clearing one's throat for a very long time.

"His Majesty burns documents regularly," Voss said carefully. "It's within his right."

"These were hand-drawn. Pre-Sundering script. Places I've never heard of." She leaned forward. "Master Voss, I have memorized this kingdom. Every province. Every bridge. There is no such place as Carrowmere. I checked the gazetteer twice last night, and then I checked the index of the gazetteer, and then I checked whether someone had stolen the relevant page, because that seemed more likely than the alternative."

"And what is the alternative, Highness?"

"That the page isn't missing. That it was never written. That there are places that used to exist and don't anymore, and everyone above a certain age has agreed not to mention it."

Voss looked at her for a long moment. Isolde had the distinct sensation of being weighed—not as a princess, but as a question. Then he sighed, and the sigh seemed to come up from his boots.

"You have your mother's way of asking things," he said. "She'd corner me in here too, back when she—" He stopped. Adjusted his spectacles. "What exactly do you want to know?"

"Everything you're allowed to tell me. And then we'll see about the rest."

That earned her something that was almost a smile. "Come, then. And mind the ladder on row nine. It's a liar."

* * *

They walked deep into the archive, past the public shelves with their tidy histories—*A Chronicle of the Reign*, *The Unification Wars*, *Saint Aldric and the Taming of the Wilds*—and into a section Isolde had never seen, though she'd been coming to this building since she was six years old and had once gotten lost in it for four hours, an incident still spoken of in hushed tones by the under-librarians.

This section was different. Older. The books here weren't shelved so much as *kept*, wrapped in oilcloth, stacked flat like bodies. The air smelled of cedar and cold stone, though there was no window through which cold could enter.

"Officially," Voss said, unrolling a canvas sheet across a reading table, "this wing doesn't exist. Officially, the royal family's duties are ceremonial, agricultural, and martial, in roughly that order of enthusiasm. Unofficially—" he tapped the canvas, "—there is this."

Beneath the cloth lay a map of Thornfell. Not the Thornfell Isolde knew. This one showed the familiar borders—the mountains like a jagged crown along the north, the sea to the south—but drawn *inside* them, faint as breath on glass, was a second set of lines. Rivers that didn't match the real rivers. Roads that led to towns that weren't there. And around the whole country, following the coastline and the peaks precisely, ran a single unbroken line of ink so fine it might have been drawn with a spider's leg.

"What is that line?" Isolde asked.

"The Veil." Voss said it the way one says the name of a difficult relative. "Or rather, the record of it. The line isn't on the land, Highness. It's around it. Enclosing it."

"Enclosing it from what?"

Voss was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into the register men use in churches and sickrooms. "From outside. Or perhaps inside. The texts disagree, and the texts are all we have, because the ones who built it are gone." He drew out a slim volume, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, and opened it to a page dense with that round, letterless script—and beneath it, in cramped modern hand, a translation.

Isolde read. Then she read it again, because the first time her eyes had slid off it the way eyes slide off a word you don't want to understand.

*...the gods were not banished but bound. The Sundering was not a war but a sealing. The royal line does not rule Thornfell; it keeps it. Wardens, not kings. The throne is a lock, and the bloodline is the key that must never be turned.*

"They're wardens," she said slowly. "We're wardens. My family. For—" She looked up. "For *what*, exactly? What's being kept in?"

"The old gods." Voss's mouth twisted. "Every god the church burned in effigy, every shrine they tore down, every name struck from the records—that wasn't piety, Highness. That was bookkeeping. You can't imprison a god in a country unless you first make the country forget the god exists. Belief is a door, you see. As long as anyone remembers them, remembers *truly*, there's a crack in the wall."

Isolde sat down. She hadn't planned to; her legs simply filed a formal request and were granted. "My father knows this."

"Your father is the ninth warden since the Sundering. Your brother will be the tenth, if the line holds." Voss hesitated. "There is more. About what the wardenship *costs*. What it feeds on. I've read those pages, and I'll tell you this much now so you can decide whether to ask me the rest: it is not a duty anyone in your family has performed innocently. Not for a long time."

The lamp flickered. Outside, faintly, came the sound of the palace waking—grooms, kettles, a guard yawning through his rounds. The ordinary world, carrying on, unaware that it was apparently the lid of a jar.

"Why tell me any of this?" Isolde asked. "You've kept this secret for thirty years. Why break it for me over breakfast?"

Voss took off his spectacles again and held them up to the lamplight, inspecting a scratch that had presumably been there for a decade. "Because three nights ago," he said, "a prayer got answered."

She stared at him. "Prayers get answered all the time. The priests say so constantly. It's most of their material."

"The priests say prayers get answered the way the surveyor says roads get built—confidently, and about things he's never personally witnessed." Voss set the spectacles down. "I mean a prayer was *answered*, Highness. A dying man at a roadside shrine in the eastern passes. Fever that should have killed him by morning. He woke whole. And the shrine-stone—" he paused, and Isolde saw, with a strange lurch, that the old man's hands were shaking, "—the shrine-stone was warm. First time in a generation that anyone has recorded a warm stone. I have correspondents. Retired priests, village midwives, one extremely reliable smuggler. They write to me because I'm the only person in this kingdom who kept the old records instead of burning them." He met her eyes. "Something is waking up behind the Veil. And your father spent this morning burning the maps of everywhere it might go."

* * *

Isolde did not sleep that night either, but this time she could have told you why.

She sat in her rooms with a single candle and a stolen piece of parchment—stolen in the sense that she had taken it from her own stationery cupboard, which after today felt like a fitting rehearsal—and she made a list. Lists were how she thought. Corvin teased her for it; Corvin solved everything by shouting at it until it apologized.

*Things I know,* she wrote.

1. The Veil exists. It encloses Thornfell.
2. The old gods are imprisoned here. The royal family are their jailers.
3. The jailers' power comes from somewhere bad. (Ask Voss. Bracingly.)
4. Something answered a prayer. The wall may be cracking.
5. Father is destroying the old maps. Either hiding the cracks or hiding the doors.

She stared at item five for a long time, then added:

6. Corvin knows. I'm nearly sure of it. He's been odd since Midwinter—polite at Father, which Corvin never is unless he's afraid of him.

That one hurt to write, so she underlined it, which somehow made it hurt less, like pressing on a bruise to prove you were the one in charge of it.

The dutiful thing—the Isolde-shaped thing she had been all her life—would be to bring this to her father. Present her findings. Ask how she could help keep the lock turned. She was, after all, very good at duty; she had a whole shelf of commendations for it, arranged chronologically like trophies of a war she'd won against her own inclinations.

But duty, she was beginning to suspect, had been defined for her by the jailer. And the jailer fed on something. And nobody had ever told her what.

She blew out the candle, waited for her eyes to adjust, and looked out the window toward the eastern mountains, black against the stars. Somewhere out there was a warm stone, and a man who shouldn't be alive, and a crack in a wall her family had spent nine generations pretending wasn't there.

"Right," she said softly, to the list, to the mountains, to herself. "Then I suppose I'd better find out what we've been keeping."

In the morning, wearing a servant's cloak and carrying a forged travel pass she had produced in under an hour using skills she fully intended to deny under oath, Princess Isolde of Thornfell walked out through the kitchen gate and turned east.

Behind her, in the archive, Master Voss noticed the absence of one slim grey volume from his locked case, and found, in its place, a note in a fine, resolute hand:

*I'll bring it back. —I.*

He laughed until he had to sit down, and then he began, quietly, to pack his own bag.
