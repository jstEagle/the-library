## 4. Smuggler's Roads

The bell for second watch had not yet rung when Zephyrine slipped out of the taster's quarters with a satchel of bread, a knife, and nine years of carefully cultivated nothing.

He had rehearsed this. That was the thing about being invisible—you learned the shape of your own absence so well you could wear it like a coat. The night guard on the kitchen stair knew him as a man who took evening walks to settle his stomach, which was true, in the way that most useful lies are built on a foundation of truth. The gate sergeant owed him three favors Zephyrine had never once called in, because a favor unspent is a door that stays unlocked. He walked out of the palace the way smoke leaves a room: no one could say precisely when he'd gone.

Only when the cobbles gave way to mud did he let himself breathe.

*You run well,* said the voice.

Zephyrine stopped in the middle of the lane. A dog two yards off looked up at him, decided he was not interesting, and went back to its dreams.

"I'm not talking to you," he muttered.

*You're talking to someone. You've been muttering since the third bridge.*

"That was thinking. Thinking has a different texture."

*Does it? Describe it.*

He opened his mouth to explain—thinking was like stirring cold soup, slow and private, whereas *this* was like hearing his own name called from another room—and realized with dawning horror that he was, in fact, having a conversation. In the street. With a voice.

"Lightning," he said instead, and started walking again. "That poison tasted of lightning. No apothecary in Thornfell catalogs lightning. And then you—" he jabbed a finger at his own temple, which helped no one, "—you said my childhood name. Nobody living knows my childhood name."

*Nobody living,* the voice agreed, with something that might have been amusement. It sounded like wind through a doorway, like the particular hush when you cross from one room into another. *I know it because I have stood in every threshold you have ever crossed. Doorways remember what passes through them, little taster. I am what doorways remember.*

"And what do I call you?"

A pause. The kind of pause that meant the answer mattered more than the questioner understood.

*Vess.*

"Vess," Zephyrine repeated. It sat strangely on his tongue, the way all holy things do—too light and too heavy at once. "And what does a god want with a food-taster?"

*Not a god wants. This god needs.* For the first time, the voice lost its easy door-hush and went thin. *Someone is poisoning the king with things that were never meant to exist inside a body. Someone is testing me, Zephyrine Delacroix. Testing whether I wake whole or wake broken. And they used your tongue to find out.*

The road bent uphill toward the tanneries, and the smell of the city changed—smoke and hides and river rot. Zephyrine walked faster.

"Explain. Slowly. I'm a simple man. I taste things and occasionally die."

*You have not died once in nine years. Do you know how unlikely that is? Two hundred and eleven dishes. Fourteen confirmed poisons. Six that should have killed you outright.* Vess's voice wrapped around him like a draft under a door. *Your body is a lock that keeps learning new keys. Whatever they fed you tonight was a key made for gods, not men. Your tongue told them whether the lock still holds.*

"So I'm not a taster. I'm an experiment."

*You are a hinge,* Vess said. *And hinges get used by everyone who wants a door to open.*

---

By dawn he was past the tannery flats and into the hill country, where the farms grew stony and the roads grew honest about being footpaths. He had a plan, of sorts. It was a bad plan, held together with stubbornness and one very good secret: Zephyrine Delacroix, crown food-taster, invisible man, knew more hidden ways through Thornfell than any cartographer alive.

It came with the job, oddly enough. A taster must know every road a dish travels from kitchen to king—the back stairs, the dumbwaiters, the servant corridors, the hands it passes through—because poison enters by any of them. Nine years of mapping meals had taught him to map everything else. Cities were just kitchens with worse lighting.

But the mountains were different. The mountains he knew from before—from the childhood nobody living remembered, from a smuggler named Brannoc who had carried him over passes so old the roads themselves seemed embarrassed to exist.

"The shrine-roads," he said aloud, testing it.

*Yes,* Vess breathed, and there was hunger in it, real hunger, the first unguarded thing the god had shown. *The old ways. They built them for us, before the silencing. Every threshold stone between here and the high country is a place I can stand. Follow my voice and I will carry you where maps go blind.*

"And if I say no? If I walk to the coast instead and take ship somewhere with sensible gods and no voices?"

*Then I will spend the rest of your life being very polite in your head, and we will both be miserable.*

Despite everything—the fear sitting in his chest like swallowed stone, the certainty that soldiers would come asking after a missing taster—Zephyrine laughed. It startled him. He couldn't remember the last time he'd laughed without checking first who heard.

"You're a terrible god."

*I'm a minor god. We're all terrible. It's in the charter.*

* * *

They argued the whole way up.

That was the shape of it, Zephyrine learned. Vess did not command; Vess negotiated, endlessly, the way water negotiates with stone. When Zephyrine wanted to take the shepherd's track above Millbrook, Vess insisted on the dry streambed below it.

*Shepherds sell names,* the god said. *Streambeds don't talk.*

"Shepherds also know where the ground doesn't swallow you."

*So do I. I am extremely old, and thresholds are my entire personality.*

In the end Zephyrine took the streambed, mostly because arguing with a voice only he could hear was making him feel insane, and partly—and this was the part he didn't examine too closely—because the voice sounded tired. Not divine-tired. *Lonely*-tired. The tiredness of something that had been shut behind a door for a generation, listening to muffled footsteps pass.

The streambed climbed all day through grey stone and thin pine, and near dusk it delivered them—him—to a place that stopped Zephyrine mid-stride.

A threshold stone. A real one, older than the kingdom, standing alone where the streambed widened into a shelf of flat rock. It was waist-high, worn smooth, carved with lines that might have been writing or might have been rain given patience. And the moment Zephyrine stepped within arm's reach of it, the voice changed.

It wasn't coming from inside his head anymore. It was coming from *everywhere*, low and resonant, like a chord struck in an empty hall.

*Oh,* said Vess, and the single syllable contained relief the size of a decade. *Oh, that's better. Forgive me. Speaking through your skull is like whispering through wool.*

Zephyrine sat down heavily on the rock. His legs had opinions and all of them were complaints. He ate bread and hard cheese while the light went amber and then violet over the peaks, and the presence beside him—because it was beside him now, distinctly, occupying the stone the way a cat occupies a lap—said nothing at all, which was somehow louder than speech.

"You felt that," Zephyrine said finally. "The waking. All of you. What happens when the rest wake fully?"

Silence, long enough that he thought the god wouldn't answer.

*Thornfell was built to hold us,* Vess said at last. *Every wall, every law, every bell silenced and every shrine toppled—they were not piety inverted, little taster. They were mortar. And mortar cracks when the thing it holds pushes back.*

"Then I should run farther than the mountains."

*Or you should stop running toward the edges and start walking toward the center. There is a door, Zephyrine. There has always been a door. And I think—I am nearly certain—that you can hear it.*

He looked at the carved stone, at the violet sky, at his own hands, which had held two hundred and eleven dishes and never once trembled until now.

"I'm a food-taster," he said. "My greatest ambition was to die of old age, unnoticed, in a warm room."

*Ambitions change,* said Vess, gently, terribly. *Mine used to be a priesthood of forty thousand. Now I'd settle for one stubborn man who argues well.*

Zephyrine pulled his cloak around his shoulders and stared up at the first stars.

"One condition," he said. "If we're doing this—whatever this is—you tell me things straight. No riddles, no divine mystery theater. I've spent nine years around kings. I'm allergic."

*A god who speaks plainly,* Vess murmured. *What a pair we'll make.*

* * *

Three days higher, the weather turned against them, and so did the world.

Snow came sideways off the col in the grey hour before dawn, and Zephyrine, half-frozen and wholly lost, huddled beneath an overhang that Vess swore was on the way to the shrine-road proper. His fire wouldn't catch. His bread was gone. And below in the valley, faint but unmistakable, he could see torchlight moving in a line that was much too organized to be hunters after deer.

"They've connected it," he said quietly. "The poison. Me."

*Perhaps.*

"Don't comfort me. You're terrible at it."

*I'm excellent at it. I'm simply honest.* A pause. *They will check the tasters' quarters first, then the kitchens, then every road out of the capital. But they will not think to check the old ways, because the wardens forgot the old ways existed. Arrogance is a gift to the hunted.*

Zephyrine watched the torches. Twelve, maybe fourteen men, moving with the patient discipline of soldiers who expected their quarry to be stupid.

"Why me?" he asked, and it came out smaller than he intended. "There are smugglers who know these roads better. Priests who kept the faith in cellars. Why does the god of doorways need a man whose only talent is not dying?"

The snow hissed against the stone above him. When Vess answered, the voice was closer than it had ever been—not in his skull, not in the air, but in the space between, the way warmth lives between skin and blanket.

*Because you swallowed death fourteen times and asked it to teach you its manners. Because you prayed over a dying friend and meant it, and meaning it cracked you open wide enough for me to slip through. And because—* the voice hesitated, and Zephyrine had the strange sense of something vast choosing to be small, *—because when I said my name, you didn't flinch. Everyone else who hears a god's name flinches. You just complained about my manners.*

"That's not a reason. That's an insult I failed to take."

*It's the same thing,* said Vess, *in the old country.*

Below, the torches reached a fork in the valley road and paused. Zephyrine held his breath. Then, as one, they turned away—toward the coast road, toward the obvious—and the dark took them.

He let the breath out slowly.

"They'll circle back."

*Of course. Which is why we won't be here.* The presence shifted, and Zephyrine felt it the way you feel a door open in a far room—a pressure change, a possibility. *There is a smuggler's camp two days east, in the ruins of a bell-tower. Smugglers, exiles, and at least one person who owes Brannoc money. If anyone in Thornfell knows what is waking and how to survive it, it will be people the kingdom already threw away.*

"A crew of criminals. My plan was to avoid attention."

*Your plan was to die warm and unnoticed. Plans change.* And there it was again, that thread of humor, worn soft like a stone in a pocket. *Come, little taster. Walk with me a while longer. I promise the company improves.*

Zephyrine shouldered his empty satchel. Somewhere behind him, the capital was waking to find its taster gone and its king unpoisoned, and questions were being asked in rooms where questions ended careers—or lives. Somewhere ahead, a ruined bell-tower full of strangers waited, and beyond it, a door he could supposedly hear.

He took one last look down the valley, at the country that had owned him for nine years without ever seeing him.

"All right," he said. "But if your smugglers turn out to be worse than the court, I'm filing a complaint with whatever passes for heaven."

*Heaven,* said Vess, sounding genuinely amused for the first time, *is currently a crack in a jar lid, and I live in it. Complaints will be received in the order they are screamed.*

Zephyrine laughed again—twice in one week, a personal record—and turned east, into the snow, toward the sound of a door only he could hear.

Behind him, far below and closing fast, a second line of torches entered the valley. These moved differently. Quieter. Better.

He didn't see them. But the threshold stones did, and what the stones remember, Vess remembers—and the god said nothing at all.
