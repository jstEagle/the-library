## 5. The Company of the Broken Bell

The smuggler's road out of the capital smelled of wet wool and onions, and Zephyrine had never been happier to smell anything in his life.

Nine days he'd walked. Nine days of keeping his hood up and his tongue still, of buying bread from strangers with hands that shook only a little, of sleeping in ditches and waking to check that Vess's voice was still there — a low hum behind his teeth now, like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop ringing. The god didn't speak often. When it did, it said things like *left at the split oak* or *the water here is clean* or, once, memorably, *you are walking like a man who has never been followed, which is a lie you should stop telling your shoulders.*

"You could have led with that," Zephyrine muttered into his collar.

*I am leading with everything,* Vess said placidly. *You are simply a slow reader.*

He came down off the ridge at dusk on the ninth day, following the voice's instruction to look for a shrine-road marker that wasn't a shrine-road marker: an old boundary stone with a bell carved on its face, the clapper chiseled out by some pious vandal a generation ago. Below it, tucked into the fold of the valley where the trees grew crooked and nobody official ever bothered to look, there was firelight.

And around the firelight, voices. Arguing.

Zephyrine crouched behind the boundary stone and did what nine years at the king's table had trained him to do: he listened.

"—all I'm saying," a woman's voice was saying, rich and gravelled, the voice of someone who had shouted over storms for decades, "is that if we'd taken the coastal run like I wanted, we would not currently be eating boiled boot leather."

"We are not eating boot leather," said a second voice, younger, male, wounded in the particular way of a man whose competence has just been questioned. "We are eating *nettle stew*, which is a food."

"It's a punishment with broth."

"Captain—"

"Maret," the woman corrected. "Captain when you're right. You're not right."

A third voice joined in, dry as old paper: "For the record, I warned about the checkpoint at Marrow Ford. I stood in the rain and I said, Maret, the ford will be watched, and you said, Osk, no one watches fords for a crew of four nobodies, and then—"

"Yes, thank you, Osk."

"—we were watched at the ford."

"*Thank you*, Osk."

There was a pause, and then two more voices spoke at once — identical voices, high and quick, finishing each other's sentence the way some people finish each other's bread:

"So we're agreed the stew needs salt."

"It needed salt three hours ago, Pip."

"It needed salt before it was stew, Tam."

Zephyrine closed his eyes. He had spent nine years surrounded by courtiers who spoke in silk and meant knives, and there was something so violently, restoratively honest about people who argued about salt that he nearly laughed aloud behind the stone.

Then the hum behind his teeth sharpened, and Vess said: *They have one of mine.*

"What?"

*A bell. Broken, but mine. Go down and knock.*

"Knock? On what? They're in a camp in the middle of—"

*On the air, if you must. Knock somewhere. Be polite. And do not tell them what you are until they feed you. Hungry people bargain badly, and you look like a starved cat.*

"I don't—"

*You do. Go.*

Zephyrine took a breath, stepped out from behind the stone, and knocked — absurdly, gently — on nothing at all, because it seemed rude to keep a god waiting.

Every voice around the fire stopped at once.

* * *

They had him against a tree within thirty seconds, which was, he would reflect later, impressive for a crew of four and a half nettle-based meals.

The captain was a broad woman with grey shot through her black braid and a patch over her left eye, holding a knife that looked less like a weapon than like an extension of her arm she happened to be able to detach. The priest — Zephyrine knew defrocked clergy when he saw them; the posture gave it away, spine straight even while pinning a stranger, hands that kept wanting to make blessing-shapes and being denied — held a lantern up to Zephyrine's face. The twins flanked him with the synchronized menace of cats deciding whether to play with something.

"Well?" the captain said. "Talk fast. It's dark, we're hungry, and I've buried men for less than sneaking up on my camp knocking on *air*."

"That last part wasn't—" Zephyrine stopped. "I heard you arguing about salt."

The twins exchanged a glance. "Everyone argues about salt," said one.

"It's the universal argument," said the other.

"He's not wrong," the priest murmured.

"Maret," the captain said, and something in her tone made the whole tree-line feel colder. "Name and business, or I start guessing, and my guesses involve knives."

"Zephyrine," he said. "And my business is—" He hesitated. Vess hummed, patient, amused. *Feed first,* the god reminded him. But looking at the knife, and the eye-patch, and the way the priest's lantern-hand hadn't wavered once, Zephyrine found that lying felt suddenly, exhaustingly pointless. "My business is that I'm told you're carrying something broken that belongs to a god. And I think I can help."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere down the valley a nightjar called, twice, and gave up.

Then the captain laughed — one short bark, entirely without humor. "Osk. Did you hear that? The starving cat wants to help us with the Bell."

"The starving cat also knows it's called the Bell," Osk said quietly. He stepped closer, lantern raised, and studied Zephyrine's face with the unhurried attention of a man reading scripture. "Nobody knows it's called the Bell. We call it that because we're sentimental idiots. To everyone else it's a cracked bit of bronze we can't sell and won't scrap." His eyes narrowed. "Who told you?"

"A friend," Zephyrine said.

"What friend?"

Zephyrine opened his mouth, closed it, and settled on: "A very old friend. With strong opinions about thresholds."

Osk went very still. Then, slowly, he set the lantern down on a stone, turned to the captain, and said, in the voice of a man reporting a fire: "Maret. Ask him to taste the stew."

"What?"

"Ask him to taste the stew."

Maret stared at him. "Osk, if this is some priest thing—"

"It is absolutely a priest thing. Ask him."

So Maret, muttering curses in at least two languages, fetched a bowl of the infamous nettle stew and thrust it at Zephyrine along with a spoon, as though feeding a dangerous animal. "Taste. If you die, that's your own fault."

Zephyrine tasted it. It was, objectively, terrible — bitter, oversimmered, faintly smoky from a bad cooking fire. But underneath all of that, woven through it like a thread through cloth, he tasted something else. Something that made the back of his neck prickle and the hum behind his teeth rise to meet it.

"There's ash in it," he said slowly. "From a burned shrine. You cooked near a shrine-fire, or used a pot that had. There's—" he searched for the word and found the true one, the one he'd sworn off using since the lightning-poison, "—there's divinity in it. Old. Faded. Like a scent left in a coat. It won't hurt anyone. It's barely anything. But it's there."

He looked up. Four faces stared at him. The twins' mouths were open in perfect unison.

"How," Maret said, "does a starving cat taste *divinity*?"

"I'm a food-taster," Zephyrine said. "Was. For the crown. Nine years." He set down the bowl. "You learn to notice things."

"Food-tasters don't taste gods," said one twin.

"They really don't," said the other.

"This one does," Osk said softly. He crouched in front of Zephyrine, and up close Zephyrine could see the faded brand on the inside of his wrist — the sun-and-key of the silenced order, scored through with the official line of defrockment. "Friend with strong opinions about thresholds. Say it again."

"Vess," Zephyrine said, and the name came out of him like warmth after cold, and every person at that fire flinched — even Maret, even the twins — the way people flinch at a name they'd been taught was too holy to say and too dangerous to forget.

Osk sat back on his heels. "Well," he said. "That settles the starving-cat question."

* * *

They fed him properly after that — actual bread, hard cheese, a strip of dried fish apiece, hoarded portions split five ways without discussion, which told Zephyrine more about these people than any interrogation could have. He sat close to the fire because they made him, Maret saying, "Cold tasters make mistakes," in a tone that permitted no argument, and the twins sat on either side of him like bookends, staring.

"You survived the lightning dish," one said.

"At the palace. In spring. Everyone says the taster died."

"He didn't die," the other said. "We checked. Pip wrote to his cousin in the kitchens."

"Pip's cousin is unreliable."

"Pip's cousin is *thorough*, Tam."

"I'm Pip," the first twin said to Zephyrine, pointing at himself.

"He's Tam," the second said, pointing at Pip.

"They always do that," Maret said. "Don't trust either of them."

Zephyrine, who had spent nine years being invisible in plain sight among people whose kindnesses were all currency, felt something in his chest loosen that he hadn't known was clenched. He ate his fish. He let them stare.

It was Osk who finally brought it out — the Bell. He unwrapped it from oilcloth with the care of a man handling an infant, and set it in the firelight between them all.

It was small, no bigger than a fist. Bronze gone green-black with age, cast in the shape of a temple bell but worn smooth, as if generations of hands had held it for luck. A crack ran from crown to lip, thin as a hair, and when the firelight caught it, Zephyrine's breath caught with it — because through the crack he could see, or almost see, or *almost remember*, something vast and quiet moving behind the world like a whale under ice.

"It was the shrine-bell of Hollowmere," Osk said. "Before the Silencing. My grandmother rang it. Her grandmother rang it. When the edicts came, the last bell-keeper broke it rather than let the wardens take it — a broken bell can't ring, and what can't ring can't be melted down for their cannons." His jaw tightened. "She was wrong, of course. They took everything else anyway. But this stayed hidden. Passed hand to hand for a generation. Smugglers carry it now, because smugglers are the only ones who move freely enough, and because—" he glanced at Maret, "—well. Sentimental idiots."

"What does it do?" Zephyrine asked. "Broken?"

"It listens," Osk said. "Or it did. A whole bell calls. A cracked bell only *listens* — catches the echoes of whatever the gods say to each other, when they bother to say anything at all. Which, for twenty years, was nothing. Silence. We carried a dead relic and told ourselves it was faith." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and fierce. "Until three months ago. When it started humming."

Zephyrine's skin prickled. Behind his teeth, Vess hummed too, in harmony.

"Humming how?" he asked.

"Like this." Osk touched the crack, and the Bell sang — a thread-thin note, wavering, aching, the sound of a thing trying to remember its own voice. And Zephyrine knew the note. He had heard it every night for nine days, behind his teeth, in his dreams, in the space between heartbeats.

"It's listening to *him*," Pip whispered.

"To both of them," Tam whispered.

Maret leaned forward, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow, her one eye fixed on Zephyrine with the flat assessing weight of a woman pricing cargo. "Here's my thinking," she said. "Three weeks ago, my crew got hired to move a crate for a client who paid too well. I know better than to trust clients who pay too well, but winter was coming and I'm a fool with a ledger. The crate was warden supply. Sealed, official, heavy. And when we cracked it — because of course we cracked it, we're criminals — every item inside was soaked through with something wrong. Food that tasted like storms. Wine that tasted like grief. Dried meat that made Osk here fall to his knees praying, and Osk hasn't prayed since they took his collar." She tapped the Bell. "This started screaming that night. Quiet-like. But screaming."

"Divine poison," Zephyrine said quietly. "Concentrated. Bottled."

"That's our guess. Question is *why*. Why is the crown bottling gods, and where's it going, and what happens to whoever drinks it?" Maret spread her hands. "We need a taster. Not a poison-taster — the crown's full of those, and they'd all be dead or mad by the second sip. We need a *god*-taster. Someone who can tell us what's in a crate before it kills us, or worse." Her eye narrowed. "And then a starving cat walks out of the dark knowing the Bell's name and tasting shrines in my stew. So. Zephyrine. What's your price?"

And there it was. The transaction. Zephyrine had known it was coming — everything among desperate people was transactional, he'd learned that young, learned it in the taster's quarters where survival was traded in sips — and yet some foolish part of him, the part warmed by shared fish and arguments about salt, had hoped—

Vess stirred, gentle. *Answer honestly. These ones can smell a lie like smoke.*

"No price," Zephyrine said.

Maret's eyebrows went up. The twins said "No price?" in unison, scandalized.

"I'm running from the crown," Zephyrine said. "They'll figure out soon enough that their poisoned crate and their vanished taster are the same story, and when they do, they'll hunt me harder than any smuggler they've ever chased. I need a road only criminals know, and a crew that knows how to disappear." He looked around the fire — at the captain with her knife, the priest with his broken god, the twins with their matched grins. "You need a taster. I need smugglers. That's the trade. That's all the trade."

Maret was silent a long moment. Then she snorted. "Humble. I hate humble." She spat into the fire, thought, and stuck out her hand. "Crew shares are equal thirds split six ways, you get a quarter-share till you've proven you won't get us killed, complaints go to Pip and Tam who will ignore them, and if you ever taste something in my food and don't tell me immediately, I'll feed you to the river. Deal?"

Zephyrine took her hand. Her grip was like a door slamming warmly shut. "Deal."

"Welcome to the Company of the Broken Bell," Osk said, and there was a smile under his beard, the first Zephyrine had seen from him. "We named it ourselves. Referenced above: sentimental idiots."

"It's a good name," Zephyrine said.

"It's a *terrible* name," said Tam.

"It's going to get us hanged," said Pip.

"It's already gotten us *nearly* hanged twice," said Tam.

"And yet," said Pip.

And yet. Zephyrine sat back, fire-warmed for the first time in nine days, and let himself — carefully, the way he'd learned to swallow poison, one small dose at a time — let himself feel something like belonging.

He was asleep within the hour, full and safe and surrounded by strangers, and it was the best sleep he'd had in nine years.

* * *

He woke before dawn to the Bell singing.

Not humming — *singing*, the cracked note rising sharp and urgent, and Osk was already up, holding it in both hands, his face grey in the pre-light. Maret stood beside him, knife drawn for no enemy she could name. The twins clung to each other awake, which Zephyrine suspected they never did.

"What is it?" Zephyrine breathed. "What's it saying?"

Osk didn't answer. He tilted the Bell toward Zephyrine, toward the hum behind his teeth, and Vess rose up through him like water finding its level, and for one clear moment Zephyrine heard what the Bell heard:

A voice, far away, thin with distance and terror, calling across the dark of the country.

Calling for help.

"It's another god," Osk whispered. "It's a god calling from the capital. And Zeph—" He swallowed. "It says the wardens know about you. It says they're already on the road."
