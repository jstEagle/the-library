## 6. What the King Ate

The trouble with being the second child of a king, Isolde had long ago decided, was that nobody bothered to lie to you well.

Corvin got the real secrets. Corvin got the war councils and the treasury briefings and the door keys that actually opened things. Isolde got embroidery tutors and a smile and the distinct impression of being a decorative object that had somehow learned to walk. For nineteen years she had made her peace with this. She embroidered. She smiled. She walked.

Then she found her father burning maps in his private study at three o'clock in the morning, and her peace went out the window like a startled cat.

She hadn't meant to see it. She'd been awake because of the storm — Thornfell storms came off the mountains like an invading army, all thunder and no apology — and she'd gone down to the kitchens for warm milk the way she did on bad nights, and she'd seen light under the study door where there should have been none. She'd expected to catch him at correspondence. Kings, she assumed, were merely insomniacs with better stationery.

Instead he stood at the hearth feeding papers into the fire one at a time, unhurried, almost tender about it, the way a man feeds a beloved dog. And when a page curled and caught, the light through it showed her lines. Roads. Rivers. A coastline she had never once seen on any map in any schoolroom in the palace, though she had memorized every map in every schoolroom in the palace by the age of twelve, mostly out of spite.

Her father watched the coastline burn and said, very quietly, to no one: "Not yet. Not yet."

Isolde took her milk back upstairs untasted and lay awake doing arithmetic. Her father was sixty-one years old. He had been king for thirty-four of those years. In thirty-four years, she had never heard him say *not yet* about anything except dessert.

* * *

By morning she had constructed a plan, which was generous word for what it was. It was more of a decision wearing a plan's coat: she would go to the archivist, Master Ellery, and she would ask questions until someone told her the truth or arrested her, and either outcome would be more interesting than embroidery.

Master Ellery kept the deep archive beneath the east wing, down stairs that smelled of cold stone and older paper. He was a small man, round as a wine cask, with ink permanently staining the first knuckle of each hand like a disease he'd caught from his profession and refused to cure. He had taught Isolde her letters. He was also, she suspected, the only person in the palace who liked her for herself rather than for her proximity to a crown, which made betraying him awkward and trusting him inevitable.

"Your Highness." He bowed over the ledger he was carrying, which was the size of a paving stone. "To what do I owe—"

"I need everything you have," she said, "on the Veil."

The ledger did not drop, exactly. It descended with dignity, the way a felled tree descends. Ellery stared at her for a long moment, and Isolde watched something move behind his eyes — fear first, then calculation, then, worst of all, recognition. As if he had been waiting nineteen years for this exact question and had hoped, privately, it would never come.

"The Veil," he repeated.

"Don't do the thing," Isolde said. "The thing where you say 'I'm sure I don't know what you mean' while looking at the door to make sure no one's behind me. You're already looking at the door. I can see you looking at the door."

Ellery looked at the door. Then he looked back at her, and sighed the sigh of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. "Come inside," he said. "And help me carry the ledger. My spine is eighty years old even if the rest of me is only sixty."

* * *

What he showed her filled four hours and emptied her of nearly everything she believed about her own name.

There had been a country before Thornfell. That much the oldest records admitted in careful, hedging language, the way people write when they're afraid of their own pens. There had been gods — not the sanctioned, domesticated saints the official liturgy allowed, but gods, plural, wild, numerous, *loud* — and then there had been a war, or something shaped like a war, and then there had been a Veil drawn around the land like a bandage around a wound, and nothing in and nothing out, forever.

"And the royal family?" Isolde asked. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. Smaller.

"Wardens," Ellery said simply. "Keepers of the seal. The first king did not conquer Thornfell, Highness. He built its walls. All of them. Every border, every mountain pass that leads nowhere, every road that loops back on itself — those aren't geography. They're stitching." He turned a page toward her: a diagram, old and beautiful and terrible, showing the kingdom as a jar, and within the jar, pressed against the glass, shapes. "The gods didn't die in the war. They were *bottled*. And the wardenship is fed."

"Fed how?"

Ellery was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he did not look at her. "Power, Highness. Long life. The strength to hold a seal for generations without rest. It has to come from somewhere. Nothing is free — least of all captivity."

Isolde thought of her father. Sixty-one years old and straight-backed, sharp-eyed, still riding at the head of the hunt while other kings his age were being carried. She thought of her grandfather, who had lived to ninety. Her great-grandfather, ninety-three. A dynasty of men who simply refused, decade after decade, to do the decent thing and die.

"Oh," she said. It was a very small word for the size of the hole opening under her.

"There's more," Ellery said gently, "and it's worse, and I think you should sit down for it, but I suspect you won't, so I'll just tell you. Your father keeps a private ledger. Red binding, brass corners, kept in the locked drawer of his study desk. If you're going to do whatever it is you're clearly going to do, you'll want to see it yourself. I've only read fragments, and a fragment of a thing like this is more dangerous than ignorance. It lets you stop halfway."

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You could lose your post. Your head."

Ellery smiled, and for a moment the round little archivist looked ancient, older than the archive, older maybe than the lie. "Because I have spent forty years cataloguing a prison, Highness, and calling it a library. Because someone in your family has to ask. And because—" he hesitated, then finished, quieter, "—because your grandmother asked me the same question you just asked, thirty years ago, and I was a coward, and she died not knowing. I find I mind that more than I expected to."

* * *

Getting into her father's study was insultingly easy, which was itself a lesson. The lock on the great oak door was ceremonial; the real lock was on the desk drawer, and that yielded to the hairpin Isolde had been using on the pantry since she was nine, because the pantry held the candied plums and her father had never once imagined anyone would steal from *him*, and that was the whole of the man in a single sentence, wasn't it. He could not conceive of theft because he could not conceive of anything outside his ownership.

The drawer contained the ledger. Red binding, brass corners, exactly as promised. It also contained a knife, a folded letter sealed with black wax, and a small glass vial of something that moved when she looked away from it. She left the knife. She took the ledger and, after a moment's consideration, the letter too, because a girl committing treason might as well be thorough.

She carried both to her rooms, barred her door, sat by the window where the storm-light was best, and opened the ledger.

It was written in her father's hand. That was the worst part, at the start. She knew that hand. It had written her birthday letters. *My dearest Isolde* — the same looping capitals, the same confident downstrokes, now recording:

*Third month, ninth day. Attempt upon my life at the harvest feast. Poison in the wine — arsenic, crude, traceable to a merchant of Lowmarket. Survived, as designed. The seal drank well tonight. I feel ten years younger.*

She turned the page.

*Fourth month, second day. Second attempt, arranged through Corvin's channel as agreed. The taster boy swallowed most of it — unfortunate, but the boy is durable, they always are, that's why we keep them. Enough reached me. The wardenship holds.*

Isolde stopped reading. She set the ledger face-down on her knees and looked out the window at the rain for a while, because her hands were shaking and she did not want to smudge the evidence.

*As designed.* *Arranged through Corvin's channel as agreed.*

Every poisoning attempt she had ever heard whispered about — the harvest feast, the midwinter banquet, the infamous affair of the soup that had sent three servants to the stocks — every single one, staged. Her father poisoning himself, or having himself poisoned, in careful doses, and the poison going somewhere, feeding something, and the *taster* —

The taster boy. She didn't know his name. Nobody knew his name; that was rather the point of him. Nine years, Ellery's fragments had said, the crown had kept a food-taster for nine years, swallowing doses meant for the king, surviving them all, growing *durable*. Growing into what? A vessel, one fragment had said, before the word cut off. The gods needed vessels to speak through. The wardens needed — what? A lightning rod? A cup?

They were keeping a boy the way you keep a candle wick. Dry, trimmed, ready to be lit.

And Corvin knew. *As agreed.* Her brother, who had taught her to skip stones, who had once taken a whipping for her when she'd broken the chapel glass, had grown up into a man who signed off on feeding their father's immortality with staged murders and a servant's body.

She found, examining herself carefully, that she was not surprised. That was perhaps the saddest discovery of the entire night. Some part of her had known for years that Corvin wanted the throne more than he wanted anything else, and had simply declined to look directly at it, the way you decline to look at a limp.

She picked the ledger back up and read it to the end. The last entry was recent. Three weeks old.

*The seal thins. The gods stir — two voices heard at the mountain shrines, per report. We will need a greater feeding soon. Perhaps the time has come to stop pretending. Corvin urges patience. Patience is for men who intend to remain men.*

Underneath, in different ink, fresher: a list of names. Shrines. Hollowmere. The Thorn Road. Places being crossed off one by one, methodically, like a shopping list.

Like a hunting list.

* * *

She was still sitting there when the knock came — three soft raps, Corvin's knock, the one he'd used since childhood when he wanted her to sneak down to the kitchens. For one absurd moment she almost laughed. Old habits. Old ghosts.

"Isolde?" His voice through the wood, low. "Father says you missed supper. Are you ill?"

She looked at the ledger in her lap. At the letter with the black seal, unopened, in her pocket. At the rain running down the glass like the country itself trying to get out.

"I'm fine," she called back, and hated how easily the lie came, and understood suddenly that she had inherited more from her father than his eyes. "Just tired."

A pause. "All right. Sleep well." Footsteps, receding. He didn't push. He never pushed; pushing was inefficient, and Corvin had chosen efficiency the way other men chose religion.

She waited until the corridor was silent. Then she dressed practically — riding clothes, boots, hair braided tight the way Maret-the-stable-mistress had taught her years ago for foxhunting, back when foxhunting was the most dishonest thing she did. She took the ledger. She took the letter. She took the small purse of coin she'd been saving against an emergency she'd never been able to imagine, and here it was, arriving right on schedule.

At the window she paused, one leg over the sill, and looked back at her room: the embroidery frame with its half-finished lilies, the books, the pretty useless beautiful cage of it.

"Sorry," she told the lilies, and meant it, and climbed out.

The stable boy on night duty was seventeen and devoted to her in the uncomplicated way of boys and dogs, and between a silver piece and her most winning smile she had a horse saddled and unsaddled-gossip-free in ten minutes. She rode out through the east gate, past the sleeping guard who owed Corvin money and therefore looked at nothing, ever, as a matter of policy, and onto the mountain road as the storm broke apart overhead and the stars came out like witnesses.

She did not have a plan. She had a direction — north, toward the shrines on her father's list, toward whichever name hadn't been crossed off yet — and a ledger full of proof, and a fury so clean and complete it felt almost like joy.

Behind her, Thornfell slept on inside its stitched borders, dreaming its long stolen dream, and its king slept too, fed and young and patient, and its prince slept knowing everything and choosing it, and none of them — Isolde vowed, leaning low over the horse's neck as the road began to climb — none of them would sleep easy much longer.

Somewhere ahead, in the dark of the passes, a boy who tasted lightning was running too. She didn't know that yet. But the road was narrow, and the country was smaller than its maps pretended, and the gods, waking, had begun arranging meetings.

Hers was waiting at a shrine called Hollowmere.
