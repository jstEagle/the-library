## 7. Lightning in the Wine

The cache was hidden under a shepherd's hut that had burned down eleven years ago, which Maret said made it the safest cellar in Thornfell, because nobody builds on a bad memory.

"Except wardens," Osk murmured, kneeling in the ash with his palms flat against the cold stones. "They love building on bad memories. It's practically their trade."

The hut sat in a fold of the Greyhollow hills where the road forgot itself twice before reaching it—once at the river ford and once at a stand of birches that Pip swore had been oaks when she was small. Tam said she was making that up. Pip said he'd know if she were making it up, because they shared everything, including doubt. Zephyrine had stopped trying to follow these conversations by the third day.

He crouched now beside the fire ring while Maret pried up the hearthstone with a crowbar and the particular tenderness of a woman greeting an old friend. Beneath it: steps going down into dark, and out of the dark, a smell.

Zephyrine's nose had been his fortune and his curse for nine years. He could smell arsenic in a custard from across a banquet hall. He could smell hemlock ground fine enough to pass for flour. He could smell, once, in the king's own cellars, a vial of widow's-mercy so old its poison had turned sweet as overripe pear—and he had poured it out rather than report it, because some poisons deserved retirement more than discovery.

What came up those steps smelled like none of that.

It smelled like the air after a storm that hasn't happened yet.

"You feel it too," Maret said, watching him. Not asking. She'd lost her left eye years ago—to what, nobody had yet been brave or foolish enough to ask—and her remaining one was sharp as a fishhook. "Your face just did something I've only seen on men about to be sick."

"I'm not going to be sick," Zephyrine said. "I'm going to be worse than that."

* * *

The cellar was warden work through and through: clean stone, dry air, shelves of slate instead of wood, and crates stenciled with the crown's cipher—a tower inside a circle, which everyone in Thornfell was taught meant *safety* and which, Zephyrine was beginning to suspect, meant *jar*.

Maret set Pip and Tam to inventory. Osk lit no lamps but carried a stub of candle anyway, cupping it like a bird he didn't trust. And Zephyrine did what he had been brought along to do, the thing that had turned four strangers into something dangerously close to employers:

He tasted.

It was a strange little ritual by now, three weeks into this arrangement. Pip would hand him a stoppered flask or a wrapped parcel, and he'd touch the tip of his tongue to it—just the tip, the way you test a kettle—and wait. Most of the cargo was ordinary. Salted pork. Dried cherries. A cheese so hard Tam used a corner of it to wedge a shelf level. The wardens moved supplies the same as anyone; hunger was hunger, even under a crown built on a secret.

But there were other parcels. The ones wrapped in grey silk instead of canvas. The ones Pip handled with two fingers and a grimace, saying they felt *wrong*, like a door that opens onto a wall.

Zephyrine touched his tongue to the first grey-silk parcel on the fourth crate and the world went white.

Not pain. He knew pain intimately; pain and he were old colleagues who nodded to each other across crowded rooms. This was different. This was—

*A voice.*

*"There you are,"* said Vess, and the voice was not sound. It was the moment between lightning and thunder stretched wide enough to live inside.

Zephyrine's knees hit the cellar floor. He heard Pip shout his name, heard Maret bark something about not touching him, heard all of it from very far away, the way you hear rain from underwater.

*"Don't fight it,"* Vess said. *"You've been sipping me for weeks, taster. Every threshold you crossed, every doorway, every crack in the wall where the light comes through wrong—I've been leaning on you like a drunk leans on a fence post. Tonight I'd like to stand."*

"That's not standing," Zephyrine tried to say. His mouth wasn't working right. Or maybe it was working entirely too well, because what came out was not words but light, thin silver threads of it, spilling past his teeth.

*"Hold still,"* said the god of thresholds, gently, the way Brannoc used to say it before lancing a boil. *"This is going to feel like being born, and I apologize for that. Nobody enjoys it. Even gods remember."*

* * *

He saw the Veil.

That is the only word he ever found for it afterward, though he tried several, lying awake in smugglers' camps with his hands shaking around a tin cup. *Membrane* was closer. *Skin* was closer still, and uglier, and true.

Thornfell had skin.

He hung above it—or what felt like above, though there was no above, no below, no direction at all except *inward*—and looked down at his country and saw it whole for the first time. The mountains he'd fled through were wrinkles at the knuckle of a closed fist. The capital was a knot of scar tissue, dense and pale, where the Veil gathered thickest. The rivers ran in lines like old stitches. And everywhere, everywhere, the membrane stretched: a shimmering, breathing boundary between the world he knew and a vast dark outside it that was not empty. It was *full*. It pressed against the skin of Thornfell like a crowd presses against a locked door.

And the skin was thinning.

He could see it the way you can see a soap bubble beginning to fail—not torn, not yet, but gone silvery-pale in patches, stretched to translucence over the mountain passes, over Hollowmere, over a hundred places where the old shrines stood. Through those patches, light leaked. Not sunlight. A warmer, older light, patient as embers banked for a generation.

The outlawed gods. Pressing their hands flat against the inside of the sky.

*"You see it now,"* Vess said, and the god's voice came from everywhere, including, uncomfortably, from inside Zephyrine's own chest, using his heartbeat as a drum. *"A jar with a loosening lid. Your kings sealed us in nine-and-thirty years ago and called the jar a country. They fed on the seal—the pressure of us, the strain of it. It made them strong. It made them long-lived. It made them kings."*

"The poison," Zephyrine thought, or said, or was. "The lightning-taste. That was—"

*"Us. Leaking through. Everything that crosses the Veil picks up a little of the outside, and the outside has been pressing harder every year. Your tongue is the finest instrument in Thornfell, taster. Of course you tasted the truth before anyone else. You've been tasting the walls your whole life. You just didn't know the walls had a flavor."*

Zephyrine drifted along the inner curve of the Veil, helpless as a leaf in a millpond, and looked for his own life in it. There—the capital, the palace, the tasting table where he'd swallowed nine years of small deaths so a warden wouldn't have to feel them. From here it looked obscene. All those poisons, drawn off the king's food and into his body, and he'd thought himself a shield. But a shield protects. What he'd actually been was a *strainer*: catching what the king couldn't hold, keeping the jar's contents pure, keeping the pressure even.

Keeping the lid on.

"Oh," he said, in the place without directions. "Oh, I've been working for them."

*"You've been surviving,"* Vess said, and there was something almost kind in it, or as near to kind as a thing made of doorways and dawn-light could manage. *"There's a difference, and the difference matters. Listen. The patches where the Veil thins—they're not accidents. We're waking, and where we wake, the skin wears through. Hollowmere. The shrine-roads. The places people still whisper the old names. Your friend Brannoc's fever broke because a god leaned through a thin place and answered a joke of a prayer. The crown knows. The wardens have always known. And they are preparing to do what they did thirty-nine years ago, but this time—"*

The vision lurched. Zephyrine saw, for one terrible instant, the far side of the Veil: the fullness beyond, the countless presences pressed against the skin, and among them shapes that were not patient, not banked embers, not kind. Things that had been shut out so long they had forgotten how to be anything but *shut out*.

*"—this time, sealing the jar means killing everything already awake,"* Vess finished quietly. *"Every awakened god. Every vessel that carries one. Starting, I should think, with a food-taster who has recently become very loud."*

* * *

He came back to himself on the cellar floor with Pip's hand fisted in his collar and Tam holding a flask of water to his lips like they were feeding a foal.

"—breathing, he's breathing, look, his color's coming back—"

"He was *glowing*," Pip said. "Tam. Tam, he was glowing out of his *mouth*."

"I saw."

"Are we keeping him? Can we keep a man that glows?"

"He's not a goat, Pip."

"He glowed more than the goat did."

"Enough." Maret crouched over him, one eye and one absence both fixed on his face. Behind her, Osk had gone very still, candle forgotten, wax running over his knuckles unheeded. The defrocked priest was looking at Zephyrine the way a man looks at a well he'd been told was dry.

"What did you see?" Osk asked.

Zephyrine sat up slowly. His body felt borrowed and recently returned, everything slightly rearranged. His mouth tasted of ozone and, underneath it, absurdly, of dried cherries.

"A jar," he said. His voice came out scraped. "I saw Thornfell from the outside. It's—it's a jar, Osk. Sealed thirty-nine years ago. The royal family are the lid. They've been feeding on the seal." He looked down at his own hands, the taster's hands, steady for nine years in front of kings and now shaking in front of a smuggler, a priest, and twins. "And I've been helping keep it sealed. Every poison I swallowed for the crown—that was me straining the jar's contents. Keeping the pressure even. I wasn't protecting the king. I was *maintaining* him."

Silence, except for the candle guttering.

Then Tam, softly: "The maps."

Everyone looked at him. Tam never spoke first; that was Pip's office. He held up one of the old road-maps they'd bought off a dead peddler in the last town, and his hand was not steady either.

"They're redrawing themselves," he said. "I told Pip I was imagining it. Roads moving. Villages fading. I thought the ink was bad." He swallowed. "If the country's a jar, and the jar's failing—maybe the country doesn't know what shape it is anymore. Maybe it's forgetting."

"It is," Zephyrine said. The certainty arrived whole, delivered straight from the place behind his ribs where Vess now lived like a lodger who paid rent in terror. "Vess says the Veil is thinning fastest where the gods wake. Hollowmere. The shrine-roads. And the crown is going to re-seal it, and re-sealing means killing every god that's woken and every person carrying one." He met Maret's eye. "Which is me. I'm sorry. I didn't know I was cargo when you took me on."

Maret was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, rough bark, nothing amused in it.

"Cargo," she said. "Boy, I've run contraband through three kingdoms and half a war. Do you know what I've learned about cargo?" She jabbed a thumb at the crates of warden supplies, the grey silk, the salt pork, the impossible cheese. "Cargo doesn't choose. Cargo doesn't argue. Cargo doesn't glow out of its mouth and then sit up and apologize for the inconvenience." She stood, joints cracking, and offered him her hand. "You're crew. Crew gets a vote. So vote: what do we do with a cellar full of the king's secrets?"

Zephyrine took her hand and let her haul him up. Around him the cellar waited—slate shelves, stenciled towers, the smell of storm-that-hadn't-happened rising off the grey silk parcels. Somewhere above them the hills went on forgetting their own roads, and somewhere beyond the skin of the world, patient things pressed their hands against the inside of the sky and waited to be let out.

"We take everything," he said. "Especially the grey silk. If the wardens are shipping it, it matters to them. And if it matters to them—" he touched his tongue, absently, to the memory of lightning, "—then it matters to whatever's coming next."

Osk snuffed the candle at last. In the dark, his voice was very quiet, and very old.

"Then we'd best move tonight. Because I've read the old texts, taster, and there's one thing the texts agree on." A pause. "When the gods start knocking, the wardens always answer. And they don't answer politely."

Above them, faint through the ruined hearthstone, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky.

Vess, in the marrow-deep place where he now lived, said only: *"*They've noticed you, little strainer. Run.*"*
