## 8. Corvin's Hunt

The order came on vellum, in his father's own hand, and it was the handwriting that frightened Corvin most.

His father had always written like a man signing things — quick, impatient loops, the letters of someone who considered the act of writing beneath him and wanted it over with. This was different. The strokes were slow. Deliberate. Each letter stood at attention as though it had been drilled there. *Find the vessel. Put out the light before it spreads.* And at the bottom, where the royal seal should have been pressed in wax, the wax itself seemed to have been pressed too hard, the seal sunk deep, as if the hand that stamped it had needed the table's help to stand up.

Corvin read it three times by candlelight, then folded it into quarters and did not sleep.

*Vessel*, the letter said. Not assassin. Not traitor. Not even heretic, which was the word the priests used when they wanted a burning to feel official. Vessel. As if the man he was hunting were not a man at all but a jug, and the crime were not murder but spillage.

He knew who the vessel was. Everyone in the palace who mattered knew, though nobody said it above a whisper: the food-taster. Delacroix. The quiet one with the careful hands who had swallowed nine years of the crown's poisons without complaint. Corvin had passed him in corridors a hundred times and never once thought of him as anything other than furniture that happened to eat.

Now his father wanted him dead, and the order had been signed by something that wrote like a schoolmaster.

* * *

They rode out of the capital at dawn, forty soldiers and a prince, into mountains that the maps called the Spine of Thornfell and the old people, when they could be found and persuaded to talk, called something else entirely.

Sergeant Halloway rode at Corvin's right, a broad, weathered woman who had served his mother back when his mother had been alive enough to have a guard, and who was therefore one of perhaps three people in the world permitted to speak to him plainly.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I slept."

"You've got the look of a man who slept standing up in armor, which is what you do when you don't sleep." She handed him a strip of dried apple from her saddlebag without being asked. "Eat. You scowl better on a full stomach."

Corvin ate the apple and scowled. "You've read the orders."

"Everyone's read the orders. You read them aloud at muster, sir. That's generally how orders work."

"What do you think of them?"

Halloway chewed for a while, considering. "I think I've followed this king for twenty years and he's never once asked us to kill a taster. Tasters are useful. Tasters are *expensive*. You don't burn your tools because one of them got splinters." She squinted up at the peaks. "And I think these roads are wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong like a face you know that's been rearranged. There was a shrine at the fork ahead. Little one, stone, pigeons. My lads used to leave offerings there when we patrolled this stretch — half of them superstitious, all of them sensible." She nodded at the fork ahead, where the road bent between two shoulders of rock. "There's no shrine."

There wasn't. There was a foundation, a rectangle of pale stone, and pigeon droppings on the rocks above it, and nothing else. As if the shrine had been picked up and carried away, or as if everyone had agreed, all at once, to forget it had ever been built.

"Maybe it fell down," said young Lieutenant Perrin, riding up behind them with the eager, carrying voice of a man who had been promoted recently and intended to enjoy it.

"Stone doesn't fall down and take its own foundations with it," Halloway said.

"Maybe it was moved."

"By whom? To where? With what cart?" She turned back to Corvin. "Sir, I'd like it noted that I think this whole business smells like a cellar that's been shut too long."

"Noted," Corvin said. "Keep it noted quietly."

They rode on through country that grew stranger by the mile. A village appeared on no map any of them carried, and its people watched them pass with the flat, unblinking calm of deer deciding whether to run. An orchard stood in full blossom although it was weeks too early, and the blossoms were white as bone, and when the wind moved through them the sound was less like petals than like pages. Perrin made a joke about it. Nobody laughed. Even Perrin looked relieved that nobody had.

That night, camped in a hollow below a ridge, Corvin took out the second thing he had brought besides the orders: a scrap of paper, folded small, that he had taken from his father's study three nights ago, when the king had been sleeping — or whatever the king did now that passed for sleeping.

It was a fragment of a burned map. His father had fed an entire atlas to the fire in the private study, page by page, with the unhurried care of a man feeding a fire he trusted. Corvin had rescued one corner from the grate. It showed a stretch of the northern mountains, and across it, in his father's hand, in that same terrible schoolmaster's script, someone had written: *thin here.*

Thin. Like ice. Like cloth. Like the wall of a house that was no longer entirely a house.

"Prince Corvin." Halloway crouched beside him at the fire. "You want to tell me what we're actually doing?"

"We're hunting a fugitive."

"And?"

"And that's the mission."

"Sir." She said it gently, which from Halloway was more alarming than shouting. "I held you when you were six years old and threw a fit because your pony looked at you funny. I've earned one 'and.'"

Corvin turned the map fragment over in his fingers. The firelight made the ink shift, or seemed to; he decided not to decide whether it had.

"My father is afraid," he said finally. "Not angry. Afraid. Kings aren't supposed to be afraid, and mine has forgotten how to hide it. He's sending forty men into the mountains to kill a food-taster, and he sealed the orders like he was bricking up a window." He paused. "And I think whatever he's afraid of, I'm afraid of it too. Because I've seen him eat breakfast every morning of my life, and last week I watched him hold a cup of wine and not drink it, just hold it, for the length of an entire conversation, staring at it like it might be listening. My father loves wine. He *lectures* about wine. He's never once in my life left a cup untouched."

Halloway was quiet for a long moment. "What are you saying he is?"

"I'm saying I don't know. I'm saying I'm hoping a food-taster in the mountains can tell me." He almost smiled. "Which is a sentence I never expected to say."

* * *

They found the ambush site on the fourth day, and it was already over.

The shrine-road narrowed where it crossed a stream — a natural choke point, exactly where a competent officer would set an ambush, and Corvin had been planning it since the fork with the missing shrine: block the far side, sweep the slopes, take the vessel alive if possible. His father's orders said kill. Corvin had decided, somewhere around the blossom-orchard, that alive was a better first draft, and that he would deal with the discrepancy later, or possibly never.

But when they came around the bend, the ambush had already happened. To someone else.

There were signs of a fight — churned mud, a dropped pack, a wheel-rut where something heavy had been hauled away at speed. And there were soldiers' bodies. Crown soldiers. From another column. Corvin recognized the livery on the nearest one, and then he recognized the man, or half of him: Captain Verel, who had ridden out of the capital two days ahead of them with a second company, because his father — because *it* — had sent two columns, and told neither about the other.

Verel's sword was still sheathed. That was the detail that sat in Corvin's stomach like a swallowed stone. Whatever had killed him, he hadn't died fighting men.

"Perrin," Corvin said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Count them."

"Eleven, sir." Perrin had gone pale under his freckles. "Whole company was twenty."

"Tracks?"

"Gone, sir. Rained in the night, and—" Perrin stopped. Frowned. Crouched. "Sir, the tracks go *toward* the ridge. All of them. Ours won't be hard to follow, but theirs just... end. Like they walked up the rock and the rock took them."

Halloway stood over one of the bodies, arms crossed, and did not touch it. "Sir," she said quietly, "there's no blood on the ground. Eleven men, no blood. And look at their faces."

Corvin looked. He wished he hadn't. They weren't faces of terror. They were faces of *attention* — mouths slightly open, eyes wide and fixed upward, as if each man had been interrupted mid-thought by something so interesting that dying had seemed a reasonable price for continuing to watch.

"They saw something," Corvin said.

"They saw something," Halloway agreed, "and then they stopped being men who see things."

Perrin had retreated several prudent steps and was holding his sword anyway, which Corvin understood; a sword in the hand was a kind of prayer when you had no god to pray to. "Sir, should we — follow? Up the ridge?"

Corvin looked at the ridge. The morning mist lay along it in a long, unbroken line, like a seam. Like the place where two pieces of something had been sewn together and the stitching was showing.

Every instinct he had — the instincts of a soldier, of a prince, of a son who still, against all evidence, loved his father — said *ride home*. Report the column lost. Let the mountains keep their secrets. Go back to the palace and watch his father very carefully at breakfast and never, ever let him see that Corvin had noticed the untouched cup.

But eleven men were dead on a shrine-road, and a food-taster was somewhere above that mist-line with answers, and his father had sent two columns to kill one unarmed man, and you did not turn around from arithmetic like that. Not if you were the kind of person who wanted to be able to look at yourself in the mornings.

"We follow," Corvin said. "Slow. Halloway, I want the column strung out — if the mountain wants to swallow us, it can work for it. Perrin, mark our path. Chalk on the stones, every fifty paces. If the road forgets us, we're going to make sure *we* don't."

"Yes, sir." Perrin hesitated. "Sir — mark the path for whom? If something takes us—"

"Then for whoever comes after," Corvin said. "My sister, probably. Isolde notices everything. It's her worst quality and someday it'll save all our lives."

They climbed. The mist took them a few yards at a time, cool and close, tasting faintly of lightning — or Corvin imagined it did, imagined it because of the reports from the capital, the poison no apothecary could name, the taste that survivors described as a storm on the tongue. Behind him, chalk marks bloomed white on gray stone, fifty paces apart, a trail of small stubborn signatures: *we were here, we were here, we were here.*

Halfway up, Halloway drew rein beside him and spoke without looking at him, which was how she delivered her most important observations.

"That taster," she said. "Delacroix. Nine years he ate poison for your father. Never complained, never asked for anything, near as anyone knows. And now the crown sends two companies of soldiers to put him down like a sick dog." She clicked her tongue to her horse. "I've hanged men I liked less for smaller reasons, sir. But I'll tell you true — I don't much like the shape of this hunt. We're not chasing a criminal. We're chasing a witness. And somebody upstairs is very keen that he stop talking."

Corvin said nothing. Above them, the mist thinned for a moment, and through it he glimpsed the ridge-top: a line of standing stones, ancient and leaning, and among them — impossibly, absurdly — a small figure in travel-stained clothes, watching them. Watching *him*. Even at that distance, Corvin would have sworn the man raised one hand. Not in threat. In something almost like greeting. Almost like *hurry up*.

Then the mist closed, and the stones were gone, and the chalk marks behind them glowed faintly white in the gray afternoon, fifty paces apart, all the way down the darkening road.

"Sir?" Perrin whispered. "Did you see—"

"I saw," Corvin said. He spurred his horse forward. "Come on. Before it changes its mind about letting us up."
