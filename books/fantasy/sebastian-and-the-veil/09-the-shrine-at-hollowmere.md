## 9. The Shrine at Hollowmere

The disguise had cost Isolde three coppers, a ribbon, and most of her dignity.

She had traded the ribbon for a hooded traveling cloak from a peddler woman who looked her up and down with the frank appraisal of someone who had seen every kind of lie walk past on two legs, and who had decided—correctly—that this particular lie was harmless. The coppers had bought a bowl of stew and directions to Hollowmere. The dignity was gone by the time she realized that walking like a princess was not something one could simply stop doing. Her spine had been trained into royalty by twenty years of tutors with sticks and patience, and it refused to slouch no matter how she coaxed it.

"You walk like you're balancing a book on your head," said a goat.

Not literally, of course. But the old woman driving the cart Isolde had shared for the last two days of the journey had said exactly that, in exactly those words, while chewing a stem of some weed Isolde chose not to identify. "My granddaughter walks like that," the woman had added. "She's studying to be an advocate. You'll be wanting to lose that before anyone looks at you twice."

So Isolde practiced slouching against the cart's rail, badly, while the hills rolled gold and brown around them and the air grew thinner and smelled of pine and cold stone. She thought about her father's face as he fed the maps to the fire. She thought about the ledger hidden in the lining of her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, its columns of staged poisonings marching across the pages like soldiers. She thought about Corvin's expression when she'd asked him—only once, carefully—if he knew what their father did in the nights.

He had looked at her the way one looks at a child who has wandered into a room where adults are speaking.

*You don't want to know,* his eyes had said. *And I wish I didn't.*

She wanted to know. That was the whole trouble with her. It always had been. When she was six she had taken apart the music box their grandmother gave her because she needed to see what made the song, and she had never gotten it back together again, and her father had laughed and said, *There's my girl,* and she had understood even then that he meant: *there's the one who opens things.* She wondered now whether he'd known, even then, what she would eventually try to open.

Hollowmere announced itself first by smell—woodsmoke and wet stone and, underneath it, something sweeter, like honey left too long in the sun—and then by sound: water, a great deal of it, moving slowly somewhere out of sight. The road bent around a shoulder of granite and there it was.

Isolde had expected ruin. Everything she'd read in the archivist's stolen notes suggested the shrines had been broken a generation ago, desecrated on royal order, their stones pulled down and their names scraped from record. What she saw instead was a shrine that had clearly been pulled down and then, somehow, put itself back together. Not entirely. Not neatly. The colonnade stood at angles no architect would forgive, pillars leaning like tired men against each other, and the roof was half moss and half slate in a truce neither side seemed to honor. But it stood. And the pool at its heart—the hollow mere itself, presumably—was so still and so black that the leaning pillars lay reflected in it perfectly, whole and upright, as if the water remembered how things were supposed to be and was quietly correcting the record.

A village huddled around the shrine's skirts, forty houses maybe, smoke rising from chimneys, laundry strung between eaves. Ordinary people doing ordinary things. Except that here and there, tucked into doorways and nailed above lintels, Isolde saw small objects she recognized from her father's burning maps: bells. Tiny ones, cracked ones, clay and tin and one magnificent bronze thing the size of a fist hanging over the well, split down the middle like a walnut.

Nobody rang them. They just hung there, silent, like birds with folded wings.

Isolde pulled her hood lower and walked in.

* * *

The trouble with spying, she discovered within the hour, is that it requires a talent for being unremarkable, and Isolde Delacroix had never once in her life been unremarkable at anything, including failure.

Her first attempt at blending involved sitting on the low wall by the well with a piece of bread, which she hoped conveyed *traveler resting*. A grandmotherly woman immediately brought her cheese, asked if her mother knew where she was, and told her the entire history of the village's dispute with the miller over water rights, a saga involving two lawsuits, one elopement, and a drowned pig. Isolde learned more than any spy should about the pig.

Her second attempt involved asking, casually, whether there was an inn, and was informed that Hollowmere hadn't had an inn since the toll-road closed, but the widow Ansa let rooms, and wasn't it funny, another traveler had come through not three days ago asking odd questions, a big fellow with a priest's collar and no parish, very polite, ate like a horse.

Isolde went very still. "A priest?"

"Defrocked, if you ask me," the widow's informant—a boy of perhaps ten, who had appointed himself the village's chief intelligence officer—said darkly. "But polite. He had a bell." The boy lowered his voice to a register of tremendous significance. "A *cracked* one."

The cracked bell. From the notes. The symbol of the silenced gods, carried by those who still dared to remember them. Which meant—

Which meant the people her father wanted were here, or had been, and Isolde had stumbled into the middle of the story she'd been reading sideways for a month. She felt the peculiar vertigo of a scholar whose book has suddenly reached up and grabbed her sleeve.

She spent the afternoon circling the shrine, cataloguing. There: fresh candle stubs in niches that official doctrine said had been empty for thirty years. There: offerings of bread and honey on the pool's rim, arranged with the shy precision of people relearning a forgotten etiquette. And there, carved into the base of the tallest leaning pillar, half-erased but legible in the long evening light, a word in Old Thornic that made her breath catch:

*VESSEL.*

Beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought: *the door knows its own.*

She was copying the inscription into her notebook with a stub of pencil when the light changed—not sunset, though the sun was going down, but something else, a brightening behind her, golden and wrong—and when she turned, the pool was glowing.

Just faintly. Just along its rim, a thread of light like a filament in a lamp, tracing the circle of the water as though someone had poured dawn into it. The reflections of the pillars shivered. Somewhere in the village a dog began to bark, and then, absurdly, all the little cracked bells above the doorways began to hum—softly, harmonically, a chord with no bell-ringer, a sound like a held breath finally released.

Isolde stood absolutely still, notebook forgotten, and watched a god wake up in front of her.

It lasted four heartbeats. Then the light guttered out, the bells fell silent, and the pool was only water again, and Isolde discovered she was crying without having decided to, which was mortifying, and also that she was no longer alone.

"That," said a voice behind her, "was either a miracle or a very elaborate trap, and either way you're standing in the middle of it."

* * *

There were five of them, and they had come out of the shrine's shadowed colonnade the way weather comes over a ridge—all at once and from everywhere.

The big man in front wore a defrocked priest's collar and an expression of gentle apology, which Isolde would later learn was simply his face. Behind him: a one-eyed woman with a captain's stride and a knife already loose in her hand; two young men so alike they might have been one person reflected, both grinning in a way Isolde distrusted on principle; and a slight, dark-haired young man hanging back at the edge of the group, watching her with an intensity that prickled along her skin like static.

The priest stepped forward, hands open. "You're not crown," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm not crown," Isolde said, which was true in spirit and false in letter, and she hated herself a little for the phrasing.

"She's lying," said the one-eyed woman cheerfully. "Look at her boots. Nobody lies *that* well by accident."

"They're not—" Isolde glanced down. They were, admittedly, extremely good boots. "They were expensive," she admitted. "That doesn't mean—"

"It means exactly what it means," the captain said. "Maret. Captain Maret, late of several ships and no navies. And you're either a spy, a runaway noble, or the world's most overdressed pilgrim, and I've met all three, and only one of them's worth feeding." She turned to the twins. "Pip. Tam. Search her bags."

"You can't—" Isolde began.

"We really can," both twins said together, and did, with the brisk efficiency of people who had done this many times, and Isolde stood rigid with fury and humiliation as the oilcloth-wrapped ledger came out of her satchel into the firelight.

The mood changed instantly. The captain's humor dropped away like a mask coming off. The priest leaned in, read a line, and went pale. And the slight young man at the edge—Zephyrine, she would learn, though nobody offered names yet—took two steps forward, took the ledger from Pip's hands, and began, with the terrible fluency of a native reader, to turn pages.

Isolde watched him read her father's private accounting of nine years of staged poisonings, watched his face do something complicated and quiet, and thought, with the strange clarity of the doomed: *he's the taster. He's the one it happened to. He's the vessel.*

"The door knows its own," she said aloud, stupidly, because her mouth had always moved faster than her judgment.

Every head turned. Zephyrine looked up from the ledger. "What did you say?"

"The pillar. Inside. There's an inscription—Old Thornic—it says *vessel*, and under it—" She swallowed. "I think it means you. I've been following the same trail you have. From the other end. I'm not your enemy."

"Mm," said Captain Maret. "Osk, what do we do with enemies who say they're not enemies?"

"Feed them," said the priest, "and watch their hands."

"And friends?"

"Also feed them. But sleep better."

"Ransom," Maret said decisively, snapping her fingers. "Ransom's cleaner. Noble runaway, good boots, family'll pay. We take her to the border, send a letter, collect enough to refit the *Broken Bell* twice over. Everybody eats. Nobody gets stabbed. I'm voting ransom."

"You can't vote to ransom me," Isolde said. "I haven't agreed to be ransomed."

"Doesn't work that way, love."

"It genuinely doesn't work any way," said one of the twins—Tam or Pip, Isolde couldn't tell and suspected they enjoyed this—"because we're not actually kidnappers, we're smugglers, and smuggling a princess back to her own family for money is just tax collection with extra steps—"

"She's not a princess," said Zephyrine.

Everyone stopped. He was still holding the ledger, and he was looking at Isolde with an expression she couldn't parse—recognition, yes, but recognition of what? She had never seen him before in her life.

"Aren't you?" Maret asked him. "You know her?"

"No," Zephyrine said slowly. "I've never seen her. But I've *tasted* for her family for nine years, and I know the palace kitchens' accounts, and I know what the king pays for boots like that." He held her gaze. "You're Isolde. The warden's daughter."

The word *warden* landed in the silence like a stone in a well. Isolde heard it fall all the way down.

"Yes," she said. No point pretending now; the disguise had died with the boots. "Yes. And I'm the one who stole that ledger out of his study, and I'm the one who's spent a month finding out what my family is, and I am asking you—" her voice did something humiliatingly close to cracking, and she pressed on anyway— "*please*, don't ransom me. Ransom means sending me back to him. And I will burn this ledger myself before I go back to him."

The humming started again.

Soft, at first—so soft she thought she'd imagined it—but growing, spreading, the cracked bells over the doorways waking one by one, and the pool's rim kindling with that thread of impossible dawn-light. Everyone froze. Osk whispered something that might have been a prayer or a curse. And the light gathered, and bent, and pooled—not in the water this time but in the air beside Zephyrine, shaping itself into something almost like a figure, almost like a voice, and when it spoke, it spoke through him, his lips moving around words in a voice layered beneath his own like a second person standing inside his shadow:

"*She opened the pillar. She reads what was erased. The door knows its own—and its own includes the daughter who asks.*"

The light went out. Zephyrine swayed; the priest caught his elbow. And in the ringing silence afterward, Captain Maret looked at Isolde for a long moment with her one sharp eye, and Isolde braced herself for the knife or the rope or the ransom note.

"Well," Maret said at last, sheathing the knife with a click of finality. "Can't ransom someone the gods have vouched for. Bad for business." She jerked her chin toward the village. "Somebody get her some stew. And somebody find out why our friend here tastes lightning in everything lately, because I've got a bad feeling it's connected to whatever *that* was." She paused, and her eye glinted. "And somebody keep watch on the north road. Because if a princess can find us here..."

She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't need to. Isolde finished it herself, cold settling into her stomach as she accepted the bowl of stew the twins thrust at her, both of them grinning at her now as if she were a fascinating new specimen:

If she could find them—so could Corvin. And Corvin had never in his life gone anywhere without soldiers.

Far off, down the valley, so faint it might have been wind, Isolde thought she heard dogs.
