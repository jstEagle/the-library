## 10. Toll of Memory

The dream began, as most of Zephyrine's dreams had begun lately, with a door.

It stood alone in a field of grey grass that moved without wind, and it was not a grand door—no gilded arch, no carved saints. It was a cottage door, the kind you'd find on a shepherd's hut, painted a blue so faded it was nearly an apology. Someone had hung a copper bell above it. The bell had no clapper.

"You're early," said Vess.

Zephyrine turned. The god of thresholds sat on the doorstep of the impossible door with his elbows on his knees, looking—for once—not like lightning wearing a man's shape, but like something smaller. A young man in a traveler's coat gone soft at the seams. A face you'd forget on purpose, the way you forget the name of a village you passed through once. His eyes were the only wrong thing: they held the particular shine of a keyhole, as if you could look through him into somewhere else.

"Early for what?" Zephyrine asked. His voice in the dream felt borrowed, slightly too large for his mouth.

"For the conversation we're going to have to have." Vess patted the step beside him. "Sit. It's easier sitting down."

"I've died in dreams before," Zephyrine said. "I know how they go. You sit down, and then everything gets worse."

"Yes," Vess agreed pleasantly. "That's more or less the shape of it."

Zephyrine sat. The grey grass whispered against itself, though there was no wind, and he understood suddenly that it wasn't grass at all but ash, or perhaps names, sifting down from some height he couldn't see. The door behind them did not open. It didn't need to. He could feel what was on the other side of it the way you feel weather through a wall—a vastness, patient, green.

"Tell me," he said.

Vess picked up a pebble from the doorstep and turned it over in his fingers. "You remember the first poison you ever tasted? Professionally, I mean."

"Bitterleaf and arsenic, in a plum tart. I was nineteen. I threw up in a hedgerow and told the steward it was excellent."

"And the last one?"

"The lightning one." Zephyrine's mouth still remembered it, that ozone tang, the taste of a sky about to make a decision. "That was you, wasn't it. In the wine."

"That was me *waking*," Vess said. "There's a difference. A vessel is a door too, Zeph. You've been opening by degrees for nine years. Every poison you swallowed made the wood thinner." He set the pebble down with great care, the way a man lays down a card he knows will win the trick. "So here is the conversation. The Veil-door can be opened. Not forced, not burned, not prayed open—it must be *paid* open. And the toll is memory."

Zephyrine waited for the rest of the sentence, the part where it got better. It didn't come.

"All of it?" he asked finally.

"All of it. Everything you are. Your mother's hands kneading bread. The smell of the capital in autumn. Brannoc teaching you to pick a lock with a fishhook. The way Maret says *aye* when she means *absolutely not*. The way Osk prays when he thinks no one's listening, which is to say constantly, and badly." Vess's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "A life, paid out whole. The door doesn't take pieces. Pieces are what got Thornfell into this mess in the first place."

"And the person who pays?"

"Wakes on the far side as a stranger. Alive. Healthy. Free." Vess looked at him then, straight on, and the keyhole-shine of his eyes seemed almost apologetic. "They simply won't know why any of it mattered. Won't know who any of you were. You'd walk out of a country that has stopped existing, into a country that has never heard of you, carrying nothing at all."

The grey ash-names sifted down. Somewhere far off—or perhaps very near; distance worked strangely here—a bell rang without a clapper.

"There's more," Zephyrine said. It wasn't a question. Gods never gave bad news in one installment.

"The door opens from inside," Vess said. "Only from inside. Which means someone must stay at the threshold while it opens—to hold it, to keep it honest—and everyone else goes through first." He paused. "And the country doesn't survive the opening, Zeph. That's not cruelty. That's architecture. Thornfell *is* the prison. When the lock turns, the prison goes with it. Everyone who wants to leave must leave before the last stone forgets it was ever a stone."

Zephyrine sat with that. In the dream, sitting with things was all there was to do; there was no tea to make, no dishes to taste, no small useful work for his hands. He hated it.

"How long?" he asked.

"Days. Perhaps a week. The land is already forgetting itself—you've seen the maps."

Pip and Tam's maps. Roads that redrew themselves overnight, villages that appeared on no ledger, a ferryman two towns over who had insisted, weeping, that his hometown had a name, he was sure it had a name, couldn't anyone tell him its name?

"And if nobody pays the toll?"

"Then the door stays shut, the king re-seals the Veil, and every god still waking is put back in the dark. And every vessel." Vess smiled, thin as a knife laid flat. "Which is you. So. Those are the options. I'd recommend thinking quickly, but I've watched your kind think for nine thousand years and rushing has never once improved the outcome."

*Your kind.* Zephyrine looked at him. "You say that like you're not one of us anymore."

Something crossed Vess's face—quick, buried, true. "I've been a threshold a long time," he said. "Thresholds don't get to be people. They just get to be stood in." He stood, brushing off his coat, and the dream shivered around him like water around a dropped stone. "Wake up. Tell them. All of it. Don't soften it—they'll know if you soften it, and they'll never forgive you for the softening, even if they forgive you for the news."

* * *

He woke in the loft of the Wayhouse at Hollowmere with Pip's elbow six inches from his nose and Tam snoring below like a landslide rehearsing. For a moment he lay still and let the ordinary world reassemble itself: the smell of tallow and old timber, Maret's one eye already open across the loft because Maret slept the way cats do, in shifts and suspiciously. Rain on the roof. Osk murmuring prayers into his collar by the banked fire, badly, as always.

"Maret," Zephyrine said.

"Aye," she said, which meant *absolutely not*, which meant she'd already read his face and knew.

"We need to talk. All of us. Now, before—" He stopped. His mouth wanted to soften it. He made himself not. "Before I lose my nerve."

She was up in three motions, silent as a debt collector, kicking the twins awake with a tenderness that consisted entirely of accuracy.

They gathered in the common room below, where the innkeeper had long since given up pretending not to know what kind of guests these were, and had left a jug of small beer and a plate of yesterday's bread as a sort of diplomatic offering. Osk lit a stub of candle though dawn was greying the windows, because Osk believed holy conversations required light, even fake light.

Zephyrine told them. All of it, in order, without softening: the door, the toll, the whole price of memory, the country that would stop existing, the stranger who would wake on the far side holding nothing.

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight and grain, like good wool.

"Well," said Tam.

"Don't," said Pip.

"I haven't said anything!"

"You've said *well*, and when you say *well* in that voice, the next thing is arithmetic, and I will not sit through arithmetic at dawn."

"It's just—" Tam spread his hands. "One memory-toll. One person pays it, right? That's the exchange rate. So the question is purely logistical. Who's the least valuable—"

"*Tam.*"

"—in terms of, you know, *memories*. Pip, honestly, between the two of us, mine are mostly yours anyway—"

"That's the worst thing you've ever said, and I watched you eat a candle in Marrowford."

"It was beeswax! It was practically food!"

"Enough." Maret hadn't raised her voice. She never had to; her voice came pre-raised, like a blade that arrived already drawn. She looked around the table, and her single eye rested on each of them in turn, and each of them, Zephyrine noticed, flinched slightly under it, even Osk, even the twins, even—though he'd have denied it—Zephyrine himself.

"Nobody," Maret said, "is paying anything. We didn't come all this way to feed one of ours to a door."

"It's not being fed," Zephyrine said quietly. "It's a choice. That's rather the point. Vess was clear—the door won't take what isn't given."

"Then the door can stay shut."

"And the king wins. And every god goes back in the dark, and every vessel—" He let that hang. "Maret. You've seen what he does to vessels. You pulled what was left of the girl in Coldharbour out of the canal yourself."

Maret's jaw worked. "I know what he does. I also know what a family looks like, and a family doesn't draw lots."

"Nobody said lots," said Tam.

"Everyone's thinking lots," said Pip. "Even Osk is thinking lots. Look at him. He's doing penance-lots. He's decided it should be him and he's already writing the sermon."

Osk, who had indeed been staring into the candle with the particular radiance of a man composing his own martyrdom, started violently. "I have *not*—"

"You have. You've got the face. The face you had when you volunteered to hold the rear at the cache."

"That was tactics—"

"That was the face, Osk."

Zephyrine let them go on. It was strange, he thought, how love sounded exactly like bickering when you listened to it closely enough. Nine years in the palace he had eaten at tables where no one argued, because arguing meant caring enough to risk offense, and no one at the king's table cared about anything but surviving the next course. This—Tam accusing Pip of hoarding the good memories, Pip threatening to pay the toll specifically so she'd never have to hear another of Tam's plans, Maret glaring at all of them like a lighthouse that had opinions—this was the richest table he'd ever sat at, and the food was yesterday's bread.

"It should be me," he said.

The bickering stopped the way a dropped glass stops.

"Because I'm the vessel," he went on, before anyone could mount the barricades. "Because my memories are already half the toll—I've spent nine years swallowing poison, and Vess says the poisons thinned the door in me. I'm the closest thing to a hinge this company owns. And because—" He looked down at his hands. Taster's hands. Hands that had kept a murderer fed and alive for nearly a decade. "Because I've done the arithmetic too, Tam. I counted. What I'd be losing. And do you know what I found?"

No one answered.

"I don't lose anything I haven't already lost. My mother's dead. Brannoc's dead. The capital was never mine—I was furniture there, well-fed furniture. But you lot—" His voice caught, and he was furious at it, and pushed on. "You lot are the first thing I've ever chosen. And if the price of you all getting out is that I walk out the other side not knowing why I'm smiling—then that's not a loss. That's a trade. I've made worse trades for less."

The silence now was different. Warmer and more dangerous.

"No," said five voices at once, in five different keys.

"See, this is the problem," Maret said, leaning forward, planting both scarred fists on the table. "Everyone in this room has volunteered. Did you notice that? Twins want to split it between them like a bill. Osk's been volunteering since before you finished talking. Even Isolde—" she jerked her chin toward the corner, where the princess sat wrapped in a borrowed shawl, very still, very much listening, "—has that look. The royal look. The *I shall handle this* look."

"I do not have a look," said Isolde.

"You have a whole wardrobe of looks and that's the state one."

"I am simply observing," Isolde said, with dignity, "that a door requiring a warden's blood willingly given is a door designed by wardens, and I know rather more about wardens than anyone present, including the god. If my family built the lock, my family's blood may be the key. It seems only just."

"It seems only *convenient*," Maret shot back. "You've been trying to get yourself killed usefully since the day we met you. Ransom me, you said. Use me as bait, you said. I've captained smugglers twenty years, girl, I know a self-destructive aristocrat when I sail with one."

For a moment Isolde looked genuinely stung—and then, horribly, she laughed, a short unwilling laugh, and the tension in the room cracked along with it.

"So we're agreed," Pip said. "Everyone volunteers. Nobody accepts. Magnificent. We'll stand at the door arguing until the country finishes forgetting itself, and we'll all dissolve mid-sentence, and our last words will be *it should be me*."

"Not necessarily," said Osk.

Every head turned. Osk had gone pale in the candlelight, and he was looking not at any of them but at the middle distance, at something he was reading off the air.

"The toll is memory," he said slowly. "All of it. A whole life. But Vess said the door takes what's *given*. Freely. Whole." He swallowed. "What if—what if it doesn't have to be one person's whole? What if it's a whole, singular? One complete payment. Made of... shares."

The idea landed in the room like a live coal.

"Split it," Tam breathed. "Not between two people—all of us. A piece each. Small pieces. I'd give up—hmm, I'd give up my memories of turnip harvests, easily, there's nothing there—"

"It doesn't work like a subscription, Tam—"

"How do you know? Has anyone *asked*?"

Zephyrine closed his eyes and reached, the way he'd learned to reach these past weeks—down past his own pulse, to the place where Vess lived, thin as a draft under a door. *Are you listening?*

*I am always listening,* said the god, from everywhere and nowhere, from the keyhole-light behind his own eyes. *It's my entire profession.*

*Can the toll be shared?*

A pause. Long enough that Zephyrine felt the others watching him, saw—when he opened his eyes—them lean in, waiting on his face the way sailors wait on weather.

*No,* Vess said at last, and his voice was very quiet, and very sorry. *I checked. Believe me, little hinge, I checked before I ever told you. The door is warden-made. Warden-math. It wants one soul's whole worth, weighed and paid. It was built to be cruel that way. Cruelty was the point—no prisoner was ever meant to afford the exit.*

Zephyrine opened his eyes. Five faces. Six, counting his own reflection faintly in the window glass, rain-streaked and tired.

"Well?" Maret demanded.

"He says no. It has to be one person. Whole." He rubbed his eyes. "And he says—we should hurry. The maps aren't the only thing forgetting. Hollowmere's shrine-road went off Tam's chart last night. The country's coming apart faster than we thought."

"Then we move today," Maret said, and the captain-voice came down over her like armor. "East road, through the fens, hard riding. We argue about sacrifice on horseback like civilized people." She jabbed a finger around the table. "But hear me, all of you. Nobody decides anything alone. Whatever happens at that door, we decide it together, or not at all. That's the ship's law and it's the family law and I'll keelhaul the first—"

The window exploded inward.

Glass, rain, and a burning rag—the innkeeper's sign, the little painted bell, tumbling flaming onto the common room floor. From outside, up the wet street, came hoofbeats. Many. And beneath them, cutting through the rain, a sound Zephyrine knew from nine years of palace service and had hoped never to hear in the mountains:

A crown trumpet. Sounding the hunt.

Maret was moving before the second note. "Out the back. *Now.*"

* * *

They scattered into the grey dawn like cards thrown from a losing hand—twins one way with the maps, Osk hauling the villagers toward the shrine-cellars, Isolde dragging the innkeeper's children, Maret shouting the old smuggler cant that meant *scatter and rendezvous*, and Zephyrine running last, looking back once down the flooded street at the column of riders emerging from the rain.

At their head, a figure in the king's colors, unhurried, certain, with a prince's circlet dull under the stormlight.

Corvin had found them.

And the country, Zephyrine thought wildly as he ran, the country that was already forgetting its own roads—was about to learn what it forgot fastest of all.
