## 11. The Burning of Hollowmere

Zephyrine woke before dawn to the smell of bread and the sound of Osk arguing with a goose.

This was not, in itself, unusual. Hollowmere's shrine-geese had taken a collective dislike to Osk within an hour of the crew's arrival — perhaps because he'd once been a priest of the Bell and had, in a moment of weakness, told them so. Geese were famously devout. They had opinions about fallen clergy.

"You are a bird," Osk was saying, in the reasonable tone of a man losing an argument to poultry. "You have no theological standing."

The goose honked. Zephyrine decided it had won on points.

He sat up under the shrine's eaves, where he'd slept for three nights now, and looked out over Hollowmere. It was a small place — thirty households folded into a valley like coins into a purse, terraced gardens climbing the slopes, the old shrine at its heart with its cracked bell that no one rang anymore because ringing it was technically illegal and also because the bell made a sound like a spoon dropped down a well. The villagers knew what the crew was. They pretended not to, which was the local dialect of kindness.

Maret was already up, naturally. Maret treated sleep as a rumor spread by lazy people.

"Corvin crossed the Ashford bridge yesterday," she said without preamble, handing him tea. "Two hundred soldiers. He's coming this way."

"How do you know?"

"A farmer's boy sells us eggs. Farmers see everything. Armies are terrible at sneaking."

Zephyrine held the tea and felt the warmth of it fail to reach anywhere important. Two hundred soldiers did not come to a mountain valley for smugglers. Smugglers warranted twenty men and a grudge. Two hundred meant the word *vessel* had finally traveled from the palace to the prince's ear, and the prince had come to cut it out at the root.

"He's your friend's son," Maret said, watching him. She only ever called Isolde *your friend* when she wanted Zephyrine to feel the weight of something. "Will he burn it?"

"I don't know." Which was true, and useless, and the worst kind of answer. "He hesitated at the ambush on the shrine-road. He let Pip and Tam go when he didn't have to."

"People hesitate," Maret said. "Armies don't." She drained her own tea, and her one eye caught the first grey light. "Get Osk. Wake the village. We're leaving before full light or we're not leaving at all."

* * *

They were not leaving before full light.

It was nobody's fault, which was somehow worse. The villagers of Hollowmere were not cowards, but they were people with goats and grandmothers and root cellars full of a year's work, and you cannot explain to a grandmother that her turnips are less important than a theology she's been forbidden to hold for forty years. So there was arguing, and packing, and old Mother Hedwig refusing flatly to abandon her bees, and by the time the column finally started up the shepherd's path toward the high pass, the sun was fully risen and painting everything gold and forgiving, as if the day had decided to lie about itself.

Zephyrine walked at the rear with Vess murmuring under his ribs like a second heartbeat.

*They are close,* the god said. Not alarmed. Gods of thresholds took a long view of doors closing. *The prince is half a mile behind the ridge.*

"I know."

*You could speak to him. You carry my voice now. Doors open both ways.*

"He came to kill me, Vess."

*Yes.* A pause, thoughtful, ancient, oddly gentle. *And yet you keep looking back at the ridge.*

Zephyrine didn't dignify that with an answer. Behind them, Hollowmere lay in the morning haze, chimneys smoking breakfast into the sky, the shrine's cracked bell catching light. Half the villagers had refused to go — not out of loyalty to the crown, but because running felt like confession. So maybe sixty souls remained below, and every one of them had fed the crew, hidden them, lied for them.

Isolde fell into step beside him. She'd been disguised as a village girl for two weeks now, badly — her hands were too clean, her posture too much like a drawn sword — but among smugglers, bad disguise was practically a uniform.

"If Corvin burns the shrine," she said quietly, "it will be because he thinks it's mercy. Burning a thing is faster than arguing about it. He learned that from our father."

"That's not comforting, Isolde."

"It wasn't meant to be." She glanced back at the valley. "I've been thinking about writing him a letter."

"A letter."

"Explaining. That the vessel — you — isn't what father says. That the gods waking might be the best thing that's happened to Thornfell in a generation."

"And how would that letter start? *Dear Corvin, sorry I ran off with smugglers and a heretic, please don't commit arson*?" Zephyrine surprised himself; the joke came out almost warm. Almost like they were friends. Somewhere in the last two weeks, without his permission, she had become someone whose safety he counted alongside his own.

Isolde smiled faintly. "I'd workshop it."

Then the wind changed, and brought the smell of pitch and torch-oil down the ridge, and neither of them joked again.

* * *

Corvin watched Hollowmere through the gap in the rocks and hated every minute of it.

Two hundred men. He'd asked for fifty and been given two hundred, along with orders written in his father's hand: *the vessel and all who shelter it. Leave nothing standing.* His father's handwriting had always been beautiful — Corvin had envied it as a boy — and it occurred to him now, standing in the cold with fire in his fist, that he had never once seen that handwriting shake.

"Orders, sir?" Captain Drewe was thirty years a soldier and had all the imagination of a boot.

"The shrine first," Corvin heard himself say. "The bell. Then the houses. The villagers are to be driven out, not—" He stopped. Started again. "Not harmed. If any soldier harms a villager, I'll have him flogged in front of the others."

Drewe raised an eyebrow a fraction of an inch, which in Drewe was a full speech. "And the vessel, sir?"

"We'll find him." Corvin turned toward the valley, and found, annoyingly, that his sister was in his head. *He's not what father says,* she'd written, years ago, in a different argument about a different cruelty. *Nobody ever is.* He'd thought she was being soft. He was beginning to suspect she'd been being accurate.

"Sound the horns," he said. "Let them run first. I want the valley empty before the first torch lands."

It was, he would think later, the last order he gave as a man who still believed in the country giving the orders.

* * *

The horns came at midmorning, and after the horns, the fire.

Zephyrine stood on the high path and watched the smoke rise from Hollowmere and understood, with a clarity that felt like swallowing glass, exactly what he had done. He had come here. The crew had come here. The vessel the crown hunted had slept under the shrine's eaves eating stolen eggs and arguing with geese, and now the geese were scattering and the bell tower was burning and Mother Hedwig was screaming about her bees while Tam carried her bodily up the switchbacks.

"We go back," Osk said.

Everyone looked at him. Osk was the crew's conscience, which mostly meant he lost arguments, but he was already unbuckling the pack with the cracked bell-talisman inside, jaw set in the particular way of a defrocked man who had finally found a use for the vestments.

"Osk," Maret said, in the voice of a captain doing arithmetic, "there are two hundred soldiers down there."

"There are sixty villagers down there and a burning shrine and a god who lives in doorways." Osk shouldered the pack. "Someone has to open the way out. That's me. That's the whole job, Maret. It was always going to be me." He looked around at them — at Pip and Tam clinging together, at Maret's stone face, at Isolde, at Zephyrine — and grinned, sudden and crooked. "Pray for me. Apparently that works now."

"Osk—" Zephyrine started.

"Don't." Osk held up a hand. "Don't apologize yet. Apologize after, if I make it, and I'll tell you it wasn't necessary. That's the deal."

Then he went down the mountain, alone, toward the burning, and Zephyrine stood there and let him, and would spend a long time learning how heavy it was to have stood there and let him.

What Osk did next, they pieced together afterward from villagers' accounts and the testimony of two soldiers who deserted rather than describe it twice. He walked into the burning churchyard through the smoke, and he rang the cracked bell — the real one, in its tower, the illegal one — with the talisman held against it, and the sound it made was the spoon-down-a-well sound multiplied into something enormous, something that bent the flames sideways and opened, in the words of one deserter, "a road where there wasn't a road." And the villagers of Hollowmere walked out of the fire along that road, sixty souls plus four goats and one extremely indignant hive, and not one of them burned.

The soldiers tried to take the priest who had done it. Of course they did. Osk could have run the road himself — Vess swore afterward, in Zephyrine's half-dreams, that the road would have carried him — but he went back for Mother Hedwig's bees, because he was Osk, and when the soldiers closed around him he set down the talisman in the grass where the villagers could find it, and put up his hands, and said, loudly enough to be heard over the fire:

"I'm a priest of the Bell. Write that down. Get it right."

* * *

They got him to the fort at Greywatch by nightfall.

Isolde watched the prisoner wagon from the ridge until it vanished, her nails bitten to nothing, her mind moving the way it moved over her father's ledgers — fast, cold, finding the pattern. Osk would be interrogated. Osk knew names, routes, faces. Osk knew *her* face, though he'd never once said so, which she suspected was his idea of courtliness.

There was exactly one person in Thornfell with authority to pull a prisoner from Greywatch, and she had spent two weeks pretending not to be that person.

"I'm done disguising," she announced, to the campfire, to the company, to the night in general.

Maret looked up. "Meaning what, princess?"

"Meaning I'm riding to Greywatch in the morning wearing my own name, and I'm asking my brother for Osk's life, and he's going to give it to me, because Corvin is many things but he is not our father." She said it firmly, the way you say things you need to be true. "He hesitated at the ambush. He ordered no harm to villagers. There's still a man in there."

Zephyrine, sitting across the fire, met her eyes. He wanted to say: *don't.* He wanted to say: *they'll use you, they'll leash you, you'll walk into Greywatch and become collateral.* But he remembered the ambush too — the moment Corvin's crossbow had tracked Pip and then swung away, deliberately, to a tree — and what came out instead was: "Take my knife. And take Tam."

"Why Tam?" Tam asked.

"Because you're small, you're quiet, and you can forge a seal better than the chancellery does. If talking fails, I'll want options."

Tam looked deeply touched. Pip looked betrayed. "We're a matched set," Pip protested.

"For burglary, yes," Isolde said. "For treason, one of you."

* * *

Greywatch fort crouched in a pass like something the mountain had coughed up. Isolde rode to its gate with her hair uncovered and her signet ring on her right hand, and the gate guards went from bored to terrified in approximately four seconds, which she found, spitefully, restorative.

Corvin came down to the yard himself. Up close he looked worse than she remembered — grey-faced, sleepless, a man carrying two hundred soldiers and one bad order and trying to pretend the load balanced.

"Isolde." Her name in his mouth sounded like an accusation. "You've been living with smugglers and heretics."

"I've been living with *people*, Corvin. Try it sometime." She kept her chin level. "I'm here about the prisoner. The priest. Osk."

"The heretic who opened a road through fire in front of my entire command." Corvin laughed, short and disbelieving. "Do you know what father will do to me if I release him?"

"Do you know what father *is*?" Isolde stepped closer. "I've read your ledger, Corvin. The private one. Every poisoning you survived — staged. Every drop of 'assassin's venom' — a tithe. We weren't raised by a king, brother. We were raised by a warden fattening itself on a prison, and the prison is waking up, and when it does, every debt comes due at once. Releasing one priest is the smallest payment on that account you'll ever get to make."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — Corvin wavered. Isolde saw it happen, saw the boy who used to sneak her honeycakes surface through the prince like a fish rising. His hand actually lifted toward the sergeant-at-arms.

Then a horn sounded from the pass above, and a rider came down at a gallop with dispatches from the capital, and Corvin read them, and Isolde watched her brother die a little behind his eyes as he read.

"Father's coming," Corvin said quietly. "Tomorrow. With the Court Inquisitor. He'll want the priest broken and named, and the vessel found, and he'll want it done in front of witnesses." He folded the dispatch. When he looked up, the wavering was gone, sealed over like ice on a pond. "I can't give you the priest, Isolde. I can't even give you safe passage out of this pass. Go. Tonight. Before he arrives." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word — the only part of him that hadn't been sealed over. "Because I don't know what I'll be able to stop him from doing to you either."

"Corvin—"

"*Go*, Isolde."

She went. It was the second failure of the day, and it tasted worse than the first, because the first had cost a priest his freedom and this one had cost her the last comfortable lie she owned: that her brother was still, underneath everything, hers.

* * *

They rode through the dark, and Zephyrine did not sleep, and somewhere past midnight he stopped pretending otherwise and climbed to the lip of the pass alone, where the wind came through raw off the snowfields.

Vess rose in him like water finding its level.

*You blame yourself,* the god observed.

"Osk is in a cell because of me. The shrine is ash because of me. Isolde rode into Greywatch for a man she'd known a fortnight and got turned away because of the company she keeps, which is my company." Zephyrine's breath fogged. "Say something divine and consoling."

*I am a god of thresholds. I do not console. I keep count.* A pause. *But hear this, taster: you have spent nine years swallowing poison so that another could live unharmed. Today you swallowed the harder thing — you stayed on the path when love begged you to run down it, because running down it would have spent sixty lives on one brave fool's gamble. Osk chose his road. You chose not to block it. Both are devotions. Only one of them feels like guilt.*

"That's not consoling."

*No. But it's true, and truth is the only currency I carry.* The god's presence shifted, sharpened, and suddenly Zephyrine could feel it — far off, beyond Greywatch, beyond the pass — a vast attention turning toward them through the dark, slow as a glacier, hungry as winter. *Something else, then, since you insist. He is coming. Your king. Tomorrow, and not as a man. The wardenship is failing him and he is letting it eat him rather than let the prison open. Whatever you imagine tomorrow holds — imagine worse, and then act anyway.*

Zephyrine stared north, where the stars hung over Greywatch like watchfires that belonged to no army.

Behind him, footsteps: Maret, wrapped in her cloak, one eye catching starlight. She handed him the cracked talisman, warm from her pocket.

"Osk would hate us moping," she said. "So. New plan. We hit Greywatch before the king arrives, we pull Osk out, and we run for the heart of the country, where the door is." She paused. "And Zeph — whatever the god just told you about tomorrow? Don't tell Pip. Pip tells Tam. Tam panics. Tam panicking wakes the whole camp, and I am *so tired*."

Despite everything — the ash, the cell, the brother who couldn't be reached — Zephyrine laughed. It came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh.

"Greywatch," he said, testing the weight of it. "Two hundred soldiers, a fortress, and an inquisitor."

"One eye," Maret said, "and a grudge. We've fought worse odds with fewer feelings." She turned back down toward the camp. "Get some sleep, taster. Tomorrow we steal a priest."

Above them, far off in the dark, something that wore a king began to move.
