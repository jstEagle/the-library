## 12. Interrogation of a Priest

Pain, Osk had learned in his first forty years, was a poor conversationalist. It interrupted. It repeated itself. It never once asked how *you* were doing.

He hung from his wrists in a cell at Fort Greywatch and tried to treat the whole business as a bad sermon. The soldiers who worked him over were, he had to admit, diligent. Thorough. They lacked only theology.

"You keep asking me for names," Osk said, when they'd given him water and a moment to breathe. His voice came out like gravel being politely introduced to more gravel. "But you haven't told me yours. Where's your courtesy? In my old order we broke bread before we broke bones."

The interrogator — a lean man with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of someone who did accounts — didn't look up from his ledger. "Captain Vessar. I'm not here to befriend you."

"A shame. I make an excellent friend. Terrible prisoner, but excellent friend."

Vessar turned a page. "The woman with one eye. The twins. The taster. Names, Father."

"Not Father anymore. Defrocked, remember? You people were very thorough about that too." Osk shifted his weight, which sent a bright new argument up through his shoulders. He winced and kept talking, because talking was the one thing they hadn't figured out how to take. "And I'll tell you what I told the last fellow: I don't know any one-eyed women. I don't know twins. I've never met a taster in my life. I am a lonely itinerant heretic with no one in the world, which is why I talk so much."

The soldier by the door snorted. Vessar didn't.

"Your hands are burned," Vessar said mildly. "Bell-caster's burns. You poured that talisman yourself, or repaired it. That's relic-work. That's heresy-work." He finally looked up. "I don't actually need the names, you understand. I need enough to justify what comes next. So consider the names a kindness. To both of us."

Osk considered this. Then he considered the small stone window high in the wall, where evening light came down the color of weak tea, and he thought — as he had thought every hour since Hollowmere burned — of Maret swearing, of Pip and Tam arguing over whose forgery was worse, of Zephyrine standing very still with that listening look on his face, like a man hearing music no one else could.

They had gotten out because Osk had stood in the smoke and shouted psalms at soldiers until they remembered they had orders to arrest him alive. He'd counted it a fair trade at the time. He counted it differently now, hanging in the dark, but he'd count it the same way again tomorrow. That was the whole trick of devotion, he'd decided long ago. You didn't feel it. You just kept choosing it until feeling caught up.

"I had a teacher once," Osk said instead. "Old Brother Aldous. He said the gods went quiet because we got loud. All of us, all the time, wanting things." He smiled with split lips. "You want names. I want my arms back in their sockets. Neither of us is getting what we want, so let's be quiet together. It's what Brother Aldous would have done."

Vessar wrote something down. "Take him down. Give him the floor tonight. We start properly in the morning."

The soldiers lowered him without gentleness. As they dragged him toward the inner cells, past the guardroom, past a brazier where men warmed their hands, Osk heard two of them talking low — the way men talk when they think a broken priest isn't listening.

"—not just the vessel. *All* of them. Every god that's woken. The king wants them gathered—"

"Shut it. Walls have ears and this one has a priest."

Osk filed it away, the way he'd once filed away heresies: carefully, in the dark, where nothing rotted.

* * *

The floor was stone and the blanket was a formality, but Osk had slept on worse during his missionary years, and besides, pain made an excellent lullaby once you stopped arguing with it. He lay on his back and did what he always did when his body was someone else's property: he prayed.

Not the crown's prayers. Not the sanctioned, sanitized little verses they let you whisper at coronations. The old ones. The ones that named the gods by their small, true titles — Vess of the thresholds, Hallow of the hearth-embers, the Ninefold Mother of doors and debts. Prayers that had been contraband for a generation, worn smooth as river stones by mouths that weren't supposed to have them.

He was somewhere in the middle of the litany when he realized the cell was listening back.

It wasn't a voice. It was more like the way a room changes when someone enters it — a pressure, an attention. The candle flame leaned toward him though there was no draft. And beneath the ache in his shoulders, beneath everything, he felt something vast and slow turn over, like a whale passing under a rowboat.

*Defrocked,* said the attention, with something that might have been amusement. *They unmade you, and you pray anyway.*

"It's not for me," Osk whispered. "It's for them. The ones I won't name. Keep them walking. Keep them fed. If you're taking requests."

The attention settled over him like a hand. *You are a strange little door of a man.*

"I've been called worse by better."

*Listen, then, since you listen well.* The flame guttered upright. *What your captors plan is older than their king. The Veil was sealed with blood willingly given — warden's blood, freely offered, so the seal drinks loyalty and calls it law. To re-seal it now, with the gods waking, they must give it more. Much more. A god for each anchor-point. And a vessel for each god.*

Osk lay very still. "Zephyrine."

*The taster. Yes. Nine years of swallowed poison made a chalice of him. The king means to pour him out at the Veil-door, along with every other soul the gods have touched. The seal will drink them all and Thornfell will forget its own edges, and whatever remains inside will belong to the wardens forever.*

"And if the seal fails?"

*Then the country ends, and everyone inside must choose before the last door closes.* The attention withdrew a little, leaving cold behind. *You asked me to keep them walking. I can do that much. The rest is yours, strange door. Doors only work from both sides.*

The candle steadied. The presence faded like a tide going out, and Osk lay in the dark doing arithmetic he hated: the fort's garrison, the mountain passes, the days it would take word to reach the crew — if he even knew where they'd gone, which he didn't, which was rather the point of not naming them.

Somewhere north, Zephyrine was walking toward a door with a price tag on it, and the price was him.

"Well," Osk said to the ceiling. "That won't do at all."

* * *

Morning brought Vessar and the proper instruments, and Osk discovered that his body had opinions after all. He fainted twice. The second time he woke to water and vinegar and Vessar's voice, still patient, still ledger-dry.

"The vessel," Vessar said. "The taster. Where does the crew keep him?"

"He doesn't like being kept," Osk rasped. "He's very independent. You'd hate him."

"Father Osk. Formerly of the Order of the Quiet Bell." Vessar set down the instrument and, to Osk's surprise, sat on the stool across from him like a man settling in for a long winter. "I read your trial record. Did you know that? Before they gave me this posting. You refused to recant even when they offered you your parish back. Everyone else took the deal. Every single one. You were the only fool in the room."

"Someone has to be. Otherwise the word loses all meaning."

"Mm." Vessar studied him. "Here's what puzzles me. You've been defrocked nine years. Your order is dead, your god is outlawed, your bell is cracked. By any reasonable accounting, you lost. And yet you sit there looking like a man who's won something. What exactly did you win, Father?"

Osk thought about it honestly, because the question deserved honesty and because lying hurt worse than the instruments anyway.

"Tuesday evenings," he said at last. "There was a woman in my old parish, Widow Sera, who made plum brandy and cheated shamelessly at cards. My order had a rule against gambling, so we played for buttons. She died three years into the silence, and the last thing she said to me was that the gods had better come back soon, because heaven without card games sounded like a punishment." He swallowed blood. "That's what I won. Tuesday evenings. That's what your king is sealing away, Captain. Not heresy. Tuesdays."

Vessar was quiet for a long moment. Then he stood, gathered his instruments, and paused at the door.

"The garrison rotates at dusk," he said, not turning around. "The east stair is unwatched between bells. I mention it for no reason whatsoever."

He left. Osk stared at the closed door for the length of ten heartbeats.

Then he started laughing, which cost him two ribs' worth of protest, and couldn't stop for some time.

* * *

Dusk came down grey over Greywatch, and the fort settled into its evening rhythms — boots on stairs, the change of watch, the clatter of the mess hall. Osk waited in his cell, testing each joint like a man checking a fence before climbing it. Ribs: complaining but load-bearing. Wrists: ruined, functional. Legs: a theological debate he chose to win.

The lock on his cell was old iron, and old iron, he'd found, responded to faith and a hairpin in roughly equal measure. He'd palmed the hairpin from the guardroom floor two days ago, when they'd dragged him past. Patience was also devotion.

The corridor beyond was empty. The east stair, as promised, was unwatched between bells — though "unwatched" turned out to mean one boy, sixteen at most, standing at the landing with a spear he clearly hadn't been issued instructions for.

They looked at each other.

"You're the prisoner," the boy said.

"I'm frequently accused of it." Osk kept his voice easy, his hands visible, his weight ready to fail gracefully down the stairs if it came to that. "And you're the reason the east stair is 'unwatched.' They gave you a spear and a prayer and hoped the prayer would do the work."

"They said if anyone comes up, I ring the bell."

"And will you?"

The boy looked at the bell rope. Looked at Osk's burned hands, his ruined wrists, the blood on his collar. Something young and unhappy moved behind his eyes.

"My village was Hollowmere," he said.

Ah, thought Osk. There it is.

"We heard the shrine burned," the boy went on, quieter. "My mother's cousin lived there. Nobody would say what happened. The officers laugh about it when they think we can't hear." His grip shifted on the spear. "Did you know the people there? Did they get out?"

"Some did. Because a woman with one eye went back into the smoke for strangers, and a pair of twins forged evacuation papers so good the soldiers saluted them." Osk held the boy's gaze. "I can't promise you the world gets better, son. I promised that once, professionally, and it turns out I was overbilling. But I can tell you the gods are waking up. I've felt it. And things are waking up on the other side too, in that palace, and those things are why your officers laugh."

The boy's jaw worked. "If I don't ring the bell, I'm a traitor."

"If you ring it, you're a good soldier guarding a stairwell while a man dies for knowing too much. If you don't—" Osk shrugged, and paid for it. "Well. Traitor's just a word for someone whose loyalty points the other way. I've been one for nine years. The company's excellent."

A long silence. Somewhere below, the mess hall roared at some joke.

"The kitchen yard gate," the boy said finally. "The latch sticks; you have to lift while you pull. There's a goat track behind the midden heap. It goes down to the river road." He hesitated. "The captain — Vessar — he left his cloak in the guardroom. Grey wool. If anyone sees a prisoner in rags, they shoot. If they see a captain walking fast, they salute."

Osk looked at him for a long moment. "What's your name?"

"Dannel."

"Dannel. When the gods finish waking up — and they will — find a way to be somewhere they can see you. You've earned an introduction." He limped past the boy, then stopped. "And Dannel? The bell rope. Give it a tug in about an hour. Say you thought you saw movement on the ridge. Send them chasing shadows while I chase goats."

The almost-smile the boy managed was the first thing in four days that hadn't hurt.

* * *

The cloak fit badly and smelled of another man's pipe smoke, and Osk had never been grateful for anything more. He went down the east stair with his head up and his stride purposeful, the way authority walks, and two soldiers in the passage actually nodded to him. One said, "Evening, Captain." Osk said, "Carry on," in his best disapproving-clergy voice, and nearly wept at how well it worked.

The kitchen yard gate stuck, exactly as promised. Lift while pulling. He whispered an apology to the gate — habit, now, and getting less foolish by the day — and felt, faintly, amusedly, the gate forgive him.

The goat track was less a track than a suggestion the mountain had once made. Osk descended it by moonlight, half falling, wholly praying, ribs announcing every step. Behind him, right on schedule, a bell began ringing in Greywatch, and torches bloomed along the walls, and voices shouted about movement on the ridge — away from him, toward nothing.

At the bottom, where the track met the river road, he stopped and leaned against a milestone and breathed, and let himself feel all of it at once: the pain, the fear, the ridiculous gratitude. Above the fort, the stars were coming out thick over the mountains, and one constellation — the old one, the Threshold-Keeper, the one they'd renamed and redrawn on every official map — seemed to hang lower than it should, brighter, attentive.

*North,* said the night, in the voice of a god who was mostly patience. *They went north. Follow the river to the fork, then the old shrine-road. Walk, strange door. You have Tuesdays to save.*

Osk pushed off the milestone, adjusted a dead man's cloak, and started north along the river, limping and laughing softly to himself.

Behind him, Fort Greywatch burned torchlight against the dark, hunting a shadow on an empty ridge. Ahead, the mountains waited, and somewhere in them a taster was walking toward a door with a terrible price on it — and a crew of fools who had no idea yet what was coming for them.

"Hold on, you impossible people," Osk told the night. "I'm coming. And I'm bringing extremely bad news."
