## 13. The Map That Eats Itself

*Pip*

Pip had stolen her first map at seven years old, off a corpse that didn't need it anymore, and she had loved maps ever since in the way you love anything that tells you the world is bigger than the room you're stuck in. Maps were promises. *Here is a road. Here is a river. Here is somewhere else.* A map was a lie you could hold in your hands and trust anyway.

So when she unrolled the old surveyor's chart across the table in the Hollowmere waystation and found that the village of Ashenford had moved three miles west overnight, she did what any sensible forger would do.

She blamed Tam.

"Tam," she said, very calmly, "did you touch my map?"

Her brother looked up from his half of the same chart—the twins always worked in halves, one inked, one checked—and frowned. "I haven't touched your map since Tuesday."

"It's Thursday."

"Then no." He leaned over, squinting at the little cluster of rooftops she was pointing at. "That's not where Ashenford is. Ashenford's on the mill stream. That's—" He stopped. He took out his own copy of the survey, made two years back from a merchant's plates, and laid it beside hers.

On both charts, Ashenford sat on the mill stream. On both charts, Ashenford now sat three miles west, on a stream neither of them could name, in a fold of hills that hadn't been there before.

"That's not possible," said Pip.

"No," agreed Tam, who was the twin who stated the obvious so Pip didn't have to feel foolish doing it. "But there it is."

She should have laughed it off. She'd seen forged maps, misprinted maps, maps drawn by drunk cartographers who'd never actually walked the roads they sold. But this wasn't smudged or shifted like bad printing. The hills around the new Ashenford were *detailed*. Little hachures for slope, a tiny tree-symbol for a copse, a dotted footpath leading up to—she bent close—a shrine symbol. Inked with the same confident hand as the rest of the chart. As if it had always been there.

As if the map had decided, sometime in the night, that this was the truth, and corrected itself.

"Osk," Pip called toward the back room, "what do you know about living documents?"

Osk appeared in the doorway wiping ink from his fingers—he'd been writing out prayers again, the ones nobody remembered how to finish, and getting them wrong in ways that made Zephyrine go quiet and strange. He looked at the charts for a long moment without touching them, the way he looked at everything holy or horrible.

"I know," he said slowly, "that a map is a kind of memory. And I know what happens to memories in this country lately."

Nobody said the rest of it. Nobody had to. They all carried it now, the way you carry a stone in your boot: the Veil thinning, Vess waking in Zephyrine's skin, valleys going quiet on the wind.

"Get Maret," said Pip.

* * *

*Tam*

Tam liked numbers because they stayed put. Two plus two was four in Thornfell, and it would be four anywhere else, if anywhere else existed—which, given recent evidence, Tam was no longer prepared to swear to.

He counted things when he was nervous. That night he counted the crew's stores (flour, eleven pounds; hope, unweighed), and then, because sleep wouldn't come, he counted the villages on the master survey of the eastern marches, the one Maret had won in a card game from a customs officer who'd cheated worse than she did.

Two hundred and six named settlements east of the Spine Road.

He counted them twice, because the first time he got two hundred and four, and Tam did not make counting mistakes.

The third time: two hundred and five. Different missing each time. He marked each count in pencil, in the margins, small and neat, and by the third hour past midnight his margins looked like a fever chart, which, he thought, was exactly what it was. The country had a fever. The names were sweating off.

He woke Pip by throwing a pebble at her bedroll, an old habit from childhood that she had never once forgiven.

"Pip. The villages are going."

"They're not *going*, they're moving, we established this—"

"Not the moving ones. The *gone* ones." He showed her the margins. She read them the way she read everything, fast and greedy, and then went still in a way that frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Which ones are missing?" she asked.

"Different every time. That's the worst part. It's not like they burn down—you'd lose the same one. It's like..." He searched for it. "Like someone reading a long list and their eye skipping. Random skips."

"And when you look for the skipped one?"

Tam hesitated. This was the part he'd been turning over since midnight, the part that had finally driven him to throw pebbles. "I can't remember what's in the gaps. Not just the names. I try to picture the road between Marlow's Cross and Tarn Bridge, and there's a stretch in the middle that's just—smooth. Like porridge. I know the distance is forty miles but I can't tell you what's along it. I've *walked* it, Pip. Four years ago. There was a tollhouse."

"There's always a tollhouse."

"There was a tollhouse and I cannot remember what the man looked like or even if there was a man, and it's not forgetting, it's—" His voice did something embarrassing. He pushed through it. "It's like the memory was never mine to have. Like something reached in and took the middle out of my own head."

Pip was quiet for a while. Then she said, in the voice she used when she'd decided something and wanted company in the decision, "We need to test it properly. Controlled conditions."

"You want to run an experiment on the apocalypse."

"I want to run an experiment on the *map*. The apocalypse can wait its turn."

* * *

*Pip*

The experiment, as Pip designed it, was simple enough to explain to a child and terrible enough that she explained it to no one but Tam.

They took the master survey and copied out, in their own hands, twenty village names from the eastern marches—names, nothing more, just ink on a scrap. Then they hid the scrap in Tam's boot, told no one, and waited.

Three days later, six of the twenty names had vanished from the master survey. Not crossed out. Not faded. The space where they'd been was filled with blank vellum-colored nothing, smooth as an egg, and the roads that had connected those villages now simply... ended, trailing off into hachured wilderness that grew more detailed every time Pip looked at it, as though the map were embarrassed by the gap and inventing scenery to cover it.

Of the six vanished names, Tam could read four off the scrap in his boot. The other two he stared at with the flat bafflement of a man reading words in a language he'd spoken yesterday.

"Coldbrant," he said. "I know Coldbrant. Wool town, up past the fells. And this one—" he tapped the fifth name, "—this is letters, Pip. It's letters and I don't know them."

"Read it aloud."

"*Hest—* something. Hestmarch? Hallow?" He shook his head hard, like a dog shedding water. "It slides. You look right at it and it slides."

Pip took the scrap. The fifth name sat there in Tam's careful hand, and she felt the same sliding sensation, a word-shaped hole wearing a word's clothing. She thought of Zephyrine describing how poison tasted of lightning, of Osk saying *a map is a kind of memory*, and she understood, with a cold that started in her fingertips and worked inward:

The country wasn't losing villages the way a man loses coins. It was losing them the way a dying man loses days—waking with less of itself, and never knowing what was gone.

And the maps knew. The maps were honest witnesses, redrawing themselves nightly, trying to keep up with a truth that kept shrinking under them.

"We show the others," Tam said.

"After one more test."

"Pip."

"One more." She held up a finger before he could argue. "Maret will ask me if it's real or if we forged it. You know she will. It's what she pays us for—knowing what's real. So we make it undeniable."

What she settled on was crueler than she liked, and she did it anyway. They rode out—not far, just to the crossroads hamlet of Dun Ellet, two hours' easy walk, where a woman named Sorrel sold them eggs every week and complained about the price of salt. Pip bought eggs. She chatted about the weather. She memorized Sorrel's face, her crooked left thumb, the chipped blue glaze on her door-lintel, and that night she wrote it all down in her journal, every detail, and drew the hamlet's layout from memory: nine houses, a well, a shrine-stone with ribbons.

Nine days later, Dun Ellet was not on any map.

Ten days later, they rode back along the route, and Pip counted the milestones and found one too few, and the road bent where the road had never bent, and Tam said, quietly, "This is where the well was," and pointed at a stand of birches that had definitely not been there, because Pip would have remembered birches, Pip remembered *everything*, that was her whole gift—

Except she didn't. She stood in the birch shade and reached for the shrine-stone with its ribbons and found a soft place in her mind where it should have been, a smooth egg-colored nothing, and her own handwriting in her own journal described it perfectly and meant nothing at all, like a letter from someone she'd never met.

"Sorrel," she said out loud, testing it. "Sorrel. Crooked thumb. Blue lintel."

The words were true. She could feel that they were true, the way you feel a coin is gold by biting it. But there was no face attached anymore. Just the shape of a fact with the person scooped out of it.

Tam put his arm through hers, which was what he'd done since they were six and she was furious and he was frightened, roles they'd never quite swapped since.

"We tell them now," he said. "Right now. Today."

"Yes," said Pip, and hated how small her voice came out. "Yes. Now."

* * *

*Tam*

Telling the crew was worse than Tam had feared, mostly because Zephyrine took it the hardest, and Tam had braced for Maret instead.

Maret heard them out at the table with the cracked bell-talisman sitting between everyone like a guest of honor, and when they finished she was silent for a moment, then said only: "Show me the margins." She read Tam's fever-chart counts twice, ran one thick finger down the columns, and nodded once, the nod she gave to weather reports and other things you couldn't fight, only prepare for. "Good work. Both of you. We move faster from here. We stop sleeping in towns."

Zephyrine, though. Zephyrine had gone gray at the part about the sliding word, the name that wouldn't be read, and Tam watched him mouth something to himself—*Coldbrant, Coldbrant*—and then watch it slide away behind his eyes in real time. The taster pressed his palms flat on the table like a man steadying himself on a ship.

"I've eaten for that town," he said. "There was a wool factor who sent the palace a cheese every autumn, and I tasted every one. Nine years of cheese from—" He stopped. Frowned. "From..."

"From Coldbrant," Tam said gently.

"Was it called Coldbrant?"

And that was the thing that made the whole room go quiet, the thing Tam would carry afterward like a splinter: not that the country was forgetting, but that it forgot *outward*, from the edges in, and the people inside it felt nothing except a smoothness where their lives used to be. Somewhere a woman with a crooked thumb was maybe still selling eggs—or maybe there was no woman, no hamlet, no *was*, just birches and a bent road and a milestone too few, and no one in all of Thornfell mourning her because no one could hold her name long enough to grieve.

Except the twins. Except two forgers with a hidden scrap in a boot and a journal full of letters from strangers, keeping faith with the disappeared the only way they knew how: in ink.

"We write them down," Pip announced to the room, chin up, voice steady, and Tam saw her decide it even as she said it. "Every name we catch vanishing. Every detail we can hold. We're the record now. If Thornfell won't remember itself, then by every god waking and outlawed, *we will*."

Osk bowed his head over the cracked bell. Maret said, "Add paper to the supply list." Zephyrine looked at the twins for a long moment and said, softly, "Thank you," in the voice of a man thanking someone for holding a rope he was hanging from.

Only Vess, stirring somewhere behind Zephyrine's eyes, said anything true and useless at once, in that voice like a door opening in an empty house:

*"You cannot write fast enough, little scribes. But write anyway. The writing matters, even where it lands nowhere. Especially there."*

Tam wrote that down too.

That night, checking the master survey one last time before sleep, he counted the eastern settlements one final time. One hundred and ninety-eight.

And at the bottom margin of the chart, in a fine, faint, unhurried hand that belonged to no cartographer alive, someone had added a single line of text that had not been there that morning:

*Thornfell remembers less of itself each day. Ask the door why.*

Tam sat very still. Then he woke his sister, and together, by candlelight, the twins began to copy out every name that remained.
