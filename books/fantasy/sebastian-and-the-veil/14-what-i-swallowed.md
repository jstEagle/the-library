## 14. What I Swallowed

The fever came at dusk, the way honest things do—without ceremony, without apology.

Zephyrine had felt it building all day: a heat behind his eyes like a lamp turned too high, a taste in his mouth that wasn't there. Not copper, not bitterness, none of the forty-one flavors of poison he'd catalogued over nine years. This was older. It tasted the way a struck bell sounds, if you could hold sound on your tongue.

"You're doing the face," Pip said from across the campfire. "The one where you look like you're chewing a wasp."

"I'm always doing that face."

"Yes, but this time it's *worse*." She pointed her spoon at him. Tam, beside her, nodded solemnly and kept copying the map they were redrawing for the third time this week, because the third time this week the roads had moved.

Maret looked up from sharpening a knife she did not need sharpened. "Fever?"

"Perhaps."

"Vess?"

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps isn't an answer, taster."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "It's a profession."

Osk laughed from his bedroll—the defrocked priest had a laugh like a door swinging on a well-oiled hinge, warm and slightly crooked—and Maret went back to her knife, satisfied that no one was dying *yet*, which in the Company of the Broken Bell counted as good news.

But Zephyrine knew what it was. He'd known since noon, when the taste had first arrived riding on nothing at all. It was the poisons. All of them. Nine years of small cups and swallowed deaths, sitting in him like coins in a miser's purse, and something—Vess, or the thinning Veil, or simply his own body finally presenting its bill—had begun to count them.

He wrapped himself in his cloak a little apart from the fire, at the edge of the shrine-ruin they'd made camp in, and let the fever take him.

* * *

The first dish was quail.

He was twenty-two again, three days into the crown's service, standing in the white-tiled tasting room with his hands behind his back like he'd been taught, and the quail arrived under a silver dome and smelled of rosemary and ambition. He swallowed. The poison in it was nuxbane, subtle, meant to kill slowly enough that the assassin would be three kingdoms away. He survived it because that was the job, and the king ate quail that night, and somewhere in the city a man who had spent a month perfecting that sauce learned that his work had failed.

Zephyrine had never thought about that man before.

In the vision—or the memory, or whatever the fever was serving him—he saw him. A narrow kitchen, a narrow man, hands trembling as he heard the news. And then he saw the guards come for the man two days later anyway, because the crown did not tolerate failed assassins any more than successful ones, and the man went to the block still wearing his apron strings.

*Did you taste him too?* asked a voice that might have been the fever, or the quail, or himself. *You swallowed his death. Did it flavor the meat?*

"That's not fair," Zephyrine said aloud, to the dark, to the fire, to no one. "I didn't kill him."

*No,* said the voice. *You just made his killing necessary.*

The next dish came whether he wanted it or not.

* * *

They came all night, in the order he'd swallowed them, which was also the order he'd tried hardest to forget.

The wedding soup, laced with heartsbane, that killed the poisoner instead of the bride because Zephyrine's tongue caught it and the guards were quick. He relived the poisoner's face—a girl, barely older than him, weeping as they dragged her off, and he understood in the vision what he had refused to understand at the time: she hadn't been paid. She'd been *coerced*. Her little brother was held somewhere. Zephyrine could have said so. He could have spoken up, told the magistrates, begged mercy. He had said nothing, because food-tasters who spoke up stopped being invisible, and invisibility was the only armor he owned.

The spiced wine at the winter court, where he'd detected the rotroot and spat it into the flagon, and the whole hall had applauded, and the king had smiled at him with teeth like a drawer of knives, and Zephyrine had glowed with pride for a full day before learning that the man accused of the poisoning had been the wrong man entirely, hanged within the week, while the true poisoner dined well and grew fat.

The honey cakes. The eel. The funeral stew that had nearly gotten him, the one that left him three days in the infirmary learning to breathe again, during which no one visited except a chambermaid who left bread by his bed and never told him her name. In the vision he finally saw what happened to her: nothing. She kept leaving bread. For years. Small kindnesses, unasked, unthanked—and he had never once learned her name, because knowing people was dangerous, and not-knowing them was how you stayed alive.

*You built your whole self out of swallowing,* the voice said, gentle now, which was worse. *Every dish a brick. Every silence mortar. Look what the house is made of.*

"Shut up," Zephyrine whispered.

*I'm only asking what I've been sent to ask.*

And then the fever shifted, and it was no longer the voice of the poisons but the voice he knew better than his own heartbeat these days—Vess, the small god of thresholds, who spoke through him the way wind speaks through a doorway, and who sounded, tonight, genuinely sorry.

*You are dying a little, taster. The old doses are waking up. Nine years of deaths you carried so a warden wouldn't have to carry them, and the Veil's thinning has loosened their grip on you. They want out. Like everything else in this country.*

"Lovely," Zephyrine managed. His mouth tasted of every bad decision he'd ever eaten. "Can you stop it?"

*I can do better.* A pause, the way a friend pauses before offering you something enormous. *When the toll comes—and it is coming, you know it is—I can take these first. The poisons. The dishes. The dead men's faces. Call it a partial payment. You would keep yourself and lose the weight. You would sleep, Zephyrine. You have not slept in nine years; you have only lain down between tastings.*

The offer hung in the dark like a lantern.

And oh, it was beautiful. That was the obscene thing about it. He could feel the shape of the life it offered: mornings without counting exits, meals without testing the first bite against his tongue like a duelist's salute, laughter that didn't cost anything afterward. A man who had never stood in a white-tiled room and been applauded for surviving.

*Say yes,* Vess said softly. *No one would blame you. Least of all me. I am a god of doors, and doors exist so that things may pass through and be gone.*

* * *

Somewhere in the middle of the night, Osk found him.

Zephyrine didn't hear him approach—one of the few mercies of the fever—but suddenly there was a blanket being tucked around his shoulders with the brisk incompetence of a man who had done field medicine on smugglers and knew exactly how much fuss anyone would tolerate.

"You're talking to the fire," Osk said.

"The fire started it."

"Mm." The ex-priest sat down heavily on the ruined altar beside him, which he did sometimes, claiming it was blasphemy and therefore nostalgic. "Bad night?"

"They're all here," Zephyrine said. His voice came out scraped. "All the dishes. All the—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know how many people died *because* I survived? Not poisoned by me. Not even near me. But every plot I foiled, someone else paid for it. Someone always paid. The crown doesn't forgive failure, and it doesn't forgive success either, it just—" He laughed, and it hurt. "Nine years, Osk. I thought I was keeping my head down. Turns out I was just keeping everyone else's head down for them."

Osk was quiet for a while. Firelight moved on his face.

"When they defrocked me," he said at last, "it was for preaching that the gods still listened. Eleven years I'd served the Quiet Choir, and one sermon—one—about how silence isn't the same as absence. They took my vestments, my name in the rolls, my right to speak a blessing." He smiled at the fire. "You know what I kept?"

"What?"

"The sermons. Up here." He tapped his temple. "They couldn't reach those. And every morning since, I get to choose again whether to believe what got me thrown out. Every morning it costs me something to say it. And every morning I say it anyway." He looked over. "That's the whole religion, Zeph. Everything else is furniture."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be comforting. It's supposed to be *true*. Comforting is what Vess is offering you right now, isn't it?" Osk's eyes were sharp despite the hour. "To take the memories. Lighten the load before the toll."

Zephyrine said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Hm," said Osk.

"Don't 'hm' at me. You don't know what's in my head tonight."

"No," Osk agreed. "But I know what's in yours generally, and it's this: you think if you put the pain down, you'll put the *people* down with it. The dead cook. The bread-bringing maid. Brannoc, teaching you which mushrooms lie. You think the memories are a package deal, and you're right." He stood, joints cracking like green kindling. "Pain's the receipt, boy. You don't tear up the receipt unless you mean to return what you bought."

He shuffled back toward his bedroll, then paused.

"For what it's worth," he added, "the maid's name was Sera. I did the last rites at Hollowmere eight years back. She asked after 'the taster who lived.' Never said why. Just asked." And he left Zephyrine alone with that, which was either cruelty or a gift, and with Osk it had always been impossible to tell the difference.

* * *

Near dawn, Vess returned.

Zephyrine felt the god arrive the way you feel someone sit down beside you on a bench—no sound, just a change in the weight of the air. Through the last rags of fever he watched the fire burn low and waited.

*Well?* Vess asked.

"Sera," Zephyrine said. "Her name was Sera."

*Yes.*

"Eight years she asked after me and I never knew. If I take your offer—if I hand you the poisons, the dishes, the dead—you'll be taking her too. The bread. The one kindness in nine years that asked nothing." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "And Brannoc. And the girl who wept on the way to the block, whose brother I never found. And the wrong man they hanged for the winter wine, whose face I can't even picture anymore, which means I owe him double." He took a breath. "You'd be taking all of it, wouldn't you. Not just the hurt. The *hurt is how I find them*."

*Yes,* Vess said again, and did not dress it up. Gods of thresholds, Zephyrine was learning, did not lie at their own doors. *That is the nature of the toll. Memory does not sort itself into what you want to keep. It comes out whole, like a tooth.*

"Then no."

*Zephyrine—*

"No." He said it more firmly, and found, to his surprise, that beneath the exhaustion there was something solid underneath, something that had been there all along waiting for him to stand on it. "I've spent nine years swallowing things so other people wouldn't have to feel them. That was the job, and I hated the job, but I did it *honestly*. And now you're offering me the one deal a taster should never take: to spit out the taste and pretend the cup was clean." He looked into the fire. "I'd rather hurt. Do you understand? I'd rather carry every dish, every death, every name I never learned—I'd rather hurt *on purpose* than wake up light and empty. Because the moment I forget what things cost, I'm just another warden. Just another person fed on someone else's pain, calling it peace."

The silence lasted long enough for the fire to settle twice.

Then Vess laughed—not the vast bell-sound of the Veil-vision, but something smaller, warmer, almost human. *There it is,* the god said. *I wondered when you'd say it. I confess I hoped you would.*

"You *hoped*—you offered me a bargain hoping I'd refuse?"

*I am a god of thresholds, taster. My entire existence is watching what people carry through doors, and listening to what they refuse to leave behind.* The warmth in the voice deepened. *The toll will come. Soon. And when it does, it will take what it takes, and I will not be able to soften it. But tonight you chose your weight, freely, with your eyes open. Whatever happens at that door—whatever you lose—you will lose it as a man who knows exactly what everything cost him. That is worth more than you know. Possibly more than I know, and I am nominally omniscient within approximately four feet of a doorway.*

"That's a very specific omniscience."

"I contain multitudes," Vess said, "but only near hinges."

Despite everything—the fever, the dead, the ache behind his ribs like a second heart—Zephyrine laughed. It came out cracked and half-strangled and real, and across the campfire Pip stirred, muttered "*he's laughing at the fire again*", and rolled over.

The fever broke as the sky went grey. Zephyrine sat watching the embers, holding his nine years like a man holds a bag of coins he has decided not to spend, heavy and jingling and entirely his.

It was Tam who brought the news, running from the ridge where he'd taken the dawn watch, map flapping forgotten in his fist.

"Riders," he panted. "On the south road. Flying the princess's colors—but it's not her, it's a herald, royal seal and all." He swallowed. "They're asking for parley. Specifically—" he checked the scrap of paper in his hand, frowning, "—specifically requesting the vessel by name. And there's a second message. Private. Marked for the lady traveling with us under, uh—" he squinted, "'—under the alias of *Goodwife Marren of Nowhere in Particular*, which honestly, Isolde, we all knew.'"

Zephyrine stood, legs unsteady, tongue still tasting of lightning and old grief.

Isolde had gone home to the capital. To her father's table.

He knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent his life detecting what was hidden in food, exactly what kind of meal awaited her there—and exactly how much she believed she could swallow.
