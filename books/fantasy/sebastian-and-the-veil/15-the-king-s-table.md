## 15. The King's Table

The parley banner went up on the north road at dawn, and by noon Isolde was riding through gates she had sworn, three months and one lifetime ago, never to see again.

She had expected to feel fear. What she felt instead was a strange domestic embarrassment, as though she were returning to a childhood home and finding it smaller than memory insisted — except the home in question was a palace built on top of a prison, and the family business was eating gods.

"Smile," murmured Veyra, her maid-in-name-only, riding at her flank in borrowed livery. Veyra had been a smuggler before she'd been a servant, and before that, judging by her knife-work, something worse. "You look like you're attending your own funeral."

"I attended enough of those this year," Isolde said. "One more won't kill me."

"That," said Veyra, "is precisely the attitude I'm paid to worry about."

"You're not paid anything. I feed you."

"I'm fed *because* of my worrying. It's a barter economy."

Isolde smiled despite herself, which was Veyra's whole method and she knew it. The gates swallowed them, and the smell of Thornhold came back all at once: beeswax and cold stone and, underneath, faintly, like rot beneath fresh paint, something older. She hadn't noticed it as a girl. Now it sat in her nose like a warning she couldn't translate.

They took her weapons at the inner gate. All of them, including the little bodkin sewn into her sleeve, which she surrendered with theatrical grace while the guard captain pretended not to be impressed. They did not take her rings. She noted that. Her father had always been sentimental about her hands — *a warden's daughter should have unmarked hands*, he used to say, turning her palm over in his big ones, studying it as if reading something written there. She had thought it affection.

She no longer thought very much about her father was affection.

* * *

They gave her the Amber Room, which was either courtesy or a cage, and in Thornhold the distinction had historically been decorative. Through the window she could see the palace gardens, the hedge maze she'd gotten lost in at seven, the fountain where Corvin had pushed her in fully dressed at nine and been thrashed for it by a governess who clearly feared their mother more than the crown.

Corvin. Her brother, who knew what their father ate, and had chosen the throne over the truth. Who had burned Hollowmere. Who had Osk in chains until he didn't anymore, because Osk had escaped through means Isolde still didn't fully understand — an unlikely guard, the smugglers said, though they'd refused to elaborate, which meant Pip and Tam were involved somehow, which meant it had been ridiculous and effective in equal measure.

A knock. Not the triple rap of protocol but the single soft knock of someone who had decided long ago that protocol was for people with less power.

"Come in, Corvin."

Her brother entered looking like a man who had slept badly for approximately one season. He was still beautiful in the way their family was beautiful — too symmetrical, too composed, portraits waiting to happen — but there were grey threads in his temples now that hadn't been there in spring, and his eyes had the flat shine of someone who had seen something and decided, deliberately, to keep seeing it.

"You look well," he said.

"I look armed."

"They took the armaments."

"I have others."

"I know." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to honesty either of them would manage for a while, so they let it sit between them like a candle neither wanted to admit they needed.

"Why are you here?" Isolde asked. "Under parley. You could have refused. Father would have refused."

"Father doesn't refuse you anything. That's part of the problem." Corvin moved to the window, hands clasped behind his back — the posture of a prince reviewing troops, or a man keeping his fingers from shaking. "He's delighted you've come home. Genuinely. Which frightens me more than his rages ever did."

"He has rages?"

"He has *weather*," Corvin said. "Rages imply a person underneath having them."

That landed somewhere cold in Isolde's chest. She thought of the ledger she'd found — every poisoning staged, every survival feeding the wardens' hunger — and of her father reading accounts by lamplight with an appetite she'd mistaken for diligence.

"What does he eat now?" she asked quietly. "When there are no staged poisonings. When the gods started waking and the old meals stopped working."

Corvin was silent so long she heard the fountain outside, absurdly cheerful.

"Don't dine hungry," he said finally. "And don't drink first. He'll pour for you himself, and whatever he pours, drink slowly, and—" His voice caught on something. He smoothed it flat. "Watch him when he thinks no one's watching him. That's all I'll say. I shouldn't even say that much."

"Corvin." She crossed to him. Up close he smelled of ink and horse and something scorched, faint, like cloth held near a flame. "There is still a version of this where you ride out of here with me."

He looked down at her, and for one unguarded moment her brother — the boy who'd pushed her into fountains and lied to governesses and split stolen apples with her under the table during boring ceremonies — looked out through the prince's face.

"There is a version," he agreed, "where I burn this whole palace down around us both. I think about it most nights. Then I remember what's under it, and what happens if the wardenship fails mid-burn, and I go back to being a good son." He stepped away from her, and the boy went with the step. "Wear something nice tonight. He notices."

* * *

The King's Table, when Isolde was small, had been a place of warmth. Roast meats and honeyed roots, her father's laugh rolling down the hall, her mother — quiet, watchful, gone these six years — pressing extra sweetbread onto her plate when the ministers droned. She had built her whole childhood self out of dinners at that table.

Now it was set for two, at the far end of a hall built for sixty, and the candles made pools of light with darkness thick enough to swim in between them.

Her father stood when she entered. Aldric Delacroix, King of Thornfell, Warden of the Veil — though the second title existed nowhere on paper, only in burned maps and private ledgers and the architecture of everything. He looked exactly as she remembered. That was the first wrong thing. Six years since her mother died, three months of civil unraveling, gods waking under the country like moles under a lawn, and the king had not aged a day. Not a new line, not a silvered hair. Time slid off him the way rain slides off oiled canvas.

"Isolde." His voice filled the hall without effort. "My daughter comes home."

"I come under parley," she corrected, taking her seat.

"Parley is just dinner with lawyers." He gestured, and unseen servants brought the first course — a pale soup, delicate, smelling of leek and cream. He ladled it himself, which he never used to do. Kings did not serve; kings were served. But his hands moved with the ease of long practice, and Isolde understood suddenly, with a lurch, that the king had always served at this table. The king had always poured.

"Taste it," he said warmly. "I want to know if it's still good. Your palate was always the best in the family."

*Testing me,* she thought. *Or testing the food.* She took a spoonful. Leek, cream, salt, and beneath it — nothing. A perfectly engineered absence of anything wrong. She realized he was watching her not-taste-poison the way other men watched a race.

"It's good," she said.

"Of course it is. I supervised." He smiled, and it was his smile, the one from the warm years, and for a heartbeat she nearly believed in him again. That was the trap of fathers: they kept the original keys to you.

Dinner proceeded. He asked after her health, her travels, her opinions on mountain weather, and each question was gentle and each answer was weighed, and gradually Isolde noticed the shape of the evening: he wasn't interrogating her. He was *courting* her. There was a proposal coming, wrapped in leek soup and candlelight.

It arrived with the second course, roast partridge.

"Your brother is adequate," her father said, carving with surgical neatness. "But adequate is not what the years ahead require. The Veil thins. The prisoners wake. Someone must hold the wardenship after me, and Corvin flinches at the cost." He laid a slice of partridge on her plate like a man laying a card face-up. "You don't flinch. You walked into Hollowmere after the burning. You read the ledgers. You found the maps. You're the only child of mine who ever went looking for the truth instead of away from it."

"And the truth is that we're jailers," Isolde said. "Feeding on our prisoners."

"The truth is that we are *load-bearing*," her father said, and there — for half a syllable — his voice did something voices shouldn't do. It layered. Like two speakers sharing one throat, one deep and one deeper. "Every kingdom is a story told to keep something quiet, Isolde. Thornfell tells its story louder than most because it has more to keep quiet. Remove the wardens and the story collapses, and everything inside it collapses with the story. You've seen the villages vanish. That is a country forgetting itself. That is what mercy looks like, unchecked."

"So betray the vessel," Isolde said. "That's the offer. Zephyrine Delacroix, delivered to your table, and in exchange I get Corvin's chair."

"In exchange you get the truth, complete, and the power to survive it." Her father leaned forward into the candlelight, and his eyes were kind, and his eyes were bottomless, and both at once, which should not have been possible. "I am offering you the only honest inheritance this family has ever had. Your brother will spend his reign lying to himself about what he is. You could simply... know."

And she looked at him, really looked, past the beloved geometry of his face, and saw what Corvin had told her to see: the thing wearing a king.

It was subtle. It was horribly subtle. It was the way his shadow fell slightly wrong against the chair-back, pooling toward him rather than away. The way the candle flames bent toward his mouth when he spoke, like men leaning in to hear. The way the hall's darkness didn't press in around him but *waited*, attentive, like a dog at heel. And beneath the king's skin, visible now that she knew to look — visible the way a watermark is visible when you tilt the page — something vast and patient and *hungry*, wearing Aldric Delacroix the way a hand wears a glove, except the glove had been worn so long it had grown into the shape of it, seams and all.

He was not possessed. Possession implied a struggle. This was a tenancy of centuries. Whatever wore the crown of Thornfell had worn it since before there was a Thornfell, since the prison was built and a jailer-shape was needed, and it had raised children the way a farmer raises oxen: with care, with warmth, with a clear eye on eventual use.

"You're not my father," Isolde said. Her voice came out steady. Later she would be proud of that; now it seemed to belong to someone else.

"I am every inch your father," the thing in the king said fondly. "I chose every lesson. I let you break your arm falling from the orchard wall so you'd learn to judge heights. I arranged the winter you spent ill so your mother would stay close to you instead of asking questions about the lower vaults. Love is a technique, my darling. I am simply better at it than most."

Something in Isolde's chest, some last warm room marked *Father*, closed its shutters. It hurt. She let it hurt. Vess had told her once, through Zephyrine's sleeping mouth, that grief was a threshold too — you had to walk through it, not around it, or you carried it forever on your shoulders like wet wool.

"Well," she said, "in the spirit of family honesty, then. I decline. And I'd like dessert."

The thing wearing her father studied her for a long moment, and then laughed — the real laugh, the hall-filling one, and the candles guttered with the force of it. "There she is. Corvin folds; you fold *back*. Oh, I did save the best for last." He rose to fetch the dessert himself, of course. He poured the wine himself, from a dark bottle, into two cups, and handed her hers with a flourish.

Isolde had planned for this hour for nine days. She had practiced the motion four hundred times in a smuggler's camp with Maret timing her and Osk praying over her and Pip and Tam losing count of her repetitions because they kept arguing about whether the trick was physically possible. It was simple: the ring on her right hand — the one her father had always admired, *unmarked hands, a warden's daughter* — hinged at the bezel. Inside the bezel, a paste of distilled lightning-toxin, refined twice, concentrated past any dose the apothecaries' tables considered survivable. The same toxin that had nearly killed Zephyrine in chapter one of all of this, back when it was only a strange flavor and a voice saying his name.

The dose that killed a god's vessel in miniature might, Osk had hoped, with a priest's cautious arithmetic, do something to the thing that ate gods.

She turned the ring as she lifted the cup, the way she'd turned it four hundred times, and tipped the paste into the wine, and drank deeply of her father's toast, and watched him drink his own untouched cup — because the toxin was in *both* cups; she had dosed hers as well; Maret had insisted; *never poison a cup you wouldn't drain yourself, girl, it's how amateurs die* —

Nothing happened.

For three heartbeats, nothing happened. Isolde's pulse counted them off like a headsman.

Then her father set down his cup, frowned at it with mild scholarly interest, and said, in the layered voice, the double voice, the voice with the floor dropped out of it:

"*Ah.*"

The candles went out.

Not blew out — went out, all of them, simultaneously, and the hall's darkness stood up. In the black, her father's outline remained visible, lit from within, a faint gold seeping through his skin like light through a lampshade, and Isolde saw the shape of what was inside him for one full, endless second: coiled, immense, folded through the palace's bones the way a tapeworm fills a gut, its attention swinging toward her with the slow inevitability of a lighthouse beam.

"*Lightning,*" it said, tasting the word. "*How thoughtful. The last gift of the storm-child. We remember the storm-child.*" A pause, and when the king's voice resumed it was warm again, amused, terribly gentle. "You poisoned your king, Isolde. At his own table. Under parley." He picked up the cup and examined it almost tenderly. "Do you know what we are? We are the thing the gods were locked up to protect the world *from*. Did you imagine a dose fit for a godling would trouble us?"

The candles relit themselves, all together, politely, as if nothing had happened. Her father's face was entirely human again. He dabbed his mouth with a napkin.

"I find I'm not angry," he said. "I'm *delighted*. This is the most honest meal we've had in thirty years." He stood, straightening his cuffs. "Guards will escort you to your room. New arrangements will be made in the morning. Do try the honey-cakes before you go — Cook has outdone herself."

At the door he paused, without turning, and added conversationally:

"Your friends reach the Veil-door in perhaps five days. I do hope they hurry. The re-sealing requires vessels, plural, and I have been so hungry lately."

* * *

Veyra was waiting in the Amber Room, sharpening a knife with the serene rhythm of a woman who had already assumed the worst. She took one look at Isolde's face and put the knife down.

"We're leaving tonight," Isolde said. "Through the cistern route. I need a horse by the third mile-marker and I need you to stop smiling."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're absolutely smiling."

"It's relief," Veyra said. "You went in to dinner with a monster and came out with all your limbs and a plan-shaped expression. In my experience that's a triumph." She was already packing. "What did you learn?"

Isolde stood at the window. Below, the gardens lay silvered and still, and beyond the walls the dark country stretched away toward the mountains, toward Hollowmere's ashes, toward a door out of a world that her father intended to nail shut with everyone she loved still inside it.

Five days.

"I learned he isn't afraid of us," she said quietly. "Any of us. He's afraid of *time*. We have less of it than Zephyrine thinks." She pulled the ring from her finger — spent, hinge sprung, bezel empty — and closed her fist around it. "Saddle the horses, Veyra. We ride through the night."

"And if the roads are watched?"

"Then we ride anyway." She turned from the window, and Veyra — who had known princesses, generals, and one archbishop of frankly criminal temperament — later swore the temperature of the room dropped. "He called me his best work. Let's show him he was right."
