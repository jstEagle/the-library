## 16. Flight Through the Forgetting

The world ended politely, in patches.

That was the thing nobody had warned us about—not Vess, not Osk's stolen papers from the fort, not even Pip, who had spent three sleepless nights with the self-eating maps and could recite their behavior like scripture. I had imagined the Forgetting as something violent: a roar, a collapse, a wall of nothing chasing us across the hills. Instead it was quiet. A valley would simply stop agreeing to be there. The road beneath our horses would go soft and gray at its edges, like bread left out too long, and then the trees on either side would forget which way was up, and then there would be no either side at all—just a pale seam in the air where a place used to keep itself, humming faintly, like a struck bell that couldn't remember why it rang.

We rode ahead of three seams and one of them was gaining.

"Left!" Tam shouted from the rear, where he'd appointed himself seam-watcher by virtue of having the best eyes and the worst nerves. "No—sorry—right! Right! It forked!"

"Seams don't fork," Pip said grimly, kneeing her horse up beside mine.

"This one does. I'm looking right at it."

"That's not a fork, that's two seams converging, which is worse—"

"I know it's worse, that's why I said fork!"

Maret rode point and did not turn around for any of it, which was how she expressed love. Every so often she'd raise one hand—a fist meant *stop*, an open palm meant *go*, and once, memorably, a gesture involving all five fingers that meant *if anyone asks me another question about seams I will put us all in one*.

Osk rode beside Isolde. That had been its own small miracle: we'd pulled him off a supply wagon outside Fort Marrow two days ago, thinner than a psalm and missing half his beard, and he'd laughed—actually laughed—when he saw us, before his legs remembered they weren't supposed to work anymore. Now he sat his horse like a man who'd been reminded what sitting was, and he kept glancing at Isolde with the expression of a priest who has discovered that prayer works but only when you're not paying attention.

"You're staring," Isolde told him.

"I'm praying," Osk said. "It looks similar."

"It doesn't."

"Well. My theology got rustic in the fort."

I let them bicker and kept my eyes on the horizon, where the mountains around Thornfell's heart were doing something mountains should never do: leaning inward, slowly, the way a crowd leans toward a fight. Vess stirred under my ribs, a warmth like swallowed sunlight.

*Close,* the god murmured. *The heart-road remembers us.*

"The heart-road had better do more than remember," I muttered. "It had better exist."

*Everything exists somewhere,* Vess said, with the serene unhelpfulness of a being who has never once had to find a horse in the dark. *Even things that have stopped existing here. You are carrying several yourself.*

I decided not to examine that. Nine years of swallowing poison teaches you a certain discipline about things you can't fix before breakfast.

We crested the ridge at noon, and Maret finally reined in, and none of us pretended we didn't see it.

Behind us, where the village of Alderfen had been when we passed through yesterday—where Osk had bought apples and overpaid, because prison had made him sentimental about markets—there was no village. There wasn't even an absence shaped like a village. There was just green going gray-green going pale, a soft-edged wound in the land maybe half a mile wide, humming its lost-bell hum. And inside my head, where nine years of memories lived, something itched. I reached for the name of the innkeeper who'd sold us those apples and found a smooth blank place instead, like a tongue probing a pulled tooth.

"It takes memories too," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "When a place goes. Anyone who knew it loses knowing it."

Pip went white. "Tam. Did we ever—" She grabbed her brother's sleeve. "The ferryman at Sorrel Water. Did we ever actually pay him back?"

"We paid him back," Tam said. "In spring. With interest. I remember the interest."

"Do you? Do you actually remember, or do you remember remembering?"

They looked at each other in genuine horror, and despite everything—the seams, the dying country, the fact that I could feel Vess coiling tighter under my breastbone like a hand closing around a door-handle—I laughed. It came out ragged, but it came out. Maret glanced back at me, one-eyed, and the corner of her mouth moved a quarter inch, which from Maret was a standing ovation.

"Ride," she said. "Grieve later. The heart-road's through the pass at Grey Anvil, and if the map's honest we've got until tomorrow night."

"And if the map's not honest?" Isolde asked.

"Then we've got until whenever the lying stops working," Maret said, "which, given whose map it is"—she nodded at Pip—"could be any moment."

"My maps are *excellent*—"

"*Ride.*"

* * *

We made the pass by dusk, and that was when Corvin found us.

Later, I would understand that he'd been tracking us since the burning of Hollowmere—that he'd let Osk's escape go unpursued, that he'd dismissed half his soldiers with excuses about garrison rotations, that he'd been riding alone or nearly alone through a country coming apart at its seams, following us not like a hunter but like a man following a rumor of hope. But at the time, all I knew was that we rounded a shoulder of black rock above the Anvil road and there were torches below us, a dozen riders drawn up across the trail, and at their front, unhelmeted, torchlight on his face, the crown prince of Thornfell.

Maret's sword was out before his name finished leaving my mouth.

"No one moves," she said, in the voice she used on storms and mutineers. "Zeph. Get behind me."

"He's not here to kill us," I said.

"You can't know that."

"I know." And I did, in the way I'd come to know things through Vess—not sight exactly, more like temperature. The riders below radiated fear and exhaustion and something else, something taut and desperate, and Corvin most of all. He looked up at me across forty feet of darkening air, and he didn't look like the man who'd burned Hollowmere. He looked like a man who'd watched Hollowmere burn and hadn't slept since.

"Zephyrine Delacroix," he called up. His voice carried strangely in the pass; the rock ate some of it and gave back an echo that sounded older than he was. "Food-taster. Vessel. Whatever they're calling you now."

"Taster's fine," I said. "I worked hard for it."

Something flickered across his face that was almost a smile. Almost. "My father sent me to kill you. Sixty men and a warden-blade. I have twelve men and no blade, and half of my twelve are here because they'd rather see the far side of this country than defend it." He dismounted, slowly, hands empty and visible, and walked forward until Maret's knuckles whitened on her grip. "I'm not asking you to surrender. I'm asking you to listen. Please." And the *please* cracked down the middle, and I understood that this was a man who had run out of everything except this.

Isolde urged her horse down the scree before anyone could stop her—which was characteristic, and also the reason Maret had voted twice to ransom her.

"Corvin." She swung down in front of him. Up close, in the torchlight, the resemblance between them was startling and sad: same jaw, same dark brows, hers worn open and his worn shut. "You came alone."

"I came mostly alone." He looked at her for a long moment. "You look terrible."

"You look worse."

"Prison agrees with no one," Osk offered piously, from above.

Corvin ignored him. "Izzy." His voice dropped low enough that I had to lean forward in the saddle to hear. "Father is going to seal the Veil. Not open it—*seal* it. He's gathered the rites. He means to burn every awakened god back into the stone and every vessel with them, and when he's done, Thornfell goes back to being a jar with a lid, and everyone inside keeps living their little lives not knowing they're in a jar, forever." He swallowed. "I wanted that. Gods help me, a month ago I wanted that. Order. Walls. A country that stays put."

"And now?" Isolde asked quietly.

"Now the walls are falling on people." He turned and pointed back down the valley we'd climbed out of, toward a darkness that was darker than night—the seam where the village of Grey Anvil's lower town had stood that morning. "Four hundred people in that lower town. Gone this afternoon. Not dead—worse than dead, unwritten. Their own mothers can't remember their faces. I rode through and a woman asked me if I'd seen her son and described him perfectly and then forgot the description while I stood there. Her *own mouth* forgot." His voice shook. "And I thought—if the Veil closes again, that's what we all are. Just waiting our turn to be forgotten by ourselves."

Nobody spoke. Above us the stars came out, and I noticed—with a lurch that had nothing to do with the horse—that there were fewer of them than there should have been. Thornfell had never had many stars; the Veil saw to that. But tonight even those few were guttering, dimming, going out like candles in a draft as the sky over us forgot it had ever agreed to hold them.

Vess pressed upward through my chest, warm and urgent. *He speaks true. The king's rite would not save them. Nothing sealed saves anything. It only postpones the forgetting and calls the postponement mercy.*

"Your Highness," I said, and Corvin looked up at me, and I saw him brace himself for whatever a vessel of a god was going to say. What came out was just the taster. Just me. "You want us to close the door."

"Yes." He said it fast, before courage left him. "Not the way Father means it—not a sealing, not a sacrifice. I mean—don't open it. Turn around. Take your crew, take Izzy, take the gods' little spark you're carrying, and let the country keep its shape. If the door opens, Thornfell ends. Everyone who isn't through it when the last god leaves—everyone still inside—goes with it. There are *millions* of people inside, Delacroix. Farmers. Children. People who have never done anything to anybody except live here." He spread his hands. "I know what Father is. I've known since Hollowmere. I'm not defending him—I'll help you stop him, if it comes to that. But stopping him isn't the same as opening the door. Find another way. Any other way. Buy the country time. Move the door. Slow the waking. *Anything.* Because if you walk through that door and it opens, you aren't saving the faithful. You're saving a handful of smugglers and abandoning a nation."

The words settled over us like snow. Down the line, his twelve soldiers sat their horses and said nothing, and I realized they'd heard every word, and agreed, and followed him anyway into the dark after a vessel they'd been ordered to kill. That kind of loyalty doesn't come from orders.

Maret broke the silence first, because of course she did. "And how many people fit through a door, Highness? Before it shuts?"

"I don't know."

"How long does it stay open?"

"I don't know."

"Who holds it open?"

"I—" Corvin stopped. Looked at the ground. "I don't know that either."

"Then you're not asking us to close the door," Maret said, not unkindly, which for Maret was a register she saved for funerals and foaling mares. "You're asking us to answer questions nobody's answered yet, and you'd rather we closed it than guessed wrong. I understand that. I've captained ships. Guessing wrong sinks everybody." She sheathed her sword, deliberately, so he could watch her do it. "But I'll tell you what I know. I've seen this crew carry people who had no business being carried. Osk, there, weighed less than the bell. We carried him anyway. We don't leave people behind. We also don't leave *everybody* behind, which is what your father's plan amounts to. So we're going to that door, and we're going to find out what it costs and who fits, and if there's a way to hold it wide enough for millions, we'll find that too—or die finding out. That's the whole of our policy. It's not much of one, but it's honest."

"She's right," Isolde said. "Corvin. Come with us."

He flinched. "I can't. My men—" A glance back at the twelve. "Someone has to get the villages ahead of the seams. Warn them. Point them at the heart-road. I can't open doors, but I can move people, and I'm—" the bitter half-smile again— "apparently the only royal left willing to be useful."

"You were always the dutiful one," Isolde said softly.

"One of us had to be."

They clasped arms, brother and sister, in the torchlight, and it occurred to me that I was watching the House of Warden dissolve in real time—not with thunder, but with a handshake and a goodbye, which is how most kingdoms actually end.

Corvin turned to me last. Up close his eyes were bloodshot and steady. "Delacroix. When you reach the door—and you'll reach it—ask it my question. Who fits. Ask it loudly. And if the answer is 'no one,' then close the damn thing and curse me for asking."

"If the answer is 'no one,'" I said, "then closing it changes nothing except how long the forgetting takes. You know that."

"I know." He exhaled. "Ask it anyway. Hope's not a strategy. But it's a good pace-setter."

He mounted, raised one hand to his men, and paused—looked back once at Isolde, once at me, and once, oddly, at the space just over my shoulder, where Vess sat coiled and listening. Then he wheeled his horse downhill into the dark, and his twelve riders followed, and the torches shrank and shrank and became stars, and then became fewer stars, because even torches forget.

*He will hold the roads as long as the roads hold,* Vess said quietly, inside my ribs. *Few kings have done more with less.*

"He's not a king," I said aloud, nudging my horse onward. "That's rather the point."

Above us, the guttering stars wheeled slow and dim. Ahead, through the pass at Grey Anvil, the mountains leaned together like conspirators, and somewhere past them, at the center of a country that was forgetting itself one valley at a time, a door waited with our names on it—and a price tag none of us had read yet.

Maret lifted her fist. We rode.
