## 17. Maret's Price

The door was smaller than Maret had expected.

She'd carried the idea of it for thirty years, the way you carry a stone in your boot—always there, always shaping how you walked. In her mind it had grown enormous: a wall of night, a wound in the world, something that would need a ship's anchor chain and a prayer to hold open. Instead it stood in a hollow at the heart of Thornfell, no bigger than a garden gate, framed in silver birch that grew with their roots braided together like fingers laced. Through it she could see green. Actual green. Grass under an unbroken sky, rolling away toward hills that had no business existing this close to the Veil.

"Well," said Pip. "That's it? I forged a coronation seal once that was more impressive."

"It's not the size," Tam said. "It's the toll."

"Thank you, Tam, very comforting."

"They're the same person," Osk murmured. "They've been finishing each other's sentences since Hollowmere. It's worse than before."

Maret let them argue. She was looking at the door, and the door, in some way she couldn't name and didn't want to, was looking back.

Vess stood beside it—or Vess stood *near* it, which was different. The god of thresholds wore Zephyrine's body like a coat two sizes too large, and even now, even after everything, Maret's eye kept sliding off him. Not her eye. Her other one. The one that remembered.

"You've all felt it," Zephyrine-Vess said quietly. "What the crossing costs. I won't dress it up. The door takes memory. Not randomly—it takes what binds you hardest to what you're leaving. And someone must remain on this side until the last soul has passed through. Someone must hold it."

"Hold it how?" Isolde asked. She'd been quiet most of the day, riding behind them with her brother's soldiers' cloaks still smelling of smoke on her shoulders. "Hold it like a door, or hold it like—" She stopped. She'd seen what happened to things the Veil forgot. They'd all watched a valley unwrite itself two days ago, whole orchards going soft and gray like fruit left too long in a cellar.

"Like a warden holds," Zephyrine said. "But freely. That's the difference. That's the only difference, and it's everything."

"And the memories," Corvin said. His voice was flat. He'd been flat since the burning of Hollowmere, since Isolde had stood in front of him and he had chosen, finally, at the last ugly moment, to lower his sword instead of raising it. "Full price. What does that mean?"

Zephyrine turned to look at Maret.

He didn't say anything. He just looked at her, and there was something in his face—not pity, worse than pity, *recognition*—and Maret understood that Vess had told him. Or shown him. Gods of thresholds probably kept accounts. Probably had ledgers older than kingdoms, and somewhere in hers was a line item from thirty years ago, written small, in a child's hand.

"No," Maret said aloud, to no one, to herself.

"Maret?" Pip's voice went careful. Pip's voice always went careful when it mattered; the girl could forge a duke's signature without a tremor but couldn't lie to family to save her life.

"I said no." Maret squared her shoulders and turned to face them all, because if she was going to do this, she was going to do it standing up, facing them, like a captain. "You're all thinking it, so we may as well have it out. Someone has to stay. And I'm telling you now, before anyone gets noble about it—that's my post. It was always going to be my post. I've been to this door before."

* * *

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Then everyone started talking at once.

"—been here *before*, what does that mean—"

"—you never said—"

"—thirty years, Maret, thirty years you sailed with us and never—"

"*One at a time.*" Maret put two fingers in her mouth and whistled, sharp as a bosun's call, and they shut up, because some habits outlive countries. "Sit down. All of you. Osk, there's a fallen birch, make yourself useful."

They sat. Even Corvin sat, folding himself down onto the grass with the awkwardness of a man who'd been taught to sit on thrones and hadn't yet learned grass. Isolde sat next to him. Their shoulders almost touched and didn't.

Maret stood. She wasn't sure she could tell this sitting down.

"I was born in Saltmere," she said. "Fishing village, east coast, before the Veil started eating the coast. You won't find it on any map now. Pip and Tam would know why." She nodded at the twins, who had gone very still. "When I was seven, the herring stopped coming. Then the boats started coming back empty. Then the boats started coming back without the men who sailed them. My mother took me inland, following stories her own mother told her, about a door in the mountains. About a way out."

"The shrine-roads," Osk said softly. "The old pilgrim ways. I've found fragments in the defrocked archives—"

"Aye, your fragments. We walked them. Mother and me. Took us a season. And when we got here—" Maret gestured at the hollow, at the birches, at the small bright impossible door, "—there were others. A dozen families. Maybe more. And a priest, or something like one, who knew the rite. He told us the truth straight, which I've respected ever since: the door opens, but it takes payment. Memory. What you love best about where you came from, it eats first."

"My mother went through first." Maret's voice did something strange on the second word, and she let it. "I watched her walk through, and I watched her turn around on the far side, and I watched her *not know why she'd turned around*. Just stood there, looking at a door, like a woman waiting for a cart that's already gone. Then she walked on into the green, and she didn't look back, because there was nothing left in her that knew to look back for."

"What did you pay?" Isolde asked. Quietly. Like asking a wounded soldier where it hurt.

"My eye." Maret touched the patch, an old habit, an old comfort. "Not the eye itself—the door doesn't want flesh. It wanted the memory of my mother's face. It took it out through the eye, the priest said, because that's where I'd kept it. Every face I've seen since, I see with one eye that remembers faces and one that doesn't. You get used to it. You learn to draw maps of people instead of pictures. Voice, hands, the way they stand. I could pick any of you out of a crowd blindfolded, but ask me to describe Pip's face and I'll give you her laugh instead."

Pip made a small sound. Tam put a hand on her shoulder.

"So when I say I've paid before," Maret went on, "understand what I'm saying. I know the weight of it. I was seven years old and I paid a child's share—a *partial* toll, just enough to cross, because the priest rationed us, families first, and children paid least. I've owed the door the balance for thirty years." She looked at each of them in turn. "And I'm telling you the account comes due now, and I intend to settle it myself, and none of you are going to talk me out of it, so save your breath for walking."

* * *

They talked her out of it for four hours.

Not continuously—they broke for water, for a cold meal of hard bread and dried plums, for Tam pacing the perimeter out of pure nerves—but the argument ran like a tide, in and out, wearing at her.

Osk led with theology. "The rite requires a willing warden, yes, but the old texts suggest willingness must be *informed*. You're deciding based on a child's memory of the toll—"

"I informed myself for thirty years, priest. Every time I looked at a face and got a voice instead."

"Then let me go," Osk said simply. "I'm the one who believes. Let belief pay."

"Belief doesn't hold doors. Stubbornness does. I'm the stubbornest person on this crew and we both know it."

Corvin tried strategy. "The king is coming. My father will be here within a day, maybe less. Whoever holds the door will be holding it against *him*. You need someone who knows how he fights."

"And that's you, princeling?"

"That's me," Corvin said, and didn't flinch. "Let me stay. I'm the one he wants. I'm the blood he needs. If the wardenship runs in our line, then I can turn it inside-out from this side better than anyone—you saw what Zephyrine did, but I was *raised* on the rites, I know their shape—"

"You're twenty-three years old and your father is a monster wearing a crown, and you want to volunteer to hold a door against him alone." Maret shook her head. "Noble. Stupid, but noble. Your sister would kill me."

"My sister has opinions," Corvin admitted.

"My sister," said Isolde, "is right here, and agrees with Corvin about exactly nothing except this." She stood. "The door needs a holder who isn't needed on the other side. On the far side, they'll need builders. Leaders. People who can hold a frightened country of refugees together—and whether we like it or not, the refugees will follow the bloodline that caged them unless someone gives them something better to follow. Corvin and I renounce the throne, fine, but renouncing doesn't stop people from kneeling. *Staying* stops it. One of us stays."

"Absolutely not," Corvin said. "Both of us don't stay. One heir survives, minimum. That's arithmetic."

"Oh, wonderful, arithmetic, that's how our family always expressed love—"

"It's how our family survived—"

"Your family ate gods," Zephyrine said mildly, from beside the door, and they both shut up.

Which left the crew. Which left Pip and Tam, who hadn't argued strategy or theology at all, who had spent four hours doing something much more dangerous: making plans.

"We've been talking," Pip said.

"Don't," Maret said.

"You don't even know what—"

"You've got the same look you had in Corvasser when you decided to forge the harbor-master's seal without telling me. Don't."

Tam sighed. "Fine. Out loud, then, since you'll drag it out of us anyway. Twins pay as twins. That's the whole point of being twins—we're redundant. If one of us crosses and forgets the other, the other remembers enough for both. So one of us stays, one goes, and the family stays whole either way. Flip a coin."

"No," Maret said.

"You don't get to—"

"*No.*" And her voice cracked across the hollow like the bell they'd carried half across Thornfell, the cracked bell, the broken relic that had rung true at every threshold that mattered. "You're nineteen. Both of you. Nineteen years old, and you think forgetting is arithmetic. You think you can split a person down the middle and the halves will add up." She was on her feet without remembering standing. "I've *seen* the far side of a partial toll. I've lived thirty years watching people love me and wondering which parts of me they're loving, because I can't remember my own mother's face, and you want to volunteer for that at nineteen to spare me at forty-seven? No. Sit down. Both of you. That's an order."

They sat. Pip was crying, silently, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like the tears were a forgery she'd been caught producing.

And then Zephyrine spoke, and the whole argument changed shape.

"There's another way," he said. "I found it in the rite. Vess showed me. The door doesn't care *who* holds it. But the toll—it weighs. It takes proportionally. A full price from someone who's already paid partial..." He looked at Maret, and his face was gentle and terrible. "It might take nothing at all. Or it might take everything. There's no record. No one's ever come back to the door twice."

"Then I'll be the first," Maret said. "I've been first at worse."

"You don't know what 'everything' means—"

"Aye, I do." She turned to face him fully. "Everything means all of you. Every smuggled cargo, every storm we rode out, every time Pip cheated at cards and Tam let her, every sermon Osk whispered over the dead when he thought no one was listening, every one of Isolde's terrible jokes, every time the princeling pretended he wasn't fond of us. Everything means I cross that door knowing none of it, and you lot spend the rest of your lives being strangers to me." Her jaw set. "I know exactly what it means. I've known since I was seven years old, watching my mother look through me like weather. And I'm telling you—I'd rather be a stranger to you than bury one of you. That's the whole speech. That's all there is."

Nobody spoke.

The wind moved through the birches, and through the door, and on the far side the green grass bent and lifted, and somewhere beyond the hills a bird sang in a country that had never heard of Thornfell.

* * *

It was Isolde who broke the silence, because of course it was. The princess had a genius for the ill-timed necessary thing.

"You're wrong about one thing," she said.

Maret raised an eyebrow.

"You said you'd rather be a stranger to us than bury one of us. But that's not the choice." Isolde stepped forward, chin up, in the exact posture she used for negotiating with people who outranked her, which lately was nobody. "The choice is: who loves this crew enough to stay, knowing the cost? And captain, I've watched you for months. You count heads. Every camp, every road, every ambush—you count us, and you don't sleep until the number's right. You'd hold that door and count us through, every single one, and you'd do it smiling, and then you'd walk into the green knowing nothing and we'd have to introduce ourselves to you like salesmen." Her voice wobbled and steadied. "That's not sacrifice. That's theft. You'd be stealing our chance to grieve you properly."

"Grieve me? I'd be right there—"

"As a stranger! Worse—as a stranger who used to be our captain, and doesn't know it!" Pip was on her feet again. "Do you know what we'd be carrying? Crossing into a new country and having to tell *you* who you are? Having you look at us like—like customers?"

"Better than looking at your graves," Maret snapped.

"We're not planning to die!"

"Nobody plans it! Brannoc didn't plan it! Osk didn't plan to be taken at Hollowmere! Nobody *plans*—" Maret stopped. Breathed. The old discipline, the bosun's discipline: panic kills more sailors than storms. "Nobody plans it," she said again, quieter. "But somebody pays. That's what I learned at seven. Somebody always pays, and the ones who love you best volunteer first, and the trick of command is choosing who before they can choose themselves."

"Then here's my choice," Zephyrine said.

Everyone turned. He had been quiet a long time, standing by the door with Vess moving behind his eyes like a lamp behind glass, and when he spoke there was a doubled quality to his voice, taster and god together.

"I've tasted nine years of poison so a king wouldn't have to. I swallowed death in small doses until death got bored of me. And I have spent this entire journey learning that the things I did to survive made me the only door the gods had left." He smiled, tired and crooked. "I am, by every measure that matters, already a threshold. Let me be one properly. Let me hold it. And the rest of you—" his gaze went around the circle, and Maret saw his eyes shine and his mouth keep steady, "—the rest of you go through, and live loudly, and build something worth the price."

"No," said Maret.

"No," said Pip and Tam together.

"No," said Osk, gently, "because the rite names a warden, and wardenship ran in royal blood, and Zeph, love, you're not—"

"He's more warden than any of us," Isolde said. "He's held more thresholds than—"

"*Enough.*"

The word came from the door itself. From Vess, speaking through no throat at all, resonating up through the ground, through the birch roots, through the soles of their boots. The grass shivered. The cracked bell, hanging from Maret's pack, rang once, unprompted, a clear high note.

All of them froze.

"You argue like a family," Vess said, and the god's voice was warm, and sad, and old beyond counting. "Thirty years I have watched this door. Kings came with chains and called it duty. Priests came with knives and called it mercy. Armies came with fire and called it law. And you come with *arguments*—each of you begging to pay another's debt." A pause, long as a season. "In nine thousand years, no one has ever done that before. Do you understand? No one. The door has opened eleven times. Eleven times, and every crossing before yours was bought with someone else's blood, someone else's chains, someone else's *conscription*. And you stand here fighting over the honor of the toll like it's a birthday cake."

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

"So hear the terms," Vess said. "The door will not choose. The door has never chosen. Choose yourselves, tonight, and mean it—and know that whichever of you remains, the others will carry them across. That is the oldest law of thresholds, older than me: what passes through a door together arrives together. Whatever the toll takes from the one who stays... the ones who cross can give it back. Piece by piece. Story by story. If they choose to. If they love enough to do the work."

Maret felt something shift in her chest, old and rusted and suddenly loose.

"Give it back," she repeated. "Memories can't—"

"Nothing is ever truly lost past a threshold," Vess said. "Only *unheld*. And anything unheld can be held again by another's hands." The presence withdrew slightly, the pressure easing, and Zephyrine swayed, and Isolde caught his elbow. "Now. Argue until dawn if you must. It seems to be how you say what you mean."

* * *

They argued until dawn. But it was a different argument now, and by the end it sounded less like a fight and more like something else—something Maret had no clean name for, though she'd heard its shape in rigging-song and funeral feasts and the particular way Pip and Tam bickered when neither wanted to admit they were scared.

Because here was the truth Maret kept circling and refusing to land on: Vess had changed the mathematics. If the crossing could be given back—if the crew could carry her memories across and return them, story by story, on the far side—then staying wasn't erasure anymore. It was a loan. A hard one, a cruel one, but a loan.

And still. Still.

Near midnight, while the others slept in shifts, Tam came and sat beside her at the dying fire. Neither spoke for a while. That was the thing about Tam—knew when silence was the honest option.

"Pip's asleep," Tam said finally. "Crying in her sleep, but asleep."

"She'll hate me in the morning."

"She'll hate you for a week. Then she'll forge you a new identity with a tragic backstory and feel better." Tam poked the fire. "Captain. Ask me the real question."

Maret looked at the kid—nineteen, soot under the fingernails, the forger's calluses, the face she knew by voice and hands and the set of the shoulders because one eye had never once shown it to her. Nineteen. Younger than her toll was old.

"Why didn't you argue harder?" Maret asked. "Either of you. You wanted to stay. You had the coin-flip plan. Why fold?"

"Because Pip figured it out. Before Vess even said it." Tam fed a twig to the fire, watching it catch. "If you stay and forget us, and we cross and remember you—we get to give you back. Every day, a piece. First your name. Then your voice. Then the story of the storm off Corvasser, and how you laughed at Osk's sermons, and how you counted heads. We'd get to fall in love with you twice, Captain. Once for us, and once for you." Tam shrugged, and the shrug wobbled at the edges. "Pip says that's not a sacrifice, that's a courtship. She says we should be so lucky."

Maret laughed—short, rough, surprised out of her. And then, because the fire was low and the kid was young and the hour was honest, she said the thing she'd sworn not to say.

"I don't want to forget your faces."

Tam went very still.

"I've spent thirty years with one eye that keeps faces and one that won't. Yours and Pip's are the first faces in thirty years that both my eyes agreed on." Maret stared into the coals. "And tomorrow I'm supposed to volunteer to lose them anyway. To lose all of it. And the god says you can give it back, and maybe that's true, and maybe it's a kindness gods tell mortals to make the toll weigh less. Either way, someone stays, and I have commanded people for twenty years, and I will not send a child through that door to do a captain's job."

"Then we won't vote," Tam said quietly. "We'll do what crews do."

"Which is?"

"Cry about it, and then follow the captain." Tam stood, brushed off grass, and paused. "For what it's worth—whatever gets decided at dawn—Pip and I already wrote it down. Both versions. Forged documents, sealed, dated. Whichever one happens, we'll swear it was unanimous and legally binding." A grin, quick and crooked in the firelight. "You raised us on paperwork, Captain. We fight with what we have."

Maret threw a pinecone at her. Tam caught it, pocketed it, and went back to her bedroll, whistling.

Dawn came gray and slow through the birches. The crew gathered at the door—Isolde with her arms crossed, Corvin beside her wearing his stubbornness like borrowed armor, Osk with his cracked bell in his hands, Pip and Tam shoulder to shoulder, Zephyrine pale and steady with Vess just beneath his skin.

Maret stood before them all, and found, to her complete lack of surprise, that she had prepared a speech, and that it was a good one, and that it didn't matter, because Pip was already talking.

"Right," Pip announced, brandishing a folded paper like a warrant. "Motion from the crew. Proposed and drafted at approximately midnight, witnessed by both twins, notarized by Osk under duress—"

"You weren't under duress," Osk said.

"I was emotionally coerced, which counts. Motion reads as follows: whereas our captain has already paid partial toll at this door, and whereas the door accepts prior payments toward balance, and whereas—Tam, read the whereas part, you wrote the good whereas—"

"Whereas the captain's remaining balance constitutes a *debt owed by the door*, not by the captain," Tam recited, deadpan, "we propose the crew assume the debt collectively, in installments, payable in memory, story, and general stubbornness, beginning immediately upon crossing and continuing for the rest of all our lives."

There was a pause.

"That's not how debts work," Maret said.

"It's exactly how debts work," Pip said. "We're smugglers. We invented creative accounting. Sign here."

And Maret looked at the paper—genuine ink, genuine seals, Pip's flawless forgery of Maret's own signature already waiting at the bottom, presumptuous and perfect and so entirely *them* that her throat closed up—and she understood, finally, that she had lost. That she'd lost the moment she pulled five strangers out of a flood and called them crew. That love, in this ridiculous family, was expressed as argument, and arguments ended in signatures, and signatures meant you belonged to each other whether you liked it or not.

"One condition," she managed.

"Name it."

"When you give it back—my mother's face first." Her voice held. Barely. "I want to know what I've been missing."

Pip's grin faltered into something softer. "First thing," she promised. "Before your name, even."

"Before my name?"

"Faces before names, Captain. That's the order we agreed on." Tam was smiling too, wet-eyed. "You'll wake up on the far side and the first thing you'll know is a woman's face, laughing, saying your name in a way you won't recognize yet—and then we'll come find you, and finish the job."

Maret signed. Her real signature, over Pip's forgery, the two marks nearly identical, which Pip took as the highest compliment of her career.

And it was settled—almost, mostly, in the way of things that are settled until the moment they aren't—when the ground trembled, and the birds went silent, and far to the south, over the ridgeline, a light rose that was not dawn.

Zephyrine staggered, pressed a hand to his temple, and said, in a voice that was half his own and half something vast:

"He's coming. The king is coming—and he's brought the Veil with him."

Maret looked at the door—small, silver-birched, impossibly green—and at her crew, her family, her witnesses, and felt the old captain's calm settle over her like a coat.

"Right," she said. "Everyone knows their jobs. We hold the threshold till the last soul crosses—and whatever comes through that southern ridge, it does *not* touch this door."

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, the cracked bell began to ring.
