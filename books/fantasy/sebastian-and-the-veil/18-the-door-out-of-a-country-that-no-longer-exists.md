## 18. The Door Out of a Country That No Longer Exists

The door was smaller than the end of the world should have been.

That was Zephyrine's first thought, standing in the bowl of a valley that no longer appeared on any map—Pip and Tam had checked three times, holding their self-eating charts up to the grey light and laughing in the way people laugh when the alternative is screaming. The Veil-door was a gap between two standing stones, hip-high, wide enough for one person at a time if that person apologized to the stones first. Through it: green. Not Thornfell-green, which was the green of things growing inside a jar, but an open, unguarded, ridiculous green, spilling light like a spilled cup.

"It's a garden gate," Tam said, disgusted. "The door out of reality is a *garden gate*."

"It's a threshold," Vess said, through Zephyrine's mouth, before Zephyrine could stop it. His voice came out doubled, his own and something older underneath, like two bells struck together. "All doors are small from the outside. That's how you know they're honest."

Zephyrine swallowed the god back down and rubbed his throat. Nine years of tasting had taught him every sensation a body could produce, and channeling Vess felt most like swallowing lightning and then being politely asked to hold it in his cheeks.

"Sorry," he croaked. "He does that."

"He also called my bell honest," Maret said, "which is more than I've managed." She stood at the threshold with her cracked talisman in her fist, one eye on the door and the other eye's absence aimed at the valley mouth, where the road they'd come by was busy forgetting itself. You could watch it happen now without the twins' maps. The ridgeline to the east simply wasn't, and then the memory of it wasn't either, and Maret frowned and said, "I used to smuggle brandy over that ridge," and then, ten minutes later, "Did there used to be a ridge?"

"There did," Pip said gently. "Brandy and all."

"Damn," said Maret. "It was good brandy."

* * *

They had perhaps a day. Vess said so, and Vess had stopped lying to them somewhere around Hollowmere, which Zephyrine had decided to take as a mark of character.

"The country is coming apart faster than we planned," the god had said, sitting behind Zephyrine's eyes like a lodger who paid rent in visions. "When the last of us crosses, Thornfell will not fall. Falling implies a direction. It will simply have never been."

"And everyone still inside?" Isolde had asked, very quietly, the way she asked all her worst questions.

"Will have never been either."

So they'd sent word down every smuggler road Osk's contacts could reach—runners, pigeons, one extremely fast mule named Duchess—and the refugees had come. All night they came, out of valleys that were already half rumor: farmers with their chickens in baskets, an entire convent of exiled nuns singing under their breath because loud hymns were still illegal and old habits die hard, a tax collector who kept apologizing, children asleep across pack-saddles. They came down into the forgetting valley and saw the little door and the green beyond it, and they did not cheer. They got in line. There is a particular kind of quiet that people make when they have understood, completely, what is behind them.

Zephyrine walked the line with a ladle of broth, because if the world was ending you might as well feed people while it did. Osk moved beside him murmuring blessings—not the outlawed kind, just ordinary ones, *may your feet stay warm, may the mule behave*—and the nuns smiled at him like a lost lamb returned, and Osk went pink to the ears.

"You're blushing at nuns," Zephyrine said.

"I'm *defrocked*, not dead," Osk hissed. "Some habits of the heart survive defrocking. Unlike my dignity, which died at Hollowmere."

"It didn't—" Zephyrine started, and then stopped, because Osk's wrists still bore the marks of the fort, and some jokes are bridges built only so both people can cross them once. He put his hand briefly on the priest's shoulder instead. Osk nodded. That was enough.

At the head of the line, Isolde was doing what she had done for two days straight: organizing. She had a list. Of course she had a list. The princess who had burned her inheritance in a single sentence—*we renounce it, Corvin and I, we renounce the wardenship and everything it ate*—had immediately produced a charcoal stub and begun triaging an apocalypse.

"Widows and children first, then the infirm, then everyone else by village. No pushing, no hoarding, and if one more man tells me women and children first while shoving past a grandmother I will have him flogged with his own belt." She looked up at Zephyrine. "You're smiling."

"I'm watching a princess herd a nation through a keyhole."

"I'm not a princess. I renounced it."

"You're a princess with excellent penmanship. Renouncing doesn't undo twenty years of training."

She almost smiled back. Then her gaze went past him, up the valley, to where the air had begun to do something wrong—and the smile died in both of them at once.

* * *

The king arrived at dusk, and he did not arrive like a man.

There was no army. There didn't need to be. The light bent toward him as he came down the valley road, and the road remembered itself just long enough to carry him, and then forgot again behind his heels like a servant erasing footprints. He wore his crown and his grey traveling cloak and his face, and all three of those things were wrong in the same way a mask is wrong: fitted well, worn recently, belonging to someone else.

Zephyrine had tasted for this man for nine years. He knew the king's palate better than the king's own tongue—the preference for bitter greens, the hatred of honey, the way he took his wine. And looking at him now, Zephyrine understood with a lurch that he had never once seen him eat with pleasure. Wardens didn't eat. They *fed*. Every staged poisoning, every survival celebrated in the chronicles, every toast to the king's miraculous constitution—it had all been meals, and the thing wearing the crown had dined on the gods' captivity for a generation, and now the gods were leaving and it had come to close the pantry door.

"Hold the line," Maret said quietly, and the crew moved without being told. Osk to the left with his cracked bell raised. Pip and Tam flanking the door itself, arms linked across the threshold like the world's smallest, most profane gatehouse. Isolde stepped forward, alone, into her father's path.

"Father," she said.

"Isolde." The king's voice was warm. It had always been warm. That was the horror of it; it had never once been cold, even ordering executions, even burning shrines. Warmth was the uniform. "Step away from the door, child. What comes through it will unmake you."

"What comes through it is *us*." Her voice didn't shake. Zephyrine, who had watched her interrogate archivists and bargain with soldiers and fail beautifully to save Osk, thought: *she learned that steadiness from him, and she's using it against him, and maybe that's the truest revenge there is.* "You fed on them for thirty years. You let a whole country forget why the walls were built. And now that the prisoners are walking out, you want to weld the cell shut and call it salvation."

"I want my people to *exist*," the king said, and here was the terrible thing: he meant it. Whatever he was, whatever wore him, the wanting was real. "Without the Veil, Thornfell is nothing. Without the Veil, none of you are anything. I am offering you continuity, daughter. Kneel, and be a warden after me. Your brother refused. Look what refusing cost him."

"Corvin chose the truth," Isolde said. "You'd know, if you'd ever tried it."

Something moved behind the king's face then—not anger. Hunger adjusting its grip. And Zephyrine felt Vess rise in him like water finding a crack, and heard the god say, in the doubled bell-voice, in front of everyone:

"*Warden. You sealed us in a jar and drank the light. Did you think the lid would hold forever? Did you think jars don't dream of breaking?*"

The king looked at Zephyrine—at the vessel, not the man—with an expression of such ancient, patient contempt that Zephyrine's knees tried to resign.

"Little taster," the king said. "Do you know how many poisons I've watched you swallow for me? Ninety-one dishes. I counted. Each one a small loyalty, freely given. You built your whole self out of service to my table." He smiled. "And now you stand at my door and call yourself free. Nothing that has eaten from my hand is free. Step aside, vessel, or I will close this door with you poured into the mortar."

"Ninety-two," Zephyrine said.

The king blinked.

"You missed one. Chapter fifteen of your own ledger. The quail in ash, the night the ambassador from—well, from nowhere, actually, since nowhere doesn't exist, which I suppose explains the recipe." Zephyrine's heart was hammering and his mouth had gone dry and he kept talking anyway, because nine years of invisibility had taught him exactly one weapon and he intended to spend all of it. "You were distracted that night. You looked *afraid*. I've tasted fear in a hundred mouths and I know what it does to the appetite. You were afraid of the door then too. You've been afraid your whole reign. So spare me the mortar speech, Your Majesty. You're not closing a door. You're bolting yourself in with everything you starved."

For one heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—the mask slipped, and what looked out of the king's face was old and cramped and *starving*, and Zephyrine thought: *there you are.*

Then the king raised his hand, and the valley began to die on purpose.

* * *

It was not like the slow forgetting of the last days. This was deliberate, a hand smoothing wet sand. The standing stones around the door leaned. The line of refugees screamed and surged forward, and Maret roared "THROUGH, THROUGH, KEEP MOVING, YOU'RE NOT PAID TO SIGHTSEE"—they weren't paid at all, but nobody argued—and the crew held.

Vess came up through Zephyrine like a sunrise through a broken roof.

Later he would remember it in pieces: the taste of ozone flooding his mouth until every breath was a swallowed storm. Light running down his arms in branching veins, lightning drawn gentle, lightning *asked* rather than taken. His own voice gone entirely, replaced by the doubled bell, saying words that were not orders but were somehow impossible to disobey:

"*Threshold. Hold.*"

And Zephyrine held. He planted himself in the gap between the stones, arms spread, and became the hinge. The land pulled at him—the king pulling, unwriting the ground under the door, trying to erase the threshold along with everything else—and Vess pushed back through Zephyrine's body, and the battle happened in a place with no geography at all, a place made of *between*: between stone and stone, between one step and the next, between the last moment a thing exists and the first moment it never did.

It hurt. He would be dishonest to pretend otherwise, and honesty was rather the theme of the day. It hurt like swallowing ninety-one poisons at once and having them all wake up and introduce themselves. Somewhere in the roar he heard Osk shouting scripture at the dark, wrong scripture, made-up scripture, *the Lord of Doorways walketh before thee and He is very fond of hinges*—and heard Pip yell "that's not canon!" and Osk yell back "NEITHER IS ANY OF THIS"—and absurdly, horribly, wonderfully, the laughter gave him strength the lightning couldn't.

Maret fought at the edge of the light with her cracked bell swinging, and where the bell's sound touched, the unwriting slowed, stuttered, forgot to forget. "Cracked things still ring!" she bellowed, to the king, to the dark, to God presumably. "Ask anyone who's married!"

Tam and Pip passed refugees through two at a time now, hauling children by the collar, shoving the tax collector through bodily while he apologized to everyone including the door. Isolde stood in the open with her father's attention full upon her, unarmed, unarmored, saying over and over in a clear carrying voice: "Look at what you're doing. *Look at it.* If there is anything left in there that ever loved us—look at it."

And the king faltered.

Only for a step. Only for a breath. But the unwriting paused, and in the pause Zephyrine—poured out, burning, hanging on the threshold like laundry on a line—saw the whole shape of the thing at last. Saw what the door wanted. Saw what it had wanted all along, why it stayed small and honest, why it demanded a toll: not cruelty. Balance. A door out of a dying country needed a weight on its side of the scale, or everything fell through at once, gods and monsters and the starving thing in the crown alike, out into the green and the open sky.

Someone had to be the weight. Someone had to hold the door until the last soul crossed, and pay for the holding.

"Oh," Zephyrine said, out loud, in his own voice, small under the thunder. "*Oh, that's what the toll is.*"

Vess, inside him, said nothing—which from Vess was a shout.

Beyond the king, far up the valley, a rider crested the remembering road at a dead gallop, and even dissolving, even half-blind with lightning, Zephyrine knew the set of those shoulders. Corvin. Coming fast. Coming with his sword drawn—or coming with something else; there was no time to tell, because the king recovered, and the dark came down like a lid, and the door groaned in its stones, and Zephyrine dug his heels into a country that was forgetting how to hold him and *held*.

"Maret!" he shouted, and his voice tore on the word. "How many left?"

She swept the line with her one eye. Behind the refugees: nothing. Behind them, where villages had been: smooth, patient, empty nothing, waiting to be introduced to itself.

"Forty," she called back. "Maybe fifty. And one prince at a hard gallop, and one king I'm about to hit with a bell."

"Keep them coming," Zephyrine said, and spread his arms wider, and burned.
