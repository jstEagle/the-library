## 19. Loyalty Over Truth

The door was a wound in the air.

That was the only way Zephyrine could describe it, standing at the heart of a country that was busy forgetting itself. Behind him, the last green valley of Thornfell unraveled like a sweater someone had found the loose thread of—hills going soft at their edges, rivers running backward into mist, whole orchards dissolving into the smell of apples and then not even that. Ahead of him, through the Veil-door, there was nothing he could see and everything he could feel: open sky, unprisoned ground, the vast unhurried breathing of a world that had never been nailed shut.

And between those two things, the king.

He had stopped pretending to be a man somewhere over the mountains. What wore the crown now was tall in the way that a shadow is tall when the sun is low—a king-shaped absence, seams of dark light splitting along his arms where the wardenship showed through, the way pus shows through skin. His voice came from everywhere, which was rude, and from nowhere, which was worse.

"You cannot leave," it said. "There is no *leave*. There is inside, and there is nothing."

"See, I've heard this pitch before," Maret called out, one hand on her sword, her remaining eye fixed on the thing that had been Isolde's father. "A smuggler tried it on me once. 'No way out of Thornfell, girl.' He's dead now. Mostly of his own bad business practices, but still."

The crew stood in a ragged line before the door, which was exactly what Zephyrine had asked them to do and exactly what they should not have agreed to do. Osk, back among them for less than a day, thin as a winter branch and grinning anyway. Pip and Tam shoulder to shoulder, each holding one half of the cracked bell-talisman. Maret. Isolde, soot-streaked, her borrowed soldier's coat burned off at one sleeve. Corvin, who an hour ago had been pointing a sword at them and now stood on their side of it, looking like a man who had jumped and was waiting to find out how deep the water was.

And Vess, burning through Zephyrine like dawn through a window, holding the door open with everything the little god of thresholds had—which, it turned out, was a great deal more than minor.

*The rite,* Vess said inside him, strained as a rope under load. *Zephyrine. The rite is waking up. It knows what it wants.*

Zephyrine felt it too. The door was not just a door; it was a lock, and locks are made by lockmakers, and the lockmakers had built into its bones a single instruction, old and patient and hungry: *a warden's blood, willingly given, seals the Veil forever.* He could feel the instruction the way you feel a splinter—not in the flesh, but underneath it, in the place where Vess spoke to him. The rite wanted blood. Warden's blood. Given freely. And then the lid would come down on the jar, and every god still sleeping under Thornfell would sleep there until the end of all things, and everyone who hadn't crossed yet—the villagers of Hollowmere, the faithful hiding in cellars, the thousands whose names were already fading from the maps—would be sealed in with them.

"No," Zephyrine said aloud, because some things you say out loud even when only a god can hear you.

*It is the only mechanism,* Vess said. *I did not build the lock. I only know doors.*

"Then we're going to have a conversation about mechanisms," Zephyrine said, "because I've spent nine years swallowing poison for a living, and if there's one thing I know, it's that anything can be turned inside-out if you hold it right."

* * *

Here is what nine years of tasting teaches you, and no one tells you because no one wants to hear it:

Poison is not evil. Poison is just chemistry with an opinion. Every toxin has a shape—an intention, almost—and the art of surviving one is not fighting the shape but understanding it well enough to step around it. The lightning-venom that had started all of this hadn't been beaten by force. It had been *listened to*, and then politely declined.

Zephyrine looked at the rite coiled in the door the same way.

It wanted warden's blood, willingly given. Fine. There were three people within shouting distance with warden's blood in their veins, and two of them were standing right here, and one of them was currently unraveling at the edges of reality while wearing his daughter's face as a memory.

But the rite didn't actually care about *sealing*, Zephyrine realized. That was just what the wardens had used it for. The rite cared about the transaction. Blood given freely, by a warden, at the threshold, in exchange for the door doing something permanent. The sealing was a *use*, not the *mechanism*. A knife doesn't care whether you carve bread or throats.

A lock doesn't care which way it turns.

"Isolde," he said. "Corvin. I need you to listen very carefully, because I'm about to ask your family to do the one thing it has never done in four hundred years."

"Give up power?" Isolde said. Her mouth was bleeding; she wiped it with the back of her hand and managed something that was almost her usual dry smile. "Darling, I've been practicing since chapter six."

"I don't understand," Corvin said, and he didn't, and Zephyrine loved him a little for saying so plainly, because the prince had spent his whole life pretending to understand everything.

"The door needs a warden's blood, willingly given, to make something permanent," Zephyrine said. "Your father—" the king-shape screamed, high and vast, and nobody flinched, which Zephyrine thought was very brave of everyone, "—your father wants to use that to seal the Veil forever. But the rite doesn't specify *what* it makes permanent. It specifies *who pays*. So we pay. And we choose what it buys."

"And what are we buying?" Maret asked.

Zephyrine looked at the door. At the green country beyond it, invisible and certain. At the valley behind them, coming apart at the hem.

"We're buying the end of the wardenship," he said. "Not the Veil. Not the door. The *wardenship*. The office. The feeding. Four hundred years of a family eating gods for breakfast. We turn the rite inside-out. Instead of locking the prison, we abolish the prison. Instead of a warden giving blood to keep the door shut, wardens give blood to make sure no one can ever be warden again."

Silence, except for the sound of a country forgetting itself, which sounds like snow falling in reverse.

"That will kill us," Corvin said quietly. "Won't it. The wardenship is—whatever Father is now, that's what the wardenship *is*. If you unmake it—"

"It might," Zephyrine admitted. "Or it might just burn it out of you and leave you human. I genuinely don't know. I want to be honest about that, because apparently I've become the sort of person who leads rebellions, and I'd like to do it with better ethics than the last lot."

"He's always like this," Tam told Corvin. "You get used to it. You don't get used to it, actually, but you stop expecting anything else."

*It may work,* Vess said inside him, softer now, curious despite the strain, because Vess was a god of thresholds and thresholds were made of *maybe*. *It has never been tried. But neither had the first sealing, and look how well that went.*

"High praise," Zephyrine murmured.

* * *

The king understood, then. Whatever was left of the man beneath the wardenship understood, and the thing above the man raged, and both of them came at once.

The shadow of him swept across the threshold-ground like a wave, and Maret met it with her sword and a curse in dockside cant that made Pip blush, and Osk planted himself with his broken bell held high, ringing its cracked note—*tong, tong, tong*—and where the crack sounded, the shadow frayed. The silenced gods' relic, still doing its work. Still remembering what it was for.

"NOW, YOUR HIGHNESSES!" Osk bellowed, in the full parade-ground voice of a man who had once shouted sermons to congregations of three hundred and had clearly missed it terribly.

Isolde stepped forward first. Of course she did.

She drew a knife—her own, small, practical, the kind of knife a princess keeps because her father taught her never to trust anyone—and opened her palm without ceremony, without drama, the way she did everything, as though she were signing a ledger.

"I am Isolde of House Vale," she said, and her voice carried the way voices carry in places where the world is thin, "last trueborn daughter of the wardens of Thornfell. My blood kept this prison. I give it freely to end it. Let the wardenship be *unmade*. Let no one feed on the gods again. Let the door be a door, and nothing else, forever."

Her blood fell on the threshold-stone, and the stone drank it, and the door shuddered like a living thing.

The king screamed. The scream was not entirely rage. Some of it, buried deep, in the part that had once been a father who taught his daughter knife-work, sounded almost like relief.

Corvin came next. He knelt, which Zephyrine hadn't expected, and cut his palm on Isolde's knife, and pressed it to the stone beside hers.

"I am Corvin of House Vale," he said, and his voice shook, and he let it shake, which took more courage than the cutting did. "Crown prince of nothing. I renounce the throne. I renounce the wardenship. I renounce—" and here he looked up, at the towering dark thing wearing his father, and his voice steadied into something terrible and clean, "—I renounce *you*. Let it end. Let it all end. Let my family's sin end with my family's name."

Two lines of warden's blood, willingly given, pooling together on the threshold-stone.

And Zephyrine, vessel of Vess, taster of poisons, keeper of other people's deaths, put his own hand over theirs—not warden's blood, just his, ordinary and stubborn—and said the words Vess fed him, syllable by syllable, in a language older than the kingdom, older than the prison, older than the idea of locking things away:

"*What was given to keep, return. What was taken to feed, release. Turn the key inside-out. Let the lock become a latch. Let the latch become an opening. Let the opening be free.*"

The rite woke all the way up.

* * *

Later—much later, in the green country, over many dinners—Pip would try to describe what happened next and fail, and Tam would try to correct her description and fail differently, and they would eventually settle on a version involving "a great deal of light" and "Maret swearing continuously," which was accurate as far as it went.

What happened was this: the rite turned.

Everyone felt it turn, the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expected. Four hundred years of a single instruction—*blood seals the Veil*—rotated on its axis, gently, enormously, like a ship changing course, until the instruction read the other way: *blood ends the wardenship.* And the wardenship, which was not really the royal family at all but a thing that had worn the royal family the way lichen wears a tree, felt itself abolished.

It did not go quietly. Nothing that has eaten gods for four centuries goes quietly.

The king-shape convulsed. The dark light in its seams flared white, then gold, then—this was the part Pip always insisted on, and Tam always backed her up—then it came apart into shapes. Shapes rising out of the ruin of it like sparks out of a bonfire: wings, antlers, eyes, rivers, a woman made of harvest-light, a hound the size of a chapel, small bright things like minnows made of morning. The gods it had eaten. Not destroyed, after all. Swallowed. Held. Prisoners in the warden's body, the way the gods of Thornfell were prisoners in the warden's country.

They rose out of the king the way the refugees were rising out of the king's country, and the symmetry of it broke something open in the sky.

The man came back last.

For one moment—Zephyrine was sure of it, and Isolde never contradicted him, and Corvin never spoke of it at all—for one moment there stood a tired old man in a burnt crown, looking at his children with eyes that were only eyes. His mouth moved. No sound came out. But Isolde read his lips, and years later, drunk enough, she told Zephyrine what they said.

*Thank you,* the king had mouthed. *I could never put it down myself.*

Then the wardenship finished dying, and the man went with it, and the crown fell on the stones and rang like the bell, and rolled, and stopped.

* * *

The land stopped forgetting.

It happened like a held breath released. The valley behind them firmed up at its edges; the river remembered which way was downhill; the apple orchards, halfway to dissolution, snapped back into being with a sound like a thousand small sighs. Somewhere out in the unraveling country, villages that had vanished from memory and record flickered back into the maps—Pip and Tam would swear to this, would produce the maps to prove it, maps that redrew themselves one final time and then, blessedly, sat still.

Thornfell was still dying. The Veil was still thinning, the door still open, the exodus still necessary. But it would die as a country dies—as a thing ending honestly—not as a lie devouring itself.

Vess settled in Zephyrine's chest like a cat arranging itself, exhausted and smug.

*Well done,* the god said. *Terribly done, in several specific ways I will enumerate later, but well done.*

"Enumerate away," Zephyrine said. "I love notes."

*You nearly died eleven times.*

"I counted twelve."

*Thirteen. You missed the one with the shadow-whip.* A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone Vess used perhaps twice a century: *No warden has ever done that. In four hundred years, none of them asked what else the lock could do. They only ever asked it to stay shut.*

"Well." Zephyrine watched the crown lying on the stones, ordinary now, just metal. "I did spend nine years asking what else was in the food."

* * *

The crew gathered at the threshold, and for a moment nobody spoke, which for this particular crew was a historical event that Pip would later insist be commemorated annually.

Osk picked up the crown, weighed it, and set it down again with the delicacy of a man handling something venomous. "Should we bury it?"

"Bury it," Maret said. "Deep. Under something boring. A turnip field."

"A tax office," Tam suggested.

"A *very* boring tax office," Pip agreed.

Corvin stood apart, staring at his bandaged hand. Isolde went to him. Zephyrine pretended to inspect the door and listened shamelessly.

"You knew," Isolde said. "Some of it. For how long?"

"Long enough to be ashamed of," Corvin said. "Not long enough to be forgiven. I don't expect—" 

"Shut up, Corvin." She took his wounded hand, gently, the sister he'd betrayed and the brother she'd lost meeting somewhere in the middle over a bandage. "You stood on the right side of the line today. That's the only ledger I'm keeping anymore. I learned bookkeeping from smugglers; we're a forgiving sort of institution."

"We are *not*," Maret said loudly. "Isolde, you still owe me forty crowns for the boat."

"Put it on my tab."

"Your tab died with the monarchy!"

"Then invoice the republic," Isolde said, and grinned, and Corvin laughed—a startled, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut—and Zephyrine felt Vess stir warmly in his chest, because that was the sound of a threshold being crossed too.

* * *

But the door was still open, and the country was still ending, and the far green shore was still full of people who had crossed ahead of them and were waiting, and the threshold-stone still thirsted.

Because the rite, turned inside-out, had one final truth folded into it, and Vess unfolded it now with great gentleness, the way you deliver news you've been carrying for a long time.

*A door held open must be held,* the god said. *By someone. Until the last soul crosses. That is the toll. That is the price of leaving nothing behind.*

Zephyrine looked at the door. At the crowd beyond it. At the road behind, where stragglers were still coming—Hollowmere survivors, hidden faithful, a slow tail of the forgotten finding their way toward the light.

"How long?" he asked.

*As long as it takes,* Vess said. *And the toll for the one who holds it—*

"I know," Zephyrine said. "Memory. I've known since Hollowmere. I did the arithmetic a long time ago."

Behind him, Maret had gone very still, in the particular way of a captain who has just heard the shape of a sacrifice and intends to fight it with everything she has. Pip's hand found Tam's. Osk began, very quietly, to pray—the first prayer he'd prayed since his defrocking, aimed at no god in particular, or possibly at all of them at once.

Zephyrine turned to face his family, and smiled, and began, "So. Here's the thing about the bill—"

"Don't," said Maret.

"—because I've been thinking," Zephyrine went on, "about who pays."

"*Don't,*" said Maret, drawing her sword, which was, historically, how all the best arguments in the Company of the Broken Bell began.
