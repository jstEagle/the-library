## 21. A Stranger on the Far Side

The grass was the first thing that made Isolde cry.

Not the crossing itself. Not the moment when the Veil-door had folded shut behind them like a book closing on a country that no longer existed to be read. Not the terrible silence of that, or the way Maret had gripped her shoulder hard enough to bruise and said nothing at all, because there was nothing left in either of them that words could reach.

It was the grass.

Green, ordinary, ankle-high grass under an open sky with no membrane over it, no shimmer of ward-light at its edges, no sense — which she had lived inside her whole life without ever knowing she felt it — of walls. The sky went up forever. The horizon simply continued. There was no far side of anything. There was just *more*, rolling away in soft green hills, dotted with sheep that had never heard of Thornfell and would not have cared.

Isolde stood in it and wept like a child, because she had spent twenty-four years in a prison and had only just been told, and her body was catching up all at once.

Around her, scattered across the hillside like flotsam after a wreck, were the others. Perhaps three hundred souls who had made it through before Zephyrine paid the toll. Villagers from Hollowmere. A handful of Osk's old congregation. Smugglers. Farmers. Pip and Tam, sitting back-to-back on a hummock, still holding hands even though they'd both sworn they'd stopped doing that when they turned nine.

And Zephyrine, lying face-up in the grass ten paces away, breathing, alive, and gone.

* * *

He had crossed last. Of course he had. He had held the door until the very end — she'd seen him do it, seen Vess burn through him like lightning through a bell, seen his eyes go white and his feet sink into ground that was forgetting how to be ground — and then, when the last child stumbled through, he had let go.

The toll had taken him the way a tide takes a sandcastle. Not violently. Just thoroughly.

They had carried him through together — Maret at his shoulders, Isolde at his feet, Corvin stumbling beside them with his soldier's training useless against grief — and laid him down on the far side in the warm grass of a country with no name yet, at least none they knew, and waited for him to wake.

He woke at dusk.

Isolde was closest. She had appointed herself closest by the simple expedient of refusing to move from the spot, and Corvin had finally given up trying to make her eat and brought her food instead, which was the same argument conducted by other means.

Zephyrine's eyes opened. They were brown. Ordinary brown. Whatever light had lived in them during those last hours at the door was gone, banked like a fire left to ash.

He looked at the sky first. Then the hills. Then the small crowd of strangers gathered at a respectful distance, watching him the way people watch a miracle they're afraid will un-happen.

Then he looked at Isolde, and smiled politely, the way one smiles at a kind stranger.

"Hello," he said. His voice was rough from disuse, or from screaming, or both. "I'm sorry — should I know you?"

The world went very quiet.

"I don't—" He sat up slowly, wincing, pressing a hand to his temple as if checking that everything was still attached. "I don't remember much. Waking up, mostly. There's a — I keep reaching for something and it's not *there*. Like a tooth." He flexed his fingers, frowned. "There's writing on my hand."

He turned his palm up.

In Pip's untidy, unmistakable hand, in ink that had survived the crossing when nothing else had, were four letters:

**SEB**

Below it, smaller, crammed against the edge of his thumb: *your name. you are loved. do not argue.*

Isolde laughed. It came out broken in the middle, half sob, but it came out, and several people nearby flinched at the sound because most of them hadn't heard her laugh since Hollowmere.

"Sebastian," she said. "Your name is Sebastian."

He blinked at her. "You know me?"

"No," she said honestly. "Yes. Both. It's complicated."

"It usually is," he said, with a faint dry note that made her chest ache, because that note was *him*, whatever else had been scraped away, some grain of the wood surviving beneath the planing. "Am I — where am I? Who are all of you? Am I a prisoner?" His eyes sharpened suddenly, taking stock — exits, numbers, weapons — and Isolde thought, *oh, there's the taster,* the instinct outliving the man. "Because if so, this is the nicest prison I've ever seen. Though admittedly my sample size may be limited."

"You're not a prisoner," said a voice.

Maret stepped forward.

She looked terrible, which for Maret meant she looked exactly normal except that her jaw was set two degrees tighter than usual. Her remaining eye was red-rimmed and furious about it. She walked to where Sebastian sat in the grass and stood over him, arms crossed, and studied him for a long moment with the expression of a woman pricing cargo.

"Do you remember anything at all?" she asked.

"Some things." He rubbed his temple. "How to swim. How to tell if wine's been salted. That I hate the smell of lilies, though I couldn't tell you why." He paused. "A bell. A cracked bell. I don't know if that's a memory or a dream."

"A bit of both," Maret said gruffly.

"And there's a voice." He touched his chest, uncertain. "Quiet now. But there's a door somewhere. In my head. Small one, garden-sized. Someone left it open for me." He looked embarrassed. "That sounds mad. Forget I said it."

"Don't," Isolde said quickly. "Don't forget you said it. Please say more things like it."

He glanced between her and Maret, wary but game, a man deciding whether these strangers were wolves. "Who are you? Either of you?"

Maret crouched down to his level. Up close, Isolde could see the effort it cost her — the captain who never bent, bending.

"My name's Maret," she said. "I'm a smuggler. Captain of the *Broken Bell* — well, formerly. The Bell's on the other side of a door that doesn't exist anymore, so technically I'm captain of a hillside now." She jerked her chin at the crowd. "That's my crew. Or what's left of them, plus about two hundred people who weren't mine yesterday but are now. That's how smuggling works. You acquire things."

Sebastian nodded slowly. "And you know me."

"We knew you," Maret said. "You were our taster. Best I ever shipped with. Tongue like a customs house." Something moved behind her eye, quick and painful, and she mastered it. "You did a thing at the end. A big thing. The biggest thing I've seen anyone do, and I once watched a man talk his way past a dragon-toll with nothing but a cheese wheel and a lie about being its nephew."

"That sounds like a good story," Sebastian said.

"It is. You were there. You laughed so hard you knocked yourself on a beam."

"I did?"

"You did." Maret's voice stayed level. "You won't remember it. None of it. That's the price you paid, and you paid it for everyone here, and we are going to be insufferable about reminding you of every single piece of it until the day you die."

"Maret—"

"*Every single piece.*"

Sebastian stared at her. And Isolde watched something happen in his face — not memory, nothing so cruel as that, but recognition of a different kind. The look of a man standing in a warm room he doesn't recall entering and realizing, slowly, that nobody in it means him harm.

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Why would I pay that for strangers?"

"They weren't strangers," Maret said. "They were us."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the hill, because three hundred exhausted refugees do not travel well at dusk, and because Pip declared — with the authority of someone who had appointed herself quartermaster within the hour — that nobody was walking another step until everyone had eaten something hot.

Fires bloomed across the hillside like slow flowers. Tam produced a mysterious quantity of dried fish and flatbread from a pack that should not have contained them, and refused to explain, and Pip kissed him on the cheek and said "that's my brother," and for a while the camp sounded almost like a festival rather than the aftermath of a country's death.

Isolde found herself assigned — self-assigned, aggressively — to sit with Sebastian.

He ate carefully, tasting each thing twice, and she realized he was doing it unconsciously, the habit deeper than the memories. When Tam's stew proved clean, he relaxed fractionally.

"Habit?" she asked.

"Apparently." He frowned at the bowl. "I keep doing things without knowing why. Testing food. Checking sightlines. Flinching at bells — no, wait, liking bells. Liking and flinching. It's very confusing." He looked up at her. "You've been staring at me all day. Since I woke. I assume I should be flattered, but you also look like you're attending a funeral, so maybe not."

Isolde considered several answers. She was, after all, a princess; she had been trained in diplomacy since she could speak, trained to give answers that were smooth and hollow and cost nothing.

"I loved you," she said. "Not — not the way the songs mean. Or possibly partly the way the songs mean, I haven't finished working that out, and you're not allowed to hold it against me because you literally cannot remember giving me cause. But you were — you are — the best person I have ever known. You held a door open for three hundred people knowing what it would cost. I watched you do it. I hated you for it and I will love you for it until I die, and today you looked at me like I was a stranger, because you paid the toll that would have let you know me." Her voice cracked. She let it. "So yes. I'm at a funeral. Mine, a little bit. Yours too."

He was silent for a long moment. The fire popped.

"I'm sorry," he said finally. "For whatever it is I can't give back."

"It's not your fault."

"It feels like my fault. Whoever I was, he seems like the sort who'd insist." A ghost of a smile. "Did he apologize a lot?"

"Constantly. For things that were never his fault. It was infuriating."

"There," he said softly. "See? Some of me survived."

* * *

Later, when the fires burned low, Maret called the crew together — what remained of the Company of the Broken Bell, plus two royals who had never formally joined and had never once been told to leave.

Corvin came. He stood slightly apart, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man who had renounced a throne three days ago and was still waiting for the ground to feel solid. He had not spoken to Sebastian since the waking. Isolde suspected he couldn't yet. His guilt wore armor.

Osk was there too, thin and scarred from the fort, his voice mostly recovered. He had spent the evening quietly being mobbed by Hollowmere villagers wanting blessings, and had patiently explained, for perhaps the fortieth time, that he was defrocked, and had then blessed them anyway, because some vocations survive defrocking.

Maret planted herself in front of the group, in front of the fire, and cleared her throat with the force of a woman launching a broadside.

"Right," she said. "Listen up, because I'll only say this once, and Pip, put that down, it's not yours."

"It might be mine. We're twins. Everything's ambiguously mine."

"Pip."

Pip put it down.

Maret looked around at them, and for once her voice wasn't a captain's voice. It was lower. Rougher. The voice of a woman standing at a grave she dug herself.

"He doesn't remember us," she said. "Any of us. Any of it. He gave it all up so we could stand on this hill tonight, and I have been thinking about that for two days straight, and here's what I've got." She held up one finger. "We can't give it back. I've asked. Osk's asked. The little door-god — who is apparently living in his chest now, gods' teeth, this company — says what's paid is paid. Toll's toll. So we can't fix it." A second finger. "But there's a difference between remembering a man and *knowing* him. And knowing him? That we can do. That we can do every day for the rest of our lives. He thinks he's a stranger. Fine. Strangers can be introduced. I've introduced myself to hostile harbormasters with worse odds than this."

She turned and looked at Sebastian, who had drifted near the fire's edge, drawn — Isolde noticed — the way people are drawn to warmth without knowing why.

"So here's my proposal," Maret said. "Company of the Broken Bell, amended articles. One: we stay together. All of us. Two hundred farmers included, if they'll have us, and I think they will, because we're the ones who carried their kids through the door. Two: we build something. Village, port, whatever this land wants us to be — we'll ask it nicely. Three:" — and here her voice did finally crack, one hairline fracture, quickly sealed — "three, we remember him. Every day. Every story, every stupid joke, every time he cheated at cards, every time he swallowed poison for a stranger because he couldn't bear to watch anyone else hurt. He paid so we wouldn't forget *him*. So we won't. Not ever. Not one day."

Silence. Firelight on wet eyes all around the circle.

Then Pip raised her hand. "Seconded. Also I call dibs on teaching him to cheat at cards, since apparently he used to and I feel personally robbed."

"You didn't teach him, I taught him," said Tam.

"You absolutely did not—"

"Thirded," said Osk quietly. "Though I'd add: gently. He's owed gentleness. He's had very little of it."

"Fourthed," said Corvin.

Everyone turned to stare at him. Corvin, crown prince of a country that no longer existed, shrugged uncomfortably.

"He saved my sister," he said. "Twice, I'm told. And he stood in front of my father's — *thing* — and did not run." He looked down. "I ran. From all of it, for years. So. Fourthed. And I'll carry water for the camp until further notice, as penance, and no one gets to vote on that."

"Fifthed," said Isolde, and her voice only shook a little. "Motion carries."

Maret nodded, satisfied, and turned to Sebastian, who had heard every word and was looking at them all with the expression of a man watching strangers build him a house out of stories.

"Well?" Maret demanded. "Any objections from the man himself?"

Sebastian opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked down at his palm, at the four letters and the small fierce postscript.

*do not argue.*

"I'm told," he said gravely, "that I'm not allowed to argue."

"Good instinct," said Maret. "Whoever wrote that knows you better than you do. Which, currently, is a low bar, but rising."

He laughed — startled out of him, bright and unguarded — and Isolde felt something in her chest turn over, because it was *his* laugh, the toll hadn't gotten everything, it hadn't gotten the laugh.

"Then I suppose," Sebastian said, "you'd better start at the beginning."

Maret sat down beside him, cross-legged, close enough to touch. Behind her, the whole ragged circle drew in — Osk and the twins, Corvin awkward and earnest, Isolde folding herself down onto the grass at his other side — and above them the strange, wall-less stars of the new country came out in their hundreds of thousands, unwatched-over and free.

"Beginning," Maret said. "Right. Picture a harbor. Picture the worst ship in it. Picture a job offer so bad only a madman would take it..."

And she began.

* * *

Much later — past midnight, when the fires were coals and most of the camp slept — Isolde rose to check on the sick and found Sebastian still awake, sitting alone at the fire's edge, turning his marked palm over and over.

"You should sleep," she whispered.

"In a moment." He looked up at her, firelit, and there was something wondering in his face. "Isolde. That's your name. You told me."

"Yes."

"Can I tell you something strange?"

"I've had a very strange year. Lead on."

"When you say it — when any of you say my name, or hers, or Maret's, or Pip's — it doesn't ring like a stranger's name should. It rings like..." He searched. "Like a word in a language I used to speak. Gone, but the shape of it left a dent." He closed his fist over the letters on his skin. "Whatever I lost, I think some of it's still in there. Listening. Waiting."

Isolde sat down beside him and said nothing, because there was nothing that needed saying. The fire settled. Somewhere beyond the hill, in the dark of the new world, something passed overhead — vast and quiet and glad, wingbeats like a held breath released — and Sebastian lifted his head to follow it, and smiled at nothing, at everything.

"The door in my chest opened," he murmured. "Just a crack. Someone's home." He glanced at her, boyish, conspiratorial. "I think we're going to be all right, Isolde. Don't ask me how I know. I don't. I just do."

Far off in the darkness, a bell rang — clear, sweet, uncracked — though there was no bell anywhere in that valley, and no one awake to ring it.

Sebastian stood, and offered Isolde his hand, and they walked back toward the fires together, two strangers who were not, and never would be again.
