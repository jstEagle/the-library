## 22. Sebastian

The village had no name yet, which everyone agreed was fitting, because none of them had come from anywhere that had a name anymore either.

Zephyrine — though he did not know he was called that, and would not have answered to it if you'd said it — woke on the first morning of autumn to the smell of bread and the sound of Pip arguing with a goat.

"You can't eat the ledger," Pip was saying, in the aggrieved tone of a person who had been saying this for some time. "It's the *inventory*. Tam, tell your goat—"

"She's not my goat," came Tam's voice from somewhere nearer the well. "She's a community goat. She has community rights."

"Community rights do not include parchment!"

Zephyrine lay in his bed a moment longer, listening, and felt the particular warmth he had learned to feel every morning since waking in this green country under an open sky: the warmth of being known by people whose names he could not remember, in a place whose name they hadn't chosen yet, and finding that this was somehow enough. More than enough. It was like waking inside a prayer someone else had said for him.

He got up. He washed his face in the basin Isolde had carved — she had a gift for stone, which surprised no one more than it surprised her — and he looked at his left hand, as he did every morning.

On the palm, in ink gone brown and soft with months of washing, were five letters:

**S-E-B-A-S**

The rest had faded. The skin had healed over the last two letters sometime in the spring, and Osk — the serious one with the kind eyes who wore no collar but stood like a man still listening for bells — had offered to redraw them. Zephyrine had said no. He didn't know why. It felt important that what remained stayed exactly as it was: incomplete, weathered, *his*.

Sebas. That was him. He was Sebas. When children asked, he said Sebastian, because it sounded like a whole name, and because when he said it, Maret always smiled in a way that suggested she knew something about it that she wasn't telling, and he liked giving her that.

He dressed, went out into the cold gold morning, and took up his work.

* * *

His work was food. It had been the first thing he'd known how to do here, before he'd known anything else — before he'd known the names of the people who carried him out of the dark at the door's far side, before he'd known there was a door at all. Someone had handed him bread on that first day, and he had tasted it, and something in his body older than memory had said: *safe.* After that, tasting was simply what he did. He tasted the well water. He tasted the mushrooms Pip brought back from the forest edge with the confidence of a woman who had once forged royal seals and saw no reason forging mushroom identifications should be harder. He tasted everything, and once a week he cooked for all of them, and once a week they all pretended his stew was not slightly too salty, and once a week Isolde salted hers from her own hidden stash and thought nobody noticed, and everybody noticed.

This morning's task was honey. There was a hollow tree at the meadow's edge where wild bees had settled, and the village had decided — after much argument, most of it between Pip and the goat — that honey was worth the stings.

"You'll want the smoke first," said Maret, falling into step beside him. She walked with the rolling gait of a woman who had spent forty years on decks, and her empty eye-socket caught the morning light the way it always did, and Zephyrine had long ago stopped finding it strange that he found nothing strange about it. "Bees are like sailors. You don't take anything from them without offering something first."

"Do I know that?" Zephyrine asked. "Or do you know that?"

Maret considered this. It was a game they played sometimes — *what did I lose?* — though lately they played it less, because the answer had stopped mattering so much. "Does it matter?"

"No," Zephyrine admitted. "I just like hearing you say it like I taught it to you."

"You might have." Maret's mouth did the thing it did — the almost-smile, the one that held a grief so old it had worn smooth, like sea glass. "You have a taster's hands. Even now. You hold things like they might lie to you."

They smoked the bees and took half the comb and left half, and the bees rose around them in a golden haze and did not sting, and Zephyrine chose to believe this was Maret's sailor-wisdom and not, as Tam insisted, the fact that the bees of this country had heard about the gods passing through and were on their best behavior.

Because the gods did pass through. That was the other thing about mornings here.

* * *

You never saw them arrive. That was the first thing everyone learned. You would be doing something ordinary — mending a fence, arguing with a goat, salting your stew twice out of spite — and the air would go the way air goes just before rain, full and shining, and something very large and very gentle would move through the village without touching anything, the way light moves through water.

The villagers had learned not to kneel. It had taken instruction. Osk, who had been a priest in the old country and had lost the collar for asking questions, gave the instruction himself, standing in the square with tears running openly down his face: "They spent a generation in a jar. They are not coming to be worshipped. They're coming to *breathe.* Let them breathe."

So they breathed. A god of rivers paused at their stream for three days and left it sweeter. Something vast and slow crossed the valley one night in midsummer, and in the morning every apple tree had fruited two weeks early, and Pip cried about it, and claimed afterward that she had not. A small god of lost things — Zephyrine liked that one; it felt familiar — kept leaving objects on doorsteps: a button, a hairpin, once a perfect river stone with a hole through it, which Isolde strung on a cord and wore without explanation.

And Vess.

Vess came differently. Vess came quietly, the way a threshold is quiet, and Vess came mostly to Zephyrine's garden gate.

He didn't remember Vess. He wanted to be clear about that, even in the privacy of his own head, where he kept his accounts the way he'd apparently once kept them in another life: honestly. He did not remember a voice named Vess, or a membrane around a country, or a door, or holding it open while people he could not name crossed through it one by one, each of them looking back at him with faces that made his chest hurt in a way he had never been able to explain to anyone, including the healer, who had suggested grief and been wrong, because you cannot grieve what you cannot remember, and whatever lived in Zephyrine's chest was not grief. It was fuller than grief. It was a room with all the furniture missing and the shape of the furniture still pressed into the floorboards.

But the gate. The gate was his own work — he'd built it in the second month, larch wood, simple hinges — and one morning in early summer he had come out and found that it had a small door in it. Not a gate-sized door. A door the size of a hand, set into the lower panel, with a tiny iron latch and a lintel no bigger than a finger, worked so finely it must have taken a craftsman hours, except that no craftsman had come, and the wood grain ran unbroken through the little frame as if the door had grown there.

Inside, when he opened it, was nothing. Just depth. A small darkness that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of lightning.

He'd shown Maret. Maret had gone very still, and then she had laughed — a real laugh, wet at the edges — and said, "That figures," and refused to explain further, and Zephyrine had let her refuse, because he was learning that the people here carried their memories of him like candles cupped against wind, and he did not want to be the wind.

Now, most evenings, he opened the little door and left something in it. A crust of bread. A button. Once, a spoon he suspected Pip had stolen from him and denied stealing. In the morning the offering was always gone, and sometimes something was left in its place: a feather. A coin from a country none of them could name. Once, a single bee, asleep, which had seemed like a joke, and Zephyrine had laughed alone in his garden at dawn, and it had been — he wrote this in the journal Isolde had given him, in his careful hand — *it had been the best joke anyone ever told me.*

* * *

Autumn deepened. The village grew.

Refugees still came through, though fewer now, and slower — the ones who came late arrived dazed, blinking, carrying bundles of belongings from houses that no longer existed anywhere, in a country that had finished forgetting itself sometime in high summer. Tam kept the ledger of arrivals. Pip argued with the goat about eating it. Osk met each new group at the tree line and asked them the only question that mattered, gently, the way you'd ask it of someone waking from fever: "Are you hurt? Are you hungry? Good. Then welcome. The rest we'll figure out."

Corvin came in the third week of autumn.

He came alone, on foot, having sent no word, wearing clothes that had been fine once and were road-fine now, and he stood at the edge of the village for a full hour before Isolde went out to him. Zephyrine watched from the honey tree, too far to hear, close enough to see: the brother and sister facing each other in the stubbled field, neither moving, the wind pushing grass between them like a slow tide. Then Isolde stepped forward and hit him — one blow, open-handed, across the face — and Corvin took it and did not fall, and then Isolde grabbed his collar with both fists and put her forehead against his, and they stood like that for a long time, two people holding each other up.

He stayed. He was bad at fences and worse at goats and surprisingly good at organizing the woodpile, and he never once tried to tell anyone what to do, which Zephyrine gathered — from the way Maret watched him, measuring, month over month — was itself a kind of penance, and perhaps the only kind that counted.

"He asks about you sometimes," Isolde told Zephyrine one evening, at the communal fire. "My brother. He says—" She stopped. Started again. "He remembers everything. What happened at the door. What you—" Her jaw tightened. "What you did. And he can't give any of it back to you, and it's eating him alive. I keep telling him that's not what you'd want."

"What would I want?" Zephyrine asked. It was a genuine question. He asked it often, of many people, and he loved the answers, because the answers were always different and always revealed more about the answerer than about him, and slowly, mosaic-slow, the answers were building him a self out of other people's certainty.

Isolde looked at him across the fire. She had a scar now along her jaw from Hollowmere — he didn't know what Hollowmere was, but when he touched the scar once, absently, handing her a bowl, she had gone white, and he had learned not to touch it again.

"You'd want us to stop carrying it like a debt," she said finally. "You'd want us to carry it like a gift. I'm working on it."

"That sounds hard."

"It's the hardest thing I've ever done," said the woman who had poisoned a king and renounced a throne, "and I once ate dinner with my father knowing what he was."

* * *

Winter came mild, the gods' parting gift perhaps, or just luck, and on the first night of true frost the village held its naming.

It had been Tam's idea, or Pip's — they claimed it separately and simultaneously, as they claimed everything — that a village without a name was a village waiting to be erased, and they had all seen enough of erasing to want no part of it, even the benign kind. So there was a fire, and there was the good stew (slightly too salty), and there was a hat, and names went into the hat, and the goat ate three of them, which everyone agreed narrowed the field admirably.

They settled on Bellhollow. For the bell — Osk's cracked bell-shaped talisman, the relic of the silenced gods, which hung now from a post in the square and rang, when the wind caught it, a note that was broken and sweet and unmistakably alive — and for the hollow, the green valley that had received them all. Osk wept. Corvin, asked to say a few words as the former crown prince of a country that no longer existed, stood, opened his mouth, closed it, sat down, and poured everyone cider instead, which Maret declared the finest speech a warden's son ever gave.

Later, when the fire had burned to embers and most people had drifted to bed, Zephyrine sat with Maret on the bench outside her house. She had a blanket over her knees and cider in her hand and the contented stillness of a captain with no ship, which is to say she looked like a woman actively resisting the urge to reorganize the woodpile.

"Ask me," she said, after a while.

"Ask you what?"

"Anything. About before. You've gotten polite about it, these last months. You used to ask all the time." She turned the cider mug in her hands. "I miss it. Being asked. It's how I keep him — keep *you* — sharp. Memory's like a blade. Goes dull if you don't use it."

Zephyrine looked at the fire-light on her face, at the smooth old grief of the empty socket, and he asked the question he had been saving, the one that lived deepest in the furniture-shaped absence inside him:

"When we crossed. At the door. Was I — was I good to you? All of you?"

Maret was quiet for a long moment. Frost ticked in the eaves. Somewhere the bell moved in the wind and let its broken note fall.

"You held the door," she said. "Everyone else went through, and you stayed, because someone had to, and you paid the whole toll with your eyes open, and the last thing you said to me—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it, the way she must have mastered it a hundred times on a hundred decks. "The last thing you said was that remembering us was the best job you ever had. Better than the poisons. And then you forgot, and we crossed, and I swore—" She stopped. Started over, quieter. "I paid a toll once myself, when I was small. Lost my mother's face out of my own head. I know what it takes out of you. So when we got here and you looked at me like a stranger, I said — I told them all — *we'll do the remembering. All of us. Every day. Until it adds up.*"

"And has it?"

Maret smiled, and this time it reached her eye. "Look around, Sebastian. Look at what we built. What do you think?"

* * *

Spring came the way springs do in a country with open sky: all at once, extravagantly, like a secret told too loudly.

Zephyrine planted his garden. Beans, onions, the squash Isolde swore by, and a row of flowers he chose purely because their seeds had appeared one morning in the little door of the gate, wrapped in a leaf, with no note.

On the evening the beans broke ground, he knelt by the gate to open the small door and make his offering, and found the door already open.

Inside, sitting in the hand-sized darkness as comfortably as a cat in a windowsill, was a key. Small, iron, plain, warm to the touch.

And a voice — not heard so much as remembered, arriving in him the way the smell of bread arrives, from somewhere below thought — said:

*Hello, Sebastian. I kept the little door so you'd always have a way in. This is yours. The garden, I mean. The country. All of it. No tolls. Not anymore.*

Zephyrine knelt in the dirt of his garden in the long gold light, holding a key he did not recognize for a door he did not remember, and he found — this was the strangest part, the part he would write in the journal that night by candlelight, in his careful hand — that he was not afraid, and not sad, and not confused. He was full. He was a room with all the furniture missing, and tonight, for the first time, it didn't feel like absence.

It felt like space. Space for whatever came next.

From the square came the sound of Pip yelling at the goat, and Tam laughing, and Osk ringing the cracked bell for supper, and Maret's voice cutting through all of it like a keel through water, and Isolde calling his name —

"Sebastian! Wash your hands, you're eating with us, Corvin made bread and it's actually edible this time, hurry up before Pip eats the evidence—"

— his name, the one thing the toll couldn't take, written on his hand and called across gardens and worn smooth as sea glass by a hundred daily uses, until it wasn't a wound or a riddle or a debt at all.

It was just his name. And he stood, and brushed off his knees, and locked nothing, and left the little door open behind him, and went in to supper.
