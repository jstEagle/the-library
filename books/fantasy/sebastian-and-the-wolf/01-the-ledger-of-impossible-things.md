## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The starlight arrived on a Tuesday, in crates stamped with a guild seal that had been forged so well it took Ines Okafor three full minutes to find the flaw.

She found it the way she found everything: quietly, and without anyone noticing she was looking. The seal was pressed into black wax at the corner of each crate, a compass rose whose points ended in small open hands—the mark of the Vellum Concord, a trading house two realms over that officially did not exist. The forgery was exquisite. The wax had been aged with smoke and salt, the impression slightly blurred as if by a careless thumb. But the rose's northern point curved a half-degree too far east, and the little hands had six fingers instead of five.

Someone had wanted these crates to look like they came from nowhere. Which meant they came from somewhere someone was paid to forget.

Ines set down her magnifying lens, adjusted the lamp, and wrote in her neat, narrow hand: *Shipment 4471. Twelve crates, declared contents: bottled starlight, origin unlisted. Seal: Concord pattern, counterfeit (see marginal note). Condition: intact.*

Then, because she was who she was, she added: *True origin: the Salt Meridian, third shelf of the world. Bottled within the last season. Handle gently; light remembers how it was caught.*

No one would ever read that second line. It wasn't for the guild's ledgers. It was for hers.

* * *

The Guild of Unmapped Realms occupied a building that was larger inside than out, which was not magic but architecture, though Ines had long suspected the distinction was thinner than the guild liked to admit. From the street, in the harbor district of Coriel, it looked like a modest counting house: gray stone, salt-stained, its windows fogged by the sea air. Inside, it unfolded—corridors that turned twice before ending where they began, staircases that descended into rooms above them, and at the heart of everything, the map vault, three stories of shelving holding charts of places no honest atlas would acknowledge.

Ines had worked there for eleven years. She had started at nineteen, an orphan of the coastal wars with a clerk's hand and no family name worth pronouncing, and she had learned quickly the first law of the guild: *be useful, be accurate, be invisible.* The cartographers who rose did so by being seen—by guildmaster Vey, by the senior surveyors, by the enforcers in their gray-and-silver livery. The clerks who survived did so by being furniture.

Ines had made herself excellent furniture.

She knew every ledger in the building, because knowing ledgers was her work and her shelter both. She cataloged what came through the salt door: bottled starlight from the Meridian shelves, weather harvested from storms that had never happened, seeds from orchards that fruited only in dreams, ink made from the last words of dying languages. She recorded weights, counts, conditions, seals. She flagged discrepancies in a tone so mild that senior surveyors thanked her for catching errors they themselves had made. And every night, after the lamps were trimmed and the vault locked, she copied into her own private book—not the guild's truth, but the *true* truth, the one that lived underneath.

Because Ines Okafor had a gift, and the gift was this: she could read any map and see what it was actually saying. Not what it depicted—what it *confessed*. A map was a document drawn by a human hand, and human hands lied the way hearts lied: incompletely, and always in the same direction. A smuggler's chart with a coastline shifted two miles south told her where the smuggler's heart was. A guild charter with borders drawn in confident strokes told her where the guild's fear was. A treasure map with the X placed too neatly told her that whoever drew it had never been there, and wanted very much to seem like someone who had.

Maps, to Ines, were confessions written in geography. And she had never once told anyone.

* * *

"Okafor."

She looked up from Shipment 4471. Sister Hallow stood at the end of the receiving hall, gray cloak beaded with harbor fog, her face arranged in its usual expression of patient disappointment with the physical world. Hallow was the guild's chief enforcer, and she was the only person in the building who consistently remembered Ines's name, which Ines had decided years ago was more unsettling than being forgotten.

"Sister."

"The starlight." Hallow crossed the hall, boots silent on the stone. "Vey wants it cataloged tonight. There's a buyer."

"There's always a buyer."

"There's always a buyer," Hallow agreed, without humor. "Twelve crates?"

"Twelve." Ines turned the manifest toward her. "Declared origin unlisted. The seal is Concord-pattern, but it's a copy—six-fingered hands, the north point off by half a degree. Whoever pressed it has access to a genuine Concord stamp to copy from, which is either very good news or very bad news depending on which side of the trade you're standing on."

Hallow looked at the manifest for a long moment. Then she looked at Ines—a look Ines had learned to endure rather than enjoy, the look of someone checking whether a piece of furniture had moved.

"You notice things," Hallow said.

"I notice seals. It's my job."

"Mm." Hallow straightened. "Catalog it tonight. And Okafor—if anything about the shipment troubles you beyond the seal, bring it to me. Not to the surveyors. To me."

"Nothing troubles me beyond the seal, Sister."

"No," Hallow said. "I imagine that's what makes you good at your work."

She left. Ines sat very still until the sound of her boots faded entirely, then exhaled slowly and pulled her private ledger from beneath the false bottom of her desk drawer.

*She suspects I'm more than a clerk,* she wrote. *Or she suspects everyone, which is her job. Either way: smaller. Quieter. Be the desk, not the person sitting at it.*

It was a strange way to live, she knew. She had known it for years, and had made her peace with it the way you make peace with a limp—by forgetting, most days, that walking was ever supposed to be painless. Her mother had taught her to read maps before she'd taught her to read words, back when they'd lived in a survey station in the hills, back when her mother still spoke to her. That life had ended the way her mother's letters ended: abruptly, and without explanation. Ines had come to Coriel with nothing but her gift and a grief she refused to examine, and she had built herself a life with walls so thick and so well-lit that nothing true could get in.

It was, she told herself, contentment. It was certainly quiet. And quiet, in a city built on the trade of impossible things, was the rarest commodity of all.

* * *

The starlight itself was beautiful, which annoyed her, because beautiful cargo was always trouble.

She unpacked the first crate at sundown, alone in the receiving hall with the lamps turned low. Inside, packed in wool and ash-sand, stood two dozen bottles of dark glass, each no taller than her hand, each stoppered with wax and a twist of copper wire. The light inside didn't glow so much as *wait*—a slow silver churn, like moonrise compressed into liquid, turning over on itself with the patience of something that had been taken from its home and intended to wait as long as necessary.

Ines lifted one bottle and held it to the lamp.

The light inside leaned toward her.

Not toward the flame—toward *her*. She felt it the way you feel someone watching from across a room: a faint pressure against the skin, a warmth behind the sternum. Starlight, the old catalogs said, was caught at the Salt Meridian where the sky came close enough to the ground to skim, and it retained a memory of what it had been part of. Most of it stayed inert. Most of it.

"Hm," Ines said aloud, to the empty hall, because speaking to bottles was less foolish than listening to them.

She set it down and worked. Crate by crate, bottle by bottle, she counted and weighed and recorded, her pen moving in its tidy lines, her mind doing what it always did: reading the confession underneath. The packing wool came from the hill country. The ash-sand was Meridian sand, correct and proper. The copper wire was Coriel-made, bought recently, in quantity—which meant someone local had packed these crates, someone with access to the guild's own stores. The crates hadn't been smuggled *in*. They'd been smuggled *out*, and were now being smuggled back, laundered through a forged seal so that the guild could buy its own stolen goods without anyone asking why the goods needed stealing.

Ines sat back and looked at her notes for a long time.

This was the moment, she understood dimly, where a different sort of person would go to Vey. Or to Hallow, as instructed. Would lay out the evidence, watch the machinery of the guild grind into motion, and be *seen*—promoted, perhaps, or praised, or quietly disappeared, depending on whose theft it was. Eleven years of invisibility said: write the safe version. *Shipment 4471, twelve crates, condition intact.* Let the lie pass through the ledgers like water through sand.

Her pen hovered.

Then she wrote, in her private book, beneath the official entry: *The guild is buying back its own starlight under a false name. Someone inside stole it, someone inside is selling it, and someone inside wants the transaction clean. Three someones. Or one someone wearing three coats.*

She underlined it once, closed the book, and slid it home beneath the false bottom.

That was all she would do. That was all she *could* do—she was a clerk, and clerks who noticed too loudly became examples. She had watched it happen once, early in her years here, to a junior surveyor named Pell who had asked why a certain route appeared on no charter yet carried fresh boot-wax on the vault floor every month. Pell had been reassigned to a station so remote it appeared on no map at all, which Ines now suspected was not a figure of speech.

Be the desk. Be the lamp. Be the ledger that records and never speaks.

She finished the count near midnight. Forty-one bottles short of the declared three hundred, which she noted without surprise—someone had already skimmed the shipment once, before it ever reached the salt door. She resealed the crates, signed the manifest with her small precise signature, and carried the final page to the night drop outside the guildmaster's office, where it vanished into the slot with a sound like a held breath released.

The corridors were empty. The lamps burned low and blue in their niches. Ines walked back through the sleeping guild with her shoulders relaxed and her steps soft, past the map vault with its three stories of confessed geographies, past the sealed doors of the senior surveyors, past the salt-warded entrance where the fog pressed gray against the windows like a face trying to remember what it looked like.

At the vault door she paused. Habit. She checked the lock, checked the ward-seal, ran her thumb along the salt line in the doorframe—the guild's oldest defense, older than the charter, older than Vey himself, though the charter claimed otherwise. The salt was intact. The wards hummed their low note. Everything was exactly as it should be, in a building full of impossible things guarded by people paid not to see them.

Ines went up to her room—a narrow cell under the eaves, a bed, a shelf, a window that faced the harbor—and she did what she did every night before sleep. She opened her private ledger to a blank page and wrote the day's true account, the one no one would ever read, the one that existed only because some part of her refused, stubbornly, irrationally, to let the real record die.

*Tonight,* she wrote, *the starlight leaned toward me. I don't know why. I have decided not to wonder about it, because wondering is the first step out the door, and I have spent eleven years learning to love this room.*

She stopped. Looked at the sentence. Wondering is the first step out the door.

She hadn't meant to write that. She crossed it out—or rather, she drew a single line through it, the way cartographers marked features that existed but were not to be spoken of, and blew out the lamp.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, of a road.

It was not a road she knew, and yet her dreaming self walked it with the certainty of a woman walking her own street. It ran through country that kept changing its mind—hills that were forests that were shorelines, a sky overhead that frayed at the edges like old cloth, threads of blue pulling loose and drifting upward into a darkness that wasn't night. Far ahead, something glinted: a spring, or a well, or a wound in the world, silver and patient.

And on the road behind her, someone was following. She couldn't turn to look. She could hear him—footsteps that were sometimes footsteps and sometimes the soft pad of paws, breath that was sometimes breath and sometimes a low, aching whine—and she felt no fear, only a grief so specific and so enormous that she woke with wet eyes and no idea why.

The fog against her window had thickened. Below, in the harbor district, the bells rang the hour before dawn, and under the bells, faintly, from the direction of the salt door, came a sound that did not belong to any hour: a heavy body striking wood. Once. Twice. Then a scratching, desperate and weakening, like something that had crawled a very long way to reach a door it could no longer open.

Ines lay still in the dark, heart hammering, and told herself it was the fog, the tide, a crate shifting in the receiving hall.

The sound came again. And beneath it, barely audible, a voice—human, cracked, and utterly lost—saying words she would spend the rest of her life remembering:

*"Please. I need the keeper of the covenant."*

Ines Okafor, who had spent eleven years being furniture, sat up in bed and reached for her boots.
