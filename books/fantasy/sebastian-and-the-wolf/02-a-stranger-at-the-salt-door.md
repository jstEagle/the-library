## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

The fog came off the harbor the way lies come off a bad witness—slowly, then all at once, until nothing true remained visible.

Ines Okafor watched it swallow the street from her window on the third floor of the Guild of Unmapped Realms, where she had lived for six years in a room the size of a generous coffin. Below her, Lamplighters Row dissolved into gray wool. The salt-warded door at the guild's entrance glowed faintly through it, the way it always did after dusk—a pale, patient shimmer, like the memory of moonlight pressed into stone. Salt kept things out. That was the first lesson every apprentice learned, before ink, before vellum, before the ethics of borders. *Salt keeps out what has no name.*

She had spent the evening doing what she did every evening: cataloging. Tonight's shipment was bottled starlight, forty-one vials from a realm called Vess that existed, according to the guild's ledgers, only on Thursdays. She wrote the entry in her small, precise hand:

*Shipment 4471. Forty-one vials, starlight, bottled. Condition: dimming. Provenance: Vess (Thursdays). Received by: I. Okafor, Second Cartographer, Third Rank.*

Third rank. She had refused promotion twice. Promotions meant visibility, and visibility meant being asked questions, and questions were how careful people died. Ines's entire architecture of survival rested on three principles: be accurate, be quiet, and never let anyone see what you could see.

Because Ines could see. That was the trouble. Any map she looked at, she read its true shape beneath whatever the cartographer had drawn—the smuggler's route hidden under a merchant's chart, the drowned city under the fishing map, the lie under the ledger. The guild thought her gift was merely excellent. Only Ines knew it was dangerous, and only Ines knew to hide it, because she had once, at nineteen, made the mistake of telling her mother what she saw in the guild charter, and her mother had gone white as unmarked paper and told her never to say it again. Her mother had been dead four years now. Ines had not said it again.

She capped her ink, blew out the lamp, and stood for a moment in the dark listening to the fog press against the glass. It was a good life, she told herself, the way you tell yourself anything often enough that it stops requiring belief. Warm room. Steady work. Invisible hands.

Then the screaming started.

Not human screaming. She knew human screaming; the harbor provided a steady education in it. This was something between a wolf caught in a snare and a man saying a word he couldn't finish—and it came from directly below, from the salt door itself.

Ines was down the stairs before she decided to go down the stairs, which frightened her more than the sound had. Careful people did not run toward screams. Careful people barred doors and waited for morning and reported what they'd heard in tidy sentences to tidy superiors.

The front hall was empty except for the door, and the door was wrong.

The salt-ward shimmer flickered—she had never seen it flicker—and against it, half in and half through, was a body. A man, mostly. He wore a traveler's coat ruined past identification, and his left arm ended at the elbow in ruin, and where his right hand should have been there was something else: fur to the wrist, claws sunk into the doorframe, fingers that were fingers and also not. His face was pressed sideways against the ward, and the ward burned him where they touched—she could smell it, hair and skin, a sweetish terrible smell—and still he pushed, because clutched against his chest with his remaining whole hand was a roll of oilcloth sealed with dripping wax, and he held it the way drowning men hold driftwood.

He was trying to get *in*. Through a salt ward. Which nothing without a name could cross.

Ines stood at the top of the last stair with her hand on the banister and did the arithmetic of her life. If she went back up, barred her door, and reported this in the morning, she would remain exactly who she had been: I. Okafor, Second Cartographer, Third Rank, invisible, safe, slowly erased. If she opened the door—

The man's eyes found hers. They were amber, wrong for a human face, and full of a fever-bright desperation that needed no translation in any language or realm. His mouth moved. Blood on his teeth. What came out was barely a word at all:

"*Keeper.*"

The word went through her like a needle finding a vein she hadn't known she had.

She didn't remember crossing the hall. Later she would reconstruct it—the cold of the floor through her socks, the way the ward's light washed her hands silver—but in the moment there was only her hand flat against the door, and the salt shimmer parting around her palm like water around a prow, and the stranger spilling through the gap she made, all his weight coming against her, driving them both down onto the flagstones in a tangle of ruined coat and blood and that impossible roll of wax-sealed maps.

The ward snapped shut behind him. The glow steadied. Somewhere upstairs, a clock marked the hour with perfect indifference.

"Help," the man said, in a voice that was half growl, "or don't. But take it." He tried to push the oilcloth roll into her hands. His clawed fingers couldn't manage a grip. "Take—it—"

"You're bleeding on the guild floor," Ines heard herself say, absurdly, "which is against eleven separate regulations."

Something that might have been a laugh broke apart in his chest into coughing. More blood. Too much blood. The wounds across his torso weren't knife wounds; they were long parallel gashes, three together, again and again, like something had raked him with a fence made of knives. And his arm—his missing arm wasn't torn off. It was *dissolved*, the stump smooth and strange, as if the flesh had simply declined to continue existing past the elbow.

"What did this to you?"

"The guild," he said, and smiled with those bloody teeth, and fainted.

* * *

Sebastian dreamed, when he dreamed at all, of an oath.

It was always the same oath, though he could never hold more than fragments of it, the way you can't hold water in a fist. He stood in a place of white stone and running light—a well? a spring?—and voices spoke words in a language older than language, and he answered, and his answer was *yes*, and his yes was the largest thing he had ever done, larger than his body, larger than his name—

His name. He reached for it in the dream and found the shape of the reaching but not the thing reached for. There was a name. It fit him like a key fits a lock. Someone had used it once, warmly, in a kitchen that smelled of—

Fog. Running. The taste of iron and pine. A woman's voice saying *run, Sebastian, don't look back*—and there, that was the name, *Sebastian*, given to him by a voice already breaking—and then fire, and a friend's face going hard and cold as slate, and a knife that wasn't a knife but a *clause*, and the curse closing over him like a door.

Between moonrises, a wolf. Between moonsets, a man who remembered less each time. That was the sentence. That was what the oath cost those who broke it. He had known that when he swore it. He had sworn it anyway.

And now he was falling through fog toward a door made of salt, holding the only thing left worth holding, and someone was opening the door. Someone with steady hands and ink-stained fingers and eyes that looked at him and *saw* him—not the wolf, not the monster, not the bounty, just a man dying on a floor—and a voice saying something about regulations that was so dry and so human he wanted to weep.

*Keeper,* he had said. He didn't know why he knew it. The knowing sat in him like a splinter too deep to dig out: *this one. This is the one. Deliver it to the keeper.*

He surfaced from the fever in fragments. Once, to the sensation of being dragged—heavier than any woman should have managed—across a floor, propped against something, and a voice muttering, "*There are wheelbarrows in the cellar, of course there are wheelbarrows in the cellar, why do I know where the wheelbarrows are.*" Once, to cool cloth on his brow and lamplight behind a silhouette bent over him. Once, to the woman sitting three feet away with a loaded crossbow across her knees, watching him with an expression of profound professional displeasure, and saying, "If you turn fully wolf in here, I want you to know I've read about how to kill one, though admittedly only in a bestiary with significant marginal errors."

Once, to her unwrapping the oilcloth roll halfway, just enough to see what it was, and going very still.

"It's blank," she whispered. And then, in a different voice entirely, the voice of someone seeing the truth under a drawing: "No. It's not."

* * *

Ines had hidden him in the map vault because it was the only room in the guild with no windows and a lock on the inside, and because the vault's deep shelves of rolled charts muffled sound, and because—she admitted this only once, to herself, at four in the morning, sitting on the floor with her back against a shelf of coastal surveys—because some part of her had stopped consulting the rules somewhere around the moment the salt ward parted for her hand.

That was the other thing she wasn't thinking about. Wards didn't open for people. Wards especially didn't open for people who had spent six years making themselves as unremarkable as possible. The salt recognized names, the texts said. It kept out the nameless. Which meant that either the ward was failing—which would be a catastrophe worth waking the entire guild over—or it had recognized *her*.

She chose, carefully, not to think about that either.

Instead she worked. Work was her native language. She cleaned his wounds with distilled spirit and stitched the worst of the gashes with the fine silk thread she used for repairing vellum, and if her hands shook, they shook precisely. She built a pallet from folded charts—sacrilege, technically, but the sacrilege seemed smaller than everything else happening tonight. She noted, with clinical detachment, that his wounds were already knitting at a speed no human body managed, and that the fur receded from his wrist whenever he slept deeply and returned when the fever climbed.

And she studied the map.

Unrolled on the vault floor by lamplight, it appeared blank—clean vellum, nothing on it. But Ines's gift did not consult appearances. Underneath the blankness she could see it: lines, dense and interlocking, a network of roads and rivers and borders that connected places she knew to places she absolutely did not, drawn in a hand that was somehow both precise and desperate. It was a map of passages. Of threads. And threaded through all of it, binding it, ran a single continuous line that pulsed faintly when she looked too long, like a vein in living skin.

At the center of the map, where a capital city should be, was a symbol she had seen exactly once before: in the guild's oldest charter, in the frame behind Guildmaster Vey's desk. A circle of three interlocking marks. Three keepers.

"This isn't possible," she said aloud, to the sleeping man, to the vault, to herself. "This covenant was dissolved twenty years ago. It's in the histories. It's in our own records."

The man slept on, breathing raggedly, his bandaged stump twitching with dreams she suspected she was better off not entering.

She should wake Guildmaster Vey. That was the correct action, the regulated action, the action of I. Okafor, Second Cartographer, Third Rank. Vey was kind—everyone said so, Vey's kindness was legendary, Vey remembered apprentices' birthdays and forgave late fees and had personally paid for Ines's mother's funeral rites when the survey station burned. Vey would know what to do with a dying wolf-man and an impossible map.

Vey would take the map for safekeeping.

The thought arrived unbidden and settled in her chest with a cold weight, and Ines examined it the way she examined any anomaly on a chart: calmly, thoroughly, hating what it showed. Why had that thought come with dread attached? Why had her gift, unasked, flickered over six years of memories of Guildmaster Vey's kindness and found—nothing false, exactly, but a *layering*, kindness laid over something else the way a forged border is drawn over a real one?

"You're feverish," she told herself. "You've been awake for thirty hours. You're trusting a bestiary-level instinct over twenty years of—"

From the pallet, the man stirred. His amber eyes opened, unfocused, fixed on the ceiling of the vault, on the shadows between the shelves where ten thousand unmapped roads lay coiled in the dark.

"I was supposed to bring it to the keeper," he rasped. "Twenty years I carried it. Or—" confusion crossed his face, pain deeper than the wounds. "A year. I don't—I can't remember how long. The curse eats the counting." His eyes came around to her, and focused, and Ines felt seen in a way she had spent her whole adult life preventing. "You opened the ward."

"Anyone could have—"

"No," he said simply. "They couldn't."

He tried to sit up, failed, and settled for turning his head toward her. Up close, in honest lamplight, he was younger than she'd first thought, and exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with the body. "Listen. They'll come for me. The ones that did this—they wear guild colors, but they aren't guild, or they're the part of the guild nobody writes down. When they come, they'll ask who helped me. Give them my description. Say you found the map on the steps. Lie beautifully—you look like someone who'd be good at it."

"I don't lie," Ines said.

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. "Everyone maps. Some people just use words." Then his face tightened, and his hand—the clawed one—closed over her wrist, gentle despite everything, and his voice dropped to something urgent and fraying. "The map burns itself into whoever inherits it. I've watched it happen. I need you to understand what I'm about to do, and I need you to know I'm sorry, because I don't think I have until morning, and the next keeper has to be chosen while I'm still—"

"Don't," Ines said. "Whatever it is, don't. I'm not anyone. I keep ledgers. I'm third rank. I am *nobody*—"

"That," Sebastian said, and now the fever-light in his eyes was almost tender, almost pitying, "is the most useful thing about you."

Outside, far above the fog, the clouds were tearing open, and the moon—three days past full, fat and white and merciless—came through the vault's high air-shaft in a single bright blade.

The man looked at it. All the color left his face, such color as remained.

"Oh," he said quietly. "I'm out of time."

His grip on her wrist tightened. The wax seal on the map, cooling on the floor beside them, began—impossibly—to glow.

And the last thing Ines registered, before the light took them both, was the sound of the guild's great bell beginning to ring, though no one was ringing it.
