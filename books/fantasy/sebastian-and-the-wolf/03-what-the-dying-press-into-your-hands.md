## 3. What the Dying Press Into Your Hands

The map vault had no windows, which was why Ines noticed the dawn.

It came to her as a change in the air pressure of the guild above—a settling of the building's bones, the creak of staircases as the night clerks went off shift and the day clerks came on. Somewhere overhead, someone was grinding ink. Someone else was arguing about the proper humidity for storing a chart of the Sighing Archipelago. The guild woke around her in its ordinary rhythms, and none of it knew that she was sitting on an upturned crate in the deepest room of the building, keeping vigil over a dying man who was not entirely a man.

Sebastian had not woken in the night. He had burned instead—fever rolling off him in waves that made the candle gutter and the frost on the vault's iron door weep down its face in long silver runnels. She had done what little she could: watered him with a sponge, packed his wounds with the moss she used for preserving vellum because it was all she had, talked to him in a low voice when the thrashing got bad, though he never heard her. Once, near the smallest hour, he had said a name—not hers—and then a string of words in no language she knew, except that her gift, her cursed useful gift, translated them anyway. *I kept it. I kept it whole. Forgive me for what comes next.*

She did not want to know what came next.

The map lay on the floor beside her where he'd dropped it, still sealed in its wax. All night she had felt it the way you feel a hand resting on the back of your neck—patient, expectant. Maps did not usually look at people. This one looked at her.

When the fever finally broke, it broke all at once, like a rope parting under strain. Sebastian went rigid, arched, and then collapsed into a stillness so complete that for three full seconds Ines thought she had failed at the only rule-breaking act of her life and killed him anyway.

Then his eyes opened.

They were gray, the gray of a chart left out in weather, and they found her immediately, unerringly, as if he had known exactly where she would be sitting.

"You stayed," he rasped.

"Someone had to make sure you didn't die in the guild's map vault," she whispered. "That would have been very difficult to catalog."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "You're laughing. Good. You'll need it." He tried to push himself up on one arm and failed, slid back against the shelving, and she saw the effort cost him something worse than pain—the color drained from his face, and the half-healed wounds along his forearm had begun, impossibly, to darken again, edges going black as ink dropped in water.

"No," he said quietly, looking at his own arm with something like irritation rather than fear. "No, it's taking me."

"What is? What's taking you?" She was on her knees beside him before she remembered to be afraid of him. "I can get the healers—I shouldn't, but I can—"

"The healers are the last thing this guild will allow near me." His hand closed around her wrist. His grip was fever-hot and shaking, but underneath the shaking was something forged, something that had held weight for years without breaking. "Listen to me. We don't have time for the version of this conversation where you believe me slowly. Vey is coming. I felt the wards shift an hour ago—he knows I'm here, and he knows what I'm carrying. When he arrives, everything I say after that point will be worth nothing. So I say it now."

"Vey? Guildmaster Vey?" Her voice came out thin. "Why would the guildmaster—"

"Because twenty years ago he broke an oath older than this building," Sebastian said, "and everything the Guild of Unmapped Realms has been since—that monopoly, those salt-warded doors, those ledgers you keep so beautifully—is built on top of the break. You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't. Your gift won't let you lie to yourself forever."

Ines opened her mouth to deny it and found nothing there. Because she *had* seen it. Three years ago, cataloging the charter collection, she had read the guild's founding document and felt the way she always felt around a forgery—a faint wrongness, like a chord played a quarter-tone flat. She had told herself she was tired. She had told herself a great many things.

"I don't understand any of this," she said. It was true, and it was also a coward's sentence, and she hated hearing it in her own voice.

"Then let me give you the shape of it, cartographer, since shapes are your language." He drew a breath that whistled. "There were realms once that touched each other—drifted close the way ships do, trading light across the gap. A covenant bound them, so the drifting wouldn't become tearing. The covenant needed keepers. Sworn names, written into the binding itself. There were three." His eyes closed briefly. "There were three."

"And now?"

"Now there is one space left unfilled, and one man who cannot hold it, because the man who broke the covenant cursed him for trying to mend it." He lifted his arm, the darkening one, and turned it so she could see: beneath the blackening skin, faint as watermark, lines were moving. Lines like roads. Like borders. "Between moonrises I am this. Between moonsets I am the other thing. And I am running out of both."

Ines's heart was doing something painful and arrhythmic. "You're the wolf," she said stupidly. "From the door. The—the half of you—"

"I'm both halves. That's the curse. Neither whole, neither dead." He laughed once, without humor. "Vey's work. He always was elegant."

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Ines felt it then—a pressure in the vault, a gathering, like the moment before a storm front crosses a valley. The map on the floor had begun to glow through its wax seal, a slow amber pulse, steady as a heartbeat.

Sebastian followed her gaze. "Ah," he said softly. "It's decided. Good. I was afraid I'd have to argue with it."

"It? Sebastian, what is it deciding?"

He took her hands. Both of hers, in both of his, and his grip steadied as if the trembling had passed into her. Up close his eyes were terrible and calm, the calm of a man standing on a shore watching the wave that will drown him come in, and being almost grateful for the view.

"Ines Okafor," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded old, sounded *sworn*, sounded like it had been said in this exact cadence by other mouths in other centuries. "Daughter of Adaeze Okafor, keeper of the second name. Last heir of the Covenant of Threads. I, Sebastian Vael, first keeper, being spent, transfer what remains of my trust to you. Take the map. Take the burden. Take the road I couldn't finish."

"No," Ines said. The word came out very small. "No, you've made a mistake. I'm nobody. I file things. I make myself small on purpose. I'm not—you want someone who—"

"I want someone whose gift reads truth," he said, "because the covenant is made of nothing else. I want someone who chose invisibility and survived it, because the road ahead eats the visible. And I want—" his voice cracked, just once, "—I want the daughter of the woman who died holding the line, to know that the line was real. That's all. That's the whole of my argument. The rest isn't mine to offer. It's yours to refuse."

The map pulsed.

And Ines, kneeling in the dust of the guild's most forbidden room, understood with sudden total clarity that refusing had stopped being one of the available options somewhere between midnight and dawn, and that she had perhaps always known it would end like this—that some part of her had spent thirty-one years practicing smallness precisely because some older part of her had always suspected she was shaped like something large.

"I don't consent," she whispered. It mattered to say it. It mattered that it be true in the record, whatever record there was. "I want that noted. I don't consent."

"Noted," Sebastian said gravely. "By every ledger that matters."

He pulled the map from the floor, broke the wax seal with his thumbnail, and pressed it into her open hands.

It burned.

Not like fire—like *writing*. Heat moved up her palms and into her wrists and she felt it tracing, line by line, the way her pen felt when she copied a chart she loved, except inverted, except she was the page. She cried out. She tried to drop it and could not; her fingers had closed around it as if her own hands had signed something without consulting her. Light bled between her knuckles. Through the burning she could see it—actually see it now, the way she saw all maps, but a thousandfold deeper—the covenant laid out like a living thing, three rivers of sworn names converging, two of them cut, cut, *cut*, and the third running dry toward a space shaped exactly like her.

She heard herself screaming from very far away. She heard Sebastian saying, low and steady, "Breathe. Breathe, keeper. I'm here. I'm here until the last of me."

And then it stopped.

The map was gone. Not dropped, not hidden—gone, dissolved into her skin, and when she turned her hands over she found the covenant written across both palms in fine silver lines, roads and rivers and three converging threads, one of them ending in a small mark over the heel of her right hand that might have been a signature or might have been a scar.

The silence afterward was enormous.

Ines became aware, gradually, of several things. That she was on the floor and did not remember falling. That Sebastian was cradling her head, having caught her, and that his arms were nearly too weak to manage even that. That the blackening had climbed past his elbow. That the vault door, at the top of the stairs, had begun to glow with ward-light—the salt wards acknowledging a master key turning in the lock above.

"He's here," Sebastian breathed. "Faster than I hoped. Listen—the healers will come down first, and they will not be healers. Whatever they offer you, whatever they call it—sedation, protection, 'a quiet room to recover'—it will be erasure. Vey cannot undo what's written in your skin, but he can take the woman who understands it. Do you hear me? The words survive. The reader doesn't have to."

Footsteps on the stairs. More than one set. Measured, unhurried, terrible in their patience.

"Sebastian." She grabbed his collar. The silver lines on her palm flared where they touched him, and she felt—absurdly, horribly—one clean image pass between them, unasked: a hillside, a fire, two young people laughing over a chart. His memory or hers; she couldn't tell. "What do I *do*?"

"Run. Later. When you can. Trust the roads only you can see." His gray eyes held hers. "And when you meet the wolf—because you will, moonrise is coming, and I won't be able to stop it—look at him before you decide what he is. Look at him the way you look at maps. Find the true shape underneath."

The door at the top of the stairs opened.

Guildmaster Vey came down alone.

She had seen him a hundred times—at convocations, at the annual sealing of the ledgers, gliding through the scriptorium offering mild encouragement to the junior clerks. He was a comfortable-looking man, silver-bearded, soft-spoken, famous for remembering everyone's name. She had liked him. She had *liked* him, and the realization of how completely that liking had been managed—curated, like an exhibit—made her sick.

Behind him, filling the doorway, stood four figures in undyed robes with their hoods back. Their faces were pleasant and empty as unscaled paper. The guild called them restorers. Ines had always assumed they restored damaged charts.

Vey's gaze went to Sebastian, and for one unguarded instant Ines saw what lived under the grandfather warmth: something old, and tired, and frightened, and utterly ruthless about its own survival.

"Sebastian." Vey sighed, and it sounded almost genuine. "Twenty years, and you still insist on doing everything the hard way."

"You taught me that," Sebastian said from the floor, smiling with bloodless lips.

"And you've given it away regardless." Vey turned to Ines, and the warmth came back up like lamplight, seamless, horrible. "Child. Ines, isn't it? Third drawer, eastern stacks, the one who rebinds the water-damaged folios so lovingly. You must be terrified. Come upstairs. We'll get you tea, and a healer, and you'll sleep, and by tomorrow this will feel like a strange dream. I promise you"—he spread his soft hands—"no harm will come to you. The guild protects its own."

*The guild protects its own.* How many times had she heard that phrase, warm as bread? How many times had it been the entire architecture of her life?

Ines looked at her palms. The silver covenant gleamed there, patient, permanent, hers. She looked at Sebastian, whose breathing had gone shallow and whose eyes were fixed on her with a fierce, fading attention, memorizing her, she realized. Committing her to whatever memory he had left.

She looked at Guildmaster Vey, and her gift—the gift she had spent a lifetime pretending was a minor talent for tidy work—read him the way it read all maps, all documents, all drawn and written things. And what it showed her, layered beneath the kind face like a forgery beneath a charter, was a man standing astride a broken bridge, collecting tolls, praying daily that no one would ever ask him why the bridge fell.

"I know what you are," Ines said. Her voice shook. She let it. "I've always known. I just didn't let myself finish reading."

Something flickered behind Vey's eyes—the first honest expression she had ever seen on him, and it was fear.

"Take her gently," he said to the restorers.

Sebastian's hand found hers in the dust and squeezed, once, with the last strength he had. Above them, through stone and iron and salt, the sun cleared the horizon—and somewhere far below the guild, in the deep places where the old roads still ran, the moon began, inexorably, to rise.

* * *

The restorers' hands were cold and impersonal, and Ines learned quickly that struggling was what they were prepared for. So she didn't struggle. She went limp, she stammered, she let them half-carry her up the stairs into the grey morning light of the guild's infirmary corridor—and all the while, her new-lit sight was working, reading the building she had walked through for eleven years as if for the first time.

Every wall had a true shape underneath. Every corridor had a twin. The guild was built on the catacombs of the old covenant-keepers, and the builders had walled up the doors and drawn false walls over them—but false walls were still drawings, and drawings were her native language.

There. Behind the linen closet on the infirmary's east side, a door that wasn't a door, sealed with a sigil her mother's handwriting seemed to recognize.

They put her in a white room with a comfortable bed and a carafe of water and one window, barred, facing the sea. One of the restorers remained, seated by the door, hands folded, watching her with those pleasant blank eyes. Waiting, she understood, for the sedative in the water to do its work. She could smell it now—faintly sweet, like the preservative they used on starlight bottles.

Ines sat on the bed. She smiled her invisible smile, the one that had kept her safe for eleven years, and said, "Could I have a moment? To pray?"

The restorer considered this. Nodded, and turned politely toward the wall.

Ines poured the water out the window, gap by careful gap, and sat with her back straight and her eyes closed and her silver-marked palms open in her lap, and inside the folded space behind her heartbeat she traced, over and over, the shape of the hidden door, the route down through the catacombs, the road out of the city that appeared on no chart in the guild's possession because it existed only in the covenant now written into her skin.

Below the horizon, the sun sank by degrees. Moonrise was coming. Sebastian had told her to look at the wolf before she decided what it was.

First, she thought, folding the plan smaller and smaller, tighter and tighter, like a chart made to fit in a pocket—

First she had to get out of the guild alive.

The restorer turned back around. "Time to sleep now," it said kindly.

"Yes," said Ines Okafor, last heir of the Covenant of Threads, and reached for the water pitcher.
