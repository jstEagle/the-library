## 4. The Guildmaster's Kindness

They took Sebastian to the infirmary on a litter of oiled canvas, and Ines followed because no one thought to stop her.

That was the trick of being invisible, she had learned over eleven years in the Guild of Unmapped Realms: people did not bar the door to someone they had already decided not to see. She was the woman who catalogued shipments, who corrected decimal errors in toll ledgers, who drank her tea alone at the second-floor window where the light was best for reading vellum. When she walked behind the healers carrying the half-dead stranger, their eyes slid past her the way water slides past a stone. She could have been carrying a mop.

The map's mark still burned on her left palm. She kept her hand curled shut so no one would ask about it, though the heat pulsed through her fingers like a second heartbeat, and twice she caught herself pressing the fist against her sternum as if she could smother it there.

*Heir of the Covenant of Threads.* He had said it in front of Guildmaster Vey himself, and Vey had gone very still — not shocked, Ines realized later, replaying it. *Receiving.* Like a man hearing a figure quoted that he had already calculated.

Now she stood in the doorway of the infirmary ward and watched them work on Sebastian. He lay under a sheet drawn to his chest, and even unconscious he did not look entirely human. The wolf was in him the way grain is in wood — visible when the light crossed him right. His forearms were furred to the elbow with silver-grey. His canines, when his mouth fell open on a fevered breath, were wrong. And along his throat ran a scar she recognized from her own ledgers: a brand shaped like an unspooling thread, the covenant-mark, the one the guild's histories described only in the past tense.

*Bound realms meant bound roads,* the old texts said. *Bound roads meant bound tolls. The Covenant of Threads was dissolved by mutual accord two generations past.*

Dissolved by mutual accord. Yet here was its first keeper — if the dying man could be believed — branded and cursed and bleeding on guild linen.

"Ines." Sister Calloway, the head healer, appeared at her elbow with a basin of reddening water. "You shouldn't be here."

"I'm recording the incident," Ines said, and produced her ledger, which made everything true. "Standard procedure for unsanctioned arrivals through the salt door."

Calloway frowned but didn't argue. Procedure was the guild's true religion; even its lies were filed correctly. Ines opened the ledger to a blank page and wrote nothing, letting the pen hover while she watched the healers pack Sebastian's wounds with moss that smelled of cold iron. The gashes across his ribs were not knife wounds. She had catalogued enough confiscated weapons to know what claws looked like when they went deep.

His own claws, or someone else's? The question arrived unbidden and would not leave.

"His fever's climbing," Calloway murmured to her assistant. "And there's something wrong with the blood. Look at the way it dries — like it's trying to be two things at once."

Ines wrote: *Patient stable. Awaiting guildmaster's instruction.* Lies, all three clauses, entered into the record in her own careful hand. It frightened her how easily the sentences came.

* * *

Guildmaster Vey summoned her at the ninth bell, when the fog off the Salt Reach had thickened enough that the guildhall's windows showed nothing but white.

She had been summoned to Vey's study perhaps six times in eleven years — always for inventory discrepancies, always brief, always ending with Vey saying some version of *the guild relies on your precision, Mistress Okafor*, which was praise shaped exactly like a leash. She climbed the spiral stair with her fist still curled around the burning palm and told herself the fear in her stomach was ordinary. Anyone would be afraid, summoned after a night like this one.

Vey's study occupied the tower's crown, and it was the most honest room in the guildhall: maps everywhere, real ones, old ones, hung open like skins drying. Most guild members never saw them. The maps the guild sold in its public gallery were decorative fictions — pretty coastlines, plausible mountains — because the actual geography of the unmapped realms was a monopoly asset, and monopolies survived on scarcity. Ines had spent her career among the fictions downstairs and her secret gift among the truths up here, reading shapes that other eyes couldn't hold.

"Mistress Okafor." Vey stood at the window with his back to her, a spare grey man in a spare grey coat, hands clasped behind him like a scholar pretending not to be a general. "You've had an eventful night."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

"The dying man. Tell me everything he said. Exactly. Word for word." He turned then, and his eyes — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had outlasted four rivals and two wars — settled on her with a weight she had never felt from him before. Not looking *through* her this time. Looking *at* her. It was worse.

Ines told him. She gave him the words in order: *heir of the Covenant of Threads, the map will show you, don't let them file it.* She watched Vey's face as she spoke, and because her gift was for reading shapes — the true shape beneath any drawn line — she saw what moved beneath his composure. Not surprise. Confirmation. Grief, maybe, of a very old and well-managed kind. And underneath both, threaded through them like dark wire:

Relief.

"He was delirious," Vey said when she finished. "Covenant-talk. Superstition from before your time, before mine, honestly. The guild has kept the peace between the realms for sixty years without any covenant, and we've done it by *filing things properly*." A thin smile. "You understand filing, Mistress Okafor."

"I try to, Guildmaster."

"The map he carried. The wax-sealed one." Vey extended his hand, palm up, an easy gesture, almost paternal. "It's guild property now. Recovered from an intruder at a warded door. I'll want it for safekeeping."

There it was. Ines looked at his open hand and felt the mark on her own palm answer it, heat rising like a tide.

Because the map was not in the evidence locker. The map was against her skin.

When Sebastian had pressed it into her hands in the vault — before the healers came, before Vey's too-quiet arrival — the wax seal had split, and the parchment inside had done something parchment does not do. It had poured itself into her. Not burned, exactly. *Read itself into her*, the way ink reads into paper, and what remained in her hands was a blank sheet and a mark on her palm shaped like a coastline she didn't recognize, a border, a thread. She had told no one. She had wrapped the blank parchment in oilcloth and hidden it in the false bottom of her tea chest, and she had spent the hours since dawn telling herself she'd confess everything to Vey, of course she would, Vey was the guild and the guild was the only solid thing in her life —

— and now Vey stood with his hand out, asking for a map that no longer existed, and Ines understood with a lurch that she had already chosen. Sometime between the salt door and this stair, without deciding, she had become a person who lied to the Guildmaster.

"I'll retrieve it from evidence directly," she said.

"No need." Vey lowered his hand without hurry, and the absence of urgency was its own message: he knew she was lying, and he was permitting it, and both of those facts were now instruments he held. "Sister Hallow has it in hand."

Cold walked down Ines's spine. Sister Hallow, the enforcer, the woman whose reputation was built on the phrase *no loose threads*. If Hallow had the map — the blank map, the emptied husk — then Hallow would also have questions about why it weighed nothing and read nothing, and questions were Hallow's trade.

"You look pale," Vey said gently. "Sit. Please." He gestured to the chair across from his desk, and Ines sat because refusing would be a louder act than sitting. From the desk drawer he took a small tin and set it between them: ginger candies, the kind he kept for junior clerks during audit season. The kindness of it was so precisely calibrated that it made her teeth ache. "You've served this guild faithfully since you were nineteen years old. Your mother served before you."

The sentence landed strangely. Her mother had been a surveyor, lost in the Glasswood when Ines was a child — that much was family legend, thin as fog. But Vey said *served* the way a man says a word he has checked the spelling of.

"The guild protects its own," Vey continued. "That is the whole of our charter, whatever romantics say about doors between worlds. Passage, order, protection. So hear me, Ines Okafor, and hear me kindly: there are people — above my station, if you can believe it — who heard the word *covenant* spoken in my hall last night, and who will come asking. When they do, the safest place for anyone named in that madman's delirium is inside the guild's full protection. Do you understand what I'm offering?"

"Protection," Ines said.

"Complete protection." Vey smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "All it costs is your honesty. Anything you're carrying from last night — anything at all — brought to me, sealed, filed, forgotten. That's all. No punishment. No questions beyond the first ones. Just the truth, given freely to the guild that keeps you." He slid the tin of ginger candies an inch toward her. "Loyalty is a road, Mistress Okafor. It's easiest to walk it in daylight."

* * *

She walked back down the spiral stair in daylight, such as it was — fog-light, sourceless — and her mind was doing the thing it did with difficult maps: pulling back, zooming out, looking for the shape under the lines.

Vey had asked for the map before confirming it existed. No — he had sent Hallow for it before Ines reported it. Which meant Vey knew what Sebastian carried before Sebastian ever reached the salt door. Which meant either the guild had been hunting the man, or the guild had been waiting for him.

And one more thing, the thing that had been scratching at her since the study, since long before, if she was honest: the charter.

Every guild clerk memorized the Charter of Unmapped Realms in their first year. Ines had copied it out four times as a probationer, beautiful copperplate on cream stock, and it hung framed in the entry hall where every visitor could read the guild's founding promise. She passed it on her way to the records floor, and today — for the first time in eleven years — she stopped in front of it and *looked*.

Her gift was not magic, she had always told herself. It was attention. Where other cartographers saw lines, she saw decisions — every stroke a choice, every choice leaving a residue, the way a palimpsest keeps its ghosts. She had used it to spot forged toll-stamps and smugglers' charts. She had never once pointed it at the guild's own documents, because pointing it there had never once occurred to her. You did not audit the ground you stood on.

Now she stood, and she looked, and the frame fell away.

Beneath the charter — under its ink, under its vellum, layered like sediment — there was another document. Older. The vellum of the charter had been stretched over it the way a new map is pasted over an old one, and the original bled through in places only she could read: a different preamble, a different promise. Not *passage, order, protection*. Something larger. Words in an older hand arranged around a central device she knew from exactly one place — the brand on a dying man's throat.

A thread, unspooling, passing through three points.

Three names had been written at the three points. Two were struck through with a single clean line each — deliberate, formal, the way a ledger strikes a closed account. The third name was not struck through. The third name was smudged, worn, as if a thumb had worried at it for years, trying to blur it without crossing it out.

Ines leaned close enough that her breath fogged the glass. The glass fogged, and cleared, and she read the third name.

Her vision swam. She put a hand against the frame to steady herself — her left hand, the marked one — and the burn flared so hot she gasped, and a passing clerk asked if she was quite all right, and she said yes, fine, dust in my eye, and smiled the small forgettable smile of Ines Okafor, invisible woman, precise clerk, loyal servant of the guild.

But walking away, she counted what she now knew, the way she counted a shipment, item by item, because counting kept panic at bay:

One. The guild's charter was a forgery laid over the covenant's corpse, and someone had gone to extraordinary lengths to make the forgery seamless — except to her.

Two. Two of the covenant's three keepers were struck from the record, and the third keeper's name had been hers, written long ago by a hand she almost — *almost* — recognized.

Three. The Guildmaster had offered her protection in exchange for truth, from an institution whose foundational document was a lie, while Sister Hallow carried a hollowed-out map through the halls below.

Four. Somewhere in this building was a man who had called her *heir* with his last lucid breath, and she had left him alone with healers who answered to Vey.

Ines stopped on the landing between floors. Below her, the records hall hummed with its ordinary morning — pens scratching, carts rattling, the great machine of the guild filing the world into obedience. Above her, the fog pressed against the high windows like something waiting to be let in.

Eleven years she had kept her head down and her ledgers clean, and she had called it safety. Standing on that landing, palm burning, she understood for the first time that invisibility had never been a shield. It was a shelf. Things put on shelves were stored. And things that were stored belonged to whoever owned the building.

She turned, not toward the records floor, but down — toward the infirmary, taking the stairs two at a time, her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield that had never once been one.

She was halfway there when she heard the bell.

Not the ninth bell, not the hour bell — the *ward* bell, the one that rang only when the salt door's protections were breached or tested, a thin urgent sound she had heard twice in eleven years, both times drills. This was no drill. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, rolling up through the guildhall's stones from somewhere far below the foundations, came a sound Ines had no ledger-category for: a long, low, animal howl, rising from the direction of the infirmary, where the moon was not yet up but something inside a fevered man was already answering it.
