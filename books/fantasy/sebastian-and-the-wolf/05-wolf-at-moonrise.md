## 5. Wolf at Moonrise

The moon would rise in forty minutes. Ines knew this because she had spent the last hour pretending to inventory the starlight shipment while actually watching the sky through the vault's single arrow-slit window, and because Sebastian had told her, twice, in a voice that kept sliding between a man's and something rougher: *When it comes up, I won't be able to help you. Whatever I am under it—I can't help you.*

She had believed him the first time. She was choosing not to believe him the second.

"Say it again," she said now, cinching the last buckle on the pack she'd assembled from things no ledger would miss: a coil of surveyor's cord, two flasks of water, a stub of tallow candle, and the folded-space diagram she'd copied out of Ledger Nine with hands that hadn't stopped shaking since the guildmaster's visit. "The part about the catacombs."

Sebastian sat propped against the map cabinet, one arm pressed to his bandaged side. The wound beneath had stopped bleeding hours ago, but the skin around it had begun to change—she'd seen it when she changed the dressing, silvered fur creeping along the edges like frost claiming a windowpane. His eyes were fever-bright.

"There are three ways down," he said. "The stairs behind the scriptorium, which they watch. The well shaft, which is watched by gravity. And the catacomb door in the old chart room, which hasn't been watched since before your guildmaster was born—because everyone forgot it existed."

"Everyone forgot," Ines said. "Except you."

"I forget most things." He smiled, and it cost him something; she could see it cost him. "But I remember doors. Doors are how you get in and out of places, and getting in and out of places used to be my whole—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Somewhere behind his eyes, a sentence had hit a wall it couldn't climb. "*Used to be* something. That's all I have. A shape where a life used to be."

Ines knelt beside him and checked the bandage though she didn't need to. It gave her hands something to do that wasn't trembling.

"The moon," she said. "Tell me what happens. All of it. Don't be kind about it."

"All right." He exhaled slowly. "It starts as heat. Like standing too close to a forge. Then my bones decide they were only ever suggestions. Then—" his voice flattened, went clinical, a man reciting the weather report of his own damnation, "—then there isn't a me anymore. There's hunger with legs. It remembers nothing except what it wants, and what it wants is usually warm and moving." He looked at her then, and his eyes were clear and terrible. "You should leave me here. Lock the vault. Let them find a wolf in a locked room and draw their own conclusions. You could still go back to being invisible, Ines. You're very good at it. I've watched."

"You've been unconscious for most of two days."

"I watched before that," he said softly. "I think I've been watching you for longer than that. Longer than makes sense."

She didn't ask him what he meant. She was afraid the answer would be *nothing*, and more afraid it would be *something*, and either way the moon was coming up and there was no time for answers that rearranged a person.

She stood, shouldered the pack, and took the candle.

"Chart room," she said. "Now. Before I change my mind and do the smart thing."

* * *

The old chart room smelled of dust and dead ink, its shelves holding maps so obsolete their realms had likely crumbled or drifted beyond recall. Ines found the door exactly where Sebastian said—a seam in the paneling so old it had become the wall—and pressed her palm flat against it the way her gift taught her to press against any surface that might be lying.

The wall wasn't lying. But it was hiding.

Beneath the plaster, faint as a watermark, she felt the true shape: a stair descending in a slow spiral, air moving through it, stone remembering footsteps. Her cartographer's sight read it the way other people read a face. She traced the hidden outline with her finger, spoke the counter-phrase every guild child learned as a game—*the map under the map, the way under the way*—and the seam sighed open.

Cold air breathed up at them, smelling of wet stone and something older, mineral, like the inside of the world.

"Three flights," Ines whispered. "Then the gallery, then the river gate. If we move fast—"

The light through the high window turned the color of struck brass.

Sebastian made a sound she would never afterward be able to describe—not a word, not a scream, something between a man's gasp and an animal's warning—and dropped to his knees on the top step.

"Ines." His voice had gravel in it now. "Run ahead. Don't look back. Don't let it see your face before it decides what it is tonight."

"No."

"*Ines.*"

"I'm a cartographer," she said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I don't abandon things mid-route."

He laughed—a broken, awful sound—and then the heat took him.

She saw it happen and her mind, treacherous, precise, catalogued it anyway: the way his spine arched and reorganized itself, vertebra by vertebra, like a hand reshuffling bones; the way his hands split and darkened and became paws; the way the man's face stretched, lengthened, grew a muzzle, fur flooding up his throat like ink through water. The bandage burst. The shirt tore along seams no tailor ever meant. In the space of twelve heartbeats—the she counted them, God help her, she counted—the man she'd pulled through the salt door was gone, and on the stair above her stood a wolf the size of a pony, black as a hole cut in the world, with eyes that held no Sebastian at all.

Only hunger. Only the want of warm and moving things.

The wolf looked down at her.

Every instinct Ines owned screamed at once. Her body did the honest thing: she froze, breath locked in her chest, the candle shaking in her grip so its flame stuttered shadows across the walls. The spiral stair was narrow. There was nowhere to go. Below her, the catacombs opened into darkness; above her, teeth.

The wolf's nostrils flared. It tasted the air—her fear, her blood, the warmth she radiated like a beacon—and its lips peeled back from those impossible teeth, and it came down the first step toward her.

*Don't run,* some distant scholarly corner of her mind observed. *Running marks you as prey. Stand still. Stand very—*

The wolf reached the second step. Its head lowered. Hot breath washed over her hands.

And then it stopped.

Stopped the way a man stops mid-stride when a name is called across a crowded street—abruptly, completely, as if something outside the world had spoken. Its ears went forward. Its head tilted. And in those black eyes, deep down beneath the hunger, something surfaced. Something looked *out*.

The wolf stared at her face.

Not at her throat. Not at the soft vulnerable geography of a kill. At her *face*—at the line of her jaw, the set of her mouth, the tears she hadn't permitted until this exact moment—as though reading a document written in a language it had forgotten but had once known fluently.

A sound came out of it. Low, confused, almost wounded. Not a growl. Something closer to a question.

"I know," Ines whispered. Her voice cracked on it. "I don't understand it either."

The wolf took one step closer, close enough that its fur brushed her sleeve, close enough that she could feel the enormous engine of its heartbeat—and it pressed its muzzle, briefly, impossibly gently, against her palm. Against the hand that still bore, faint and healing, the burn-scar of the map Sebastian had pressed into it two nights ago.

Then it turned, and bounded down the spiral into the dark, and waited at the bottom, tail low, watching her with an expression that on a human face she would have called *ashamed*.

* * *

Inside the curse, there was no language. There were only pressures.

There was the pressure of the moon, huge and white behind everything, like a second sun made of commandment. There was the pressure of hunger, vast and simple, wanting warm meat, wanting running, wanting the red arithmetic of the hunt. And underneath both, deeper than both, there were fragments—shards of a life floating in the dark like ice in black water:

*A door. Salt on the threshold. The smell of old paper and someone's careful handwriting.*

*Fire. A tower burning. A voice screaming his name—his real name, the one the curse had eaten—and him unable to answer.*

*A woman's laugh. Where? When? Whose?*

The wolf-form ran because running was all the fragments allowed. Down through the catacombs, past alcoves of stacked bones and drowned statuary, following the cold thread of air toward the river gate. The man inside it clung to the fragments the way a drowning sailor clings to wreckage. *Remember the door. Remember the fire. Remember the—*

Then the scent again. The woman. The one from the salt door, the one whose hands smelled of ink and iron-gall and whose pulse he had listened to for two days through fever dreams without knowing why.

The hunger said: *warm, close, easy.*

Something older than the hunger said: *no.*

It wasn't a memory. Memories had burned away; the curse had seen to that, scouring him clean as bone. This was something the curse had failed to reach—something buried too deep, woven too far in, below the level where forgetting could dig. It rose in him now like a lamp surfacing through dark water, and it was not a thought, because thoughts needed words, and words were gone.

It was an image. One image. Clearer than anything else in the whole flooded wreck of him:

*A woman's face. Younger, maybe, or the same age—it didn't matter. Dark-skinned, sharp-eyed, ink on her fingers, standing in front of him with her chin lifted in that particular stubborn angle, saying something he couldn't hear across the distance of years. And the feeling attached to it, the feeling that came with the image like thunder comes with lightning:*

*Trust. Specific, chosen, hard-won trust. Trust that had survived something.*

The image was her face. It was Ines's face.

The wolf stood at the bottom of the stair, trembling with the collision of it—hunger screaming *kill*, and this older thing, this sunken lamp, whispering *you already chose her, whoever you were, whatever you were, you already chose*—and when she descended into the dark with her little candle and her terrified, unyielding spine, the choice made itself again, effortlessly, the way a river finds the sea.

He spared her. No—he corrected himself, in whatever broken grammar remained to him. He didn't spare her. Sparing implied she had been in danger. She had never been in danger. Not from him. Perhaps that was the only thing the curse had never managed to take.

They moved through the catacombs together, the woman with the candle and the beast beside her, and somewhere above them the guild slept on, dreaming its forged charter, guarding its stolen roads.

At the river gate, rusted hinges shrieking, the night opened before them—cold, enormous, scattered with stars. The moon hung fat and rising over the rooftops of the city, and beyond the city, far off across the dark fields, Ines could see it even from here, could feel it through the scar on her palm: a thin place in the world, a shimmer where the sky frayed like worn cloth.

East. Toward Ashfall. Toward whatever remained of the covenant her blood remembered and her mind did not.

The wolf looked at her. She looked at the wolf. Between them lay everything neither of them could say.

"Stay close," Ines said, stepping through the gate into the open night. "And if the hunger gets loud—we'll deal with it. Together."

Somewhere beneath the fur and the fury, in the flooded dark where a man used to be, Sebastian held onto one fragment and refused to let it sink: her face, lit by candleflame, choosing him twice—once at the salt door, and once on the stairs, with the teeth an inch from her hands.

The moon climbed higher. They ran east.
