## 6. The Road That Isn't on Any Map

The road began at the edge of the guild's drainage ditch, where the water went gray and forgot to be water.

Ines saw it the way she saw all true things: not with her eyes, which insisted there was nothing there but mud and bramble and a fence post leaning like a drunk, but with the older sight behind them—the one that read the shape of a place the way other people read a sentence. And the sentence written across that muddy field said *road*, in letters old enough to predate the field, the fence, possibly the ground itself.

"Step where I step," she told Sebastian. "Exactly where I step."

He moved behind her like a man walking through deep water, favoring his left side where Hallow's blade had kissed him two nights ago in the catacombs. The moon was still three nights from full; he wore his human skin like an ill-fitting coat, and every few minutes his jaw tightened as if something inside him were testing the seams.

"You're certain it's real," he said. Not a question. A man checking the knots on a rope before trusting it with his weight.

"I'm certain I can see it," Ines said. "Whether that's the same thing—" She stepped over a fence rail that did not exist on the road, and her boot came down on packed earth that had never been rained on. The difference traveled up through her bones: the road felt *old* underfoot, old and patient, the way the vault floors of the guild felt after centuries of careful feet.

Behind her, Sebastian's breath caught.

"What is it?"

"It smells like—" He stopped, frowned, pressed his knuckles to his sternum as though pressing a door shut. "Nothing. It smells like nothing. Like a room someone emptied." His voice went strange. "Why would I know what that smells like?"

Because you've walked this road before, Ines thought, and did not say. Because the curse took your memories but apparently left your nose intact, and your nose is telling you things your head can't hold. She'd been cataloguing these small returns for two days the way she catalogued everything—silently, precisely, in a ledger she kept behind her eyes. The way he'd hummed three bars of a road-song no one sang anymore. The way he'd flinched from the sigil on a milestone they passed, then stared at it like a man trying to remember why he was angry.

The road unspooled ahead of them through country that grew less certain with every mile. At first the fields on either side were ordinary—wheat, hedgerow, a distant church spire—but by midmorning the wheat was growing in colors wheat should not come in, a bruised violet at the tips, and the church spire had begun, very slightly, to flicker. Not vanish. Flicker. Present, absent, present, like a candle guttering in wind that touched only it.

"That's the drift," Ines said quietly, when Sebastian slowed to stare. "Two realms pulling apart. Where they overlap, things get... undecided."

"Undecided how?"

She pointed at a farmhouse they were approaching. It stood solid enough at first glance—thatch, stone, smoke rising from its chimney. But the smoke rose *sideways* on one side of the house and straight up on the other, as if the sky itself couldn't agree on which way was up. And in the upper window, briefly, Ines saw a woman shaking out a linen sheet—and then saw the window empty, sunlit, dust motes turning—and then the woman again, mid-motion, as if the morning were being replayed by hands that kept losing their place.

"The people who live there," Ines said, "are living two mornings. Or half of each. When the realms finish separating, one version goes with each realm, and whichever one isn't anchored—" She didn't finish. Some sentences deserved to die unfinished.

Sebastian looked at the farmhouse for a long time. "And this is happening everywhere?"

"Everywhere the covenant used to hold." She heard her own voice go flat and official, guild-voice, the voice she used for inventory. It was easier than the alternative. "Passage between realms has always required agreement between them. The covenant was the agreement. Twenty years ago it broke—or was broken—and since then everything has been held together with pins. Guild tolls. Chartered crossings. Little folded pockets of stability that we sell to whoever can pay." She kicked a stone off the road's invisible edge; it fell into the mud of the ordinary world and vanished. "Everyone else gets this."

"And your guildmaster knew."

"My guildmaster signed the charter that replaced it." The words tasted like the salt-warded door of the guildhall: clean, briny, final. "I keep coming back to that. Forgeries take effort, Sebastian. Someone sat down with the original covenant in front of them and copied around it, carefully, so no one would notice what was missing. You don't do that by accident. You don't do that in a panic. You do it at a desk, with good light and steady hands."

He was quiet for a while. Then: "You sound like you're describing yourself."

"I know." She almost smiled. "That's the part that frightens me most."

* * *

They walked the road until the light went strange.

Not dark—strange. The sun hung where it should have hung for early afternoon, but its color kept sliding, gold to copper to a pale green like the underside of leaves, and shadows pooled in the wrong directions and sometimes pointed toward the light instead of away from it. Twice they passed milestones. Both times, the words carved into them rearranged themselves as Ines tried to read, settling only when she stopped trying and let her older sight take the lettering whole: *ASHFALL, 9*. Below that, smaller, in a hand she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water—*the roads remember what the mapmakers forget.*

Her mother's hand. Her mother had walked this road, or written on it, or both. Ines filed the recognition away in the ledger behind her eyes, in the drawer marked THINGS TO SURVIVE FIRST AND FEEL LATER, and kept walking.

It was near dusk when they crested a low rise and the land opened out ahead of them, and Ines stopped so abruptly that Sebastian nearly walked into her back.

"Oh," she said. It was all she could manage.

Below them, in a bowl of hills, lay Ashfall—which should have been a border town, gray-roofed and ordinary, chimneys smoking, lamplighters beginning their rounds. And it was that. But above it, the sky was *tearing*.

There was no gentler word for it. One realm ended over the town and another realm began, and where they met, the seam showed—not a line but a wound, a long ragged gash running east to west across the heavens, through which Ines could see *elsewhere*: a second sunset burning against a different configuration of stars, mountains hanging upside down like teeth, a slow aurora bleeding colors that had no names she knew. The seam pulsed faintly, expanding and contracting, and with each pulse the town below flickered—roofs doubling, streets splitting into two versions of themselves that lay over each other like misprinted pages.

And falling from the seam, drifting down through the evening air like snow, were flakes of light. Small, soft, slow. They landed on the roofs and did not melt. They landed on the fields and the grass beneath them turned translucent, showing the soil's own ghost. As Ines watched, one drifted down and settled on the shoulder of a farmer leading a cow along a lane, and the man reached up without looking, brushed it off absently, and kept walking—as if ashfall were simply weather, simply Tuesday, simply the price of living where two worlds were divorcing.

"So that's the name," Sebastian murmured beside her. "Ashfall." He said it like a man identifying a body.

"They named the town after the symptom," Ines said. "Or the symptom named the town. I've seen references in the ledgers—border settlements that predate the break. There are eleven of them strung along the old seams. This is the largest." She found herself doing the thing she hated, the guild thing: reciting facts to avoid feeling them. She made herself stop. She made herself look properly at the sky-wound instead, at its slow pulse, at the second sunset bleeding through it.

When she was a girl, she had thought her gift was a kind of seeing. Now, looking at the frayed heaven above Ashfall, she understood it was also a kind of hearing, because the seam made a sound—a sound below sound, felt in the teeth rather than the ear—and what it said, in the language of stressed things about to fail, was: *not much longer.*

"How long?" Sebastian asked. He'd heard it too, then. Whatever else the curse had stripped from him, it had left him attuned to the world's wrongness, perhaps because he was one of its wrongnesses.

"I don't know. Weeks? Seasons? The ledgers say the drift accelerates once a seam becomes visible to the untrained eye." She swallowed. "That's tonight, apparently. Anyone down in that town can see it now. Which means the guild knows, which means Vey knows, which means—"

"Which means the race got shorter." He rolled his shoulder, wincing. "Then we shouldn't stand on a hill admiring it."

"No," Ines agreed. But she stayed one more moment, because awe is a debt and she wanted to pay it honestly. The seam was terrible and beautiful in equal measure, the most important thing she had ever seen with either of her sights, and some cartographer's bone in her body ached to chart it, to measure it, to give it coordinates and a name in the ledger of impossible things. *Fraying at approximately four degrees per season,* she thought. *Visible to the unaided eye. Population at risk: everyone.*

Then the awe curdled, as awe does when it looks too long, and what remained underneath was dread—cold, specific, personal. Because she could see, with the older sight, what the flakes of falling light actually were. Not ash. Not snow. They were *names*. Tiny, worn-smooth, unlabeled—the names of things losing their definition as the realms pulled apart, shedding identity the way a rotting fruit sheds skin. Every flake that landed and didn't melt was a river forgetting it was a river, a street forgetting its own length, a person in that town waking tomorrow slightly unsure of their own face.

The covenant hadn't just bound realms together. It had *agreed* them into coherence. And Vey had let the agreement lapse for a monopoly on tolls.

Ines started down the hill faster than caution recommended, and Sebastian lengthened his stride to keep pace with her, and neither of them spoke again until the first flakes of falling light began landing on their own shoulders, cool as coins from a dead man's pocket.

* * *

Ashfall smelled of woodsmoke and tallow and, underneath, a faint ozone tang like the air before lightning—the smell of a sky working too hard. The town had the look of places that have decided to be brave in public: shutters closed, doors barred, lamps lit early, and everywhere little shrines—candles in windowsills, sprigs of rowan nailed over lintels, chalk circles drawn on thresholds by hands that hoped geometry was a prayer God honored.

The inn was called the Second Sunrise, which Ines found either very brave or very grim, and its common room was full but quiet, the way rooms are quiet when everyone inside is listening to the same silence. Conversation died when they entered and resumed in fragments when they seated themselves in a corner. Ines counted the exits—front, kitchen, a stair to rooms above—out of habit, and then counted them again out of new fear, because somewhere behind them Sister Hallow was not stopping, and Corvo's face floated up in her memory, the fence's last look as the enforcers took him, and she pushed the guilt back down into the drawer marked LATER.

A serving woman brought stew and dark bread and stood a moment too long, staring at Sebastian. Not at his face—at his hands, resting on the table. At the fine silver scarring webbed across his knuckles, the marks the curse left even in his human skin.

"You'll want a room on the east side," the woman said at last, to Sebastian, not Ines. "West side faces the seam. Light comes through the walls on the west side, some nights. Wakes folk." She set down the bread. "Moon's near full."

"We noticed," Ines said evenly.

"Mm." The woman's eyes moved to Ines, took in the guild-gray traveling cloak with the badge torn off—torn off badly, threads showing—and something in her expression shifted from hospitality to arithmetic. "We don't ask questions here. Costs extra not to. But I'll tell you what everyone tells everyone, free of charge, since you're new." She leaned in slightly. "Whatever you're running from—it's slower than the sky is. Mind that."

She left. Sebastian exhaled slowly. "Friendly town."

"Frightened towns are honest towns," Ines said. "Everyone here has already lost the argument with denial. It's the safest company we could keep." She tore the bread, handed him half, and realized as she did it that the gesture had become easy somewhere in the last hundred miles—that she had stopped keeping a ledger of what she owed him and what he owed her and simply begun dividing things in half. The observation unsettled her more than the seam overhead. She put it in the drawer.

They ate. Around them, the common room murmured: a carter complaining that the road east had developed a hill overnight that no one remembered; a grandmother telling children, in the tone of a bedtime ritual, that if the lights fell on them they were to *stay still and think their own name as hard as they could*; two merchants arguing over whether the seam had widened since last week (it had; everyone knew it had; saying so aloud was different).

And then, near the hearth, a man began to sing.

It was an old song—old in the way the road had been old, older than the town, and Ines's older sight flared involuntarily, reading the song's shape the way it read maps: *this is a binding-song, this is half of a pair, this is the answering verse to something.* The man's voice was rough and the words were half-lost, worn smooth like the falling flakes:

*Oh the seam shall knit and the sunder mend,*
*when three keep true and one keeps faith—*

He faltered there. Frowned. Picked at the fire with a poker.

"That's as far as anyone remembers," the serving woman said, passing with a tray. "Rest of it went with the rest of it."

But Ines wasn't listening to her. Across the table, Sebastian had gone white to the lips, his spoon frozen halfway to his mouth, and his lips were moving—silently, helplessly—shaping the next lines of a verse he could not possibly remember, in a rhythm his body knew and his mind did not.

"*Three keep true,*" he whispered, barely audible, staring at nothing. "*and one keeps faith, and the third—*" His hand clenched on the table hard enough to whiten the scars on his knuckles. "Ines. The third verse. I almost—I can *almost*—"

Above them, through the roof, through the west-facing wall, light seeped suddenly into the room—pale, sourceless, wrong—like dawn arriving twenty years early and from the wrong direction. Every candle in the inn guttered. Every conversation stopped. Outside, from the lane, came a sound Ines would carry for the rest of her life however long it proved to be: the whole town of Ashfall, at once, going silent to listen to its own sky tear a little wider.

The seam had pulsed again. Closer, this time. She could feel it in her teeth.

Sebastian's eyes found hers across the table, and in them was terror of a very particular kind—not of the wolf, not of Hallow, but of the almost-memory dissolving even now behind them like a flake of light on warm stone.

"Ines," he said. "I think I'm one of the three."
