## 7. Debts in Ashfall

Sebastian had learned, over twenty years of being two creatures stitched into one skin, to measure his days by what he could hold onto. As a man, he held onto names. As a wolf, he held onto smells. And in the hours between moonrise and dawn—those gray, wet hours when he was neither and both—he held onto nothing at all except the fact that he was still holding on.

Ashfall smelled like iron and burnt sugar. The border town sat where two realms rubbed against each other like millstones, and everything in it was slightly wrong: bread that tasted faintly of someone else's childhood, shadows that fell a half-second late, a sky that frayed overhead like old rope unraveling into threads of impossible color. He had been here before. His body knew it before his mind did—the way his feet turned left at the tanner's row without instruction, the way his hackles rose at the sight of a particular crooked chimney, the way something in his chest clenched around a word he couldn't reach.

*Corvo.*

The name surfaced whole, unasked for, the way some memories did—floating up from whatever drowned place the curse kept them, gasping for air.

"You know this town," Ines said beside him. She had her ledger out, walking with her eyes half-closed, reading the streets the way other people read faces. "Or rather, you *will* know it. There are roads under these roads that haven't been walked yet. I can feel them pulling."

"Then we're not lost," Sebastian said.

"We're never lost." She said it lightly, but her knuckles were white around the ledger's spine. Three days since the catacombs, three days of running, and she still flinched every time a door opened behind them. "That's not the same as being safe."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

They came into Ashfall's market square as the light failed, which was a mistake he should have known better than to make. Border towns kept strange hours; the real business of Ashfall began when the sky started to fray visibly, when the seams between realms glowed like heat-cracks in glass and the honest folk went indoors. What remained in the streets after dark were the people who traded in what leaked through those seams—and the people who traded in debts.

The stall was tucked beneath the crooked chimney his body had flinched at. A lean man sat behind it sorting objects that shouldn't have existed in the same box: a compass that pointed at regrets, six eggs of clear crystal with storms turning inside them, a child's shoe that whispered. He looked up as they approached, and Sebastian watched recognition move across his face like weather.

Not across his face. Across his wolf-shape. Because Corvo—whoever Corvo had been to him, whoever Sebastian had been to Corvo—wasn't looking at the man standing in front of him. He was looking through the man at the animal underneath, at the shape that moonrise would carve out of him in an hour's time, and what moved across his features was not surprise.

It was satisfaction.

"Well," said the fence, folding his hands atop his crate of impossible things. "Twenty years, four months, and eleven days. I'd given up counting, but apparently my ledger didn't." He tapped a black book at his elbow, its pages thick as a brick, its cover scarred by candle wax. "You owe me a debt, keeper."

The word *keeper* landed in Sebastian's chest and detonated silently. Beside him, he felt Ines go very still.

"I think you've mistaken me for someone else," Sebastian said carefully.

"Oh, I don't think so." Corvo rose. Up close he was older than his voice, gray threaded through a black beard, one eye clouded pale as a fish's belly. "I've sold enough false relics to know the true ones when they walk into my square. You have the look of a thing that made an oath and got caught in the machinery of it. That's a smell, friend. It doesn't wash out." His good eye slid to Ines, then back. "And you've brought me the heir. Even better. Debts compound."

"We're leaving," Sebastian said, and took Ines's elbow.

"Of course you can leave." Corvo didn't move to stop them. That was the worst part, later—that he never once moved to stop them. "Walk away. But walk away knowing that I know what you are, and that by tomorrow morning everyone who matters in Ashfall will know too. The guild pays well for news of runaway heirs and cursed keepers. Sister Hallow's enforcers pay better. And there are others, less official, who pay in ways you don't want to imagine." He smiled, and it was almost kind, which made it worse. "Or we can settle our accounts like civilized people. One name. That's all I ever asked for. One name, and we're square, and I'll throw in safe passage to the Glasswood as interest."

Sebastian stopped. He hadn't decided to stop; his body had done it for him, the way it turned left at the tanner's row. Something in the fence's words had snagged on a hook deep inside him, and now he stood suspended, feeling the barb work.

"What name?" Ines asked. Her voice was steady. She had moved half a step forward, putting herself between him and the fence without appearing to notice she'd done it.

"The name of the person who broke the covenant." Corvo said it simply, the way a merchant quotes a price. "Twenty years ago, someone betrayed the Covenant of Threads. Sold it, or shattered it—I never learned which. But I knew all three keepers once, in the way a fence knows valuable things: briefly, and from below." He touched the black book. "One of them owed me. Not money. A name. The name of the traitor. They died owing it, and their debts pass to—" his clouded eye fixed on Sebastian, "—whoever carries their curse."

The square had gone quiet around them, or perhaps it had always been quiet and Sebastian was only now hearing it. Overhead, the sky tore a little wider. Through the gap, for one vertiginous second, he could see another realm's stars, arranged in constellations no one alive had named.

"I don't remember," Sebastian said.

The words came out of him flat and ugly, and he heard immediately how they sounded—not an explanation but an excuse, the whine of a debtor pleading poverty. Corvo heard it too. The fence's expression didn't change, which was its own kind of answer.

"No," Corvo agreed. "I suppose you wouldn't."

* * *

They ended up in a room above a tavern whose sign showed a fish drowning in air—a border-town joke, or a border-town truth—and Sebastian sat on the edge of the narrow bed with his hands hanging between his knees and hated himself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional. Twenty years of practice. He could hate himself in his sleep.

He could hear Ines moving around the small room, unpacking, checking the window latch, doing the small competent things she did. He listened to her the way a drowning man listens to the shore.

"It isn't your fault," she said finally. She didn't turn from the window. "What you can't remember."

"Isn't it?" His laugh came out wrong. "I made a bargain with something, Ines. People don't get cursed by accident. Somewhere in me is the reason I did it, and the reason is probably shameful, because the curse itself is shameful. I live half my life as an animal. I wake up in ditches. I've killed things I can't remember killing." He flexed his hands. Beneath the skin, he could feel the moon already pulling, a tide rising in his blood. Two hours, maybe less. "And now it turns out I'm also a deadbeat debtor. Twenty years, four months, eleven days. He counted."

"He wanted you to know he counted."

"That's what fences do." Sebastian looked up at her. The fraying sky bled colored light through the window and laid it across her face, green and violet, making her look like something out of one of her own maps. "You should understand what you're traveling with. I'm not a man with a curse, Ines. I'm a debt with legs. Everything I was is locked behind the curse, and everything the curse is, is locked behind whatever I did. Corvo's right. There's no version of this where I'm worth what it costs to—"

"Don't." She turned from the window. Her voice hadn't risen, but something in it had sharpened to a point. "Don't do the arithmetic for other people. I spent six years doing that math about myself—calculating exactly how invisible I needed to be, exactly how much of myself it was safe to spend. It's a liar's sum. It always comes out to zero."

"This is different."

"Why? Because it's you?"

"Because it's *true*." He stood, too fast, and the room seemed to tilt around him—or maybe that was the moon, coming up somewhere behind the clouds, dragging at him like a hook through the sternum. "Ines. Listen to me. In an hour I'm going to be a wolf standing in this room, and the only reason you're alive right now instead of dead in a ditch outside Vellmarsh is that the wolf likes your face. That's it. That's the whole treaty between us: the animal remembers you kindly. I don't get to be trusted. I get to be *managed*."

She crossed the room and stood in front of him, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her eyes and the stubbornness under the exhaustion.

"Say the true thing," she said quietly. "Not the dramatic one. The true one."

He opened his mouth to give her the dramatic one—it was ready, it was polished, twenty years in the making—and found, to his horror, that the true one was already in his throat.

"I'm ashamed," he said. "That's the true thing. Not afraid. Ashamed. Everyone in that square tonight could see what I am. Corvo saw it and smiled, because I'm worth more broken. And you—you look at me like I'm a map you want to read properly, and I keep waiting for the moment you find the forgery in me. The lie underneath. Whatever I did. Whatever I sold or shattered. Because it's down there, Ines. It's in the locked room, and someday the door opens, and you'll wish you'd managed me instead of trusted me."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, Ashfall's night-market murmured; somewhere below, the tavern-keeper was arguing with a customer about whether a bottled memory had gone flat.

"Ines," he said, because the silence was unbearable, "you should take the map and go. You can find the Wellspring faster alone. Hallow is hunting the pair of us; she'd struggle to track one cartographer. Every hour I'm with you, I make you—"

"I'm staying."

"—harder to—"

"I heard myself the first time." She set her hand flat against his chest, over the sternum, over the tide-pull of the moon. "I'm a cartographer, Sebastian. Do you know what that means? It means I don't abandon terrain just because it's difficult. Difficult terrain is where the true maps come from." Her hand pressed firmer. "You keep talking about what's locked inside you like it's a verdict. But I've seen your fever dreams. I was there in the vault when you talked in your sleep. You know what you said, over and over, all night?"

"No."

"*'Hold the thread. Hold the thread. Don't let go.'*" She looked up at him. "Whatever you did twenty years ago, whatever Corvo's waiting to collect—that's not the sound of a man who sold anything. That's the sound of a man who was holding on when it broke. Those aren't the same crime. And I'm not going to sentence you to the worse one just because it's the only story anyone's willing to tell."

The moon cleared the clouds.

He felt it happen—felt the man in him fold up like a letter, felt the wolf rise through him the way water rises through a wick. The last thing his human eyes registered was Ines's face, still close, unflinching, her hand still pressed against his chest as though she could hold the thread herself if she had to.

Then the world went wide and loud and smelled of iron, burnt sugar, rain coming, and her.

* * *

The wolf sat in the corner of the room and shook, because the change hurt, because it always hurt, because pain was the tax on existing twice.

Fragments drifted. They always drifted, in the gray hours—pieces of a life floating past like wreckage after a shipwreck. A tower with bells that rang in colors. A woman laughing while she drew a line that became a river. Fire, and someone screaming *not the charter, save the charter*—

And the face. Always the face. Dark-skinned, sharp-eyed, ink on her fingers, looking at him with an expression he had no human words for anymore but which the wolf understood completely, the way wolves understand loyalty: as a scent, as gravity, as home.

Except the face wasn't a memory. That was the terrible thing he'd only just admitted to himself tonight. The face belonged to a woman standing ten feet away, alive, breathing, watching him with her hand still half-raised from touching the man he'd been.

The wolf knew Ines Okafor's face from a life he had never lived with her. Or from a life he couldn't reach. Either way, the knowing was older than their acquaintance, deeper than three days of flight, and it terrified him in the way only true things terrify liars.

She knelt in front of him. Most people, even brave ones, kept a wall of furniture between themselves and him after moonrise. She never did. She simply knelt, at the exact distance a person kneels near a dog they trust, and opened her ledger across her knee.

"I can't sleep yet," she said, conversationally, the way you talk to a wolf and pretend it's normal. "I'm mapping today's road while it's fresh. And there's something I need to say where the man can't interrupt me, so I'm saying it to the wolf, who is famously a better listener."

The wolf huffed. It might have been agreement. It might have been laughter.

"Here's what I've worked out." She uncapped her pen. "Corvo doesn't actually expect you to remember the name. If he did, he'd have asked differently—gently, quickly, before the guild found you. Instead he waited twenty years and demanded it in public, in front of witnesses. That's not a debt collection. That's bait. He wants to know whether you *can* remember, because if you can, you're worth more to certain buyers intact than broken." She wrote as she talked, quick sure strokes. "Which means the trap isn't the debt. The trap is what happens when you try to pay it. Trying to dig into that locked room of yours—through force, through magic, through whatever a fence like Corvo knows how to buy—that could tear the lock out along with the door. And you might be the only key to the covenant we've got."

The wolf tilted his head. She glanced up, caught his gaze, and something passed between them that had no need of language.

"So here's my plan," she said. "We don't pay him. We don't run either—we run and he sells us to Hallow. We do the third thing, the cartographer's thing." She turned the ledger toward him. On the page was the market square, drawn in her precise hand, and beneath it, annotated in tiny letters, the crooked chimney, the stall, the sightlines, the alley behind the tanner's row, and a single circled note: *fences always have back doors. fences always owe someone worse.*

"We go back tomorrow and negotiate," she said. "Man to fence. With leverage."

The wolf stared at the page, then at her, and felt something he hadn't felt in twenty years of being two creatures stitched into one skin.

Useful. Fought over, hunted, cursed, ashamed—but useful, and believed in, and not alone.

He leaned his great head against her shoulder, briefly, the way wolves apologize and thank and promise all at once, and she let him, and outside the fraying sky split open again, spilling another realm's unnamed stars across Ashfall—and far off, at the edge of hearing, boots were coming up the tavern stairs, more pairs than any innkeeper would send with good news.
