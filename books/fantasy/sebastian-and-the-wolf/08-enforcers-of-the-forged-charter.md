## 8. Enforcers of the Forged Charter

The debt Corvo wanted was a name Sebastian could not give, and the argument over it had carried them out of Ashfall's crooked streets and onto the salt flats before dawn. By the time the sun cleared the eastern ridgeline, they had put six miles of cracked white earth between themselves and the town, and Corvo had come with them—not out of loyalty, Ines suspected, but because whatever he knew about Sister Hallow had made staying in Ashfall suddenly unattractive.

"Enforcers don't leave witnesses," he'd said, spitting into the dust as they walked. "You think I got rich selling relics and rumors in a border town by being brave? Hallow takes the guilty and the innocent both. The innocent are easier. They don't run."

So now there were three of them crossing the flats: Corvo ahead in his patched coat, walking like a man who could smell trouble on the wind; Sebastian behind him, gray-faced and silent, the wolf-shape still clinging to him even in human form, moving with that low-shouldered caution of something hunted; and Ines at the rear with her satchel of ledgers, watching the horizon behind them every thirty steps.

She saw them first.

At first she thought it was heat shimmer—a ripple above the salt where the air bent wrong. But the morning was cold, the light thin and honest, and what she saw did not behave the way heat behaved. It moved against the wind. It kept its shape.

"Sister Hallow," she said quietly. "Behind us. Half a mile. Maybe less."

Corvo stopped so abruptly that Sebastian nearly walked into him. "How can you possibly—"

"The road." Ines pointed back the way they'd come, at the pale line of the old salt road winding toward Ashfall. "There's a fold in it. A shortcut through a space that's been folded twice—guild work, old guild work, from when the charter was still real. Anyone walking it comes out three miles ahead of anyone walking straight." She swallowed. "They know the folds. They built them."

Sebastian turned. Even at half a mile, across flat white nothing, Ines could see what he saw: figures resolving out of the distortion, seven of them, walking in perfect formation, their gray cloaks the exact color of the haze at the edge of a map where the cartographer gave up.

"They're not hurrying," Sebastian said.

"They don't need to." Corvo's voice had gone flat and professional. "That's Hallow's method. She doesn't chase. She closes. You'll find the road ahead cut off before you ever see her do it." He looked at Ines with something new in his face—not respect exactly, but the recalibration of a man discovering the merchandise he'd dismissed might actually be valuable. "You're the heir. The covenant's heir. What can you do?"

"I'm a ledger-keeper," Ines said. "I file things."

"You folded a room around us in your vault," Sebastian said. "I remember. Before the fever took the rest, I remember that. Walls that weren't walls."

"That was—" She stopped. Lied to herself for one breath about how much she understood. Then stopped lying, because Sister Hallow was gaining and self-deception was a luxury with a price tag. "That was a trick from the ledgers. The guild keeps techniques in the same books as the maps. Folded-space work. Most clerks never learn them—they're restricted reading. But I read everything. That was always my problem. I read everything."

"How fast," Corvo said, "can you read under pressure?"

Ines looked back at the seven gray figures. At the way the salt flats seemed to lean toward them, the landscape itself tilting subtly in their favor, as though the world had already decided whose side it was on.

"Faster than you'd believe," she said. "Walk faster anyway."

* * *

The problem, as Ines laid it out while they half-ran across the salt, was this: folding space required two things. A place where reality was already thin—the old covenant roads, the border frays, any seam between realms—and an anchor. Something written. Something named.

"I can't just fold the desert," she said. "Nothing here has a name. Salt doesn't get mapped; it all looks the same. That's why nobody lives out here. That's why it's safe. And that's why we're going to die on it if I can't find a seam."

"There's a seam," Corvo said. He'd gone quiet, calculating, and Ines recognized the expression because she wore it herself over ledgers. "Northeast. There's a place called the Tollhouse—an old waystation from before Ashfall, when the covenant roads were still maintained. It sits on a junction. Three roads meet there, and one of them isn't a road anymore. It goes nowhere. Or everywhere. The locals won't walk it."

"A dead covenant road," Ines breathed. "A thread that used to be tied and came loose."

"If the realms are drifting apart," Sebastian said slowly, "then the roads between them would be..."

"Unraveling. Yes." Ines felt the idea take shape in her mind the way a map took shape under ink—that particular click of territory becoming legible. "An unraveling road is the thinnest possible place. If I can anchor a fold there, I can push us through into... somewhere else. Somewhere off the map. And close it behind us."

"And the toll?" Corvo asked. "Places like that always have a toll."

Ines didn't answer. She was already pulling her field ledger from her satchel, flipping pages as they ran, her eyes skimming her own cramped handwriting. *Anchor requirements: true name or sworn text, inscribed at the seam. Duration proportional to weight of the name.* Her mother's handwriting surfaced beneath her own in memory—the field notes she'd read only once, in the survey station, in another lifetime three days ago. *Never anchor a fold with anything you cannot afford to lose.*

"We'll pay it," she said, and closed the book.

* * *

The Tollhouse was a ruin with pretensions. Its stone shell still stood, roofless, its doorways gaping onto the salt like the eye sockets of something buried upright. Inside, the three roads met in a courtyard of cracked flagstones, and Ines felt the third road before she saw it—a pull behind her sternum, a vertigo, the sensation of standing too close to a stairwell in the dark.

The dead road left the courtyard heading north and simply *stopped*—not faded, not eroded, but stopped, its flagstones ending in midair at the edge of a shimmer that hurt to look at directly. Beyond the shimmer was neither salt nor sky but a kind of held breath, a place the world hadn't finished deciding about.

"It's beautiful," Sebastian said quietly.

"It's a wound," Corvo said. "It's what happens when the healers quit."

Ines barely heard either of them. She was kneeling at the seam, her palm flat against the last flagstone, feeling for the texture of the thin place the way she'd been taught to feel for the grain of paper. There. A vibration, faint and arrhythmic, like a heartbeat recovering from a long illness. The road remembered being whole. The road wanted to be whole again.

She opened the field ledger to a blank page and began to write.

"What are you doing?" Sebastian crouched beside her.

"Building the anchor." Her pen moved fast, sketching the geometry of the fold in the shorthand the guild used for impossible architecture—arrows that pointed inward, circles that were also doors. "I need to describe this place precisely enough that the description becomes a kind of cage. A pocket. We step in, the fold closes, and when it opens we're somewhere else entirely—miles away, maybe further. Folded space doesn't respect distance the way it respects intent."

"And the toll?"

She kept writing. "The anchor burns up. Whatever I write it on, whatever I use to bind it—it's consumed. That's the price. Nothing holds a fold for free." She glanced at her satchel, at the row of ledgers inside, each one years of her careful invisible life. "I brought spares."

"Ines." Sebastian's voice dropped low. "Look at me."

She looked. His eyes were amber in this light, the wolf showing through, and there was something in his face she couldn't afford to examine right now.

"Whatever you're planning to sacrifice," he said, "don't lie to me about it. Not you. Everyone else in my life has lied to me—including whoever I was before the curse ate my memory. Don't be one more person who decides I'm better off not knowing."

Ines's pen stopped.

The truth was simple and terrible: the anchor needed weight. A blank page wouldn't hold. A spare ledger of inventory lists wouldn't hold. The fold needed a name with history in it, text with meaning soaked through—and the only thing in her possession dense enough, true enough, heavy enough was the one thing she'd promised herself she'd never surrender.

Her private ledger. The one with the journey in it. The one with the page about Sebastian she kept rewriting because she couldn't make the words honest.

"It's fine," she heard herself say. "It's just a ledger."

Sebastian held her gaze for a long moment. Then, blessedly, the universe interrupted.

"They're here," Corvo said from the doorway. "Gods preserve fools and debtors. They're *here*."

* * *

Sister Hallow came through the Tollhouse's southern gate without hurry, and her enforcers spread through the ruined courtyards the way water finds the low places—inexorable, unbothered, certain of the outcome. Up close, their gray uniforms resolved into detail Ines had spent her whole career not noticing: the charter sigil stitched on every breast, the forged seal rendered faithfully in thread, layer upon layer of beautiful counterfeit authority.

Hallow herself was smaller than Ines expected, and older, with a face like a folded map—all its features pressed flat into lines that told a longer story than they appeared to. Her eyes found Ines immediately, and Ines had the disorienting sense of being *read*—of standing in front of someone else's gift the way strangers stood in front of hers.

"Heir Okafor." Hallow's voice was quiet, almost kind. "You've caused a great deal of paperwork."

"I'm told that runs in my family," Ines said, rising slowly, keeping her body between Hallow and the open ledger on the ground.

Something moved behind Hallow's flat expression—amusement, perhaps, or grief wearing amusement's clothes. "Your mother said something very similar, the last time I was ordered to bring her in." She stepped forward. Her enforcers stepped with her, closing the angles, sealing the exits that mattered. "Surrender the map. Surrender the curse-bearer. Come home, and I promise you will be treated gently. Vey wants you *informed*, Ines. Not harmed. There's a difference, and I have spent twenty years learning to enforce it whether or not I believe in it."

"You know," Ines said. "Don't you. About the charter. You've seen what I saw—you've read deep enough to know the guild's founding document is a forgery laid over the covenant."

Hallow's enforcers shifted, almost imperceptibly. Hallow didn't move at all.

"I know," she said, "that order is better than truth. I know that realms bound together share their wars, their plagues, their hungers. I know that the covenant, when it lived, demanded blood regularly and gave back chaos generously. And I know that you are twenty-two years old and have read fewer ledgers than I have burned." She extended one gloved hand. "But I am prepared to be wrong. Hand me the map, child, and let me show you what mercy looks like when it's organized."

Behind Ines, without her seeing him move, Sebastian's hand closed around the field ledger. Around the fresh page. Around the anchor-in-progress with its incomplete geometry.

And Corvo—Corvo, who owed debts and collected them, who had followed them out of cowardice and calculation and something he probably hadn't admitted to himself—Corvo stepped sideways, deliberately, into the sightline between Hallow and Sebastian.

"Well," Corvo said loudly, to no one, to everyone, "this has been lovely, but I'll just be—"

"Hold him," Hallow said.

It happened fast after that. Two enforcers moved on Corvo with the smooth economy of people who had done this a thousand times; Corvo fought the way a fence fights, dirty and desperate and briefly magnificent, and bought perhaps four seconds. Four seconds in which Hallow's attention split, in which the formation loosened, in which Sebastian shoved the field ledger into Ines's hands and said, not softly at all:

"*Finish it.*"

* * *

Ines finished it.

She wrote with the pen tearing the page, pouring everything into the anchor—the Tollhouse's true description, the dead road's wound, the precise coordinates of the thin place, and beneath it all, because the fold needed weight and she had nothing else heavy enough to give, the words she'd rewritten eleven times and never gotten honest:

*He looks at me like he remembers a life neither of us lived. I am beginning to suspect one of us is wrong about which one of us is dreaming.*

The page flared white.

The fold opened.

Reality peeled back along the dead road's edge like paper parting from a spine, revealing not a place but a *passage*—a corridor of compressed elsewhere, gray and humming, smelling of ozone and old ink. Ines grabbed Sebastian's arm. He grabbed back. For one instant, one perfect instant, they were both moving toward the opening—

And then Ines looked back, because she was who she was, because she had spent her whole life reading the fine print and the fine print was a habit deeper than survival.

Corvo was down on his knees between two enforcers, blood on his mouth, his patched coat torn at the shoulder. He wasn't struggling anymore. He was looking directly at her, and his expression held no accusation at all—which was somehow infinitely worse—and he said, clearly, across the ruined courtyard:

"The debt's paid, keeper. Tell them the fence went down buying time for royalty. Make it a good story." Then, as the enforcers hauled him up: "*Go!*"

"Ines—" Sebastian pulled at her arm.

She went.

The fold swallowed them. Behind them, the last thing she saw was Hallow's face turning from Corvo to the closing seam—calm, unhurried, already calculating—and the last thing she heard was Hallow's voice, pitched to carry through a gap in the world:

"Run well, heir. I'll bring you her debt next time."

Then gray light, humming silence, and the passage closing behind them like a throat.

* * *

They came out on a hillside under a green-gold sky, in a country Ines did not know, amid grass that whispered in a language almost but not quite like any tongue she'd read. Miles away—or years away; folded distance made liars of measurement—a city glimmered on a river, its towers slightly wrong, its edges flickering at the borders the way the frayed sky had flickered over Ashfall.

Sebastian sat down heavily in the grass, breathing hard, human-shaped, shaking.

Ines stood with the field ledger in her hands. The anchor page was ash—fine, soft ash fused into the binding, leaving a perfect silhouette of absence where her words had been. Where *he* had been, in the small dishonest way she'd managed to write him.

"We left him," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "We left Corvo. He stepped in front of them for us—for people he'd known for two days—and we left him on his knees in the dirt."

"Yes," Sebastian said. Simply. Without absolution, without excuse. He'd learned that much about her already: she didn't want comfort, she wanted accuracy.

"He'll give them everything," Ines went on. "He'll tell Hallow our route, our plans, everything he knows about the Wellspring, everything—" She stopped. Pressed the ash-silhouette page flat with her thumb. "No. No, he won't. Did you hear him? He was performing. Even beaten down on his knees, he was *performing*—the fence to the end. He'll sell them something that sounds true and costs us nothing, because that's what Corvo does, that's the only currency he's ever traded in, and gods help me, I think he chose it on purpose. I think he *decided*."

"Then why does it feel like losing?"

"Because it is." Ines finally sat, in the whispering grass, in the green-gold wrongness of a realm she'd only ever known as marginalia. "Because the cost of running was supposed to be abstract. Ledgers, maps, distances. Numbers. I thought—I genuinely believed—if I was careful enough, clever enough, invisible enough, the bill would never come due. And now there's a man bleeding in a guild cell who paid it for me, and a page of my own handwriting burned to ash, and Hallow knows my mother's name, and—" Her voice caught. She hated that it caught.

Sebastian moved closer. Not touching her. Just near, the way a fire is near.

"She said she'd bring me his debt," Ines said. "Next time. Which means next time is coming, and sooner than we'd like, and we're standing in a realm that's coming apart at its edges with half a plan and no third keeper and—" She stopped again. Looked down at the ash page. Looked at the words she could still recite from memory, the ones she'd never gotten honest, burning a hole in the place where the paper used to be.

"Sebastian," she said. "What did you read? When I was writing. What did you see?"

He was quiet a moment. The green-gold light moved on his face.

"Enough," he said, "to know that when we find Maren, and we will, and when we reach the Wellspring, and we will—I'm not letting you pay any more tolls alone."

Far to the north, where the dead road had pointed, the sky flickered—a stutter in the firmament, a heartbeat missed, the visible shudder of a realm losing its grip on the ones beside it.

Ines stood up, shouldered the satchel, and started walking.
