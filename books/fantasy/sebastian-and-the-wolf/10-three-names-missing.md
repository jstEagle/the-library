## 10. Three Names Missing

The survey station smelled of dust and old ink, and beneath both, faintly, of a woman Sebastian had never met.

He stood in the doorway while Ines moved through the rooms of her mother's abandoned life, touching things the way he'd seen her touch maps—carefully, as if the objects might lie to her if she gripped them too hard. A brass theodolite on its tripod, lenses fogged with years. Shelves of field journals, spines cracked. A cot with a blanket folded into a shape so precise it looked less like tidiness than like a message left behind: *I meant to come back.*

Outside, the wind coming off the Glasswood carried the smell of smoke that wasn't there. The sky to the east still bore the bruise-colored seam where Ashfall's realm rubbed against the next one, fraying like rope under load. Every hour they wasted, that seam widened. Every hour, somewhere, a road that had existed for a thousand years quietly stopped existing.

And still Ines lingered among her mother's things, because grief, Sebastian was learning, kept its own schedule and did not care about fraying skies.

"Ines." He said it gently. "The map."

"I know." She didn't turn. Her fingers hovered over the folded blanket without landing on it. "I just—" She exhaled. "She slept here. She actually *slept here*, between saving the world or whatever she was doing, and she folded her blanket like a soldier, and I spent twenty years thinking she abandoned me for cartography."

Sebastian had no memory of his own mother. He had no memory of anything before the curse except in fragments—the fire in the Glasswood, Vey's young face across a table, an oath spoken in a room that hummed. He knew what it was to have your past taken from you. He did not know what it was to have it handed back all at once, wrong.

So he said nothing, which was, he was beginning to understand, sometimes the only honest thing a man could offer.

* * *

They spread the map on the surveyor's table by lamplight, weighting the corners with theodolite parts and a tin of cold tea leaves. It was the same map Sebastian had pressed into her hands at the salt door, bloodstained and wax-sealed, the one that had burned itself into her palm and left the scar shaped like nothing—or like something, depending on how the light fell.

Ines unrolled it slowly now, differently than before. Before, she had been reading it as a cartographer: routes, borders, the Wellspring marked at its heart like a pupil in an eye. Tonight she read it as an heir.

"Watch the margins," she said. "Not the map. The margins."

Sebastian leaned over her shoulder. He could feel the heat of her, could smell woodsmoke and lamp oil and, underneath it, the particular scent the curse had taught him to track even through fever and hunger—the scent of the covenant itself, which clung to her skin like rain clings to stone. His wolf stirred at the base of his skull, restless, recognizing something. He pressed it down.

The margins were covered in writing. Not her mother's hand—he'd seen that now, in the field notes, a cramped forward-leaning script—but older, formal, each letter drawn with the care of someone who understood that names, written correctly, were load-bearing.

"The Covenant of Threads," Ines read aloud, "requires three sworn keepers. One to hold the memory of what was joined. One to hold the key of passage. One to hold the truth of the map." Her voice caught slightly on the last word. "Three names. Witnessed and bound."

Three lines followed. Three spaces where names should be.

The first name was struck through with a single clean line: **SEBASTIAN VOSS**.

He hadn't known his own surname until that moment. It landed strangely—not like recognition, but like finding a coat in a closet that fits perfectly and belonging to someone else. *Voss.* He turned the sound over in his mouth. Nothing answered. The fire-memory flickered and went out.

"That's you," Ines said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd known his name was missing a piece; he'd told her once, in the catacombs, that even his own name felt like a borrowed coat.

"The first keeper," she continued, tracing the struck line without touching it. "*Holds the memory of what was joined.*" She looked up at him, and in the lamplight her eyes were very dark and very steady. "That's why they took it from you. That's why the curse eats your memories. You're not cursed, Sebastian. You're *bound*. The covenant took your memories as surety when it broke—or when it was broken—and the wolf is what's left holding the vault shut."

The wolf in him turned over, slow and enormous, like something waking in deep water. Sebastian's hands had gone cold. "Then who struck my name out?"

"No one." Ines shook her head. "Look at the second line."

The second name was also struck through, but the line through it was different—shakier, drawn and redrawn, gouged deep enough to tear the vellum. **ALTHEA OKAFOR**.

His breath left him. "Your mother."

"My mother." Ines's voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant the opposite of calm. "Second keeper. *Holder of the key of passage.* And someone crossed her out hard enough to tear the page." She stared at the gouged line. "This isn't administrative. This isn't a record being updated. Someone hated this name, or feared it, or—" She stopped. Started again. "Or needed the binding broken badly enough to cut out the person holding the key."

The station was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, wind found a gap and made a low whistling note, like the building itself breathing.

"And the third line?" Sebastian asked, though some animal part of him already knew, because Ines had gone rigid beside him, because her scarred palm had risen halfway to the map and stopped, hovering, the way a hand hovers over a stove before the burn.

The third space was not struck through. The third space held a name written small and sure, in the cramped forward-leaning script he recognized from the field notes:

**INES OKAFOR**.

Below it, in the same hand, a date. Twenty-two years old. Two years before Ines had been born.

"She wrote it before I existed," Ines whispered. "Before I was even—" Her finger touched the date, barely, the way you touch a wound to confirm it's real. "She stood at this table, or one like it, and she wrote my name into a covenant I'd never heard of, swearing me to something I never agreed to, and then she died, and nobody ever—" 

Her voice failed. Not dramatically. It simply ran out, like a road.

Sebastian watched her absorb it, and it was the strangest thing he had ever witnessed, stranger than his own transformation, stranger than the fraying sky: watching a woman discover that her entire careful, invisible, self-erased life had been built on top of a signature.

* * *

She didn't cry. He'd expected her to, and was ashamed of expecting it. Instead she sat down on her mother's stool, pulled the field notes into her lap, and began to work—because that was what Ines Okafor did with unbearable things, he realized. She catalogued them. She gave them entries and cross-references. She made them legible so they couldn't ambush her.

"First keeper holds memory," she said, flipping pages. "Mother held passage. Third keeper holds the truth of the map." She glanced up. "You see it? The covenant isn't three people. It's three functions. Memory, passage, truth. Take away any one and the whole structure fails. Take away two—"

"It fails completely," Sebastian finished. "Which is what happened."

"Which is what happened." She set the notes down. "Someone removed you. Someone removed her. And the third keeper—" she looked down at her own name, written by a dead woman's hand, "—the third keeper was two years old and didn't know she existed to be removed. So they didn't need to remove her. They just let her grow up folding blankets in a guild archive, believing she was no one."

"You're not no one."

"I know that *now*." It came out sharper than she intended; he saw her regret it, saw her decide not to take it back. "But don't you understand what this means? I've spent my whole life choosing to be invisible. Choosing the ledgers, choosing the margins, staying so far inside the guild's shadow that not even Sister Hallow could find a seam to grab. And the entire time, the choice was never mine. My mother made it for me before I drew breath. She looked at her infant daughter and decided: *this one will hold the truth of the map.*" Ines laughed, and there was nothing funny in it at all. "I thought I was self-erasing. I was just... following instructions I'd never read."

Sebastian came around the table. Slowly, the way you approach a wild thing—which was absurd, because she was the steadiest person he knew, but the steadiness right now had a hairline crack running through it, and he'd learned that cracks like that were where the wolf in himself lived too.

"Ines." He waited until she looked at him. "I don't remember my life. I don't remember taking this oath, or breaking it, or whether I broke it. But I remember one thing clearly—you know that. One image the curse never managed to bury."

"Your face," she said softly. "From a life you don't remember living."

"My face looking at *you*." He tapped the third line, gently, above her name. "Whatever I was, whoever I was—I knew you were coming. Maybe not your name. Maybe not your face. But the covenant knew. My mother—" he stopped, surprised by the word, by how natural it felt, "—whoever raised me, whoever swore me in, they must have known a keeper would rise after Althea. And the map has been waiting for you the way a lock waits for a key. That's why it burned into your hand. That's why only you can read the roads that aren't on any map. This isn't destiny falling on you tonight, Ines. Destiny signed for you twenty-two years ago. Tonight is just the delivery."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then: "That's a horrible thing to say to someone."

"Yes," he agreed. "But it's true, and you're the keeper of truth. Seems like you should hear it first."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go when it was ready. She looked back down at the map, at the three lines: one struck clean, one struck savage, one waiting.

"There's something else," she said. "Look at the ink."

He leaned in. The two struck-through names had faded to brown, old-blood colored. Her own name was different—the ink still dark, still wet-looking, though it had been written decades ago. As he watched, it seemed to deepen, drinking the lamplight.

"It's fresh," Ines murmured. "It's been fresh for twenty-two years. Because the oath isn't broken until the last keeper refuses it." She sat back. "My mother didn't write my name to trap me, Sebastian. She wrote it to hold the door open. As long as that ink stays wet, the covenant can still be reforged. Three keepers. Memory, passage, truth. We have a memory that's locked in a wolf. We have a passage-key whose holder was murdered for it. And we have truth, sitting at this table, arguing with a dead woman's handwriting."

"We need Maren," Sebastian said.

Ines blinked. "What?"

"The second name. Althea held the key of passage. If your mother's dead, the function needs a new holder—or the old one restored somehow. But there were records, weren't there? In the guild? Keepers, priests, people who tended the crossings before Vey closed them." He was reaching, pulling at fragments, the fire-memory stirring again, giving him shapes without faces. "There was someone. A priestess. I can almost—" He pressed his knuckles to his temple. "A chapel. Empty. She kept one of the names."

Ines was already moving, pulling ledgers from her pack, her grief converting itself to velocity in real time. "Maren," she said. "Sister Maren Aldous. Defrocked eleven years ago. The guild's records call her a relic-forger, but the file was flagged by Vey personally, which means she mattered, which means—" She looked up, and the crack in her steadiness had sealed over with purpose, and she was magnificent, and he hated that he noticed, and noticed that he hated it. "Which means she might be our second keeper."

"Might be."

"Might be is more than we had an hour ago." She rolled the map with decisive hands, and then paused, her thumb resting over the third line, over her own name in its patient, wet-dark ink.

"For what it's worth," Sebastian said, "she didn't choose for you. Not really. Writing a name isn't the same as binding it. The covenant needs consent—that much I remember, that much the wolf hasn't eaten. She held the door open. Walking through it is still yours."

Ines looked at him for a long moment, lamplight moving in her eyes.

"Then let's go knock on a disgraced priestess's door," she said, "before the sky finishes coming apart."

She snuffed the lamp. And in the sudden dark, on the table behind them, the ink of the third name gleamed once—wet, willing, waiting—like a door held open exactly as long as it takes for someone to decide to walk through.
