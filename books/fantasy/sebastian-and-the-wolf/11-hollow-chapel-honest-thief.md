## 11. Hollow Chapel, Honest Thief

Maren Vos had been a liar for eleven years, and she was very good at it.

That was the trouble with selling fake relics in a town like Ashfall: the buyers wanted to believe so badly that the lie barely required craftsmanship. A sliver of "saint's bone" cut from a pig's knuckle. A vial of "tears of the Wellspring" that was river water and a pinch of salt. She'd sold the same splinter of the True Loom to four different pilgrims in one season, and none of them had ever come back to complain, because coming back would mean admitting they'd been fooled, and faith could not survive that admission.

So she sat behind her trestle table in the hollow chapel at the edge of Ashfall's poor quarter—hollow because its bell had been stolen before she was born, hollow because its congregation had drifted away when the sky started fraying, hollow because she had hollowed it out herself, selling off the pews, the altar stone, the leaded windows—and she waited for the desperate and the grieving to bring her their coin.

The chapel still had a roof. That was its only remaining virtue, and on days when the fraying above Ashfall grew bad enough to hear—a sound like ice groaning on a winter lake, though there was no lake for a hundred miles—Maren found herself grateful even for that.

She was polishing a brass reliquary she'd bought from a scrap dealer and aged herself with vinegar and ash when the doors opened, and two figures came in out of the gray afternoon: a woman with ink-stained fingers and a map case slung across her back, and behind her, hunched into a traveling cloak, a man who moved wrong.

Maren knew every wrong way a body could move. She'd spent years among the devout and the desperate, and she could read grief, guilt, and fear the way the woman across her table read maps. But this man moved like someone carrying a second body inside his own, something heavy and restless that shifted its weight at the wrong moments. His hood had slipped, and beneath it she saw the scars along his jaw—not knife scars, not burn scars. Bite-shaped. As if he'd been worrying his own throat with teeth.

"Well," Maren said, setting down the reliquary. "If you've come for absolution, I'm afraid we're out. Sold the last of it Tuesday."

"We've come for you," the woman said.

"People always do. Usually too late." Maren gestured at the empty nave, the scorch marks on the floor where the altar used to be. "As you can see, my practice is modest. I have relics of the Covenant of Threads—" she picked up a small tin medallion, stamped with three interlocking circles, "—guaranteed genuine, blessed by a priestess who no longer exists, at two coppers each or three for five."

The woman didn't smile. She set both hands flat on the table, and Maren saw the map burned into her right palm—the lines of it sunk into the skin like an old scar, though Maren would have sworn the woman couldn't be more than thirty. Something cold walked up Maren's spine and sat down.

"I know what that is," Maren said quietly.

"Then you know I don't need to buy your medallions." The woman's voice was steady, but Maren heard the effort underneath it. "Maren Vos. Formerly of the Order of the Unbroken Thread. Sworn keeper of the second name."

"Don't." The word came out of Maren sharper than she intended, and she hated how fast it came, how ready it was, like a blade she kept oiled for exactly this occasion. Eleven years, and she still reached for it first. "Whatever you were told, whatever he told you—" she flicked a glance at the man, who hadn't spoken, hadn't moved, only watched her with eyes that were amber where they should have been brown, "—I'm not that person anymore. That person is dead. You're standing in her grave, as it happens. I rent it out cheap."

"You struck your own name from the covenant," the woman said. "Why?"

"Because striking it hurt less than watching it fail." Maren heard her own voice go flat, professional, the voice she used on pilgrims. It was the voice of a woman selling lies, and she knew it, and she kept using it anyway. "The covenant was already breaking. The realms were drifting. We swore ourselves to hold them together, and then the borders frayed anyway, right under our hands, and people died in the gaps—whole villages, whole *roads*, places that stopped being anywhere. My fellow keeper went mad trying to mend a thread with her bare fingers. So yes. I took my name out. I walked away. And the world did not end."

"The world is ending," the woman said simply. "You just haven't looked up."

Maren looked up. Through the gap where the eastern window had been, the afternoon sky hung over Ashfall, pale and ordinary, streaked with cloud. She looked back down. "I see sky."

"You saw it in Ashfall's square yesterday," the man said. His voice was rough, low, like something recovering from disuse. "The shimmer over the well. Where the colors didn't sit together."

Maren's polishing cloth went still in her hand. She had seen it. She had seen it and told herself it was heat haze in autumn, which even she had known was a lie, and she had gone inside and sold two more vials of fake tears and not thought about it, because not thinking was the whole architecture of her life now, load-bearing and carefully maintained.

"I sell souvenirs," she said. "To sad people. That's my crime. Find another keeper."

"There are no other keepers," the woman said. "There's me, and there's him, and there's the space where you used to be." She unslung her map case and drew out the map—sealed once in wax, Maran saw, the seal broken now, the parchment covered in threads of light that moved when you weren't looking directly at them. Two names were struck through in old ink. Ines Okafor, said the third line, written in a different hand entirely, in ink that seemed fresher than the paper it sat on. And beside it, a scorched blank space. A fourth space, gouged out, torn nearly through the parchment.

"That's yours," the woman said. "Where your name was."

"I can see where it was," Maren snapped. "I put the knife through it myself. Do you know how hard it is to cut a name out of covenant-parchment? It fights you. It *bleeds*." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It bled for three days. I still have the scar." She held up her left hand, the white line across the palm. "So no. Whatever you're doing, whatever you think I owe the world—I paid. I paid in advance. Ask me anything else."

The man spoke again, and his voice was quieter now, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "What did you keep, Maren? Before you left?"

And that—that was the question she'd spent eleven years not answering, the question buried under vinegar-aged brass and pig-bone saints, and she felt the whole careful structure of her life shudder under it like the chapel roof under wind.

"Nothing," she said. "I kept nothing. I was a coward with a title."

"No," he said. "Cowards don't bleed for three days."

Maren stood up so fast her stool went over backwards. "Get out. Get out of my chapel. Take your map and your burned hand and your—your *eyes*—" the amber gaze hadn't wavered from her once, and it made her feel flayed, seen all the way down to the oath she'd swallowed and never digested, "—and get out."

They went. The woman paused at the door and said, "My name is Ines. If the sky opens, come find me at the Drowned Lamp inn." And then they were gone into the gray street, and Maren stood alone in her hollow chapel surrounded by her honest dishonesty, shaking.

She righted the stool. She sat down. She picked up the polishing cloth and worked at the reliquary until her reflection swam in the brass, a middle-aged woman with a liar's comfortable face, and she did not look up at the window, and she did not look up, and she did not—

The sound came at dusk.

Not the groaning of the fraying, the ice-on-a-lake sound she'd learned to ignore. This was different. This was a *crack*, sharp and clean as a whip, directly overhead, and every dog in the poor quarter began to scream at once.

Maren ran to the empty eastern window frame and looked up, and her legs nearly went out from under her.

The sky over Ashfall had split.

Not metaphorically. Not in shimmer, not in haze. There was a seam running from horizon to horizon like a tear in wet cloth, and through it she could see—she pressed her hands against her mouth—through it she could see *another sky*. A different color. Stars out in daylight, but wrong stars, arranged in constellations that hurt to look at, that slid and refused to resolve, and beyond them the suggestion of land hanging upside-down like a reflection in troubled water. And along the edges of the seam, the familiar blue of her own sky was *unraveling*, thread by thread, pulling loose and drifting upward like smoke.

Below her, in the streets, people had come out of their houses and were pointing and screaming and praying to gods whose names Maren had spent years selling in tin. A child was crying. An old man had dropped to his knees in the mud. Somewhere a bell that wasn't the chapel's stolen bell was ringing wildly.

And Maren Vos stood in the doorway of the church she'd gutted, holding nothing, wearing nothing, protected by nothing, and understood with total clarity that every lie she had ever sold had been a down payment on exactly this moment—the moment when the truth arrived and demanded the balance.

Her hand ached. Not the left, the scarred one. The right—the one she'd pressed to the covenant parchment eleven years ago, the one that had held the second name, *Maren Vos, keeper of the western thread,* before she'd taken the knife to it. It ached like a wound reopening, and when she turned it over, half expecting to see blood, she saw instead faint light moving under the skin, tracing lines like a map remembering itself.

"Oh," she said aloud, to nobody, to the unraveling sky, to whatever was left of the order that had sworn her and lost her. "*Oh, damn it.*"

Because here was the thing about being an honest thief: eventually you stole something you couldn't fence. Eventually the goods came due. She had spent eleven years telling herself that walking away was survival, that cowardice was just wisdom with worse lighting, and the sky had just torn open above the town she'd hidden in to call her a liar to her face.

She could stay. She could bolt the chapel door, pull the shutters over the empty windows, tell herself the crack was far away and the threads were someone else's problem. She had gotten very good at that. It was, in fact, her only genuine skill, honed over a decade: the art of not looking up.

But she had bled for three days once. Cowards didn't do that.

Maren grabbed her coat, swept the fake relics off the table without ceremony—they scattered and clattered, worthless, finally worthless, and she felt lighter than she had in years to see them go—pulled the tin medallion of the three interlocking circles out of the wreckage, and shoved it in her pocket anyway, because sentiment was allowed, and locked the chapel door behind her out of pure habit, and then laughed at herself, unlocked it, and left it standing open to the street like a wound that had decided to heal honestly.

The Drowned Lamp was six streets away. The sky tore a little wider with every step she took, and by the time she reached the inn's crooked sign, threads of the other sky's starlight were drifting down through the seam like snow falling upward in reverse, and the crowd in the square below had stopped screaming and started doing something worse: waiting.

Inside, the woman called Ines looked up from a table by the fire, and the wolf-eyed man beside her went still, listening to Maren the way you listen to a lock turning.

"I want my name back," Maren said, and her voice cracked in the middle again, but this time she let it. "And I want to know why it's burning under my skin after eleven years. And I want—" she stopped, swallowed, started over, because honesty apparently got easier with practice, "—I don't know what I want. But I know I'm done selling lies, and I know the sky broke over my head tonight, and I know that's not a coincidence. So put me back on your map, cartographer. Before I lose my nerve. I give it an hour."

Ines slid the parchment across the table. The scorched space where Maren's name had been torn away gaped like a missing tooth.

"It will cost you," Ines said quietly. "Everything costs, with this thing. What did you keep, Maren? All those years ago. What did you keep that you won't tell me?"

Maren looked at the map, at the moving threads of light, at the gouged space shaped exactly like the hole she'd carried inside herself since the day she cut her name out—and above them, through the inn's smoky windows, the seam in the sky pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat counting down.

"I'll tell you on the road," she said. "While I still remember it myself."

She laid her right palm flat on the parchment, on the empty space, and waited to see whether the covenant would take back a thief.
