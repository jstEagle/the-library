## 12. Sister Hallow's Offer

The rain came down on Ashfall like a verdict.

Ines felt it before she saw it—that particular pressure in the air that meant someone was walking through her maps. Not the wolf; she'd learned his signature by now, a low warm hum like a struck bell. This was colder. A needle drawn along the edge of every route she'd charted since the ford, closing them off one by one with the patient precision of a hand she knew too well.

"Maren," she said quietly. "She's here."

Sebastian looked up from the fire he'd been coaxing to life in the ruined granary's corner. Three days since Maren had slipped away from their camp at the Glasswood's edge—three days of Sebastian saying *she needed time, priestesses grieve slowly* while Ines said nothing and mapped the roads out of town twice over, because her gift had whispered to her what his hope refused to hear.

The needle stopped. Right outside.

The granary door opened without a knock.

Sister Hallow stood in the frame with rain streaming off her grey cloak, and for once—for the first time since Ines had known her—she carried no weapon drawn, no writ, no warrant. Her hands were empty at her sides. Behind her, the street was empty too. No enforcers. No cordon. Just the rain hammering the cobbles and the frayed sky above, where the seam between realms glimmered like a wound that wouldn't close.

"Cartographer," Hallow said. "Keeper." She inclined her head—a small, precise motion, and Ines realized with a lurch that it was deference. Actual deference. "May I come in? I'm alone. You can verify that."

Ines could. The map in her mind showed the street bare as a fresh page. It also showed her three exits from the granary, two of which Hallow almost certainly knew about, and one—the old grain chute into the canal—which even Ines had only found because she'd read its shape in the building's bones.

"Come in," Ines said, and put herself between Hallow and Sebastian anyway.

Hallow's eyes flicked to the wolf-shape of him, hunched by the dying fire, amber eyes catching the light. Something moved across her face—not disgust. Grief, maybe, or its well-practiced cousin.

"You've been hard to find," Hallow said. "That's not a compliment I give lightly. Vey has spent twenty years making sure nothing in this guild is hard to find."

"Then why did you spend four days failing?" Ines kept her voice level. Her hand, hidden in her cloak, rested on the folded scrap of ledger paper she'd charmed into a blade's worth of edge. "You could have taken us at the ford. You chose not to kill us there. You let Corvo live when you took him. And now you walk in alone, unarmed, in the rain." She stepped closer. "You wanted something you couldn't take by force. So ask."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and the fire's wet hiss. Then Sister Hallow did something Ines had never seen her do in eleven years of guild service.

She sat down on the floor. Cross-legged, like a novice at instruction, her back against the doorframe so they could all see her hands.

"I'm going to tell you the truth," she said. "Not the guild's truth. Not the charter's truth. The one underneath, the one I've carried for twenty years like a stone in my boot. And then you can decide what to do with me, because after tonight I don't much care what happens either way."

* * *

"You were nine years old when your mother died," Hallow began. "You were told it was a surveying accident. A collapse in the Glasswood. Your mother, Adaeze Okafor, last sworn keeper of the Covenant of Threads, died holding a rope over a crevasse." She shook her head slowly. "There was no crevasse. There was no accident. There was an order, signed by Guildmaster Vey, and six enforcers who carried it out, and I was the seventh who refused—and was made to watch."

The fire popped. Sebastian had gone very still, and Ines could feel the change in him, the wolf rising under his skin like water under ice. But he didn't move. He was watching her, not Hallow—watching how she took it.

"Why?" Ines heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away. "Why would he—she was his keeper. She was the covenant's keeper. He *knew* her."

"Because the covenant was costing him everything he'd built." Hallow's voice was flat now, stripped of ceremony. "You've seen the charter, haven't you? The forgery. You read it in his own office—I know you did; the vault wards logged your sight. So you know the guild wasn't always what it is. Before Vey, we were stewards. We maintained the passages between realms, we kept the tolls honest, we served the keepers. It was humble work. It was *good* work." Her mouth twisted. "And then Vey stood in the Wellspring chamber as a young man and understood something no guildmaster had understood before him: that if the realms drifted apart—if the covenant failed—the only way anyone could cross between them would be through the guild. Every passage. Every road. Every toll. Forever."

"He didn't break it by accident," Ines said. The words came out of her like something pulled. "He broke it on purpose."

"He unmade it thread by thread. He spent fifteen years corrupting the ledgers, erasing the true routes, discrediting the keepers, buying the loyalty of every archivist who might have noticed. And when your mother found out—when she came to him with proof, begging him to stop—" Hallow closed her eyes. "He smiled at her. I remember that. He smiled, and he thanked her for her service, and that night he sent men to the Glasswood."

Ines became aware that she was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. The firelight seemed very bright and very far away, and somewhere beneath the roaring in her ears she registered Sebastian's warmth beside her, his shoulder just touching hers, anchoring her to the room.

"The curse," Sebastian said. His voice was rough, half-wolfed even in human shape these days. "Me. Was that him too?"

Hallow looked at him with something terrible in her face. "You were the first keeper. The oldest oath. Vey couldn't kill you—you were bound into the covenant itself, and killing a keeper would have shattered the borders decades early, before the guild was ready to profit from the gap. So he did worse. He took your name out of the world and sewed the wolf into the space it left. Every moonrise, you forget. Every dawn, you're a little less the man you were." She paused. "He called it mercy, once. To my face. He said a keeper who couldn't remember his oath couldn't be betrayed by it."

* * *

The rain eased toward midnight, but nobody suggested Hallow leave.

Ines sat with her back against a grain sack, the bloodstained map open across her knees though she wasn't reading it. She didn't need to. Everything Hallow had said, her gift had already confirmed in fragments—the forged charter layered over the original, the routes that terminated in dead ends no honest surveyor would have drawn, the way Vey's kindness had always sat slightly wrong, like a map whose rivers flowed uphill. She had seen the shape of the lie for weeks. She had simply never let herself name it.

Because naming it meant losing the guild. And the guild was the only home she had ever let herself have.

"You knew," she said finally. "All those years. You enforced his charter. You hunted people for him. You took Corvo." The guilt rose sharp and hot—Corvo, locked in some guild cell because he'd demanded a debt from a man who couldn't remember owing it. "Why tell me now?"

"Because I hunted your mother's daughter for three weeks," Hallow said, "and every mile of it, I kept thinking: this is how it starts again. Another keeper running. Another keeper dying in the dark while the guild pretends to grieve." She drew a breath. "I told myself for twenty years that staying inside was the only way to limit the damage. That a good enforcer could soften a bad system from within. Do you know what that is, cartographer? That's the same lie you told yourself in the ledgers. Keep your head down. Be useful. Be invisible. The machine will spare you if you feed it."

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same." Hallow's voice didn't rise; that was the worst of it. It stayed gentle, and gentle was a knife. "Loyalty to a thing that lies to you isn't virtue. It's just fear wearing a uniform. I wore mine for twenty years. How long have you worn yours?"

Ines looked at her hands. At the scar on her palm where the map had burned itself into her skin—a thin line now, shaped like nothing, shaped like everything. Eleven years in the guild. Eleven years of bottled starlight and careful columns and deliberate self-erasure, and she had called it contentment. She had called it safety.

Her mother had been invisible too, in the end. Invisible, and then dead, and then lied about.

"If I believe you," Ines said slowly, "then everything changes. If Vey killed her—if the guild is built on that—then I can't go back. Not ever. Not to negotiate, not to hide, not to survive. There's no version of this where I hand over the map and he lets me live, is there?"

"No," Hallow said simply. "There isn't. I'm sorry. I truly am. I liked you, Okafor. You were the best cartographer the guild has produced in a generation, and I mean that as a fact, not flattery. Which is why I'm telling you the truth instead of bringing the cordon." She tilted her head toward the door, toward the dark streets beyond. "They're coming at dawn. Vey knows where you are—he's known since the ford. Maren sold him the Glasswood crossing, and Hallow's word carries further than you'd think." A ghost of bitter humor crossed her face. "My word. Apparently I'm still useful to him. For now."

"And after dawn?"

"After dawn, I'm either the woman who brought him your head, or the woman who warned you and ran." Hallow met her eyes. "I've decided which. But I wanted you to decide first. Freely. With the whole truth in front of you. Your mother deserved that much, and never got it. You'll get it if I have to sit in this rain-soaked barn until the sky falls in."

* * *

Later—much later, when Hallow had withdrawn to the doorway to keep watch out of habit, when the fire had burned down to a red heart—Sebastian came and sat beside Ines in the dark.

"You don't have to decide anything tonight," he said.

"Don't I?" Ines laughed, and it came out cracked. "Dawn's in five hours. The Wellspring is nine days' hard travel. The third keeper is a disgraced priestess selling fake relics, the second keeper is a wolf who forgets himself every moonrise, and the first—" her voice caught on it— "the first keeper is me, according to a dead woman who saw it coming before I was born. Yes. I'd say tonight is exactly when I have to decide."

"What I meant," Sebastian said, "is that you don't have to decide alone."

She turned to look at him. Firelight moved in his eyes, and behind it, faintly, the wolf watched her too—patient, ancient, certain of her in a way that should have frightened her and instead felt like the only solid thing left in a night where every foundation had been pulled away.

"Hallow offered me truth," Ines said quietly. "Vey offered me kindness. And the horrible thing, Sebastian, the thing I can't stop turning over, is that both offers were real. He genuinely believes the broken covenant was mercy. He genuinely thinks he saved the realms from each other. And I believed in him for eleven years because he was kind to me, and kindness felt like proof." She pressed her palm flat against the floor, against the scar. "But truth doesn't need me to believe in it. Truth just needs me to write it down. And I'm a cartographer. Mapping things that shouldn't exist is the only thing I've ever been good for."

"So we go to the Wellspring."

"So we go to the Wellspring." She exhaled. "And Corvo—we get him out. Whatever it costs. I'm done paying for our survival with other people."

Sebastian's hand found hers in the dark, wolf-callused and warm. "Then we'd better move fast," he said, "because the sky over Ashfall just cracked open again—and this time, I don't think it's going to knit back on its own."

Through the granary's broken roof, Ines saw it: the seam above the town splitting wider than she'd ever seen it, light pouring through like blood through bandages, and beneath it, distantly, the sound of Maren's chapel bell ringing wild and unstoppered, calling every soul in Ashfall out into the streets to look up at the coming apart of the world.

"Dawn's cancelled," Ines said, and reached for her map.
