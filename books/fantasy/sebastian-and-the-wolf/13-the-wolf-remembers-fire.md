## 13. The Wolf Remembers Fire

The Glasswood announced itself long before they reached it.

Sebastian smelled it first—a sweetness like burnt sugar riding the wind, wrong for a forest, wrong for any living thing. Then Ines stopped at the treeline and went very still in the way she did when her cartographer's sight was working on something the rest of the world couldn't see.

"It's not wood," she said quietly. "Not anymore. Look at how the light moves through it."

He looked. The trees were glass—every trunk, every branch, every leaf fused into translucent amber and green, so that the forest caught the afternoon sun and scattered it into a thousand burning shards. Walking beneath that canopy would be like walking inside a lantern. The ground crunched underfoot even from here, a sound like stepping on old ice.

"Fire did this?" he asked.

"Fire and something else." She unrolled a corner of the map—the covenant map, the one that had burned itself into his hands and then into hers, though the scars on his palms had faded to pale ghosts while hers still glowed faintly when she slept. "The Glasswood sits where three realms used to touch. When the covenant broke, the borders didn't just drift apart cleanly. They *tore*. And everything caught in the tear got... rendered down. To its simplest truth." She touched one of the glass trunks through the map's edge, as if she could feel it. "Wood burns to glass when the fire is hot enough. This whole forest is what's left after something burned all the way down."

Sebastian said nothing. But something in his chest had gone tight and cold, because the word *fire* had landed in him like a stone dropped down a well, and he was still waiting to hear it hit bottom.

They made camp that night just inside the treeline, where the glass canopy filtered the moonlight into soft green pools. Maren had gone ahead toward the shrine road, muttering about wards and old debts, leaving the two of them alone with the fire Ines built—small, careful, contained in a ring of stones she'd arranged with a surveyor's precision.

Sebastian sat across from it and could not stop looking at it.

"You're staring," Ines said, not looking up from the map.

"I know."

"Is it the wolf? Fire bothers the wolf?"

"No." He turned his palms up, studied them. The old scar tissue there had ached all day, a deep bone-itch like the memory of a burn. "Fire doesn't bother me. That's the strange part. It should. Everything else bothers the wolf—iron, salt, running water at certain hours, church bells in some towns. But fire..." He shook his head slowly. "Fire feels like *home*. And I don't know why, and I hate that I don't know why."

Ines set down the map. In the green-filtered light her face was half shadow, half stained-glass color, and she watched him with the particular attention she gave to things she intended to record honestly. He had learned to recognize that look. He had learned, over these weeks on the road, to be afraid of it and grateful for it in equal measure.

"Tell me what you see," she said. "When you look at the fire. Not what you think. What you see."

He looked.

And the well in his chest finally let the stone hit bottom.

* * *

Flames, but not these flames. Flames the color of dawn, rising from a basin of black stone, and around the basin three figures standing in a triangle, and he knew—he *knew* with a certainty that arrived like a blade between the ribs—that he was one of them.

*We swear by thread and name,* someone was saying, a woman's voice, warm and tired. *By the ink of our own keeping.*

His own voice answered, young, steady, sure in a way he had never been sure of anything since waking in this cursed life: *By the ink of my own keeping.*

And the third voice—

The third voice laughed. A dry, clever laugh, the laugh of a man who found the world slightly absurd and forgave it for being so. *You two are so solemn about it,* the third voice said. *It's only an oath, Sebastian. We'll keep it the way friends keep anything—together, badly, and for longer than we should.*

*Vey.*

The name came out of Sebastian's mouth before he knew he was speaking it, out loud, here, now, in the Glasswood camp with the small careful fire crackling between them.

Ines went rigid. "What did you say?"

"Vey." His heart was slamming. The vision was still moving behind his eyes, layered over the present like a second exposure: the black basin, the dawn-colored flame, the woman with kind tired eyes whose face kept sliding away from him like water. "The guildmaster. Vey was—" He pressed his fists against his eyes. "There were three of us. Three keepers. At a well of fire that wasn't fire. And Vey stood beside me and *laughed*, Ines. He called us friends."

The silence stretched. When he lowered his hands, Ines was watching him with an expression he couldn't read—something between hope and grief.

"The covenant needs three sworn keepers," she said softly. "The map showed me. Two names struck through. Mine written in the third space." Her hand drifted to her palm, to the wolf-shaped scar there. "If you were one of the first..."

"Then one of those struck-through names is mine." He heard his own voice go flat and strange. "I'm not a stranger who stumbled into this, Ines. I'm a piece of it. A broken piece. I was there when it was made, which means I was there when—" 

When it broke. When Vey broke it.

The rage arrived without warning, sudden and total, a red tide that rose from the same well the memories had fallen into. His hands shook. His vision sharpened, colors bleeding, the fire doubling and trebling in his sight—and beneath his skin he felt the wolf stir, not with the usual mindless hunger but with something colder and more purposeful, something that wanted to *run*, to hunt, to put miles of dark forest behind it and then more miles until it reached a guildhall and a man with a dry clever laugh and then—

"Ines." His voice came out roughened, half-growled. "I need you to move away from the fire."

She didn't move. Of course she didn't. "Why?"

"Because I don't trust what I am right now, and you're the thing in this world I least want to hurt."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly mastered, replaced by that stubborn steadiness he had come to rely on more than he could say. She picked up the iron poker from the edge of the campsite and held it loosely, not pointed at him, just *present*. A promise and a warning both.

"Then tell me the rest," she said. "All of it. While you're still mostly you. Because Sebastian—if Vey was your friend, if you were the first keeper, then whatever comes back to you next isn't going to be gentle. And I'd rather hear it from your mouth than watch it eat you alive."

* * *

So he told her. Or rather, the telling happened to him, and she witnessed it.

The fragments came the way ice breaks—unevenly, in sheets, along fault lines he hadn't known were there.

A tower above a city of white bridges, where he had kept the eastern thread of the covenant for eleven years, walking its length each morning like a man walking a garden row. A woman named Sera—the keeper of the southern thread, the one with the kind tired eyes—teaching him to read the covenant's weave the way Ines read maps, by touch, by patience, by refusing to accept the first answer the pattern offered.

And Vey. Always Vey. Younger than the man Ines had described, no grey in his beard yet, brilliant and restless and *hungry* in a way Sebastian had loved him for, once, because the hunger had seemed like ambition, like appetite for the world, before he understood it was appetite for *control*.

"He argued with us," Sebastian said. The words came slowly, dredged up from somewhere deep and silted. "Sera and me. Every convocation, every renewal of the oath. He said binding the realms together meant binding their suffering together too. That a plague in one realm bled into three. That a war in one dragged all of them under. And we told him—that was the *point*. That the covenant wasn't a chain, it was a *promise*. That no realm would ever have to face its worst day alone again." He laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "Gods. I remember believing that. I remember it like it was yesterday, and I can't remember my mother's face, and I can't remember my own surname, but I remember *arguing with Vey about mercy*."

Ines had come closer without either of them noticing. She was sitting beside him now, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her, and she had put down the poker. Her hand hovered near his arm—not touching, waiting, the way she waited with everything, as if the world would give her permission eventually.

"What happened to Sera?" she asked.

The well gave up another sheet of ice, and this one cut him on the way through.

*Sera, standing at the Wellspring, her thread fraying, the borders already beginning to pull. Sera saying: someone has loosened the knots from the inside. Sera looking at him with terrible dawning comprehension, saying his name—not Sebastian, another name, a keeper's name, a name that had been carved out of him like a brand reversed—and then*

Fire. White fire. The Wellspring flaring. Sera's scream cutting off mid-syllable. And Vey standing at the edge of the light with his hand still raised, tears on his face, *weeping as he did it*, saying: *forgive me, it's the only kindness left.*

Sebastian came back to himself on his knees in the glass-needle loam, and Ines's arms were around him, and he was shaking so hard his teeth chattered.

"He killed her." The words scraped out of him raw. "He killed her at the Wellspring and he *wept* doing it, and then he came for me, and I ran, and the curse—" His breath hitched. "The curse isn't a punishment, Ines. I thought—I've spent twenty years thinking some enemy cursed me, some rival, some monster. But it was Vey. It had to be. He couldn't kill the first keeper outright—the covenant wouldn't allow it, the threads were still too strong—so he *unmade* me instead. Split me down the middle. Man by night, wolf by moonrise. A keeper who can't keep anything. He took my name and my shape and my memory and he left me a riddle walking around in a skin."

"And the third name on the map," Ines whispered. "The one struck through besides yours. That was Sera."

"Yes."

They stayed like that for a long time, the fire burning down between them, the glass canopy overhead holding the moonlight the way a wound holds light. Sebastian became aware, gradually, that his shaking had stopped, and that Ines's hand had found his, and that neither of them had decided for this to happen—it had simply happened, the way water finds level.

And that was when the terror came.

It crept in under the rage the way cold creeps in under a door. Because the rage was clean; the rage had a target; the rage pointed forward, toward Vey, toward the Wellspring, toward the reforging. But underneath the rage, quieter, was this: *every memory that returns is a memory that replaces something else.*

He had woken into this life with nothing, and Ines had been the first thing the nothing had been given. Her face in the fog outside the salt door. Her stubbornness in the catacombs. The way she mapped their journey in a private ledger she thought he didn't know about, and the way she sometimes looked at a blank page like it was asking her a question she couldn't answer honestly. Two months of a life built grain by grain on top of the ruin of the old one.

And the old one was coming back. Sheet by sheet. And when it finished coming back—when Sebastian the keeper stood whole again in his own past—what would be left of the man who had met Ines Okafor at a salt door and chosen her before he knew there was anything to choose?

"If I remember everything," he said, and his voice sounded far away to himself, "will I still be the person who—"

He stopped. Couldn't finish it. *Who chose you first. Who trusts you with the wolf. Who—*

"Don't," Ines said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Don't ask me that question tonight." She was looking at the fire, and in the dying light her eyes were bright and wet and absolutely steady. "Because I've been asking myself a version of it since the vault, and I don't have an answer, and I refuse to spend the hours before dawn being afraid of you. You're allowed to be angry at Vey. You're allowed to grieve Sera—you *should* grieve her, she deserves grieving. But you are not allowed to stand here and treat the man I know as a temporary condition." Her grip tightened on his hand. "Memory isn't a replacement, Sebastian. It's an addition. Whoever you were, you're also whoever you chose to be with no past at all. I intend to hold you to both."

He stared at her. Something in his chest that had been clenched for twenty years—since the white fire, since the curse, since the first moonrise when he'd woken four-legged and screaming—loosened by a single degree.

"You should write that down," he managed. "In your private ledger. The page you keep rewriting."

Her breath caught. "You know about that?"

"I'm a wolf, Ines. I smell ink."

For a moment—one suspended, impossible moment—she laughed. It was a small laugh, startled out of her, and it broke the night open, and he thought: *whatever comes, whatever returns, whatever Vey took and whatever the Wellspring demands—I had this. I have this.*

Then the fire died.

Not guttered—*died*, all at once, snuffed as if a great hand had closed over it, and the darkness that rushed in was absolute, and the Glasswood around them began to ring. Every glass trunk, every glass leaf, humming a single low note like a struck bell, and Ines was on her feet with the map already unrolled, and her face in the sudden silver wash of moonlight had gone the color of ash.

"The threads," she breathed. "Sebastian—the threads are *singing*. Something's happening at the borders. Something's accelerating."

Far to the north, beyond the glass trees, beyond the hills, the sky lit briefly with a color that had no name—and the ringing of the Glasswood resolved, unmistakably, into a word.

Into a name.

Hers.
