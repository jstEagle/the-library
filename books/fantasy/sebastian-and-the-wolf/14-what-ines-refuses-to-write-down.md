## 14. What Ines Refuses to Write Down

The fire had burned itself down to a red heart by the time Sebastian fell asleep, and I finally took out the ledger.

I kept it at the bottom of my pack, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the spare shirt and the tin of salt I'd stolen from the guild kitchens on our first night out. It was a small book, guild-issue, its cover stamped with the compass-rose that every apprentice received at their swearing-in. Mine had been blank for six years. A cartographer who wanted to stay invisible did not keep private maps. Private maps were evidence. Evidence was a noose. I had learned that lesson before I learned to write cursive.

Now it was half full.

I unwrapped it with the care of someone handling something volatile, which it was. Not the bottled-starlight kind of volatile, not the kind that singed your eyebrows when you mishandled it. The other kind. The kind that burned slow and went all the way down.

Across the top of the current page, in my smallest hand, I had written: *Route Journal — Glasswood Crossing to the Ford of Sorrows.* Below it, entries in the tidy shorthand the guild used for field surveys, compressed until they looked like nothing at all: distances paced, bearings taken off the sun and the fraying sky, water sources marked, the places where the ground hummed wrong underfoot because two realms were pulling apart like wet paper. Anyone who found it would see a surveyor's notes. Dry as chalk. Useless to Sister Hallow even if she tore my pack apart looking for it.

That was the design. That had always been the design.

And then there was the other page.

It sat near the back of the ledger, and I turned to it the way you turn to a wound you've been told not to touch. The paper there was soft from handling, grayed at the edges where my thumb had worried it, and it bore the ghost-layers of everything I'd written and crossed out and written again. Ink over ink over ink, until in certain lights the whole page looked bruised.

*He* began the latest attempt. I read it by firelight, hating every word.

*He sleeps facing the door. He gave me the larger blanket without being asked about it. When the wolf comes up in him at dusk he walks away from me first, always, so that whatever happens happens far from camp, and he comes back at dawn smelling of frost and pine and looking at me like he's checking whether I'm still—*

I stopped. My pen hovered. *Still what?* That was the sentence that wouldn't finish. That was the sentence I had started eleven times, in eleven different hands, some of them pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn through.

Because if I wrote *still alive*, that was a lie of smallness. If I wrote *still trusting me*, that was closer but still wrong, because trust wasn't the thing in his eyes at dawn. And if I wrote the true word—the one sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal—then the ledger stopped being a surveyor's notes and became a confession, and confessions could be read.

I looked across the fire at him.

Sebastian slept on his side with one hand curled near his face, and even in sleep his brow was furrowed, as though the curse worked on him even now, sifting through what remained of him for anything it could take. Twenty years of the wolf had eaten him down to fragments. He remembered fire and a friend's laugh and almost nothing else. He didn't remember the covenant. He didn't remember Vey's face, though rage found him whenever Vey's name came up, rising in him like water finding a crack. He didn't remember me.

Except sometimes he looked at me like he did.

That was the coal. That was the word I couldn't write. Because the honest sentence wasn't about him at all—it was about me—and honest sentences about myself were the one genre of map I had spent my entire adult life refusing to draw.

* * *

I had been invisible for a long time, and I want to be precise about what that meant, because people mistake invisibility for safety and it isn't, it never was, it is only a slower way of disappearing.

In the guild, I was the woman who catalogued shipments. Bottled starlight, jars of frozen thunder, crates of border-dust from realms whose names had been struck from the rolls. I entered them in ledgers with flawless penmanship and made no friends and asked no questions, and Guildmaster Vey once said—in that warm voice of his, the voice that was itself a kind of forgery—that I was the most reliable clerk the guild had ever employed. Reliable. Which meant: predictable. Which meant: no threat. I had built that reputation brick by brick, and I had been proud of it, and the pride should have warned me. You don't feel proud of a hiding place unless part of you believes you deserve to be hidden.

My mother had understood that. I hadn't known she understood it until the survey station, until her field notes, until I read her handwriting describing the exact same discipline I'd taught myself—*keep the ledgers dull, keep the doors shut, keep your name out of other people's mouths*—and then, three pages later, watched her break every rule she'd set herself to go die at the Wellspring trying to hold the realms together. She had been invisible too. She had been invisible right up until the moment invisibility cost more than visibility, and then she chose, and the choice killed her, and I had spent twenty years mistaking her death for an argument against choosing.

Sitting by that fire in the Glasswood, watching Sebastian breathe, I understood at last that it had been an argument *for.*

But understanding a thing and writing it down are different acts entirely. Writing makes it real. Writing makes it findable. And the truest sentence I knew—the one under all the crossed-out drafts—was a liability of the worst kind, because we were being hunted, because Hallow was behind us somewhere with Corvo in chains and my route in her pocket, because anything I committed to paper could be taken from me and used.

You cannot ransom a man with a feeling. But you can ransom him with proof of a feeling, and Hallow struck me as exactly the sort of person who would know the difference.

So I sat with the pen above the page and did what I had done eleven times before.

I crossed it out.

One clean line through *He sleeps facing the door,* through the blanket, through the walking-away-at-dusk, through all of it, back to the margin. The page now had twelve attempts on it, layered like sediment, like the guild charter with its forged lines drawn over the covenant's true ones. I almost laughed at that. Even my cowardice had layers. Even my erasures were becoming a text someone could read—if they knew how to look, if they had the sight, if they held the page up to the right light the way I held maps up to the truth.

Which was, of course, exactly what I was doing to myself. Holding my own page up to the light. Reading the shape underneath the lies.

And the shape underneath was this: I loved him.

There. Written nowhere. True anyway.

Not the storybook kind—I'd catalogued enough bottled starlight to be immune to the storybook kind. This was quieter and worse. It was the love of someone who had watched another person be dismantled and had decided, without ceremony, without asking anyone's permission, that whatever pieces remained were worth staying for. It was the love of a cartographer for a country that had been burned over: you loved it not for what it showed you but for what you knew it had been, and for what it might grow back into, given time and rain and someone stubborn enough to keep the fires off.

He stirred in his sleep and murmured something—not words, just sound, the low note the wolf made living somewhere under his human throat—and my hand shook hard enough that the pen skipped.

I hated that. I hated that my hand shook. Six years of steady strokes, of flawless entries, of being the most reliable clerk the guild had ever employed, and this man asleep by a dying fire could make my pen skip like an apprentice's.

*This is what it costs,* I told myself. *This is the toll.*

Everything true had a toll. My mother had paid hers at the Wellspring. Maren had refused to pay hers and was paying interest instead, in flinches and fake relics and the particular exhaustion of a woman pretending her vows hadn't mattered. Even Vey—especially Vey—had paid, trading the covenant for a monopoly, trading the world's wholeness for the guild's profit, and calling the trade mercy because the alternative was admitting it was greed. There was no version of this road where nobody paid. There was only the question of what you'd pay, and when, and whether you'd do it with your eyes open.

Mine, apparently, was a page in a ledger.

I turned back to the front of the book, to the route journal, and forced myself to work. That was the bargain I'd struck with myself years ago: feelings got crossed out; roads got recorded. The Glasswood crossing went in—pacing, bearing, the stretch where the trees grew in rings like fingerprints pressed into the world. The fire, briefly, clinically: *forest fire, natural origin, passed east of us by nightfall.* No mention of how Sebastian had gone rigid at the smell of smoke. No mention of what he'd said after, when the memory broke over him like a wave—*I was the first keeper, Ines. Vey stood beside me when I swore. He held the lamp.* No mention of how his voice had cracked on *friend*, or how he'd looked at me afterward with terror in his eyes, and I had thought at first he was afraid of Vey, and only later understood he was afraid of remembering—that every fragment the curse gave back was also a piece of whatever we might have been, burning away.

None of that went in the journal. Journals got seized. I knew that now better than ever, with Corvo in Hallow's hands and my folded-space trick spent and our faces known in every town between here and the Wellspring.

Instead I wrote the road. And then, because I am weak, because I have always been weak in exactly one direction, I turned to the back page one more time.

Twelve crossed-out drafts stared up at me.

I wrote a thirteenth.

*He doesn't remember me, and I am starting to understand that it doesn't matter. Whatever we were before, whatever the covenant took from him, the man who walked away from camp at dusk so the wolf wouldn't reach me—he is not a remainder. He is not what's left over. He is someone I would choose again from nothing. From less than nothing. From a fog-bound night and a bloodstained map and a stranger collapsing against a salt door.*

*I love him. Write it plain. If Hallow takes this book, let her read it. Let her know exactly what she can take from me, and let her learn what I already know: that a thing written down honestly cannot actually be erased. You can burn the page. The entry stands.*

My hand was steady for that last line. Steadier than it had been for any route entry, any shipment catalogue, any perfect invisible sentence in six years of guild service.

I did not cross it out.

I closed the ledger, wrapped it in oilcloth, and pushed it to the bottom of the pack—and then, after a moment, pulled it back out and moved it to the top, where my hand could find it fast. If we ran, I wanted it with me. If we were taken, well. Let it be found. Some things you stop hiding the moment you understand that hiding them is the same as losing them.

Across the fire, Sebastian opened his eyes.

Not all the way—just a sliver, the way he always woke, a habit of twenty years of sleeping as prey—but enough. Enough to see me sitting up with the oilcloth bundle still in my lap, enough to see my face doing whatever it was doing, which I suspected was not the face of a woman reviewing route notes.

"You're keeping a journal," he said quietly. Not a question. His voice was rough with sleep and with the wolf, which never fully left it anymore.

"Surveyor's habit."

"Mm." He watched me for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the Glasswood ticked and settled, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, the ground hummed its wrong-note hum—the realms pulling apart, the miles between here and the Wellspring stretching like taffy. "Does it have me in it?"

My chest did something complicated. "Every good map has its landmarks," I said.

He smiled—a small, tired, real smile—and closed his eyes again. But just before sleep took him back, he said, almost too soft to hear:

"I hope I'm drawn kindly. I don't remember enough to defend myself."

I sat there a long time after his breathing evened out, the ledger warm in my hands like a live coal, and told myself the ache in my chest was just fear of the road ahead. The ford was two days' march. The water there ran loud enough to drown out footsteps. And Maren had been quiet all evening—too quiet, the way people get quiet when a decision is being made inside them that they haven't announced yet.

I wrote one last line in the journal, dated it, and underlined it twice:

*Ford tomorrow. Trust no one's silence.*
