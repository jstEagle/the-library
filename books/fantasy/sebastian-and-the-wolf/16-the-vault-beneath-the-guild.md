## 16. The Vault Beneath the Guild

The Guild of Unmapped Realms had a hundred ways to keep people out and exactly one way to let them in, and Ines Okafor had spent eleven years learning both.

She stood now in the fog at the bottom of Cartographer's Hill, hood up, the salt door looming above her like a mouth. Behind her, Ashfall's fraying sky was a day's hard ride away; ahead of her, every corridor she'd ever walked was waiting with its wards lit and its enforcers armed. Sister Hallow would have told Vey by now that Ines had run. Hallow would not have told him where.

*They'll expect me to stay gone,* Ines thought. *A fugitive doesn't come home.*

But a fugitive didn't know what she knew. A fugitive hadn't spent a decade cataloging shipments of bottled starlight, hadn't memorized which ledgers were true and which were theater, hadn't learned—because her gift had never let her unlearn it—the true shape of every map the guild kept, including the one drawn on its own floor.

Her mother had died trying to repair the covenant. That sentence had been sitting in Ines's chest since the survey station, heavy as wet stone, and it had hardened overnight into something sharper: *her mother had died here.* The guild's records would say otherwise. The guild's records said a great many things.

Ines pulled off her hood, squared her shoulders into the posture of a tired clerk returning from an errand, and walked up to the salt door.

The ward tasted her before she reached it—a prickling along her jaw, the old familiar scrutiny. Salt-wards read intention the way maps read terrain. She let her intention be small and gray: *I am coming back to my desk. I am sorry I left. I want my ledger.*

The door opened for her. Of course it did. Vey wanted her found, and the fastest way to find a person is to make her own home a trap.

"Good," Ines murmured, and stepped inside.

* * *

The entrance hall smelled of ink and beeswax, unchanged, and that was almost the cruelest thing. Eleven years of her life lived in this smell. Junior clerks glanced up from their copying desks, and she gave them the nod she'd given them ten thousand times—*nothing to see, nothing happening*—and they looked back down. Invisibility, she thought, was a skill like any other. It did not rust.

Two enforcers stood at the foot of the stair to the vaults. She'd expected that too. What she hadn't expected was that they nodded to her first.

"Sister Hallow said you'd come back." The taller one—Brannic, who owed her three favors for correcting his route-surveys before anyone noticed his errors—couldn't quite meet her eyes. "She said to bring you straight up."

"To the guildmaster?"

"To her office first." He hesitated. "Ines. Whatever you're doing—"

"I'm going to my desk," she said pleasantly, "to finish the starlight inventory I abandoned. If Sister Hallow wants me, she knows where my desk is. It has been in the same place for eleven years."

It was a stupid gambit and a good one, because it was so boring no one had planned for it. Brannic exchanged a look with his partner, and the partner shrugged the shrug of a man who did not want to physically escort a senior cartographer past her own workstation over an inventory dispute. Bureaucracy was a fortress too, and Ines had its blueprints.

She walked past them, down the east corridor, through the map room with its hanging charts of realms that officially did not exist, and into the long gallery of ledgers where she had spent her invisible life.

Then she kept walking, past her desk, to the far wall.

Here was the part that required her gift.

To any eye, the wall behind the last ledger-case was plastered stone, same as every other wall. But Ines had always seen what maps truly were, and a building was only a map you stood inside. The guild's charter—the great framed document in the hall upstairs—was a forgery layered over something older; she'd seen that weeks ago, the way you see a palimpsest when the light is right. And buildings, like documents, remembered what had been written over them. Under the plaster, under the stone, the original footprint of the guild showed through: smaller, older, and containing a room that no longer appeared on any floor plan.

A vault. Beneath the guildmaster's wing. Sealed, but not destroyed—because Vey, whatever else he was, was a man who kept things.

The problem was the wards between her and it. Three of them, if her reading of the building's bones was right. The first was the corridor itself, which recorded every passage in a ledger somewhere below. Fine. Let it record her. She was supposed to be at her desk; she would simply be a clerk taking the long way.

The second was the door to the guildmaster's wing, warded against everyone but Vey and his inner circle. Ines stopped in front of it and pressed her scarred palm flat against the wood.

The map had burned itself into her skin the night Sebastian died at her feet—not died, she corrected herself, *was taken*, and the thought made her jaw tighten—and the mark it left was more than a scar. It was a signature. Her mother's handwriting, in covenant-ink, naming Ines Okafor keeper. The wing-door's ward was guild-work, young and obedient, and guild-work recognized authority the way dogs did. It read her palm, found something older than itself, and got out of the way with a shiver she felt through the wood.

*Sorry, Vey,* she thought. *Your dog just rolled over for someone else.*

The third ward was the one that mattered. Somewhere below the guildmaster's study, guarding the hidden vault, there would be work that was not guild-work at all. Work from the time of the covenant. And covenant-work would not be fooled by a forged charter or a clerk's gray intentions.

She descended the private stair behind Vey's study—she knew it existed because she had once cataloged a shipment delivered to "the lower archive," a room that appeared on no plan, and had traced the delivery route backward out of pure professional spite—and came at last to a plain iron door in a plain stone corridor that smelled of cold and old paper.

The ward on it was not a lock. It was a question.

She felt it form in the air around her, patient and enormous, older than the guild, older than Vey, older than her grief: *Who swears?*

Ines lifted her scarred palm.

"Ines Okafor," she said. "Daughter of Adaeze Okafor. Keeper, third name."

The silence stretched so long she heard her own heartbeat. Then, deep beneath the floor, something turned over—a sound like a vast page being smoothed flat after centuries of being clenched—and the iron door swung inward without a touch.

* * *

The vault beneath the guild was small, dry, and lit by lamps that burned without oil, and it contained the honest history of the world.

Ines stood in the doorway for a long moment, letting her cartographer's sight adjust, the way you let your eyes adjust to a dark room. Every document in here rang true. That was the only way she could describe it—after years of reading forged charts that shimmered slightly wrong at the edges, these pages sat still in her perception like stones in a stream. The original Covenant of Threads lay open on a reading table in the center, its three name-spaces visible even from the door: two struck through in old blood-brown ink, the third bearing her mother's hand, and beneath it, fresher, her own name written in fire.

But it was the shelves around the walls that stopped her breath.

Ledgers. Hundreds of them. The guild's true accounts—the ones that had never passed through her hands, the shadow-twin of everything she'd spent her life cataloging. Passage-fees collected on roads the guild swore it didn't control. Realms sealed off and the tolls charged to reopen them. Payments. Names. Dates.

And one slim volume, set apart on its own stand, its spine cracked from frequent use.

Ines crossed the room and opened it, and the cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the vault's temperature.

It was a maintenance log. Dated twenty-two years back. Entries in a neat, disciplined hand she recognized from a thousand guild forms—Vey's hand, younger, already precise. And the entries were about a woman.

*Subject continues survey of the borderlands despite counsel. Subject located the second fracture at the Glasswood ford. Subject requests access to covenant materials. Denied.*

*Subject has contacted the priestess Maren. Surveillance increased.*

*Subject has sworn herself keeper. This is a direct threat to guild autonomy. Council concurs.*

*Arrangement made with the bandits of the Ashfall road. Payment authorized. No guild hand to be evident.*

*Adaeze Okafor deceased. Charter narrative prepared: death by misadventure during unsanctioned survey. Circulate to all stations.*

Ines read it twice. Then a third time, because the words refused to mean anything the first two times, sliding off her mind like rain off waxed canvas.

*No guild hand to be evident.*

Her mother had not died in some tragic accident on a lonely road. Her mother had been murdered by contract, on the orders of the man who called Ines *child* and offered her tea and protection, and the guild had written the lie into its records with the same tidy efficiency it used for starlight inventories, and the lie had sat in a file for twenty-two years while Ines grew up orphaned and grateful and *invisible*, believing invisibility was safety—

Believing, all this time, that the guild had merely failed her mother.

It hadn't failed her. It had *filed* her.

Ines became aware, distantly, that she was standing very still in the way she stood when she was about to do something irreversible. Her hands were not shaking. That frightened her more than shaking would have. The anger in her wasn't hot; it was cold and clear and perfectly level, like the line of a meridian, like a blade laid flat on a table. It did not want to scream. It wanted to *map*. It wanted to find the exact coordinates of every crime in this room and write them down where no forgery could cover them.

She set the maintenance log down with exaggerated care, because if she did not handle it carefully she was going to tear it apart, and tearing evidence was a cartographer's sin.

Think. Think like a ledger.

Hallow had offered her truth in exchange for—what? Surrender, probably, or testimony against Sebastian. Hallow's truths were currency, and currency could be counterfeited. But this—this was not Hallow's truth. This was the vault's truth, primary-source, unwitnessed, in Vey's own hand. Even if Hallow had sent her down here as a trap, the trap had handed her the one thing that could not be unfound.

And Vey. Vey had argued, in that warm reasonable voice, that the covenant's fall was mercy. Bound realms meant bound suffering. The guild merely profited from the gap. He had almost made her believe he was half-right.

He had murdered her mother and called it administration.

Ines looked at the Covenant of Threads on its table, at her mother's struck-through name and her own burning one, and understood at last what inheritance meant. Not destiny. Not a burden assigned by fate. It meant that someone had died so that this document could wait here, patient as stone, for her to walk in and pick it up. Her mother had chosen truth over safety and paid the full price, and the guild had buried the price in a maintenance log, and Ines had spent her whole life trying to be safe in the house of the people who killed her.

*Invisibility was never safety,* her mother's field notes had said, or nearly. *Only a slower death.*

Well. Ines was done dying slowly.

She worked fast after that, and her hands were steady as drafting tools. She could not carry the covenant itself—it was bound to the Wellspring, and besides, taking it would tell Vey instantly what she'd found. Instead she did what she did best: she copied. Her gift made it easy; she read the true shape of each page and wrote it into her private ledger, the compact folded-space script she'd invented for mapping roads that weren't on any map, until the entire covenant and the twenty most damning entries of the maintenance log lived in a book small enough to hide in her coat.

Last, she took the maintenance log itself. Evidence walked out with her or it stayed here to be burned the moment Vey knew she'd been below. She wrapped it in oilcloth and slid it inside her shirt, against her heart, where it lay like a second scar.

At the vault door she paused, looking back at the little room of honest paper—the only honest room in the whole guild, hidden directly beneath the liar's feet. There was a poetry to that which Vey would hate.

"One more thing," she said aloud, to the ward, to the covenant, to her mother's ghost in the ink. "When this is over, this room gets windows."

The lamp-flames bent, very slightly, as if in agreement.

* * *

She was three steps up the private stair when she heard voices above—Vey's, warm and unhurried, and another voice answering, dry as ash.

Sister Hallow.

"—found at the survey station, Guildmaster. The wolf escaped us at the ford, but the girl doubled back. She's in the building. My people saw her pass the east corridor not twenty minutes ago."

A pause. Ines flattened herself against the stairwell wall, in the dead angle beneath the spiral, and listened to her own pulse.

"She came home," Vey said, and there was something in his voice she had never heard before—something almost tender, and infinitely worse than anger. "Of course she did. She's a cartographer, Hallow. We are the only map she has ever trusted." A soft laugh. "Find her. Gently, if she allows it. And clear my study—I have a feeling I'll want privacy tonight."

Footsteps moved away. Hallow's voice, farther now, giving quiet orders.

Ines stood in the dark of the stairwell with her mother's murder pressed against her chest and Vey's kindness still ringing in her ears, and she made the coldest, clearest decision of her life.

She wasn't leaving yet.

Not until she had seen his face when he learned what she'd found. Not until the ledger was balanced.

She began, silently, to climb.
