## 17. Vey's Confession

The lamp in Guildmaster Vey's study had always been green.

Ines had never questioned it. Green glass, green light, the color of ledger-ink drying, the color of the guild itself. She had sat across that desk a hundred times as an apprentice, hands folded, answering questions about shipping manifests for bottled starlight with the careful monotony of a girl who wanted to be overlooked. The green light had made her feel safe then—small things under small lamps, that was the whole philosophy of her life.

Now she stood in the same room with the original Covenant of Threads unrolled on the desk between them, its three names glowing faintly like embers refusing to die, and the green light looked different. It looked like the light at the bottom of deep water. It looked like drowning.

Vey closed the door behind her with a soft click.

"You came back," he said. "I confess I didn't think you would. Runners run. That's not a criticism—it's a compliment to your instincts. Your instincts were excellent. It's your conclusions that concern me."

"Where is Sebastian?"

"In a place where no one will hurt him." Vey moved past her to his chair but did not sit. He rested both hands on its back, and Ines noticed—for the first time in twelve years of proximity—that his knuckles were scarred, old white seams crossing them like survey lines. Hands that had once done fieldwork. Hands that had once held a pen over something other than ledgers. "Maren is with him. Sister Hallow keeps them comfortable."

"Comfortable," Ines repeated. The word tasted like the salt door. "You mean contained."

"I mean alive." He said it simply, and that simplicity was the most frightening thing in the room. "Which is more than the covenant ever did for them. Sit down, Ines."

She didn't sit. She kept the map-vault key pressed against her palm inside her sleeve, and she kept her eyes moving: the window, the door, the seam in the floorboards beneath the rug where she'd found the hidden documents—the records, the letters, the order signed in Vey's own hand that had sent her mother into the Glasswood with a survey team of two and no return route.

Vey followed her gaze to the rug. Something moved through his face—not guilt exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a wound he'd stopped counting years ago.

"Yes," he said quietly. "You found them. I wondered when you would. You read a room the way you read a map, don't you? Your mother did too. It's the one inheritance I couldn't forge my way out of giving you."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on the word. "Don't you dare put yourself in the line of inheritance. You killed her."

"I sent her on a survey." He straightened. "The Glasswood was burning that season. Everyone who walked in knew the risk. She knew it better than anyone—she'd mapped the fire-roads herself, before you were born."

"The records show there was no fire that season. You forged the weather reports. I *read* them, Vey. I read everything you wrote for twenty years, and I know your hand better than you do. When you lie, you press too hard on the downstrokes." She pulled the folded papers from her coat and laid them beside the covenant, one by one, each page landing with a sound like a small verdict. "The weather reports. The requisition for her team's supplies—approved, then cancelled, then approved again so the trail would tangle. And this." The last page. The order. His signature, pressing too hard.

For a long moment the green lamp hummed. Somewhere below them, the guild's night shift turned over its pages; she could hear the scratch of pens through the floor, dozens of them, the sound she had slept to for half her life, suddenly revealed as the sound of a machine grinding.

Then Vey laughed.

It wasn't a cruel laugh. That was the horror of it. It was tired, and rueful, and it aged him ten years in a breath, and Ines understood with a lurch that she had never once seen the real man—only the guildmaster, the office, the green lamp. The real man was standing in front of her now, exhausted, and he had been waiting a very long time for someone to find the papers.

"So," he said. "Hallow told you about the charter. The forgery. Yes—I know she did. She's been circling me for years, waiting for someone like you to hand her the knife. Tell me, Ines. Did she tell you *why*?"

"She said you broke the covenant to keep the passages. To make the guild the only road between realms. Monopoly. Profit." Ines recited it flatly, because reciting was armor. "Twenty years of tolls on every crossing, every shipment, every exile who needed to vanish from one world into another. I've catalogued the shipments. I know what the gap pays."

"And how much of it have you seen me spend?" Vey spread his hands. The study around them was spare to the point of austerity—one desk, one lamp, one chair, shelves of chart-standards worn soft with handling. "Look at this room. Look at it. Every guildmaster before me built a wing, commissioned a portrait, gilded the Salt Door until it hurt to touch. What have I added in twenty years?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing," he agreed. "Because the money was never the point. The gap paid, yes—we took our tolls, the guild ate well, I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But profit was the *excuse*, Ines. Not the reason. May I show you the reason? It's the only thing I have left to give, and you're the only person left alive who might understand it."

He reached beneath the desk—beneath the same floorboards, the second hiding place she hadn't known existed—and drew out a thin volume bound in grey leather, cracked along the spine, its corners rounded by decades of handling. A survey journal. Field notes. She knew the format intimately; she'd grown up on her mother's.

"This was mine," Vey said. "Before I was guildmaster. Before any of it. I was a passage-surveyor. Third rate, honestly—no gift for true shapes, just diligence. But diligence gets you assigned places the gifted won't go. And forty-one years ago, they sent me to map the borderlands during the last binding. The covenant was still whole then. Three keepers. One of them—" his thumb moved over the journal's cover, almost tenderly— "was a young man named Sebastian, though nobody called him that yet. Names come later, out here. Out in the frays."

Ines's breath went shallow. "You knew him."

"I carried him out of a fray on my back when he was nineteen and freshly cursed, screaming that he could still feel the realm he'd been born in dissolving around him like sugar in rain." Vey opened the journal. "Sit down now, Ines. Please. This isn't a story that stands up well."

She sat.

* * *

He showed her the drawings first. Ink sketches of the borderlands under the old covenant—and Ines, whose gift let her see the true shape of anything drawn, felt her stomach turn over, because the shapes were *beautiful*. Two realms lying against each other not like neighbors but like interlocked fingers, their borders woven, their weathers trading colors across the seam. A market street in Ashfall where the sky above the rooftops belonged to three different dawns at once, layered like silk. Children's drawings copied from life: kids playing a game that required stepping between realms mid-hop, laughing, because the borders bent for them like water.

"It was like that everywhere," Vey said. "Along every seam. The realms breathed together. Trade, yes, and migration, and all the commerce the guild later claimed to protect—but also this." He tapped a sketch of a funeral procession crossing a border to bury a woman in the realm where she'd been born. "Grief could follow people home. Love could. Debt could. Everything crossed, because everything was held. That's what the covenant *was*. Not a wall. A loom. And three keepers were the shuttle."

"What happened to it?" Ines asked, though part of her already knew, and part of her already wished she didn't.

"The loom snagged." He turned the page, and the drawings changed. The same market street, but the triple dawn was tearing, one sky pulling away from the others like wet paper. The children's game had a new drawing beside it—a child mid-step between realms when the seam shuddered, and the sketch showed only one shoe landing. "Every binding has a cost. Nobody writes that part down, because the cost isn't paid in gold. It's paid in *stuckness*. In suffering that can't drain away because the borders that would let it drain are sewn shut."

He laid the journal flat and looked at her with eyes that were, horribly, kind.

"Famine in the Vellum Marches, thirty-eight years ago. The marches are bound to the granary-realm of Osterhale—you know this, it's in the charter's history appendix. Under the covenant, the famine *spread*. It was required to. The hunger in the marches pulled grain across the border and the shortage in Osterhale deepened, because the loom shares everything, Ines. Joy and plague alike. Eleven thousand died in the marches, and when the keepers finally loosened the threads enough to quarantine—just enough, a mercy-loosening—another four thousand died in Osterhale of the panic and the price-gouging and the hoarding. Fifteen thousand dead, total, because the covenant demanded the suffering be *shared*."

"That's not—" Ines started.

"Let me finish. Please. You owe me that much; I'll owe you the rest." His voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, going quiet and level as a plumb-line. "A blight, two decades ago, that hopped borders in nine days because the roots of the Glasswood are woven together. A war in the Salt Reaches that couldn't stay contained because the covenant carried the refugees into six neighboring realms and the resentment carried the war. I stood in refugee camps, Ines. I drew maps for mass graves. I'm good at that—diligent. And every time, I thought: the keepers could stop this. Three people, holding three names, could loosen the weave wherever the weave was strangling us. And every time, I asked, and every time the answer was the same."

"*The binding must hold,*" Ines said. She'd read it in the covenant itself, felt it in the ink: the oath's iron clause. *No thread may be loosened for mercy, for mercy unravels.*

"No thread may be loosened for mercy," Vey repeated. "Your mother said those exact words to me. She said them gently. That was the worst of it—she was gentle about it, she wept, she brought food to the camps herself, she gave until she had nothing. But she would not bend the weave. None of them would. Because they were right, you understand. They were *completely right*. Mercy-loosening does unravel; the mathematics are unforgiving; every exception becomes a doorway becomes a breach. They chose the many over the few, forever, with open eyes. Saints, all three of them. And I stood in the graves and decided that sainthood was a luxury purchased with other people's blood, and that somebody had to be the villain instead."

The green light hummed. Ines found her hands had gone cold around the edge of the desk.

"So you broke it."

"I *unbound* it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole confession." Vey came around the desk and stood over the covenant, and he didn't touch it. He looked at it the way a man looks at a judge. "I didn't tear the threads. Tearing would have shattered the realms; even I wasn't mad enough for that. I cut the *keep*. I removed the three names from active oath—struck two, hid the third—and without sworn keepers, the covenant went dormant. Still holding, barely, out of habit, the way an abandoned house stays standing for a while. But no longer *enforcing*. No longer sharing. The famine could be quarantined. The blight could burn out in one realm. The wars could end at borders again."

"And the fraying?" Ines's voice shook. "The sky coming apart over Ashfall? The cities flickering out of alignment? Was that in your arithmetic, Guildmaster?"

The flinch was small but real. He covered it by turning toward the window, where the night over the guild's rooftops showed a faint wrongness at the horizon—a shimmer, barely visible from here, that she knew up close was a realm losing its grip on the next one.

"Yes," he admitted. "That was in the arithmetic. Dormancy decays. I calculated the covenant had sixty years of habit left in it, perhaps eighty, and I told myself that was time enough—invent a replacement, train new keepers under guild control, build a managed binding, one with mercy-clauses written in properly this time. A covenant designed by accountants instead of saints." He smiled without humor. "I was wrong about the timeline. The decay accelerated when your mother started trying to repair it alone. Her attempts stressed the old weave—like tugging a knot tighter while trying to untie it. So yes. Her death bought the realms perhaps ten fewer years than my plan allowed. I've had a long time to sit with that particular sum."

"You sent her to die for slowing down your decay."

"I sent her to stop her, and the Glasswood did the rest, and I have written her name in the margin of my private ledger every year on the anniversary, and none of it changes what I did." He turned back to face Ines fully, and she saw that his eyes were wet and that he made no attempt to hide it, and that this too was a kind of calculation, and that knowing it was a calculation did not make it less real. "There it is. All of it. The guild profited from the gap—that's true, and I let it, because a guild that eats well asks no questions. But ask yourself, Ines Okafor, cartographer, reader of true shapes—look at me with that gift your mother gave you and tell me what shape you see."

She looked. It wasn't voluntary. Her sight opened the way it always did, peeling surfaces, reading structure—and what she saw in Vey was not the shape of greed, which she knew well from a thousand smugglers' maps, all appetite and no architecture. What she saw was load-bearing. Conviction running through the man like a spine. Twenty years of tolls and forged charters arranged around a single grim belief, the way a fortress arranges itself around a keep.

He believed it. Gods help her, he believed every word.

And the worst part—the part that made her want to be sick—was that he was half right. She had read the covenant. She had felt the iron of that mercy-clause, the beautiful, merciless logic of it. Bound realms meant bound suffering. Fifteen thousand dead in two famines. A child's single shoe. Her own mother choosing the many over the few until it killed her. The covenant was noble the way a glacier is noble: magnificent, ancient, and utterly indifferent to whoever froze in its path.

"You almost have me," Ines said quietly.

Vey's head tilted. "Almost?"

"You're half right, and the half that's right is doing all the work, and the half that's wrong is—" she stood, slowly, pushing back from the desk, and her legs felt strange, too tall, too visible, "—is that you decided *for everyone*. Fifteen thousand dead under the covenant, yes. Counted, mourned, weighed. And how many have died since? How many towns fell off the edge of a fray because a border drifted and nobody noticed until the wells turned to saltwater? How many people stepped between realms and only one shoe landed? You didn't end shared suffering, Vey. You ended *shared accounting*. You made the suffering invisible instead of common, and invisible suffering is the specialty of people like me—people who keep their heads down, people who file the manifests, people who catalogue bottled starlight and never ask where the dark comes from." Her voice was rising now, and she let it. "You didn't kill the covenant because it was cruel. You killed it because its cruelty had witnesses. Keepers are witnesses. An oath is a witness. And you—" she pointed at the forged charter hanging framed above the hearth, gilt-edged, beloved, "—built an entire institution on making sure no one would ever check the original again."

Silence. Below them, the pens scratched on, hundreds of tiny sounds, the sound of a lie being maintained stroke by stroke.

Vey regarded her for a long moment, and then he nodded, slow, almost approving, and that frightened her more than rage would have.

"There she is," he murmured. "There's the keeper. You know what strikes me, Ines? Twenty years I've watched you in my green light, filing and cataloguing and making yourself small, and I thought—there's a woman practicing invisibility like a discipline, like a vow. And I approved, because invisible people don't check floors. But it was never cowardice, was it? It was training. Your mother trained you the only way left—by example, by absence. She taught you what watching costs, so you'd learn to watch anyway." He stepped aside, deliberately, clearing her line to the door. "So. You've heard the confession. You've seen the shape of me, and I've seen the shape of you, and we both know how this ends. You're going to say something noble about the Wellspring, and I'm going to have to decide whether to stop you."

"Sebastian and Maren," Ines said. "Where."

"The deep vault. Hallow's jurisdiction—she insisted, after the ford. She doesn't trust my cells anymore." He smiled thinly. "Neither should you. Understand what I offer and what I don't: I am not going to unlock doors for you. That would be sentiment, and sentiment is how covenants strangle realms. But I will tell you this, freely, because you've earned it and because I want it witnessed by the only honest reader I have left." He leaned forward, and the green lamp threw his shadow enormous across the forged charter on the wall. "The decay is accelerating tonight. My instruments have been screaming since sundown. Whatever Hallow intends for your wolf and your priestess, whatever trial or interrogation or *example*—it will not matter in a week, because in a week there may be no Ashfall to try them in. The realms are coming apart faster than my plan can catch them, faster than any plan can catch them, and I am an old man standing in front of a flood with a ledger."

"Then stand aside."

"I never stand anywhere else." For one naked moment he looked his age entirely—forty years of graves and margins and green light settling onto him at once. "Go down, Ines. Take the east stair; Hallow's people watch the west. And when you reach the Wellspring—if you reach it—remember that the covenant you're restoring will demand what it has always demanded. Everything shared. Including the suffering. Including yours." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Ask yourself whether fifteen thousand dead was truly worse than what you're about to sign."

Ines walked to the door. Her hand was on the latch when his voice caught her one last time, gentle as her mother's ghost:

"One more thing. The margin of my private ledger, every year—her name, and beneath it, the same sentence. Would you like to know what it says?"

She didn't turn around. "No."

"'She was right,'" Vey said anyway, to her back, to the closing door, to the dark stair. "'She was right, and it didn't save anyone.'"

The east stair was cold and black and smelled of salt, and Ines went down into it at a run, with his arithmetic clawing at her heels and her own name waiting somewhere below her, struck through or not yet written, in a vault where the lamps burned white and merciless and true.
