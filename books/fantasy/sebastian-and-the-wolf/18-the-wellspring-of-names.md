## 18. The Wellspring of Names

**Ines**

The vault beneath the Guild of Unmapped Realms had three locks, and Ines had spent her whole life learning them.

The first was iron and honest—a key she'd lifted from Vey's own belt while he slept in his chair, drunk on the certainty that no one would dare. The second was a riddle written in the guild's oldest cipher, the one they taught only to senior cartographers, and she had copied it into her private ledger at fifteen because she could not bear to leave a pattern unsolved. The third lock was not a lock at all but a warding drawn in salt and silver thread, and it recognized her before her fingers touched it—recognized the bloodline, the inheritance Sebastian had burned into her palm—and simply *opened*, like a door deciding she had always lived behind it.

Beyond it: a cell with no windows, lit by a single lamp burning starlight oil. And inside it, a wolf.

He rose when she came down the stairs, hackles high, and for one terrible heartbeat she saw him as Hallow must have seen him—as a thing to be caged, catalogued, *filed*. Then his eyes found hers, amber and human and unbearably familiar, and the wolf lay down flat against the stone floor, chin on his paws, watching her work the manacles at his foreleg with the patience of someone who had learned that hope was a currency you couldn't afford to spend quickly.

"Hold still," she whispered. "The shackles are keyed to the warding. If I break them wrong, they'll take the leg."

*Then take your time,* his eyes said. Wolves could not speak. But she was a cartographer; she read true shapes even in silence.

The first shackle gave with a sound like a struck bell. The second resisted, biting into fur gone gray at the edges where the silver had been working at him for two days, and Ines felt something cold and clarifying move through her—not rage exactly, but its settled, patient cousin. Vey had put these on him. Vey, who had once corrected her penmanship gently, who had called her *the steadiest hand in the guild*.

"Almost," she murmured, and the shackle fell away, and the wolf stood up into a man in a single grinding motion—bones reshuffling, fur receding, Sebastian kneeling on the cold floor in the ruins of his shirt, breathing hard, human from the waist up and wolf-eyed still.

"You came back," he said. His voice was rust. "I told you not to."

"I know." She offered her hand. "I've decided to stop doing what I'm told. It's remarkably late in life to develop the habit, but here we are."

He took her hand and let her pull him upright, and for a moment neither of them moved—the lamp hissing, the vault humming with old wards, the whole guild sleeping above them on foundations of lies. Then footsteps sounded on the stairs, and both of them turned, and Ines reached for the knife at her belt—

—and Maren stepped into the lamplight, hood back, hands raised, and behind her the stairwell door swung shut on the unconscious body of a guild turnkey.

"Before either of you says anything," Maren said, "yes, I betrayed you at the ford. Yes, I regret it. No, I didn't come here out of guilt—I came because I watched Sister Hallow interrogate Corvo for six hours, and I understood what restoration of vows actually means in her mouth." Her jaw tightened. "It means becoming her instrument again. I've been an instrument before. I'd rather be a person this time, even if it kills me."

Sebastian's gaze went to Ines. *Do you trust her?*

Ines thought about the fraying sky over Ashfall. About her mother's field notes, the ink gone brown with age: *Three keepers, or none.* About every map she had ever drawn honestly, and how honesty never once made the road shorter or safer.

"No," she said aloud. "But I trust what she's seen. And right now that's worth more." She looked at Maren. "You turned witness against Hallow?"

"I told the conclave everything. Vey's forgery. The monopoly. The arranged death of Amara Okafor." Maren's voice did not waver, though her hands did. "Half of them didn't believe me. The other half believed me and didn't care. So I stole the route to the Wellspring out of Hallow's own saddlebag, and here I am, selling my last relic: myself."

Ines unrolled the map across the cell floor—the covenant map, her mother's map, the one that had burned itself into her skin and then, mercifully, released her. Its lines were moving now, she realized. They hadn't done that before. Rivers crawled. Coastlines breathed. And along the borders between realms, the ink was *fraying*, threads pulling loose like a tapestry coming apart under its own weight.

"It's accelerating," Sebastian said quietly. He was staring at the map the way a man stares at a wound. "When I was cursed, the drift took years between visible changes. Now look at it."

Ines looked. Where the realm of Ashfall met the Glasswood, the border line had thinned to almost nothing. Where the Salt Reaches met the drowned country, whole districts of ink had simply vanished—cities, roads, names, gone as if they had never been drawn.

Cities flickering out of alignment. She had seen it from the ridge above Ashfall: a village that existed at dusk and did not exist by full dark, its lamps burning in a place that no longer agreed to hold them.

"How long?" she asked.

Maren consulted the stolen route notes. "At the current rate? Three days until the Wellspring itself falls outside every realm at once. After that, there's nothing to reforge the covenant *to*." She paused. "There's a faster way than the road. Through the Folded Pass. It's not on any chart because it isn't safe—it's a seam where two realms already overlap. If we cross while they're drifting, the seam will be—" she searched for the word, "—*opinionated*."

"Opinionated," Sebastian repeated.

"Realms have weather," Maren said. "This one has moods."

Ines rolled up the map and slung it across her back. Above them, faint through layers of stone, a bell began to ring—the alarm bell, the one that meant *intruder in the vaults*, which meant the turnkey had been found, which meant their three days had just become considerably fewer hours.

"Well," Ines said. "We've been opinionated our whole lives. Let's go meet one."

* * *

**Sebastian**

The Folded Pass was a place that could not decide what it was, and Sebastian loved it immediately, the way a cursed man loves anything that shares his condition.

They ran through it by moonlight—or by what passed for moonlight here, a light that came from no source and shifted color like a bruise healing. On his left, the walls were granite veined with quartz. On his right, the same walls were white coral crusted with salt, and the two versions existed simultaneously, arguing, and where his boots fell the ground chose one truth or the other at random. Twice the path ahead split into two corridors occupying the same space, and twice Ines closed her eyes, laid her scarred palm flat against the air, and said, "This way," with such quiet authority that the seam obeyed her.

The wolf in him wanted to howl at it. The man kept the wolf leashed. Barely.

Behind them—far behind, but not far enough—hounds bayed. Not guild hounds. Worse: *gap-hounds*, things that lived in the spaces between realms and had grown fat and bold as those spaces widened. Hallow had loosed them, or the fraying had, and it hardly mattered which. What mattered was the sound, which was less a bark than an absence shaped like hunger.

"They're gaining," Maren said. She ran well for a disgraced priestess, her stolen relics rattling in her pack like a profane choir. "Of course they're gaining. Everything gains on us. That's been the entire journey."

"Then we stop being chased," Sebastian said, "and start being somewhere else."

He felt it before he saw it—the moon climbing toward its rise, dragging his curse up through his marrow like a hook through deep water. Two days since the shackles. He'd forgotten, in the vault, in the relief of her hands on the silver, that the curse had not lifted with his freedom. It never forgot him. It was forgetting-proof, unlike him.

"Ines." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "The moon."

She turned, running backward for three strides, and he watched her do the arithmetic—moonrise in minutes, the seam too narrow to fight through in wolf-form without tearing it wider, the hounds closing—and watched her refuse to flinch.

"The alcove," she said. "Twenty paces. We hold there until you change, then we run as three-and-a-wolf."

"I could hurt you."

"You could," she agreed, and there was no fear in it, only the flat honesty he had come to build his days on. "You haven't yet. Instinct isn't destiny, Sebastian. I have it on excellent authority." She tapped her own chest, where her mother's name used to live, where her own name had been written years before she agreed to anything.

The alcove was a hollow in the coral-granite wall, barely wide enough for the three of them. He wedged himself into the deepest corner as the moon crested whatever passed for a horizon in this undecided country, and the curse took him the way it always did—not painfully anymore, which was somehow worse, pain would at least have been *his*—and the world narrowed to scent and sound and the drum of his own heart.

The hounds arrived ninety seconds later.

There were five of them, pouring around the bend of the pass like spilled ink given ambition, and the wolf rose to meet them with a sound that was half snarl and half grief. He was aware, distantly, of Maren shouting words that were probably prayers and probably blasphemies, of Ines drawing the folded-space trick from her ledgers—folding the corridor's mouth into a knot the hounds had to spend precious seconds unwalking—but mostly he was aware of the fight, teeth and weight and the terrible economy of violence, and then it was over, four hounds fled yelping into the seams and the fifth lying still, and the wolf stood panting in the bruise-light with blood in his mouth that was not his own.

And Ines walked toward him.

Not cautiously. Not with the calculated slowness people used around dangerous animals. She walked toward him the way she walked toward everything—with a ledger in her eyes, assessing, *recording*—and she pressed her palm flat against his chest, over the heart, and said, "Still you in there?"

The wolf leaned into her hand.

*Always you,* he thought, in the wordless language of the curse. *Even when I wasn't. Even when I couldn't remember why.*

"Good," she said, as if she'd heard. "Because the Wellspring is close. I can feel the map pulling." She glanced at the sky, where the bruise-light was beginning to flicker—actually flicker, like a lamp running out of oil. "And I think the realms are starting to notice us."

* * *

**Maren**

Maren had sold fake relics for eleven years, and she knew false holiness the way a counterfeiter knows currency: by its weight, its temperature, its smell. The Wellspring of Names, when they finally reached it in the gray hour before dawn, was the first genuinely holy thing she had encountered since the night she lost her vows.

It sat in a valley that belonged to no realm and all of them—a bowl of white stone at the center of which rose a spring that did not flow with water but with *light*, slow and silver and thick as honey, rising from some depth with no bottom and spilling over into channels that ran outward in every direction, each channel the exact width of a sentence. Names, Maren realized. The channels were names. Every river, every road, every city and person and wolf, all of it spoken out of this place once, long ago, and the spring had never stopped speaking.

Except now it was faltering. The light stuttered. Whole channels had gone dark, and where they'd gone dark the valley walls showed gaps—places where you could see through the world into a gray nothing that was not darkness but *absence*, the raw unwritten substance underneath everything.

"Oh," Maren said. Her voice came out very small. "Oh, I sold fake relics in a town built over *this*."

"You couldn't have known," Ines said.

"That's not the point." Maren's hands were shaking, and she let them. Eleven years of pretending had taught her exactly one honest skill: knowing when the performance had to end. "The point is I felt it. All that time in the hollow chapel, I felt something underneath the floor, like a word stuck in my throat, and I told myself it was indigestion. I *told myself*—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Vey broke the covenant so the guild could charge tolls between realms. And I broke mine for a warm meal and a locked door. We're the same sin at different prices."

"No," Sebastian said. He had changed back with the setting of the moon, and he stood at the edge of the spring with the covenant map open in his hands, and the map's frayed threads were reaching toward the light like roots toward water. "Vey broke his to own the gap. You broke yours because you were afraid. Fear can be reforged. Ownership just gets inherited." He looked up at her. "That's why you're here. That's why all three of us are here. Broken keepers. The covenant doesn't need perfect vessels, Maren. It needs willing ones."

"Willing," Maren repeated. "That's a strong word for people who were dragged here by prophecy and guilt."

"It's the only word it accepts," Ines said quietly. "I've read the original document. Under Vey's floor. The oath doesn't ask if you're worthy. It asks if you'll sign."

The spring stuttered again, harder this time, and the whole valley lurched—not physically, but *cartographically*, Maren realized; the geography itself hiccupped, the far rim of the bowl suddenly forty miles further away and then suddenly not, and in the space between, she glimpsed the city of Ashfall hanging in the sky like a reflection in disturbed water, its towers upside-down, its lamps burning in a place that had stopped agreeing to be real.

And beneath them, from the pass they had come through, the baying started again. Gap-hounds. Dozens. And behind the baying, faint but unmistakable, the measured tread of boots—Sister Hallow's enforcers, and behind them, riding something that made the gray absence ripple, a presence Maren recognized with a lurch of old terror.

Guildmaster Vey himself. Come to see his life's work finished.

"He can't be allowed to reach the spring," Sebastian said.

"He won't." Ines knelt at the water's edge—the light's edge—and unrolled the map fully, and the three missing names glowed on it like embers: two struck through, one waiting. Her own name, in her mother's handwriting, patient as seed. "But the oath takes time. It takes *memories*. And we are about to be very short on both."

She looked up—at Sebastian, at Maren—and Maren saw in her face the thing she had spent eleven years selling counterfeits of and had never once seen real: a person entirely resolved.

"Whatever happens in the next hour," Ines said, "stay beside me. All three of us sign, or none of us do. That's the covenant. That was always the covenant." She held out her hand, scarred palm up. "Keepers. One last time, together."

Maren took it. Her hand was shaking. It didn't matter.

Sebastian took the other side, and the spring surged toward their joined hands like a tide answering a moon, and the first hound cleared the valley rim.

The Wellspring opened its mouth to speak.
