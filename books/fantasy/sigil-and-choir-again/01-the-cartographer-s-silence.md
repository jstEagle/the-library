## 1. The Cartographer's Silence

The bell over the door of Montrose Maps & Curios had a crack in it, and Astrid liked it that way. A cracked bell rang wrong—flat, a little sour—and after fifteen years she had learned to trust the things that sounded broken. Broken things got overlooked.

She was inking the coastline of Vessamar when the wardens came.

She knew before she looked up. Not because she heard them—the cracked bell hadn't rung—but because her left forearm went cold, all at once, like a hand pressed flat against winter glass. The sigils under her skin did that sometimes. They were supposed to be dead. She had spent a decade and a half convincing them they were dead, starving them, wrapping them in routine until they forgot what they were for. But they still woke when power came near, the way old dogs lift their heads at footsteps that aren't for them.

Three wardens in grey-and-ash livery, boots wet from the morning fog off the Gullet. The one in front carried the ledger. The other two carried the long iron rods they called truth-sticks, though everyone in Greyhollow knew they didn't find truth. They found residue. They found the ghost-sweat of magic on doorframes and coin and skin.

"Inspection," said the lead warden. He was young. New inspections always came with young wardens; the older ones had learned that the lower city shops were graveyards of nothing, and asked to be posted elsewhere.

Astrid set down her pen with great care, as if the line she'd been drawing mattered more than anything else in the world. "Third time this year," she said. "You boys are thorough."

"Statute requires quarterly," he said, already moving along her shelves.

The shop was small and deliberately dull. Maps hung from every wall—not maps of Greyhollow, which would have been useful, but maps of places that did not exist. The Amber Coast of Lirreth. The Nine Valleys of Senn. The Sunken Duchy, rendered in loving detail with depth-soundings marked in fathoms no ship had ever sailed. Customers bought them for the pleasure of looking at somewhere else. In a city where looking too closely at your own streets could get you reported, imaginary geography was the only honest escape left.

Between the maps sat the charms. That was the dangerous part, or should have been. Little braided cords, river-stones wrapped in copper wire, dried lavender sewn into cloth stars. Sleep charms. Luck knots. Protection-for-travelers, the kind mothers pinned to children's coats. Every charm sold in Greyhollow had to be inert—blessed by nothing, powered by nothing, a superstition with a price tag. Astrid made sure hers were. She made each one by hand, and each one she made, she unmade in her head, folding away any stray intention before it could settle into the object like damp into plaster.

It was the most exhausting discipline she knew: to work with your hands while keeping your soul perfectly still.

The young warden picked up a luck knot and turned it over. His truth-stick hummed faintly against his hip—a sound like a struck wineglass, almost too high to hear. Astrid's cold arm ached.

"These do anything?" he asked.

"They do exactly what the tag says." She nodded at the little paper labels in her own careful cartographer's script: *For comfort only. Contains no enchantment.* "I'm required to write it. You're required to check it. We're both very law-abiding this morning."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. He was maybe twenty-two. He would have been seven when the Purge came through Greyhollow, when the pyres burned in Cordwainer's Square for eleven days and the smoke turned the gulls strange colors. He wouldn't remember the smell. Everyone older than thirty remembered the smell.

He ran the truth-stick along the shelf. The hum stayed flat.

Behind him, his partner drifted toward the back room, where the door stood half open on darkness and the smell of ink and old paper. Astrid felt her heartbeat change—not faster, but deeper, like a bell being struck somewhere below her ribs.

"The storeroom," she said lightly. "Mind the step. There's a box of unsold stock you're welcome to poke through, but I warn you it's mostly misprinted coastlines. I drew a bay where there should be a peninsula and now I can't look at it."

The second warden grunted and went in anyway. Of course he did. They always did.

* * *

Here is what Astrid kept in the storeroom, beneath three crates of misprinted maps and a false floorboard she had installed herself with a mallet and four hours of held breath:

A ledger bound in grey leather, its pages filled not with accounts but with sigils. Her sigils. The working-drawings of the Order of the Quiet Flame, copied out in her own hand during the last night of the Purge, when she was twenty-four years old and the only member of her order still breathing. Warding-circles. Memory-knots. The binding-script that let a bearer hold more than a human mind should hold. Eleven years of apprenticeship, reduced to forty pages, hidden under a floorboard in a map shop in the grayest quarter of a gray city.

And here is what she did not keep anywhere, because it could not be kept anywhere: the living versions of those same marks, sunk into her own skin like seeds into frozen ground. Along her forearms, up past her elbows, across her collarbones, down the ladder of her spine. Invisible unless she willed them visible, and she had not willed them visible in fifteen years. She had gone further than hiding them. She had practiced forgetting them, the way you practice forgetting a language, letting the grammar rot, letting the vocabulary fall out of use until only the shapes remained—shapes with no meaning attached, like letters from an alphabet nobody reads anymore.

Most days, it worked. Most days she was Astrid Montrose, widow of no one, daughter of no one notable, seller of harmless charms and beautiful lies about nonexistent coasts. Most days the sigils were just old scars that itched when it rained.

But the wardens' truth-sticks were iron drawn through cold-forged loops, and iron remembered how to ask questions, and her sigils—even starved, even sleeping—were still, at bottom, answers.

In the storeroom, the second warden's stick began to sing.

It was soft. Barely a note. He moved a crate aside and passed the rod over the floorboards and the note swelled slightly, the way a tuning fork will answer its twin across a quiet room. Astrid heard it from the front of the shop and her whole body went still in a way she hoped looked like boredom.

"That floor's got old iron nails in it," she called. "Whole building does. Slum construction—you could pull a horseshoe magnet out of any wall in the Gullet."

The humming stopped. A pause. Then the warden emerged, wiping dust on his trousers, looking faintly embarrassed, and the moment passed the way such moments always passed: narrowly, stupidly, on a technicality.

The young warden with the ledger stamped her inspection card. *No irregularities.* He said, "Quarterly again in spring," and she said, "I'll bake something," and he almost smiled, and then they were gone, and the cracked bell didn't ring because they didn't touch the door hard enough, and Astrid stood alone in her shop full of invented places and did not move for a long time.

* * *

That evening she pulled up the floorboard anyway.

Not to move the ledger—moving it was more dangerous than leaving it; things that changed position got noticed by the kind of eyes that watched for change. She lifted it to look, the way you visit a grave. Forty pages of her order's whole architecture, drawn in an ink she'd mixed with ash from the last pyre, because grief makes you sentimental about materials.

The Order of the Quiet Flame had believed that memory was the only true magic—that everything else, fire and flight and the raising of stone, was just memory wearing costumes. Remember a thing completely enough and you could make it real again. Forget a thing completely enough and it had never existed at all. The Purge had proven half of that doctrine true. Eleven days of burning, and afterward the city had forgotten so thoroughly, so willingly, that within five years people spoke of the mage-orders the way they spoke of fairy tales: fondly, vaguely, with a small shiver of embarrassment.

Astrid had survived by doing to herself what the city had done to its history. She had folded her memory of the working-sigils down to embers. She had built a life so small and so repetitive that there was no room in it for anything to catch.

She was good at it. That was the terrible thing. She was genuinely good at being invisible.

She sat on the storeroom floor with the ledger open on her knees and the lamp turned low, and she thought about the young warden's face when the truth-stick sang. Not suspicion. Curiosity. He'd looked at the floorboards the way she looked at coastlines that didn't exist—with the hunger of someone who suspected the world was bigger than his maps allowed.

Fifteen years ago, she had been that hungry. Then Cordwainer's Square, and the smoke, and Master Ellery going to the pyre without screaming because screaming would have been a use of power, of a kind, and the order did not spend itself cheaply.

She closed the ledger. She pressed her palm flat against the boards, over the space where it lived, a gesture that had become—she realized with a small, cold shock—almost devotional. Almost prayer.

"You're safe," she told the book, or the marks under her skin, or whatever part of her still listened. "We're safe. That's the whole job now. Safe and silent and small."

Her forearm, where the first sigil lay coiled like a sleeping adder, gave one slow throb of cold, and said nothing back.

* * *

Rain came in off the Gullet two nights later, the kind of horizontal autumn rain that made Greyhollow feel less like a city than like a ship taking on water. Astrid closed early. She was banking her lamp, counting the day's coins into their tin, when she heard the knock.

Not on the door. On the *wall.*

Three knocks, low, at the base of the shop's side wall—the wall that backed onto Fetter Lane, where no honest foot traffic came after dark. She stood motionless, coins forgotten. Her arm went cold all at once, colder than it had gone for the wardens, cold enough to burn, and underneath the cold something *turned over*, the way deep water turns over in autumn, bringing up things from the bottom.

Nobody knocked on walls. Nobody came to Fetter Lane in this weather except smugglers and worse, and neither of those knocked—they entered or they didn't.

The knock came again. Three times. And then—through the wall, through the rain, through fifteen years of deliberate silence—a voice. Small. A child's voice, cracked with cold, speaking words that Astrid's body recognized before her mind did, the way a body recognizes falling.

The First Tongue.

Four syllables. She couldn't have translated them if you'd paid her; she had buried the dictionary too deep. But her sigils translated them for her, waking one by one along her arms like lamps lit down a dark corridor, and what they said, in the oldest grammar of the oldest language, was:

*I carried it here. Open the door.*

Astrid Montrose stood in her shop of invented coastlines with her heart slamming and every buried mark on her body singing awake, and understood, with the calm clarity that arrives precisely when it is least welcome, that the life she had spent fifteen years building had just ended.

She went to the wall. She put her hand against the wet stone.

She opened the door.
