## 2. A Knock in the First Tongue

The rain came down on Greyhollow like a verdict.

Astrid heard it first as a change in the sound of the city—the usual drip and clatter of the lower quarter sharpening into something harder, drumming on slate roofs, gushing through the gutters that ran along Candlemaker's Row. By dusk the streets had emptied. Even the wardens, who patrolled in all weather out of principle, had retreated to their posts to dry their boots.

She counted it a mercy. Rain meant empty streets. Empty streets meant she could light a second candle without drawing attention to the glow in her window, and draw—just for herself, just for an hour—a coastline that had never existed. She'd been working on it for three nights: a bay shaped like a cupped hand, islands scattered across it like dropped coins, a river delta fanning out at the wrist. Places with no names yet. Places no warden could burn anyone for imagining.

She was shading the headland when the knock came.

Three knocks. Soft. Almost apologetic.

Astrid's pen stopped mid-stroke, leaving a blot of ink like a small wound on the paper. Nobody knocked on her door after dark. Her neighbors knew her as the charm-seller who kept odd hours and odder company, which is to say none. The wardens had already come this month—routine inspection, the third of the year—and Tam's people only sent word by daylight, through the baker's boy, never directly.

Three knocks again. Weaker this time.

She sat very still, listening past the rain. Beneath it, faint but unmistakable: breathing. Small, ragged breathing, the kind that comes from someone who has been running for a long time and doesn't know how to stop.

*Don't answer it,* said the voice of fifteen years' discipline. *Whatever it is, it isn't yours. You've survived this long by keeping your circle small and your door shut.*

But she was already moving. That was the thing about herself she'd never managed to kill, no matter how thoroughly the Purge had tried: some part of Astrid Montrose still believed that when someone knocked, you opened the door.

She unbarred it a hand's width, chain still fastened, one hand cupped around a candle stub.

A child stood on her step.

Small—seven years old, maybe eight, though it was hard to tell under the mud. Caked in it, head to foot, hair plastered flat against a thin face, eyes so wide and dark they seemed to hold all the night's water. The child wore no coat. Bare feet, split at the heels, bleeding thin pink threads into the puddle below the step. And shivering—not the ordinary shiver of cold, but something deeper, rhythmic, like a bell struck over and over somewhere inside the body.

"I'm closed," Astrid said. "Come back at market hours."

The child looked up at her. Didn't beg. Didn't cry. Just looked, with an expression far too old for the face wearing it—an expression Astrid recognized with a lurch, because she'd seen it in mirrors once, long ago. It was the look of someone deciding whether to trust a stranger with the only thing they had left.

Then the child spoke.

Not Greyhollow's gutter-tongue. Not the high speech of the council districts. Not any language sold or taught or permitted anywhere in the world Astrid had lived in for fifteen years.

The child spoke the First Tongue.

Two words. Only two. But they went through Astrid like a blade finding the gap in armor she'd forgotten she was wearing.

*Vaelith ora.*

*I see you. I see what you carry.*

The candle flame in her hand bent sideways, though there was no draft. And beneath her sleeves, beneath fifteen years of ash and habit and careful silence, the sigils woke.

She felt them the way you feel a limb returning from numbness—the hot flood of blood, the terrible sweetness of sensation. They started at her wrists and climbed, line by line, glyph by glyph, the old marks her order had inked into her skin at initiation, dormant since the night the Chapterhouse burned. They lit pale gold through her skin, visible even in the dark, even through wool, and Astrid stood frozen in her own doorway while her own body betrayed everything she had spent half a lifetime burying.

*No.* She slammed the door before she could think better of it, threw the bar, pressed her back against the wood, and slid down it until she was sitting on the floor with her knees drawn up, watching the gold light pulse beneath her forearms like veins of fire under ice.

*Vaelith ora.* Fifteen years since anyone had spoken those sounds aloud in her hearing. Fifteen years since the last time, when Maera had said them over the roar of the fire, pressing the order's last secret into Astrid's hands and telling her to run. The language didn't translate cleanly—it wasn't built for clean translation—but the meaning arrived whole, the way a blow arrives: *I see you. I see what you carry.*

And the sigils answering meant the words were true. The First Tongue didn't wake sleeping marks. It woke sleeping bearers.

Outside, the small ragged breathing continued. Then a sound worse than knocking: the soft collapse of a small body folding onto wet stone.

Astrid closed her eyes. *Walk away. Let the rain take care of it. Let the wardens find the child tomorrow and ask harmless questions of a homeless orphan, and let your name stay out of it, and live another year, and another—*

The sigils flared hotter, as if arguing.

"Damn you," she whispered—to the marks, to Maera, to whatever god arranged such jokes—and unbarred the door.

*

The child weighed almost nothing.

Astrid carried her inside—she thought of the child as *her* within moments, though she'd have denied it—and laid her on the cot by the hearth, stripping off the ruined clothes and wrapping her in the one blanket Astrid owned that wasn't full of map-drafts. The mud had gone past dirt into something like armor; it took three washings of the cloth to get the child's face clear. A girl, then. Thin as a winter branch. A bruise flowering on one shoulder, older than tonight. Old rope burns at both wrists.

Someone had tied this child. Recently. Someone had dragged her a long way.

Astrid worked in silence, feeding the fire, warming water, moving with the brisk efficiency of someone who had once been trained to triage the wounded and had never entirely lost the knack. The girl drifted in and out of sleep, and each time she surfaced, her eyes found Astrid first—found her, and held, and something in them eased, as if confirming a suspicion.

It was on the third waking that Astrid made her mistake. She reached to tuck the blanket higher, and the girl caught her wrist—small fingers, shockingly strong—and turned it over, exposing the forearm where the sigils had faded back to invisibility. The girl traced one line with a fingertip, following the curve of a glyph Astrid hadn't consciously thought about in a decade.

Then she spoke again, and this time it was more than two words.

The First Tongue poured out of her—low, unhurried, a river finding its old channel. Astrid understood perhaps one phrase in ten, and every phrase she understood landed like a stone in still water. *Ember. Carried. Waiting.* The girl's voice never rose above a murmur, and yet the hearthfire leaned toward her, flames bending like grass in wind. The candles straightened. Somewhere in the walls, the house itself seemed to settle, the way a dog settles when its master comes home.

And Astrid's sigils answered. Gold light rippled up both arms, across her collarbone, down her spine, a slow tide of recognition, and with it came memory—not summoned, not wanted, simply *arriving*, the way weather arrives. Maera's hands steadying hers over the first glyph she ever inked. The Chapterhouse library, ten thousand volumes of bound memory, smelling of cedar and smoke. The taste of consecrated wine at her swearing-in. All of it flooding back with the clarity of things that happened yesterday, because for fifteen years those memories had been packed in salt and now the salt was dissolving.

"Stop," Astrid breathed. "Stop, please—"

The girl stopped. Looked at her with those too-old eyes. Said one word, gently, in trade-tongue this time, her first word in any language Astrid could fully parse:

"Wren."

Her name. Or the name she offered. Astrid stared at her—at the mud still ghosting her hairline, the rope-burned wrists, the impossible calm—and felt the full shape of the trap close around her heart.

Because she knew what the girl was now. Or she knew enough. There was only one thing in the world that spoke the First Tongue without being taught it, only one inheritance that passed hand to hand through bloodlines and hiding places and graves. The order had guarded a legend of it: the last dragon, the one that hadn't died in the Sundering but had folded itself away into memory, waiting for a bearer. Every novice learned the story. Every novice learned the corollary, too, the one Maera used to deliver with grim humor:

*If the ember ever finds you, run. Because where the ember goes, the hunters follow.*

Wren watched her understanding arrive. Watched it the way you watch a storm front cross a valley.

"You can't stay," Astrid said aloud, uselessly, to a child who might not speak trade-tongue at all. "Do you understand me? Whatever you're carrying—they burned my entire order for less than a tenth of it. There are wardens on every corner and an Inquisitor whose name mothers use to frighten their children, and if they find you here, they won't just burn you. They'll burn everyone who ever bought a charm from me. Everyone who ever drew me water." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it. "I survived fifteen years by being no one. Do you understand? No one."

Wren regarded her steadily. Then she lifted one small, mud-stained hand and pressed it flat over her own chest—over the sternum, over the heart—and though her shirt was dry now, borrowed linen, Astrid saw it: a faint glow through the fabric, deep red-gold, pulsing in rhythm with the girl's heartbeat. Slow. Patient. Ancient.

An ember. Literally. Sleeping behind a child's ribs.

The sight should have terrified her. It did terrify her. But underneath the terror, in a place she'd boarded over and painted grey, something else stirred—something that had been starving for fifteen years without knowing its own hunger. Wonder. Raw, undisciplined, forbidden wonder, rising in her like water into a cracked foundation.

The sigils blazed. Wren's ember pulsed. For one moment the little room held a harmony between them, two instruments tuned to the same lost note, and Astrid felt—distinctly, unmistakably—the dragon's memory turn its attention toward her across whatever vast distance separated a sleeping mountain of mind from a rain-lashed room in Greyhollow's lowest quarter.

It felt like being read. Like being remembered by something older than remembering.

Astrid sat down hard on the floor beside the cot.

"All right," she said, to Wren, to the ember, to the sigils, to fifteen dead years. "All right. One night. One night, and then we figure out what in the world you are and who sent you, and then—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Then I don't know."

Wren's small hand found hers in the dark. The grip was surprisingly strong. And when Astrid looked up, the girl was watching her with an expression that needed no translation in any tongue: *I know what I am. I know what it costs. I chose your door anyway.*

Outside, the rain kept falling. And far across the city, in a tower above the Inquisitorial court, a woman who had once shared a novitiate bunk with Astrid Montrose woke from a dreamless sleep with the certain, sickening knowledge that something old had just opened its eyes in the lower quarter—and that she would need to find it before it learned to speak.

* * *

Astrid did not sleep.

She told herself she was keeping watch, and that was true enough, but watchfulness would have been satisfied with a chair by the window. Instead she sat on the floor beside the cot with her back against the bedframe, and every time Wren stirred, Astrid's whole body tightened like a snare being set.

The girl slept the way soldiers sleep: lightly, completely, ready. Twice she cried out in a language that was not a language—sounds that bent wrong in the air, that made the candle gutter and the shadows lean—and twice Astrid put a hand on her brow until the sounds subsided. Each touch left a faint warmth on Astrid's palm, and each warmth sank through skin into sigil into bone, and by the third hour Astrid had stopped pretending she couldn't feel the marks humming under her sleeves like bees in a wall.

*One night,* she kept telling herself. *In the morning I'll take her to Tam. Tam knows everyone. Tam will know what she is, who sent her, how to move her out of the city before the wardens—*

But even that plan tasted like a lie, because she already knew what Tam would say. Tam would look at the ember glowing through the child's shirt and start calculating freight costs.

Toward the smallest hour, Wren woke for good. No crying, no gasping—her eyes simply opened, dark and calm, and found Astrid in the dark as if darkness were no obstacle at all.

"Hungry," she said. Trade-tongue, accented strangely, each word placed with the care of someone handling borrowed tools.

Astrid fed her bread softened in broth, and the girl ate with the controlled deliberation of someone who had learned never to eat quickly, because eating quickly got it taken away. Astrid watched her do it and felt something in her chest tear quietly along an old seam.

"Who gave you those wrists?" Astrid asked, nodding at the rope burns.

Wren considered the question with the seriousness of a scholar. "Men," she said finally. "They were taking me somewhere. To sell." A pause. "The ember didn't like it."

"The—" Astrid's mouth went dry. "You know what you're carrying."

"It talks." Wren said it the way other children reported the weather. "Not words. More like... dreaming at me. It's been dreaming louder lately. Louder and louder, and then tonight it dreamed so loud I couldn't hear anything else, and my feet just walked." She looked up. "They walked here. I didn't pick this door. It picked me."

*The ember chose.* Astrid looked at the small figure wrapped in her blanket, at the glow beneath the linen rising and falling with her breath, and understood that she was not looking at a child who had found her. She was looking at an inheritance that had selected its next custodian—and, apparently, its next courier.

"Why me?" she asked, and hated how raw her voice came out. "There are a hundred doors in this quarter. Why mine?"

Wren tilted her head, listening to something Astrid couldn't hear. When she answered, she slipped back into the First Tongue, and this time Astrid caught the shape of it clearly, the way you catch a familiar face in a crowd after years:

*Because you are the last one who remembers how to listen.*

The sigils flared gold from wrist to shoulder, and outside, far off across the drowned rooftops of Greyhollow, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell, not the alarm bell, but the sharp triple-stroke of the wardens' mustering call, ringing through the rain at an hour when no honest muster had ever been called.

Astrid was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, snuffing candles, pulling the shutter closed. Through the slats she watched lantern-light bob along Candlemaker's Row—not the loose drift of a patrol but the tight formation of a search party, moving door to door.

Moving toward her street.

Behind her, Wren's ember pulsed once, bright and slow, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Like something ancient, waking, and glad.
