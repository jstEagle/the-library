## 3. What the Sigils Remember

The child slept like something drowned.

Astrid had laid her on the cot beneath the back-room window, stripped off the mud-crusted coat, worked the tangles out of black hair with a wooden comb and a patience she hadn't known she still possessed. Wren hadn't stirred once. Not when Astrid pressed a damp cloth to her forehead, not when she peeled back the small shirt to check for bruises—there were always bruises on children who arrived at doors in weather like this—and not when the fire in the grate guttered and needed feeding.

That was the thing. That was the thing that kept Astrid standing in the doorway with her arms crossed tight over her sternum, watching the child breathe.

Wren hadn't stirred when the fire guttered.

Fifteen years of careful living had taught Astrid to read rooms the way other people read letters. The hearth was banked low; the draft from the ill-fitting window cut across it at an angle; any ordinary fire would have spat and shrunk to embers by now. Instead it burned steady and tall, leaning—not toward the window, but toward the cot. Leaning toward the child like a dog leaning into its master's hand.

Astrid went to the window and shut the shutter hard enough to rattle the glass. The fire leaned anyway.

*Say nothing,* she told herself. *Feed her, warm her, put her out at dawn with bread and directions to the parish house. Whatever came out of her mouth tonight, whatever it lit up under your skin—it was exhaustion. It was suggestion. You are a charm-seller who sells charms that do nothing, and tomorrow you will sell more of them.*

She said this to herself the way she said all of her best lies: clearly, and with conviction, and without for one moment believing a word.

Her forearms itched.

She'd felt it first at the door—the moment the child's mouth opened and those words fell out, words in no language sold or spoken in Greyhollow, words Astrid's body recognized before her mind could flinch away. The sigils had answered. Under fifteen years of ordinary skin they had answered, waking in sequence like lamps being lit down a corridor, and for one vertiginous heartbeat she had stood in her own doorway seeing the faint gold tracery glow through her sleeve cuffs, bright as testimony, bright as a pyre.

Then dark again. Then just a tired woman with a wet child on her step and rain running off the eaves.

But the itching stayed. It sat along her forearms now like ants under cloth, and when she rolled her sleeves back—slowly, hating herself for doing it, doing it anyway—the marks were there in the lamplight, exactly where they had always been. Faded to almost nothing, the color of old scars, of watermarks on paper. But *arranged*. She could see it even now, even dulled: the way the lines wanted to run together, the way each small glyph strained toward the next like plants toward light.

They were awake. They were waiting.

Astrid pulled her sleeves down and made tea she didn't drink.

* * *

Toward the smallest hours, the child began to speak in her sleep.

Not the First Tongue—that would have been terrible enough, that language like struck flint, like the sound a mountain makes deep underground. No, this was worse, because it was ordinary. Small murmured fragments in a common dialect, the voice of a child narrating a dream: *the road, the road, don't let go of the road,* and then something about a woman, *she has your eyes, she has your—* and then a name that wasn't a name, a sound that slid through the ear without catching, and then silence so complete Astrid found she had been holding her breath.

*Don't let go of the road.* As if someone had carried her a long way. As if the carrying had cost something.

Astrid knelt beside the cot. Told herself she was checking for fever. Pressed two fingers to the child's wrist—pulse quick and light, a bird's pulse—and then, because there was no pretending anymore about what she was doing, because some part of her had already known since the doorway, since the words, since the fire leaning—she drew back the blanket and lifted the thin shirt over the small heart.

There was a mark on Wren's chest.

Not ink, not bruise, not birthmark. A mark like a coal seen through paper: a darkness at the center ringed with orange-gold, and it moved. Slowly, with a rhythm slightly out of step with the child's breathing, the edges of it pulsed—brightening, dimming, brightening—like breath taken by something much larger sleeping underneath. And around it, fine as spider silk, lines ran outward across the child's ribs and collarbones and down both arms, branching and branching, a whole hidden architecture of script too small and too old to read, covering her like frost covering a window, like roots holding a tree.

Astrid knew what she was looking at. That was the unbearable part. Fifteen years of forgetting had not been forgetting at all, only storage, and the knowledge came up out of her now whole and cold: a flame-mark. A living ember seated over the heart, grafted into the bearer's blood, growing its script through the body year by year like rings through wood. She had seen one before—once, from across a burning square, on the chest of a dying man the wardens had opened like a letter to find it.

They had burned him for three days. The ember had outlived the pyre.

And this one was in a child. In a mute child who had walked out of the rain speaking the First Tongue, whose fire leaned into her sleep, whose mark pulsed with the patience of a thing that had been traveling a very long time.

Astrid sat back on her heels. Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, the way you watch weather happen to someone else.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, the sum arriving unbidden after fifteen years of refusing to count: the flame-marks were carried. Passed hand to hand, bearer to bearer, down through the bloodlines of the old orders, hunted in every generation, always moving, always hiding. Which meant this child had been sent. Or fled. Or *followed* something—to this door. To the last door in Greyhollow behind which a sigil-bearer might still be living.

The ember hadn't landed here by accident. Embers didn't land. They were *carried*, and carriers chose—or were chosen—and somewhere out in the flooded dark of the lower city there was an answer to the question of how, and every possible answer ended the same way:

Someone had known. Someone had brought Wren to *her*.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin through the shutter seams. Astrid had not slept. She had spent the night in the chair across the room with her ledger open on her knee, making entries she immediately crossed out, and sometime past the deepest hour she had done the thing she swore she never did anymore.

She had gone down into the cellar.

Under the floor stones, under the false bottom of the salt barrel where the wardens' inspectors had looked twice and found nothing twice, lay a tin box. Inside the box: a folded map of a city that had stopped existing, drawn in her own hand in the last month before the Purge, when she'd still believed maps could hold things in place. And wrapped in oilcloth around the map, a strip of dried hide bearing the master-sigil of her order—her order's seal, the true one, the one that had been carved into the doorposts of the Chapterhouse of the Choir on the morning it burned.

She hadn't touched it in fifteen years. She took it upstairs now and set it on the table beside the sleeping child, and she sat with her hands flat on either side of it, not touching it, and waited to see what would happen.

Nothing happened for a long time. Rain. Fire. Breathing.

Then the child turned over in her sleep, and her arm swung out and hung down off the edge of the cot, small fingers loose and open, and the tips of those fingers came within a hand's width of the hide on the table.

The sigils woke.

All of them. Not the faint watermark tracery of the night before but full and blazing, gold-white light rising through Astrid's skin from wrists to elbows to shoulders, and the pain was not pain exactly but *memory*—that was the cruelty of it, that was the design of it, sigils were memory made flesh and when they woke they woke everything they held.

The Chapterhouse, whole. Bells in the morning. The smell of hot wax and cedar smoke. Master Ellery's voice saying the words of binding while forty novices stood in the round and the floor sang under their feet. Veyra—

Veyra, seventeen years old, laughing, mud to her knees, holding up a cracked sigil-stone like a trophy, saying *we're going to change everything, Astrid, we're going to make them listen*—

Veyra's face in the firelight of a different night, older, harder, lit from below by a burning building, watching the Chapterhouse fall with tears cutting clean lines through ash on her cheeks, saying nothing, doing nothing, *letting*—

"Stop," Astrid whispered.

The sigils didn't stop. Memory poured. The choir singing the morning rolls of the dead. The taste of ash on the wind for a week afterward. Master Ellery's hands on her shoulders in the last hour, pressing the last of his working into her skin, saying: *carry it, carry it, forget it well enough to live*—and she had. She had forgotten so well she'd built a whole life on top of the forgetting, a shop and a ledger and a kettle and a name, and now it was all coming up like floodwater through floorboards—

The child's fingers closed.

Just that. A sleeping child's hand curling shut near the edge of the table, nowhere near touching anything. And the blaze cut off like a snuffed lamp, and Astrid came back to herself bent double over the table, gasping, tears on her face she had no memory of crying, her forearms glowing faintly even now, fading, fading, sinking back down to watermarks.

On the cot, Wren opened her eyes.

They were dark eyes, nearly black, and they fixed on Astrid with an unhurried attention no child should own—looking, Astrid thought wildly, not at her but *through* her, reading her the way she had read the mark on the small chest. Reading the sigils. Reading what they remembered.

Wren's lips moved. No sound came. But Astrid heard it anyway, felt it arrive inside her skull in the First Tongue, one word, patient as the pulsing mark over the child's heart:

*Bear.*

"No," Astrid said aloud, and her voice cracked in the empty room. "No. I'm not. I buried it. I buried all of it. Whatever you're carrying, whoever sent you—you've come to the wrong door."

The child watched her. The fire leaned. The mark over her heart pulsed its slow, patient rhythm, brighter now than it had been in the night, and Astrid understood with sickening clarity that it had brightened because *her* sigils had woken—that the two things were connected, had always been connected, that the ember had come here seeking the last living sigil-work in Greyhollow the way a spark seeks tinder.

And tinder, once touched, does not get to decide whether it burns.

Astrid stood on legs that didn't feel like hers. She wrapped the hide-strip back in its oilcloth. She put it in the tin box, and she got as far as the top of the cellar stairs before she stopped, because downstairs the child had begun to hum—a low, wordless note, a note that matched exactly the frequency of the fire in the grate, and outside the window the rain had stopped, and in the silence Astrid could hear, very far off, at the edge of perception, bells.

Not church bells. The bells had a different timbre, a bronze-deep resonance she knew better than her own heartbeat, bells that had melted fifteen years ago in a fire that had consumed everyone who could have heard them.

The choir bells of the Chapterhouse of the Choir, ringing across a city where they no longer existed.

Ringing, she realized, in time with the pulsing of the mark over a sleeping child's heart.

Astrid put the box back under the floor stones, and the false stone over the box, and stood a long moment in the dark of her cellar with her palms pressed flat against the cold earth, listening to bells that could not be real, feeling the sigils itch beneath her sleeves like seeds splitting in spring.

Someone had sent this child to her door. Someone had known what slept under her skin. And if someone in Greyhollow still remembered enough to send an ember looking for a bearer, then someone else in Greyhollow—someone with wardens, someone with lists, someone with a pyre-crew on retainer—might remember too.

By nightfall, she told herself. By nightfall I'll put her out with bread and directions to the parish house.

It was already a lie. She could feel it settling into place, comfortable as an old scar: the shape of the choice she'd spent fifteen years avoiding, sitting down inside her chest like a guest who intended to stay.

Upstairs, the child slept on, and the ember over her heart burned steady, and waited.
