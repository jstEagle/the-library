## 4. Greyhollow's Long Law

The flare came at the ninth bell, and Inquisitor Veyra felt it in her teeth.

She was in her office above the Warden's Hall, working through a ledger of inspection reports—chimney soot tested for ash-taint, cellar doors checked for chalk-marks, forty-one names of suspected charm-sellers to be visited before month's end. Ordinary work. The slow, grinding machinery of the Long Law. She had written most of that law herself, clause by clause, in the years after the Purge, and she knew its rhythms the way a sailor knows weather. Nothing in Greyhollow surprised her anymore.

Then the flare.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. A pressure behind the eyes, a taste like struck flint on the tongue, a warmth that crawled up from the base of her spine and bloomed between her shoulder blades—the exact place where, twenty years ago, her novice sigils had been inked. She set down her pen very carefully, as though sudden movements might frighten it into happening again.

There was no mistaking it. She had spent fifteen years learning what magic felt like from the outside: the static hum of a ward being laid, the sweet-rot smell of a glamour, the way candles guttered wrong when someone nearby was carrying more than candlelight inside them. The wardens' instruments could detect residue, traces, evidence of work already done. But this—this had been *live*. Something kindled, or remembered how to kindle, somewhere in the lower city.

Veyra rose, went to the window. Below her, Greyhollow spread out in its tiers: the high city with its slate roofs and magistrate towers, the merchant quarter with its gas lamps just flickering on for evening, and below all of it the lower city, a sprawl of leaning tenements and flooded alleys where the tide crept up through old canals that no one had dredged since the Purge. Rain was coming in off the harbor, gray sheets of it, erasing the rooftops one by one.

Somewhere down there, in all that wet dark, something had burned.

* * *

The archive beneath the Warden's Hall held every record of the Purge: confessions, denunciations, execution ledgers, the charred inventory of the Sigil Order's chapterhouse. Veyra kept the keys herself. No one entered without her. Not because she treasured the records—she did not—but because she had learned, over fifteen years of reading them, that memory was the most dangerous substance in the city. More dangerous than any sigil. People forgot why they burned people. They forgot the screaming. They began to say things like *perhaps we were too harsh*, and *surely the order wasn't entirely corrupt*, and then some clever relic-dealer was selling pressed-flower charms in the market and no one thought to ask what was pressed inside them.

So she kept the archive cold and locked and herself the only key.

Tonight she took down the novice rolls. Her own name was there, near the bottom of the final intake: *Veyra Sarn, admitted aged eleven, sponsored by the Chapter of the Quiet Flame.* And two lines above it, in the same clerk's hand:

*Astrid Montrose, admitted aged eleven, sponsored by the Chapter of the Quiet Flame.*

They had arrived in the same cart, from the same river village, both of them fatherless or motherless or worse, both of them marked by the traveling proctors as sigil-sensitive. Astrid had been the loud one. That was the thing no one understood now, when they spoke of the last sigil-bearer as if she were a legend—Astrid Montrose had been *loud*. She laughed too much. She asked questions in class that made the older mages go quiet and thoughtful. She once re-inked Veyra's practice sigils overnight, correcting them, and left a note: *Your lines are afraid of themselves.* Veyra had been furious. Then she had studied the corrected lines until dawn, and never told anyone.

For six years they had shared a dormitory cell, a study desk, a single tin cup passed back and forth when one of them was sick. They had been novices together under Magister Corvane—no, that was later, he came after—and under old Magister Hale, who taught them that sigils were not power but *permission*: the world agreeing, briefly, to be legible. Astrid loved that. *Permission,* she'd said once, lying on the roof of the scriptorium with her hands behind her head. *Like the world is a locked door and we're the key asking nicely.*

And then the Purge.

Veyra stood in the cold archive with the roll book open and let herself remember it fully, once a year or so, the way you press on a bruise to check it's still there. The chapterhouse burning. The proctors dragging novices out by their hair. The pyres in the market square, seven of them, burning for three days because sigil-marked flesh would not take flame cleanly—it resisted, it *argued*—and the smell had drifted all the way to the harbor.

She remembered Astrid's face across the square. Astrid had escaped the chapterhouse—they never learned how—and stood in the crowd of the condemned's neighbors, hooded, watching. Watching Veyra, who had been pulled out of the line. Who had recanted. Who had knelt in the ash and sworn the Oath of Ash: *I renounce the sigil, I renounce the order, I renounce the lie that men may speak to the world and be answered.*

Astrid had watched her kneel. Had not looked away. Had not spat, or wept, or shouted. Just watched, with an expression Veyra had spent fifteen years failing to name, and then turned and walked into the smoke, and had not been seen since—not until rumor began, years later, of a charm-seller in Greyhollow's lower city who drew maps of places that didn't exist.

Mercy toward magic is betrayal of the burned.

That was the sentence Veyra had built the Long Law around, the load-bearing clause of her whole life. Seven pyres. Three days of burning. Forty-one names in her current ledger who might, might, *might* be doing small cruelties with borrowed power—and every one of them forgiven too easily by a city grown comfortable. Every year the statutes got harder to enforce. Every year the council murmured about proportionality, about modernity, about whether burning was still—she had heard the word used at the last session—*proportionate*.

They had never smelled it. That was the difference. They had never stood in the square for three days while human beings argued with fire.

* * *

She took a squad out at the tenth bell, four wardens and a sniffer-hound named Bracken, and they worked down through the merchant quarter into the lower city, where the rain had turned the cobbled lanes to streams and the gas lamps made yellow smears on the water. The flare's direction had been rough—southeast, near the canal district—but sigil-work faded fast in rain, and by the time they reached the tanners' row the trail was an hour cold at best.

Still, they knocked. That was the method. Door to door, ledger open, polite as tax collectors. *Any illness in the house? Any dreams? Any objects purchased recently from unknown sellers? Any words spoken that you did not understand yourself?*

That last question was Veyra's addition, in the law's third revision. Because the oldest reports from the Purge era spoke of a language the mages used among themselves, older than the order, older than the kingdom—the First Tongue, the scholars called it, the speech that bound dragons to memory, though by the end even the scholars doubted dragons had ever existed except as mage-fabrication. Speaking it aloud was a statutory offense of the first rank. No one had been charged under that clause in fifteen years. It existed in the law the way a sword exists on a wall: to be seen, not used.

At the fourth house—a chandler's widow, a stooped woman with wax-scalded hands—the widow said, unprompted, "Heard something odd tonight, Inquisitor. Down by the water. Thought it was a child crying, but—" She hesitated, wringing her hands. "It weren't crying. It was words. Words I didn't know, but my ears knew them somehow. Like hearing your mother's voice in a stranger's mouth."

Veyra kept her face still. "Which way?"

"Toward the old mapmaker's street." The widow frowned. "Though why anyone'd live down there past the flood-line—"

They were moving before she finished.

* * *

The old mapmaker's street dead-ended at the canal wall, five houses, three of them abandoned to the tide. Veyra stood in the rain at the mouth of the lane and felt it again—that faint residual warmth, the way you feel a fire has been in a room recently emptied. Not the flare itself. But its echo, soaked into the stones. Someone in one of these houses had either worked magic tonight or been near it.

"Ledger," she said, and the warden holding it read out the residents: a net-mender, a widow, and at the end of the lane, number five, a woman licensed for *charms, harmless; maps, decorative*.

Montrose.

Fifteen years of nothing. Fifteen years of reports that amounted to gossip—a woman who kept to herself, whose charms never failed but never did anything, whose maps showed coastlines no ship had ever found and which she sold, cheerfully, as fictions. Veyra had read those reports and filed them and told herself the same thing she told the council: if Montrose still carried her gift, she was wasting it on trinkets, which was its own kind of death. A mage who chose uselessness had already executed herself. There was no cruelty in leaving her to it.

But now the flare. Now the widow's words. Now a child's voice speaking a dead language in the rain, within fifty yards of the last sigil-bearer of the burned order.

"Knock," Veyra said.

The warden knocked. Rain hammered the lane. Lamplight moved behind the shuttered window of number five—a shadow crossing, quick, the shadow of someone moving toward the door and then stopping, deciding. Veyra waited, letting the silence do its work. Fear opened doors faster than fists.

Footsteps. The bolt.

The door opened a hand's width, on a chain, and warm lamplight fell out into the rain along with the smell of ink and tallow and—faint, so faint the hound stirred but did not bark—something else. Something like a room where a fire had been.

"Ma'am?" Astrid Montrose looked exactly like her reports: mid-thirties, ink-stained fingers, an ordinary face built for forgetting. Only her eyes were the same as the roof of the scriptorium, the same as the square full of smoke. Watchful. Patient. *Loud* underneath, still, the way a drowned bell is loud.

"Routine inspection," Veyra said. "Licensed seller of harmless charms. We're checking licenses in the canal district."

"You checked me in spring."

"Laws don't sleep."

"No," Astrid said. "They don't."

Veyra looked past her, into the room. One lamp. A worktable with maps half-finished, coastlines curling off the parchment into blankness. A chair by the fire. And beside the chair—her stomach dropped, slow and cold, like a stone lowered into a well—a second cup. A child's cup, chipped, with milk skin dried at the rim.

"You have a guest," Veyra said.

"My niece," Astrid said, without a beat's hesitation, and the lie was so smooth and so calm that Veyra knew it was rehearsed, or true, or both. "Visiting from upriver. Asleep."

"The lower city floods every spring," Veyra said. "Strange place to send a child."

"Family isn't sensible, ma'am."

Behind Veyra, Bracken whined and sat down hard, facing the house, ears flat—the hound's signal for residue, faint but present, recent but fading. Veyra weighed it. She could demand entry. She had the right; the Long Law gave her the right to open any door in Greyhollow, and the chain on this one was a technicality she could snap with a word. She could walk in, wake the child, question the child, search the maps for the ones that were too accurate, run her thumb along the shelves until she found the charm that wasn't harmless.

And then what? If Montrose was clean, she'd have humiliated a licensed citizen and earned another year of council murmuring about *excess*. If Montrose was guilty—if the flare was hers, if the child was something other than a niece—then she'd have flushed a fifteen-year hunt into the open with one warden squad and no backup, in a flooded lane, at night, and anything with that much old power in it would not come quietly.

Or—third option—the child was exactly what she appeared to be, and the flare had come from somewhere else entirely, and the real bearer was slipping away through the flooded streets while Veyra stood here counting teacups.

"License current," she said finally. "Charms inventoried quarterly?"

"Every quarter. You can check the ledger."

"I will." She stepped back into the rain. "Keep your door chained, mistress Montrose. It's an unsafe night. There are strangers about, saying strange things."

Something moved behind Astrid's eyes—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Good night, Inquisitor."

The door closed. The bolt slid home. The chain rattled, once, like a small defiant bell.

* * *

Back at the Hall, Veyra did not sleep. She sat with the novice rolls open under the lamp, her finger on the two names, and made herself think like the woman she had chosen to become instead of the woman she had been.

If the child spoke the First Tongue, the child was either trained—which was impossible, the language had died with the order—or *carrying* something. The old texts were clear on that much, whatever else they embellished: the First Tongue was not learned. It was borne. It lived in a speaker the way a fever lives in blood. The dragon-bond, the memory-binding—all of it nonsense, probably, but the core fact had survived every purge and every skeptical committee: sometimes a person was born speaking words no one taught them, and those words were old, and those words were *powerful*, and every such person in recorded history had ended the same way.

On a pyre, arguing with the flame.

Unless someone hid them. Unless someone fed them and cupped a hand over their light and lied to inquisitors with a steady voice about nieces from upriver.

Veyra closed the book. She went to the window. Dawn was a gray suggestion over the harbor, and the lower city was waking beneath it—net-menders and chandlers and charm-sellers, all the small stubborn lives of the drowned streets, none of them knowing that the Long Law had teeth again.

Mercy toward magic is betrayal of the burned. She repeated it like a prayer, and found, for the first time in fifteen years, that the prayer needed repeating twice before it held.

She wrote the order before the doubt finished cooling: double watch on the canal district, a discreet eye on the charm-seller at number five, and a summons to the relic-dealer Tam—licensed twice revoked, informant twice bought—to ask him who had brought a mud-caked child into the lower city, and what they had been paid, and who had paid them.

Because if a carrier existed, a trail existed. And trails ran backward through the dark to wherever the flame had started, and Veyra had spent her whole life learning one lesson above all others:

You do not put out a fire by watching the spark. You find the dry timber. You find everyone who ever touched it. And you burn the road behind it, clean, so that nothing is ever left to carry the light forward again.
