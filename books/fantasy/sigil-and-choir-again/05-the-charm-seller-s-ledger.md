## 5. The Charm Seller's Ledger

Tam Voss kept his shop where the map said there was nothing.

That was the point. Greyhollow's cartographers had given up on the Saltrow district a generation ago, after the river changed its mind and drowned half the streets, and so the wardens' lists had no entry for a crooked lane behind the tannery where a man sold things that had no business existing. Astrid had been coming to him for eleven years, first for charms to sell in her own stall—harmless ones, luck-knots and fever-salves with just enough of the old craft in them to work and not enough to burn—and later for something rarer: information.

She went at dusk, when the tannery stink was thickest and even the beggars stayed indoors. Wren she left at home with three locks turned and a bowl of stew that would be cold by the time she returned, and instructions written out twice because the child couldn't read yet—or wouldn't; Astrid hadn't decided which frightened her more—and a warning delivered in the only language they truly shared now, one word of the First Tongue pressed into the girl's palm like a coin: *stay*.

Wren had looked up at her with those grey eyes, ancient and seven years old at once, and answered in the same tongue. Not *yes*. Something else. A word Astrid's buried lessons almost surfaced to meet, then sank again, leaving only its shape: something like *until*, or *unless*.

She'd stood in her doorway for a long moment afterward, rain starting, telling herself it meant nothing. Telling herself a great many things lately.

* * *

The bell over Tam's door was made from a melted-down warden's badge, and it didn't ring so much as mutter. Inside, the shop smelled of lamp oil and old iron and the particular dust that gathers on things people died holding. Shelves climbed to the ceiling in unstable towers: candle-snuffers from burned chapels, rosaries with the sigils filed off, a child's spinning top carved with a protection rune someone had tried to gouge out and failed.

Tam sat behind his counter as always, a broad soft man with ink-stained fingers and the face of someone who had made peace with being forgettable. He was writing when she came in, and he didn't look up until the door was shut and bolted.

"Astrid Montrose." He set down his pen. "Three months since you darkened my ledger."

"I've been busy."

"So I hear." His eyes went past her shoulder, to the empty doorway, then back. "Word in the low streets is the charm woman took in a mud-child who talks funny. Word also says the charm woman hasn't smiled since."

"Your word travels fast."

"My word travels *carefully*." He rose, joints cracking, and poured two cups of barley tea from a pot that had seen better decades. "Sit. You have the look of a woman about to ask me something expensive."

She sat. She wrapped her hands around the cup because they wanted to shake and she'd spent fifteen years teaching them not to. "Wren came to my door four nights ago. In a storm. Alone. Barefoot, half-frozen, mud to the eyebrows. No one walks out of the marshes in that weather, Tam. Especially not a child who can't speak Common but can speak—" She stopped. Even here, even with the melted badge muttering overhead, some words were too heavy to lift. "You know what she speaks."

"I know what they say she speaks." Tam lowered himself back onto his stool. "And I know what I told the last person who asked me about it, which was: go home and plant turnips."

"Who was the last person?"

He drank his tea. He was very good at drinking tea in ways that were actually silence.

"Tam."

"You don't want to know," he said. "That's the honest answer. The expensive answer is different, and you already know what it costs, because you've paid it before, and you're going to pay it again right now, and we'll both pretend this is commerce instead of what it really is."

"What is it really?"

"Digging." He set down the cup. "You want to know how she got to your door. Which means you want to know where she came from. Which means you want the chain. And Astrid—" he used her name the way other men used warnings, "—chains have hooks at both ends."

"Show me."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he got up, crossed to the shelf behind him, and pulled down a ledger bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth at the corners. It was thick. Thicker than any record of sales should be, and she understood before he opened it that this was not a record of sales.

"This is my inheritance," Tam said. "My mother kept it before me, and her father before her. Three generations of Vosses selling the unsellable, and every relic that passes through our hands gets an entry. Where it came from. Who carried it. Where it went." He laid his palm flat on the cover. "Most entries are dull. A saint's fingerbone that's actually a pig's. A love philtre that works because the buyer believes. But some relics are *live*, Astrid. Some carry more than charm. And those get their own pages."

He opened the book to a ribbon-marked page near the end, and turned it around to face her.

At the top, in his own cramped hand, was a date from eleven days ago. Below it, a description: *Ember-carrier, female child, approx. 6–7 years. Delivered Saltrow marsh edge, night of the storm. Fee waived.*

Below that, a single line: *Carrier prior: Maren Ostwick, dyer, Ashlane. Deceased.*

Astrid read it twice. "Maren Ostwick." The name snagged somewhere under her ribs. "I knew that name. She bought fever-salve from me. Two years running. Quiet woman. Good hands."

"She had good hands because she needed them," Tam said. "Dye-work keeps sigil-craft supple, if you know how to look. She wasn't a bearer, exactly. More a... waystation. The flame passed through her on its way to somewhere safer, and your doorstep was somewhere safer, though the flame chose that, not me. I only moved the package the last mile."

"You brought her to me." Astrid's voice came out flat. "You put her on my doorstep."

"I put her at the marsh edge. What walked the rest of the way walked on its own legs, and I'd wager my whole shop it wasn't lost." He tapped the page. "I've known for years the ember would land somewhere in Greyhollow eventually. It always lands near a bearer-line. And whose line runs through this city deeper than anyone's?" He didn't say her name. He didn't have to. "I did the arithmetic fifteen years ago and I've been waiting ever since. Hated waiting. Hate myself a little for the fee-waived line, too, but a man has to keep his hand honest in his own book."

Astrid stared at the page. The tea had gone cold in her hands without her noticing. "Who was before Maren?"

Tam turned the page backward.

*Carrier prior: Odile Ferran, midwife, Rivergate. Deceased.* And below that, in smaller script: *Held the ember 4 years. Died of 'fever.' Wardens listed cause as lung-rot. Cause was lung-rot.*

Another page. Another name. A lamplighter in the Weft district, deceased, held it two years. A ferryman before him, deceased, held it nine months. A schoolteacher. A gravedigger. A nun of a chapel that no longer existed, and beneath her entry, in handwriting older than Tam's—a woman's hand, fine and fierce—entries that went back further still, names in a style of script Astrid recognized from documents she'd helped carry out of burning buildings fifteen years ago.

"How far back?" she asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

Tam turned pages. Slowly. Letting her see each one, because he was a careful man and he understood that horror needs time to arrive properly. Names. Dates. Occupations. Durations of custody. Causes of death, most of them official and all of them wrong. A cooper. A seamstress. A boy of nineteen who'd held the flame for six weeks before the river took him, and the river, Tam's mother had noted in the margin, *does not usually take boys who grew up on it*.

Page after page. Decade after decade.

"The Purge was thirty-eight years ago," Tam said quietly. "This chain starts four years before that. Forty-two carriers. Forty-one dead. Every one of them ordinary. Every one of them hidden. Every one of them found anyway." He closed the ledger gently, like shutting a coffin lid. "Do you understand what you're looking at? This isn't bad luck. Luck doesn't keep a ledger this neat. Someone has been hunting this flame for half a century, Astrid. Patiently. Methodically. Across borders, across regimes, through the Purge itself—which, I'll note, killed mages by the hundred but never once touched this chain until recently, because whoever hunts the ember doesn't care about mages. They care about *this*."

"And the forty-second carrier," Astrid said, "is sitting in my kitchen eating cold stew."

"The forty-second carrier is the first one to reach a true bearer since the order fell." Tam met her eyes. "Which means either the hunt failed for the first time in fifty years—or it succeeded, and the flame walked exactly where someone wanted it to walk."

The lamp guttered. Outside, rain worked at the roof tiles with patient fingers.

"Why show me this?" she asked. "You could have taken my coin and told me nothing. You've done less for more."

"Because of the margins." He reopened the ledger to the oldest section, the fierce woman's handwriting, and turned it toward her one final time. "Look at the notes. My grandmother wrote them. She tracked the chain for thirty years and she noticed what I'm going to tell you now, and then she stopped taking entries for live relics entirely, and she made my father swear, and he made me swear."

Astrid looked. Between the carrier entries, in the margins, small annotations in faded brown ink:

*Interval between deaths shortening.*
*Carriers held longer in early decades. Now weeks. Hunter learning faster than prey hides.*
*Last three carriers all routed toward lower Greyhollow. Coincidence noted. Coincidence doubted.*

"They're not just hunting it," Tam said. "They're herding it. Every death squeezes the flame toward a narrower country. Toward this city. Toward the bearer-lines. Toward—" he spread his hands, "—you. Fifty years of pressure funneling one small fire into one small room, and four nights ago, the room got a knock on its door."

Astrid thought of Wren's grey eyes. Of the word pressed into her palm that wasn't *yes*. *Until. Unless.* A child who hadn't wandered out of the marshes. A child who had arrived.

Or been delivered.

"The wardens can't be doing this," she said. "Veyra's statutes burn mages, sure, but this is something else. This is patience. This is *method*. The wardens kick doors; they don't run fifty-year chains across borders."

"No," Tam agreed. "They don't." He began putting the ledger away, sliding it back into its slot between a taxidermied owl and a jar of teeth. "Which leaves the question you're going to lose sleep over: if it isn't the wardens, then who in this broken world wants the last dragon's heart badly enough to kill forty-one people for it—and carefully enough to leave the wardens chasing their own tails the whole while?"

He bolted the door behind her when she left, and she heard him moving inside for a long time afterward, the sounds of a man barricading himself against a question he'd finally let out of its cage.

* * *

The rain had thickened while she was inside. Astrid walked home through it without hurrying, because hurrying drew eyes, and because her legs felt like borrowed things.

Forty-two carriers. She kept counting them as she walked, the way you press a bruise. Forty-one graves scattered across half a century, most of them unmarked, all of them mislabeled. Fever. Lung-rot. The river. Ordinary deaths stacked like sandbags around one extraordinary secret, and every one of those ordinary people had probably died thinking they'd been unlucky. That they'd caught the wrong sickness, taken the wrong road, trusted the wrong stranger. None of them had known they were links in a chain being reeled in by hands they'd never see.

And now the chain ended at her door.

She stopped under the eave of the tannery wall and let the rain hammer the hood of her cloak. Because there was a worse arithmetic underneath the first one, and she'd been avoiding it since Tam opened the ledger, and standing in the dark seemed like the place to finally do it.

If the hunt was real—if it was fifty years deep and still running—then it hadn't stopped four nights ago. It hadn't paused because the flame reached safety. Safety wasn't part of the design. The design was delivery. So either the hunters had lost the trail at her threshold—

—or they knew precisely where the flame slept, and were deciding what to do now that it had landed in the one pair of hands in Greyhollow that might actually know how to use it.

Either way, the quiet was over. Either way, the next move belonged to someone else, and it would come soon, and it would come through a door or a window or a warden's list, and she had maybe days. Maybe hours.

She thought of Veyra—not the Inquisitor, the statue of law the city had built out of her grief, but Sera, seventeen years old, laughing at her own terrible handwriting in the novices' scriptorium—and shoved the memory down where she kept all the others, in the flooded cellar where she stored the life she'd buried.

Then she straightened, and walked the last three streets fast, and climbed her stairs, and unlocked her three locks.

The apartment was warm. The stew bowl sat empty in the sink, washed clean—washed *carefully*, the way Maren Ostwick must have washed her dye-vats, the way forty-one careful dead people had lived their careful hidden lives. Wren sat cross-legged on the floor by the cold hearth, and she was drawing in the ash with one finger: lines, curves, a spiral that Astrid's skin recognized before her mind did, because the sigils along her forearms had woken beneath her sleeves and were answering, faintly, like bells heard through water.

Wren looked up. And in the First Tongue—the language with no future tense, the language that binds dragons to memory, the language that should have been beyond a child of seven—she said a sentence Astrid understood completely, and wished with everything she had that she didn't:

*"Something is coming up the hill. It is not weather."*

Outside, far off in the direction of the wardens' quarter, a bell began to ring.
