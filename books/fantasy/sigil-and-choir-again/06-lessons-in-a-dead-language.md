## 6. Lessons in a Dead Language

Wren dreamed in a language she couldn't speak awake.

In the dream there was no mud, no rain, no door of the sigil-woman's house. There was only warmth — a vast, slow warmth like lying against the flank of some enormous sleeping animal — and words moving through her like water through a reed. She didn't make the words. The words made themselves, and she was only the place they happened.

*Vael-ith-oren. Vael-ith-oren. Sura.*

She woke before dawn in the narrow bed under the eaves, her mouth still shaped around sounds that had no business in a child's mouth, and lay listening to the strange woman below her stir the fire.

The sigil-woman — Astrid, though Wren hadn't been told the name yet, only seen it scratched inside a locket the woman wore and thought she didn't show anyone — had given her soup and a blanket and three days of silence. Three days of watching. Wren knew what being watched felt like. She'd been watched her whole life, ever since the old ones who kept her died and the road people passed her along like a coin nobody wanted to spend.

But this watching was different. This watching was afraid.

Wren understood fear the way fish understood water. She lived in it. What she didn't understand — what she had never once understood — was why the thing inside her frightened people so much more than she did.

She was small for nine. She had a chipped tooth and a scar on her knee from falling off a cart two winters ago. She could not speak — not the way other people spoke, not the grey clatter of Greyhollow street-talk that she heard through windows all day long. Her voice had never worked right. But the other language worked fine. The other language had always worked fine, and it was getting louder.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Astrid sat her down at the table and put a slate in front of her. Chalk. A rag.

"We're going to try something," the woman said. She had grey eyes that moved too much, always checking the window, the door, the seams of the room. "I'm going to draw marks. If any of them mean anything to you — anything at all — you point. Can you do that?"

Wren nodded. Pointing was easy. Pointing was most of what she had.

Astrid drew a spiral. Then a line with three crossbars. Then something like a bird seen from above, wings folded. Then a circle with a dot in it, and when the chalk touched the dot, Wren's whole arm went hot from shoulder to fingertip.

She pointed. Then, because pointing wasn't enough, because the heat was climbing her throat like a swallowed coal, she opened her mouth and let it out:

"*Sura.*"

The candle on the table flared straight up, a finger of flame standing perfectly still, and every hair on Wren's arms rose, and Astrid went white as flour and knocked the chair over getting up.

"Stop," Astrid said. "Stop stop stop—"

Wren hadn't known she was doing anything. That was the worst part. The word left her and the world answered, and she stood between them like a wire between two poles, feeling everything pass through her and none of it belonging to her.

The candle guttered back down. Astrid stood with her hand pressed flat against her own chest, breathing hard, staring at Wren as if Wren were a door she'd nailed shut fifteen years ago and just heard creak open.

"*Sura* means hearth," Astrid said slowly, mostly to herself. "It means the first fire. The one you light so the dark has somewhere to stand back from." She looked at Wren. "Where did you learn that word?"

Wren shrugged. It was an honest answer. There was no place. There was no lesson. There was only the language, living in her the way blood lived in her, and no way to explain that with a shrug or anything else.

Astrid sat back down. She turned the slate over and drew nothing for a long time.

"You can't say it again," she said finally. "Not unless I ask. Not here. Not anywhere. Do you understand me?"

Wren did understand. That was the terrible joke of her life: everyone assumed that because she couldn't talk, she also couldn't understand, and she understood nearly everything. She understood that the word *sura* had made a candle stand up like a soldier. She understood that Astrid's hands were shaking. She understood that whatever lived in her mouth was older and larger than this whole city, maybe larger than the whole world, and that it had picked her — small, mute, chipped-toothed Wren — for reasons she would never be told.

She nodded anyway. Nodding made the grown-ups calm.

But that night, alone under the eaves, she whispered it once into the blanket, just to feel it again. *Sura.* And the embers in the grate below her floor sighed and brightened, and something far away — farther than the walls, farther than the city, farther than the mountains she'd only seen drawn on maps — something far away turned over in its sleep and noticed her.

* * *

The dreams got worse. Or better. Wren couldn't decide which word fit.

She dreamed of scales the size of doors. She dreamed of a sky the color of a bruise and cities burning in rows like candles on a birthday cake nobody wanted. She dreamed of a voice so deep it wasn't sound anymore, just pressure, just meaning arriving whole:

*I am not dead. I am folded. Folded is not ended. Folded is waiting.*

She woke crying without knowing why, and Astrid was already in the doorway with a lamp, because of course she was; the woman slept about as deeply as a cat on a drum.

"Again?" Astrid said.

Wren nodded.

"What did you see?"

How to answer? Wren held up her hands and made them into wings, then folded the wings closed, tucked them against her chest, and closed her eyes.

Astrid sat down hard on the edge of the bed. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and the lamp hissed, and rain ticked against the shutters.

"It folded itself away," Astrid whispered. "Fifteen years we thought — the histories say it died at Dragonrest. They sang about it dying. They built the whole Purge on the back of a dead dragon." Her laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It didn't die. It *hid*. And now it's dreaming through a child."

Wren tugged her sleeve. When Astrid looked down, Wren pointed at herself, then at her own heart, then made the folding gesture again, smaller this time, small enough to fit inside a girl's ribs.

"No," Astrid said. "No, that's not—" She stopped. Started again, gentler, the way you talk to something wild you don't want to spook. "What's in you isn't the dragon. It's too small. It's..." She pressed her lips together. "It's an ember. A coal carried out of a burned house. Someone's been passing it hand to hand for a very long time, and it landed in you."

Landed in you. As if Wren were a doorstep. As if she were weather.

But it didn't feel like being landed on. It felt like being *kept*. Like the ember had looked at all the hands it passed through and chosen hers, and Wren, who had never been chosen for anything except carrying buckets and staying quiet, found that she did not want to give it back.

That scared her worse than the dreams did.

* * *

Astrid began teaching her. Carefully, in fragments, the way you feed a fire you're afraid of.

Not the First Tongue itself — Astrid refused that absolutely, crossing her arms like a gate whenever Wren tried. Instead she taught the shapes. Sigils. She drew them on the slate, then on scraps of paper, then, when she thought Wren wasn't looking, on her own forearm with a needle and ink, and Wren saw that the marks on the woman's skin weren't decoration. They were sentences. Whole paragraphs of them, faded, half-scarred over, like writing someone had tried to erase and failed.

"Why?" Wren couldn't ask. But she pointed, one evening, at the inked forearm, and Astrid pulled her sleeve down fast.

"Because I used to be somebody else," she said. "Somebody who talked too much."

Wren tilted her head. *And now?*

"And now I sell charms that don't work and maps of places that don't exist, and I keep my head down, and that's how I've stayed alive while everyone I loved burned." She said it flatly, like a list read off a ledger. Then she caught herself, glanced at Wren, and softened by exactly one degree. "You should know what you're carrying the weight of. So. Lessons."

The lessons were strange. Astrid would hold up a sigil and Wren would feel the First Tongue rise in her throat like a bubble in deep water, and Astrid would say "*No*" before it broke the surface, and Wren would swallow it back down, and the swallowing hurt, like holding a sneeze made of lightning.

But sometimes Astrid wanted the word. Sometimes she needed it.

"That one," she said one night, tapping a mark like a river delta. "What does your language call it?"

"*Ith-maren,*" Wren said, careful, quiet, keeping her voice small the way you keep a match cupped in your hands. "*Ith-maren* means... remembering forward. Giving a memory to someone so it keeps living after you."

Astrid went very still.

"That's what a sigil *is*," she breathed. "That's what we — that's what the order *was*. We thought we invented it. We thought we were clever, binding memory into marks." She stared at her own arm. "We were copying. All of us, all those centuries — we were children scratching copies of copies, and the original was..."

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Wren finished it inside herself, in the language that needed no voice:

*The original was me. Was us. Was the one who folded.*

The lamp flames all leaned toward Wren at once, every wick in the room bending like grass in wind, and Astrid snuffed them with her bare fingers, fast, one-two-three, and stood in the dark breathing.

"Not here," she whispered. "Never here."

* * *

The trouble was that Wren couldn't always keep it down.

The language didn't ask permission. It surfaced the way floodwater surfaces, finding every low place in her. A bell ringing in the market square became, for half a second, the tolling of a temple whose name had been unwritten from every book. Bread rising in a neighbor's oven became the slow warm breath of something vast turning in its sleep. Once, hearing a dog bark three streets over, Wren briefly understood every dog in Greyhollow — their grievances, their territories, their ancient compact with humans going back to the first cold nights — and she put her head between her knees in the middle of the lane until it passed.

People noticed things. People noticed the girl who flinched at bells. People noticed that candles behaved oddly around her, that frost formed fern-patterns on windows she stood near even in summer. An old fishwife grabbed Astrid's wrist outside the chapel and hissed, "That child of yours has a look. You want to keep a look like that indoors, in this city."

Astrid kept her indoors after that.

Wren didn't mind, mostly. Indoors there were lessons, and slates, and Astrid's dry voice saying *again, slower*, and the astonishing discovery that when Wren pointed at a sigil and gave it its true name, the mark would glow faintly gold for a heartbeat — faintly, safely, a coal wrapped in ash — and Astrid would look at it the way starving people look through bakery windows.

But some nights the language came up whether she wanted it or not.

Those nights she dreamed of the folding. Always the folding. The great body curling inward, wing over wing over wing, scale by scale becoming something else — not flesh becoming stone, but flesh becoming *story*. Becoming memory so dense it had weight. She felt the moment of it in her teeth: the choice, deliberate and enormous, to stop being alive in the ordinary way and become instead a thing that waited inside other people's remembering.

*Why?* she asked the dream, the way you can ask things in dreams.

And the dream, patient as mountains, answered:

*Because the world was about to forget what it owed. Because fire that no one remembers becomes only fire. But fire that is remembered —*

The dream never finished. It never finished, and Wren woke each time with the unfinished sentence sitting in her chest like a stone with a warm center, and she lay in the dark understanding that she was not the keeper of the ember.

She was the *sentence*. The unfinished one. The part that came after *but fire that is remembered*—

And someday, someone would have to speak her whole.

* * *

One gray morning near the end of the week, Astrid wrapped her in a too-big coat and took her out — not to the market, but down, down through streets that got narrower and damper, to a shop that smelled of brass and old smoke. A man behind the counter had a face like a closed fist and eyes that missed nothing.

"This is Tam," Astrid said quietly. "He's going to help us find out how you got to my door. Who carried you. Who carried the—" she glanced at the shuttered window "—who carried you."

Tam looked at Wren for a long time. He didn't smile. Most adults didn't bother smiling at a mute child; they treated her like furniture that occasionally required feeding.

Then he said, softly, almost under his breath, a word.

Not Greyhollow talk. Not anything Wren had heard spoken aloud by a living mouth.

First Tongue. Badly pronounced, accent thick as mud, but real: *vael-ith-oren* — *the folded one waits*.

Every candle in the shop lit itself at once.

Tam blew them out with shaking hands, cursing in three languages, and Astrid gripped the counter and stared at him, and Wren stood between them with her heart hammering, thinking: *he knows the words but they don't listen to him. Why don't they listen to him?*

Because they weren't his. She understood that instantly, the way she understood everything the language touched. He'd learned them secondhand, stolen, memorized like a merchant memorizes prices. The words only truly listened to one person in the room, and the words had just announced her to every sensitive ear in Greyhollow like a struck bell.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a whistle sounded. Then another, answering it. Then a third, closer.

Astrid's head snapped toward the window. Whatever she saw in the street made the blood leave her face.

"Wren," she said, very calmly, taking her hand. "Time for the next lesson. It's called running."
