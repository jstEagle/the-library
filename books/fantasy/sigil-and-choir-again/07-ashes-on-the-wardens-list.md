## 7. Ashes on the Wardens' List

The knock came at the wrong hour, and that was how Astrid knew.

Tam knocked at dusk, never later, because Tam was a coward in all the practical ways and proud of it. He'd told her once, over a cup of watered wine he pretended was expensive, that cowardice was just foresight wearing a cheaper coat. So when three heavy fists struck her door long past midnight—rain hammering the shutters, Wren asleep under the good blanket on the cot—Astrid's body understood before her mind did. Her hands went cold. Her feet were already moving.

She crossed the shop floor without lighting a lamp. Fifteen years of habit had built a map of her own home into her soles: the loose plank, the display case with its harmless charms, the counter where she sold maps of coastlines no ship had ever found. She put her ear to the door.

Boots. More than one set. And beneath them, the wet creak of leather that wardens wore—their coats treated with ash-oil so that nothing clung to them, not rain, not blood, not prayers. She'd worn one herself, once. It had smelled like cold hearths.

"Open in the name of the Long Law." A voice like a ledger being closed. "By order of Inquisitor Veyra."

Her own name hadn't been spoken yet. That was the only mercy in the room.

Through the back, then. She moved to Wren, pressed a hand over the child's mouth before her eyes even opened—Wren woke silent, which was its own kind of terrible, a child who had learned early that sound was a debt collected violently—and lifted her. The girl weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and river water and something older underneath, an ember-sweetness that made Astrid's buried sigils itch along her forearms.

*Not now,* she told her skin. *Not yet.*

The rear window stuck; it always stuck. She wrenched it open with a sound like a bone setting, flinched, waited. The knocking continued, methodical, patient. Wardens loved patience. It was the one virtue their work allowed them.

She dropped Wren into the alley first, then followed, pulling the window down behind her until it caught with an inch of gap. Let them find the shop. Let them read her shelves of fake charms and her maps of nowhere. There was nothing there to hang her with—she had spent fifteen years making sure of it, sanding herself down grain by grain until what remained was a woman who sold trinkets and drew pretty lies for sailors.

Except for the ledger entry she'd sent Tam three days ago. Except for the question she'd asked him about the child. Except for everything.

She took Wren's hand and ran.

* * *

The undercity took them the way the sea takes a swimmer who knows the currents—familiar, indifferent, hungry.

Greyhollow had been built twice: once on the hill by people with money, and once below, in the drowned bones of the older city, where the river had been let in during the founding wars and never persuaded to leave. The smugglers' paths ran through it—waist-deep channels between sunken rooftops, rope lines strung across black water, stairs that rose out of the flood like the spines of buried animals. Astrid had mapped this place honestly, once, years ago. Then she'd burned the map, because a map of the undercity in the wrong hands was worth more than a confession.

Now she wished she'd kept it. Memory would have to serve.

"Stay on the line," she whispered to Wren. "Hand over hand. If you slip, don't scream. Sinking is quiet here."

Wren nodded, eyes enormous in the dark, and gripped the rope.

They moved through water the temperature of regret. Somewhere above, boots would be finding her shop, breaking her locks, reading her life in the lamplight they carried. Astrid tried to feel afraid and found instead that she felt awake—a horrible, electric wakefulness she hadn't permitted herself since she was twenty-two years old and the order's sanctum came down around her ears in fire.

That frightened her more than the wardens did.

At the third crossing, where the channel widened into a flooded square with a bell tower leaning out of it like a drunk against a wall, she heard them behind her. Lantern light bounced off the wet stone, distant but gaining. Four sets of boots at least. They'd found something. Of course they'd found something—Tam would have folded inside an hour, sweet cowardly Tam, whose foresight wore the cheapest coat of all.

"Wren." She crouched in the water, holding the girl's shoulders. "There's a grate ahead, under the arch. I need to see it, and I can't carry a lantern."

Wren looked at her. And Astrid saw the moment the child understood—not the words, but the shape beneath them. Wren raised one small hand and opened her fingers, and a light came up out of her palm. Not flame. Something gentler and far worse: a glow the color of the First Tongue sounded, if sound could be gold, warm and old and utterly unmistakable to anyone trained to see it. Anyone like Veyra. Like every warden who had ever passed the trials.

"No," Astrid hissed. "Not that. Never that."

But the light showed her the arch, and the arch showed her the grate—rusted shut, exactly as she remembered—and behind her the lantern-light swung into the flooded square, and there was no time to be careful anymore.

Fifteen years of care ended in four seconds.

She put her bare hand flat against the rusted iron and spoke the word she had not spoken since the Purge. Not the First Tongue—she didn't have that. Just a working word, a hinge-word, second-rank grammar from a dead curriculum: the word for *remember being open.*

The grate remembered. Rust fell away from it like dead skin, hinges groaning back through decades of corrosion to some younger self, and the gate swung wide with a shriek she felt in her teeth.

And her sigils woke.

They came up her arms like dawn coming up over water—line after line of pale fire rising beneath the skin, the old marks she'd bound with scar-tissue and denial, answering a call they'd waited fifteen years to answer. She gasped. It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part. It felt like a fist unclenching that she hadn't known was clenched. It felt like the first breath after drowning. Power moved through her, small and precise and *alive*, and every cell in her body said *yes, more, again*—

"Astrid!" Wren's voice, thin with fear—for her, not of her, which undid Astrid completely. "They're coming!"

She shoved Wren through the grate into the passage beyond, followed, and pulled the gate shut behind them. Then, hands shaking, she laid two fingers on the iron and spoke the closing-word, the lock-grammar, and the rust returned—not as it had been, but thicker, fused, welded by heat she hadn't consciously summoned. The metal glowed dull orange in the dark, cooling slowly into something no warden would pry open in less than an hour.

An hour bought with a beacon. The heat-signature of a forged lock would show in any warden's scrying-glass like a wound.

"Down," she breathed. "Fast."

They went down. The passage sloped into deeper water, then shallower, threading beneath the hill itself, through corridors the founders had cut and abandoned. Astrid counted turns by the old mnemonic rhymes—*left at the weeping stone, right where the ceiling drinks*—and marveled at herself even as she did it, at how much of her she'd actually kept. She had thought she'd buried the whole woman. But the knowledge had only gone dormant, coiled in her like the sigils themselves, waiting for one reason to move.

Or one person. She looked at Wren paddling grimly beside her, chin up, water to her ribs, refusing to complain because complaining cost air. One small mute child who had walked across half a country carrying a dragon's death in her chest and had chosen, out of everyone alive, Astrid Montrose's door.

"Why me?" she asked aloud, before she could stop herself. The echo ate the question.

Wren looked up. Her lips moved—no sound, never sound, though Astrid was beginning to understand the silence wasn't emptiness, it was *pressure*, a language too heavy for a child's throat—and her finger traced something on the surface of the water. Light followed the fingertip, faint gold, writing a character Astrid knew.

It was the sigil for *heir*. The one the choir had used, in the last days, when they argued about who would inherit the flame if the worst came.

"You don't know what you're saying," Astrid whispered. "You can't know."

Wren just looked at her with those too-old eyes, and the light sank back into the water, and somewhere above them a scrying-glass was filling with the orange glow of a forged lock, and an Inquisitor who knew Astrid's face better than anyone breathing was reading a name off a list.

* * *

They surfaced near the tannery district, in a drainage culvert that stank enough to make wardens wrinkle their noses, which was its own kind of defense. Dawn was a gray threat beyond the smokestacks. Astrid hauled Wren out onto the stones and knelt there a moment, dripping, shaking—from cold, she told herself, only cold.

Her arms still glowed faintly. She couldn't make it stop. The sigils lay just under the surface now, banked but not buried, like coals raked over but never doused. When she flexed her fingers, the lines brightened. When she exhaled slowly, deliberately, the way the order had taught novices to dampen their aura, they dimmed—but slower than they should have. Slower every time.

"It's you," she said quietly. "It was never gone. It was waiting for you."

Wren tilted her head, water dripping from her cropped hair, and pointed—at Astrid's arms, then at her own heart, then made a shape with both hands like a bowl held out. Offering. Or asking.

"I know what you're asking." Astrid laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through and became something else. "No. I don't know anything anymore. I thought I'd killed it in myself. I thought—I spent fifteen years building a person with no magic in her, and she lasted one night."

The truth of it landed as she said it, and she sat down hard on the wet stone. Because it hadn't felt like duty or desperation back there in the dark. It had felt like *coming home*, and home was the thing she'd sworn the Purge had burned past reclaiming. Every warden in Greyhollow was proof of what magic cost. Every statute Veyra had written was written in the ashes of people Astrid had loved. She knew the arithmetic. She'd recited it to herself for fifteen years like a rosary.

And her hands still wanted to burn.

"We need to keep moving," she said, standing, because standing was easier than thinking. "Daylight's the enemy now. I know a roof—old safehouse, probably compromised, definitely filthy. We'll lie low until dark, then—" Then what? Flee the city? Toward whom? Every road out of Greyhollow ran past a checkpoint, and her face would be on a list by midmorning, ink barely dry, and beside it—

Beside it, she knew with sick certainty, would be a description of a child. *Small. Dark-haired. Mute. Do not approach alone.*

Unless Veyra had already guessed the rest. Unless the ledger Tam kept had names in it going back further than Astrid dared imagine, a trail of carriers stretching back through the decades, each one found and burned while the flame slipped free and ran on. How many children had stood where Wren stood? How many doors?

She looked down at Wren, who was watching her with the patience of someone who has waited out worse silences.

"We're leaving the city," Astrid said. "Tonight. Before the net closes. There are roads the wardens don't watch because the wardens don't know they exist, and I—" she touched her own forearm, where the sigils pulsed like a second heartbeat, "—I remember all of them."

Wren smiled. It transformed her whole face, made her briefly, unbearably a child.

Then, from the direction of the tannery yard, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm-bell—that had a different voice, a panic-voice. This was the proclamation bell, rung only when the Inquisitor's office issued a public charge. Astrid had heard it eleven times in fifteen years. Eleven burnings.

The bell tolled nine times, and stopped.

Nine strokes meant a name posted. A hunt declared. She climbed to the lip of the culvert, risked one glance across the yard, and there it was: the notice-board by the tannery gate, already hung, a single sheet of paper lifting in the morning wind. Even at this distance she could read the header, because she had helped design the format, years ago, in another life:

*BY ORDER OF INQUISITOR VEYRA — CHARGES OF HIGH HERESY.*

And beneath it, two descriptions. One of a charm seller named Astrid Montrose.

One of a child.

Astrid dropped back below the wall, and her heart was doing something strange—not hammering but slowing, going deep and steady, the way it had gone in the flooded dark when the power rose. Because terror was a luxury and she'd spent hers. What was left was simpler and colder: a name on a list, a child at her side, and fifteen years of buried fire waking up in her blood, whispering that it had never really left at all.

"Get ready," she told Wren, taking the small cold hand. "We go at full dark. And Wren—whatever you see me do tonight, whatever I become to get us out—"

The child squeezed her fingers. Once. Hard.

"—that's the idea," Astrid finished, and turned toward the dying stars, and began, at last, to plan like a mage.
