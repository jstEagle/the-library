## 9. The Choir Beneath

Wren stopped walking three streets past the tannery and turned left without looking up.

Astrid had learned to read the child's silences the way other people read weather. There was the silence of hunger, which came with a particular set to the small shoulders. The silence of fear, which made Wren press close against her hip like a burr. And then there was this — a stillness so complete it seemed the girl had stopped being a body at all and become an ear.

"Wren." Astrid crouched, keeping one hand on the knife she'd bought from Tam along with the rope and the oilcloth bundle she hadn't opened yet. "We can't stop here. The wardens sweep the tannery district at dusk."

Wren's mouth moved. No sound came out — it never did, not in any tongue Astrid could speak — but the shapes were unmistakable now that Astrid's ear had been re-tuned by two weeks of listening. *Down.*

"Down where? There's nothing down there but drainage."

Wren took her hand. Her grip was fever-warm, and beneath the warmth Astrid felt the faint pulse of the ember over the child's heart, quicker than it should be, quick as a bird's. It had been quickening for days, ever since they'd crossed under the aqueduct out of Greyhollow proper, and Astrid had told herself it was excitement, or fear, or the stew Tam's cousin had fed them. She was very good at telling herself things. Fifteen years of practice.

The child pulled her toward a seam in the street where an old flood-gate stood rusted open, its iron etched with ward-marks worn nearly smooth. Astrid knew those marks. She had drawn their ancestors a thousand times in a classroom that no longer existed, on parchment that had burned, under the eye of a teacher whose name she refused to say aloud even in the privacy of her own skull.

"You don't know what's down there," Astrid said. "I don't know what's down there."

Wren looked up at her with the terrible patience of children who know exactly what they know. Her lips shaped two syllables of the First Tongue, soft as breath, and the ward-marks on the flood-gate lit faintly gold, like coals breathed on.

Astrid exhaled slowly through her nose. "Fine," she said. "But if it's a warden garrison, I'm blaming you entirely."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs should. That was the first wrongness. Greyhollow's undercity was a maze Astrid knew well enough — smugglers' runs, drowned cellars, the flooded chapel where Tam stored his best stock — but this stair descended past all of them, past the smell of river-silt, past the layer where stone gave way to older stone, black and seamless, worked by hands that predated the city's founding charters. The walls began to carry marks again: sigils, hundreds of them, layered like sediment. Some she recognized from her training. Many she didn't. All of them were dead — cold, unlit, abandoned mid-stroke as though their makers had been called away.

Or killed away.

"Wren." Her voice came back flat off the stone, too fast, as if the dark were shallow here when it wasn't. "How do you know this place?"

The child walked ahead with the certainty of someone following a sound only she could hear. Twice she paused at junctions where three tunnels branched, head cocked, and chose without hesitation. Astrid followed because the alternative was climbing back into a city where her name sat on a warden's list, and because — she admitted it only grudgingly, in the deepest cellar of herself — some part of her had been walking toward this place for fifteen years without knowing the address.

The air changed first. Warmth, then scent: rain on hot stone, cedar smoke, the green smell of crushed herbs. A smell Astrid had last breathed in the chapterhouse at Vell's Crossing, the night before it burned. Her knees went strange. She put a hand on the wall and the wall was warm, blood-warm, and beneath her palm she felt a rhythm. Slow. Enormous. Patient.

Like something breathing in its sleep.

"Oh," Astrid said quietly. "Oh, you clever, stubborn old thing."

Wren turned back to her, eyes shining in the dark, and pointed down.

* * *

The cavern opened like a held breath.

It was vast beyond reason — a hollow beneath the roots of the mountains, ribbed with columns of black stone that might have been carved or might have grown. And it was full of light. Not torchlight. Not the ember-glow of the flame-mark. This light came from everywhere and nowhere, a diffuse silver-gold luminescence, like moonlight filtered through honey, and it moved. It drifted in slow currents, gathering and dispersing, and as Astrid stepped onto the cavern floor she realized the currents were voices.

They resolved the way a chord resolves. One moment: shimmering, formless. The next: singing.

Not words at first. Just tone — dozens of tones braided together, rising and falling in patterns that made Astrid's sternum ache with recognition. Then, gently, the braid parted and she heard them individually. A woman's voice, low and dry, humming a counting-song. A man laughing mid-phrase, the laugh folding seamlessly back into melody. A child's voice, high and clear, carrying a line of the First Tongue that Astrid had learned at nine years old and never forgotten, never once, not through fifteen years of selling harmless charms and drawing maps of places that don't exist.

She knew these voices.

Not all of them. But some. Master Ellery, who had taught her the grammar of binding. Sister Maren, who'd smelled of lavender and burnt sugar. The novice Dannel, who'd been seventeen, who'd begged, who'd —

Astrid's legs gave out. She sat down hard on the warm stone and the choir sang around her and the tears came without permission, hot and silent, because the Purge had taken everything else but it had never managed to take the sound of them. She had carried their deaths for fifteen years. She had never once let herself remember their lives.

"They're here," she whispered. "They're all here."

Wren knelt beside her and pressed one small hand flat between Astrid's shoulder blades, right over the oldest of her dormant sigils, and the touch was like a key turning.

The choir changed.

The drifting light gathered, drew inward, condensed into shapes — not bodies, nothing so crude, but suggestions of presence, figures of luminance standing in loose ranks across the cavern floor. Hundreds of them. More than hundreds. Every mage the Purge had taken, every hedge-witch dragged from her kitchen, every scholar pulled from his library, every child too young to understand what the pyres were for. They had come here, Astrid understood suddenly, the way water comes home to the sea. When the order fell, when the flame went into hiding and the dragon folded itself into memory, the dying had poured themselves into that same memory. Bound to it. Kept by it.

Waiting.

One figure came forward. Tall, robed in light, and when it raised its head Astrid saw Master Ellery's face rendered in silver and gold, younger than death had left him, kind in the way he had been kind — which was to say, unsparingly.

*Bearer-heir,* the choir said, all of them at once, and the words did not arrive through her ears but directly behind her eyes, in the voice of memory itself. *Daughter of the Ninth Circle. Keeper of the buried hand. You have hidden long enough.*

"I hid because you told me to," Astrid said. Her voice cracked on it. "You — the ones who survived. *Hide it. Bury it. Live.* Those were the last orders anyone gave me."

*And you obeyed beautifully,* said another voice, and it was Maren's, warm and rueful, and Astrid made a sound that was half laugh and half wound. *Fifteen years of harmless charms. We watched. We sang you through the worst nights, though you couldn't hear us.*

"The worst nights," Astrid repeated. "You were watching while I—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There were years I wanted to follow you. Into whatever this is."

*We know,* said Ellery's light. *We grieved it. But grief is not a door we may open. Only the flame opens doors.*

The light shifted then, all of it, the whole congregation flowing toward the cavern's far end, and Astrid rose to her feet because something in her body insisted on standing. At the far end, half-drowned in shadow, lay what she had felt breathing since the stairwell: a shape vast beyond naming, scaled in darkness shot through with veins of dim gold, curled like a sleeping dog the size of a cathedral. The last dragon. Not dead. Folded. Its chest rose once every minute, maybe less, each breath a season.

And above its heart, suspended in the air like a drop of dawn refusing to fall, burned the flame.

Small. That was the horror of it and the mercy both. After everything — the carriers who had died passing it hand to hand, the decades of flight, the child who had carried it mute through the rain to a stranger's door — after all of it, the flame was the size of a candleflame. Guttering. Astrid could see the edges of it fraying, gold threads lifting off into the dark and dissolving before they reached the stone.

*It dims,* the choir said, and for the first time the harmony broke, voices falling out of alignment, grief making ragged music. *Memory cannot hold fire forever. The bearer must speak it whole, or it goes out, and everything it carries goes with it. Every name. Every truth the Purge buried. Every song. Gone as though it never was.*

"What does 'speak it whole' mean?" Astrid asked, though some part of her already knew, had known since the night the First Tongue lit her skin like sunrise. "What's the price?"

The choir was quiet. Even the dragon's slow breath seemed to pause.

*That answer belongs to the threshold,* Ellery's light said gently. *Not tonight. Tonight we ask only this—*

The ranks of the luminous dead parted, and the flame itself drifted forward, crossing the cavern floor toward her, trailing its fraying gold, and Wren made a small sound and grabbed Astrid's hand and Astrid squeezed back and did not run, though every survival instinct she'd honed for fifteen years screamed at her to run.

*Come home, Astrid Montrose,* the choir sang, and oh, it was beautiful, it was the chapterhouse at evening, it was lamplight and chalk dust and Maren's laugh and Ellery saying *yes, precisely so* when she got an answer right, it was belonging, it was everything she had amputated to survive, offered back to her whole. *Take up the flame. Become what you were always meant to be. We will teach you. We will stand beside you. You will never be invisible again. You will never be alone.*

The flame hung in the air before her, close enough to warm her face, and Astrid looked into it and saw — she would swear this later, would swear it on anything — she saw the world as it was meant to be. Cities unburned. Truth spoken plainly in market squares. Magic moving through the world like weather, ordinary and clean. Wren grown, fluent, free, teaching other children in the open air.

All of it hers, if she reached out. All of it one grasp away.

Her hand was already rising. She watched it rise the way one watches someone else's hand.

"Astrid." Wren's voice — her real voice, the one she almost never used, small and cracked and human. Not the First Tongue. Just a child's word, a child's fear. "Astrid, it's crying."

Astrid blinked. Looked closer at the flame, past the beauty, past the offered world.

The gold threads fraying off its edges weren't dissolving. They were falling. Like ash. Like feathers off a dying bird. And threaded through the choir's magnificent harmony, so deep beneath it she'd mistaken it for resonance, ran a single sustained note of pain — the dragon's note, the flame's note, the sound of something that had been holding on for a hundred years and was very tired, and wanted, more than anything, simply to be carried home.

Not to be wielded. To be *carried.*

Astrid lowered her hand.

"Not tonight," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I need to know the price first. All of it. Before I touch it, I need to know exactly what it costs."

The choir's light flickered. For just a moment, the harmony thinned, and beneath the welcome Astrid heard something else — something that sounded almost like sorrow, or apology, or warning.

*Then come to the threshold,* Ellery's light said. *And hear the arithmetic.*

Behind them, far above, muffled by a hundred feet of mountain stone, came a sound Astrid knew better than she knew her own heartbeat: the boom of a warden's ram against a flood-gate, and the thin, carrying cry of horns in the dark.

Veyra had found the door.
