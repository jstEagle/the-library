## 11. Old Friends, Drawn Blades

The market district on a salt-fair morning smelled of brine and frying onions and the particular desperation of people trying to sell things before the wardens came to tax them. Astrid moved through it with her cart rattling behind her, the same cart she'd pushed for fifteen years, its wheels worn into a rhythm she could follow blind.

She was selling maps today. That was the beauty of maps—nobody burned you for drawing coastlines that didn't exist. The wardens had learned to read charms in a charm-seller's stall; they had never once looked twice at ink.

"Coast of Serel," she told a fishwife, unrolling a sheet. "Warm water. No Greyhollow winters. No tithe."

"No such place," the fishwife said, but she was already counting coins.

"No such place," Astrid agreed, "which is why no one can arrest you for dreaming about it."

The woman laughed, paid, took her dream, and went. Astrid rolled the next map and felt the prickle at the back of her neck that fifteen years had taught her to trust above any other sense.

Wardens. Not the routine kind—the bored ones who shuffled through stalls kicking over jars—but the close kind, the quiet kind, moving through the crowd like eels under ice. Three of them. Not looking at stalls.

Looking at faces.

Her hands did not shake. She'd trained them out of that long ago. She began rolling up her inventory with the unhurried motions of a woman who intended to be here all day, and she calculated the exits: the fish-gutters' alley to the east, the covered stair down to the tannery row, the—

"Astrid."

Not a warden's voice. A voice from a lifetime ago, saying her name like it still had a right to.

Veyra stood at the mouth of the lane between the candle-seller and the rope-merchant, dressed not in warden grey but in the black-and-silver of the Inquisitor's office, which fit her the way a blade fits a sheath made for it. Fifteen years had put lines around her eyes and silver at her temples, but she stood exactly as she had stood at nineteen, at twenty-two, at the last morning of the old world: spine straight, chin level, watching Astrid as if Astrid were the only fixed point in a spinning room.

"Inquisitor," Astrid said. Loud enough for the nearby ears. Cold enough for the one ear that mattered.

"You look well." Veyra stepped closer. The crowd parted around her without knowing why. "Greyhollow agrees with you."

"It's a generous city."

"It's an obedient city." Veyra's gaze flicked to the cart, to the maps, to Astrid's hands. "I'd forgotten how much ink you go through."

*She knows something.* Or she suspected nothing and this was only cruelty, only the old game they used to play across the novices' common room, each one needling the other to see what would surface. Astrid couldn't tell anymore. That was the worst of it—that she had spent so many years being nobody that she'd lost the ability to read the person who had once known her better than anyone alive.

"If you're here to inspect my merchandise," Astrid said, "the maps are geographically impossible and morally harmless."

"I'm not here for your merchandise." Veyra lowered her voice, and the market noise seemed to lean away from them, giving them privacy neither of them wanted. "I'm here because three nights ago something flared in the lower city. Something old. And every record I keep—and I keep *all* of them, Astrid, you'd be astonished what I keep—says there is exactly one person left alive who could make that flare."

Astrid smiled the smile of the charm-seller, the small warm useless smile. "You flatter me. I draw pictures of places that don't exist."

"Yes," Veyra said softly. "You always were good at that. You drew me a map of the order's vaults once, when we were thirteen, entirely from memory, and it was perfect down to the third cellar. Do you remember?"

She remembered. She remembered everything, that was her curse—every lesson, every shared meal, every night the two of them had lain on the observatory roof naming constellations that didn't exist either, inventing their own sky because the real one wasn't good enough for them.

"I remember a girl who burned," Astrid said. "That's what I remember."

Something moved behind Veyra's eyes—fast, buried, gone. "Then you remember correctly. Come walk with me. Ten minutes. If you're innocent, ten minutes costs you nothing."

Every instinct said run. But running confirmed, and Veyra had three wardens in the crowd, and Astrid had a child hidden in a cellar on Tanner's Row with instructions to stay silent until moonrise. She could not lead the hunt home. So she walked.

* * *

They walked the length of the salt-fair and out along the seawall, where the wind off the Greywater made eavesdropping impossible and conversation difficult enough to hide in. Veyra said nothing for a long time. Below them, the tide worried at the pilings.

"Do you know what I do now?" Veyra asked finally. "People think I hunt mages. I don't. Mostly I file reports. I certify that the city is clean, year after year, and the council signs them, and everyone sleeps. I am a clerk of ash, Astrid. I count the dead and confirm they stay dead."

"That sounds restful."

"It is not." Veyra stopped at the seawall's end and turned to face the water rather than face her. "Three nights ago my instruments—yes, I have instruments, the statutes permit instruments that don't work by magic—registered a resonance pattern in the lower city. I have one reference sample for that pattern. One. It's from the night the Chantry of the Veiled Hand burned, and it was recorded by a novice named Sera Veyra, who held the recording-crystal while her best friend ran back inside to save the archive."

The wind pulled at Astrid's coat. She kept her eyes on the water.

"You went back in," Veyra said. "For books. For *paper*. And the roof came down, and they pulled you out half-dead, and I stood there holding a crystal full of the sound of our whole world ending, and I swore—" Her jaw tightened. "I swore that sound would never happen again. Whatever it cost. Whoever it cost."

"So you built the statutes."

"I built the statutes." Now she turned, and her eyes were the same grey they had always been, the grey of the sea before weather. "And they worked. Fifteen years, Astrid. Fifteen years without a single pyre. Do you understand what that means? Before the Purge, someone burned every month. Sometimes every week. Children, sometimes. I have their names memorized. All four hundred and eleven."

"I know," Astrid said quietly. "So do I."

"And now something is flaring in my clean city, and I find your name in Tam Ossa's ledger—not his real name, yours, your *real* name, written in a hand I would know anywhere because you used to write my essays for me when I was sick—and I have to ask myself what my oldest friend is doing connected to a relic-smuggler and a resonance signature that hasn't existed since the night everything burned."

"The ledger says my name?"

"The ledger says a great many things." Veyra's voice flattened. "It says a flame has been passed hand to hand for decades. It says the carriers keep dying. It says the thing is *here*, Astrid. In Greyhollow. In my city. And I am asking you—as the person who knew you before you had anything left to hide—to tell me where it is."

There it was. Laid on the seawall between them like a drawn blade.

"And if I tell you," Astrid said, "what happens to whoever carries it?"

"They come to me. They are protected. There will be no burning—I have ended burning, I will not restart it—"

"You'll cage them instead. Study them. Take the flame apart to see how it works, and then take the carrier apart, gently, humanely, with forms signed in triplicate."

"Yes," Veyra said simply. "If that's what it takes. Because the alternative is that it stays hidden, and grows, and one day it doesn't flare—it detonates—and everything I built burns with everyone else in it." She stepped closer. Close enough that Astrid could see the fine scar at her hairline, the one from the night of the fire, the one Astrid herself had bandaged with torn sleeve-cloth while the Chantry fell. "Tell me where it is. Please. I am begging you with the last piece of the girl I used to be, and then I will put that girl away forever and be only the Inquisitor, and I will find it without you, and it will be worse for everyone. Especially for whoever has it."

Especially for whoever has it.

Astrid thought of Wren in the cellar on Tanner's Row, small and silent, drawing sigils in spilled flour because the shapes comforted her, speaking words in her sleep that made the candles gutter. A child. They would call her a vessel. They would call her containment. They would use words with no blood in them while they drained whatever blood remained.

"You want a location," Astrid said.

"I want the truth."

"The truth." Astrid laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You want the truth? Here is some truth, Veyra. The night of the fire, I didn't go back for the archive. I went back for *you*. You'd gone in after the crystal, after the records, because you couldn't bear to let the order's memory burn—and I went in after you, and I found you unconscious under a beam, and I dragged you out, and then I went back a second time for the archive because I knew you'd never forgive yourself if it burned. That's the truth. I nearly died twice that night, and both times it was for you."

Veyra had gone very still. "That isn't what the report says."

"The report was written by men who needed a hero and got a heretic instead. You know how reports work. You write them now."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—the Inquisitor's armor cracked, and Astrid saw underneath it the nineteen-year-old who had cried into her shoulder on the observatory roof because her mother's letters had stopped coming, the girl who believed with her whole furious heart that memory was sacred and losing it was the only true death. Then the crack sealed. It always sealed. That was what grief did when you fed it law instead of mourning.

"Where is the flame, Astrid?"

"I don't have it."

"Don't lie to me."

"I'm not lying." And this, at least, was almost true—she didn't have it. Wren did. Wren *was*. Let Veyra turn every word over later and find no fracture in it. "I don't have it. I've never had it. If something flared in the lower city, it wasn't mine."

"Then you won't mind coming with me. Answering questions formally. Under seal."

"I mind very much." Astrid stepped back along the seawall, putting distance between them, her hand resting casually near the cart handle where a certain knife lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the maps. She hated that her hand knew it was there. She hated that Veyra's eyes flicked to it and understood. "Fifteen years ago I watched you choose, Veyra. You chose the statute over the sisterhood, the law over the living. I chose differently. We both got what we chose. Don't ask me to stand in your ashes with you now."

"The flame will kill this city."

"The flame might save it. You wouldn't know the difference anymore. You've spent so long guarding the grave you've forgotten there was ever anything alive in it."

Veyra's hand moved—not to a weapon, worse, to the signet at her belt, the seal of office. "Astrid Montrose. By the authority vested in the Office of Inquiry, I—"

"Don't." Astrid's voice dropped low, and beneath it, without her willing it, the old power stirred—a thread of command woven through the syllables, the First Tongue's plain cousin, the speech that made air listen. "Don't say the words. Because if you say them, I will stop you, and I have fifteen years of practice at stopping, and one of us will not walk away from this seawall, and it will break something in me that I need intact. Walk away, Veyra. For tonight. For the four hundred and eleven names. Give us both one more night of being clerks."

The wind screamed past them. Veyra's fingers rested on the signet. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"You were the best of us," she whispered. "Do you know that? When they list the fallen, in my head, privately, you're first on the list. Astrid Montrose, died in the fire, age twenty-six. I mourned you. I built a whole life on top of your grave. And if you make me dig it up—if you make me learn that you lived, that you hid, that you let me grieve while you sold *charms*—" Her breath shuddered. "I don't know what I'll do. I genuinely don't know. That should frighten you more than my wardens do."

"It frightens me more than you'll ever know," Astrid said.

Veyra closed her eyes. Opened them. Took her hand off the signet.

"One night," she said. "One night, Astrid. At dawn I file the resonance report whether you've helped me or not, and after that I am hunting you with everything the office has, and I will not stop, and I will not be merciful, because mercy toward magic is betrayal of everyone who burned. You taught me that. On this wall, when we were girls. You said *some things matter more than love*."

"I was wrong," Astrid said. "About so many things."

She picked up the cart handles and walked, and did not run, and every step was a war against running. Behind her, Veyra stood at the seawall's end until the crowd swallowed Astrid whole.

* * *

Tanner's Row smelled of lye and hides, and the cellar door opened to Astrid's knock in the pattern they'd agreed: two, pause, three, pause, one. Wren's face appeared in the gap, pale, flour still dusting her sleeves.

Astrid got the door shut behind them and bolted it and stood with her forehead against the wood, breathing.

Wren touched her arm. *What happened,* the gesture said.

"She knows I'm alive." Astrid slid down the door until she was sitting on the cold stone, and Wren folded down beside her, knees to chest, the way children sit when the world gets too tall. "She knows my name is in Tam's ledger. Tomorrow she files the report, and after that there will be wardens at every gate and a price on both our heads, and we cannot stay in Greyhollow another day."

Wren was quiet. Then, slowly, she raised her hands and spoke—not in gesture but aloud, in the First Tongue, two soft syllables that made the candle flames bend toward her like flowers toward sun.

*I know,* the syllables meant. *It was always going to end this way.*

"How could you possibly know that?"

Wren pointed at the floor. Beneath them, beneath the city, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something vast shifted in its sleep—and far away, in the mountains north of Greyhollow, Astrid felt the echo of it answer, felt the choir stir, felt the road open ahead of them like a wound.

North. Dragonrest. There was nowhere else.

"We leave at moonrise," Astrid said. "Pack nothing you'd cry over losing."

Wren nodded—and then paused, head tilting, listening to something Astrid couldn't hear. Her eyes went wide.

"What is it? What did it say?"

Wren's hands trembled as she answered, and her voice, when it came, was not entirely her own:

*"The choir says the Inquisitor is already riding north."*
