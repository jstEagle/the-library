## 12. The Relic Road

Tam's shop smelled of tallow and old iron, and it had never smelled like fear before.

Astrid stood at the counter while he worked the back of the ledger with a knife, prying up the false bottom where he kept the things he'd deny owning if anyone asked. Wren sat on a crate of smuggled candlewax, swinging her legs, watching everything with those too-old eyes. The child hadn't spoken since the raid on the undercity—not in any tongue—but her hand kept drifting to her chest, to the ember-mark beneath her shirt, as if checking that it still burned.

"It's not a road," Tam said, not looking up. "I want you to understand that first. People hear *road* and they think ruts and milestones and an inn every twenty miles. This is a wound in the country that smugglers have been crawling through for two hundred years, and half of them never came out."

"Then why do you know the way?"

"Because I'm the one who sells the maps." He finally looked up, and his face was older than it had been three days ago. "That's the joke of it, Astrid. You sell maps of places that don't exist. I sell maps of places that do, and nobody's supposed to find them."

He slid a sheaf of oilcloth-wrapped papers across the counter. Astrid unrolled one. Ink on waxed hide, dense with notation—cistern depths, gate timings, which sections of the old aqueduct would bear a person's weight and which would drop them forty feet into black water.

"The Relic Road," Tam said. "Runs from the drowned chapel under Tanner's Row all the way up through the karst to the Dragonrest foothills. The wardens know fragments of it. They've sealed maybe a third. The remnant mages know other fragments. Between the two of them, there's maybe sixty miles nobody alive has walked end to end." He tapped the map. "You'll be the first. Congratulations."

"Tam." She kept her voice level. "Who else is looking for us?"

He was quiet a moment. Then he pulled his ledger around and opened it to a page near the back, and turned it so she could read.

Names. Dozens of names, in his cramped hand, each with dates. Not sales—a different kind of record. *Carried the flame: dead. Carried the flame: dead. Vanished, presumed dead.* Going back decades, in ink that changed color as the years changed hands, because different clerks had kept it before him.

"I told you about the trail," he said. "Dead carriers, hand to hand. What I didn't tell you is that there are two kinds of hunters on that trail. The wardens burn what they find. But the others—" he tapped a name near the top, *Maren Ostwick, 3rd of Frostmoon* — "the others *collect*. There are people who've spent their whole lives waiting for the flame to surface again. Remnants of the old orders. They don't want it destroyed and they don't want it hidden. They want it *used*."

"For what?"

"For the war," Tam said simply. "The one the dragon ended. They think it was stolen from them. A victory taken away before it could be finished." He closed the ledger. "Astrid, when your order burned, the Purge didn't just kill mages. It left survivors. And some of those survivors decided that if the world was going to hate them anyway, they'd give it something to hate."

Wren made a small sound. Both of them looked over. The child had gone very still, one hand pressed flat against her chest, and her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely—the look Astrid had learned to dread, the look of someone listening to a voice too large for the room.

"What is it?" Astrid crouched beside her. "Wren. What do you hear?"

The child's mouth moved. No sound came out. Then, faintly, in a whisper that wasn't quite her own voice:

"*The road remembers feet.*"

Astrid's skin prickled. "What does that mean?"

Wren blinked, came back to herself, and shrugged the shrug of a child who has said something enormous and doesn't know it. But Tam had gone gray.

"That," he said, "is why you leave tonight."

* * *

They left through the drowned chapel, because Tam insisted the front doors were already being watched.

The chapel had flooded two centuries ago and never been pumped out; the nave lay under four feet of green-black water, and the smugglers had strung guide-ropes between the submerged pews. Astrid waded in to her thighs, Wren on her back with thin arms locked around her neck, both of them carrying nothing but the maps, a week of food, and the clothes they stood in. Behind them, Tam held a shuttered lantern and did not follow past the threshold.

"This is where I stop," he said. "Old knees. Older debts." He hesitated, then added, "There's a man at the far end. Karst-mouth waystation, third day in. Calls himself Brannoc. Give him my name and he'll feed you. Don't tell him what you're carrying."

"Why not?"

"Because everyone on this road is running from something or toward something, and the ones running toward something always want to know what you've got." Tam raised the lantern a fraction, so its light caught his face one last time. "Fifteen years I sold your charms, Astrid Montrose. Harmless little things. I always wondered what you were really hiding under that counter." A dry almost-smile. "Now I know, and I wish to God I didn't. Go."

She went.

The water deepened, then shallowed, then gave way to a passage where the ceiling came down low enough that she had to stoop, Wren sliding off her back to walk clinging to her hand. The dark was total except for the single hooded lamp Tam had pressed into her arms. Somewhere behind them, faintly, came the sound of boots on stone—wardens in the upper streets, sweeping Tanner's Row, finding nothing but a shuttered shop and a dealer with a flawless alibi.

For now.

"Wren," Astrid whispered. "If I say run, you run. If I say hide, you get flat against the wall and don't breathe loud. Can you do that?"

The child nodded against her arm. Then, after a moment, in the First Tongue, soft as water: "*Don't be afraid of the dark parts. The dark parts are where the remembering lives.*"

"I'm not afraid of the dark," Astrid said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and neither of them said anything more about it.

* * *

The second day, the road stopped being a tunnel and started being a country.

The karst opened into caverns vast enough to have weather—mist that drifted and thinned, air that moved with something like wind, ceilings lost in darkness overhead where water dripped with the patience of centuries. The smugglers' marks appeared on the walls: chalk arrows, knotted cords, niches holding stubs of candles for those who came after. Astrid navigated by Tam's maps and by Wren's hand tightening or loosening on hers, because the child always seemed to know, a half-second before Astrid did, which fork was wrong.

At noon—or what she decided was noon, down here where noon meant nothing—they rested in a gallery where someone had once built a shrine. Old wax, old ash, and carved into the wall, in a script Astrid recognized with a lurch: sigil-work. Her order's work. Fifteen years dead, and here someone had scratched a protective circle into living rock, hoping it would hold.

It had. That was the strange part. Faint, starved, but holding—a thread of the old power still woven into the stone, keeping the gallery dry, keeping the dark honest. Astrid sat looking at it for a long time.

"You could fix it," Wren said suddenly, in the common tongue, startling her. The child was watching the circle too. "Make it bright again."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because bright things get seen." Astrid pulled the map roll from her pack and pretended to study it. "Down here, being seen is dying."

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. "The lady in the fire didn't hide."

Astrid's hands went still on the map. "What lady?"

"The one who sings under Greyhollow. In the choir place." Wren touched her chest. "She says hiding is how the flame got small. She says—" the child frowned, translating something vast into a child's words — "she says a kept thing isn't a saved thing."

"And what do you say?"

Wren looked at her with those too-old eyes. "I say I don't want to be kept."

Astrid rolled up the map. She didn't answer. There was no answer that wasn't either a lie or a confession.

* * *

They reached the Karst-mouth waystation on the third day, and Brannoc was dead.

Not long. Hours, maybe. He lay across the doorway of his own station with a warden's crossbow bolt in his throat, and the station behind him had been searched with the thoroughness of people who knew exactly what they were looking for and hadn't found it. Supplies scattered. Floorboards pried up. A cold firepit.

Astrid drew Wren back into the shadow of the passage and stood there breathing, doing the arithmetic. Wardens, this deep? Veyra's reach shouldn't extend past the city seals. Unless—

Unless Veyra herself had broken her own statutes to come out here. Unless the Inquisitor wanted this done quietly, off the books, before the council could ask questions.

Or unless it wasn't wardens at all, and someone wanted it to look like wardens.

"Stay behind me," she whispered, and stepped over the dead man, and went in.

The station was a single room cut into the karst: bunks, a stove, racks of dried food, a rain-cistern. Someone had lived here a long time; there were carvings on the wall, tallies and names and one line repeated over and over in different hands, going back generations:

*The road remembers feet.*

The same phrase Wren had spoken in the drowned chapel. Astrid stared at it, cold spreading through her chest. The smugglers' proverb. An old one. Or—an old *warning*. Words that meant something more than travelers had ever intended, passed down until they'd worn smooth, waiting for someone who could hear what was underneath.

Wren touched the carving. Her fingers traced the letters, and beneath Astrid's own skin, the dormant sigils stirred—warm, attentive, like a dog lifting its head.

"There's a door," Wren said.

"What?"

"Behind the stove. There's a door that isn't a door." The child was already moving, pulling open the cold stove, reaching into the flue. Stone grated. A section of the back wall swung inward on hinges someone had oiled within the last year, revealing a crawl-passage sloping upward into fresh-moving air.

Someone had maintained this. Recently. Someone who wasn't Brannoc.

Astrid stood in the dead man's station, in the smell of his blood, and understood that the Relic Road was not empty. That whatever was hunting them had come through here—and that whoever kept this door oiled had come through here too, and recently, and had known exactly who would follow.

On the floor by the secret door, half-hidden in the dust, lay a token: a small iron badge stamped with a sigil Astrid hadn't seen since the night her world burned.

The mark of the Ninth Choir. The remnant mages.

They were expected.
