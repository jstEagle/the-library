## 13. War Remnants

The net came out of the dark like a spider's thought.

Wren felt it before she saw it—a wrongness in the air, the taste of copper and old smoke—and then the world turned sideways. The smugglers' road vanished. Astrid's hand, which had been gripping hers one heartbeat ago, was gone. She hit the ground hard on her side, wrapped in cords that weren't rope at all but something finer, something that hummed against her skin and drank the small warm light she'd been carrying in her chest since the night it first woke her.

She screamed for Astrid.

No sound came.

That was the other thing about the cords: they took her voice too. Not just muffled it—*took* it, pulled the words out of her mouth before they could become words, so that all around her the forest rang with silence shaped exactly like a child crying. Wren thrashed. The cords tightened. Above her, shapes detached from the trees—people, or people-shaped things, moving with the unhurried confidence of those who have done this many times.

A woman crouched beside her. Her face was weathered and kind in the way of someone who has decided to be kind as a matter of policy rather than feeling. She peeled back the netting from Wren's mouth with two fingers.

"Don't scream," the woman said. "It wastes your breath, and we haven't hurt you. We won't." A pause. "We need you breathing."

"Where is she?" Wren's voice came out cracked and small. "Where's Astrid?"

"Nearby. Alive. Being reasonable, I hope." The woman glanced over her shoulder. "My name is Sefa. You're going to meet Corvane now. He's been waiting a long time for you."

"I don't want to meet anyone."

Sefa smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Nobody ever does."

* * *

They marched her through the forest until the trees gave up and the ground turned to black glass.

Wren had never seen anything like it. The road they walked was smooth and dark and faintly luminous, and when she looked closer she saw that it wasn't stone at all—it was sand fused into mirror, mile upon mile of it, and here and there shapes rose from it like drowned things surfacing: archways, staircases, the ribcage of a tower, all of it glass, all of it melted into new forms by heat that must have come from the sky itself.

This had been a city once.

The First Tongue stirred in her without permission, the way it always did, rising up her throat like water finding its level. She didn't speak it aloud. But she understood what the glass was saying anyway, because the language didn't live only in her mouth anymore—it lived behind her eyes, and it read the world whether she wanted it to or not.

*We were called Vessia,* the glass said. *We made beautiful things.*

And beneath that, older and darker, pressed down like a footprint in cooling wax:

*Then the sky burned us.*

"There it is," said a voice ahead of them, deep and warm as banked coals. "There's the look I wanted."

He stood at the center of the ruined city where the glass road widened into a plaza, and he was not what Wren expected. She had imagined a monster from Astrid's careful silences—something gaunt and hungry. Instead Corvane was broad and silver-bearded, with laugh lines carved deep around his eyes, dressed in traveling leathers gone soft with age. He looked like a grandfather. He looked like someone who should be teaching children to fish.

He looked at Wren the way Astrid looked at maps of places that didn't exist.

"You're reading it," he said softly. "Already. Look at her face, Sefa—the city's talking to her and she understands every word." He knelt, slow and unthreatening, bringing himself to Wren's height. Up close his eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and very old. "Do you know what this place is?"

Wren said nothing.

"It's a lesson," Corvane said. "The last one standing. There were forty-one cities like this once. Now there's this, and dust, and stories nobody believes." He spread his hands at the glass plain around them. "Vessia. Three hundred thousand souls. In one night, the dragon ended it—not because Vessia attacked it, not because Vessia deserved it, but because Vessia was *winning*. Because magic was free here, and growing, and the dragon had grown old and afraid of what we might become."

"That's not true," Wren whispered.

"No?" Corvane tilted his head. "What did your keeper tell you? That the dragon was wise? Gentle? That it folded itself into memory out of mercy?" His voice gentled further, which somehow made it worse. "Child, I watched the sky burn over Vessia. I was six years old. My mother pushed me into a cellar and the last thing she ever said to me was *don't look up.*"

The First Tongue moved in Wren's chest, and against her will, an image surfaced:

*A dragon vast as weather, wings eclipsing three moons, and beneath it a city of white towers raising shields of light—and the shields holding, holding, holding, until the dragon spoke one word in its own tongue and the shields became rain.*

Wren gasped. The image was true. She knew it was true the way she knew her own name. And it was also—she groped for the shape of it, the way Astrid had taught her to test a map against the land—

It was also incomplete.

"The memory doesn't lie," Corvane said, watching her face. "That's the cruelty of it. Everything I'll show you is real. Every death, every burning, every city of glass. The dragon's own memory confirms it." He rose. "Come. Walk with me. I'll show you the rest, and then you can decide what to believe."

"I want Astrid."

"And you shall have her back, whole and unharmed, when we're finished." He offered his hand. "I give you my word, and my word is the only thing I've kept clean these sixty years."

Wren didn't take his hand. But she walked.

* * *

Corvane led her through the glass city while the First Tongue translated everything, treacherously, beautifully.

Here had stood the Hall of Winds, where mages had caught storms in bottles and sold fair weather to farmers three kingdoms away. Here the Loomhouse, where they wove light into cloth. Here the nursery school, and Wren knew it was the nursery school because the glass there held shapes no adult would make—small handprints pressed into the melting walls, preserved forever, dozens of them, hundreds.

*Don't look up,* the glass remembered. *Don't look up. Don't look up.*

Wren stopped walking. Her legs simply refused.

"They were winning," Corvane said quietly, beside her. "Understand that. This wasn't a war of aggression—they were defending themselves. The dragon came because it feared them. And when their shields failed—" he gestured at the handprints— "*that* is what failure looked like. Sixty years I've walked these ruins. Sixty years learning the First Tongue the hard way, syllable by syllable, from dead stones, because the living bearers were hunted to nothing. And do you know what I learned, child? What the whole truth comes down to?"

He crouched again, and his pale eyes held hers, and they were kind, and that was the most frightening thing about him.

"The dragon hoards memory the way misers hoard gold. It took the world's truths and locked them inside itself, and it went to sleep, and the world forgot. Forgot Vessia. Forgot the war. Forgot that magic was ever *theirs*, human-made, human-born—until cowards rewrote history and called it peace." He straightened. "And now the flame has chosen you. A child who can hear dead cities speak. Do you imagine that's accident? The flame has been running from hunters for sixty years, passing hand to hand, dying carrier after carrier—and it landed on *you*. It wants finishing. It wants someone to speak the word that wakes the dragon and ends the hoarding forever."

"And then what?" Wren asked. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

"And then memory returns to the world. All of it. Every burned name restored, every hidden truth spoken, every power the statutes buried given back to the people who were born carrying it." Corvane's voice swelled, and for a moment the grandfather mask slipped and something vast moved underneath it, something with wings. "No more Greyhollows. No more burnings. No more children taught to be ashamed of what they are. The war doesn't end, Wren—it *finishes*. There's a difference. Ending means someone lost. Finishing means everyone wins."

The words settled over her like warm water. She hated how good they felt. Somewhere under her ribs, the flame leaned toward him like a plant toward sun, and Wren realized with cold horror that part of her wanted to believe him—not because his story was complete, but because he was offering her the thing she had never once had in her whole small mute frightened life:

Belonging. Purpose. Someone saying *you are not too much; the world was too little.*

"Astrid says the dragon folded itself into memory to save it," Wren managed. "Not to steal it."

"Yes," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "That's what the order believed. Your Astrid's order. The same order that stood in Vessia's shields and let them fail, because they'd decided the world wasn't ready for what humans might become." Something flickered across his face—old grief, or old rage, worn smooth by decades. "Good people, Wren. Truly. They loved the world so much they chose to amputate it. And their heirs have been keeping the amputation bandaged ever since, calling the wound peace."

* * *

They brought her to a fire that evening, at the heart of the glass city, and the remnants gathered around it—maybe thirty of them, scarred and quiet and watchful, wearing their forbidden sigils openly for the first time in their lives. An old man showed Wren a trick of light between his fingers. A girl not much older than her made a candleflame dance into the shape of a bird. They smiled at her. They saved her the best seat.

*This is what family feels like,* said the part of her that had never had one.

And the flame purred.

But the First Tongue kept translating, because that was its nature, and the glass city kept speaking, because that was *its* nature, and underneath the warmth of the fire and the smiles, Wren heard what the ruins had been trying to tell her all day, the thing Corvane's story left out:

*We were winning,* the glass admitted. *And we grew cruel with winning. We took the storm-bottles to sea and broke them over enemy harbors. We wove light into cloth and cloth into banners and banners into armies. The dragon did not come because it feared our beauty. It came because we fed a mountain village to the Loomhouse to see what woven light would do to living flesh.*

Wren sat very still by the fire.

Both stories were true. That was the trap of it. Corvane hadn't lied to her—he'd shown her half a truth polished until it gleamed, and the missing half was buried right here under his feet, in the handprint glass, in the harbor-storms, in everything the First Tongue read whether she willed it or not.

She looked up and found Corvane watching her across the flames, and his expression was patient and fond and absolutely certain, and Wren understood suddenly, completely, the way children understand some things all at once:

He wasn't going to let her choose.

The word *finish* meant *him*. The flame would pass through her hands into his, the way water passes through a channel, and she would be the channel, and channels don't get asked. Channels get used. And afterward there would be no Astrid to run to, because Astrid's order was the enemy in this story, and enemies get—

Across the fire, Sefa caught her eye and smiled the policy-smile.

Wren smiled back.

And began, silently, in the language that bound dragons to memory, to count the guards.

* * *

Later, when the camp slept, Corvane came to her alone. He sat beside her bedroll without asking, the way family does, and that was exactly how it was meant to feel.

"You read the glass today," he said. "All of it. Even the parts I'd have spared you." He shook his head, admiring. "Sixty years, and the flame finally finds a bearer who can hear the whole choir. Do you know what that makes you?"

"Tired," Wren said.

Corvane laughed—a real laugh, warm and startled out of him. "Yes. I imagine it does." He was quiet a moment, watching the embers. Then, gently: "When the time comes, child, you won't even have to decide. The flame will know its purpose. You'll simply open your hands, and sixty years of hiding will end, and no one will ever hunt a child like you again." He rose, and paused, and looked down at her with those kind gray eyes. "Sleep. Tomorrow we go north, to Dragonrest. Tomorrow the waiting ends."

He walked away whistling, soft and tuneless, an old man going home.

Wren lay in the dark with the flame beating under her ribs like a second heart, and she listened—to the guards' breathing, to the wind moving through the glass towers, to the dead city whispering its two truths at once—and she made herself a promise in the First Tongue, where no one could hear it, not even the fire:

*I will finish something. But it won't be his war.*

Far off to the south, faint as a struck match, she felt wardens moving. Many of them. Fast.

And moving faster than any of them, silent as forgiveness, someone else—someone whose footsteps she knew better than her own heartbeat, coming north through the dark.

Wren closed her eyes and smiled.

*Astrid.*
