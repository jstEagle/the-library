## 14. Wren's Choice

The remnant camp smelled like woodsmoke and old blood, and Wren learned to love it anyway.

That was the trick of it—she understood that now, three days in. Corvane's people fed you belonging the way her mother had once fed her bread: a little at a time, so you kept coming back hungry. They gave her a cot in a tent with two other children who didn't flinch when she spoke words that made the fire lean toward her. They gave her a name for what she was—*bearer*—and they said it like a title instead of a curse.

They gave her soup with actual meat in it.

Wren had spent six years being passed from hand to hand like something hot, and nobody had ever wanted her close before. She knew that was exactly why she should be careful. Knowing it didn't help.

"You're thinking too loud," said Sef, one of the tent-children, a boy with a burn scar crawling up his neck like ivy. "Corvane says bearers think in the old language even when their mouths are quiet."

"Corvane says a lot of things," Wren signed, because speaking around these people still felt like spilling water from a cracked cup. Her hands were getting better at it. Astrid had taught her the basics on the road, back when it was just the two of them and the rain and the long gray miles.

Sef grinned. "He does. But he's right about you. I saw him look at you at supper. Like you were the last egg in the basket."

*Like you were the last egg,* Wren thought later, lying awake while the camp settled into its night-noise—the pop of fires, the mutter of sentries, somewhere far off the low groan of the mountains they were camped against. Eggs got eaten. That was the whole point of eggs.

She should have left then. She almost did. She got as far as the edge of the tent-flap, one hand on the canvas, before she heard voices by the central fire and stopped—not to listen, she told herself, just to wait until the sentry walked his circuit clear.

Corvane never seemed to sleep. He sat by the biggest fire with his lieutenants, a thin man made mostly of angles and certainty, his grey cloak pinned with a sigil Wren recognized from Astrid's stories: the broken circle of the old order, worn open on purpose, like a wound someone refused to let heal.

"The wardens are two days behind us," one of the lieutenants was saying. "Veyra's moved off the relic road. She's cutting for the pass."

"Let her." Corvane turned his hands over the fire, palms down, the way a man warms himself—or blesses something. "Veyra wants the flame dead. The dead are easy to step over. It's the living flame we have to be quick about."

"And the girl?"

"The girl is a door." Corvane smiled, and even across the distance, even in the dark, Wren felt the smile land on her skin like a moth. "A door doesn't get to choose what walks through it."

Wren stood very still among the shadows of the supply tents, and the First Tongue rose in her unbidden, the way it always did when her fear got big enough—the old words surfacing through her mind like drowned things coming up for air. *Memory is not a vessel. Memory is a river. You cannot own a river. You can only drown in it.*

She didn't know where the words came from. That was the worst part, the part she'd never told anyone, not even Astrid. The language moved through her and sometimes it knew things, and every time it happened she felt less like a girl and more like a bell someone else was ringing.

She slipped back to her cot. She lay in the dark. And she decided—coldly, carefully, the way Astrid had once decided which charm to sell and which to burn—that in the morning she would watch Corvane very closely, and she would find out what kind of man wanted to walk through a door.

* * *

In the morning, Corvane showed her the visions.

He did it gently, which somehow made it worse. He sat beside her at breakfast—a real kindness, handing her the honey first—and said, "You've seen fragments. Bits and pieces. The language leaks through you and you catch whatever drifts past. Am I right?"

Wren nodded slowly.

"I can give you the whole picture." He held out his hand, palm up. On it rested a shard of black glass, veined with faint gold, humming in a register Wren felt in her teeth rather than heard. "A piece of the old world. Touch it, and I'll show you what the dragon ended. What your order died protecting. What they burned fifteen years of Greyhollow to bury."

Around them, the camp went quiet without going still. People kept eating, chopping, mending—but every eye had angled toward her, and Wren understood that this was a ceremony, that she'd been brought here for this, that everything since the soup with meat in it had been leading to a black shard held out in an open palm.

*Take it,* whispered something in her chest, warm and enormous. The ember over her heart stirred like a bird turning in its sleep.

She touched the glass.

The world tore open.

Cities of glass, yes—she'd caught glimpses of those before, in the leaky fragments Corvane had mentioned. But now she saw them whole: towers like frozen fountains, bridges spanning valleys in single arcs of light, and everywhere people, thousands upon thousands, speaking the First Tongue as casually as breathing. Magic wasn't hidden then. Magic was weather. Magic was plumbing. Magic was the song everyone hummed without thinking.

And then the skies caught fire.

Not metaphor. Actual fire, falling in sheets, and beneath it dragons—she hadn't understood before how many there had been, a sky full of them, wheeling and burning—and beneath *them*, men with sigils carved into their own flesh, deep and wrong, doing things to the world's memory that made the vision itself flinch. The glass cities came down like chandeliers. The rivers ran backwards. Somewhere in the middle of it all, one dragon—vast, wounded, older than the rest—gathered every memory that remained, every name and face and song, and folded itself around them like a mother around a child, and went down into a mountain, and closed its eyes.

And the world forgot. Because the dragon took the remembering with it.

Wren came back to herself on her knees in the dirt, gasping, Sef holding her shoulders, Corvane crouched in front of her with tears standing in his eyes—and she believed the tears, that was the horrible thing, she believed they were real.

"Now you know," he said softly. "Fifteen years ago, Veyra and her kind burned my order to keep the world asleep. They called it peace. It was amnesia. It was a mass grave with laws written on top of it." His hand hovered near hers, not touching. "The flame you carry could wake it all. Every truth. Every name. Everything they stole."

*Yes,* said the thing in her chest. *Wake it.*

But Wren was looking at his eyes. At the place where the tears were. And she noticed what she should have noticed days ago, what the First Tongue had been trying to tell her all along in its wordless way:

Corvane wasn't looking at her with grief. He was looking at her the way Tam had looked at the rare relics locked in the back of his shop—with inventory. With appraisal. With the particular hunger of a man calculating what a thing would fetch.

And when he blinked, behind his eyes, she saw—for half a heartbeat, the way the language showed her things—black glass. A whole man made of it. A figure seated on a throne of folded memory, vast as the dragon had been vast, wearing Corvane's face stretched over something much larger, and the world below him kneeling in the ashes of the second war, the finished war, the one he intended to win.

*Bearer,* the vision-Corvane said, in the First Tongue, in her own voice. *Kneel.*

Wren pulled her hand back from the shard so fast she scraped it on the edge. Blood welled up, bright and ordinary and hers.

"You saw," Corvane said. Not a question. His voice had changed—still gentle, but gentleness stretched over steel now, the same trick as the tears. "Good. Then you understand what's at stake. The flame needs a bearer strong enough to speak it whole. Fifteen years of carriers, and every one of them broke. But you—" he reached out, and this time he did touch her, cupping her chin in his cold fingers, tilting her face up, "—you might be enough. And if you are, little door, you won't suffer. You'll simply open. What comes through will be me."

The camp was utterly silent now. Even the fires seemed to hold their crackle.

"That's not what the dragon wants," Wren managed. Her voice came out small. She hated it.

"The dragon is dead, child. Dead things don't want. Dead things are *used*." He smiled again, and this time he didn't bother warming it. "You've been alone your whole life. Passed along. Hunted. I'm offering you a family, a purpose, a throne at the right hand of a god. All you have to do is stop pretending you're a person. You've never been very good at it anyway. Have you? Six years mute. Six years with nothing inside worth saying."

And that—that was the moment. Not the throne, not the stolen flame, not even the threat. It was the cruelty, casual and precise, aimed at the exact wound he'd spent three days bandaging. Wren felt something shift in her chest, and it wasn't the ember. It was smaller and harder and entirely her own.

Astrid had never once told her she was empty. Astrid had taught her sign-language with ink-stained fingers and laughed at her own bad jokes and given her maps of places that didn't exist and said, *draw your own someday.* Astrid had lied to an Inquisitor's face for her. Astrid was probably, right now, somewhere beyond the ridge line, terrified, outgunned, and coming anyway.

False family fed you meat and called you a door. Real family drew you coastlines and called you a mapmaker.

"No," Wren said aloud.

It was the first full word she'd spoken in the camp. It came out rusty and cracked and it didn't matter at all, because everyone heard it.

Corvane's fingers tightened on her chin. "Pity," he said. "I'd hoped to do this kindly."

* * *

They put her in a warded tent after that—which was, Wren thought grimly, an admission of exactly how much they feared her. Sigils stitched into the canvas in thread that drank light. Two guards outside. Sef wouldn't meet her eyes when they brought her food, and that hurt more than the wards.

She tried the walls for a day. The stitches ate everything she pushed at them; the remnants knew their craft. But they'd made one mistake, the same mistake everyone made with her: they thought silence meant emptiness. They posted guards who talked. And the First Tongue didn't need her voice—it needed only her listening, and the ember's warmth, and time.

On the second night, she reached through the ground itself, down into the sleeping memory of the mountain, and asked the earth—in the old words—to remember where the loose stones were.

The wall of the tent came apart along a seam no stitcher had thought to ward, because it wasn't a seam. It was a fault line, ancient and patient, and the mountain remembered it perfectly.

She was three tents from freedom when the horns started.

Not for her. The sentries were facing south, torches swinging, and down the slope of the valley came lights—lanterns in a disciplined grid, dozens of them, moving in the formation Astrid had described with a shudder: warden columns, Veyra's columns, closing on the camp through the dark.

The camp exploded into motion. Remnants snatched up blades and glass-shards and ran to the perimeter; someone loosed a bow into the night; a fire-pitcher detonated against a rock and lit the whole hillside orange. Through the chaos Wren ran the other way, uphill, toward the goat-track Sef had once pointed out as the escape route, and she was almost clear—almost at the tree line—when she heard Astrid's voice, ragged with smoke, shouting her name from the rocks above.

Astrid came down the track at a slide, cloak torn, a warden's baton burn seared across her sleeve, and grabbed Wren by both arms and looked at her face and said, breathless, "Are you hurt? Did they—did he—"

"He wanted to wear me," Wren signed, fast, because the fighting was spreading up the slope. "Like a coat. He showed me. He's going to take the flame and become the dragon and start the war again, and he thinks I'm the door."

Something went hard and cold in Astrid's face, the old sigil-bearer surfacing through the charm-seller like a blade through water. "Then we run," she said. "Now. While they're killing each other."

Behind them, the camp burned. Ahead, the pass waited—Dragonrest, the choir, the price neither of them had finished naming. And somewhere in the smoke below, Wren felt a pressure turn toward her like an eye opening: Corvane, alive, furious, ascending, already reaching for the flame through the bond the black shard had opened between them.

He spoke one word down that bond, in the First Tongue, calm as a man setting a table.

*Soon.*

Wren took Astrid's hand, and they ran.
