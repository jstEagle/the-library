## 15. Veyra Breaks the Statutes

The report arrived at the fourth bell of the morning, and Veyra read it three times before her hands stopped shaking.

*Corvane. Alive.*

She had signed his death warrant herself, eleven years ago—a remnant mage with a burned face and a talent for surviving things that should have killed him. The warrant had gone out to every warden post in the eastern provinces. It had come back stamped *unresolved*, the way all such warrants eventually did, filed in the great gray cabinets of the Hall of Statutes where unresolved was simply another word for forgotten.

Now he was not unresolved. Now he was in the Dragonrest mountains with a company of armed remnants, and according to Tam's interrogation—the interrogation that had taken six hours and cost the relic dealer two fingers—he was moving toward something in the high passes that Veyra's own statutes forbade her to name.

The flame.

She set the report down on her desk, next to the statute book she kept there like a weapon, and looked out the window at Greyhollow waking below her. Smoke from a thousand chimneys rose straight in the windless cold. Somewhere down in the tannery district a bell rang the hour. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city she had spent fifteen years building into something safe, something *clean*, brick by brick and law by law.

And beneath it, under everything, the truth she had been refusing since the night Tam finally broke: magic had never died in the Purge. It had only learned to hide from her.

"You knew," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the younger woman who had stood in this same office thirty years ago with ink still wet on her novice's oath. "You always knew."

Because she had. That was the rot at the center of it, the thing she could no longer look away from. She had known the statutes were incomplete when she wrote them. She had known that burning a hundred mages in the market square did not burn what they carried—that grief and gift alike passed down bloodlines like eye color, surfacing generations later in some child who had done nothing, who knew nothing, who would stand in a warden's cart and scream for a mother who couldn't save her.

She had written the laws anyway. She had told herself they were mercy—the alternative to panic, to pogroms, to the old wars where cities of glass had melted into slag. Order instead of chaos. And then she had enforced them so long, so thoroughly, that she had forgotten they were ever a choice.

Tam's voice came back to her across the interrogation table, wet and broken but still insolent even at the end: *"You didn't kill it, Inquisitor. You just drove it into the dark. And things in the dark grow teeth."*

Corvane was the teeth.

* * *

The council convened at the sixth bell, and Veyra watched them fail her in real time.

Chancellor Ossler spoke first, as he always did, his voice carrying the particular weariness of a man who had built a career on other people's crises. "Dragonrest is beyond our jurisdiction. The mountain accords—"

"The mountain accords were written when we believed the mountain was empty." Veyra laid Corvane's file on the table. "There are forty armed remnant mages in those passes right now, marching toward an artifact of catastrophic power. Every day we deliberate is a day closer to a second Purge—one we might lose."

"Then we lose it," said Magistrate Corrin flatly. She was old enough to remember the wars; that was supposed to make her cautious, and instead it made her fatalistic. "We lost the first one too, girl. We survived because the dragon ended it. If the flame wakes again, that is the world's affair, not Greyhollow's."

"The world's affair." Veyra heard her own voice go quiet, which the council had learned to fear more than her shouting. "When the last bearer surfaced in Harrowgate, twelve years ago, it was *our* wardens who burned her. When the carriers died in the border towns, it was *our* statutes that hunted them. We made ourselves the world's executioners whenever it suited us, Magistrate. We do not get to be its bystanders now."

Ossler steepled his fingers. "What are you proposing?"

"A warden company. Fast, light, mounted. We ride for Dragonrest and we end this before Corvane reaches whatever he's reaching for."

"With what authority?" Corrin asked. "The statutes give you power within the city walls and the lowland provinces. Beyond that—"

"I know exactly what the statutes give me." Veyra gathered her papers. "I wrote them."

She left the chamber before they could vote. She already knew how they would vote. Caution was the religion of men who had never stood in front of a pyre, and she had long ago stopped expecting courage from people whose worst memory was a budget shortfall.

In the corridor outside, Captain Dree waited with her squad already assembled—she had sent the order before dawn, before the report was even fully read, because some part of her had known what the council would say. Twelve wardens in traveling grays, horses saddled, faces carefully blank.

Dree was twenty-nine, born four years after the Purge, and she had never once asked Veyra why the statutes existed. She simply enforced them, the way tide enforces shoreline. "Council said no?" she asked.

"The council hasn't finished saying it."

"And we're going anyway."

It was not a question. Veyra looked at the twelve of them—young, loyal, armed with iron and law and nothing else—and felt the full weight of what she was about to do settle onto her shoulders like a yoke.

"Yes," she said. "We're going anyway."

* * *

They rode out through the Salt Gate as the morning fog burned off, and Veyra did not look back at the city.

By the third day, in the hill country above the Merrow road, she let herself think about Astrid Montrose.

Fifteen years, and she still caught herself doing it—reaching for the memory the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. They had been novices together in the old chapterhouse, back when the order still had a name anyone dared speak. Astrid had been the brilliant one, the one the masters said would carry the sigil-work into a new century. Veyra had been the careful one, the one who memorized doctrine and asked hard questions and noticed, early, that the questions made the masters uncomfortable.

She had loved her. That was the simplest and most unforgivable fact. Not the way the ballads meant it—or maybe exactly the way the ballads meant it, she had stopped parsing the difference decades ago—but with the whole, unguarded devotion of someone who had no family except the order, and then no family except Astrid.

And when the order fell, when the wardens came with their lists, Veyra had stood in the courtyard and watched the fires and understood—with a clarity that felt like dying—that someone had given the wardens those lists. Someone inside.

She had never learned who. She had spent fifteen years making sure it would never matter, building a world so lawful, so gray, so utterly without magic that no list would ever be needed again. That was the confession she had never spoken, not to her confessors, not to the council, not to the dark at three in the morning: the statutes were not justice. They were penance. She had burned the world's wonder down to ash because she could not bear a world that contained both Astrid Montrose and a courtyard full of fire.

And now the reports said Astrid was in the mountains too. Running toward the same flame, with a child.

*A child.* Tam had described her before he broke completely—a mute girl, mud-caked, speaking a language no living person should know. Veyra had ordered the description circulated with the standard warrant, *bearer-adjacent, detain for questioning*, and had told herself that questioning was all it would be.

But she had seen what happened to bearer-adjacent detainees. She had signed those orders for fifteen years. There was no version of *questioning* that ended well for a child who spoke the First Tongue, and she knew it, and she had sent the warrant anyway, because the machine she had built only had one gear.

That was the thought that rode with her up into the hills, that gnawed at her beside the night fires while the young wardens slept: *the machine only has one gear, and I built it that way, and now it is grinding toward a child and I am the only one who can stop it, and stopping it means breaking everything I am.*

* * *

On the fifth day they found the first body.

He lay in a culvert beside the old pilgrim road, a remnant scout by his tattoos, dead perhaps two days. Warden Pell crouched over him and came up pale. "Warden weapons, ma'am. Or close enough. Someone reached here ahead of us."

"Not someone." Veyra studied the wound—a clean thrust, professional, the kind her own training produced. "A warden company went rogue before we did. Ossler. He voted caution to my face and dispatched a kill team behind my back."

Dree spat. "So we're racing two enemies now."

"We're racing everyone." Veyra looked north, where the Dragonrest massif had begun to resolve out of the haze—black peaks, snowfields, and somewhere in those heights, a cave mouth that half the world's buried history pointed toward like a compass needle. "Corvane wants the flame to start a war. Ossler wants it dead before it can embarrass him. And the choir—" she stopped herself, because saying *the choir* aloud in front of her wardens was a confession she wasn't ready to make. "And there are others. Ride on."

They rode. The country climbed and narrowed. The farms gave way to pine, the pine to scree, and the air took on the thin metallic taste of altitude. On the seventh night, camped in a hollow below the tree line, Veyra sat awake with the statute book open on her knees—not reading, just holding it, feeling its weight, the way you'd hold a stone you were deciding whether to throw or keep.

Around her, the wardens slept. Dree kept first watch, a silhouette against the stars.

"Ma'am." Dree's voice, low. "Can I ask you something I've never asked you?"

"You've earned one question in nine years. Spend it carefully."

"Why do the statutes exist? Really. Not the doctrine answer."

Veyra turned the page without seeing it. Below them the valley dropped away into darkness, and far to the north, faint as breath on glass, the sky above Dragonrest held a glow that was not dawn and not firelight and not anything her laws had a name for.

"Because once," she said, "I watched the person I loved most stand in a courtyard, and I could not save her, and I swore the world would never make me choose like that again. And then I spent thirty years learning that the swearing was itself the choosing." She closed the book. "That's not the doctrine answer."

"No," Dree said quietly. "It isn't."

At midnight, Veyra took out her commission—the document bearing the council seal, the one that gave her authority to the edge of the lowland provinces and not one step farther—and fed it page by page into the cookfire.

Dree watched her do it and said nothing, which was its own kind of oath.

* * *

They crossed into the high passes on the ninth day, and the world changed.

Snow. Silence. A silence so complete Veyra could hear her own heartbeat, and under it—faintly, impossibly—something else. Voices. Not words yet, not quite, but the sense of voices, layered and distant, like a congregation singing in a cathedral she couldn't find the door to.

Pell heard it too. So did the horses; they pressed their ears flat and needed constant urging.

"What is that?" Dree whispered.

Veyra knew. She had known since Tam's interrogation, since the word *choir* had surfaced in the dealer's broken ramblings. Every mage the Purge had burned, every carrier hunted down across three generations—all of them, persisted somehow in the mountain's memory, singing to whoever came near enough to hear.

Her laws had filled graveyards. The graveyard was singing.

"It's nothing," she said, and heard how hollow it sounded. "Keep moving."

They crested the shoulder of the pass at midday and saw, two ridges below, the remnants' camp—forty tents, watchfires, sentries walking the lines. And beyond it, half-hidden in a fold of the mountain, the cave mouth: a black archway rimmed with frost that did not melt, breathing a light that pulsed slow and deep, like a heart.

Between Veyra and all of it, on the ridge opposite, movement. Banners she recognized with a sick lurch—the Chancellor's gray and gold. Ossler's kill team, already in position, already too late to stop, already hunting the same quarry.

And threading up the switchback trail between the camps, small and quick and unmistakable even at distance, two figures. One tall, one small. One carrying a satchel of maps.

One child, walking toward the heart of the mountain with a woman Veyra had spent fifteen years trying to forget.

"There," Dree breathed, hand on her sword.

Veyra did not move. The choir's voices had grown louder, and now, for the first time, they resolved—not into words, but into something worse. Into recognition. As if the mountain knew her name. As if every soul she had burned was watching her climb toward them, patient, waiting to see what she would do with the last unburned thing she had left: the choice.

"Inquisitor?" Dree prompted. "Your orders?"

Veyra drew a long breath of thin, cold air, and gave the order that undid thirty years of law in four words:

"We go down. Now."
