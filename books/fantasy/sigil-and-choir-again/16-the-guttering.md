## 16. The Guttering

The mountain announced itself three days before they reached it.

Astrid felt it first in her sigils—a low hum beneath her skin, like a struck bell still ringing somewhere deep in the stone. Wren felt it too. The child had gone quiet on the second morning out of the relic road, quiet in a way that frightened Astrid more than any fever, because Wren's silences had always been full of language. This one was empty.

By the third day the child could not keep pace.

They climbed through pine that gave way to scree, through scree that gave way to snow, and Wren stumbled every twenty steps and caught herself on Astrid's sleeve with fingers that had gone cold as river stones. The flame-mark over the child's heart—once a living coal Astrid could feel warming her palm through layers of wool—had dimmed to something like the last light in a banked fire. Not dead. But deciding.

"It's guttering," Astrid said aloud, because saying things aloud made them real, and real things could be fought. "The flame is guttering."

Wren looked up at her. The child's lips moved around shapes that produced no sound at all—not even the First Tongue now. That was the worst of it. For weeks the language had poured out of Wren unbidden, waking Astrid at night with syllables she hadn't heard since her order burned, words that made her tattoos crawl and her old grief sit up in its grave. Now there was nothing. The dragon's memory was folding inward, drawing down into whatever vault it kept at the center of itself, and Wren—the vessel, the carrier, the last link in a chain of hunted hands stretching back decades—was being emptied along with it.

"Nearly there." Astrid lifted the child onto her hip, though Wren was seven years old all bone and stubbornness and far too heavy for it. She carried her anyway. She would carry her up the whole mountain if it came to that.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar that had no business existing—worked stone, sigil-cut, older than Greyhollow, older than the Purge, older maybe than the city laws that had turned magic into kindling. A threshold marker. Someone had built a door here once and the mountain had swallowed everything but the frame.

Astrid built a small fire with ordinary wood and ordinary friction, refusing even now to use the small bright magics she'd rediscovered in the flooded undercity. Not here. Not so close. Here, power would be a shout in a room where something enormous slept.

Wren lay against her side and did not sleep. The child's eyes stayed open, fixed on the dark above them where the stars came down close and hard, and after an hour Astrid realized the girl wasn't looking at the sky at all. She was looking *through* it.

"Wren."

The child's hand found hers. Pressed something into it—a pebble, smooth and warm despite the cold. When Astrid turned it over she saw the sigils on her own wrist flare faintly in answer, and understood: not a pebble. An egg of memory. The dragon's hoard shedding grains of itself as it dimmed, like a dying man's pockets turning out.

Inside the warmth of the stone, she felt a fragment of image: a valley green past any green she knew, rivers running silver, and a shadow crossing the sun with wings wide enough to shade a city.

Astrid closed her fist around it until the warmth hurt.

"You're going to be fine," she told the child, and hated how much her voice sounded like a lie wearing a coat.

* * *

The choir reached them just before dawn.

It didn't come as sound. It came the way weather comes—a pressure change, a taste in the air, the hair rising on your arms before the first thunder. Astrid was awake and had been for hours when every sigil on her body lit at once, soft gold, and the air above the dead doorframe rippled like water disturbed by a dropped stone.

Voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

She knew what they were. She had heard them once before, in the cavern beneath Greyhollow, when the echo-choir of every mage killed in the Purge had named her bearer-heir and offered her the full flame and she had refused—refused knowing refusal meant this, exactly this, the slow starvation of the thing she was trying to save.

*Astrid Montrose.*

Her own name, spoken in the voice of Master Ellery Vane, who had taught her cartography of impossible places, who had burned screaming in the square outside the Sigilhouse while Astrid hid in a root cellar and lived. Fifteen years, and hearing his voice still stopped her heart mid-beat.

"We're here," she said. "We came. We're at the threshold—"

*We know where you are.* The chorus spoke as one and as many, overlapping, the way surf does. *We have always known. It is why we called.*

"Well, I'm listening. Tell me how to feed it. Tell me what it needs. There must be something—" She was talking too fast and she knew it. "Songs? Rites? My order kept rites for tending flame, half of them are still in my head, I never let them go—"

*Astrid.*

The choir said her name again, gently, the way you say the name of someone standing at the edge of something high.

*You know what it needs. You have known since the cavern. Since before the cavern, if you are honest, since the night the child knocked and your skin remembered what it was for.*

The fire crackled. Wren breathed shallow and slow against her ribs. Somewhere below them, days back down the trail, Veyra was climbing with a rogue squad of wardens, and Corvane was climbing with his remnant fanatics, and every road up this mountain funneled to this one dead door. Astrid had known that too. Had counted the hours.

"The price," she said quietly. "Say the price."

*Everything,* said the choir, in a hundred voices that had all loved her once. *Everything the bearer remembers. The flame cannot be carried by strength or by worthiness—it can only be carried by willingness, and willingness must cost what fear costs most. To bear the fire the final distance, you will give it your memories. All of them. Piece by piece, as you walk. Your order. Your childhood. Your griefs, your loves, the faces—*

"Stop."

*—the names—*

"I said stop."

The choir went silent, or seemed to. The sigils dimmed to embers along her arms. In the hush Astrid could hear the wind combing the scree, and Wren's breath, and underneath both, faint as a pulse through stone, the vast slow heartbeat of the thing sleeping in the mountain's heart. Fainter than it should be. Fainter every hour.

She sat down heavily beside the child.

Fifteen years ago she had buried her gift under routine and harmless charms and maps of places that don't exist, and she had thought that was sacrifice—that invisibility, that erasure of self. It had been nothing. It had been a rehearsal. Because now the bill was coming due in full, and the currency was not her freedom or her comfort but the entire architecture of who she was. Every lesson Ellery Vane taught. Her mother's kitchen. The smell of ink. The weight of Wren asleep against her side on the relic road, the exact tuneless humming the child did while drawing. Veyra's face—young, laughing, mud-splattered, before the burning and the statutes and the fifteen years of opposite sides.

If she paid it, she would walk into the dragon's heart a stranger carrying a stranger's errand, and come out—or not come out—having saved a world she would no longer recognize as worth saving.

And if she refused?

The arithmetic was simple and it was terrible. Refusal meant the flame gutted out within a season. And the flame was not only fire—it was memory, hoarded memory, the stored truth of everything the Purge tried to burn out of the world. When it died, all of that died with it. Every voice in the choir, silenced forever. Every truth suppressed, lost past recovery. Magic wouldn't return as war or weather or anything at all; it would simply end, quietly, and the world would keep its honest gray ground and never know what had been taken from it.

And Wren would die with it. The flame was keeping the child alive—Astrid could see that now, had perhaps always seen it. The mark over her heart was a hearth, and a hearth going cold takes the house down with it.

There were three ways to end a flame. Let it starve. Snuff it. Or feed it so completely it becomes something new.

Two of those ways destroyed everything. One required her to destroy herself instead.

"Astrid?" 

She looked down. Wren's eyes were open, fever-bright, fixed on her face. The child's mouth worked. No sound came—but her hand rose, trembling, and pressed flat against Astrid's sternum, right over the place where a bearer's sigils pooled brightest.

And into that touch, the last flicker of the First Tongue moved through the child like a shudder through a rope:

*Who carries me home?*

Not the dragon asking. Wren asking. Seven years old, mute, hunted her whole short life, handed a burden no adult in forty years had survived carrying—and asking, with the last of her borrowed fire, not *will you save me* but *who carries me home*. As if the answer mattered. As if the child needed to know whose hands, whose name, whose story would be spent on her behalf, so she could hold onto it, so someone would remember even if the bearer couldn't.

Astrid gathered her in. Held her the way you hold the one thing left.

"I do," she said. "I carry you home."

Above the dead doorframe, the air rippled gold, and the choir began—softly, like an orchestra tuning—to sing the walking-song of the bearers, the one that had not been sung in forty years.

And down the mountain, faint but unmistakable on the wind, came the sound of many boots on scree, and behind them another sound, wrong and growing, like wings made of stolen fire.

They had until dawn. Perhaps less.
