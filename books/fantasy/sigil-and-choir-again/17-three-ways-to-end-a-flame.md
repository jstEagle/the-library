## 17. Three Ways to End a Flame

**Astrid**

The pass at Dragonrest was narrower than her maps had promised. Her maps had never been drawn by anyone who came back.

She climbed with Wren's small hand locked in hers, the child's breath coming in white rags, and felt the flame under Wren's ribs guttering like a candle in a doorway. Three days ago it had burned steady. Two days ago it had begun to flicker. Now, as they crossed the last saddle beneath peaks the color of old bone, Astrid could feel it through her palm—each heartbeat of the child a little weaker, a little colder, the ember spending itself against nothing.

*Refusal means the flame gutters out within a season,* the choir had told her, and it had not lied. It had simply lied about how long a season was.

"Almost," she said, because Wren kept looking up at her for permission to keep going. "We're almost there."

Wren didn't answer. Wren hadn't answered in hours—not in gesture, not in the First Tongue, not in anything. The language that moved through the child had gone quiet, and that silence frightened Astrid more than the wardens' horns behind them or the shapes pacing them along the ridgeline to the east. A silent flame wasn't resting. A silent flame was dying.

The threshold opened before them near dusk: a cleft in the mountain's face where the rock changed character entirely, from granite to something veined and dark and faintly warm, like standing too close to a sleeping animal. The choir's echo reached them here, faint as sound through water—voices without faces, every mage the Purge had fed to the fire, humming a note that lived below hearing.

Astrid stopped at the lip of it.

This was the moment. She had known it would be, since Tam's ledger, since the flooded undercity, since the night a mud-caked child spoke words that lit her skin like sunrise. Everything narrowed to this: walk into the mountain carrying the flame, pay the price the choir had named, and lose everything she remembered.

She had said goodbye already. She'd done it wrong—too fast, on the road, shoving her maps into Wren's pack while the boy watched with those enormous eyes—but she'd done it. *My name is Astrid Montrose. I was born in Vael Harbor. My mother sang badly and my father mended nets.* Say it until it sticks somewhere outside your own head, because inside it wouldn't survive what was coming.

Then the horns sounded again, closer, and from the eastern ridge came light that had no business existing: sigil-fire, green and gold, burning in patterns she hadn't seen since her order died.

Corvane's remnants had found another way up.

* *

**Veyra**

Veyra had broken every law she ever wrote to get here, and the strange thing—the thing she would carry to whatever grave was waiting for her—was how easy it had been.

Six wardens behind her, handpicked, sworn by candlelight to an oath that existed in no statute. Boots that left no requisition trail. A forged order bearing the council's seal, which she had pressed herself with hands that did not shake. Fifteen years of building the machine of Greyhollow's law, and when it mattered, she'd simply stepped around it, the way you step around a beggar in the market street.

Because Corvane was real. The reports were real. Somewhere ahead of her a man was tearing pieces off the oldest fire in the world and calling himself its heir, and Veyra understood—with the clarity of a woman reading her own autopsy—that the vacuum he filled was shaped exactly like her statutes. You cannot outlaw a river. You can only drive it underground, and then act surprised when it comes up poisoned.

"Smoke," said Brannic, her second, pointing.

Not smoke. Sigil-light, climbing the eastern ridge in deliberate spirals. Veyra knew those patterns. She had studied them obsessively for fifteen years, cataloguing everything she'd helped destroy, the way a penitent memorizes the face of everyone she's wronged.

"He's already at the threshold," she said.

"Then we run."

They ran. The pass floor was scree and frozen meltwater, and the air grew warmer as they went, which was wrong, all of it wrong—mountains cooled as you climbed, but Dragonrest breathed, and every warden in her squad felt it and none of them said so.

At the final bend Veyra raised a fist and the squad stopped, and there it was: the cleft in the rock, glowing faintly from within like a kiln door left ajar. And before it, three figures.

Astrid Montrose, gray-streaked and gaunt, standing between the mountain and the world.

Wren, the child, swaying on their feet like a candle about to go out.

And above them on the eastern spur, descending with terrible patience, Corvane—wrapped in stolen light, his shadow cast enormous against the snow, wearing power the way a drunk wears someone else's coat.

Veyra drew her blade. It felt absurdly small.

"Astrid!" she called, and her voice cracked on the name like ice on a pond.

* *

**Wren**

The voices in the mountain were fading, and Wren wanted to cry but couldn't remember how crying worked.

That was the truth of the last two days, though there was no one to tell it to. The flame didn't just burn. It *ate*. It ate warmth first, then color—Wren had watched the sunset go gray at the edges, like ash falling over everything—and now it had started eating names. Wren knew Astrid's face. Wren could not, for the life of them, remember what Astrid's name meant, or why it mattered, only that it mattered enormously and that forgetting it felt like dropping something precious down a well.

*Carry me,* said the faint voice under the stone. *Hurry.*

*I'm trying,* Wren thought back, in the Tongue, in the only language that had ever felt like theirs. *I'm so tired.*

And then the mountain's mouth filled with people who wanted different things, and Wren learned what the end of the world sounds like: three kinds of footsteps converging on one small dying light.

Below, wardens in gray, blades out, and at their front a woman whose face made something flare in Wren's chest—recognition, but not Wren's recognition. The flame remembered her. The flame remembered hating her and loving her in the same breath, and it pushed both feelings up into Wren's body until their knees buckled.

Above, Corvane, coming down the ridge with light leaking out of him at the seams, smiling like a man arriving at his own coronation.

And beside Wren, Astrid, stepping forward, putting her body between the child and both armies at once, which was ridiculous, which was hopeless, which was the bravest thing Wren had ever seen anyone do.

"No further," Astrid said, to all of them, to the whole world. "No one takes this child."

* *

**Astrid**

Corvane reached the threshold first, because gravity favored him—he simply stepped off the spur and let the stolen flame carry him down, landing in the scree with a sound like a felled tree.

He looked worse than the last time Astrid had seen him, at the camp, before Wren fled. The power was eating him. Everyone could see it. His skin had a translucent quality at the wrists and throat, and when he smiled, the light behind his teeth was not entirely his own.

"Bearer-heir," he said warmly. "You brought it. After all that running, you carried it right to the door. I want you to know I appreciate the efficiency."

"It's not yours."

"It was never *yours*, either." He spread his hands, and the green-gold light crawled across them like insects. "It belonged to the dragon, and the dragon abandoned the world to go sleep in a hole. Someone has to hold what it dropped. The question is only whether it's held by a woman who spent fifteen years pretending magic doesn't exist, or by someone willing to *use* it."

Behind him, his remnants fanned out along the rocks—two dozen of them, sigils burning, hungry-eyed. Ahead, Veyra's wardens formed a line with steel and prayer-wards. And Astrid stood in the narrow space between, with a dying child and a dying flame and no weapon except the one she'd sworn not to use.

"You're burning up," she told Corvane quietly. "Can't you feel it? The flame rejects you. Every piece you tear free costs you more than it gives."

"I feel *alive*." His voice cracked upward into something shrill. "For the first time since the Purge, I feel like the world has a place in it for me—"

"Horns," one of the remnants shouted, and then the pass erupted.

* *

**Veyra**

Later, Veyra would never be able to assemble the battle into a coherent order. Memory gave it back to her as fragments, each one lit too brightly, like pages held too close to a flame.

Brannic going down with a remnant's sigil-fire sheeting off his shield-arm, screaming about his daughter in Greyhollow.

The mountain itself groaning as Corvane tore at the threshold's seal, the whole pass shuddering loose avalanches of old snow.

Astrid's hands moving in patterns Veyra knew from fifteen years ago—from the novices' hall, from shared lessons by candlelight—and forbidden light answering them, rising off the ground in ribbons, and Veyra feeling her heart break cleanly in two, because it was beautiful, it had always been beautiful, and she had spent her whole life making beauty punishable by fire.

"Take the child!" Corvane roared to his people. "The woman is irrelevant!"

Two remnants broke toward Wren. Astrid turned, and the ribbons of light became a wall, and for one moment—one shining, impossible moment—it held.

Then Corvane hit it with everything he had.

* *

**Wren**

Wren fell. The wall broke. Hands grabbed—rough hands, remnant hands—and the flame inside Wren flared in terror, and terror tasted like burning cities, like glass rain, like everything Corvane had shown them in his visions but *true* this time, unedited, the war the dragon had ended pouring through a child's body because a child's fear had opened every door.

Wren screamed in the First Tongue and the scream was not a scream. It was a word. One of the old words. The kind that rearranges things.

The remnants holding Wren were thrown ten feet and did not get up.

The mountain shook harder.

And through the chaos—through the wardens advancing and the remnants countercharging and Corvane laughing with a mouth full of borrowed sun—came a fourth sound, small and human and utterly out of place:

Tam's voice, shouting Wren's name.

* *

**Astrid**

He should have stayed in Greyhollow. He should have stayed behind in the smugglers' camp, safe with his ledgers and his cowardice, and instead here he came up the switchback trail with six hired crossbows and the expression of a man doing the single bravest thing of his thoroughly compromised life.

"TAM!" Astrid screamed. "GO BACK!"

"Never was any good at going back!" he bellowed cheerfully, and his crossbows loosed into the remnant line, buying chaos exactly where chaos was needed—between Corvane and the threshold, between Wren and the hands reaching for the child.

It bought seconds. It bought maybe thirty seconds.

Astrid used every one of them. She scooped Wren up—the child weighed almost nothing now, terrifyingly little—and ran for the cleft in the mountain, and the choir's note rose to meet them, and behind her she heard Veyra shouting orders that were not about killing her, that were about *clearing a path*, and she had no time to wonder what that meant.

Corvane understood at once. He turned from the melee, from Veyra's wardens, from everything, and came for them in a straight line, burning holes in the snow with his footprints.

Tam stepped into that line.

Astrid saw it happen the way you see a cart crash—slowly enough to be helpless, quickly enough to be forever. Tam planted himself, raised his crossbows, and fired both into the oncoming blaze of a man becoming a god. The bolts burned to ash a foot from Corvane's chest. Tam didn't even try to dodge after that. He just stood there, arms wide, grinning his market-stall grin, and said, loud enough for the whole pass to hear:

"Ledger's closed! Everything paid!"

Corvane didn't slow. He didn't even seem to notice. He passed through Tam the way weather passes through a fence, and Tam folded, and Astrid—carrying Wren, carrying the last hope of everything—could do nothing, nothing, nothing but keep running.

The cleft swallowed them. The choir rose around them like water closing overhead. And the last thing Astrid saw of the world was Veyra, blade drawn, standing alone between Corvane and the mountain's mouth, holding a line she had spent fifteen years saying should never be held—

—for her.

For Astrid. For the flame. For everything Veyra had sworn to destroy.

Then the mountain closed, and the price came due, and somewhere behind them Tam Voss lay still in the snow of Dragonrest, having finally, at the end of a long dishonest life, sold something worth what it cost.
