## 18. Corvane Ascendant

Wren had learned the First Tongue the way other children learned to be afraid—slowly, then all at once. And now she heard it screaming.

It came from Corvane's hands.

He stood at the center of the shattered pass, where Tam's blood still darkened the stones and Veyra's wardens lay scattered like dropped dolls, and he held a fistful of light that did not want him. The flame he'd torn from the ember's edge writhed in his grip, and it was *speaking*, and only Wren could hear what it said.

*Wrong. Wrong. Wrong.*

The mountain groaned beneath them. Snow shook from the high crags in long white banners. Somewhere below, deep in Dragonrest, something vast turned over in its sleep, disturbed by the theft happening at its threshold.

"Corvane." Astrid's voice cracked across the chaos. She was on her knees beside Wren, one arm thrown protectively across her shoulders, and there was ash on her face and terror in her eyes—the specific terror of someone who had seen this before, long ago, in another life. "Let it go. You don't understand what you're holding."

"I understand perfectly." Corvane smiled, and it was the worst thing Wren had ever seen, because it was a kind smile. He had been kind to her. That was the trap of him; she understood that now, too late and not too late, both at once. "I've studied this for thirty years, Montrose. I've read every burned book your order failed to save. The flame chooses bearers, yes. But choice—" he flexed his fingers, and the light screamed louder, "—is just a door. Doors can be forced."

The stolen portion of the flame sank into his palm like water into sand.

And Corvane began to rise.

* * *

Wren felt it happen in her chest first, where her own small ember lived—a sympathetic tearing, like hearing a bone break in another room. She doubled over, gasping, and Astrid caught her, pulled her close, and Wren pressed her face into the wool of Astrid's coat and tried not to listen.

But listening was all she was. Listening was what she *was*.

Through the First Tongue, she heard the memory flood into him. That was what the flame carried—not power, not really, not the way Corvane imagined it. It carried *remembering*. Ten thousand years of it. Every dawn the dragon had ever watched break over the world. Every name of every person who had ever spoken to it in the old language. Cities that no longer existed, whole and glittering and loud. The smell of the world before men built walls against magic. The sound of the first oath ever sworn.

It poured into Corvane like a river through a crack in a dam, and for one moment—one shining, terrible moment—he was transfigured. His grey eyes went gold. His scarred face smoothed. He looked, Wren thought with a child's terrible clarity, like a statue of himself, an idealized version carved by someone who loved him.

"There you are," he whispered. "There you *are*."

Then the flame began to burn him.

* * *

It happened slowly enough that Wren could watch each stage, and fast enough that watching felt like falling.

First his shadow changed. It stretched wrong, too long for the pale morning light, and it grew edges—wings, a tail, a long serpentine neck—and then it detached from him entirely and lay on the snow like a second body, twitching.

Then his voice changed. When he spoke again, words came out doubled, layered: his own voice underneath, and above it a vast dry whisper like wind through a canyon, speaking syllables Wren knew but could not have pronounced without bleeding.

*"Bearer,"* the canyon-voice said, using Corvane's mouth. *"Bearer. Bearer."*

"Not bearer," Corvane said, laughing, and the laugh had cracks in it already. "*Owner.*"

The mountain answered him. A fissure split the pass fifty yards away with a report like the sky snapping, and heat breathed up out of it—Dragonrest's deep heart, stirred and angry. Wardens who had been crawling toward cover froze. Even Veyra, standing apart with her warden's blade drawn and her face gray as old paper, took a step back.

Corvane rose higher. Not walking—*rising*, lifted on threads of gold light that lanced out of his back like the ribs of some unfolding thing. Memory made him monstrous, Wren realized. Not because memory was evil, but because he was trying to hold ten thousand years in a vessel shaped for one small life. He was a cup under a waterfall. The waterfall didn't care. The waterfall simply kept coming, and the cup simply kept breaking, and the breaking looked, from the outside, like ascension.

His skin began to glow along the seams. Literally the seams—lines Wren hadn't noticed before, hairline fractures running up his forearms, across his throat, over his cheekbones, as if he were porcelain and something inside him was lit.

"Look at me!" he shouted at the wardens, at Veyra, at the mountain, at the world. Gold light poured from his mouth when he spoke. "Look what your Purge threw away! Look what you burned! Fifteen years of ash and fear, and THIS—" he spread his arms, and wings of remembered fire unfurled behind him, vast and beautiful and wrong, "—THIS is what you were afraid of! You should have been *worshipping* it!"

"You're killing yourself," Astrid said quietly. She had gotten to her feet, and she had put herself between Corvane and Wren, and her voice was the steadiest thing on the whole shaking mountainside. "That's what the choir tried to tell me. The flame isn't a weapon, Corvane. It's a *trust*. It burns anything that takes instead of carries."

"The choir." He spat the word, and sparks fell from his lips and hissed in the snow. "The choir are ghosts. Ghosts keep nothing. Ghosts *are* nothing. I am going to be the dragon, Montrose. Not its bearer. Not its heir. The dragon itself. New. Improved. Awake."

Inside Wren's chest, her ember flared hot with grief.

Because she could hear what he couldn't. Through the First Tongue, through the connection she'd carried since the night she appeared on Astrid's doorstep, she could hear the flame's true voice beneath its screams—and the flame wasn't angry. That was the horror of it. The flame was *sorrowful*. It had done this before. It knew exactly how this ended. It was burning Corvane with the same gentle, irresistible patience with which a mother pulls a child's hand from a stove, and it was grieving him even as it did it, because every bearer who reached for it greedily had once been someone who only wanted, desperately, to matter.

*Little speaker,* the flame said to Wren, in the space behind her eyes where the language lived. *Little throat. Tell them what I am.*

"What are you?" Wren whispered aloud, and her voice came out strange, resonant, doubled the way Corvane's was—but hers rang clean where his cracked.

Every head on the mountainside turned toward her. Even Corvane. Especially Corvane.

"You're not power," Wren said, and the words moved through her like cold water, and she let them. "You're a promise. You're everything the world promised to remember. And promises can't be *owned*. They can only be kept or broken." She looked at him—at the glowing seams, at the shadow-wings thrashing in the snow, at the man inside the monument dissolving. "You're breaking it."

"Child." Corvane's voice was mostly canyon now, his own barely audible beneath. He drifted closer, and the heat coming off him made the snow around Wren's boots retreat in a shrinking circle. "Sweet child. I offered you a family. I showed you the truth of the war. Come with me and I'll teach you everything. We'll rule the remembering together."

And here was the thing Wren would carry for the rest of her life, the thing she would one day teach to others in a soft room full of candlelight: for half a heartbeat, she wanted to say yes.

Not because of the power. Because of the *belonging*. Because Corvane had looked at her—mute, mud-caked, unwanted Wren—and seen a speaker of the First Tongue, and for a few days she had known what it was to be seen. The temptation wasn't the throne. It was the chair pulled up to the table.

She thought of Astrid's arm around her shoulders. Of maps of places that didn't exist, drawn by a woman who had hidden for fifteen years so that a stranger's child could stay safe. Of Tam, dead in the snow twenty feet away, who had died buying seconds with a smuggler's grin and a smuggler's knife.

"No," Wren said.

The word came out in the First Tongue, and it landed on the mountain like a bell struck once.

Corvane flinched. Actually flinched—as if the language itself, spoken cleanly by a worthy throat, was a rebuke his stolen flame couldn't shield him from. The seams in his skin brightened. One of them split along his jaw, and what leaked out wasn't blood. It was light, and in the light, for an instant, Wren saw images: glass cities falling, skies on fire, the ancient war he'd shown her in visions—all of it pouring back OUT of him, rejected, returning to sender.

"No," she said again, in plain Greyhollow speech this time, because some things needed both languages. "You don't want to share the remembering. You want to BE it. And the flame knows the difference."

* * *

What happened next, Wren would never fully be able to describe, though she would spend years trying, drawing it in diagrams, teaching it syllable by careful syllable.

The mountain opened.

Not far—not more than a handspan, a seam of red light cracking open in the rock at the pass's heart, right where the dragon's dreams pressed closest to the surface. But through that seam came a sound: the Choir. All of it. Every mage the Purge had killed, every voice bound into the dragon's memory, singing one sustained note that was also a word that was also a name.

Corvane's name.

Not the name he used. The name he'd been born with, before he remade himself, before the remnants, before the doctrine—the small human name of a boy who had once, presumably, been afraid of the dark like anyone else.

And the flame in him *answered*.

It tore free.

Not all of it—he'd fused too much of it into his bones for that, and that fusion was the wound that would kill him—but the living part, the willing part, ripped out of his chest in a gout of golden fire and dove for the seam in the mountain like a salmon going home. Corvane screamed, and the scream was in two voices, and one of the voices sounded almost relieved.

He collapsed. The wings of light guttered and died. The shadow-thing in the snow dissolved into ordinary darkness. What remained was a man on his knees in the ruined pass, steam rising off his cracked and cooling skin, gold light still leaking from the seams of him like heat from a dying forge.

Astrid moved first. She always moved first—that was the thing about her, Wren was learning. Everyone else stood frozen in the aftermath, wardens and remnant mages alike, and Astrid walked straight through the falling snow toward the smoking ruin of Corvane and knelt down in front of him.

"You fool," she said, and her voice broke on the second word. "You magnificent, stupid fool. I told you. I told you what it does."

Corvane looked up at her. The gold was draining from his eyes, leaving them ordinary brown, human, frightened. "I saw it," he whispered. "All of it. The whole world remembering. It was—it was *beautiful*, Montrose. Why won't it let us keep beautiful things?"

"Because we break them." Astrid's hand hovered over his cracked cheek, not quite touching. "Because keeping isn't ours to do. Only carrying."

Behind them, Veyra stepped forward, blade still drawn, her face a battlefield of things Wren couldn't read—triumph and horror and something older than either. "Step away from him, Montrose. He's broken the statutes a hundred times over. He dies by the law."

"He dies regardless," Astrid said, without turning around. "Your law has nothing left to take."

And Corvane laughed—a real laugh, cracked and wet and horribly human—and gripped Astrid's wrist with a hand that was already going gray at the fingertips. "Listen to her, Inquisitor. Listen. Your statutes, my war, her hiding—fifteen years of everyone deciding what the flame should be, and it turns out it decides for itself." Blood-flecked light bubbled at his lips. "It's going home. Did you feel it? It's going *home*, and it doesn't need any of us."

The seam in the mountain pulsed. Deeper now, wider, breathing slow and vast, and through it Wren felt the ember in her own chest lean like a compass needle finding north.

The flame had returned to the dragon. But her ember—the seed, the carrier, the piece that had traveled through bloodlines and dead hands and fifteen years of hiding to reach her—her ember was still here, still beating against her ribs like a second heart, and it was pulling.

Toward the seam. Toward the dark warm corridors beyond it, where corridors of forgotten memory waited, and where, somewhere at the end of them, a resting heart the size of a cathedral beat once a century and waited for a bearer strong enough to speak the First Tongue whole.

Wren looked at Astrid. Astrid was already looking at her, and Wren saw the exact moment the understanding arrived—saw it land in Astrid's face like a stone dropped into still water, ripples spreading outward through everything: her eyes, her mouth, the set of her shoulders.

"Oh," Astrid said softly. "Oh, no. No, Wren, there has to be another way."

There wasn't. They both knew it. The choir had told them the price at the threshold, and the price had not changed, and the flame was guttering, and the season was nearly spent.

But that reckoning belonged to the next hour, and the hour after that. For this moment, there was only the mountain settling, the snow falling into the seam's red glow, Veyra lowering her blade with the expression of a woman whose entire architecture of certainty had just been demolished, and Corvane—dying, human, unrepentant to the last—reaching up with his graying hand to touch Wren's face.

"Speaker," he rasped. "Finish it. Finish what cowards couldn't."

Wren took Astrid's hand instead.

Together, they turned toward the opening mountain, and the light inside it rose to meet them like a tide.
