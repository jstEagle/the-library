## 19. The Bearer's Price

The mountain did not have a door. It had a wound.

Astrid stood before it in the grey hour before dawn, Wren's small hand locked in hers, and watched the seam of stone where Corvane's stolen fire had cracked Dragonrest open like an egg. Heat breathed out of the gap—not fire's heat but memory's heat, the warmth of ten thousand mornings that had happened to other people in other lives. The choir sang inside it, faint now, a candle guttering in a draft.

*You are late,* the voices said, and even their reproach sounded like welcome.

"I know," Astrid said aloud. Her voice came out scraped thin. She hadn't slept. None of them had. Tam's body was two miles down the pass, wrapped in his own coat because there had been nothing else to wrap him in, and every time she closed her eyes she saw the moment the bolt took him—the way he'd turned first, deliberately, putting his back between the crossbowmen and the child. The relic dealer who'd spent thirty years selling other people's pasts had spent his last one buying a future for a girl he'd known four days.

Wren tugged her hand. Looked up. In the pre-dawn light the flame-mark over the child's heart glowed through her shirt like a coal under ash, and Astrid could see how much dimmer it burned than it had a week ago. How much *smaller*. Wren's lips were cracked. Her eyes had shadows under them deep enough to drown in. When she spoke—and she spoke less and less now, hoarding what strength remained—her words came out in the First Tongue more often than not, as if the language were eating everything else in her mouth.

She signed something with her free hand. The old shorthand they'd built on the road: *Are you sure?*

Astrid laughed, or made a sound near enough to it that the mountain probably couldn't tell the difference.

"No," she said. "Absolutely not. Not even slightly."

Then she knelt, so their eyes were level, and began to say goodbye.

* * *

There was no time for ceremony. That was the cruelty of it. Somewhere below them in the pass, Veyra was climbing with a squad of wardens who no longer answered to any council, carrying instruments designed to snuff the flame permanently—which meant, as far as Astrid understood the arithmetic, killing whatever lived in Wren's chest and taking most of the world's buried truth down with it. And somewhere behind *them*, limping and blistered and burning from the inside out, came Corvane, who wanted the same door Astrid wanted, for reasons that ended thrones and began wars.

She had maybe an hour. Maybe less.

So she sat down on the cold stone at the mountain's threshold with Wren across from her, and she unpacked her satchel the way she used to unpack it at market stalls fifteen years ago—except this time the wares were herself.

"Here." The maps first. She'd kept them wrapped in oilcloth since Greyhollow, since Tam's shop, since before. She unwrapped them now and spread them across her knees one at a time, and Wren leaned in, and for a few minutes they were just a mapmaker and a girl looking at impossible coastlines.

"This one I drew when I was nineteen." A shoreline that curled into spirals, bays nested inside bays like the chambers of a shell. "I told everyone it was the coast north of Vellmark. It wasn't anywhere. It was the inside of my own head, and I didn't know that yet." She smoothed the paper flat with her palm. "Keep it anyway. If you ever find a place shaped like this, you'll know I got somewhere right."

Wren took it with both hands, careful as a communicant.

"The harbor charts next. The ones with the depth-soundings nobody's ever taken." Astrid had drawn those during the worst year of her hiding, when the silence pressed so hard she'd thought she might go mad in her little shop among her harmless charms. "They're true, by the way. All the depths are real. I don't know how I knew them. That's the thing about us—we always knew things we weren't supposed to."

She talked, and Wren listened, and Astrid understood that she wasn't really giving the girl maps. She was giving her the shape of a person. Because tomorrow—or an hour from now, or whenever the mountain finished with her—Astrid Montrose would walk into the dragon's heart and feed it her memories piece by piece until there was no one left who remembered drawing spirals at nineteen, and the only version of her that survived would be the one standing in front of her holding paper.

"You'll want to know what I'm leaving out," Astrid said. "Don't. Whatever you invent about me will be better than the truth. The truth is mostly a woman hiding in a shop being afraid for fifteen years." She paused. "And four days that weren't."

Wren's chin trembled. She was trying very hard, Astrid saw, to be the grown-up in this conversation, the way children do when the adults around them start saying unbearable things calmly. She signed: *It isn't fair.*

"No," Astrid agreed. "Nothing about you has been fair since the day someone put a dragon's death in your chest. Fairness is a story we tell children so they sleep." She reached out and tucked a strand of mud-stiff hair behind Wren's ear. "But here's another story. The order I belonged to—they had a saying. *What is given freely is never lost; it only changes hands.* I thought it was nonsense when I was a novice. Pretty words to make sacrifice sound like bookkeeping." Her throat tightened. "It turns out it's the truest sentence anyone ever taught me. Everything I'm giving you, Wren—it doesn't disappear. It just changes hands. You're the ledger now."

* * *

The name was harder than the maps.

"You'll need my name," Astrid said. They had moved closer to the wound in the rock; the choir's song was louder here, and under it, faintly, a vast slow breathing, like the tide of some ocean a thousand miles beneath the stone. "Not for sentiment. For practicality. People will ask who taught you the Tongue, who found the dragon, whose maps you carry. Tell them Astrid Montrose. Tell them she was a sigil-bearer of the Choir of Embers, the last one, and that she was afraid almost all the time and did the necessary things anyway." She smiled crookedly. "Tell them she was stubborn. That part's load-bearing."

Wren shook her head. Signed, fierce and fast: *I won't forget you. You can't make me.*

"Wren—" Astrid caught the small hands, stilled them. "That's exactly backwards. Listen to me. The price isn't that *you* forget me. The price is that *I* forget me. Everything I am goes into the flame so the flame has something to burn besides itself—besides *you*. That's the whole point. That's the trade." She lifted Wren's chin. "You keep everything. That's your burden now, and I'm sorry for it, and it's also the greatest gift anyone in this broken world is going to receive. Fifteen years ago the Purge tried to burn every memory of magic out of existence, and it nearly worked, because the people who carried those memories died holding them instead of handing them on. You won't die holding them. You'll *teach*. That's the difference. That's the whole war, won or lost, right there—in whether memory gets handed on or buried."

The choir stirred at that. *Bearer-heir,* the voices murmured, approving, and Astrid felt the word land on her shoulders like a yoke fitted to her exact measurements.

"But," Wren signed slowly, and then, because signing wasn't enough, she spent precious strength and rasped it aloud in the First Tongue—a short phrase, three syllables, and Astrid felt each one land on her skin like a struck bell, waking sigils she'd already begun to surrender.

*I will remember for both of us.*

Astrid closed her eyes. Of course. Of course the child would say it that way, in the old tongue, where promises were structural—where a vow spoken in the First Tongue didn't describe a fact but *built* one, load-bearing as an arch. Wren had just made herself a pillar. Had just sworn, in the language that bound dragons to memory, that whatever Astrid lost, the loss would be witnessed. That someone would stand outside the forgetting and keep faith with the woman walking into it.

"That's cheating," Astrid said, voice cracking. "That is absolutely cheating, and it's the kindest thing anyone has ever done to me, and I want you to know I see it."

Wren smiled. It transformed her whole exhausted face.

* * *

They heard Veyra's wardens before they saw them—bootfall on scree, the iron discipline of it, forty minutes below and climbing hard. And behind that, farther off, a wrongness in the mountain's trembling that could only be Corvane, dragging his half-burned ascension up the other flank. The hour had shrunk to minutes.

Astrid stood. Brushed off her knees. Faced the wound in the rock, and let herself feel it all at once, quickly, the way you tear off a bandage: the terror, yes, and the grief, and underneath both—she was almost embarrassed to admit it, even privately—the relief. Fifteen years of hiding was ending. Whatever waited inside that mountain, it was not another small room where she'd have to pretend to be nothing. She would rather be unmade honestly than preserved falsely. She suspected she'd been walking toward that conclusion her entire adult life without letting herself read the map.

"One more thing," she said, turning back to Wren. "When it's done—if it works, if the dragon wakes and gives the memory back gently, the way the choir says it might—people will change. They'll wake up knowing things. Old griefs, old truths, the suppressed history of the Purge, all of it loose in the world again like weather." She crouched one last time, eye level. "Some of it will be ugly. Some of it will be about people they loved. Your job—your *only* job—is to teach them to hold it without burning each other. Can you do that?"

Wren nodded. Then hesitated, and signed one more question, small and devastating: *Will it hurt you?*

Astrid considered lying. Decided that a woman about to pay with her entire self could afford one honest answer.

"Yes," she said. "I think it will hurt. But I think it will hurt the way a long-held breath hurts when you finally let it out. Like something that needed to happen for years finally happening." She kissed the top of Wren's head, mud and smoke and all. "Now go to the ledge above. High ground. Whatever comes out of this mountain, you watch it from somewhere safe, and you stay *you*, and you live a long life full of unfair, beautiful, ordinary days. That's an order from the last sigil-bearer of the Choir of Embers."

Wren held on for one more second—one fierce, crushing, child-sized second—and then let go, and climbed, and turned at the top to watch.

Astrid faced the wound alone.

The choir rose to meet her, and its song was different now—no longer the echo of the dead but something older, vaster, the dragon's own dreaming turning toward her through corridors of stone. *Bearer,* it said. *Come and be spent.*

She stepped into the mountain.

The light took her gently, the way dusk takes a field, and far above on the ledge a child stood watching the place where she'd vanished, one hand pressed flat over the ember in her chest, keeping the promise she'd sworn in a language older than grief—

—as, deep in the dark, the first memory left Astrid Montrose: her mother's kitchen, sunlight on copper pots, gone like breath from a mirror.
