## 20. Into the Dragon's Heart

The door in the mountain's throat was not a door.

It was a scale the size of a chapel door, black-green and veined with gold, and it opened the way an eye opens—without hinges, without sound, folding inward to reveal a corridor of light that was not light. Wren stood at its edge, small and mud-streaked, her hand pressed flat against Astrid's palm.

"You don't have to watch," Astrid said.

Wren's answer came in the First Tongue, three syllables that meant: *I will remember you for both of us.*

Astrid almost smiled. Almost. "That's the whole point," she said. "I need someone to."

She stepped through.

* * *

The corridor behind the scale was made of memory—not a metaphor, not a dream. The walls were memory. They were built from it, mortared with it, lit by it: ten thousand years of forgotten things stacked in strata like river silt. Astrid walked and the walls breathed around her, and she saw what they were made of.

A harvest festival in a country whose name had been scrubbed from every map. A mother's lullaby sung in a tongue no living throat remembered. The exact blue of a sky on the last morning before a city burned—the sky itself preserved here, whole, because no one else had bothered to keep it.

The dragon had hoarded everything the world chose to forget.

"Hello," Astrid said aloud, because silence felt like disrespect. Her voice echoed strangely, as though the corridor were tasting it. "I've come to give something back."

The flame stirred beneath her ribs.

Since the pass—since Tam's body going still in the snow with his ledger clutched to his chest, since Veyra's wardens turning back at the threshold, since Corvane's stolen portion of fire burning itself hollow out of him like a moth eating its own cocoon—the flame had ridden inside her, patient and terrible, waiting for her to keep her word.

Now it pulsed once, and the first payment came due.

* * *

The Choir of Ashes met her at the first junction—a hundred voices braided into one, the echo of every mage who had burned in the Purge, bound into the dragon's memory like insects in amber.

*Bearer-heir,* they sang. *You came.*

"I came." She kept walking. "Don't make it ceremonial. I'll lose my nerve."

*Nerve is the first thing we take,* said a voice she recognized. Sister Maren, who had taught her the grounding sigils, who had burned on the second night of the Purge while Astrid ran. *We start small. We start kind. That's how the price stays payable.*

"Then be unkind," Astrid said. "Start with the order."

The flame rose in her chest like water finding a drain.

And the Order of the Quiet Hand left her.

Not all at once. In pieces, the way a house comes down. First the doctrine—the nine vows recited in Latin-cadenced First Tongue, gone like breath off glass. Then the faces: Brother Callum laughing with ink on his fingers; the Abbess's hands folded over her sigil-scarred wrists; Maren herself, young, scolding Astrid for sloppy linework in the margins of her practice charts. Astrid felt each one lift free and pour down the corridor's walls, where it settled into the strata and glowed briefly gold before dimming to permanence.

She had expected grief. What she got was worse: absence with edges. She could feel the shapes of the things leaving, could feel the holes they left behind, smooth and clean, already healed over with something that was not sadness and was not relief. Just room.

"That's it?" she asked the choir. "I just—don't miss them?"

*You can't miss what isn't there,* Maren's echo said gently. *Keep walking, Astrid. There is so much further to fall.*

* * *

The corridors narrowed. Or she diminished—it was impossible to tell which. The memories in the walls grew older, and older things were heavier; walking past them felt like wading upstream through cold water.

Her childhood went next.

She had thought she'd fight harder for that one. She had braced herself at the junction where the walls turned the color of wheat fields and low stone farmhouses, and she had told herself: *not this, keep this, this one I keep.* But when the flame reached for it, hungry and warm as a child's hand, she found she couldn't refuse. Refusal wasn't in her anymore. Somewhere between the pass and the threshold she had stopped being a woman making a choice and become a vessel keeping its word.

Her mother's kitchen went. The smell of bread, then the bread itself, then the kitchen, then the mother—her face dissolving off the wall of Astrid's mind like wet chalk. Her father's voice teaching her letters. The scar on her knee from falling out of the pear tree. The pear tree. The field. The name of the village.

*Astrid Montrose,* said the choir, testing the name like a coin bitten for gold. *Shall we take the name too?*

"Not yet," she whispered. "Let me carry it to the end. Let me know who's paying."

*As you wish. It will be waiting at the heart.*

She walked on, lighter now in a way that frightened her more than any weight ever had. She tried to recall her mother's face and found a warm shapeless glow where a face should be, and she understood, distantly, clinically, that she would never again know what her mother looked like—and that the understanding didn't hurt, and that was the cruelest part. Grief needed a hook. They were taking the hooks.

*This is mercy,* the choir murmured, reading her. *This is also horror. Both are true. Keep walking.*

* * *

There were others in the corridors.

She began to notice them at the third hour or third year—time moved strangely here—shadows standing in alcoves, facing the walls. Bearers who had come before. Not carriers like the ones in Tam's ledger, who had ferried the flame from hand to hand and died for it, but bearers: those who had entered the dragon intending to finish the journey and failed somewhere along the way, unwilling to pay the full price, stranded among their own half-remembered lives.

One of them turned as she passed. A man in the rotted finery of an empire two ages dead. His eyes were the worst thing she had seen since Greyhollow—eyes full of holes, eyes that had eaten themselves looking for something to remember.

"How far did you get?" she asked.

He considered. "I kept my hatred," he said at last. "It was all I had left, so I kept it, and it kept me. I've been standing here four hundred years hating the dragon for asking." He smiled, and there was nothing behind it. "Hate is very durable, little bearer. Feed it nothing and it thrives."

"I'll note that," Astrid said, and walked faster.

Another shadow, farther in, was a woman in grey robes with a sigil-burned brand on her wrist that Astrid's hands recognized before her emptied mind did—an old sister of her own order, centuries back, from when the Quiet Hand was young. The woman was humming. When she saw Astrid she stopped, and her ruined eyes filled with something like joy.

"You're the one," she said. "The last one. You'll speak it whole?"

"If I get there."

"We all spoke pieces," the woman said. "Every bearer gave what she could afford and kept what she couldn't, and the flame stayed broken, and the world stayed forgetting. Do you understand? Every partial payment bought another century of hiding. Another purge. Another order of children learning sigils in cellars and dying for it." She reached out—her hand passed through Astrid's arm like smoke through smoke. "Pay everything. Be no one. It's the only door."

"I'm trying," Astrid said.

"No," the woman said softly. "You're grieving. Trying comes after. Go on. I'll hum you forward."

And she did—a fragment of a hymn Astrid no longer knew but her feet apparently did, because they kept moving, steady, toward the heart.

* * *

The last stretch was the hardest, because it was the easiest.

The corridor widened into a chamber, and the chamber was not a chamber but a vast slow beating dark—the dragon's resting heart, a cathedral of muscle and memory, and at its center the flame's true body waited: the ember Wren had carried, magnified, ancient, folded through with ten thousand years of stored remembering. It hung in the dark like a held breath.

The flame inside Astrid's chest leaned toward it the way a river leans toward the sea.

"One more thing," the choir said, and its voice was gentler now, almost apologetic. "The last of your past. You saved the best for last, bearer. Shall we?"

Astrid closed her eyes.

Veyra.

Fifteen years of Veyra rose up whole: two novices on the abbey roof, sharing contraband plums, arguing about whether magic was a duty or a disease. Veyra's laugh—Astrid had forgotten she'd forgotten it—Veyra's laugh, quick and startled, like a bird getting up off water. Veyra the night the Purge came, standing in the burning courtyard in a warden's black, saying *run, Astrid, run, don't make me watch* —the only mercy anyone in that uniform had shown. Veyra in the market district three weeks ago, blades drawn, offering a child's life for a lie, and Astrid lying anyway, and meaning it.

*I hate you. I love you. I don't know which one kept me alive longer.*

The flame gathered itself around the memory like a hand closing over a candle.

"Wait," Astrid said.

Everything stilled. Even the heart's slow pulse seemed to pause.

"I want to say goodbye properly," she said. "Before I don't know who I'm saying it to."

She stood in the dark of the dragon's heart, holding the last thing she owned, and she spoke—to Veyra, who would never hear it, who was miles away and hollowing out in some grey office, ordering her wardens home from a war she'd finally admitted she'd lost.

"You were my best friend," Astrid said. "Whatever you became, whatever we did to each other—you were the person I'd have given anything to, and the person I'd have taken anything from, and I think that's the same thing. I hope the world comes back gentle enough for you to rest in. I hope someday you draw a map of a place that doesn't exist and feel guilty about it."

The memory flared—warm, unbearable, hers—and then poured out of her like water through open fingers, streaming down into the heart, joining the hoard, glowing gold among ten thousand years of kept things.

And then it was done, and she was no one.

Not dead. Not empty exactly. Empty implies a container, and the containers were gone too—her name, her history, her griefs, her loves, the fifteen years and the fifteen years before them, all paid out, all settled into the strata of the world's memory where they would wait, safe, for the dragon to wake and spend them.

What remained stood in the dark. It had hands. It knew, distantly, that it had come here to do one thing.

"Do it," the no-one said, in a voice that used to belong to someone named Astrid.

* * *

She raised those hands, and she spoke the First Tongue whole.

Not learned—spoken, because the language had always lived in the flame and the flame lived in her now, and speaking it was less like talking than like bleeding. The words came up through her from somewhere older than her throat, syllables that predated cities, that had named the mountains before the mountains finished rising. Each one cost her something she no longer had, and paid out something the world had lost, and the chamber filled with light that was not fire but remembering—

The hoarded years unlocked. The forgotten names re-inscribed themselves on the air. The harvest festival bloomed; the lullaby sang itself; the blue morning of the burned city spread wide and blameless across the dark. Everything the dragon had swallowed to keep safe came pouring home.

And beneath her, vast beyond reckoning, the dragon began to stir.

Not to rise—not yet, not in fury, not as Corvane had dreamed it and died dreaming it. It stirred the way a sleeper turns toward a window before dawn. Its heartbeat slowed, deepened, softened, gathering itself for one final act of giving rather than one more age of holding.

Astrid felt the flame leave her—or felt herself dissolve into it, the distinction having quietly ceased to matter—and for one suspended moment she was everywhere the memory went, riding the great exhale out into the world: into Greyhollow's flooded undercity where wardens were pulling survivors from the water; into a farmhouse kitchen where a woman hummed a song she didn't know she knew; into a grey office where Veyra sat alone with a resignation letter and, suddenly, inexplicably, the taste of plums on her tongue.

Then the moment closed, and the no-one that had been Astrid Montrose drifted down through layers of gold and dark, coming to rest in the deepest stratum, among the oldest kept things, where the dragon stores what it means to keep forever.

Somewhere far above, a scale the size of a chapel door was folding open.

Someone small was calling a name down the corridor—the name Astrid had asked to keep until the end, the name that had been waiting at the heart, and which the heart, being merciful after all, had sent back up instead:

"Astrid! ASTRID!"

Footsteps, running. A child's voice, fluent now in the language of dragons, shouting words that meant *come back, whoever you are, come back.*

The no-one turned toward the light.

It did not remember her.

But its feet, apparently, did.
