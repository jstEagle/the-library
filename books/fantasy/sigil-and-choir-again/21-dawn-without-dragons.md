## 21. Dawn Without Dragons

Wren felt the mountain breathe before she saw anything move.

It came up through the stone floor of the pass like a tide returning to a shore it had abandoned—slow, enormous, patient. She was sitting where Astrid had left her, wrapped in Tam's old coat, her hands still pressed flat against the rock the way Astrid had shown her. *Keep the door open,* Astrid had said. *Don't follow me in. Just keep it open.*

So Wren kept it open.

Inside the dragon's heart, somewhere beyond walls of folded memory, Astrid Montrose was giving herself away piece by piece. Wren could feel it happening—not as images, not as words, but as a kind of lightness spreading through the flame she carried. Every time Astrid let go of something, the ember over Wren's heart burned a little brighter and a little less afraid.

She had stopped crying an hour ago. Or maybe two hours. Time moved strangely on Dragonrest. The stars above the pass did not wheel the way stars were supposed to; they hung fixed, as if the whole sky were holding its breath along with everything else.

Then the mountain breathed, and the sky remembered how to move.

The first thing that changed was the cold. It lifted—not all at once, but the way frost lifts off a window when someone breathes on the other side. The air went soft. Somewhere below them, in the dark folds of the mountain, water began to move where water had been still for centuries.

Wren scrambled to her feet, coat flapping, and looked down into the great hollow of the Dragonrest threshold—and the stone began to glow.

Not firelight. Nothing so simple. The glow came from everywhere and nowhere, seeping up out of the rock itself, and with it came *voices*. Not the choir's desperate echo-chorus that had haunted the caverns below—that was gone now, released, its singers finally allowed to finish their songs. What rose through the mountain now was quieter. Older. A sound like ten thousand conversations happening at once in a language that predated language, and underneath it, steady as a heartbeat:

the dragon, waking.

Wren spoke without meaning to. The First Tongue poured out of her the way breath does, unasked-for, unstoppable: *"Vessah korath ilen. Vessah korath ilen."*

*Wake gently. Wake gently.*

The mountain heard her.

---

What happened next, no one had taught her. There was no lesson for it, no sigil, no rite. The dragon's memory simply began to come home—but not the way Corvane had wanted it to come home, torn loose and swallowed whole. Not the way Veyra had feared, unleashed as fire to burn the statutes and the burners alike off the face of the world.

It came home the way rain comes home.

It fell upward first—or that's how it looked to Wren, standing at the threshold with her mouth open and her eyes streaming. Threads of light lifting out of the mountain's skin, drifting into the night air, scattering on winds that had been waiting fifteen years for something worth carrying. Each thread was a memory. Wren knew this the way she knew her own name, because the First Tongue was memory given voice, and she could read them as they passed.

A thread drifted past her cheek, and she tasted a wedding in a city that no longer existed—the bride's nervous laughter, the smell of orange peel and woodsmoke, the groom stepping on the hem of her dress and both of them laughing harder.

Another thread: a farmer teaching his daughter the names of clouds, back when knowing the names of clouds had been a kind of magic too, before anyone decided magic needed laws.

Another: a novice's trembling hands lighting her first candle-wish, years before the Purge, before the pyres, before everything broke.

They rose by the thousands. By the tens of thousands. Fifteen years of hoarded grief, and behind that grief, centuries—everything the dragon had folded into itself when it chose to die instead of fight, every truth the world had buried to keep its peace. It all went up into the night sky like seeds from a shaken tree, and the wind took it, and carried it south, toward Greyhollow, toward every grey town and frightened village between here and the sea.

Not as fire. As weather.

Wren understood then what Astrid must have understood inside the dark, walking those corridors of forgetting: that the world didn't need its memories handed back like weapons. It needed them to fall like rain—everywhere at once, gentle enough to soak in, impossible to refuse.

*"Sleep well,"* she said in the First Tongue, and meant it more than she had ever meant anything. *"Senneth varo. Sleep, and be only yourself."*

Deep beneath the stone, vast beyond any word she had, the dragon turned over once—she felt it in her teeth, in her ribs, in the flame at her heart—and settled.

And slept.

Truly slept. For the first time since before Wren was born, the thing at the center of the world was not waiting, not watching, not holding its breath against the day someone would need it. It let go, the way a tired traveler lets go at last upon reaching home, and the mountain grew quiet around its sleeping, and the stars overhead finally, finally began to move again.

---

Corvane burned out at dawn.

Wren saw it happen from the ridge above the pass, where the remnants' survivors had gathered—what was left of them, a few dozen hollow-eyed men and women who had followed a prophet into the mountains and watched him become something else. They kept their distance from her. After what they'd seen, after what he'd become, they wanted nothing near the child who carried what he had stolen.

He lay in the shadow of the pass where the warden assault had broken against him, and he was still huge—that was the horror of it, that even dying he remained the monstrous thing the stolen flame had made him, twenty feet of twisted bone and light. But the light was going out of him. Wren could see it guttering, the same way the dragon's flame had guttered in her own chest weeks ago, because the flame did not tolerate thieves. It never had. That was written in the oldest verses, the ones the choir sang beneath Greyhollow: *the flame is not power. The flame is trust.* And Corvane had never trusted anything in his life except his own grievance.

As the sun crested the eastern peaks, he shrank.

It was slow, and it was terrible, and Wren made herself watch because someone should. The mountain of him folded down and down, the stolen centuries pouring out of him like light through a cracked jar, rising to join the great exodus of memory already filling the sky. He screamed once—not in rage, Wren thought, but in something worse. Recognition. He had wanted to be the new dragon-god, eternal and hoarding, and instead the flame had shown him exactly what he was: a man, an old angry man, holding nothing.

When it was done, there was just a body in the scree. Small. Grey. Ordinary.

One of the remnant women walked down to him—Maretta, Wren thought, the one who had slipped her extra bread at the camp and never met her eyes while doing it. Maretta knelt beside the body for a long moment. Then she pulled her cloak off and covered his face, and stood, and did not look at Wren at all as she turned away.

No one followed Corvane anymore. There was nothing left to follow.

Wren sat down on the cold stone and realized her legs were shaking, and let them shake, and did not speak the First Tongue or do anything magical at all. She just sat, a small girl on a mountain in the early light, while the wind carried other people's weddings and harvests and candle-wishes south toward the world.

---

Veyra came at midmorning.

Wren heard the wardens before she saw them—boots on stone, many boots, moving carefully the way soldiers move when they've stopped believing in what they're marching toward. She stood up and waited at the threshold, because running was finished. Everything was finished, one way or another.

Inquisitor Veyra climbed the last stretch of the pass alone.

Her wardens halted at the base of the ridge, thirty of them, their iron badges dull in the daylight. Their captain started forward, and Veyra raised one hand without looking back, and he stopped. She climbed on by herself, each step deliberate, her grey cloak snapping in the wind off the peaks. Up close, Wren saw what the wind had been hiding: Veyra looked emptied. Not defeated—emptied, like a house whose furniture has been carried out room by room until only the walls remain.

She stopped six paces away. Close enough to take Wren if she wanted. She didn't seem to want anything at all anymore.

"You're the child," Veyra said.

"I'm Wren."

"Astrid—" The name caught in Veyra's throat like a fishhook. She looked past Wren, at the glowing threshold, at the threads of memory still drifting up into the brightening sky. "She went in."

"Yes."

"And she's not coming out."

"No," Wren said. "But she's not gone either. That's the part you don't understand yet. Nobody goes into the dragon's heart and disappears. They become part of what comes back down." She touched her own chest, over the flame-mark. "You'll meet her again. Everyone will. You just won't know it's her."

Veyra stared at her for a long time. Then, quietly, in a voice stripped of fifteen years of statute and sermon: "I knew her when she was younger than you."

"I know," Wren said. "She remembers you." A pause. "Remembered," she corrected, and hated the correction, and made it anyway, because the First Tongue did not permit comfortable lies. "Until the end."

Something moved through Veyra's face then—grief, yes, but underneath the grief something older and more exhausted, the look of a woman setting down a weight she had convinced herself was holy. Wren had seen that look before. In the mirror. On the night she'd understood what the flame would cost.

"Stand down," Veyra said—not to Wren. To the wardens below, loud enough to carry on the wind. "All squads. Stand down. We're going home."

The captain's voice floated up, confused: "Inquisitor, the statutes—"

"The statutes are ash," Veyra said. "Look at the sky, Captain. Tell me what your law is going to do about *that*." She let the silence sit. "We burned people for fifteen years to prevent a war, and the war never came, and the burning was the only war there ever was. I built that. I'll answer for it. Go home to your families. Tell them the truth. It's the least any of us can do now—it's the only coin left that's worth anything."

There was a long stillness on the mountainside. Then, one by one, the wardens turned. Some of them wept as they walked. Some laughed—a strange, unhinged, relieved laughter, the sound of men putting down swords they'd forgotten were heavy. They filed down out of the pass and away toward the low country, and the mountain let them go.

Veyra stayed a moment longer. She looked at Wren—at the flame-mark, faintly visible now through the child's shirt, pulsing gently like a second heartbeat—and she did not reach for her irons, did not recite a single line of the statutes she had authored. She just looked, the way you look at a thing you spent your life fearing, discovering too late that it had been trying to save you all along.

"She used to draw maps," Veyra said. "At the novitiate. Places that didn't exist. She said someday she'd find them." Her voice frayed. "Tell her—"

"You can tell her yourself," Wren said. "Someday. When she comes back down as rain."

Veyra closed her eyes. When she opened them, she nodded once, turned, and descended the mountain alone, smaller with every step, until the pass swallowed her and she was gone.

---

They say it reached Greyhollow three days later.

Wren wasn't there, but she felt it leave—felt the great cloud of memory crest the southern ridgelines and begin its long fall across the lowlands, and she imagined it: falling over the burnt district first, because burnt ground drinks deepest. Falling into the wardens' barracks, the market squares, the charm stalls, the flooded undercity where Tam's shop stood empty with its ledger still open on the counter. Falling into the cathedral where the statutes were carved into stone, softening the letters the way rain softens old carving, until one morning the stonemasons would arrive and find the laws illegible and no one able to say when it had happened.

Magic came back to the world the way spring comes back—no proclamations, no battles, no one in charge of it. A baker in some river town would find her loaves rising twice as high and think nothing of it. A midwife would hum a tune she couldn't remember learning and the laboring mother's pain would ease, and she'd call it luck. Children in Greyhollow would play a game with chalk marks on cobbles, drawing spirals that glowed faintly after sundown, and their mothers would frown and decide, eventually, that frowning was more exhausting than letting them.

Some of the returned would be frightened of it. Some would try to seize it, hoard it, legislate it. Wren knew that. The First Tongue was honest with her; it showed her the whole shape of things, including the ugly parts. Memory did not make people good. It only made them *possible*—possible to grieve, possible to change, possible to choose differently than the last generation chose.

That was enough. That had always been supposed to be enough.

On the fourth morning, Wren walked back down into the threshold one last time. The corridors inside were quiet now, their walls of woven memory thinned to gossamer, most of the treasure given away. But in the innermost chamber, at the place where the dragon's heart had beat for a thousand years, she found what Astrid had left for her: a satchel, dry and undisturbed amid all that ancient dust.

Inside were maps. Dozens of them—coastlines that didn't exist, cities drawn in careful ink, mountains labeled in Astrid's small sharp hand. And on top of the stack, a single sheet, folded twice, addressed in the same hand:

*For Wren. Read it slow.*

Wren sat down on the floor of the sleeping dragon's heart, in the last of the light her friend had paid for, and unfolded the page, and began to read.

*My name was Astrid Montrose. You will have to remind me of that...*

* * *

Outside, the rain of memory was falling on Greyhollow for the first time in fifteen years, and in a small room above an abandoned charm shop, a woman who had forgotten everything was asleep at her desk, dreaming of coastlines, her pen moving across the paper all on its own—drawing, for the very first time, a map of a place that actually existed.
