## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The thunder arrived on a Tuesday, which Emrys Delacroix considered typical. Thunder always arrived on Tuesdays. It was as though the storms themselves had read the Vellum Guild's delivery schedule and agreed to be punctual about it.

"Forty-one jars," announced the carter, a broad woman named Hesk whose forearms suggested she had personally wrestled most of what she delivered. She set the manifest on his desk with a thump that rattled his inkwell. "Forty-two if you count the one that's been grumbling since Crossford."

Emrys did not look up from his ledger immediately. This was a discipline he had practiced for eleven years: the art of not looking up. A man who looked up too quickly was a man who cared, and a man who cared invited questions, and questions were how clerks died.

"Grumbling," he repeated, in the flat tone of a man discussing grain tariffs.

"It's been muttering at the horses." Hesk crossed her arms. "I don't like it. The other forty are quiet as church bells. This one's got opinions."

Emrys signed for forty-one jars and made a small notation in the margin: *one (1) jar, vocal.* Then he looked up, because some things required looking at, and gave her the smile he kept for exactly this purpose — pleasant, forgettable, the smile of a man whose entire personality could be summarized as *competent with numbers*.

"The Guild thanks you for your safe delivery," he said. "There will be no complications."

"There had better not be," said Hesk. "Last time I hauled for you people, the cargo was a crate of bottled dawns, and one of them cracked open in the wagon bed. I didn't sleep properly for a week. Kept waking up convinced it was morning when it wasn't."

"That would have been logged," Emrys said soothingly, "as an incident."

"It was *my wagon*, is what it was."

He paid her, tipped her, and watched her go with the particular envy he reserved for people whose jobs were honest. Then he turned to the forty-one — no, forty-two — jars of thunder stacked in his receiving room, and began to work.

* * *

Auditing bottled thunder was, in fairness, better than auditing most of what came through the Vellum Guild's receiving house. Bottled thunder at least stayed where you put it. The same could not be said for the imported weather from the southern houses, or the memory-wine that had to be counted twice because it made you forget the first count, or — and this had been the worst fortnight of Emrys's professional life — the shipment of doors that opened onto places that were not rooms.

He checked each jar against the manifest. The glass was Guild-standard: thick, smoked, stamped with the Vellum seal that looked, if you squinted, like a quill and, if you squinted differently, like a knife. Emrys had spent years perfecting the squint that saw only the quill.

Jar by jar, he held each one to the light and listened.

This was the part of the job nobody warned you about, taking inventory of small impossibilities: you had to listen to them. A jar of thunder that had gone silent was a dead jar, worth nothing, and a jar that rattled its stopper was a live one, worth everything, and a jar that had begun to *hum* in a minor key was a jar you wrote up in red ink and sent down to the sealed vault before lunch, because thunder that hummed in a minor key was thunder that had started thinking about escape.

Thirty-eight jars: the low rolling growl of healthy storm, dormant and docile. He noted them in his neat clerk's hand, column after column, the numbers marching down the page like well-behaved soldiers.

Jar thirty-nine: silent. Dead stock. He marked it for the discount ledger with a small pang of sympathy. Even thunder deserved better than to end up sold off at half price to some provincial fireworks merchant.

Jar forty: humming. Minor key. Red ink.

And then there was jar forty-one — the grumbler, the one with opinions.

Emrys lifted it carefully and held it to the window light, and the thunder inside shifted, and pressed itself against the glass like a cat against a familiar hand, and said — not in words, never in words, but in the deep sub-audible grammar of storms — something that translated, roughly, to *you again.*

"I'm afraid so," Emrys murmured.

He froze.

That was the trouble. That was always the trouble. Eleven years of careful, deliberate unremarkableness, eleven years of being the third-most-forgettable man in a building full of forgettable men, and every few months some part of him slipped — a murmured word here, an instinctive gesture there — and he had to spend the rest of the day cataloguing who might have heard.

Nobody had heard. The receiving room was empty except for him and the junior clerk, Pell, who was currently in the corridor fetching tea and asking the kettle personal questions, because Pell asked everything personal questions.

Emrys set jar forty-one down gently. *You again,* the thunder had said, and the terrible thing, the thing he would never write in any ledger, was that the thunder was right. Storms remembered him. Storms had always remembered him, the way dogs remember the one person in the house who actually knows how to throw a stick.

Because Emrys Delacroix was not, despite all evidence, merely a clerk.

He was a swordspeaker — the last swordspeaker, though the guild that had once trained his kind believed the art extinct, hunted it extinct, burned it extinct — and his gift lived coiled behind his breastbone like a drawn breath he had been holding since he was nineteen years old. Swordspeech was not talking to swords, whatever the ballads claimed. It was speaking to the *edge* of things. To anything with an edge, or a point, or a cutting intent. Blades, yes, but also storms, which cut; and winter, which cut; and oaths, which cut deepest of all.

He had sworn, at nineteen, an oath that bound the gift shut — an oath with a sigil that slept on his left wrist under his sleeve, inked in silver and old blood, quiet as a scar. As long as he kept the oath, the gift stayed folded away, and he stayed alive, and the world stayed blessedly ignorant of him.

As long as he kept the oath.

Which meant, among its many clauses, that he did not speak to storms. Even politely. Even when they greeted him first.

"Forty-one jars received," he told the manifest firmly, and corrected his earlier note: *one (1) jar, vocal* became *one (1) jar, condition: agitated, recommend vault transfer.* Clerks did not converse with inventory. Clerks recommended things in writing. Clerks were safe.

His wrist, under the sleeve, itched.

It had been itching for three days now, a faint prickling like a limb gone to sleep, and Emrys had decided — in the way of men deciding things they cannot afford to believe — that it was the weather turning. Autumn coming on. Dry skin. Nothing more.

The sigil had not stirred in eleven years. It was not going to start stirring now, over a jar of bad-tempered thunder and a change of season.

He believed this approximately sixty percent, which was, for Emrys, an unusually optimistic figure.

* * *

Pell returned with the tea at half past ten, balancing two cups and a plate of seed cakes he had absolutely not been authorized to procure.

"Master Delacroix, I've done the tally on last week's moonlight consignment, and I think there's a discrepancy."

"Moonlight doesn't have discrepancies, Pell. Moonlight has evaporation."

"Eight gallons of it?"

"Evaporative moonlight. It's a known variety." Emrys took his tea. "Show me the figures anyway."

Pell spread his papers out on the corner of the desk, and Emrys reviewed them while pretending very hard not to notice that Pell was not looking at the figures either. Pell was twenty-three, earnest, and possessed of the single most dangerous quality a junior clerk at the Vellum Guild could possess: curiosity. Not the idle kind. The structural kind. The kind that eventually led a man to open drawers that were locked for reasons.

Pell had been asking questions for six months now. Small ones, at first. Why the sealed vault had three locks instead of the standard two. Why the vault registry was kept in a cipher rather than plain script. Why Master Quillon's office windows faced inward, toward the vault, rather than outward toward the street.

Emrys had answered each question with the two great tools of his survival: boredom and paperwork. *Regulatory redundancy. Historical convention. Architectural whim.* And each time, Pell had nodded and written something down, and Emrys had gone home and lain awake wondering whether he should request the boy's reassignment before the questions got big enough to kill him.

He never did request it. That was his one ongoing failure as a survivor, and he knew it, and he did it anyway, month after month, because Pell brought him seed cakes and laughed at his dry jokes and reminded him, in a way he refused to examine closely, of himself at twenty-three — before the oath, before the burning, before he had learned that the safest shape for a man to be was a shape nobody looked at twice.

"The discrepancy's real," Pell said. "See? Entry and exit don't match. Someone took more moonlight out than came in."

"Or someone recorded less coming in than went out."

"Why would they do that?"

"Because," Emrys said, "the difference between eight gallons recorded and eight gallons actual is called profit, and profit is why the Guild has a second ledger, and the second ledger is why we have jobs. Congratulations. You've discovered commerce."

Pell laughed, but then he did the thing Emrys dreaded. He lowered his voice, glanced at the door, and asked the question.

"Master Delacroix — what's actually *in* the sealed vault?"

The tea suddenly required Emrys's complete attention. He sipped it. It was too hot. He sipped it anyway, buying time in scalded-tongue increments.

"Inventory," he said finally. "Old inventory."

"Nobody's audited it in nine years. I checked. There's no record of a full count since before I was hired. Every other holding gets audited quarterly. Why not that one?"

"Because it's sealed."

"That's not a reason, that's a door."

Emrys set down his cup. He considered, briefly, the full truth: that the sealed vault contained the founding documents of the Vellum Guild, including certain charters written in swordspeech, including — if the rumors he'd spent a decade pretending not to hear were true — the original covenant that held the long winter at bay, signed by nine realms and then hidden by the guild that had been built, allegedly, to protect it. He considered explaining that the vault was sealed not to keep thieves out but to keep questions in, that the Guild's entire edifice rested on a foundation of paper that must never be read aloud, and that a curious junior clerk was therefore the most dangerous object in the building, more dangerous than any jar of thunder ever bottled.

What he said was: "Some inventories are historical. We keep records of things we no longer trade. Like a museum."

"Do museums lock their museums?"

"The good ones, at night."

Pell grinned. Emrys felt the grin land somewhere beneath his ribs and stay there, warm and unwelcome. *Reassign the boy,* said the survivalist in him, cool and clear. *Request it tomorrow. Quillon owes you a favor from the incident with the bottled dawn.*

"You're avoiding my question," Pell said cheerfully.

"I'm a clerk. Avoidance is my core competency."

But Pell's eyes had drifted past him, to the shelf where jar forty-one sat apart from its brothers, marked in red ink, waiting for the afternoon vault transfer. And Emrys saw the exact moment the boy's curiosity snagged on it — saw the question form, bright and fatal, behind his eyes.

"What's wrong with that one?"

"It's agitated."

"Thunder can be agitated?"

"All weather has moods. You'll learn this if you stay in trade another year."

"No, but—" Pell stepped closer, and Emrys's stomach performed a slow, cold roll, "—it's almost like it's *listening*. Look. When you talk, it presses against the glass."

And it did. Of course it did. Because storms remembered Emrys, and this one had spent the whole morning listening to his voice through forty jars of glass, and now it leaned toward the sound of him like a plant toward light, and Pell — sweet, doomed, observant Pell — had noticed.

"It responds to vibration," Emrys said. "Footfalls. Kettle noise. Write it up in the condition report."

"You weren't walking just now."

"Pell."

"Sorry, Master Delacroix." But he didn't look sorry. He looked fascinated, and he wrote something in his notebook, and Emrys thought, with the weary clarity of a man watching a fire start in a neighboring field: *that notebook is going to get us both killed.*

* * *

The afternoon passed in the small ceremonies of the trade. Emrys supervised the vault transfer of jar forty, the hummer, signing it over to the vault-keepers with the triple-countersigned chit that made everything legal and nothing safe. He reconciled the moonlight figures. He ate his lunch at his desk, as always, bread and cheese and a pickle, unremarkable food eaten in an unremarkable way by an unremarkable man.

At four o'clock, Master Quillon's secretary came through with the day's internal circular, and Emrys read it twice, because the first reading had seemed impossible and the second confirmed it.

*By order of the Master of Ledgers: effective immediately, all correspondence leaving the Guildhouse is to be copied to the archive before dispatch. All correspondence. No exceptions will be granted.*

The Guild did not copy correspondence. The Guild destroyed correspondence — promptly, thoroughly, and with a satisfaction bordering on liturgical. Burning letters was practically a sacrament around here. Emrys had once watched Quillon incinerate a trade negotiation mid-signature because the ink had smudged.

Copying everything meant the Guild wanted a record. And institutions only wanted records when they expected to need proof later — or when they expected the world that might ask for proof to stop existing.

His wrist itched. Harder now. A prickle, a warmth, a faint silver heat under the skin like sun through cloth.

Emrys rolled down his sleeve, and told himself it was autumn, and failed to believe it at the rate of ninety percent.

* * *

He worked late, as he always did — lateness was camouflage; a man bent over a ledger at dusk attracted no more attention than the furniture — and by the time he trimmed the lamp, the receiving house had gone quiet around him. Quiet, except for jar forty-one, still shelved overnight pending its own transfer, still pressing against its glass whenever he spoke, still muttering its low storm-thoughts in a language Emrys had sworn never to answer.

"One day," he told it softly, hating himself, unable to help it, "they'll ship you somewhere with proper skies, and you can say all of this to the rain."

The thunder rumbled something that was almost fond.

And then, from the direction of the sealed vault — three locks, cipher registry, nine years unaudited — came a sound that Emrys had never heard in eleven years of service, and hoped never to hear again.

A key. Turning.

Followed by voices, low and urgent, and the scrape of something heavy being moved in the dark — and underneath it all, faint as a dying star, a sound that raised every hair on his arms and made the sleeping sigil on his wrist flare suddenly, shockingly, painfully awake:

Someone, down in the vault, was reading the covenant aloud.

Emrys put out his lamp, and sat in the dark, and listened.
