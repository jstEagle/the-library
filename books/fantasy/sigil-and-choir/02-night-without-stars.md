## 2. Night Without Stars

The trouble with a life built on being unremarkable, Emrys Delacroix had learned, was that it required constant maintenance. Remarkableness happened *to* you. Unremarkableness was a discipline, like calligraphy or fencing — and he was better at the first two than he would ever admit aloud.

So at half past eleven that night, he sat in the Vellum Guild's counting room with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, reconciling the quarter's figures on bottled thunder, and he made himself bored.

It was not difficult work, exactly. It was *tedious* work, which was better. Bottled thunder arrived in crates of forty, each jar sealed in wax and packed in straw, and each jar lost approximately one dram of potency per week of storage — a fact the Guild's suppliers disputed and Emrys's ledgers proved with the quiet, merciless patience of arithmetic. He had spent six years proving things with arithmetic. Arithmetic never drew blood. Arithmetic never sang.

He dipped his pen. He wrote: *Crates 12 through 19, spoilage consistent with projection. Recommend renegotiation of supplier's "freshness" surcharge.* Somewhere below, the night watch called the hour in his flat voice, and Emrys felt the small, familiar satisfaction of a page balanced.

Then the junior clerk knocked.

Emrys knew it was Pell before the knock finished, because Pell knocked the way he did everything — too earnestly, three raps where two would do, as though the door might otherwise fail to understand its purpose.

"Enter," Emrys said, and did not look up. Looking up encouraged people.

"Master Delacroix." Pell came in carrying a ledger of his own and an expression like a dog that has found something buried and cannot decide whether to be proud or afraid. "I've finished the vault intake tallies. Mostly."

"*Mostly* is not a word that appears in finished tallies."

"No, Master Delacroix." Pell set the ledger down anyway, which was the whole problem with Pell: he obeyed instructions the way rivers obeyed banks, technically, while always looking for the soft spot. "There's a discrepancy. Vault seven received a delivery last month that isn't in any manifest I can find. Forty-one crates. No contents listed. No sender. Just a seal I don't recognize and a note saying *deferred audit, by order.*"

"Deferred audits are common."

"I've been here eight months," Pell said, "and I've processed four hundred deferred audits, and every single one eventually got audited. This one's marked *indefinite.*" He turned the ledger toward Emrys. "What's in vault seven?"

"The past," Emrys said, "mostly. Old contracts nobody wants to read twice." He kept his voice at the temperature of cold tea. Inside his chest, something older than either of them sat up slightly, the way a house settles when someone walks across the attic floor. "Pell. A piece of professional advice, offered free, which should tell you how much it's worth. In this trade, the questions you're paid to ask are worth asking. The others are expensive hobbies."

"And if the answer matters?"

"Then it'll cost even more." Emrys took back the ledger, made a small correcting mark he did not need, and closed it. "Log the discrepancy as pending. Pending is a fine place for things to live. Some of my favorite entries have been pending for years."

Pell lingered. Pell was constitutionally incapable of not lingering; it was one of the hundred small reasons Emrys both liked him and expected him to die young.

"It's a strange night," Pell said finally. "Have you noticed? The cats won't come inside. All of them. Mrs. Halloway's cat, the dock cats, all sitting in the street facing east, like they're waiting for something."

"Cats are always waiting for something. It's their entire profession."

"Not like this." Pell rubbed his arms. "And the wards along the border road — Tam says the blue in them has gone thin. Like watered wine. He says you can see the mortar of the world through them."

"Tam says a great many things. Last month he said the harbor master was a selkie, and the month before that he said the same about the harbor master's wife, which suggests less a gift for detection than a type."

Pell laughed, thanked him for nothing, and left. Emrys sat alone with his balanced page and listened to the house settle around him, and told himself the itch under his left cuff was wool.

It was not wool. It had not been wool for six years, but most nights it behaved like wool, and he had gotten very good at dressing it accordingly.

* * *

At midnight exactly, the world broke its own rules, and Emrys was still holding his pen, which is why the first thing he thought — absurdly, unforgivably — was *there goes the ink.*

The light changed first. Not dimmed — *changed*, the way a face changes when you learn something bad about its owner. The lamp flames went from gold to a gray-white with no warmth in it, and every shadow in the room sharpened as though the darkness had been waiting years for permission.

Then the sigil on his wrist woke up.

Six years it had slept — a faint silver scarring in the shape of a sword laid over a word, the oath-mark of an order the world believed extinct, because Emrys had worked very hard helping the world believe it. Six years of silence, of the mark lying against his skin like a coin left on a windowsill. Now it burned. Not painfully, at first. Painfully came later. At first it burned the way a name burns in your mouth when you haven't spoken it in years and then hear it called across a crowded room.

Emrys put down the pen very carefully, the way you do when your hands want to shake and you have decided they will not.

He crossed to the window.

The stars were gone.

Not clouded. Not occluded. *Gone* — the way teeth are gone from a mouth, leaving a shape you can't stop reading. He knew the sky over Corvane's Reach the way he knew his ledgers: the Wagoneer tilted over the eastern hills, the Sisters close together above the ward-towers, the slow red eye of the Hearthstar that sailors swore by. He looked for each of them by habit and found, in every case, nothing. A black so complete it wasn't a color anymore. It was an absence with weight, pressing down on the town like a lid.

Below, in the street, Mrs. Halloway's cat was still sitting facing east. So were forty other cats, and now also several dogs, and a horse that had broken its tether and stood trembling in the middle of the road with its head raised, ears forward, listening to something Emrys could almost — *almost* — hear underneath the ringing in his wrist. A sound like a choir holding one note too long. A sound like a contract being read aloud in a language made entirely of consequences.

"Oh," Emrys said quietly, to no one, in the voice of a man whose arithmetic has just stopped adding up. "Oh, you absolute fools. What did you *do?*"

Because here was the thing about bottled thunder, and smuggled relics, and impossible cargo moving through failing border wards on Vellum Guild manifests: somebody had to sign for all of it. Somebody had to keep the books. And Emrys had kept those books for six years, telling himself that clerks were innocent, that ink was neutral, that a man who only recorded the smuggling was not himself a smuggler. He had built his unremarkable life out of that distinction, brick by careful brick.

Tonight the distinction was burning off his wrist like morning fog.

The sigil flared again — harder now, hot enough that he hissed and shoved his sleeve up. The silver lines had gone from scarred to luminous, and beneath them, faint as a pulse under skin, something moved. Words. Oath-words, the old cadence, the one that began *by starlight witnessed* —

By starlight. Which there no longer was any of.

"That's not possible," Emrys informed his wrist. His wrist declined to argue, which from a wrist was somehow worse.

Down the corridor, doors were banging open. Voices — the night staff, the under-clerks, old Mistress Corrow who had worked the Guild's receiving desk since before Emrys could write cursive. He heard her voice crack across the commotion like a whip: "*Not that way!* Nobody leaves! Master Quillon's orders — the hall is sealed until dawn!"

Sealed. Of course it was sealed. Emrys leaned his forehead briefly against the cold window glass and did the calculation he'd been avoiding for six years, the one where the numbers were people and the sum was him.

If the guild locked its doors within minutes of the sky going dark, then the guild had known the sky was going to go dark. You did not draft emergency orders between midnight and midnight-and-five. You drafted them in advance. You filed them. You probably, if you were the Vellum Guild, *audited* them.

Which meant the wards hadn't failed.

They'd been *closed.*

* * *

By half past midnight, the courtyard of the Vellum Guildhall had become the strangest inventory Emrys had ever witnessed: a bonfire of correspondence.

They brought the letters out in armfuls — decades of them, contracts and confirmations and the endless polite tissue of trade — and fed them into the great iron brazier normally reserved for melting down damaged seals. Masters and clerks alike stood in a ring around it, faces lit orange from below, feeding the fire with the grim efficiency of people doing a chore they'd rehearsed. Nobody spoke except to say "more" or "the second drawer, not the third."

Emrys stood at the edge of the ring with a bundle of letters in his own hands and burned them one by one, and read every single one first, because he was who he was.

Most were nothing. Grain tariffs. A complaint about the quality of wax. An apology to a client in Harrowmere for a late delivery of — he squinted — *nine jars of preserved applause*, which had been a strange week. He fed these to the fire without ceremony.

But three letters, pulled from the bottom of the third drawer, were written in cipher he recognized, because he had invented a version of it himself, once, in another life, with other people. Three letters, dated over the past year, all referencing something called *the Quiet Sky* and a date. Tonight's date.

He burned them too. Burning them was the correct procedure. Burning them was what a loyal clerk did. He watched the cipher curl and blacken and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm: *they are destroying the evidence of a crime, and I am helping, and the crime is currently happening above us, in the dark, where the stars used to be.*

"Delacroix."

Mistress Corrow, at his elbow, her shawl pulled tight, her face doing the thing that faces did tonight — trying to be ordinary and failing by degrees. "You're needed inside. Master Quillon wants full inventories by morning. Everything. Especially—" she paused, chose, "—especially the vault tallies."

"Vault seven?" Emrys asked, mild as milk.

Corrow's eyes flicked to his sleeve. To his wrist. One heartbeat, maybe less. Then: "All the vaults," she said, and walked away, and Emrys understood that he had just been measured, and found heavier than expected, and that the measurement had been reported somewhere.

His wrist throbbed agreement.

Inside, the counting room had filled with clerks working by lamplight gone gray, and the mood in the room was the strangest part of the whole night: it was *calm*. Terrified, yes — Pell had gone pale as vellum and was copying figures with a hand that shook — but underneath the fear ran something almost like relief. Structure. Orders. *Inventory by morning.* The guild was frightened, and its answer to fear was bookkeeping, and Emrys stood in the middle of it feeling like a man watching his own reflection make excuses.

That was the trick of it, he realized. That was the whole trick, the one he'd fallen for, the one they'd all fallen for. Terror dressed as diligence. Dread filed under routine. You kept people so busy balancing columns that they never asked what the columns were *for.* He had done it to himself for six years. They had simply done it to everyone else more efficiently.

"Pell," Emrys said quietly.

The boy looked up, grateful for any anchor. "Master Delacroix?"

"When the fire dies down, go home. Don't ask leave. Clerks leave early after emergencies; it's practically traditional. Walk the long way, past the ward-tower, and count the lights on it for me. Can you do that without being obvious?"

"Count the— why?"

"Because I balance accounts," Emrys said, "and right now the account that matters is how much light this town has left, and nobody upstairs is keeping that ledger." He slid a coin across the desk, enough for a hot drink and a plausible errand. "And Pell. If anyone asks, you went home because I dismissed you. Blame me. I'm extremely blameable; it's one of my qualifications."

Pell went. Emrys returned to his inventory, because returning to his inventory was the safest visible thing to do, and because his hands needed occupation or they were going to start doing something else.

The sigil burned in steady waves now, and with each wave the words underneath got clearer, and Emrys hated how much of the old liturgy was coming back to him, syllable by syllable, like a song you'd sworn you'd forgotten and find yourself humming in the dark. *By starlight witnessed. By winter bounded. By nine voices bound—*

Nine voices.

He stopped writing. Ink pooled under his nib and bloomed into a blot, the first blot he'd made in six years, and he stared at it as the arithmetic assembled itself with terrible, patient clarity:

Nine signatures on the founding covenant — everyone in the old order knew the number, even the children, even the ones like Emrys who'd been recruited young and taught the number before they were taught to shave. Nine realms. Nine oaths. And the covenant held the winter at bay, and the winter was supposed to stay at bay as long as the covenant stood, and the covenant stood as long as the stars—

As long as the stars *witnessed.*

The blot spread. Emrys looked at it for a long time.

Then he opened the lowest drawer of his desk, past the spare nibs and the sealing wax, to the false bottom he had installed himself, with tools, during his third year of being nobody. Inside lay a lockpick shaped like a penknife, a folded map of the guildhall's foundations drawn from memory, and a letter of resignation he had written six years ago and never delivered, addressed to a guildmaster long dead.

None of it was enough. But it was a beginning, and beginnings, unlike endings, could be audited later.

Above the counting room, the great clock ticked toward one. Outside, the town slept under a lid of perfect black, its cats keeping vigil, its wards thinning like watered wine. On Emrys's wrist, the last sleeping oath in the world pulsed in time with a choir he could almost hear, singing one held note beneath the ice of everything.

Vault seven, he thought. *Deferred audit, indefinite.*

"Well," Emrys Delacroix said to the empty room, in the warm, reasonable tone of a man about to commit his first crime in six years, "I'm afraid the deferral has lapsed."

He picked up his pen, crossed out the evening's final entry, and wrote beneath it, in his neat, unremarkable hand, the only honest line in the building:

*Discrepancy noted. Investigation begins immediately.*
