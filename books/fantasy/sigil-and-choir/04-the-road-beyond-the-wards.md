## 4. The Road Beyond the Wards

Emrys Delacroix had spent eleven years learning to be boring, and it turned out that all that practice was worth nothing on the day he actually needed to be interesting.

He fled east along the old drover's road with a stolen page of the original covenant folded against his chest and the taste of blood he hadn't meant to spill still coating his teeth. Behind him, the guildhall of Vellum burned its correspondence in great iron braziers, and ahead of him lay everything the wards had once kept out — which was now, thanks to midnight and catastrophe, everything at all.

The stars were gone. He kept checking. Every time he crested a hill or passed through a gap in the hedgerows, he tilted his head back like a man looking for rain, and every time the sky gave him nothing but a smooth black absence, as if some celestial clerk had ruled a line through the night and written *void* in the margin.

"Right," Emrys said to the sky, because talking to himself was cheaper than talking to his conscience. "Item one: survive. Item two: understand what I stole. Item three: don't die before item two."

It was not a good ledger. It had no columns. But it was honest, which was more than could be said for anything the Vellum Guild had ever recorded.

The covenant page rode against his sternum, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was warm. Not fever-warm, not sun-through-a-window warm. Warm the way a hand is warm when someone is holding yours. He had checked it eleven times since the vault, partly to confirm it was still there and partly because touching it made the burning sigil on his wrist settle down, the way a dog settles when its person comes home.

His oath-sigil had been quiet for years. Dormant. He'd told himself it was dead weight, an heirloom scar from a life he'd buried under double-entry bookkeeping and the careful art of never being noticed. Then the wards fell, the stars went out, and the sigil woke up screaming — a slow, patient burn along the old sword-cut lines of his wrist, like ink remembering it was once fire.

Swordspeech. The gift the guild pretended had died with the last generation. The gift that let a speaker bind words to steel and steel to oaths, and oaths to the bones of the world. Emrys had sworn, at nineteen, to keep it hidden until the covenant called for it, and then the covenant had gone silent for a decade, and he'd assumed — hoped — that silence meant peace.

Now he knew better. Silence meant something had its hand over the choir's mouth.

* * *

He made twelve miles the first day, which would have been respectable if he hadn't spent four of those miles going the wrong way in the dark. Without stars, without even the ward-lanterns that used to hang over every crossroads like polite glowing chaperones, navigation became a matter of guesswork and stubbornness.

Near dawn — or what he decided to call dawn, the sky merely shifting from black to a bruised charcoal — he found a drover's inn with its shutters nailed open and its hearth still lit. The innkeeper, a broad woman named Marta with flour to her elbows, took one look at him and said, "You'll want bread and you'll want it fast, and I won't ask what's in your coat."

"That's very generous."

"It's not generous. It's self-interest. Everyone who comes through here is running from something, and I've learned the ones who talk are the ones who get followed." She slapped a loaf and a crock of butter on the table. "Stars went out three nights ago. My husband says it's a punishment. My daughter says it's weather. What do you say?"

Emrys considered several answers and discarded all of them. "I say the bread is excellent."

"You're a terrible liar," Marta said, with something almost like approval. "Terrible liars live longer than good ones, in my experience. Good liars get confident." She leaned in. "There were men here yesterday. Six of them. Matching grey cloaks, boots too expensive for the road. Asking after a clerk."

Emrys's hand stopped halfway to the butter.

"They described you," Marta continued, mercilessly cheerful. "'Narrow shoulders, ink-stained fingers, walks like he's apologizing to the floor.' Very flattering portrait. I said I hadn't seen anyone like that." She straightened up. "I hadn't, technically. You look worse than your description. Grief does that."

"I'm not grieving."

"No?" She nodded at his hands, which were trembling around the loaf. "Could've fooled me. East road's watched at the ford. If I were a man apologizing to the floor, I'd take the shepherd's track north of the river and cross at the stepping stones, where horses can't follow." She turned back to her oven. "Which I'm not saying. Because I haven't seen you."

"Marta," Emrys said, "you're the finest woman on this road."

"I know. Butter costs extra."

He paid the extra. He paid it gladly. And as he slipped out the back past the woodpile, he caught himself smiling — a small, cracked thing, but real — and realized it was the first time he'd smiled since the stars went out. Kindness survived the dark, apparently. It just charged for butter now.

* * *

The shepherd's track was exactly as advertised: narrow, stony, and hostile to anything with more than two legs. Emrys crossed at the stepping stones with his arms out like a man walking a tavern beam, nearly went in twice, and emerged on the far bank soaked to the knee and profoundly grateful to be alive and cold rather than captured and comfortable.

That was when the sigil flared.

Not the slow burn of the last few days — this was a spike, sharp and specific, like a finger pressed to a bruise. He crouched behind a boulder, unwrapped the covenant page, and held it up.

The ink had moved.

When he'd stolen it, the page bore nine signatures in nine different hands, eight legible and one smudged into oblivion by some deliberate, ancient thumb. Now the smudge had shifted. It sat differently on the parchment — lower, heavier, like a stain spreading through cloth. And around it, faint as breath on glass, new lines had appeared: not words, exactly. More like the memory of words. A script that wanted to be read and couldn't quite manage it.

"Oh no," Emrys said quietly. "You're awake too."

The page warmed under his fingers, and the sigil on his wrist answered, and for one vertiginous moment Emrys felt something vast turn over beneath the world — something with many voices, all of them mid-song, all of them starving. Then it was gone, and he was a wet clerk kneeling on a rock in the dark, holding a piece of paper that purred.

"Right," he said. "Revised ledger. Item two has been promoted to item one."

* * *

They found his trail anyway, of course. Grey cloaks were patient, and expensive boots covered ground. On the third day he spotted the lantern-signal on a ridge behind him — three flashes, pause, two flashes — and understood that the hunt had stopped being a search and become a pursuit.

He ran. He was not built for running; he was built for sitting at desks and being forgettable. But terror is an excellent tutor, and he learned quickly which fences could be vaulted and which ditches could be trusted and how long a man could go on a loaf of Marta's bread and spite.

On the fourth night, cornered briefly in a hollow by two hunters who hadn't seen him yet, he did something he hadn't done in eleven years.

He whispered to his knife.

Just a word. One small word, the kind of word a swordspeaker uses to sharpen a blade's attention, nothing grander than telling the steel *be sure*. The knife slid free of his belt without a sound, and when he gripped it, the sigil on his wrist sang — actually sang, a single clear note like a struck bell — and both hunters' heads snapped toward him at once.

Emrys ran the other way.

But as he fled, heart hammering, he heard one of them call out into the dark — not an alarm, not a curse. A name.

*"Emrys!"*

A woman's voice. Familiar in a way that reached past eleven years and grabbed him by the spine.

He didn't stop. Stopping was death. But he carried the voice with him over the next ridge and into the next valley, turning it over like a coin he wasn't sure was currency anymore, and by the time he collapsed at dawn under a leaning thorn tree, he knew exactly whose it was.

"Sera," he said to the grey sky. "Oh, Sera. Of course they sent you."

* * *

Sera Voss stood at the edge of the dying orchard and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what she was doing.

Then she put the feeling away, because feelings were heavy and she had miles to cover.

Captain Dreech's orders had been precise: *Delacroix is to be recovered alive and unmarked. The page is secondary. The man is primary.* Which was strange — guild protocol valued documents above people the way rivers valued water — and strangeness in orders was something Sera had learned to notice the way sailors notice a change in wind. Not alarming yet. Just noted.

She tracked him east through country that was coming apart at the seams. The starless nights weren't just dark; they were *hungry*. She'd watched it happen in the villages she passed through: names going first, then faces, then the reasons people did things. An old man by the ford had offered her his boat and, when she asked his name, looked genuinely frightened, patting his pockets as if it might be written down somewhere. A child had drawn pictures of her family in the dirt and then, an hour later, pointed at them and asked whose they were.

And the guild had known. That was the part Sera kept circling like a sore tooth. The hunting packs had been provisioned for a fourteen-day operation before the wards fell. Rations, route maps, contingency funds. You didn't pack for a catastrophe three days before the catastrophe unless you'd packed on purpose.

*Noted,* she told herself again. *Not yet alarming.*

She found his camp on the fifth morning — or rather, she found the remains of it, and then found him, because Emrys Delacroix had always been the worst hider in their entire cohort. He slept under a thorn tree with his boots on and his knife loose in his hand, and he'd left footprints a deaf grandmother could have followed, and he'd built a fire in a hollow that smoked like a penitent.

She stood over him for a long moment, crossbow lowered, and studied his face.

Thinner. Greyer. There was a new scar near his hairline and shadows under his eyes deep enough to store things in. But the mouth was the same — the mouth that had talked their way out of a dozen scrapes during training, the mouth that had once convinced a drill-sergeant that the entire cohort deserved a rest day because of a prophetic dream Sera had invented and Emrys had elaborated with such detail that the sergeant had needed a lie-down afterward.

"You've gotten worse at this," she murmured.

His eyes opened.

To his credit, he didn't yelp. He simply went very still, the way he did when an audit came up wrong, and looked at her standing over him with dawn-grey light on her crossbow, and said, "Hello, Sera. You're pointing that the wrong way. Safety's on the left."

"The safety's off."

"Ah." He swallowed. "Then I withdraw my observation."

"Get up slowly. Hands where I can see them."

He got up slowly. Hands visible. He even turned out his own pockets with the weary competence of a man who had been searched before, producing lint, a stub of pencil, and half a loaf wrapped in cloth. "Before we proceed," he said, "I want to state for the record that I'm glad you're alive, and that your tracking has improved dramatically, and also that if you're going to arrest me I'd like to finish the bread first."

"Nobody's arresting you."

"You're a guild hunter holding a crossbow on a fugitive in open country. That's fairly arrest-shaped."

"I'm bringing you back. Alive. Unmarked." She heard herself repeat the captain's words and watched them land on Emrys like cold water, because his face did the thing it always did when a number didn't add up — a tiny flicker, a recalculation.

"Alive and unmarked," he repeated. "Not the page?"

"The page is secondary."

"Huh." He hugged the bread to his chest. "Sera. When we trained together — when we swore together — did the guild ever once value a person over paper?"

She didn't answer. The question had already been living in her head rent-free for five days; hearing it aloud just redecorated.

"Because I've read the charters," Emrys went on, quieter. "All of them, these past years, because auditing is boring and charters are at least dramatic. And I found clauses, Sera. Old ones. Swordspeaker clauses, sealed and hidden, woven into the guild's founding law like — like smuggled goods in a legitimate shipment. They knew. All this time, they knew what I was, what the order was, and they let me believe it was extinct while they sat on the machinery." His hand drifted toward his chest, toward the oilcloth bundle. "And whatever's happening to the sky, they planned it. I'd stake my ledgers on it. All of them. Even the ones that balance."

"You killed a man in the vault," Sera said. "Two days ago. We found the body."

The flicker again — but this one hurt, and he didn't hide it. "Yes."

"You swore you'd never draw in anger."

"He came at me in the dark and I had one word ready and he didn't." Emrys's voice cracked, just slightly, and he steadied it the way he balanced books: deliberately, line by line. "I've been doing the arithmetic on that man for two days, Sera. It doesn't come out clean. It never will. But I'd do it again, because what's under the guildhall is worth a debt I can't repay, and I am *going* to find out what broke the sky."

For a moment — one unguarded moment — Sera saw him as he'd been at nineteen, kneeling beside her in the cold of the oath-chapel, both of them swearing on steel they barely knew how to hold. *Until the covenant calls.* She'd thought her own sigil dead for years. Lately, on the bad nights, it had started to itch.

"Walk," she said finally, jerking her chin eastward. "In front of me. Slowly."

Emrys blinked. "You're — taking me east? Toward the border? That's away from the guildhall."

"Yes."

"That's the opposite direction from my recovery."

"I'm aware of the geography, Emrys."

"And the captain's orders—"

"Are noted." She started walking, forcing him to fall in step ahead of her, and only he would have caught the way her jaw set around the word. "We'll make the river crossing by dusk. There's a village past it where the people still remember their own names, mostly, and we can decide what happens next like adults instead of like a hunter and her quarry."

Emrys walked in silence for a hundred paces. Then, unable to help himself, in the tone he'd used across a decade of shared mess-hall tables:

"Does this mean I'm your prisoner or your colleague?"

"It means," Sera said, "that the sky is broken, my orders don't add up, my sigil hasn't slept in a week, and you're the only person alive who talks faster than my doubts. Consider yourself a temporary asset."

"Temporary," Emrys repeated, delighted despite everything. "I'll take it. Under protest. With an annotation."

Ahead of them, the eastern hills rose black against a starless sky, and somewhere beyond them, in a town that still had warmth and walls and a magistrate the legends refused to name, something waited that both of them had sworn, years ago, they'd never go looking for.

The covenant page, against Emrys's chest, pulsed once — warm, insistent, patient as an unpaid bill.

It knew the way.
