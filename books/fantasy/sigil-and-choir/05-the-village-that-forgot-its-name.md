## 5. The Village That Forgot Its Name

Sera Voss had been tracking Emrys Delacroix for six days, and in those six days she had learned three things.

First: he still salted his campsites with ash from his cookfire, exactly as he had when they were children running drills in the guildyard, because he believed — wrongly, bless him — that it confused scent-hounds. It did not. It only told her he'd had soup.

Second: he was heading east, toward the old pilgrim road, which meant he was following the covenant page the way a compass needle follows north, and that frightened her more than any hunter's report could.

Third: she was not going to bring him back alive. She had known this since the second night, when she'd sat on a cold ridge watching his fire gutter out below her and realized she was smiling. Hunters were not supposed to smile at their quarry. Somewhere between the guildhall doors closing behind her and the stars going out above her, the word *orders* had quietly stopped meaning anything.

The problem was that everyone else in the world seemed to have forgotten how to remember, and Sera was running out of people to trust with even that much.

* * *

The village appeared out of the starless dusk like something half-erased. It had no name that anyone could tell her — or rather, it had eleven names, depending on whom you asked, and none of them agreed.

"Used to be called Harrow's Rest," said the miller, a broad woman with flour to her elbows and an expression of permanent apology. "Or maybe that was the next one over. There was a sign. I'm sure there was a sign."

"There's still a sign," offered her son, who looked about fourteen and had the particular confidence of someone who has recently discovered he knows things. "It just doesn't say anything anymore. The letters went soft."

"Went *soft*," Sera repeated.

"Like bread left out," he said, pleased. "You can see where they used to be. Little dents in the wood. But if you look at them too long you forget what letter they were, and then you forget you were looking, and then you're inside having soup."

Sera decided not to look at the sign.

She paid for a bed at the inn — the innkeeper charged her in questions rather than coin, which should have warned her — and by the time the soup arrived (there was a great deal of soup in this country; soup required no remembering) she had pieced together the shape of the trouble.

It was the nights. Of course it was the nights. With the stars gone, the dark had developed an appetite, and what it ate was memory. Not all at once. Not violently. It took the way frost takes a window — creeping in from the edges, blurring first the small things. A neighbor's face would go uncertain. A birthday would slide loose from its year. And then, one morning, a man would wake and reach for his own name and find the shelf empty.

"They trade for it," the miller said, lowering her voice as though the dark might be eavesdropping through the walls. "Names. For firewood, mostly. There's a man comes through — calls himself the Kindly Broker — and he'll give you a cord of good oak for your name, and another cord if you throw in your mother's name too, since you won't be needing to call her."

"And people do this?"

"People are cold," the miller said simply. "And a name doesn't warm your hands." She paused. "Mine's Bess. I write it on my arm every morning in ink. My husband stopped writing his down in autumn. He says it felt vain." Her jaw worked. "He answers to 'love' now. It's nearly enough. Nearly."

Sera thought of the guild's sealed archives, the ledgers locked behind iron, Master Quillon's voice at the last briefing — calm as arithmetic: *The wards will fail. The consequences are projected. Our concern is the covenant page, nothing else.* Projected. They had *projected* this. Somewhere in Vellum Hall there was a column of figures estimating how many villages would sell themselves into forgetting, and Quillon had read it the way Emrys read shipping manifests.

Her loyalty, which had been cracking for days, made a sound like lake ice.

She finished her soup. It was, she noted distantly, very good soup. Someone had remembered how to make it, at least for now.

* * *

In the morning she found the Kindly Broker.

He was set up in the square beside a cart stacked with split oak, a small neat man in a coat too fine for the mud, with a ledger of his own open on a folding desk. Sera felt a professional kinship and a professional disgust in equal measure, which was, she was learning, the standard guild experience.

"You're far from home," he said pleasantly, before she'd said a word. "Vellum issue boots. I know the stitching."

"I'm looking for a man. Tall, tired, pretends to be a clerk. Carries himself like a swordsman trying not to."

"Ah." The Broker smiled. "Everyone's looking for someone. That's most of trade." He turned his ledger toward her. "But I don't sell information. I sell *storage*. Names, mostly. Memories, sometimes, if they're portable. Grief travels well. First kisses less so — they leak."

"You're buying memories off starving people."

"I'm offering warmth to people who've run out of uses for what they're selling. There's a difference, and the difference is my profit margin." He said it without shame, almost with pride, and that was the worst part — he sounded exactly like every guild auditor she'd ever admired. "Though I'll tell you this for free, since you've got honest boots: your clerk came through four days back. Bought nothing. Sold nothing. Stood in the square reading the names off my own ledger — I keep the purchases written down, you understand, somebody has to — and he read them aloud to the whole village. Every name we'd bought. Gave them back free."

Sera went very still. "That ruined your stock."

"It did," the Broker said, and there was something almost wistful under the irritation. "Half the sellers took their names back and wouldn't hear reason after. He stood there in the snow saying them like a priest absolving debts — *Maren Ostwick, Tam Broadfoot, little Issel who died in spring but her mother wanted to keep saying it* — and people wept, and then they got angry at *me*, which I resent professionally." He tapped his pen against the desk. "One man, though. One man sold anyway, right after, big fellow, took the oak and walked into the trees. Your clerk followed him. Came back an hour later looking like a man who'd lost an argument with the world."

"Where did he go?"

"East. Always east. Everyone goes east now — they say there's a magistrate out past the fens whose town is still warm, properly warm, and she takes anyone who can work." The Broker's eyes flicked up, sharp as an auditor catching a discrepancy. "You'd be going east too, then. To catch him."

"To catch him," Sera agreed, and hated how easily the lie fit, like a boot two sizes wrong that you learn to walk in anyway.

The Broker wrote something in his ledger. "For the record," he said, "I don't care. But the ink remembers, and ink is the only thing in this country that does."

* * *

She caught up with him on the third day, or rather she caught up with the shape of him — a figure walking the frozen fen-road at dusk, alone, moving with that deliberate unremarkable gait he'd perfected over fifteen years of being nobody important.

Sera did not approach. Instead she did what oath-sisters do: she fell into parallel, half a mile south, matching his pace, close enough to guard his flank and far enough to pretend she wasn't. It was a game they'd played as children in the guildyard — *shadow-watch*, Emrys had called it, insisting it was training and not, as Sera maintained, an excuse to avoid conversation.

The land here was wrong in ways she kept cataloguing and kept failing to explain. The fens were frozen solid, which happened, but the ice held shapes — faces, mostly, or suggestions of faces, pressed up from beneath like flowers in glass. When she looked directly at them they resolved into cracks and air bubbles. When she looked away she could feel them noticing her.

At dusk she made camp in the lee of a drowned chapel, and that was when the night tried her name.

It didn't announce itself. It was subtler than that — a gentle loosening, like fingers prying at a knot. *Sera.* Was that right? It sounded right. It sounded like a sound. *Sera Voss.* Voss. There had been a woman named Voss who — who something. Who was currently something-ing, in a place, for reasons—

Sera bit the inside of her cheek hard enough to taste copper, and the knot pulled tight again. Around her, in the dark, she heard other travelers doing the same thing — small sharp sounds, gasps, whispered repetitions, a child somewhere reciting a list of family members like a rosary: *Da, Ma, Gran, Uncle Pem, the baby, me, me, me.*

This was what the guild had projected. This was the column of figures. Sera lay on frozen ground with her hand on her knife and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Master Quillon had known the stars would go out, had known the dark would eat the world's memory, had signed off on it in triplicate — because a population that couldn't remember couldn't object. You couldn't riot over a theft you'd forgotten. You couldn't even miss yourself.

They hadn't broken the wards despite the cost. The cost *was* the plan.

And Emrys — stubborn, soupy, ash-scattering Emrys — had walked into that dark carrying the one document that proved it, giving names back to strangers for free, heading east toward the traitor Ilvane Corvane because the covenant needed nine signatures and he intended to collect them like a debt.

Sera laughed, once, quietly, into her collar. Fifteen years she'd spent being the good soldier, and it had taken the end of the world to show her that the best person in her life had been the clerk all along.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she'd stop shadowing and start walking beside him. She'd say something dry about his campfire technique, and he'd startle, and then he'd do the thing with his eyebrows, and then they'd have an argument about whether he was being reckless — he was — and then they'd get on with saving whatever was left.

She slept, lightly, with her name written in charcoal on the inside of her wrist, next to the old scar of her own lapsed sigil.

* * *

Dawn brought company.

Sera woke to the smell of woodsmoke that wasn't hers and found three riders at the edge of the fen-road, guild-grey, mounted, unhurried. The lead rider wore a captain's sash and a face Sera knew from briefings: Doss Marrow, promoted twice since the ward-fall, famous for bringing back quarries in pieces small enough to file.

"Sera Voss," Marrow called across the ice. His breath steamed. "Quillon sends his regards. Says you've gone quiet in your reports. Says quiet clerks are either dead or deciding, and he'd like to know which."

"Tell him I'm thorough," Sera said, standing slowly, hands visible, mind sprinting through options and finding them all bad. Three mounted hunters. Open ice. No cover within a mile.

"He also says the Delacroix matter is closed to field agents. Says the covenant page is guild property, and guild property returns to the hall, and anything assisting its return — including former friends — will be *recorded favorably*." Marrow smiled. He had the smile of a man who had read the projections and found them acceptable. "Come home, Voss. The dark's eating everything out here. At least the hall has walls."

"The hall burned its correspondence," Sera said. "I saw the smoke from the road. What kind of house burns its own memory, Captain?"

"A house planning to write new books." He drew his blade, casually, the way a man checks a watch. "Last courtesy, then. Where is he?"

And Sera — cold, tired, loyal to something larger than the guild and only just admitting it — found that the answer rose in her with no effort at all, warm as soup, certain as her own name, which she still had, which she intended to keep:

"East," she said. "Same as everyone. Same as you."

Marrow sighed and gave the order, and the ice sang as three horses came on —

— and that was when the fen opened its frozen mouth, and the faces beneath the ice all turned at once to look at who had come hunting on their road.
