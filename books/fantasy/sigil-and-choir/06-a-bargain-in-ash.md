## 6. A Bargain in Ash

The river had been a river once. Emrys could tell because the map said so, and because the bed of it still curved through the valley with the patient confidence of water that had done this for ten thousand years. But what filled it now was not water. It was a slow grey sludge that moved like cold honey, and it smelled of pennies and old cellars, and when he crouched at its edge and looked closely, he could see faces in it.

Not corpses. Faces. The way you might see faces in clouds, if the clouds were trying very hard to remember what faces were.

"Hello," Emrys said, because his master had taught him that gods were like dogs: they could smell fear, but they respected manners.

The face nearest him swam up through the sludge. It was broad and greenish and had too many chins, all of them rippling gently, like a pond disturbed by rain. Its eyes were the color of silt.

*No one says hello,* the god said. Its voice came from everywhere at once, which Emrys supposed was the one advantage of being a stagnant thing. You got the whole riverbed for acoustics. *They say give me passage. They say hurry. They say I have money. Nobody says hello.*

"I'm sorry. Hello again. My name is—"

*No.* The god's ripple shuddered, and downstream, a heron lifted off in alarm. *Don't do that.*

"Do what?"

*Names.* The face drifted closer until it hung above him like a wet moon. *You're carrying a name worth stealing, little clerk. I can taste it on you. Old name. Bound name. If you speak it here, over my water, it becomes mine by right of salvage. Everything spoken over dead water belongs to the dead water. That's the oldest law there is, older than your guild, older than your stars—"*

It paused. Something moved behind the silt-eyes, something almost like embarrassment.

"—which are not speaking much these days, I notice."

"No," Emrys agreed. "They're not."

He had been walking east for six days. He had learned things about a starless world that no ledger had prepared him for: that birds flew in circles at dusk, lost without their anchors; that children cried not from fear but from a deeper, stranger wrongness, the way animals cry before an earthquake; that the dark between the stars was not empty but *attentive*, as though something enormous had turned its head to look.

And he had learned that every crossing, every bridge, every ferryman wanted payment. Coin meant less each day. What people wanted now was certainty. Memories. Names. The small currencies of a self.

He looked down at the covenant page wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, then back at the god.

"I need to cross," he said. "East. All the way east, past the fens, to the Corvane lands."

The god made a sound like a bathtub draining in reverse.

*Corvane,* it said. *Ilvane Corvane.*

"You know her?"

*I knew her when she was young and the river ran fast and clear and full of fish that insulted me personally.* A wistful ripple passed through the whole sluggish length of it. *She used to sit on my bank and read contracts aloud. She thought I wasn't listening. She read them the way some people read poetry. With feeling.*

Emrys blinked. Of all the portraits the guild's histories had painted of Ilvane Corvane—the traitor, the oath-breaker, the woman whose name was smudged from the covenant like a sin scraped off a confession—none of them had included *read contracts aloud to rivers.*

"That sounds like her," he said carefully.

*Does it?* The god's chins stilled. *You know her?*

"Not yet. I'm going to."

*Ah.* The silt-eyes narrowed, or did whatever narrowing looks like when your eyes are made of suspended sediment. *Then we should discuss terms. Crossing my water is not free. Crossing my water while carrying a covenant of the Nine Realms is especially not free. Do you know what I was, little clerk? Before?*

"A river," Emrys said. "A fast one."

*A god.* The word came out with surprising bitterness for something with no tongue. *I had nine tributaries and a festival in my honor every spring. Girls threw wreaths. Boys swore oaths on my banks because oaths sworn on living water bind twice. And then the wards went up around the border, and the roads bent away from me, and the festivals stopped, and the wreaths rotted, and one year nobody came at all. A god is fed by attention, clerk. Did your ledgers teach you that?*

"They taught me that anything unattended gets stolen," Emrys said. "I suppose it works both ways."

For a moment the god said nothing, and the whole grey river seemed to consider him. Then, horribly, it laughed—a sound like a well swallowing a stone.

*I like you. That's unfortunate for you. Likable things cost more.*

* * *

They negotiated for the better part of an hour, sitting—or in the god's case, pooling—at the bank. Emrys laid out what he had: eleven silver bits, a tin of good tea, a spare shirt, and the clothes on his back. The god rejected all of it with escalating theatrical despair.

*Silver. He offers me silver. What will I buy, clerk? A hat?*

"The tea is genuinely excellent."

*I am a god of a dead river and you offer me leaf-water.* A chin quivered with disdain. Then, grudgingly: *Although. What kind?*

"Smoked. From the eastern slopes. There's a note of pine in it."

The god was quiet for so long that Emrys began to wonder if it had fallen asleep, if gods of stagnant water did such things. When it spoke again, its voice had gone soft and strange.

*Pine,* it repeated. *My headwaters were in pine country. Before the wards. I could smell them in the meltwater every spring.* The face sank halfway back into the sludge. *Take the tea out of the negotiation. I won't take it as payment. But leave it here when you go. As a gift. Gifts are different. Gifts say someone thought of me when they didn't have to.*

"All right," Emrys said quietly. "The tea's yours regardless."

*Now.* The god rose up, taller than a man, broader than a barn, dripping grey and smelling of deep places. *The real price. Passage east, past the fens, safe from everything swimming in me—and there are things swimming in me, clerk, since the stars went out. Things that fell off the night sky and landed in my water and haven't drowned yet, which worries me considerably. For that passage, I want a memory. A true one. Not a dream, not a wish. Something that happened to you, that mattered, that you would miss.*

Emrys's hand went, without his permission, to his wrist. Beneath the sleeve, the oath-sigil sat quiet for the first time in days—a mercy he didn't trust. Since the night the wards shattered, it had burned on and off like a banked coal, and each flare had felt like a question being asked of him in a language he'd sworn never to speak again.

"What kind of memory?" he asked, though he already knew. Gods were precise. This one would want exactly what hurt most.

*Your choice,* the god said, which was somehow worse. *But I'll know if it's thin. Thin memories curdle. I'd have to spit you back out mid-river, and the things swimming in me would be so pleased.*

Emrys sat down on the bank. The mud was cold through his trousers. Somewhere downstream, the heron landed again, having decided the giant grey horror was probably fine.

There was really only one candidate, and he had known it since the god first said the word *memory*. He had been rationing it for six days, the way you ration bread when you suspect the bakery has burned down.

"My master," he said. "Aldous Wren. He taught me swordspeech. He taught me everything."

*Ah.* The god settled lower, attentive. *The guild believes swordspeech extinct.*

"The guild believes a great many convenient things." Emrys pulled off his pack and set it beside him, a man settling in for something long. "He found me when I was nine. I was working in a counting-house in Vellum Cross, doing sums for a man who beat the other clerks. Aldous came in to change a bill of exchange, watched me catch an error in his contract—not to cheat him, just because the error itched—and he bought me dinner and asked me questions for three hours. At the end he said, 'You count the way swordsmen breathe. Would you like to learn why?'"

The words came easier than he expected. That was the trap of true memories; they wanted out.

"He trained me in a cellar under a chandlery. Swordspeech isn't a language you speak with your mouth—it's a language you speak with your whole body, with intent, with the shape of a promise. Every oath is a blade. Every blade is an oath waiting to happen. He used to say—" Emrys stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "He used to say that a kept word is the only form of justice that never needs a courtroom. That law is what men write down so they can later pretend they didn't mean it. An oath is what you mean even when it ruins you."

*Go on,* the god murmured. The whole river had gone glass-still. Even the things swimming in it, whatever they were, seemed to be listening.

"He died eight years ago. Officially of a fever. Unofficially—" Emrys's jaw tightened. "Unofficially, the guild noticed him noticing things. The same things I've spent the last week noticing. He was old and careful and it didn't matter. They're very thorough, my masters. I stood at his funeral holding the accounting books he left me, and inside the cover he'd written one line in cipher, and I didn't decode it until six days ago, standing in a burning guildhall: *If the stars fail, find the ninth signature.*"

He looked at the god.

"That's the memory. All of it. Him in the counting-house. The cellar. The way he hummed when he sharpened steel, badly, on purpose, because he said perfect pitch made perfect swordsmen insufferable. His funeral. The line in the book. Take it."

The god was silent for a long moment.

*You understand what you're offering,* it said. Not gloating now. Almost gentle. *Once I take it, it's gone. You won't remember his face. You won't remember why you flinch when you hear bad humming. You'll know facts—that you had a master, that he's dead—but the man himself, the warmth of him, the weight. Gone. I will have it, and you will have a hole shaped like him.*

"Yes," Emrys said. "That's the price of knowledge, isn't it? Everyone keeps saying I need answers. Nobody mentions the tolls."

*You could turn back.*

"Back to what? A guildhall that burns its own correspondence and hunts its own clerks?" He shook his head. "Besides. If I'm going to reforge a covenant that holds back winter, I should probably practice giving things up. I have a feeling the tuition only gets steeper."

The god regarded him for a long moment with its silt-heavy eyes. Then it extended one vast grey limb—not a hand, exactly, more the suggestion of one, the way water suggests everything and commits to nothing.

*Give me your hand, keeper of dead names.*

Emrys took it. The touch was cold and soft and strangely sad, like shaking hands with a fogbank.

The taking itself was painless. That was the obscene part. One moment the memory was his—Aldous humming, the candle in the cellar, the cipher line blooming into meaning—and the next moment it simply *wasn't*, the way a word vanishes when you repeat it too many times, leaving only the knowledge that a word had been there.

He knelt on the bank afterward, breathing hard, gripping mud.

He remembered that he had loved his master. He could not remember loving him. It was like reading about weather in a country he'd never visited.

"Oh," he said, very quietly. "Oh, that's—"

*I'm sorry,* the god said, and meant it, which somehow made it worse. *For what it's worth, I'll keep him well. He'll swim in me. Gods don't forget what we're given, clerk. We can't afford to.*

Emrys wiped his face with his sleeve, stood, and did not look at the tin of tea as he set it on a flat stone at the water's edge. The god's surface shivered with something that might have been gratitude.

*One more thing,* it said, as the grey current parted before Emrys like a curtain held open by a servant. *A free gift, since you gave me two. You carry the covenant. You've read it.*

"Every word. Eight signatures. One smudged beyond recovery."

*Eight willing hands and one erased.* The god's voice dropped, and the temperature of the air dropped with it, which Emrys hadn't known a voice could do. *Listen to me, because the last person who needed to hear this wouldn't listen, and look what it cost the world. A covenant of nine is not a document. It is a body. Eight fingers and a thumb make a poor fist. You cannot reforge it with eight signatures and good intentions. You cannot forge around the missing one. The traitor's name is not a stain on the work. It is a load-bearing wall.*

"Il vane Corvane," Emrys said slowly. "You think she has to sign it. Again. Willingly."

*I think she has to sign it truly. And I think—" the god hesitated, and a god hesitating is a terrible thing to witness, like watching a mountain doubt itself, "—I think you should ask her why she smudged it before you decide what she owes you. Debts look different depending on where you stand. Believe me. I am owed a great deal, and I owe a great deal, and from the middle it all looks like weather.*

The current carried him. For a day and a night the grey river bore him east through the fens, swift now in its way, the way grief is swift—smooth on top, all the motion underneath. He slept in the shallows of the god's attention and woke once to see faint lights moving beneath the surface, falling-star things, watching him with the patience of the drowned.

At dawn on the second day, the river set him gently ashore on a gravel bar at the edge of the Corvane lands, and withdrew, and was just a sluggish grey channel again.

But as Emrys wrung out his coat and turned east, the god's voice followed him one last time, thin across the water:

*Clerk. When you meet her—tell her the river remembers her reading voice. She'll know what it means. And tell her the fish send their regards. She'll know what that means too, and she'll either laugh or cry, and either way you'll learn more from which one than from anything she says.*

Emrys stood a moment on the gravel, the covenant heavy against his chest, the hole where his master had been aching in a way he couldn't name anymore.

Ahead, past the frost-killed reeds, smoke rose from chimneys in a tidy pattern—too tidy, ordered, deliberate, the smoke of a town that worked. And above the town, impossibly, warmly, glowed a light that was not firelight and not starlight, because there were no stars left to imitate.

Something down there was keeping people warm. Something down there had broken the world's oldest promise to do it.

Emrys adjusted his pack and started walking.
