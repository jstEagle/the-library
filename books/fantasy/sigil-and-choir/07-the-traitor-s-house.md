## 7. The Traitor's House

Emrys had expected a fortress.

What he found instead was a town that smelled like bread.

He stood at the crest of the last hill with the covenant page pressed flat against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath his coat, and stared down at Hearthfall as if it were an optical illusion. Smoke rose from every chimney in tidy, unhurried columns. Lamplight glowed in windows — actual lamplight, burning oil that someone could afford to waste. Children were sledding on the mill pond, which was not frozen solid but frozen *politely*, the ice thick enough for sport and thin enough to suggest a certain municipal generosity about temperature.

Everywhere else he had walked since the stars went out, winter was a wolf at the door. Here, winter appeared to be a housecat that had been fed on schedule and knew better than to scratch the furniture.

"Either I'm hallucinating," Emrys said to his mule, "or we've found the one place in the world where the apocalypse filed the proper paperwork."

The mule, whose name was Debt because Emrys had acquired her against his better judgment and never stopped itemizing the cost, flicked an ear and continued down the hill without consulting him.

The gate into Hearthfall was open. Not unguarded — two watchmen stood there in good wool coats with polished pikes — but open, and the watchmen waved him through with the bored courtesy of men who had never once been afraid of their jobs. One of them glanced at Debt, at Emrys's road-worn coat, at the ledger satchel he still carried out of pure occupational reflex.

"Trade or pilgrimage?" the watchman asked.

"Ledger-keeping," Emrys said. "Habitually."

"Then you'll want to register with the Magistrate's office by sundown. Everyone registers." The man said it comfortably, the way you'd say *the sun sets*. "Magistrate Corvane likes her books balanced."

Emrys felt the words go through him like a fishhook finding flesh. *Corvane.* He kept his face in the mild, forgettable shape he'd spent ten years perfecting — the face of a man who was nobody's problem — and thanked the watchman in a voice that did not shake.

Ilvane Corvane. The ninth signature. The smudge on the page riding against his heartbeat. The woman who had broken an oath older than most nations and then, apparently, retired to run a small town with excellent bread.

He led Debt up the main street, and the main street made it worse.

In the settlements beyond the wards, people huddled. They burned their furniture and then their heirlooms and then, in the worst places, their memories — trading names for firewood in villages gone soft and nameless. Emrys had passed through three such places. He had watched a woman sell her grandmother's face for a cord of split oak and walk away lighter, smiling, hollow.

Here, an old man sat on his porch carving a wooden bird, and when children ran past he called each of them by name. A baker argued cheerfully with a customer about whether the second rising counted as labor. Someone was practicing a fiddle badly and no one had thrown anything at them. There was a notice board by the well, and Emrys drifted toward it the way water drifts downhill, because notice boards were the closest thing he had to a religion.

The top posting read:

*BY ORDER OF THE MAGISTRATE: Rationing continues at winter rates until further review. Coal allotments doubled for households with infants. Complaints may be submitted in writing. Written complaints will be answered in writing. This is not a threat; it is a promise.*

Below it, a smaller notice: *Lost: one grey cat, answers to Duchess (she does not). Reward: two candles.*

And below that, in a different hand, almost shy: *Thank you, Lady Corvane, for my mother's coal. — Tamsin, age 9.*

Emrys stood before the board for a long moment, feeling something complicated turn over in his chest. He had come here prepared to hate her. He had rehearsed it across four hundred miles of dying country: she broke the covenant, she let the cold in, she was the reason the choir starved and the stars went blind. She was the villain of the oldest contract in the world, and villains should look like villains.

Instead she ran a town where lost cats got reward notices and nine-year-olds wrote thank-you letters, and the whole thing functioned so smoothly it was frankly offensive.

"It's disgusting, isn't it," said a voice beside him.

He turned. A stout woman in an apron flour-dusted to the elbows was looking at the notice board with her hands on her hips.

"The orderliness," she clarified. "You get used to it. Took me two years. I came here from Marrowdale after the wards fell — we ate our chapel pews, first month. First *month*. And then I walk in here and there's a queue for the coal office and the queue has *rope guides* so nobody cuts ahead." She shook her head. "I cried for a week. Then I registered my complaint about the rope guides in writing, and she answered it in writing, and now there are two ropes. Wider gap." The woman smiled despite herself, the smile of a person who had lost an argument to competence and enjoyed it. "You're new. You have the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a man doing arithmetic about how much this is going to cost him." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Everything costs, stranger. But here, at least, they tell you the price up front. That's more than the sky does these days." She nodded at the featureless dark above them — the starless night hung over even Hearthfall, though it seemed to press less heavily here, like a debt deferred rather than denied. "Office is the big stone house with the green door. Go before sundown. She hates registering people after dark. Says tired people sign things they shouldn't."

Emrys thanked her and walked on, and the arithmetic the baker had named was indeed running. Because here was the thing he could not stop noticing, the splinter under the skin of the whole town:

It worked. And things that worked, in his experience, always had a ledger somewhere, and ledgers always showed who paid.

* * *

From the window of the magistrate's office, Ilvane Corvane watched the new arrival cross the square, and she knew within six steps what he was.

Not his name. Not yet. But the way he moved — weight low, eyes doing the work of a sentry while his face did the work of a clerk — that was guild training, and guild training did not wash out. It faded, like ink, but hold the paper to the light and there it was.

"He's registered as a traveling accountant," said Pell, her steward, setting down the day's book. "Name of Merrick. References from a trade house in Vellum territory. Very neat references."

"Neat?"

"Neat the way a forged lock is neat. Every t crossed. Nobody's real life is that tidy."

Ilvane turned back to the window. Below, the man who was probably not named Merrick was letting a small girl pet his mule, and doing it with the grave courtesy of someone who understood that to a child, a mule was a diplomatic matter.

"Guild?" Pell asked.

"Or guild-adjacent. Or running from them. Those are often the same thing lately." She had been watching the roads fill for months now — refugees, deserters, hunters wearing merchant colors, all of it downstream of whatever the Vellum Guild had done to break the world. She had broken enough oaths herself to recognize the weather that followed. "Did he flinch at your questions?"

"He didn't answer any. He asked three of his own. About the coal rationing formula, of all things."

That surprised a laugh out of her, short and unwilling. "Of all things."

"He wanted to know how we priced warmth. Whether it was by household or by body. Said—" Pell consulted his notes, frowning. "Said 'a ration that doesn't know what it's rationing isn't mercy, it's theater.'"

Ilvane was quiet for a moment. Outside, the false dusk was settling, the sunless grey that had replaced evening everywhere since the stars died. Her town's lamps came on street by street, on schedule, because she had ordered it, because schedules were the only prayer she trusted anymore.

"A ration that doesn't know what it's rationing," she repeated softly. Yes. He was guild. Or he had been. Only someone raised inside the machine talked about it with that particular bitterness — the bitterness of a part that had grown a conscience and couldn't file the paperwork.

"Shall I have the watch keep an eye on him?"

"No," Ilvane said. "I'll see him myself."

Pell's eyebrows rose. She ignored them. She was sixty-one years old and had been a legend for fifty of those years, and she had learned that legends were mostly just women who made decisions quickly and refused to apologize at the correct volume.

She had also learned — later, expensively — which decisions deserved the slowness.

* * *

They met after sundown, in her office, over tea.

Emrys had prepared himself for many versions of Ilvane Corvane. The sorceress. The tyrant. The beautiful monster of the ballads, all silver eyes and terrible grace. He had not prepared himself for a broad-shouldered woman in a plain grey dress with reading spectacles pushed up into greying hair, pouring tea with the practiced economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times and intended to do it ten thousand more.

"You're staring," she said pleasantly.

"You're not what the songs say."

"The songs say I ate a king." She handed him a cup. "I've never eaten anyone. I find it inefficient. Sit down, Master Merrick, or whatever your mother called you, and tell me why a guild-trained clerk walks four hundred miles through a dead country carrying a document against his sternum like a second heart."

The tea was excellent. This, too, felt like an attack.

"I don't know what you mean," Emrys said.

"Mm." She settled behind her desk, folded her hands, and looked at him with the patience of a glacier. "Let me save us both some time. I know what you're carrying because I wrote half of it. Nine signatures, one realm per line, one covenant to hold the winter at bay. I signed it when I was younger than you are now. And then I struck my own name through, and everyone has been calling me a traitor ever since, which is accurate, and also incomplete." She sipped her tea. "So. You can keep lying to me, and we'll spend a pleasant evening pretending, and tomorrow you'll sneak into my records room and be very disappointed by how easy I've made that for you. Or you can show me the page, and we'll talk about the child."

Emrys set down his cup very carefully. "What child?"

"There it is." Something moved behind her eyes — old grief, worn smooth as river stone. "There's always a child. That's the term you haven't read yet, because the founders kept it off the fair copies. The covenant holds winter back, yes. In exchange, every hundred years, the choir takes a voice. A young one. A voice that hasn't finished becoming anything yet." She turned her spectacles over in her fingers. "I signed knowing that. I told myself the arithmetic was sound. One child per century against the lives of millions. I was very proud of my arithmetic." Her mouth twisted. "And then the first tithe year came, and they brought the child to the cavern, and she was singing. She didn't understand. They'd told her she was going to be part of something beautiful, and she *was glad*, and I stood there watching a little girl thank the men feeding her to the ice, and I understood that I had not done arithmetic at all. I had done accounting. There's a difference. Accounting is what you do when you've decided not to look."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, the bad fiddler had started up again, and no one had thrown anything.

"So I broke the oath," Ilvane went on. "Mid-word, mid-signature, mid-song. I froze the covenant before it could finish taking her. And the price of freezing a living contract is that everything it was supposed to do comes due slowly instead. The winter didn't fall that night. It began *falling*. A hundred years of falling. And now the stars have gone out, and I would wager my town that the guild has finally decided to collect." She looked at him directly. "Am I close, Master Merrick?"

Emrys's hands were steady on his cup. His heart was not. Because the argument was monstrous, and it was also — he hated this, hated it the way you hate a correctly balanced column that proves something awful — it was also *sound*. She had seen the same machine he served for ten years and she had put her hand into the gears, and the gears had ground the world slow instead of stopping it, and she had lived with that for fifty years in a town that worshipped her for the warmth she'd bought with a broken word.

Revulsion and admiration, sitting down to tea together, passing the sugar.

"You could have told people," he said quietly. "The real terms. Let the realms choose."

"Told them? I did tell them." For the first time, something cracked in her composure — not much, a hairline. "Nine realms, Emrys Delacroix—"

His blood went cold. She watched it land, and inclined her head slightly, acknowledging the hit.

"—yes, I know exactly who you are, the sigil on your wrist has been humming at my wardstones since you crossed the ridge, and I let you in anyway, because I am tired, and because you are the first person in fifty years to walk through my gate carrying the actual covenant instead of a ballad about me. I told the realms the terms. Do you know what they said? The northern kings said *find another child*. The southern league said *not our child*. The church said the sacrifice was holy and volunteered other people's daughters. Every single one of them agreed the price was acceptable right up until the price was due, and then they all discovered their signatures had been mislaid." She set down her cup. "People will agree to anything in the abstract. I built a town where nothing is abstract. The coal comes, the names stay, the children grow up. That is the only promise I have ever managed to keep, and I keep it daily, in writing."

"And the girl?" Emrys asked. "The first one. The tithe."

Ilvane was silent long enough that the fire needed two logs' worth of silence.

"She lived," she said at last. "Frozen mid-oath means frozen mid-everything. The choir couldn't take her, but it couldn't release her either. She's still in the ice. Still singing. Fifty years." She looked at the window, at the starless dark. "Some nights I think breaking the oath was the bravest thing I ever did. Most nights I think it was just the loudest."

Emrys looked down at his tea, at the steam rising in the lamplight, and thought about the ledger in his satchel, and the page against his chest, and the oath-sigil on his wrist that had woken screaming three weeks ago and hadn't fully slept since. He had come here to confront a traitor. He was sitting in a warm room being handed, piece by piece, the true accounts — and the true accounts were worse than the ballads and kinder than the guild's version and did not fit anywhere at all.

"Why let me in?" he asked. "Really."

"Because the guild is coming," Ilvane said simply. "For you, for the page, for the choir. My watchmen report riders on the east road — guild colors under merchant cloaks, moving fast, and one of them rides like she was trained in the same yard you were." She refilled his cup without asking. "So drink your tea, swordspeaker. Tomorrow we start deciding what we're willing to pay. And this time, we're going to read every line before we sign."
