## 8. Nine Names, Eight Willing

Ilvane Corvane had learned, over two hundred years of public life, that the secret to ruling a town was to be seen doing ordinary things badly.

So every morning she swept her own steps, and every morning she did it wrong—sweeping the grit toward the door instead of away, so that the neighbors could cluck and correct her, and correcting a magistrate made them feel like the town belonged to them too. It was a small magic, cheaper than any in her cellar, and it had kept Warmhollow warm through nine years of starless dark.

She was mid-sweep when the boy came running up from the gatehouse, breathless, hatless, and entirely too excited for someone delivering bad news.

"Magistrate! There's a man at the gate. Says he knows you."

"Everyone says they know me," Ilvane said. "Half of them are right. Describe him."

"Tired. Thin. Got ink on his fingers like he's been eating his supper off a ledger." The boy hesitated, then lowered his voice with the delicious gravity children reserve for secrets they don't understand. "And his wrist is bleeding, ma'am. Just a little. From under the skin. Like something's trying to get out."

The broom went still in Ilvane's hands.

* * *

She received him in the magistrate's hall, which was also the town's only heated room with chairs enough for a quarrel, and had him seated by the stove before she let herself look at him properly. Emrys Delacroix looked worse than the last time she'd seen him—which had been ninety-one years ago, across a burning guildhall, both of them younger than either of them had any right to have been.

"You've aged," she said.

"So have you. Remarkably well, considering."

"I cheat." She poured tea from the pot that never left the stove. "You're bleeding through your sigil. That means you've spoken an oath recently, or broken one, or stood too close to something that remembers both. Which is it?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. Sit still." She took his wrist without asking, turned it to the light, and frowned at the faint red glow beneath the skin—the shape of a word half-spoken, the mark every swordspeaker carried and most never understood. Hers had gone quiet decades ago, a scar that pretended to be nothing. "You've been carrying this unsealed all this time? In *guild territory*?"

"I keep ledgers," Emrys said. "Nobody looks at a clerk's wrists. They look at his numbers."

"And if the numbers are wrong?"

"Then they look very closely at everything else, and I'd already be dead. So I make sure the numbers are right." He said it lightly, but his hand trembled against hers, and not from cold. "Ilvane. The wards fell. The stars went out. I found the covenant in the vault, and one signature on it is smudged past reading. Nine names, eight legible. I've walked three hundred miles on the theory that the ninth belongs to you."

"Your theory has excellent penmanship," Ilvane said. "Yes. It's mine."

* * *

He didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that. Most men, handed confirmation of a two-hundred-year-old betrayal, would at least knock over their tea.

"The long winter," he said instead, carefully, the way a man walks across ice he doesn't trust. "The wards failing. The stars. You did this?"

"I *stopped* something," Ilvane said. "Whether what happened after counts as my doing—" She set down her cup. "Emrys, do you know what the covenant actually says? Not the summary they teach the juniors. The actual terms."

"I read it. Twice. On the road, by firelight, while being hunted. It's not light reading."

"Then you know about the tithe."

His jaw tightened. "Clause seven. 'A voice of the youngest generation, freely given, each hundredth winter, to feed the choir.'"

"*Freely given,*" Ilvane repeated. "That's what the charter says. Now let me tell you what it meant. I was there for the last giving, Emrys. Winter of the Red Census. Do you remember it?"

"No. I wasn't born."

"Lucky you." Her voice came out flat, and she let it. Some things deserved flatness. "There was a girl called Merrin Snow. Six years old. Daughter of a ferryman on the Liss. And in the autumn before the hundredth winter, the covenant's proxies came to her parents' house with a document and a smile, and they explained that *freely given* had been interpreted—for the last four tithes, by unanimous consent of the signatories—to mean *freely given by the family, on behalf of the child, who is too young to understand what she's giving.*"

"They signed it anyway," Emrys said quietly. "All nine."

"All nine. I signed it ninth, because I was the youngest signatory and tradition said the newest hand closes the page." Ilvane rose and went to the window, where the starless sky pressed down on Warmhollow like a lid. "I watched them take Merrin Snow up the mountain to the choir-cavern. I watched the doors close. And I heard—" She stopped. Started again. "The choir sings, Emrys. When it feeds, it sings. Every voice that ever went into it, singing together. And a six-year-old's voice does not blend. It *pierces*. It goes through stone. We heard her for three days afterward, in the walls of every house in the valley, singing a skipping song her mother taught her, getting fainter and fainter, and the other voices singing *over* her, smoothing her out, until on the fourth day there was nothing but harmony again."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a cart went by, and someone greeted the driver by name, because in Warmhollow everyone still knew everyone's name, and Ilvane had spent nine years making sure of it.

"So when the next tithe came due," Emrys said, "you refused."

"When the next tithe came due, I did something much stupider." She turned back to him. "Refusing would have been simple. Refusing would have meant arguing before the full conclave, losing, and watching another Merrin Snow go up the mountain with my objection entered politely into the minutes. Instead I did what I was trained to do. I'm a swordspeaker's daughter, Emrys. My mother spoke oaths the way other people breathe. And I knew the covenant's deepest clause—the one even the masters only half-remembered. A covenant is a living word. And a living word can be caught *mid-syllable*."

"Frozen," Emrys said. "Mid-oath."

"Mid-oath. I went into the choir-cavern alone, with my sigil unsealed and my mother's phrasing in my mouth, and I spoke the counter-word into the engine itself. I caught the covenant between one binding phrase and the next, like a door stopped on its hinge. And I held it there." She smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile; she knew that, and offered it anyway. "Two hundred years, Emrys. Two hundred years of a contract suspended in its own throat. No tithe collected. No child taken. No power flowing. The choir starving slowly, the wards running on dregs, the whole elegant machine of the nine realms winding down like a clock nobody wound—because I put my hand over the gears and said *no.*"

* * *

Emrys was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful gentleness people use around wounds. "The winter—"

"The winter is the debt come due. Yes. I know exactly what it is, because I wrote the arithmetic myself, in the years after, when I finally understood what I'd done." She sat back down across from him. "A covenant isn't charity, Emrys. It's a loan. The nine realms borrowed warmth from the choir, and the tithe was the interest. Stop paying the interest and the debt doesn't vanish. It *compounds.* Every year I held the oath frozen, the balance grew. Every unsung winter added weight. And now the note has come due all at once, and the choir is too starved to pay it, and so the cold is collecting directly—from the wards, from the stars, from the memory of anyone standing too far from a fire." She spread her hands. "I didn't break the world. I deferred payment on it, at ruinous interest, for two centuries. There are ledgers in Vellum that would weep at the elegance."

"And the alternative was a child every hundred years."

"The alternative was a child every hundred years," she agreed. "Forever. Because here is the part the founders never said out loud, Emrys, though it's written in the margins of the original if you hold it to the right light: the covenant *renews*. Each reforging extends it another thousand years. Another ten tithes. Ten children per millennium, forever, and the realms signing their names to it fresh each time with clear consciences and good penmanship." She leaned forward. "I want you to sit with that number. Ten children. Every thousand years. Forever. And every signatory telling themselves, as I was told, that it's a small price, that it's *freely given*, that the warmth of nations is worth one little girl's skipping song."

"Merrin Snow," Emrys said.

"You remember the name."

"You've said it four times. Names like that don't wash out."

"No," Ilvane said softly. "They don't. That's rather the point of them."

* * *

He studied her across the stove, and she let him, because she had spent two hundred years being studied and had long ago stopped minding. What surprised her was what she saw in his face—not the revulsion she'd braced for, not quite. Something more uncomfortable than that.

"You kept them warm," he said. "This town. Nine years of starless night, and Warmhollow has full granaries and lit windows and everyone knows each other's names."

"I hoard heat the way the guild hoards contracts. It's the same skill, honestly. You find where the warmth pools and you build a wall around it." She shrugged. "Also I sweep my steps badly on purpose. It's important for morale."

"That's—" He almost laughed, then caught himself, as if laughing at a traitor were a breach of etiquette. "That's the first sensible governance I've heard described in months."

"Don't admire me yet," Ilvane said. "Here's the part where I stop sounding like a mercy and start sounding like what I am. Ask me the question you've been circling since you sat down."

He was silent a moment. Then: "Did you know the wards would fall? Did you know the cost, when you froze it—and you did it anyway?"

"Yes," she said. "Yes, and yes." She held his gaze. "I ran the numbers, Emrys. I'm told you appreciate that. One child, every hundred years, forever—or one long winter, once, that might kill thousands. I chose the winter. I chose it knowing. I chose it because I believed—and believe—that a debt spread across the innocent in installments is *worse* than a debt paid in one terrible lump, because installments let everyone sleep. The tithe survives because it's patient. Because it's *polite*. I wanted to make the bill impossible to ignore." Her hands curled around her cooling tea. "Thousands may die of this winter. I have counted them too. I count them every night. I simply decided that a horror with an end was worth more than a horror with a schedule."

"And if the end is the world?"

"Then I was wrong," Ilvane said simply. "And I will answer for it. That's the difference between me and the guild, Emrys. Quillon would never say *I was wrong.* Quillon would produce a footnote." She stood, went to the shelf behind her chair, and took down a slim volume bound in grey leather—her own hand on every page, she saw suddenly, matching the smudged signature he'd carried three hundred miles. "But I don't think I was wrong. I think I was *early*. The covenant needed reforging anyway—it always would, eventually, because no contract should outlive its reasons. I just forced the question two centuries ahead of schedule, at the worst possible moment, with the coldest possible stakes." She set the book in his hands. "My testimony. Everything. The counter-word, the freezing, the arithmetic, the nights I've spent counting. Read it, and then decide what I am. Traitor, murderer, or the only signatory in two hundred years who actually read what she signed."

Emrys turned the book over slowly. "The reforging needs nine signatures. Eight willing, you said in your letter to—" He stopped. "Wait. How did you know we'd come? Who told you—"

"Sera Voss sent a bird from the ash country," Ilvane said. "Three days ago. She's a day behind you, traveling with guild hunters on her trail and, I suspect, a change of heart in her pocket." She allowed herself a thin smile. "Which brings us to the unpleasant portion of the evening. Because Emrys—if Sera found me, the guild found her, and the guild found her, then Master Quillon knows exactly where the eighth willing signature is sitting, drinking tea, next to the stolen covenant page."

As if the world had been waiting for its cue, the bell in the gatehouse tower began to ring—not the slow toll that meant weather, but the fast, stuttering pattern that Warmhollow used for exactly one thing.

Riders. Many riders. Coming fast.

Emrys was on his feet, the testimony clutched to his chest, his sigil flaring red through his sleeve. "How many men does Quillon have?"

"Enough," Ilvane said, reaching for her coat, "to make my town very cold tonight. Ring the bell twice more, fetch the boy with the good memory—we're going to need everyone's name before morning."
