## 9. Hunters at the Gate

Sera Voss had besieged three towns in her career as a guild hunter, and she had hated every one of them for different reasons.

The first had been a smuggling den on the Saltwash coast, and the hatred there was simple: the smugglers fought back. The second had been a monastery full of archivists who surrendered so quickly it felt like being handed a gift you knew was stolen. The third—Corvane's Rest, population four hundred and eleven according to the guild's own survey, famous for nothing but its magistrate and its inexplicable warmth—she hated most of all, because she could find no reason for it at all except that it was wrong.

"You're frowning," said Dree, beside her at the treeline. Dree was nineteen, new to her squad, and had the unshakable cheer of someone who had never yet watched a siege go badly. "Is it the town? It's a nice town."

"It's a *warm* town," Sera said. "In the middle of a starless winter. Look at the smoke. Every chimney going like a festival day, while villages twenty miles east are burning their furniture and forgetting their own children's names."

"Magistrate Corvane's doing, they say." Dree squinted down the slope at the walls. "She keeps them warm. Half the folk here worship her. The other half are too comfortable to bother."

"And doesn't that strike you as strange? In all this dying country, one town fat and cozy behind its walls?"

Dree considered this with the expression of a boy doing arithmetic he suspected would disappoint him. "The Captain says it's not our job to be strange-struck. Our job is the page."

Their job was the page. Captain Aldous Vane had made the speech that morning, standing before two hundred hunters in gray wool: the traitor Emrys Delacroix carried a document belonging to the Vellum Guild, and the Guild wanted what belonged to it, and Corvane's Rest would either hand over both thief and paper or learn what the Guild did with debtors. He had delivered it in his flat, reasonable voice, the voice of a man reading terms aloud, and Sera had stood in the ranks and thought about a village they'd passed through two days earlier where an old woman had sold her name for a cord of wood and then, when asked what she was called, had wept without knowing why.

*The guild knew,* she had written in her head, over and over, like a line she couldn't stop auditing. *They knew this would happen.*

And now here they were, two hundred strong in the snow before a warm town, demanding paper.

* * *

The parley happened at noon, because Captain Vane believed in procedure the way other men believed in gods.

He rode forward under a white banner with Sera and six others as escort, and the gates of Corvane's Rest opened, and out came Ilvane Corvane herself—and behind her, wrapped in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, hair uncombed, looking like a clerk who'd been dragged through a hedge by his own ledgers, Emrys Delacroix.

Sera kept her face still. Ten years of practice went into keeping her face still.

Emrys looked worse than the last time she'd seen him—which had been across a burning waystation, both of them pretending not to recognize each other while arrows flew, an old game between old friends, *I see you, I won't say I see you.* Now he wasn't pretending. His eyes found hers in the escort and stayed there, and something passed between them that had no words in it, or too many.

"Captain Vane," Ilvane Corvane said. She was smaller than her legend, gray-haired, with the unhurried manner of a woman who had been interrupting important men for decades. "You've brought a great many soldiers to ask for a piece of paper. I admire the thoroughness. Where I come from, we'd have sent a letter."

"The page is Guild property."

"So I'm told. And the man?"

"Also Guild property," Vane said. "Indentured since childhood. Clause forty-one."

Something moved through the ranks behind him—a ripple, small, the sound of two hundred people hearing a human being called property in daylight. Sera catalogued it. She had gotten very good, these past weeks, at cataloguing things she used to ignore.

"He's standing right there," Ilvane observed. "Property generally doesn't stand so well. Or argue. Go on, clerk. Say something insolent."

"I'm mostly cold," Emrys said, and Sera nearly laughed, which would have been fatal, because ten years ago he had said exactly the same thing at exactly this kind of parley, standing next to her, both of them apprentices hiding swordspeech behind clerical routine, and the world had been simpler and colder in all the wrong ways.

Vane's voice didn't change. That was the thing about Vane; nothing ever changed it. "You have until sundown. Deliver the page and the indentured party, and the town will be assessed fairly for harboring. Refuse, and the assessment will be conducted differently."

"Conducted how?" Ilvane asked pleasantly. "With fire? We've heard about your assessments in the east. Villages that forgot their names. Was that an assessment too?"

"That was weather."

"*Was it.*"

For a moment—one heartbeat, no more—the flat reasonable mask slipped, and Sera saw underneath it something patient and enormous, like ice deciding where to crack. Then it was gone, and Vane inclined his head as if concluding a meeting, and turned his horse.

But as he turned, his eyes crossed Emrys's, and stayed a half-second too long, and Sera understood with a lurch that Vane knew. Had known for some time. Knew about the waystation, knew about the recognition, knew—or suspected—that his second-best hunter had been circling the fugitive like a moth with a guilty conscience.

He hadn't said anything. Yet. Vane never spent information early.

That was when Sera started planning, because a hunter who knows she's already caught has nothing left to lose, and nothing left to lose is the most dangerous inventory a person can carry.

* * *

The assault came at dusk, because Vane also believed in symbolism, though he'd deny it.

They came up the western slope in three columns, shields high, torches guttering in the wind that poured off the dead sky. There were no stars to steer by. There had been none for weeks. Sera had stopped looking up months ago; it was easier than feeling the absence each time.

She held the left column's rear guard with Dree and thirty others, which meant her job was to watch the treeline for sallies, which meant her job was, conveniently, to be alone with her thoughts.

"Hunter Voss." Dree again, breath steaming. "Can I ask you something? You've served long, they say. Is it true what the easterners claim—that the starless night eats memories? That people just... lose things?"

"It's true."

"But the guild sent us *out* here. Into it. Doesn't that worry anyone?"

"We're warded," Sera said. "Guild wards." And heard, as she said it, how it sounded—the wards that had failed everywhere else, holding for exactly the people enforcing exactly the guild's will, and Dree's young face going carefully blank as he filed that away in whatever ledger the young keep.

The first arrows came off the wall then, and the shouting started, and the gentle part of the evening ended.

It was not much of a battle, as battles go. Corvane's Rest had walls and warmth and furious townsfolk defending their hearths, and the guild had numbers and discipline, and those forces roughly cancelled until the whole affair bogged down into a grinding, smoky stalemate at the west gate. Sera fought because her body knew how, moving through the drill-shapes, turning blades, breaking a townsman's wrist gently enough that he'd thank her someday, keeping her squad alive because that was still, despite everything, a thing she wanted.

Then the horn sounded from the command position—the signal she'd been dreading, the one Vane had briefed them on that morning with his flat voice: *If the gate holds by dark, we burn the granaries. Warmth is their strength. Take away the fuel and the town delivers itself.*

Burn the food. In a starving winter. To retrieve a piece of paper.

Sera stood in the smoke and the noise and felt something in her finish coming apart—not dramatically, not all at once, but the way a ward fails, quietly and completely, one thread at a time until suddenly there is nothing holding.

"Dree," she said. "When this is done, go home. Resign. Whatever you do, don't make captain."

"What? Why—"

But she was already running.

* * *

She found them at the granary road, where it was all going to happen: Vane with a dozen picked hunters and jars of guild-fire, and Emrys in front of the granary doors with a sword he clearly barely remembered how to hold, and Ilvane Corvane beside him with a kitchen knife, of all things, held like a woman who had once known exactly what to do with it.

"Surrender the page," Vane was saying, "and I am authorized to spare the town."

"You're authorized," Emrys said. His voice shook, but he kept it level, and Sera thought: *he's using it, he's actually using the swordspeech, threading the oath-grammar under his words to make them land heavier.* She could feel it from thirty feet, a pressure like a struck bell. "You're authorized. Who authorized you, Captain? Read me the clause. Read me the clause that lets you burn grain in a famine winter. Read it aloud."

And Vane—here was the terrible thing—Vane began to reach for his charter-book, because procedure was his god and even now, even here, some part of him wanted to be *right*, wanted the citation—

Sera came out of the smoke at a dead run.

Later, she would try to remember making the decision and fail. There was no decision. There was only the village with the nameless old woman, and clause forty-one, and Emrys's face at the parley, and ten years of standing very still while the guild did its arithmetic with people, and all of it arriving at once in her legs and her arm as she drew.

Vane heard her boots—he was good, he was always good—and turned, and his eyes went wide with something that was almost relief, as if some buried part of him had been waiting years for someone to finally make it simple.

"Sera," he said. Not a warning. Just her name, read aloud, like an entry in a ledger.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and cut him down anyway.

It was quick. She made sure of that. Whatever else the guild had taught her, it had taught her that mercy lived in precision. Vane folded into the snow with his charter-book still in his hand, open, and she looked down at him for one heartbeat and felt—exhilaration. That was the shameful part. Underneath the horror and the grief and the knowledge that her life had just ended in every way that mattered, some wild bright thing in her chest was singing, *finally, finally, finally.*

Then twelve hunters were staring at her, and the bright thing shut up fast.

"Voss has defected!" someone shouted. "Take her!"

What followed was less a fight than an argument conducted at swordpoint, and Sera lost it in the sense that she took a gash along her ribs and gained nothing but seconds—but seconds were the entire currency of the moment, because Emrys grabbed her arm and hauled, and Ilvane threw down the kitchen knife in disgust—"useless thing, I told them to give me a real blade"—and the three of them ran for the gate as the town's defenders surged in behind them, and somewhere Dree's voice rose above the din, young and cracking, shouting confusion orders at the squad Sera had abandoned.

Through the gate. Bars slamming down. Torchlight and shouting receding.

Then quiet, in a courtyard smelling of bread and woodsmoke, and Sera leaned against a wall and slid down it and discovered she was laughing, or crying, or both, in a way she hadn't done since she was a child.

"Well," said Emrys, crouching beside her, pressing his coat against her ribs with hands that shook. "Hello, Sera."

"Hello, Emrys." Her voice came out wrecked. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other clerk." He was grinning at her, wet-eyed, and ten years fell away between them like snow off a roof. "You killed your captain for me."

"For the ledger," she corrected. "You've always been an entry, Delacroix. A very annoying one."

Ilvane Corvane stood over them both, arms crossed, regarding Sera with the expression of a magistrate assessing a new problem. "So. The guild's second blade, cutting down its first. They'll call you traitor in every hall from here to the coast."

"They already will," Sera said. "I burned my name tonight. Might as well use the fire."

"No," Ilvane said, and something in her voice made them both look up. "Names are what the dark eats. Keep yours. That's the whole point of what we're about to do—though I confess I don't yet know what we're about to do, because our clerk here keeps insisting the answer lies under the ice, following a *feeling in his wrist*, and I have built a functioning town on the principle that feelings are not municipal policy."

Emrys pushed back his sleeve. In the torchlight, the oath-sigil on his wrist was glowing—a thin gold line, pulsing, steady as a pulse, pointing east like a compass needle made of light.

"It's pulling," he said softly. "There's something under the ice, Sera. Something old. All the voices—all the swordspeakers who ever swore—they're *singing* down there. And they're starving."

Sera looked at the sigil, and at her oldest friend, and at the wound in her side that marked the exact price of the side she'd chosen, and found—to her considerable surprise—that she was smiling.

"All right," she said. "Point the way. But if we die under a glacier chasing your wrist, Emrys, I am haunting you *specifically*."

Above the eastern hills, where no stars had shone for weeks, the darkness seemed—for just a moment—to lean toward them, listening.
