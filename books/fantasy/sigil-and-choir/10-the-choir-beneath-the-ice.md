## 10. The Choir Beneath the Ice

The sigil had been pulling for three days.

Not tugging, exactly—Emrys had spent enough years pretending to be a bookkeeper to know the difference between a ledger's pull and a debt collector's. This was the latter. A steady, patient insistence, like a hand on his wrist saying *you signed something once, and the bill has come due.*

"It's getting worse," he said, holding his arm out to the fire. Beneath his sleeve, the oath-sigil glowed through the skin like a coin held behind cloth. "Yesterday it pointed south. Today it points down."

"Down," Sera repeated, in the flat tone she reserved for things she found either stupid or alarming. With Sera, the two categories overlapped considerably.

"Down," Emrys confirmed.

Ilvane Corvane looked up from the map she'd been annotating with charcoal—the same map, Emrys had noticed, that she annotated the way other people prayed, quickly and without much hope. "There's nothing down. There's the glacier, and beneath the glacier there is several hundred feet of ice, and beneath the ice there is rock, and beneath the rock there is whatever the world keeps in its cellar and does not show guests."

"The sigil doesn't care about guests." Emrys flexed his fingers. The mark burned warmer when he spoke of it, which was either meaningful or psychosomatic, and he had long ago stopped trusting himself to tell the difference. "It cares about oaths. And I think we're standing on one."

They were camped in the lee of a ridge above the Vhalen Glacier, which the old trade routes had called the Long Stair because it descended from the mountains in a series of blue-white terraces, each one wide enough to march an army across and cold enough to make the army regret it. In the starless dark, the ice gave off its own faint light—not reflected, because there was nothing left to reflect, but generated, a pale phosphorescence like the memory of moonlight that the glacier refused to give up.

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. Emrys resented it for that.

"We could go around," Sera said. She said this every few hours. It was her version of whistling past a graveyard, except Sera did not whistle and did not believe in luck, so instead she proposed tactically unsound alternatives until reality agreed to negotiate.

"Around means four more days," Ilvane said. "Four more days of the cold doing what it's doing." None of them mentioned what it was doing. They didn't have to. The frost flowers on the tent canvas had begun forming shapes—letters, almost, though never quite legible, as if winter were trying to spell something and kept losing its place.

Emrys rolled up his sleeve.

The sigil was no longer content to glow. It had begun to move—or rather, the light within it moved, tracing lines that hadn't been there before, fine filaments branching outward from the old oath-knot like roots seeking water. All of them pointed down and slightly east.

"That's new," Sera said.

"Yes."

"I don't like new."

"You rarely do."

"One of us has to be consistent, Emrys. You've spent the entire journey becoming increasingly strange." She poked the fire with a stick, sending up a spiral of sparks. "First you started talking to your own arm. Then you started talking to my horse. Now you're talking to the glacier before we've even reached it."

"I wasn't talking to the glacier."

"He hummed at it," Ilvane offered helpfully. "Last night. I assumed it was a clerical habit."

"It was not a—" Emrys closed his eyes. "The sigil resonates. When it pulls hard enough, it makes noise. Humming is how I keep it quiet. If I don't, it sounds like—" He stopped, because he didn't have words for what it sounded like, and the absence of words from a swordspeaker was its own small embarrassment, like a locksmith locked out of his own house.

"Like what?" Sera asked.

"Like singing," he said finally. "Very far away. Underwater."

Sera and Ilvane exchanged a look over the fire. It was the look of two women who had been enemies for most of their acquaintance and allies for barely two weeks and were still calibrating how much alarm to share.

"Well," Ilvane said. "Then I suppose we're going down."

* * *

The entrance was not an entrance. That was the first thing Emrys understood about it.

They found it on the second morning, where the glacier met the mountain's shoulder: a seam in the ice, vertical, narrow, glowing faintly from within—and shaped, unmistakably, like a keyhole. Not metaphorically. The edges of the seam were cut in precise wards-and-teeth, the way guild locks were cut, the way *oath-locks* were cut, and when Emrys pressed his marked wrist against the ice, the sigil flared and the seam sighed open with a sound like a very large book being opened by someone who had been waiting a long time to be asked.

"Oh, I hate that," Sera said, drawing her sword out of pure reflex. "I want it noted that I hate that."

"Noted," Emrys said. "In triplicate."

Inside, the seam became a stair. The ice around it was ancient—older than the guild, older than the border wards, older, Emrys suspected, than the idea of borders. Cut into the walls at intervals were niches, and in each niche lay an object: a sword hilt wrapped in rotted silk. A pair of boots worn through at the heels. A clay cup, chipped, ordinary. A child's wooden bird on a string.

No plaques. No names. Just the objects, arranged with the care people give to things that mattered more than they can say.

"They brought their tools," Ilvane murmured, trailing her fingers along the wall without touching. "When they came to swear. Whatever they were leaving behind, they left here."

"Or these are what they couldn't take with them afterward," Emrys said quietly.

Nobody asked what he meant. He was grateful. He wasn't sure he could have explained—that some oaths cost you the part of yourself that needed boots, or cups, or birds on strings—and anyway, the stair was pulling him downward now in earnest, the humming in his bones rising toward something with actual melody in it, and all his attention narrowed to that.

The stair went down farther than the glacier was deep. Emrys understood this the way you understand things in dreams: without argument. The ice ended somewhere above them—they passed a stratum of frozen gravel, then bedrock veined with something luminous—and still the steps continued, hewn into stone that predated any masonry he knew, lit by veins of the same pale glow as the surface ice.

"How old is this?" Sera asked. Her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to spend anything on showing it.

"Older than the covenant," Ilvane said. She had gone pale, and her hand rested on the wall not for balance but for comfort. "The covenant was written *on* something. We always assumed the founders built this." She shook her head slowly. "They didn't build it. They found it. Like everyone else, they found it, and they put their contract on top of it and called themselves authors."

The stair ended.

* * *

The cavern opened around them like the inside of a bell.

That was Emrys's first thought, and his second, and for a long moment his only thought, because the space was vast and vaulted and every surface of it—every rib of rock, every arch, every hanging formation of stone—was carved, or grown, or *sung* into the shape of an instrument. Pipes along the ceiling. Strings of mineral spanning chasms. Hollow chambers in the walls sized like throats.

And the cavern was full of voices.

They hung in the air the way frost hangs—visible, almost, if you squinted: threads of pale light woven through the dark, thousands upon thousands of them, each one a line of song frozen mid-phrase. Some were deep and slow as tectonic patience. Some were thin and high and quick, children's voices, and Emrys's chest tightened painfully at that, because he knew what children's oaths meant, he knew the century-tithe Ilvane had refused, and here it was, written in light: generations of them, sworn young, sung early, stopped mid-word.

The choir was not silent. That was the worst of it. It sang—but at a fraction of its strength, notes stretched thin, phrases unraveling at the edges like fabric worn past mending. Every voice was still trying to hold its line. Every line was fraying.

"It's starving," Emrys said. His voice came out wrong, scraped. "The covenant runs on this. This is what holds the winter—the song isn't a metaphor, it's the mechanism, it's the *engine*, and the engine is running out of singers."

He stepped forward and the nearest thread of light turned toward him.

There was no other word for it. A ribbon of frozen song, older than his guild, older than his language, bent its attention upon him the way a sunflower bends to a window, and the sigil on his wrist blazed, and the thread *recognized* him.

Grief hit him like cold water.

Not his grief. Its grief—poured into him through the point of contact, vast and specific: the memory of being many, of singing in harmony with hundreds of bound voices, of an oath so large it had weather, of winters *answered*, pushed back, made polite. And then the thinning. Signatories dying and not being replaced. Realms falling out of the covenant one signature at a time. The tithe children, taken and returned as threads of light, added to the choir and silenced forever. And underneath all of it, patient as erosion, the question every one of those voices had been asking for centuries:

*Who will sing next?*

Emrys came back to himself on his knees. He didn't remember kneeling.

Sera had her hand fisted in his collar, hauling him back from the light-thread, her face white. "Don't touch it. Emrys. *Don't touch it.*"

"It touched me," he managed.

"What did it—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Sera recalibrated fast; it was why she'd survived as a hunter. "What did it want?"

"To know if I'm the next verse." He got his feet under him. His legs worked, mostly. "I told it—I don't know what I told it. I think I apologized."

Ilvane stood apart from them both, at the cavern's center, where the floor bore a circle of nine inset stones. Eight of the stones were dark. The ninth—the one at the due north, the place of primacy—was cracked clean through, and from the crack, faint as breath on glass, seeped a thread of light thinner than any of the others, trembling, alone.

She stared at it for a long time.

"That's mine," she said.

Emrys crossed to her. Up close, the cracked stone showed the ghost of a signature burned into it in old oath-script, broken at the middle, severed the night she had frozen the covenant mid-oath rather than let it claim another child. The thread rising from it was hers—her voice, her young voice, the one she'd pledged a hundred years ago and amputated herself from.

"It's still here," he said. "You didn't kill it. You just... stopped it from finishing."

"I know what I did." Her voice was level. Her hands were not. "I've had a century to inventory it."

"No, I mean—" He crouched beside the stone. "Look. It's fraying, but it's *here*. Everything else in this room is waiting. Holding its note. Yours is the only one that broke the phrase." He looked up at her. "Which means yours is the only one that can start a new one."

Ilvane looked down at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes that Emrys had learned, in their weeks together, to read as the magistrate calculating consequences—and then, occasionally, as the woman who had once been young enough to believe breaking a thing was the same as fixing it.

"You're suggesting I finish what I started," she said.

"I'm suggesting the song needs all nine voices, and eight of them are already standing in this room waiting for you."

"And the ninth?"

Emrys touched his own wrist. The sigil pulsed, warm, insistent, pointing—not down anymore, not anymore, but *inward*, at the center of his chest, at the throat beneath it.

The realization arrived the way the stair had arrived: all at once, without argument, in a place below decision.

"It's me," he said. "It was always going to be me. I'm the last swordspeaker. The final clause. The covenant wrote itself a contingency and I'm it." He laughed—a short, unsteady sound with no humor in it, only wonder wearing humor's coat. "All those years hiding in ledgers. Being unremarkable. Keeping my head down and my mouth shut. And the whole time I was the punchline."

"Don't," Sera said sharply.

"Don't what?"

"Don't do the thing where you make it small. Where you file it." She crossed to him and gripped his shoulders, and her eyes were bright and furious and afraid. "I've watched you price everything since the day I met you. Every cost, every risk, entered neatly in columns so it wouldn't crush you. Well, this one doesn't fit in a column, Emrys. I don't know what giving your voice to that thing means, but I know it isn't a rounding error."

"Sera—"

"And another thing." Her grip tightened. "Whatever the terms are, we haven't read them yet. You taught me that. No signature before the full text. So before you go volunteering your throat to a hole in the ground, we find the terms. All of them. Because I have read enough guild contracts to know there is *always* a clause nobody mentions until it's too late."

For a moment, Emrys just looked at her—his oath-sister, his hunter, the woman sent to drag him home who had instead walked into exile beside him—and felt the grief of the cavern rearrange itself around something warmer.

"Triplicate," he said. "You want it in triplicate."

"I want it witnessed," she said. "By someone who'll fight about it."

Ilvane cleared her throat. When they turned, she was looking not at them but at the far wall of the cavern—at a section of stone that, Emrys realized with a slow crawl of dread, was not stone.

It was a door. Iron-banded, oath-locked, ancient—and hanging open.

Beyond it, a passage sloped upward, and from somewhere along that passage came a sound that did not belong in a place like this: the scrape of boots. The clink of harness. Voices, low and disciplined, moving with purpose.

Guild voices.

And threaded among them, thin and terrible, a sound Emrys knew from his worst nights—a voice reciting contract-language in the cadence of a man reading scripture, each clause landing like a hammer:

"—binding, perpetual, non-transferable, the anchor to be delivered unto the choir alive and *willing*—"

Sera's sword was out before anyone spoke.

"They're already here," she breathed. "Quillon found it first."

Emrys rose, and the whole starving choir turned its thousand frozen faces of light toward the open door, and every thread in the cavern began, very softly, to tremble.
