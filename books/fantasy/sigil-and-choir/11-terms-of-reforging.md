## 11. Terms of Reforging

The war-table in the magistrate's hall of Corvane's Rest had been built for a different kind of arithmetic.

I knew this because I had commissioned it, forty years ago, when I still believed that a table shaped like a shield and carved with the boundaries of nine realms would help me govern fairly. It had helped me levy taxes. It had helped me settle a dispute about a bridge. It had never once helped anyone save anything.

Now it held three cups of barley tea gone cold, one stolen page of the oldest contract in the world, and four people who could not look at each other without some old wound opening like a letter.

Emrys Delacroix stood at the northern edge of the map, where his guild's territory was inked in faded brown, and he would not sit. Sera Voss sat at the eastern edge because she said sitting made her think better, which I suspected was a lie she used to avoid standing near him. The boy called Ledger — no. Wait. The boy had not yet arrived. That came later. Forgive an old woman her rearranged memories; the starless nights had been chewing at mine too, and I have learned to check my recollections against my ledgers the way other people check their reflections.

Four of us, then: Emrys, Sera, myself, and the river-god's messenger — a heron that perched on the windowsill with the patience of something that had been alive since before tables were invented, sent to witness on behalf of a god too stagnant to leave his own mud.

"Read it again," Sera said.

"I wrote it," I said. "I know what it says."

"Read it *aloud*," Emrys said quietly, "so we all hear it the same way. My master taught me that oaths spoken in silence are half-lies."

That was the first wise thing any of us said that night, though none of us recognized it as wisdom at the time. We thought it was just cruelty, which is often how wisdom arrives dressed.

I picked up the page I had spent three days drafting, and I read:

"*Be it reforged between the nine realms and the Choir Beneath the Ice: that winter shall be held at bay not by the blood of children, nor by the voice of any soul taken unwilling; but by the willing anchor of one voice, given freely and given wholly, sustained for the life of the giver, revocable only by death.*"

Silence. The heron blinked. Somewhere below us, the town's furnaces roared against the cold that pressed on Corvane's Rest like a thumb on a beetle.

"There," I said. "No more children. Every century, the guild took a child from a family that could not refuse them. I ended that. This ends it permanently."

"And replaces it with a slave," Sera said, "who volunteers."

"A volunteer is not a slave—"

"In my experience," Sera said, with the flat tone of a woman who had hunted people for a living and knew exactly what pursuit looked like from both sides, "*every* volunteer I ever met had been talked into it by someone with better handwriting."

I set the page down. "Then let's hear your alternatives."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about betraying an oath: you do not stop being a person who keeps accounts. You simply start keeping them differently. The guild kept its ledgers in ink and vellum. I had kept mine, these forty years, in furnaces and grain stores and a town that worshipped me for warmth — and I will not pretend I didn't enjoy the worship, because a woman who lies to herself about small things will lie about large ones soon enough.

But I had done the arithmetic already. I had done it before they arrived, alone, at night, with the covenant page spread out under lamplight while my town slept warm because of me and only me.

One voice. Permanently. And there were exactly four candidates in this room, if you counted the heron, which I did not, though the god behind it might have volunteered out of spite if pressed.

"Let's be practical," I said, and began the ugly work. "Sera. You're a swordspeaker's oath-sister. Your sigil burned out years ago, but the choir remembers voices it has sung with before. You'd need no training."

"No." She said it fast, the way you slap a hand over a wound. "I spent six years hunting people for the guild. If I'm going to pay for that, I'll pay in a way that lets me keep *doing things*. Anchor a song forever? That's not penance, that's a coffin with better acoustics."

"It isn't death," Emrys said.

"It's worse and you know it. Death ends. Anchoring doesn't. You'd be awake inside the ice for the rest of your life, singing to keep strangers warm, and the last thing you'd hear every night is your own voice getting smaller." She wrapped her hands around her cold tea. "I've heard the choir, Emrys. Under the ice. Those aren't songs anymore. They're *habits*. People worn down into refrains." She looked up. "Ask me to die for this and I'd consider it. Ask me to fade and I won't."

I marked her refusal in my head — not on paper; some refusals deserve the dignity of being unwritten — and turned to the heron.

"The god," I said.

The heron preened once, which I took for deliberation.

"He cannot," the heron said at last, in a voice like water moving through reeds. "My lord gave his voice to the river long ago. What remains of it is silt and temper. He could anchor nothing but a puddle." A pause. "He asked me to say that he regrets it, and also that he does not, because regret requires a voice and he spent his on a bargain considerably stupider than yours."

Gentle humor, even here. The gods are worse than clerks about deflection.

"That leaves two," I said.

Emrys finally sat down. He pulled the chair out slowly, the way men do when sitting feels like surrender, and folded his hands on the map of nine realms, covering most of the northern territories, which felt symbolically appropriate.

"Ilvy," he said — and I flinched, because no one had called me that in forty years, because the last person who called me that had been standing beside me the day I broke the world's oldest promise — "you've already refused. Forty years ago. That's what started this."

"My refusal was of the *old terms*," I said. "A child every century. I stand by it. But the new terms—" I stopped. He was looking at me with those clerk's eyes, patient and terrible, the eyes of a man who audits souls the way he audits shipments of bottled thunder.

"You drafted these terms," he said gently. "You drafted them knowing you were the obvious candidate. Magistrate. Healthy. No family. A town that would survive losing you because you built it to survive everything, including yourself." He tapped the page. "You wrote a contract designed so that you couldn't sign it without admitting you'd been running from the choir for forty years."

"I did not—"

"You absolutely did. It's very well drafted. I'd expect nothing less from the woman who forged the guild's founding charter amendments." Something almost warm crossed his face. "It's the best legal document I've ever read. It's also a confession with margins."

I looked at Sera for support. Sera looked at the ceiling. Traitor.

"Fine," I said. "*Fine.* Yes. I considered myself. I considered it for three nights, and do you know what I concluded? That my town needs a magistrate more than the sky needs another voice. That the moment I walk into that cavern, Corvane's Rest goes back to buying firewood with names, and within a generation there will be no one left who remembers why furnaces matter, or wards, or contracts, or *anything*, because the starless dark eats memory first and it eats memory like—" I caught myself shouting. I lowered my voice. "I have spent forty years being answerable to eight thousand people. That is my oath now. It may be a smaller one than yours, but I have kept it, which is more than either of you can say."

The silence after that was the honest kind — the kind where everyone privately admits the cruel thing said was also the true thing.

Emrys nodded slowly. "Then we're agreed. All four of us refuse."

"We can't all refuse," Sera said. "The covenant doesn't reforge itself. The winter doesn't care about our feelings. Every day the stars stay dead, somewhere a village forgets its own name, and eventually—" she gestured at the window, at the absolute black beyond the glass, "—eventually it forgets ours."

"So we draft better terms," I said, because drafting is what I do when I am frightened. "We find a fifth candidate. There were nine signatures originally. Some of those signatories' descendants must still live. Some realm must owe—"

"Nobody owes anything anymore," Emrys said. "The wards fell. The borders are lines on this table and nowhere else." He rubbed his wrist, where I knew the sigil lived, hidden under his sleeve like a scar he was embarrassed by. "There's no one else. There's just us, and the terms, and the fact that every term we can legally draft requires one of us to stop being a person."

"Then we don't sign," Sera said.

"Then everyone freezes."

"Then someone *chooses*," I snapped, "and stops pretending this is a negotiation! This has been a negotiation for three days! We have redrafted the anchoring clause eleven times! We have debated whether 'willing' requires joy or merely absence of coercion! I have written *footnotes*, Emrys! Footnotes, to a document whose entire purpose is to feed a voice to an ice cavern!" I stood, and my knees cracked, forty years of magistrate's chair announcing themselves. "The ugly truth of heroism is that it isn't chosen by the heroic. It's chosen by exhaustion. One of us is going to run out of arguments before the others run out of reasons, and that one will sign, and the rest of us will spend our lives calling it noble because the alternative is calling it what it is — which is the last resort of people who ran out of better ideas!"

The heron shifted on the sill. Even the god's messenger knew when to be quiet.

Emrys spoke first, softly. "You're right."

"I'm always right. It's exhausting."

"But you're wrong about one thing." He pushed back his sleeve.

And there it was: the sigil, the swordspeaker's mark, glowing faintly gold against his skin like a coal refusing to die — brighter, I realized, than it had been three days ago. Brighter every hour.

"The terms require a voice," he said. "Mine is the only one in this room that the choir was built to hold. The founders designed the covenant around swordspeech. My oath-sigil has been burning hotter since the night the stars went out. It's not asking me, Ilvy. It's *counting down*."

"No," Sera said, standing so fast her chair went over backwards. "No. We agreed — no martyrdoms until every option—"

"There are no options left. There's just timing." He smiled at us, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was gentle. "Give me until morning. Let me draft my own terms — fair ones. If I'm going to give the choir my voice, I'm going to make sure it comes with clauses. Protections for the towns. Protections for Ledger—" he stopped himself, and Sera and I exchanged a glance over the word he'd swallowed, neither of us yet knowing how much that name would come to cost us. "Protections for everyone the guild ever indentured. If my voice buys anything, it should buy *everything*."

He gathered the covenant page, rolled it with a clerk's precise hands, and walked toward the door.

At the threshold he paused. "Ilvy. For what it's worth — the child, a century ago. I've read the original terms. I understand why you broke it." He did not turn around. "I'd have broken it too. That's rather the problem. We're all the type who breaks things for mercy, and mercy doesn't hold up roofs."

He left.

Sera righted her chair and sat down heavily. "He's going to do it," she said. "Tonight. While we sleep. He'll leave a note. It'll be beautifully organized. There will be *footnotes*."

"Yes," I said.

"So what do we do?"

I looked at the heron. The heron looked at me with one ancient yellow eye, and I swear on every contract I have ever forged, the bird smirked.

"We don't sleep," I said. "And in the morning, we find out whether love or stubbornness wins."

Neither did. Which is why, at dawn, when the guild's horns sounded at the edge of my town — horns I had not heard in forty years, carrying orders sealed with Master Quillon's personal sigil — Emrys Delacroix was still in Corvane's Rest, still un-anchored, still ours.

And Quillon's herald announced, to the assembled town, that the guild had already found its anchor.

They had brought it with them.
