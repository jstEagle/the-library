## 13. The Boy Called Ledger

The Vellum Guild had a smell, Sera decided on her third day inside its walls, and the smell was ink and fear steeped together until you couldn't tell which was which.

She'd been in guildhalls before—raided two of them in her years as a hunter, back when she still believed the charters meant what they said. Those halls had smelled like paper and lamp oil, like work. This one, the mother hall at Corvane's Reach, where Master Quillon himself kept his offices, smelled wrong. It smelled like a house pretending it wasn't a tomb.

Getting in had been insultingly easy. The guild had lost hunters to defection—one hunter, specifically, one very public defection involving a captain and a courtyard and a great deal of blood—and so they were hiring replacements with the desperate speed of a leaking ship bailing water. Sera had walked up to the gate with a forged service record under the name *Marren Cade*, a scar she'd given herself with a whetstone for authenticity, and a face arranged into the particular blankness that institutions love: obedient, unmemorable, hungry.

The gate sergeant had looked at her papers for eleven seconds.

"Can you follow orders?" he'd asked.

"To the letter," Sera had said, which was even true, in a way. She was very good at letters. She just chose whose.

* * *

Her cover assignment was inventory duty in the eastern stores, which suited her perfectly, because the eastern stores shared a ventilation shaft—old, half-collapsed, officially decommissioned—with the Foundling Wing.

That was what they called it. The Foundling Wing. As if the boy had washed up on a doorstep in a basket instead of being purchased, according to a ledger entry Sera had spent two nights copying out with hands that wanted to shake:

*Item: one male child, approx. age four. Vendor: the Ashmarket brokers. Price: 40 crowns plus conveyance. Designation: Anchor Candidate. Provenance verified (voice intact, lineage scrubbed). Note: subject shows early aptitude for stillness.*

*Aptitude for stillness.* She had read that line perhaps forty times. Every time, it landed like a slap from someone wearing gloves.

The plan was simple, or simple the way most of her plans were, which was to say it would work right up until the moment it needed to. Get to the boy. Assess him. Get him out through the shaft before the night rotation changed. Ilvane was still in a cell somewhere below the tribunal floor, Emrys was—she didn't let herself think about Emrys, not here, not with his name a live coal in her chest—and every hour she spent inside these walls was an hour Quillon's machine ground closer to whatever false reforging he intended. But the boy was the hinge. Quillon had built his whole scheme around an anchor he owned outright, and if the anchor walked away, the scheme walked away with him.

So: the boy.

Sera finished her shift stacking crates of bottled thunder—the same shipment Emrys had audited, she realized, a lifetime ago in another world—and waited for the changeover bell, and slipped into the dark of the ventilation shaft with a hooded lamp and her heart beating the way it used to before hunts. Old rhythm. Familiar. She'd missed it, God help her. There was something clean about a mission when you finally admitted what side you were on.

* * *

The Foundling Wing was not what she expected, and she hated it more for that.

She had pictured a cell. A cage. Something with bars she could rage against. Instead there was a room—two rooms, actually, connected by a little archway—furnished almost comfortably. A bed with a quilted coverlet, blue and white. A shelf of books, good ones, leather-bound, their spines barely cracked. Toys: a wooden horse with a real mane of horsehair, a puzzle box, a set of painted stones arranged in patterns no child had made. A window, high and barred but wide, letting in the grey light of the starless afternoon. Someone had hung curtains. Blue curtains. They matched the quilt.

It looked like a room built by someone who had studied love from a distance and gotten nearly all of it right.

The boy sat cross-legged on the rug in the middle of it, and Sera stopped breathing for a moment, because he was practicing.

He didn't know he was practicing. That was the horror of it. He was just sitting there, perfectly still, eyes half-lidded, breath slow and even, hands resting open on his knees in the exact posture Emrys had once described to her, years ago, by a campfire—the posture of a swordspeaker settling into an oath. Nobody taught him that posture. You couldn't teach it. You were born angled toward it, and then either the gift woke or it didn't, and this child, bought at four years old off the Ashmarket, had woken.

He was maybe seven now. Small for seven. Pale in the way of children raised without sun, with a mop of dark hair cut in the neat, cheap style of institutional barbering. And humming. Barely—a thread of sound, tuneless, unconscious, the kind of hum that lives in a person's chest rather than their throat. Sera knew that hum too. She'd heard it in the choir cavern, frozen mid-song in the ice, a thousand voices all holding one note.

This boy held the same note.

She must have made some sound, or shifted her weight, or simply existed too loudly, because the boy opened his eyes and looked directly at the vent grate where she crouched.

"You're in the wall," he said. Not frightened. Curious, in the mild way of a child who has never had anything to be frightened *of*, because everything frightening had been carefully explained to him as something else. "Are you a rat? You don't sound like a rat. Rats are faster."

Sera weighed about nine options in the space of a heartbeat and discarded eight. Deception was pointless; he'd already heard her. Intimidation was obscene. So she did the thing she was worst at, which was improvising honestly.

"No," she said. "I'm a friend."

"Friends aren't allowed in my room after evening bell." He considered this. "But it isn't evening yet, so maybe the rule doesn't know."

"The rule doesn't know," Sera agreed gravely. "Can I come down? I promise I'm quieter than a rat."

He nodded, and she worked the grate loose—it hadn't been properly screwed in, which told her no one had ever imagined needing to keep people *out* of this room, only the boy *in* it—and dropped lightly to the floor. Up close he was even smaller. His wrists were thin. There was chalk dust on his fingers and, she noticed with a fresh surge of something hot behind her ribs, a small ink stamp on the back of his left hand: the guild's sigil, tiny, neat, applied daily like a brand of ownership dressed up as a sticker.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Ledger." He said it easily, proudly even, the way other children said *prince* or *captain*. "Master Quillon says it's because I'm going to hold the account of the whole world someday. Everything winter costs, and everything spring pays back. It goes through me." He beamed. "Nobody else gets a job that big."

Sera's whole body went cold and hot at once, fury and nausea fighting for the same square inch of her.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's a very important job."

"It's the most important." He scrambled up, suddenly animated, tugging her sleeve with the unselfconscious familiarity of a child who had been handled kindly his whole life and therefore trusted all hands. "Do you want to see? I'm not supposed to show people, but you're in the wall, so you're probably already in trouble, and Master Aldous says trouble should be *managed*."

There it was again. *Managed.* Kind words wrapped around iron. She followed him to the window, and he pointed up at the black sky where stars should have been.

"See? No stars. That's the debt," Ledger said, with the cheerful fluency of recitation. "A long time ago, nine realms made a bargain to hold back the winter, and the bargain got broken, so now the winter is coming to collect. And everyone's scared." He shrugged, small shoulders rising and falling. "But they don't have to be. Because of me. When the covenant is reforged, I'll stand in the middle and hold it, and my voice will go into the ice, and the choir will sing again, and the stars will come back." He turned to her, luminous with certainty. "Isn't that wonderful?"

And the worst part—the part Sera knew she would carry for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be—was that it *was* wonderful. Taken sentence by sentence, it was beautiful. A child saving the world. Stars returning. An end to fear. Some careful, patient monster had taken the truest story there was—sacrifice, redemption, the small hand holding back the dark—and had fed it to a baby in spoonfuls until the baby grew up grateful. Until the baby *wanted* it.

They hadn't caged his spirit. They'd married it.

"They've been telling you this story a long time," Sera managed.

"My whole life!" He counted on stamped fingers. "First the nurses told it, then Master Aldous, then Master Quillon himself, twice. Master Quillon has a voice like a warm bath. He cried a little when he told me. Real tears." He frowned slightly, the first shadow across his face. "I asked him once why it has to be me, when there are lots of bigger people. And he said that's exactly why—because the anchor can't be strong, it has to be *still*, and I'm the stillest person in the whole guild. He gave me a medal."

"A medal?"

"Show-and-tell day. Everyone clapped." He ducked his head, suddenly shy. "I keep it under my pillow. Do you want to see it?"

No. God, no. "Maybe later," Sera said, kneeling so they were eye to eye, and made herself do the hardest thing in her considerable repertoire: nothing. Just knelt there, breathing, letting the fury drain down into something usable. Emrys would have known what to say. Emrys spoke to oaths and gods and river-things. Sera spoke to doors, mostly, usually through them, violently. But she could do this one small thing: she could look at this child and refuse, silently, permanently, to let the story end the way they'd written it.

"Ledger," she said. "Can I ask you something? And you don't have to answer."

He tilted his head. Waiting. Stilling himself, even now, even for questions—the aptitude for stillness asserting itself like a reflex.

"When your voice goes into the ice," she said carefully, "do you know what happens to *you*? To the rest of you? Your feet, and your laugh, and the parts that like the wooden horse?"

"Oh." He blinked. "Yes. Master Aldous explained. The rest of me rests." He smiled again, serene as scripture. "Forever. In the singing. Isn't that the best place to rest? Somewhere everyone can hear?"

Somewhere in the walls of her chest, something that had been holding itself together since the courtyard, since the captain, since the night the stars went out, quietly tore along a seam. She gathered him in before she fully decided to—pulled him against her shoulder, one arm around the small bird-light weight of him—and he stiffened in surprise, because apparently nobody hugged him, they *encouraged* him, they *medaled* him, and the difference between those things was the entire size of the world.

"You're crying," Ledger observed into her collar, fascinated rather than alarmed. "Is it because of the debt?"

"Yeah," Sera said roughly. "Something like that."

* * *

They sat on the rug afterward, because standing felt like too much, and he showed her the painted stones, which turned out to be a game the nurses had taught him called *Covenant*—nine stones, one for each realm, arranged in a circle around a tenth stone painted white. The white one was him. The rules, as far as she could tell, involved the white stone never moving while all the others leaned on it.

"In the real version," Ledger confided, moving a green stone a solemn inch, "the ninth realm broke the oath. That's the traitor. Master Quillon says she's the reason for everything bad. When we find her, the reforging can happen properly." He glanced up, suddenly sharp-eyed, sharper than a seven-year-old should look. "Have you found her yet? The soldiers talk. They say there's a magistrate far south who keeps her town warm. They say she might be *her*."

Ice walked down Sera's spine. Ilvane. They were closing on Ilvane. And this child, this sweet, doomed, well-read child, had been taught to hate a woman he'd never met, so that when the moment came, he'd walk into the ice hating the right person, tidy to the last.

"I wouldn't believe everything soldiers say," Sera said. "In my experience they're wrong about most things and loud about all of them."

Ledger giggled—a real giggle, unguarded, seven years old—and the sound did more damage to her composure than any blade ever had.

Above them, somewhere in the building, a bell rang. Not the evening bell. A different one—three strokes, pause, three strokes. Sera knew that pattern. Every hunter knew that pattern. Alarm. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of boots, many boots, moving with purpose.

Ledger cocked his head. "That's the assembly bell," he said. "We only get that when something important happens." His face lit. "Maybe it's the magistrate! Maybe they caught her! Then the reforging can start, and I can—" 

"Hey." Sera caught his chin gently, turned his face back to hers. Her mind was sprinting—extraction now, tonight, through a building full of mobilized guards; or stay put, wait for the chaos, use it—but underneath the tactics something else had risen, cold and clear, and she listened to it because it had kept her alive longer than tactics had. "Listen to me very carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and then we're going to have to move fast, and you're going to have to trust me more than you've ever trusted anyone."

"Okay," said Ledger, stilling beautifully.

"You are not an account. You're not a stone in a game. You're a boy, and boys get to grow up, and I am going to get you out of this building if it burns down around us. Do you understand?"

He stared at her. For the first time since the vent grate, something flickered behind his eyes—not fear exactly, but the vertigo of a child hearing a sentence with no hooks in his education to hang it on. His mouth opened. Closed.

"But," he said, very small, "if I don't do it, who holds the winter back?"

"We'll figure it out," Sera said. "That's the secret nobody told you, sweetheart: there's always another way. It just costs more than cowards want to pay."

Down the corridor, boots were running now. A door slammed. And then—impossibly, absurdly, in a building that should have contained nothing of the kind—a sound Sera knew better than her own heartbeat: the crackle-snap hiss of swordspeech, an oath being spoken aloud, forbidden words tearing through the guildhall like light through rot.

Someone was breaking a seal.

Someone was speaking an oath in the heart of the Vellum Guild.

Sera pulled Ledger behind her and reached for her blade, grinning despite everything, because she knew that voice, and it sounded furious.

"Hold onto me," she told the boy. "It's going to be a loud night."
