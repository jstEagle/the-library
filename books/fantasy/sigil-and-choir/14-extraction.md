## 14. Extraction

The plan had three parts, which Emrys would later understand was two parts too many.

"Simple," Ilvane had said at the war-table in her magistrate's hall, drawing lines in spilled tea with one elegant finger. "Sera takes the boy. You take the page. I take the blame."

"I don't like any of those assignments," Emrys had said.

"You're not meant to like them. You're meant to survive them." She'd smiled then, the smile of a woman who had spent forty years making other people's disasters look like good governance. "Besides, I've been a villain for decades, Delacroix. It would be wasteful not to use it."

Now, crouched behind a delivery cart in the shadow of the Vellum Guild's eastern wall, watching the guildhall windows burn with lamplight against a starless sky, Emrys missed the war-table the way a drowning man misses dry land.

* * *

They had come back to the city because there was no choice left. The cold was no longer weather; it was siege. In the three days since they'd found the choir beneath the ice—since Emrys had stood in that cavern and heard the frozen voices of every swordspeaker who ever lived, singing a song with no conductor—the frost had reached the river towns. Refugees moved east in long dark lines, carrying what they could, trading names for bread in villages too frightened to ask twice.

And Master Quillon had Ledger.

Emrys had seen the boy only once, through the ice, before the false reforging began: a small figure in grey, kneeling where the choir was thickest, his mouth moving in perfect unison with voices he could not possibly know. Taught to want it. That was the part Emrys couldn't put down, couldn't set aside even to sleep. A child trained the way you train a lockpick or a ledger-book, shaped for a single purpose by men who called it kindness because they fed him well.

"We get the boy," Sera had said, and her voice had been the flattest Emrys had ever heard it, which from Sera was its own kind of scream. "We get the boy, and I burn the recruitment ledgers on my way out."

"And the page?" Ilvane asked.

"The page is nothing without signatures," Emrys said. "It's paper."

"It's *proof*," Ilvane said quietly. "Quillon doesn't need it to forge a covenant. He needs it so no one else can prove theirs is real. Understand him, Delacroix. He is not a thief. He is an auditor. He wants the record to show only one version of events—his."

So: three parts. Sera would go in through the laundry chute she'd used as a hunter for six years, find the boy in the anchor-ward, and walk out the way she came. Emrys would enter as what he had been for fifteen years—a clerk—and retrieve the covenant page from the Charter Room, where Quillon kept it under triple seal, displayed like a trophy. And Ilvane—

Ilvane would stand at the front gate in her magistrate's coat, with her magistrate's papers, and demand a formal hearing on the disposition of the Corvane estates, which the guild claimed as collateral against debts incurred during the ward-fall. It was nonsense. It was magnificent nonsense. It would fill the gatehouse with clerks and guardsmen arguing procedure while the other two did their work.

"They'll arrest me within the hour," she said cheerfully. "But they'll have to *process* me first. The Vellum Guild cannot break its own charter-law in public; legitimacy is the only asset they've never devalued. Every hour they spend filing forms is an hour you spend walking."

"That's your whole plan? Be arrested?"

"My dear man, I once delayed a border war for eleven months with a dispute over the spelling of a word. Bureaucracy is the gentlest weapon ever forged and the cruelest. Go."

* * *

The laundry chute worked. Of course it worked; Sera went first and Emrys heard, faintly, the soft double-tap that meant *clear*, and he lowered himself into the dark and the smell of lye soap and thought, absurdly, of the guildhall of his youth, when he had been a junior clerk hiding his gift behind ledgers, when safety had meant being unremarkable. He had spent so many years being unremarkable that he'd nearly convinced himself it was who he was.

Not anymore. His sigil burned on his wrist now constantly, a low warm ache, like a hand held near a fire. Since the choir, since he'd felt every dead swordspeaker's voice press against the inside of his skin, the oath had stopped pretending to sleep.

He moved through the service corridors the way he'd moved through them at nineteen, hugging walls, counting turns. The guildhall was quieter than it should have been. Too quiet. He told himself it was the cold—that half the staff had fled, that the indentured workers were locked down in their quarters, that fear emptied buildings faster than plague.

He told himself a great many things that night.

The Charter Room was on the second floor, behind doors of black vellum stamped with the guild's sigil: a quill crossed with a sword, which had always struck Emrys as the most honest heraldry in the world, if anyone had bothered to read it.

His old key still worked. Fifteen years, and they hadn't struck his name from the access rolls. Some clerk had simply never gotten around to it. There was a lesson in that about institutions, and Emrys filed it away with all the others he'd never have time to teach.

Inside, the covenant page lay in a glass case on a plinth, lit by a lamp that burned without oil—one of the impossible things the guild smuggled, turned to decoration. Nine signatures. Eight legible. One smudged into shadow, though Emrys knew the name beneath it now, had watched Ilvane write it herself in the cavern dust as practice, over and over, like a penance.

He lifted the case. No alarm. Of course no alarm—Quillon wanted them to come. The realization arrived cold and complete, the way true things do, and Emrys stood there with the page in his hands and understood that he was standing inside someone else's arithmetic.

The lamps went out.

All of them, at once, except one, and by that single flame he saw Master Quillon seated at the Charter Room's reading desk, hands folded, patient as winter.

"Clerk Delacroix," Quillon said. "Or should I say swordspeaker. Do sit. We have terms to discuss, and I find you're so much more reasonable than the Corvane woman."

* * *

Later, Emrys would try to reconstruct how long the conversation lasted. Minutes? An hour? Time behaved strangely in rooms where Quillon spoke.

The old man didn't threaten. That was the horror of him. He *itemized*. He laid out, in the tone of a man reviewing quarterly figures, exactly what the guild had invested in Emrys: the forged identity, the clerkship, the fifteen years of unremarkable safety. He itemized the cost of the ward-fall—not in lives, never in lives, but in *assets destabilized*, markets awaiting correction, the great reforging that would restore order at a reasonable margin. He itemized Ledger: acquisition cost, maintenance, training, projected yield.

"He has a name," Emrys said.

"He has a designation. Names are what we give things we intend to lose." Quillon folded his hands. "You want to save him. Admirable. But consider the alternative, Delacroix. Without an anchor, the choir dies, the winter deepens, and in four years there will be no one left to name anything. One boy, bound willingly—I stress *willingly*, we have been very careful with his education—against the survival of nine realms. Show me the ledger where that trade comes out wrong."

"The ledger where it comes out wrong," Emrys said slowly, "is the one where I'm still holding the pen."

Something flickered across Quillon's face—not anger. Disappointment. The disappointment of a teacher whose best student has failed an easy exam.

"I had hoped," Quillon said, "to offer you a place in the new order. Your oath-speech is a legacy asset. But assets that refuse valuation become liabilities." He raised one finger, and the door opened behind Emrys, and hunters filled it—six of them, in grey, and at their center a face Emrys knew.

Not Sera. Worse. Brannoc, Sera's former second, a broad, careful man who had once shared a campfire with Emrys on the road east and spoken, sincerely, about wanting to retire to a farm.

"I'm sorry, Clerk," Brannoc said, and meant it, and drew his blade anyway.

What happened next happened fast, and Emrys's memory of it would always be fragments: the desk overturning, the glass case shattering, his own voice tearing loose in words he hadn't spoken aloud in fifteen years—oath-words, swordspeech, the language that made promises into edges. The lamp-flame bent toward him as if listening. Two hunters went down clutching ears that bled, because some clauses cut regardless of distance. Brannoc was better than that; Brannoc closed the gap and fought him honestly, blade to blade, and Emrys discovered that fifteen years of hiding had not rusted the gift so much as aged it, like wine in a sealed bottle, like grief.

He won. He wished he hadn't had to. Brannoc fell against the plinth and slid down it, breathing, alive, cursing him with real feeling, and Emrys took that as a kind of mercy and ran.

He ran because the page was under his coat, pressed against his heart like a second sigil. He ran because somewhere below, Sera was in the anchor-ward, and if the guild knew he was here, they knew everything. He ran because running was the only clause left in his contract with the night.

He was three corridors from the laundry chute when he heard the bell.

Not an alarm-bell. A *procedural* bell—the guild's summons chime, rung only for formal assemblies. And beneath it, muffled by stone, a voice amplified by speaking-trumpet, announcing with impeccable diction:

"*By order of the Charter Court, the prisoner Ilvane of House Corvane, having been taken at the gate in the act of fraudulent claim, shall be arraigned at dawn before the full bench of the Vellum Guild, per the rights guaranteed under Article One.*"

They had her. They'd had her almost from the moment she sat down in the gatehouse, and they'd let the clock run anyway, because Quillon had known—had *audited*—that the page would come to him eventually, and that the surest way to draw both thieves into one room was to hold the third.

Emrys reached the chute. Sera wasn't there. He waited five seconds, ten, twenty, doing the arithmetic he hated: every second he waited was a second the hunters gained, and Sera had the boy or she didn't, and either way—

She came. She came fast and furious, sliding down the chute with a bundle in her arms, and the bundle was a child, asleep, breathing, *real*, and Sera's face above him was streaked with soot and something else.

"I've got him," she whispered. "I've got Ledger. Where's Ilvane?"

Emrys looked at her, and couldn't speak, and Sera was Sera, and understood in one breath.

"No," she said. "No, we go back. We—"

"They'll arraign her at dawn," Emrys said. His voice sounded far away, clerk-flat, the voice of a man reading bad news off a page. "Public trial. Their charter-law. They want legitimacy, Sera—they want the realms to watch them destroy her *properly*. Which means—" and here the terrible clarity came, the clarity that defeat brings when it strips everything else away, "—which means she'll be in one room, guarded, scheduled, *documented*. And every realm that hates the guild will be watching."

Sera stared at him. Then, slowly, she shifted the sleeping boy higher on her shoulder and smiled the way soldiers smile at funerals.

"You're saying we crash the trial."

"I'm saying," Emrys said, "that Quillon has made one mistake in a lifetime of perfect books. He thinks a courtroom is where he's strongest." He touched the covenant page beneath his coat, and the sigil on his wrist answered with a pulse of heat, and somewhere far beneath the city, in the ice, a thousand frozen voices seemed to lean closer. "But a trial needs witnesses. And I know every hidden clause in those charters—every swordspeaker clause they buried, every signature they voided, every debt they never entered in the ledger. Ilvane taught me that law is just a story everyone agrees to believe."

He turned toward the dark of the chute, toward the city, toward dawn.

"So tomorrow," he said, "we tell them a different story."

Above them, through the grate, the starless sky waited. Below them, the choir sang its unfinished song. And in a cell in the guildhall, a woman who had broken an oath to save children sat awake, composing her defense, and smiling.
