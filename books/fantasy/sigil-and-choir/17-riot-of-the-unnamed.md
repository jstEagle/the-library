## 17. Riot of the Unnamed

The word went through the guildhall like fire through a hayrick.

Sera heard it before she understood it—a sound rising from the lower floors, from the counting rooms and the ink-wells and the dormitories where the indentured clerks slept four to a bunk. Not a scream. Not a battle cry. Something stranger: laughter. Hundreds of people laughing at once, ragged and disbelieving, the way a congregation laughs when the preacher says something impossible and true.

*Void. Void. Your contracts are void.*

Emrys had said it from the gallery floor in a voice she barely recognized—his real voice, the swordspeech voice, the one that made the air itself sit up and listen. She had been three rows back with her hood up and a guard's tabard stolen off a laundry line, and she had felt the words go through her chest like a bell struck from inside. Every hidden clause in every charter, spoken aloud and therefore unmade. Every debt the guild had written into people's bones, cancelled by a man standing in the open with his sigil blazing on his wrist like a brand of honor instead of shame.

And then he had run, because of course he had run, because Emrys Delacroix had never once in his life enjoyed being the center of anything, and now he was the center of everything.

Sera stood in the corridor outside the tribunal chamber with her heart hammering and did the arithmetic she was good at—the hunter's arithmetic, distances and doors and seconds. The tribunal chamber: empty, its guards swept away in the first wave of the crowd. The holding cells beneath it, where they'd taken Ilvane Corvane after the trial collapsed into farce. Quillon's office on the top floor, where he would already be moving, because Master Quillon had survived forty years of guild politics by always having a second plan drawn before the first one caught fire.

Two objectives. One Sera.

She went down.

* * *

The stairwell to the cells smelled of lamp oil, which told her everything. Someone in the guild's chain of command had given the order to burn the records—again, *always again*, this institution answering every crisis with a match—and the clerks tasked with carrying out that order had simply set the oil down and walked away to join the shouting. There were ledgers stacked in the landings, unburned, their brass clasps winking in the lamplight like little smug faces.

A cell door banged somewhere below. Then another. Then a sound Sera knew from border skirmishes: the particular clang of a lock being forced by someone who had never forced a lock before and was learning quickly.

She rounded the last turn and found the undercroft in a state of joyous vandalism. Indentured workers—she counted a dozen, then more, ink-stained fingers and threadbare collars—were prying at cell doors with ledger-spines and pry-bars requisitioned from the shipping floor. A woman Sera vaguely recognized from the manifest office stood on an overturned desk directing traffic with the crisp authority of someone who had spent twenty years being talked over.

"—the tall one, the magistrate, they brought her in yesterday, third door—no, your *other* left, Tam, honestly—"

The third door gave way with a shriek of unhappy iron just as Sera reached it.

Ilvane Corvane sat on the cot inside like a queen who had been mildly inconvenienced by a revolution. Her wrists were chained—not guild standard, Sera noted; these were oath-irons, the kind that bit into covenant-marks—and there was a bruise blooming along her jaw where someone had been insufficiently reverent during her arrest. She looked up at Sera, and her expression performed a complicated maneuver: recognition, wariness, and something almost like amusement.

"Hunter Voss," Ilvane said. "I'd heard you'd gone traitor. I confess I hoped the reports were exaggerated. You were always so *good* at following orders."

"I've gotten worse." Sera shouldered past a clerk who was trying to help and took the chains in both hands. Oath-iron didn't respond to force; it responded to authority. Fortunately, as of roughly half an hour ago, the authority structure of the Vellum Guild was a smoking hole. "Hold still."

"You're going to pick a lock with your *hands*?"

"I'm going to inform the lock that its contract is void." She pressed her thumb against the shackle and said, in her plainest speaking voice, because Emrys had spent one frantic hour teaching her this in a ditch outside Ilvane's town: "The bond is dissolved. Open."

The iron shivered, considered, and fell open like a flower deciding to give up.

Ilvane stared at her own freed wrist. "That," she said slowly, "is the single most undignified piece of magic I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a river-god demand a toll in memories."

"Emrys taught me. He was very apologetic about it."

"Of course he was." Ilvane rose, rolled her shoulders, and flexed her fingers as though testing whether she still owned them. Around them the undercroft roared—another cell door surrendering, someone cheering, the woman from manifests calling for torches to be brought *upstairs*, not lit *down here*, have some sense. Ilvane watched it all with an expression Sera couldn't read, and then said, quietly, for Sera alone: "I spent nine years building a town where everyone kept their word because they wanted to. And this lot—" she gestured at the chaos, the laughter, the ledgers being pried apart like oysters— "this lot just needed someone to tell them they were allowed."

"Does that bother you?"

"It *delights* me, which is far more disturbing." She stepped out of the cell, straightened her ruined magistrate's coat, and became, visibly, a person with a plan. "Where is Emrys?"

"Fled upward. Which means Quillon will be moving too. If we don't reach the boy before Quillon does—"

"Then the boy becomes an anchor and the choir becomes a leash, and we spend the next thousand years apologizing to a child we failed to save." Ilvane was already walking. "Yes. I did the same arithmetic. Move."

They went up together, two women who between them had betrayed an order, defected from a hunt, and broken a covenant, climbing through a building full of strangers who were learning, loudly and clumsily and with tremendous enthusiasm, how to be free.

* * *

The upper floors were where the riot stopped being funny.

Sera had expected anger—had perhaps even counted on it—but what she found on the fourth landing stopped her cold. The indentured workers weren't smashing things. They were *reading*. Dozens of them, kneeling in corridors with charter-pages spread on the floor, tracing the clauses Emrys had voided, touching the words where their names were written as though checking that they still existed. An old man sat against the wall weeping over a contract, not tearing it, just—weeping, his thumb resting on the line that had bound him since he was nine years old.

"They're not destroying the guild," Ilvane murmured beside her. "They're *auditing* it."

"And when they find the pages that aren't void? The ones Quillon signed personally?"

"Then," Ilvane said, "we may yet get our smashing."

They found the smashers on the fifth floor. Or rather, the smashers found them—or found Quillon, rather, or found the space where Quillon had been, because the master's office door hung open and the room beyond had been emptied with terrifying efficiency. No papers burned this time. Papers were evidence; evidence was leverage; Quillon Vell had built his life on leverage. He had taken everything, and he had taken it *neatly*.

Except—

Sera crossed to the window. It overlooked the delivery yard, and in the delivery yard, torchlight moved in disciplined lines. Guild enforcers, maybe thirty of them, the ones whose loyalty had been purchased too deeply for one speech to undo, forming a column around two figures being hurried toward the sledge-gate.

One figure was tall, gray-robed, unhurried even in flight. Quillon walked like a man leaving a meeting early, not a man fleeing a riot.

The other figure was small. Barefoot, despite the cold. Walking with the terrible docility of someone who had been taught all his life that obedience was love.

"Ledger," Sera breathed.

Even at this distance she could see the boy turn his head once, looking back up at the guildhall—at the windows full of light and shouting, at the world that was busy freeing everyone else—as if checking whether freedom came in his size.

It did not, apparently. Not tonight.

"He's taking him to the cavern," Ilvane said. Her voice had gone flat and cold, the magistrate's voice, the voice she used for sentences. "The false reforging. If Quillon anchors the choir through that child, the covenant binds to *him*, and everything Emrys sacrificed in that gallery becomes—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Sledges. They'll take the ice road east. It's fast, but it's exposed, and the cold is getting worse."

"The cold is getting *lethal*," Sera said. Through the window, snow was falling upward—that was the only way to describe it, flakes lifting off the yard stones as though the winter itself had begun pulling the warmth out of the world in earnest. The refugees would feel it first. The refugees were already feeling it. "Half the city will be on that road within a day, chasing warmth they can't name. Quillon will have crowds at the cavern whether he wants them or not."

"Then we want them more." Ilvane turned from the window, and for a moment Sera saw why nine realms had once trusted this woman with a signature, and why she had earned the word *traitor* fair and square, and why Emrys—who hated everyone efficiently—had insisted, with the stubbornness of a man reading a very old contract, that she was the key. "We need horses, cold-gear, and a route that beats a sledge to the cavern. In a city in flames."

"In a city in *celebration*," Sera corrected. She nodded toward the corridor, where the old man with the contract had risen, wiped his face, folded his voided bondage into careful quarters, and put it in his pocket like a keepsake. Around him, people were organizing. Badly, loudly, with tremendous argument—but organizing. Someone had found the armory keys. Someone else had found the kitchens and was insisting, with the moral fervor of the recently liberated, that no revolution succeeded on an empty stomach. "They'll give us what we ask. I think they'd give us the roof beams if we explained why."

"Then explain fast." Ilvane paused at the doorway, and something flickered across her face—not doubt, exactly. Recognition. "Hunter Voss. When we catch them—and we will catch them—Quillon will offer you your old life back. Your rank, your name, your clean hands. He's very good at making slavery sound like a career."

Sera thought of the captain she had cut down at Ilvane's gate. Thought of the look in his eyes—not betrayal, worse, *understanding*, the look of a man who had known for years what she was capable of and had simply waited for the day it pointed inward. She thought of her name, Sera Voss, and how much of it had belonged to the guild, and how light the remainder felt.

"He can keep it," she said. "The name. I'm working on a new one."

Ilvane smiled—briefly, sharply, like a knife agreeing with you—and led the way down into the noise.

* * *

By the time they reached the yard, the riot had become something no one had planned and no one could stop, and it was beautiful.

The enforcers' column had stalled at the sledge-gate, hemmed in not by soldiers but by *clerks*—hundreds of them, linking arms, singing. That was the part Sera couldn't get over, the part she would carry for the rest of her life: they were singing. Some work-song from the shipping floors, probably, something about crates and customs duties, transformed by five hundred cracked and joyful voices into something that sounded like the beginning of a country. The enforcers stood in their ranks with their batons ready and their faces failing them, because you cannot charge into a song without becoming the villain of it, and even bought men know that.

Quillon's party had slipped out the postern anyway—of course they had; Quillon would no more fight a crowd than argue with weather—and Sera saw the sledge's runners flash white on the ice road beyond the wall, dwindling east toward the dark.

But the crowd wasn't watching the sledge. The crowd was watching the sky.

Or rather, the absence of sky. The starless night pressed down on the city like a lid, and now, with the wards dead and the winter deepening, people had begun streaming out of the guildhall's doors into the streets, thousands of them, the unnamed and the newly-named, holding torches and contracts and each other, all facing east. They couldn't have said why. Sera understood why. Everyone in the realms had begun to feel it lately, the pull, faint as a remembered melody—the sense that something was starving out there beneath the ice, something that had sung for them once and meant to sing again if anyone would only let it.

"They're going to follow us," Sera said quietly. "All of them. We can't stop them and we shouldn't."

"No," Ilvane agreed. "We lead them."

She turned to the crowd, raised her voice—and Sera, watching, understood that this too was a kind of magic, older than swordspeech, cruder and more durable: a woman who knew how to be heard.

"People of the Vellum Guild!" Ilvane's voice carried across the yard, and the singing faltered, and five hundred faces turned. "Your bonds are broken! Your names are your own! And the men who wrote those bonds are fleeing east with a child, to bind a power that belongs to *all of you*!" She flung out her arm toward the starless horizon. "The road is long and the cold is killing, but I say this: no one freezes while we have coats to share, and no one walks alone while we have arms to link! Who rides east?"

The answer came back not as words but as a roar, and then, immediately, as logistics—which was somehow more moving. Coats appeared. Horses were argued over. The woman from the manifest office began compiling a supply list on the back of a voided contract, and someone shouted that the brewery cellars should be opened because morale was also supplies, and someone else shouted that this was exactly the kind of thinking that had gotten the guild into trouble, and both of them were cheered.

Sera found a horse, found a second horse for Ilvane, found herself laughing—actually laughing—in the middle of a revolution, and realized that the feeling in her chest was hope. Not the tidy hope of plans. The chaotic kind. The kind that smells like woodsmoke and bad singing and doesn't survive contact with reality so much as *recruit* it.

They rode out through the postern gate onto the ice road as the first ranks of refugees fell in behind them, a ragged, torchlit tide flowing east.

Ahead, the darkness over the glacier was absolute. Somewhere inside it, a boy called Ledger was being carried toward a fate he had been taught to want, and a man named Quillon was preparing to steal a song.

Sera leaned low over her horse's neck, felt the cold sharpen until breathing hurt, and thought: *run, then. We're coming, and we are not coming quietly.*

Behind her, five hundred people sang.
