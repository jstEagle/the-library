## 20. The Ninth Signature

Ilvane Corvane had spent forty years learning to be the last person in the room to speak.

It was a magistrate's discipline: let the farmers argue, let the merchants bluster, let the petitioners exhaust their grievances like children emptying pockets of pebbles. By the time everyone else had finished talking, the truth usually stood alone in the middle of the floor, shivering and obvious, waiting for someone to pick it up.

But tonight, in the blue-lit war-room carved into the throat of the ice-cavern, no one would stop looking at her.

Eight names already glowed on the covenant page, inked in a script that was not quite ink—each signature had sunk into the parchment like a stone dropped in still water, leaving rings of faint light that pulsed when its signer breathed. Sera Voss's mark burned steady and soldier-straight. Ledger—the boy, gods help them all, the boy whose whole name was a filing system—had signed with the careful, enormous letters of a child who had been taught his handwriting might matter someday, and had never once been told why.

And at the bottom of the page, beneath eight small suns, waited an empty space the size of a hand.

Hers.

"You're staring," Emrys said quietly. He sat across the table from her, his wrists bare, the oath-sigil on his left arm visible for the first time since she'd known him—a spiral of old light that looked less like a tattoo than a scar that had decided to be beautiful. "You've been staring at it for an hour."

"I'm reading," Ilvane said. "There's a difference. One is rude; the other is due diligence."

"Forty years as a magistrate and you still think you can lawyer your way out of things."

"I *have* lawyered my way out of things. It's how I'm alive." She smoothed the page flat with both palms. The parchment was warm under her fingers, warmer than it had any right to be in a cavern where the cold came up through the floor like a question nobody wanted to answer. "I want to know exactly what I'm agreeing to before I agree to it. Which, incidentally, is the entire reason we are in this situation."

The table went quiet in the particular way tables go quiet when someone has just confessed to a crime everyone has been politely ignoring for three days.

Sera cleared her throat. "That's—"

"I know what I said." Ilvane lifted a hand before the huntress could finish. "Yes. I broke the original covenant. Yes, I froze it mid-oath rather than let it take another child every hundred years. And yes, the winter that has fallen on nine realms is, in a strictly legal sense, my fault." She looked down at the blank space. "I've had a long time to make peace with the arithmetic. What I haven't had time for is the fine print. So someone read me the fine print."

"There isn't any," Emrys said. "That's the problem. The original terms were written by nine rulers in a room that smelled of tallow and fear, and they were vague in the way frightened people are vague—they wrote 'a tithe of the young' instead of saying outright that they meant children, because saying it outright would have made it real. My guild spent two centuries exploiting that vagueness. We are not going to repeat it." He turned the page toward her. "The new terms say exactly what happens. No hedging. No tithe. One anchor, willing, permanent."

"*Permanent*," Ilvane repeated.

"The choir needs a voice it can hold onto. Someone who speaks the covenant's terms aloud, every dawn, forever, so the words stay true." His voice didn't waver. She noticed he'd stopped letting it waver somewhere around chapter nineteen, so to speak—somewhere between the duel and Quillon's unmaking, he had folded all his trembling up and put it away like winter clothes out of season. "We've each taken our turn refusing. It's tradition now. You'll want to get yours in before the signing or the page will think something's wrong."

Ilvane almost smiled. Almost. "And if no one signs?"

"Then the stars stay dark until the last hearth goes out, and the last name gets traded for firewood, and after that there won't be anyone left to remember there was ever a bargain at all." He spread his hands. "Ledger offered first, by the way. Before anyone could stop him."

"We did stop him," Sera said flatly.

"He cried about it," Emrys added, "which was the most honest negotiation this table has seen."

"And then he tried to negotiate," Sera went on, warming despite herself, despite the end of the world sitting on their shoulders like snow, "in favor of Emrys taking the role. On the grounds that Emrys is 'already mostly oath-shaped.'"

"That boy is going to be a terror in ten years," Ilvane said. "I mean that as a compliment."

She looked around the table. Emrys, hollowed out and lit through, like a lantern with too little candle left. Sera, who had cut down her own captain four days ago and still sometimes touched her sword hilt the way other people touch a bruise. Ledger, asleep against the far wall with the covenant page's glow reflected on his cheek, having been promised he could stay awake for the signing and having lost that battle in approximately eleven minutes.

They had all refused. All eight. Bravely, generously, tearfully refused—and the covenant needed nine.

*So it comes back to me,* she thought. *Of course it does. Debts always know the address.*

*

She asked to be alone with the page.

Emrys resisted—for about four seconds, which for him constituted a personal record for yielding ground—and then he and Sera withdrew to the mouth of the cavern, where the refugees' cookfires made small orange constellations against the dark, a sky people could actually afford.

Alone, Ilvane pulled her chair closer and did what she had done every working day for forty years: she read the contract.

Not skimmed. Not scanned. Read. Every clause of the new covenant, drafted over three exhausted nights by a swordspeaker, a defected hunter, a former magistrate, and a child who kept suggesting they add a clause about mandatory snacks. The language was plain—deliberately, almost aggressively plain, because Emrys had insisted that any document written to bind the world should be legible to the world it bound.

Clause one: *No tithe of children, now or ever.* She read it twice, pressing her thumb against the words like she could feel the pulse of every century's stolen child underneath. This was why she had broken the old covenant. Not ambition. Not fear. She had been young and a signatory and she had watched a village line up its seven-year-olds for selection with the grim, resigned efficiency of people choosing which lambs to slaughter, and something in her had simply said *no*, and kept saying no for four hundred years while the frozen oath starved and the wards thinned and the winter gathered itself like a debt collector studying a map.

Everyone called it betrayal. She had never managed to call it anything but the only moral act available to her at the time—which was, admittedly, also the act that had doomed them all on a delay timer. Both things were true. That was the trouble with truth: it refused to consolidate.

Clause two: *One anchor, freely given, voice and name surrendered to the covenant's keeping.*

She read that one several times too, and here she caught herself doing something shameful. Here she caught herself hoping.

Because the arithmetic was seductive, and she knew seductive arithmetic better than anyone alive. Emrys should do it—he was the last swordspeaker, the sigil-bearer, the obvious candidate. Or Sera—strong, loyal, owed nothing. Or even Ledger, whispered the old guild-logic in her ear, the logic Quillon had fed that poor boy with sugar and scripture—*the child is suited, the child was prepared, the sacrifice is efficient.*

Ilvane Corvane had spent forty years sentencing people, and she knew precisely how a sentence felt from the inside of the bench. Cold. Clean. Final. And utterly silent about the person standing in the dock.

"No," she said aloud, to the page, to the ghost of her own cleverness. "You don't get to be clean. Not yours. Not mine."

Because here was the thing she had understood since the moment Quillon's forged covenant had spat him out like bad coin: the covenant did not want a sacrifice. Sacrifice was the guild's currency—spend someone else, bank the profit. What the covenant wanted, what the choir beneath the ice had been starving for, was *answerability*. A voice that stayed. A name that could be called. Someone who, when the terms were tested in three hundred years by some frightened ruler with vague language and good intentions, would still be there to say *no, we agreed otherwise, I am the word and the word remembers.*

Punishment ended. Accountability didn't. She had built a career on knowing the difference, and now the difference was asking her to demonstrate it.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with detached professional interest, the way she'd once noticed her own hands shaking while signing a death warrant, and had concluded then—as now—that shaking hands and certain decisions were not mutually exclusive. Sometimes the shake was simply the body insisting on being honest while the mind did its work.

She took out her pen.

Not a ceremonial pen. Not a blood-quill or a sigil-stylus or whatever dramatic instrument the founders had used. Her magistrate's pen, the plain black one she had signed four thousand judgments with, the one with a chewed cap because she thought best while worrying things with her teeth. If she was going to bind herself to nine realms until the end of days, she was going to do it with the same pen she'd used to fine Farmer Ostrey three coppers for an unlicensed goat.

She thought of the town she'd ruled. They had worshipped her—not her, exactly, but the warmth she'd held for them, the functioning order, the lights that stayed lit while the world outside forgot its own name. She had let them worship. That had been her second sin, smaller and softer than the first: accepting gratitude for a warmth she'd stolen from a frozen engine. Somewhere in her town right now, probably, a family was burning a chair to stay warm and blaming the stars.

"They deserved better than a goddess," she murmured to the page. "They deserved a magistrate who answered questions. So did everyone."

The page glowed a little brighter. She chose to interpret this as agreement rather than impatience, though honestly it could have been either.

She thought of Emrys. Of course she thought of Emrys; the man had a talent for occupying thoughts the way water occupies a cellar. He hated her—or had hated her, or hated what she'd done, which amounted to the same thing worn differently—and yet he had crossed a dying country to find her, because the covenant needed her name and because, she suspected, some stubborn clerk's ledger in his heart insisted that debts must be collected from the debtor and no one else. She respected that. It was the most annoying thing about him.

If he took the anchor's burden, the last swordspeaker would vanish into a word. The guild would be dismantled and its final victim would be its own founder's heir, and history would file it neatly under *tragic necessity*, and no one would ever be answerable for anything, because the one man who could testify to everything would have traded his voice for the privilege of silence.

No. She pressed the pen to the space below eight glowing names.

Let it be the traitor. Let it be the woman who broke it. Let the covenant's living voice belong to the person with the most to answer for—that way, every dawn when the terms were spoken, they would be spoken by someone who knew exactly what happened when oaths went soft. Justice wasn't the axe. Justice was staying in the room.

"My name," she said, and her voice came out steadier than her hands, "is Ilvane Corvane. Magistrate, formerly. Signatory, formerly. Traitor, according to some; the only adult in the room, according to me. I broke the old covenant because it ate children, and I will spend whatever remains of eternity making sure the new one never learns the taste."

She signed.

*

The ink sank. Of course it sank—it was barely ink—but the sensation was nothing like the others had described. For Sera it had been warmth. For Emrys, recognition. For Ilvane it was *weight*: the feeling of a judgment passed, a sentence accepted, a door closing behind her not as a prison but as a commitment. Like stepping off a dock into water she had personally measured.

Light ran up her arm. Not pain—accountability has a texture all its own, and this was it, cool and thorough, like an auditor's finger running down a column of figures and finding, at long last, that they balanced.

The eighth ring flared. Then, between the eight small suns on the page, a ninth kindled—hers—and the whole covenant shivered like a struck bell.

And beneath them, deep in the ice, something began—very faintly, very distantly—to sing.

Footsteps. Emrys and Sera came at a run, skidding on the frost-slick floor, Ledger waking behind them with his hair sticking up like a startled hedgehog. They crowded the doorway, staring at her, at the page, at the ninth light.

"You signed," Sera said.

"I read it first," Ilvane said. "There's a difference."

Emrys was looking at her with an expression she couldn't catalogue—relief and grief and something dangerously close to respect, all fighting for the same square inch of face. "Ilvane. The anchor's voice. The dawn-speaking. Forever. Do you understand what—"

"I sentenced myself," she said. "Forty years of practice. I assure you the paperwork is flawless." She set down her pen, aligned it neatly beside the page, and permitted herself, at last, the smile she'd been rationing. "Besides, someone at this table needs to remain capable of answering questions, and between the three of you, the current total is zero."

Ledger pushed past the adults to peer at the ninth glow, eyes enormous. "Does it hurt?"

"It weighs," she said. "There's a difference."

He considered this with the gravity of a judge twice his age, then nodded, satisfied, and reached out to touch the edge of the page—

—and the singing below the ice swelled, sudden and vast, a chord rising through the rock like water through a well, and every ring of light on the covenant flared white, and the page spoke.

Not in words. In summons. Ilvane felt it land in her chest like a hand closing gently around her heart, and saw it strike Emrys harder—he staggered, gripping the table, his sigil blazing through his sleeve.

"What is it?" Sera demanded. "What's happening?"

Emrys lifted his head. His eyes had gone wide and wet and lit from within, and when he answered, his voice came out strange—reverent and terrified at once, the voice of a man hearing a song he'd been humming his whole life without knowing it.

"The choir knows," he said. "All nine names are on the page. The terms are true." He swallowed. "It wants its voice. It wants—" his hand rose slowly to his own throat— "*it wants me.*"

Outside the cavern, above a world that had forgotten the concept, something impossible flickered in the starless dark.

A single point of light. Wavering. Trying.

Like a candle remembering how.
