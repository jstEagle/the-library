## 21. Oath of the Last Swordspeaker

The choir had stopped singing.

That was the first thing Emrys understood as he stood at the edge of the ice-cavern, the cold pressing against him like a creditor's hand. For twenty chapters of his life—no, for twenty years—he had carried the sigil on his wrist like a debt he'd never read the terms of. Now he could hear the silence underneath everything, vast and patient, and he understood that the silence had been there all along. The voices frozen in the ice had not been singing to keep winter away.

They had been singing to keep *themselves* here. And they were running out.

"Emrys." Sera's voice came from behind him, roughened by the battle still echoing up the stairwell—Quillon's unmaking had left a smell in the air like burnt vellum and ozone, like a contract tearing itself apart. "Tell me you have a plan that doesn't end with you dead."

"I have a plan," Emrys said. "It ends with me mostly dead."

"That's not better."

"It's significantly better than fully dead. I've done the arithmetic."

He turned. Sera stood in the cavern mouth with Ledger's hand in hers—the boy was pale, hollow-eyed, still wearing the guild's gray livery, still flinching at loud noises—and behind them both Ilvane Corvane leaned against the ice wall, her magistrate's composure cracked down the middle like a dropped plate glued back together badly. Her signature sat fresh and black on the covenant page in Emrys's satchel. The ninth name. The last piece.

Except it wasn't. He'd known since the river-god, since the smudged line, since the moment Quillon's forged covenant had rejected him and unraveled him into ash and screaming. A covenant needed nine signatures and one anchor. Nine names willing. One voice permanent.

Ilvane had signed. Sera would have signed; he'd seen her reach for the pen before he caught her wrist. Ledger—gods, the boy had *offered*, because they had taught him to want this, had spent his whole small life dressing sacrifice up as belonging, and Emrys would sooner cut off his own other hand than let that stand.

Which left, as it always left, him.

"You don't have to explain it again," Sera said, reading his face the way she'd read it across training yards and shared campfires and one disastrous harvest festival where they'd gotten drunk on plum wine and sworn an oath of their own, a childish one, about never leaving each other behind. "I heard you explain it to the river-god. I heard you explain it to Ilvane. I heard you explain it to *me* while we were running, which, incidentally, is a terrible time for a lecture."

"I'm a clerk," Emrys said. "Explaining things at terrible times is my whole profession."

* * *

They made camp in the antechamber above the choir, because even a man walking toward his own dissolution deserved a hot meal first, and because Sera refused—absolutely, non-negotiably refused—to let him do this hungry. She built a fire from the emergency stores in her hunter's pack. Ilvane produced, from somewhere in her ruined finery, a tin of spiced tea that she claimed was tribute from her town and which tasted, Emrys thought, like someone had steeped bark in ambition.

Ledger sat close to the fire and watched Emrys with the particular intensity of a child who has been told a story and is waiting to find out if it's true.

"You're really going to stop being a person?" the boy asked.

Emrys nearly laughed. Nearly. "That's a very direct question."

"I'm not allowed to ask direct questions usually. I'm practicing."

"Well then." Emrys turned his tea in his hands. "I'm going to stop being *named*. There's a difference. A name is what other people call you. It's the handle on the drawer. What I'll be is more like—" he searched for it, clerk that he was, reaching for the precise term—"more like the word itself. The oath will need someone to hold it, the way a ledger needs a hand to write in it. I'll be the hand. And the ink. And possibly the desk."

"The desk?" said Sera.

"I'm improvising the metaphor. Don't audit me."

That got a wet, surprised laugh out of Ledger, and Emrys counted it as a victory worth more than anything in any shipment he'd ever logged. Bottled thunder, moon-silk, memory-salt—all of it had moved through his hands in neat columns, and none of it had ever mattered half so much as making one frightened boy laugh at a bad metaphor about desks.

Ilvane spoke without looking up from the fire. "There may be another way. There's always another way. Give me a year and I'll find it—I found ways out of oaths before."

"You found a way to *pause* an oath," Emrys said gently. "And the interest came due all at once, on a night when every star went dark. I've read your terms, Ilvane. I audited them myself, back when I didn't know whose signature I was squinting at under the smudge. You bought a century of mercy with a century of debt. It was the best bargain anyone's ever struck. But I'm not going to let you strike it twice, because this time the collateral would be everyone."

"And you think *you're* acceptable collateral?"

"I think I'm the only entry in the column that balances."

She looked at him then, and for a moment he saw past the magistrate, past the traitor, past the legend, to whatever tired original woman had signed her name in blood a hundred years ago because she couldn't bear the arithmetic of children. She saw him, too, he suspected. Saw the clerk and the swordspeaker and the coward who had hidden in ledgers for fifteen years, all of them agreeing on one line at last.

"You were the worst auditor the Vellum Guild ever employed," she said. "You asked questions."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment."

"It absolutely was."

* * *

He wrote letters that night. It seemed important. A man who had spent his life among documents should leave some.

To Sera: *You were the best part of the order, which was a low bar shaped like a ditch, and you cleared it by a mile. Keep Ledger. Teach him everything except how to make tea, because yours is a war crime.* He crossed out the last clause and rewrote it: *Teach him tea too. He should know what bad tea tastes like so he recognizes good.*

To Ilvane: *Your argument sounded like mercy because it was. That was always the horror of it. Sign well, serve long, and when they curse your name—and they will—remember that one clerk thought the curse was earned but the mercy was real anyway.*

To Ledger he simply wrote: *You are not a price. You were never a price. Whoever told you otherwise was wrong, and now they're ash, and you get to be a person who reads books. Start with something funny.*

And to himself—one line, because a clerk closes his own books: *Emrys Delacroix, swordspeaker, keeper of small impossibilities. Account settled.*

Sera read over his shoulder without shame, because she had never once respected the privacy of correspondence, and when she finished she said nothing for a long time. Then: "The plum wine oath. Does it count? Never leave each other behind?"

"I'm not leaving you behind," Emrys said. "I'm staying exactly where I am, forever, in a cave full of singing ghosts. If anything, you're the one abandoning me."

"That's the worst legal reasoning I've ever heard, and I've heard Ilvane argue tax law."

"She's very proud of that."

"I know. It's insufferable."

But she was crying, quietly, the way hunters cry—efficiently, hating every second of it—and Emrys put his arm around his oath-sister and let the fire pop and the ice groan overhead, and neither of them said anything else, because there wasn't anything else, and because they had both spent their whole lives learning that kept word matters more than pretty speech.

* * *

Dawn did not come. There were no stars to mark its approach and no sun behind the dark, only the slow turning of a world that had forgotten how to light itself. But Emrys felt the hour anyway, in the sigil on his wrist, which had begun to warm like a stone left too near a hearth.

He walked down into the choir alone. They had agreed on that—Sera had argued, loudly, and lost, and stood at the top of the stairs with her jaw set like a drawn bow—but at the last step he heard footsteps and turned to find all three of them following anyway.

"You said alone," he said.

"You said *alone*," Sera agreed. "I never said I'd honor it. Check your witnesses. Verbal contracts require consideration, and my consideration is that I refuse."

"That's not how consideration works."

"I'm a hunter, not a lawyer. Refuse."

So they came, the three of them, standing at the cavern's edge while Emrys walked out onto the ice floor where the voices hung suspended—hundreds of them, thousands, generations of swordspeakers frozen mid-song, their faces serene and starving, each one a name traded away to hold back the cold. He knew some of their faces from the guild's oldest portraits. Master Ondrey, who had founded the order. Little Maren, who had anchored at eleven years old, because the old terms had allowed such things, because the old terms had *demanded* them.

"I'm sorry," he told them. All of them. His voice cracked on it. "We were supposed to relieve you centuries ago. We let the paperwork lapse."

The sigil burned. Not painfully anymore—it had passed through pain into something warmer, stranger, like a hand taking his. And beneath the silence he heard it: the first faint thread of the choir answering. Not singing yet. Listening.

He unrolled the covenant page on the ice. Nine signatures, eight old and one new, Ilvane's ink still sharp enough to cut. The tenth line waited, blank, labeled in the founders' careful script: *ANCHOR — ONE VOICE, GIVEN WHOLLY, WITHDRAWN NEVER.*

"No more children," Emrys said aloud, to the ice, to the ghosts, to whatever force kept the terms of things. "No more names taken from people who wanted to keep them. Those clauses die tonight. I'm striking them." He drew his pen—his good pen, the one he'd used to audit bottled thunder—and he crossed through the old terms with three clean lines, and the ice shuddered, and somewhere far above, snow began to fall upward.

Then he knelt, and he began to speak.

Not the words he'd expected. He had prepared an oath, drafted and redrafted, elegant as a charter. But the sigil took his mouth the way a river takes a channel, and what came out was older, simpler, in a language he had never studied and somehow knew:

*I am the last, and I am enough.*
*I give my voice to the word, and my word to the winter,*
*and my winter to the spring.*
*Let no child pay. Let no name be stolen.*
*Let the stars remember what we promised.*
*Let me forget my name so theirs are safe.*
*This is the account. I sign it with my throat.*

It didn't hurt. That was the strange mercy of it. It felt like setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine. His voice went first—not silenced but *spent*, poured out of him syllable by syllable into the ice, and the choir drank it, and woke.

Oh, they woke. The sound rose through the cavern like dawn through water—every frozen voice unfurling at once, and his own voice woven among them, already indistinguishable, already home. The ice bloomed with light. Above them, through a hundred feet of glacier, something answered: a single point of white fire kindling in the black sky, then another, then a handful, then a spill of them, stars relighting one by one like lamps lit down a long street by hands they couldn't see.

Sera made a sound he'd never heard from her—half laugh, half grief—and Ledger pointed at the ceiling with both hands, and Ilvane Corvane, breaker of oaths, fell to her knees on the ice and wept like a woman finally allowed to.

And Emrys—

Emrys remained. That was the whole of it, and it was everything. He felt his name go next, *Emrys Delacroix*, dissolving like sugar in warm water, and he found he could hold onto one thing if he chose, one small keepsake, and he chose carefully, the way he'd always chosen, auditing even his own soul:

He kept the warmth of a fire on a night when a frightened boy had laughed at a joke about desks.

Then even that softened, spread thin, became not a memory but a quality—a warmth in the light of the first star each dawn, though no one would ever quite be able to say why.

The stars came back all night, one by one, patient as entries in a ledger being written by a steady hand.

And far below, in the deep places of the world, something old and stagnant stirred in its riverbed—a god who had warned him, who had bargained fairly, who had given him the knowledge that cost a memory of his master—and it turned its face toward the newly burning sky, and it smiled, and it began, slowly, to flow.
