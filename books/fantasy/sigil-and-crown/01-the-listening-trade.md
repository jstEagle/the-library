## 1. The Listening Trade

The knife knew the way. That was the first thing Felix had learned about bone—that it remembered the hand that shaped it, and if you were patient enough, it would remember everything else too.

They sat in the back room of their shop on Tallow Row, third door past the chandler's, beneath a sign that read *Ravensworth: Fine Carvings*. The sign was a lie the way most respectable things are lies. Tourists from the low country bought buttons and hairpins and little whistles that sounded like birds, and they paid well for them, and they never asked why a carver of such obvious talent had settled in Ashmoor, which was not a town so much as a wound that had scabbed over into commerce. They never asked why every piece Felix sold bore a small mark near the base—a raven with one wing folded—stamped so faintly you'd miss it unless you knew to look.

The dead knew to look.

Felix had been hearing them since before they could name what they heard. As a child, lying awake in a house whose location they no longer admitted to anyone, they'd listened to the walls talk—not rats, not wind, but voices with opinions, voices with unfinished business, voices that had been interrupted mid-sentence by something final and wanted desperately to finish it. Most people's houses were quiet. Felix's had never once been quiet, not in any of the eleven places they'd lived since leaving home, and home was a word they used only in the dark, only to themselves, only in the grammar of someone who has lost a thing so completely that even its name is contraband.

So they'd made a trade of it. Not the gift itself—never that, never openly—but the shape of it, hollowed out and dressed in craft until it looked like nothing more than skill. A client came with a message for the dead. Felix took the message into the bone, carved it letter by letter into a token the client could wear or bury or burn, and somewhere in the carving—the clients all said this, in the hushed tone people reserve for things they can't explain without embarrassment—they felt *heard*. The words went in and something answered. Grief got its receipt. Felix got paid. Nobody asked questions, because asking questions in Thornfell these days was how you ended up on a list, and lists in Thornfell ended at the edge of a pit outside the capital, where the Regent's men did their arithmetic in bodies.

The kingdom of Thornfell had died three years ago. Officially it had never existed at all now; the maps had been redrawn, the histories rewritten, the boundary stones pulled up and ground into gravel for the Regent's new roads. But countries don't die just because someone files paperwork. Countries die the way people do—slowly, in pieces, each piece still warm, still talking, still wanting things. Ashmoor was one of those pieces. A smuggler town stitched into the mountains' southern skirt, it ran on goods that officially didn't exist moving along roads that officially didn't exist, sold by people who officially weren't there, to buyers who officially had never crossed any border because there was no border to cross. Everyone in Ashmoor understood the arrangement. You kept your head down, your ledgers vague, and your loyalty liquid. Neutrality wasn't a virtue here. It was survival, same as water.

Felix had gotten very good at neutrality. It was, they told themselves some nights, the whole point of the shop's respectability—the fine carvings, the fixed prices, the polite refusal to know anything about anybody. Felix carved messages into bone. What the bones said back was between the client and their dead. Felix was just the instrument. Instruments didn't take sides.

It was almost true. It was true enough to sleep on, most nights.

* * *

Maren Kettle came in just after the afternoon bell, when the light through the front window went amber and the smell of the chandler's tallow drifted in like weather. Felix knew her the way you know everyone in a town like Ashmoor—by sight, by rumor, by the particular quality of her silence. She'd run the dye works before the war, back when Ashmoor had colors worth naming. Now she mended nets and didn't talk much, and everyone pretended not to notice that her son had been taken by the Regent's men eight months ago and had come back in a cart with his hands tied and a hole in him the size of a fist.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, working her cap in her fingers, and Felix set down the whistle they were boring and waited. Waiting was part of the trade. The dead rushed; the living needed time.

"I want—" Maren started, stopped, started again. "I heard you can do things. With bone."

"Carvings," Felix said. "Buttons, pins, tokens. Prices on the card."

"It's not a button I'm after." Her voice dropped to the register people use when they're about to hand you something dangerous. "My boy. Tam. He's been gone eight months and I never—" She swallowed. "I never got to say anything. They wouldn't give me the body till it was three days cold, and by then he was already—" Her hands twisted the cap into a rope. "I need to tell him I'm sorry. I need to tell him I didn't believe them when they said he'd done something wrong, because I didn't, I never did, and he needs to know that, wherever he is. He needs to know his mother didn't think he was a traitor."

The room went very quiet. Even the house-voices Felix always heard—the old woman in the floorboards who complained about damp, the soldier under the doorstep who counted his marching steps over and over—even they seemed to lean in.

Felix had rules. Rule one: no politics. If a client wanted to reach a dead smuggler or a dead soldier or a dead informer, that was grief, and grief was allowed. But Felix didn't carry messages *for* the dead about the living, didn't pass warnings, didn't get involved. Rule two: no names written anywhere. The tokens carried words, never identities. Rule three, unwritten but iron: Felix never, ever let a client see what happened during the actual listening. The carving was the product. Whatever moved through Felix while the knife worked stayed behind Felix's eyes, because the moment people saw the gift plainly, it stopped being craft and became evidence, and evidence in Thornfell got you killed—or worse, got you *used*, and being used by the Regent's people was a death that took years to finish.

"I can carve him a message," Felix said carefully. "Words of your choosing. Into a token you keep, or one we bury at his grave, if there's a grave."

"There's a pit," Maren said flatly. "Outside Corren's Field. They don't mark them."

A pit. Of course a pit. Felix thought of the soldier under the doorstep, who had been in a pit himself once, and how long it had taken him to stop counting steps.

"The words go into the bone either way," Felix said. "Bone doesn't need a grave. Bone finds its own."

Something in Maren's face cracked open—not crying, not yet, but the ground shifting before the flood. "You've done this before. For others."

"For others," Felix said, which was true, and also was the closest thing to a lie they'd told all day, because *for others* implied it worked, and whether it worked was exactly the question Felix spent every waking hour refusing to look at directly.

"How much?"

"Twelve coppers. Standard rate."

Maren counted them out onto the counter one at a time, and each coin made a sound like a small door closing. Then she handed Felix a scrap of paper—not paper, actually, a strip of linen with letters burned into it in the careful hand of someone who'd learned writing late—and said, "These aren't my words. These are his. He wrote me a letter before they took him. I never answered it. I want my answer carved into the bone, and I want it buried where they put him, so he gets it, even late. Even if he's—" She couldn't finish. She didn't have to.

Felix took the linen. Read it standing up, because sitting down with a dead man's last words felt like a kind of surrender.

*Tam Kettle's letter said:* Ma—I didn't do what Vance's men say. Ask Doss, ask anyone. If you get this, don't come looking. Go to your sister in Lowmark and don't tell anyone where I am. I'm sorry about the roof money. I'll make it right. —T

Dated four days before the cart came back.

Felix folded the linen along its burns and did not let anything show on their face, which was the hardest work they did all day, harder than any carving. Because the letter was wrong. Not the words—the *weight* of it. Letters from the condemned had a weight Felix knew intimately, had known since childhood, and this one was too light, too steady, too full of plans. *I'll make it right.* Dead men didn't write *I'll make it right.* Living men wrote that. Men who expected to still be alive next month wrote that.

Which meant Tam Kettle hadn't known. Which meant the letter was real, and the boy had walked into whatever happened afterward believing he'd come home.

Felix said none of this. Rule one. Rule two. Rule three. Neutrality, neutrality, neutrality. Felix quoted the price again, took the deposit, gave Maren a date—three days, the carving took as long as it took, the bone had to be the right bone, river-cured ox femur, dense enough to hold fine lines—and sent her out into the amber light with her cap wrung nearly to thread.

Then Felix locked the front door, turned the sign, and sat alone in the back room with the letter and the truth they'd been avoiding since the moment they'd read it.

Because here was the thing Felix never said aloud, the thing that lived underneath the rules like water under ice:

When Felix carved a message for the living to send to the dead, sometimes—often—the dead answered. Not in words, not exactly. In pressure. In the way the grain of the bone resisted or yielded. In the sudden knowledge that arrived uninvited, the way you suddenly know someone is standing behind you. And Felix had learned, over years of practice, to carve the answer *into the token anyway*, disguised as ornament—a flourish here, a border pattern there—so that the client received their dead's reply without ever knowing they'd received it, without ever having to believe anything that might get them hurt. Grief got its receipt. Felix kept the transaction deniable. Everybody survived.

And Tam Kettle's reply was already pressing against the inside of Felix's skull like a hand against a window, and it was not *I forgive you, Ma*, and it was not *tell her I'm at peace.*

It was a warning.

* * *

Felix lit the lamp, chose the femur from the cedar box where the good bones cured, and began.

This was the part outsiders would have paid gold to watch and Felix would have killed to prevent them watching. The knife went in shallow, following the grain, and the words of Maren's answer rose under the blade—not composed, exactly, more *surfaced*, the way a drowned thing surfaces, and Felix's job was to catch them in steel before they sank. *Tell her I know she believed me. Tell her the roof money doesn't matter. Tell her—*

The bone resisted.

Not physically. Physically it cut clean, butter-smooth, the finest material Felix had worked in months. It resisted the way a locked room resists: everything normal except the feeling of something on the other side of the door. The voice coming through the grain was Tam's voice, young and scared and trying to be brave the way the letter had been brave, but underneath it, threaded through it, around it, was another pressure entirely—older, wider, patient in the way that mountains are patient—and it was pushing Tam aside the way a crowd pushes aside a child to get to the front.

Felix had felt interference before. Crowded graves, mass deaths, places where the dead stacked up like driftwood—all of it noisy, all of it hard to carve through. But this was different. This wasn't noise. This was *attention*. Something had noticed the channel opening. Something was leaning on it from a very long way away.

*—tell her,* Tam managed, faint, like a voice down a well, *tell her I didn't do it, tell her—*

And then, in a register that was not Tam's at all, in a voice like frost forming on stone, the other thing spoke through the bone and into the marrow of Felix's own bones, and it said:

*The gods are waking, carver. And they know your hands.*

The knife slipped. Just a fraction, just enough to score a line crooked across an hour of work, and Felix sat very still in the lamplight with their heart going like a trapped bird, staring at the token, at the words, at the single ruined stroke that looked, now that they made themselves look at it, exactly like a door standing open.

Around the room, in the floorboards and the walls and the doorstep and the cedar box itself, the house-voices had gone silent. All of them. Every dead soul Felix had ever quietly catalogued, every familiar murmur that made up the constant soft static of their life—the damp-complainer, the step-counter, the dozens more whose names Felix kept in a ledger in their own head because writing them down was against rule two.

Silent. Listening. Or waiting to be told what to do by whatever had just spoken through a dead boy's grief in a carver's back room in a town that officially did not exist.

Felix finished the token. They had to—it was paid for, and Maren Kettle deserved her receipt, and besides, stopping would mean admitting something had happened, and admitting things was how invisibility ended. They corrected the slipped line, disguised it as a reed in the border pattern, pressed the raven mark deep into the base where only the dead would think to look, and wrapped the bone in wool.

Three days, they'd promised. It had taken one evening. They'd deliver it early, bury it themselves at Corren's Field, keep Maren out of the pit country entirely. Clean. Deniable. Neutral.

But that night, lying in the loft above the shop with the lamp out and the town breathing below, Felix did not sleep. Because twice—twice—in the black hours, the little carved sigils hanging on the workshop wall, the practice pieces, the failures, the pretty nonsense patterns Felix sold to tourists as *traditional mountain motifs*—

they glowed.

Faintly. Green-white, like foxfire, like the memory of light rather than light itself. Not flickering on and off. Glowing steadily, patiently, the way coals hold heat, for perhaps ten breaths each time, and then going dark again, and the house-voices staying silent throughout, and the mountains outside the window standing very still against the stars as if they too were holding their breath.

Felix lay in the dark and did the arithmetic they'd spent three years refusing to do. The gods of Thornfell had been silent for a hundred years—every shrine cold, every oracle dry, every priest long since scattered or converted or dead. The Regent's whole regime was built on that silence, on a country so thoroughly erased that even its heaven had been evicted. And something had just spoken through Felix's bone. Something had said *we know your hands.* Something was making Felix's cast-off carvings shine in the night like signals.

Invisibility had been the whole architecture of Felix's life. The shop, the rules, the neutral face, the raven mark hidden in plain sight. It had worked. It had kept them alive through the fall of a kingdom while louder people burned.

And tonight, without being asked, without being summoned, the work itself had begun to answer back.

Felix got up, dressed, and took down from its shelf the one relic they had sworn never to disturb except in the direst necessity: a satchel of oiled leather, older than the Regent, older than the war, containing a handful of fingerbones wrapped in silk—the fingerbones of Vessa Marrow, the smuggler-queen, the woman who had mapped every pass out of Thornfell and then vanished a century ago, leaving behind a legend, a bounty, and a bag of bones that Felix had bought from a dying peddler seven years ago for the price of a good coat and had never once regretted.

Felix had never tried to listen to Vessa. Some doors you didn't open because you suspected what was knocking.

The satchel was warm. It should not have been warm. Leather and bone, stored in a cold loft, and it was warm as a sleeping animal, and through the wall of Felix's skull, faint but unmistakable, rising like smoke off embers that had waited a hundred years for wind—

a voice, sharp-tongued and furious and unmistakably awake, said:

"Well. Took you long enough, carver. Sit down. We need to talk about the roads."
