## 2. What the Bones Said

Felix did not sit down.

Sitting down was a thing you did when you intended to stay, and staying was a thing Felix Ravensworth had spent thirty-one years refusing to do anywhere longer than a season. Instead they stood in the middle of the loft with the satchel held out from their body like something bitten, and said, to the fingerbones of a woman who had been dead for a hundred years:

"No."

"No?" The voice came through the leather, dry as chalk on slate. "A century in the dark and my first word back is *no*. You've been talking to me for years, carver. Little whispers. *Vessa, which pass is safest in spring. Vessa, where does the old salt road fork.* And now that I'm actually awake enough to answer, you've gone rude."

"That wasn't you answering." Felix set the satchel on the workbench, carefully, the way you'd set down a sleeping snake. "That was me reading you. Like a book. Books don't talk back."

"Books don't get warm either." A pause, and when the voice came again it was closer, as if the bones had shifted inside the leather. "Something's changed. Something's *stirred*. I felt it come up through the ground like water rising in a mine shaft. You felt it too. Don't lie to a smuggler, carver, we can smell lying the way sharks smell blood."

Felix could not argue. They had felt it — three nights ago, when Tam's voice had come through the rib-bone wrong.

They thought about Tam now, standing alone in the loft with the lamp turned low. Tam Arden, seventeen years old, drowned in his own blood in a Regent's jail cell for the crime of carrying a letter no one would name. His mother had sat across Felix's worktable two days ago with her hands folded so tight the knuckles went white, and she had put down eleven silver pieces — more than she could afford, everyone in Ashmoor knew what the widow Arden could afford — and asked Felix to let her say goodbye.

Felix had carved the listening sigil into a rib of river-ox bone, the way they'd done a thousand times. They'd opened their hearing the way they always did, that careful narrowing of the self until the living world went quiet and the dead could press through.

And Tam had spoken.

Not grief. That was the thing Felix still couldn't shake, the thing that had kept them awake since. The dead spoke grief; it was nearly all they had left, grief and love braided so tight you couldn't always tell them apart. Tam should have wept for his mother. He should have said goodbye.

Instead he had said, in a voice like cold iron dragged over stone, a voice that was his and yet not his, the way a glove is shaped like a hand but isn't one:

*The gods are waking, and they know your hands.*

Then the bone had cracked clean down the middle of the sigil, and every candle in the room had leaned sideways though there was no draft, and Tam was gone — not faded the way the dead fade, but *gone*, snatched back, like a fish pulled off a hook.

Felix had told the widow Arden that the connection was too damaged. Had given her back eight of her eleven silver. Had watched her leave with her face doing the terrible arithmetic of mothers, deciding whether to believe the kindness or see through it.

And then Felix had gone home and drunk half a bottle of plum brandy and slept badly, and woken to find the satchel warm.

* * *

"You're not listening," Vessa said.

"I'm famously good at listening. It's my trade."

"Your trade is eavesdropping on corpses and charging by the hour. Listening is different. Listening means you shut your clever mouth and hear what the world's telling you." The voice moved around Felix like smoke finding drafts. "The boy. Tell me about the boy."

So Felix told her. All of it — the widow, the silver, the rib-bone, the wrongness of the voice, the words themselves. Saying them aloud in the empty loft made them worse. *The gods are waking, and they know your hands.*

Silence from the satchel. Long enough that Felix began to wonder if whatever had woken had gone back to sleep.

"The old gods," Vessa said at last, and her voice had lost its sharpness, gone flat and careful, the way voices go when people are walking on ice. "Thornfell had nine of them. River, root, stone, salt, storm, seed, hearth, hunt, and the Doormaker. Everyone knows the list. Children learn it before they learn letters."

"I know the list."

"Do you know what happened to them?" Now the sharpness came back, but aimed elsewhere, at something a century gone. "One day a hundred years ago they simply stopped. No war, no desecration anyone recorded. Shrines went cold between one dawn and the next. Priests woke up deaf. My grandmother swore the river god used to answer her directly, and then one morning the river was just — water." A pause. "I mapped every pass out of these mountains, carver, and I never found out why they went quiet. I died not knowing. Do you understand how that sits in a person? A hundred years of not knowing?"

"And now?"

"Now the boy speaks with a voice that isn't only his, and tells *you* — you, a bone-carver in a smuggling town, a nobody, a ghost of a person — that the gods know your hands." The satchel seemed to settle, like someone leaning forward. "What have you been carving, Felix Ravensworth?"

"Messages. Names. Ordinary things."

"What else?"

Felix looked at the workbench. At the rows of finished pieces waiting for morning delivery: a locket-bone for a man who missed his wife, a small carved hand for a child's grave-gift, a name-plaque for a dog, because even in Ashmoor people loved their dogs past all sense.

And beneath those, in the drawer that locked, the other work. The sigils. The ones Felix had never sold, never shown, never fully understood the impulse behind. For years their hands had made them in the evenings — spirals and interlocking lines and shapes that hurt slightly to look at too long, carved into slivers of antler and riverbone without any plan Felix could articulate. They weren't messages. They weren't requests. They were just — patterns. Patterns that felt right the way a keyhole feels right to the key that fits it.

Felix had assumed it was a tic. An artisan's fidgeting. The way some whittlers can't stop making little boats.

"Nothing," Felix said.

"Liar," said the dead queen, comfortably. "Fine. Keep your secrets. The night will keep them better than I can."

* * *

The night did not keep them.

Felix banked the stove, checked the latch twice, and lay down in the loft above the workshop with the satchel on the far side of the room, deliberately, like exiling a cat. Sleep came slowly and left quickly.

It was past midnight when the light woke them.

Not lamplight. Not moonlight. It came up through the floorboards in thin green-white threads, and for one merciful second Felix thought: fire, the workshop's burning, move — and then they were moving, down the ladder, heart slamming, and stopped dead at the bottom step.

Every sigil in the locked drawer was glowing.

Felix crossed the room and pulled the drawer open, and the light spilled out over their hands like water over stones. Two dozen pieces of carved bone, years of private fidgeting, and every line of every pattern burned with a soft, patient luminescence, the color of foxfire, of deep water, of things that live where the sun has never been charged with visiting.

They weren't flickering. That was the worst part. Fire flickers; this glowed the way a held breath holds. Steady. Deliberate. As if each piece were a lamp someone had lit on purpose, and recently, and all at once.

Felix picked one up. It weighed nothing and everything. The spiral under their thumb was warm — not the satchel's animal warmth but a cleaner heat, like a stone left in sunlight after sundown.

"This isn't me," Felix said aloud, to the empty workshop, to the glowing bones, to whoever was listening. Their voice came out smaller than they wanted. "I didn't ask for this. I don't ask for anything. That's the whole point of me."

The bones glowed on, unbothered, unanswerable.

Felix laid them out on the workbench in a row, all twenty-four, and stood over them, and made themself look. Really look, the way they looked at a client's face when deciding how much truth a paying customer could carry. And once they made themself look, they saw what they had refused to see for years: the patterns weren't random. They weren't even patterns, plural. They were one pattern, broken into pieces, distributed across years of sleepless evenings like a sentence written one word per night by a hand that knew where it was going even when the mind didn't.

Twenty-four pieces. Twenty-four fragments. And if you squinted — if you let your eyes unfocus the way you did when reading the dead — the whole assembled shape resolved into something Felix recognized from the oldest shrine-wall in Ashmoor, the one everyone ignored, worn smooth as an egg by centuries of weather:

A door.

An open door, with light coming through it.

"Oh no," Felix said quietly. "No, no, no."

Behind them, in the satchel, Vessa Marrow began to laugh. It was not a kind sound. It was the laugh of a woman who had waited a hundred years to be proven right about something terrible.

"There it is," she said. "There's the door. Told you we needed to talk about the roads."

* * *

Felix did not sleep again. They sat at the workbench with the lamp out and the bones glowing, and they took inventory of their life the way you take inventory before a flood — quickly, ruthlessly, deciding what can be carried.

The workshop: three days' notice to Marda at the tannery, and she'd strip it to the walls. The clients: a list of names in Felix's head, people who trusted them, people whose dead they carried. The town: Ashmoor, gray and smelly and safe, safe precisely because it was beneath everyone's notice.

*Invisibility is a discipline,* Felix's mother had said — no. Not their mother. Someone had said that. Someone whose face Felix could no longer quite hold onto, which was its own small grief, filed away with the others. *You are safe exactly as long as no one looks at you. The moment they look, you're a story, and stories get told to the wrong people.*

The gods were looking. Whatever spoke through Tam Arden's stolen mouth had looked straight down through a century of silence and found Felix's hands.

And the hands — Felix flexed them now, scarred and ink-stained and steady — the hands had known. All those years of evening carvings, the compulsive patterns, the locked drawer. The hands had been writing letters to someone, and tonight, for the first time, someone had written back.

Toward dawn, the glow began to fade. Not all at once — piece by piece, like lamps being carried away down a long corridor, until only one remained lit: the oldest fragment, the first one Felix had ever carved without knowing why, six years ago, the night after they'd buried old Sorrel the ferryman and heard him say *thank you* in a voice that hadn't sounded entirely like Sorrel.

That last piece pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like a heartbeat. Like a knock.

Like something on the other side of a door, tapping politely, waiting to be let in.

Felix wrapped it in cloth and put it in their pocket, against all instinct, against every rule of invisibility they'd ever lived by. Then they picked up the satchel and set it on the table and unlaced the flap, and looked down at the fingerbones in their bed of wool — small, yellowed, ordinary-looking, the most dangerous objects in the kingdom of Thornfell.

"All right," Felix said. "Talk about the roads."

But Vessa didn't answer about roads. Her voice, when it came, was quieter than Felix had ever heard it, and it was aimed somewhere over Felix's shoulder, at the fading glow, at the east window graying toward dawn, at the mountains beyond the rooftops where the snowfields caught the first light like signal fires.

"They're calling muster," the dead queen said. "Every god that ever slept in this country is turning over in its grave-ground and opening its eyes, and the first thing every one of them is looking for—" the fingerbones seemed to shift in the wool, almost eagerly, almost fearfully, "—is the doorkeeper. They've chosen, carver. And gods help us both, they've chosen *you*."
