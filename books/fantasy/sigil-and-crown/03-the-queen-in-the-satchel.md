## 3. The Queen in the Satchel

Felix didn't sleep.

They tried. They banked the workshop fire, bolted the door twice, lay down on the cot behind the curtain with their boots still on. But the sigils kept glowing through the wall of the display case—a faint green-gold pulse, like something breathing under ice—and every time Felix closed their eyes they heard the boy's voice again, thin and wrong and certain:

*The gods are waking, and they know your hands.*

So at some hour past midnight, when Ashmoor's lamps had guttered down to nothing and the tide was slapping the pilings below the tannery district, Felix got up, lit a single candle, and took the satchel down from its hook.

It was an ugly thing, honestly. Salt-stiffened leather gone black with age, straps replaced twice, a buckle that was more rust than iron. Anyone who saw it would take it for a bone-carver's tool bag, which was exactly the point. It had hung in three workshops over eleven years, gathering dust and grease and the smell of tallow, and not one person—not the tax collectors, not the dock inspectors, not the Regent's census men who'd come through two winters back—had ever asked to look inside.

Inside were the fingerbones of Vessa Marrow.

Ten of them. Index to little finger, both hands, wrapped in oilcloth and nested in a bed of river sand because Vessa had once—through the long fog of her half-sleeping years—snapped at Felix for storing them in wool. *Wool itches,* she'd said, or rather the bones had said, in that voice that came up through Felix's palms like water through a reed. *I was a queen. Queens don't itch.*

She hadn't been a queen the way the old histories meant it. No crown, no court, no throne except the stern bench of a smuggling ketch called *The Kindly Lie*. But she'd been queen of everything that mattered: every pass out of Thornfell, every hidden road, every smuggler's stair cut into the mountains where no road should be. When Thornfell fell—the kingdom erased, its name struck from maps and records and finally from memory itself—it was said Vessa Marrow died trying to lead one last column of refugees out through the high country. Her body was never found. Her hands, though. Her hands had turned up forty years later in a peat bog near the Salt Shrine, perfectly preserved, and had passed from dealer to dealer until a younger Felix, still learning the trade, had paid an obscene sum for them without fully understanding why.

Now, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the candle between their knees, Felix understood why well enough. They needed her awake. And she hadn't been properly awake in years.

* * *

Waking a ghost was not like the messages Felix carved for clients. The paying work was simple: a name into a knucklebone, a question whispered over it, and the dead answered in their own voices, faint and flat, like people talking through a wall. Most of them only wanted to say goodbye. Some wanted to complain about the weather in whatever country they'd gone to. None of them, until last night, had ever looked *at* Felix.

Vessa was different. Vessa had never been content to answer questions. In the early years she'd talked constantly—bossing Felix about the workshop, critiquing their carving technique, narrating the geography of a kingdom that officially didn't exist whenever Felix's hands drifted near her bones. Then, gradually, she'd gone quiet. Not gone. Felix could always feel her, a low hum in the fingers, a presence like a cat asleep in a warm corner. But the talking had thinned to fragments, then to single words, then to nothing but that hum. Felix had told themselves it was natural. Bones wore out. Voices faded.

Tonight, unwrapping the oilcloth by candlelight, Felix suspected otherwise. Tonight the hum was already loud before Felix even touched her. Tonight the fingerbones were warm.

"Vessa," Felix said quietly. "I need you."

Nothing. The candle flame leaned in a draft from under the door.

"I know you can hear me. You've been louder all week. Don't pretend you haven't." Felix picked up the index finger of her right hand—the pointing finger, she'd once claimed, of the woman who'd pointed the way out for four thousand souls—and held it loosely in their left palm. "Something happened tonight. A client's son came through wrong. He wasn't grieving. He was *warning*. And my sigils are glowing, Vessa. All of them. I didn't ask them to. They're doing it on their own."

The hum deepened. Felix felt it climb their wrist, their forearm, settle somewhere behind the sternum like a second heartbeat.

"You've been going quiet for years," Felix said. "And now this. Either you know something, or you're scared of the same thing I am. And knowing you, you're too proud to be scared, so it must be the first one."

Silence. The candle burned straight up.

Then, so suddenly that Felix flinched and nearly dropped the bone, the voice came—full-voiced, close, furious, as if Vessa Marrow had been standing behind them for years waiting for exactly this opening.

*"Eleven years. Eleven years I lie there listening to you mumble at clients and burn good tallow and charge triple for grief-work, and NOW you wake me? Now, when it's already started?"*

Felix's heart slammed. "You're awake."

*"I'm FURIOUS. There's a difference, though in your workshop apparently not much of one."* The voice seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, sharp-edged, carrying the accent of the old river country, vowels broad as barge hulls. *"Show me your hands."*

"What?"

*"Your HANDS, carver. Hold them up where I can—" * A pause; Felix realized she couldn't see, exactly, not the way the living saw. She read through touch, through the bones in contact with skin. Felix cupped both hands around her ten fingerbones, careful as someone holding a bird. *"Hm. Same hands. Still scarred on the left thumb. Still carving left-to-right like a heathen. Good. That's good. That's very bad, actually, but good that it's you."*

"Explain that."

*"The boy who spoke to you. The dead one. What did he say, exactly? Word for word."*

Felix closed their eyes and gave it back: *The gods are waking, and they know your hands.*

The silence afterward had texture. It felt like the whole room holding its breath.

*"Damn him,"* Vessa said at last, and there was something under the anger now, something that Felix, who made their living hearing what lived underneath voices, recognized immediately as fear. *"Damn him for saying it out loud. Names have weight, carver. Warnings spoken aloud are invitations."*

"Invitations to what?"

*"To being found."* The bones shifted in Felix's palms—Felix would have sworn they moved, actually moved, turning as if she were pacing. *"Listen. I don't have long at full volume. Talking like this costs me, and I spent most of my savings being quiet these past years, which I did for a REASON, which reason you never once asked about, you uncurious lump of a tradesman—"*

"I asked. Twice. You told me it was none of my business."

*"It WASN'T. It's everyone's business now, so we're all equally ruined. Sit down. Are you sitting?"*

"I'm sitting."

*"Then listen, and don't interrupt, because interruptions cost me breath I don't have."*

* * *

What Vessa told him—told them, Felix corrected themselves, out of old habit, though Vessa had never cared much about pronouns and less about propriety—came out in bursts, a queen dictating dispatches while the enemy crossed the river.

*"A century ago, the gods of Thornfell went silent. Everyone thinks they died, or left, or never existed. Wrong on all counts. They went QUIET. There's a difference. A god goes quiet when it's hiding, and a god hides when it's hunted. Your Regent's predecessors didn't erase our kingdom because we lost a war. They erased us because we wouldn't give them the gods. A crown can own land and taxes and men. It cannot own a god—but it can try, oh, it can spend a century trying, and that is precisely what has been happening in the dark while the rest of the world forgot we existed."*

"The Regent," Felix said slowly. "The current one. He knows?"

*"He knows there's something under his mountains worth more than his mountains. His inquisitors have been pulling apart villages for a year now, looking for signs. Prayers answered. Candles relighting. Dead things walking. Every miracle reported gets a visit from men with gray cloaks and polite voices."* A bitter laugh, like gravel poured through a sieve. *"I knew a man with a voice like that once. I fed him to a river god. Different century. Simpler times."*

"And the gods waking now—why now?"

*"Because they're done hiding. Or because something changed. I slept through the change, carver, that's the truth of it—I went quiet to keep myself from being found, same as the gods did, and I missed things. But here's what I know, and mark it well."* The bones pressed against Felix's palms, hard, insistent. *"When the gods went silent, they didn't all go silent the same way. The small ones hid in springs and cliff-cracks and the roots of hills. But the Threshold—the great door, the true way out of Thornfell, the one road that leads somewhere the erasure can't follow—that door was sealed from the INSIDE. By the gods. Locked against the crown. And a locked door needs a key, and a key needs a keeper, and the gods chose their keeper before they slept."*

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Felix became aware that they had stopped breathing.

"They chose—" Their voice cracked. They tried again. "Vessa. What are you saying?"

*"I'm saying that the gods didn't wake up last night and notice you by accident. I'm saying that dead boy didn't stumble into a warning. I'm saying that your sigils glow because they're answering a call that's been going out for years, getting louder, and you've been too busy carving trinkets for grieving mothers to hear it."* The voice dropped, and gentled, which was somehow worse. *"Oh, carver. You thought you were invisible. You built yourself a fine little life in a fine little town and told yourself the dead were just customers. But you were born with the ears for it. Maybe made with them. And the ones who made you are waking up, and they know your hands, and they are calling you home."*

"I don't have a home," Felix said. It came out flat and automatic, the sentence they'd carried for eleven years like a coin worn smooth. "Thornfell doesn't exist. There's nothing to be called home to."

*"No,"* Vessa agreed. *"There isn't. That's rather the point. There's a door out of a country that no longer exists, and doors like that don't open for kings or armies or prayers. They open for a keeper. And if the gods are waking and reaching for you, then either you go to them—"*

"Or someone else finds me first."

*"Gray cloaks,"* Vessa said. *"Polite voices. Yes."*

* * *

Felix sat for a long time after that, hands still cupped around the bones, watching the candle burn down. Outside, Ashmoor slept the way it always slept—badly, fitfully, a town of smugglers and fence-menders who trusted no one and expected nothing. Felix had liked it here precisely because nobody expected anything. Eleven years of respectable invisibility. Eleven years of neutrality, bought and paid for.

"You knew," Felix said finally. "All those years ago, when I bought your hands. You let me buy you. Why?"

A pause. When Vessa answered, the fury was gone, and what remained was older and wearier than any ghost had a right to sound.

*"Because I died at the Threshold, carver. A hundred years ago, leading my last run out. And the door wouldn't open for me."* The words came slow, each one costing her. *"I stood in front of the last true door out of Thornfell with four hundred people dying behind me, and it stayed shut, and I never learned why, and I have lain in bogs and dealers' drawers and your dusty satchel for a century wondering. So when a young carver with god-touched hands bought my bones off a fat man in Corvess, I thought—here, at last, is someone who might walk where I walked. Someone who might open what I couldn't."*

"Why are you telling me this now?"

*"Because you're going to need to run soon, and I am the best map you will ever have."* A ghost of her old sharpness returned. *"Literally, carver. Every pass, every stair, every smuggler's road out of these mountains—they're all in here."* The bones tapped, once, against Felix's palm. *"I mapped them. I walked them. I buried caches along them. And unlike every other map of Thornfell, I was never written down, so the erasure never ate me."*

Felix looked down at the ten small bones in their hands. Fingerbones. A dead woman's grip on a country that had been unwritten out of the world.

"If I run," Felix said, "they'll burn Ashmoor looking for me. You understand that. They'll hurt people I—" They stopped. People I know. People whose sons and daughters I've spoken to across the wall of death. People who think of me as the quiet carver on Tanner's Row, nothing more.

*"They'll burn it whether you run or not,"* Vessa said, not unkindly. *"That's the thing about being found, carver. Once the search starts, staying put doesn't save anyone. It just means you're standing in the fire when it comes."* A beat. *"Pack light. Take the satchel. And for the love of whatever's left of the old country, stop charging triple for grief-work. It's vulgar."*

Despite everything—despite the glowing sigils in the next room and the boy's voice still lodged behind Felix's ribs—Felix laughed. It came out ragged, but it was real.

"Alliance, then," Felix said. "You give me the roads. I find out why the gods want me. And we both get answers about that door."

*"An alliance,"* Vessa repeated, tasting the word. *"Between a dead smuggler-queen and a bone-carver who talks to ghosts. The crown will be terrified."* But beneath the sarcasm Felix heard something else, something almost soft. *"One condition."*

"Name it."

*"When we reach the Threshold—if we reach it—you ask it why it shut me out. I've waited a hundred years for that answer. Promise me."*

Felix wrapped the fingerbones back in their oilcloth, gently, one by one, and tucked them into the sand.

"I promise," they said.

The hum in their palms settled, warm and steady, like a cat curling up. Awake now. Watching.

Outside, far down the coast road, where the dark met the sea, Felix thought they saw a light that didn't belong—not a fisher's lamp, not a signal fire, but a line of them, moving in formation, patient and unhurried, coming north along the shore toward Ashmoor.

Gray cloaks. Polite voices.

Felix blew out the candle and started packing.
