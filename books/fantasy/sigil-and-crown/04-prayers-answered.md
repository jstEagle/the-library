## 4. Prayers Answered

The river at Lowmere ran brown with spring melt, and Sera Vance stood in it to her knees because the villagers would not tell the truth from dry land.

"Again," she said. "The boy came out of the water. Where."

A woman wrung her apron into a rope. Behind her, half of Lowmere's forty souls had gathered on the bank, drawn by the spectacle of a Regent's inquisitor standing in a river in her gray coat with her boots ruined and her patience visibly ending. Vance counted them without appearing to: eleven men, nineteen women, six children old enough to be dangerous, the rest too young or too frightened to matter.

"Inquisitor," the woman said, "we've told you everything."

"You've told me a story. I want the shape under the story." Vance lifted a hand before the woman could speak again. "Drowned children do not walk out of rivers. Whatever came out of your river, it wore your drowned child like a coat. Now—where did it come out, and what did it say?"

Silence. The kind that had weight.

Vance waited. Waiting was most of her trade. People feared silence more than they feared questions; questions they could rehearse against, but silence made them fill the air with things she hadn't asked for, and somewhere in the overflow was always the truth.

It came from a girl this time, maybe nine, standing apart from the others with mud to her thighs as if she'd been playing at the water's edge all morning, which Vance suspected she had.

"It came out by the millstone," the girl said. "The one that fell off the weir. And it didn't say nothing. It just stood there dripping until its mam came running, and then it cried like regular."

"Like regular," Vance repeated.

"Like it used to." The girl shrugged, unbothered by theology. "Before."

*Before.* Before the fever took him, according to the village reeve's ledger, which recorded one Tammas Ellery, aged seven, dead of lungfever in the winter and buried beneath the yew where all of Lowmere's dead went. Not drowned at all, then—the river story was wrong in its first detail, and wrongness in details was how Vance knew a village was lying about something bigger.

Except they weren't lying. That was the trouble. She'd been in Lowmere two days now, and every account agreed in every particular except the ones that mattered, and no one was lying, which meant everyone believed, which meant something had actually happened here, and *that* was the problem the Regent's office could not afford to have.

"Show me the millstone."

* * *

The millstone sat half-buried in silt where it had fallen—or been pushed—years ago, a great gray disc furred with moss, the broken stump of the old weir behind it. The water pooled there, slow and deep, and the banks were churned with small footprints, dozens of them, days' worth. Children came here. Children always came to the places adults found unsettling; it was practically a law of nature.

Vance crouched at the waterline and let her mind go quiet and cold, the way she'd been trained, the way that had earned her the gray coat at twenty-six when most inquisitors were still grinding ink for their betters.

Start with what is known. A child died of fever in winter. A child walked out of this pool in spring, wet, alive, weeping, recognized by its mother. Between those facts lay either fraud, madness, or miracle, and Vance's career depended on proving it was never the third one.

Fraud first. Who profits? She turned the question over. Lowmere had nothing to profit from—a dying wool village, its mill broken, its young people gone downriver to the towns. No shrine had sprung up yet, no relic-sellers, no collection box. If anything, the villagers seemed less proud than afraid, which was not the posture of fraudsters. Fraudsters performed their wonder. These people kept glancing at the yew tree on the hill as if expecting the dead in it to sit up and object.

Madness, then. Mass hysteria, grief-driven delusion. Possible. Grief did strange arithmetic in isolated places. But mass hysteria left residue—contradictions, escalation, someone eventually claiming to have seen the god themselves, growing the story toward importance. Two days of interviews had produced none of that. Every account was smaller than the last, if anything. They weren't building a legend. They were trying to make it go away.

Which left miracle, and miracle left evidence, because the old gods had never learned subtlety. That was the thing the histories agreed on, before the histories were burned: the gods of Thornfell had been loud. Rivers that answered, stones that remembered, prayers that got responses whether you wanted them or not. A century of silence had made the country polite. Silence, Vance had always assumed, was permanent.

She pulled a knife and worked it into the silt along the pool's edge, an inch at a time, methodical, ignoring the crowd's murmuring behind her. Inquisitors who dug in rivers looked mad. Let them think so. Mad inquisitors got talked about, and talked-about inquisitors heard things.

Her blade struck something soft-then-hard a hand's depth down.

She brought it up dripping: a sliver of bone, flat, the length of her smallest finger, carved on both faces with lines so fine they might have been scratches until the water ran out of the grooves and the pattern resolved—interlocking knots around a central eye, cut with a steadiness no nervous amateur could manage.

Not a charm. Charms were crude, mass-produced, sold at fairs. This was work. This was *craft*, and craft meant a maker, and makers signed their work even when they thought they didn't.

She held it up to the light and turned it, and there, tucked into the knotwork at the base where only someone looking for it would look, was the mark: three short strokes descending from a horizontal line, like rain falling from a lintel. A house falling? A door raining? She'd seen it before. She'd seen it twice before, in fact—in a bone button taken off a smuggler hanged at Grayford two years back, and in a mourning piece sold in Ashmoor's market that her informant swore had come from "the quiet carver, the one who doesn't talk, the one whose pieces the dead seem to like."

Ashmoor. Of course Ashmoor. Smuggler town, tannery stink, a hundred hidden doors and not one honest record. If the old gods were waking anywhere, they'd wake where the country's forgotten roads still ran, and Ashmoor squatted on forgotten roads the way ticks squatted on dogs.

But the sigil itself bothered her more than the mark. She turned it again. It wasn't a prayer-token, wasn't a ward, wasn't any of the categories in her training. It was a *message*—the knotwork was structured like script, dense, addressed. Addressed to whom?

To the river, she realized. To whatever slept under the millstone. Someone had carved a letter to a forgotten god and sunk it in the water, and the god had read it, and answered—and the answer had been a dead child walking, whole and weeping and *alive*, and the mother's face when she described it to Vance had not been joy. It had been terror braided through joy, terror at what it meant that the world had started answering again after a hundred years of safe silence.

Because silence was policy. Silence was the Regent's first law, older than the Regent himself: the gods are gone, the gods are myth, the country is empty of anything but crown and law. A drowned child walking was not a wonder to the crown. It was a crack in the floor of the world, and cracks spread.

Vance closed her fist around the sigil and stood, water streaming from her coat hem, and looked at the crowd on the bank. They flinched, collectively, like a single animal.

"Here is what happens now," she said, pitching her voice to carry. "No one speaks of this outside Lowmere. No pilgrims come. No priests—I mean it, if a priest of anything comes up that road asking after wonders, you send word to the garrison at Ford Bridge within the day. Anyone who sells a story to a traveler answers to me personally." She let that hang. "In exchange, the crown forgets Lowmere exists. Which is, I suspect, what you wanted all along."

The reeve nodded, too quickly, and Vance read the relief in the village's collective shoulders and filed it away with distaste. They thought she'd come to suppress a miracle. She'd come to *acquire* one. But they didn't need to know that, and the difference would keep them compliant for exactly as long as she needed.

She climbed the bank, wrung out what she could of her coat, and walked up through the village to the reeve's house where she lodged, and shut the door, and sat, and laid the sigil on the table, and allowed herself—for the first time since the river—to feel the thing underneath the procedure.

Not fear. Vance had trained herself out of fear years ago, mostly. It was colder than fear. It was the feeling of a map being redrawn under your feet while you stand on it.

The gods were waking. Not stirring, not rumor, not the fever-dreams of grieving villages—waking, and answering, and doing it *badly*, clumsily, like a body remembering how to move after long sleep. A child returned through a pool because someone wrote the water a letter. What else was being written? What else was listening? And how many people like the quiet carver of Ashmoor were out there right now, hands full of bone, talking to things that should have stayed asleep?

The Regent's office had protocols for this. There was a file, a thin one, dusty—she'd read it as a junior and laughed. *Contingency: Return of Pre-Crown Cultic Activity.* Eleven pages of nonsense about shrines and relics, written by men dead fifty years who'd assumed the silence was geology, permanent as bedrock. Nobody had ever planned for the bedrock to get up and walk.

She would need authority. She would need the Regent's own seal on a writ of inquiry, broad enough to pull garrison troops, broad enough to open every record, broad enough to take people. And she would need to move fast, because whoever had carved that sigil had started something that would not stay in one village, and the crown's only advantage now was speed.

Vance took out paper and began the report, in her small exact hand, choosing each word like a surgeon choosing a blade. She wrote *anomalous restoration event* instead of resurrection. She wrote *unlicensed cultic artifact* instead of prayer. She wrote *maker identified provisionally; investigation proceeding to source.*

Source. Ashmoor. The quiet carver.

She paused, pen lifted, and looked at the sigil again—at the fine, patient, careful lines of it, the eye at the center of the knots that seemed, in the lamplight, almost to be looking back.

Whoever you are, she thought, you wrote to a god, and it wrote back, and a child lives who should be dust under a yew tree. Part of her—the part she kept locked below the procedure, the part that had joined the inquisitors at seventeen because she'd wanted, with a ferocity that embarrassed her even then, to find out what was *true*—part of her wanted to shake that carver's hand.

The rest of her knew better. Truth was not the point anymore. Truth was a door, and doors had to be controlled, and if the crown did not control this one, the crown would fall through it.

She finished the report. She sealed it. And in the morning she rode for Ashmoor with the sigil wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, and behind her Lowmere exhaled, and ahead of her the road ran straight through country that had begun, very quietly, to remember.

She did not see the girl with muddy thighs watching her go from the millstone pool, nor hear what the girl said softly to the water once the rider was gone:

"She found it. He said she would."

And the pool, very slightly, rippled—though there was no wind.
