## 5. Ashmoor Burns

The smoke came before the bells.

Felix smelled it first—tallow and pitch, the particular reek of something burning that wasn't meant to burn—and their hands went still over the half-carved rib bone on the workbench. Outside, Ashmoor was waking up wrong. Not the slow gray stir of a smuggler town easing into its morning markets, but the sharp, scattered sounds of people running.

"You feel that?" Tam's voice from the front room. Tam Broadfoot, who had sold Felix tallow and silence for six years. "Something's lit at the harbor end."

"Probably the distillery again," Felix said, and heard how thin it sounded even as they said it.

The sigils had been glowing for three days now. Every piece of carved bone in the shop, waking at dusk like lamps being lit one by one, without prayer, without payment, without Felix asking anything of anyone. They had covered them with cloth. They had buried two in the garden. The bones glowed through the cloth. The bones glowed through the dirt, a faint gold seam in the dark soil like a vein of light under skin.

*The gods are waking,* the boy had said, three nights ago, his voice coming through his mother's paid-for message like water through a cracked wall. *And they know your hands.*

Felix had told themselves it was grief talking. Grief distorted everything; Felix knew that better than most. But the glowing hadn't stopped, and this morning there were riders at the harbor end of town, and smoke, and the particular cadence of authority shouting orders in a voice pitched to carry.

"Felix." Tam appeared in the doorway, and his face was the color of old tallow himself. "Soldiers. Gray cloaks. They're going door to door up Salt Row."

Gray cloaks. The Regent's colors. Inquisitors didn't come to Ashmoor—Ashmoor was beneath the Regent's notice, had been for years, a smudge on the coast where nothing official happened. That invisibility had been the whole point. Felix had built a life inside it the way a hermit crab builds itself into a borrowed shell.

"They're asking for someone," Tam said. "A carver. A *god-speaker.*"

The word landed like a dropped anvil. God-speaker. Not bone-carver, not artisan, not the respectable fiction Felix had spent a decade polishing. Someone had talked. Or someone had read the drowned child's story out of a village two valleys east and traced the sigil on the amulet back to the maker's mark—a small raven, wings folded, cut into every piece that left Felix's bench.

Felix stood very still. On the shelf above the workbench, forty pieces of carved bone sat in their careful rows, and every single one of them began to glow.

"No," Felix whispered. "Not now. *Not now.*"

The light spilled out from under the cloth Felix threw over them, golden and unmistakable, bleeding through the weave like sunrise through a shutter. Tam stared. His mouth worked. Six years of buying tallow and mending his grandmother's hearing-bone for free, and what he saw now was not an artisan but exactly what the gray cloaks were hunting.

"That's you," Tam said. Not a question. "That's been you the whole time."

"Tam—"

"The widow Anselm's son. You spoke to him? He told her things no one could—" Tam's voice climbed, cracked. "They burned the Harrow house already. I saw it from the lane. The widow was still inside, Felix. She wouldn't leave the shop. She said her boy's voice was in those walls."

The floor did not move, but Felix felt it tilt anyway. Widow Anselm, who had paid with a ring she couldn't afford to lose, whose son's warning had started all of this. Who had come back twice since, just to sit in the shop and listen to the quiet where her boy's voice had been.

"They're coming here next," Tam said. "Everyone knows your mark. Half the town's worn your work at some point. It'll take them an hour to be at this door."

An hour. Less. And every glowing bone in the room was a beacon, a confession, a map with a raven stamped on it.

Felix moved. Later they would not remember deciding to move—only the motion itself, hands sweeping the satchel from its hook behind the curtain, the long oilcloth bundle, the knife, the strongbox left open because coins weighed more than they were worth. Vessa's fingerbones went into the inner pocket against Felix's chest, wrapped in wool, cold as always and colder this morning, as if the dead woman had gone tense.

"Don't tell them anything," Felix said to Tam, and hated the words even as they left, because it was Felix asking Tam to stand in the doorway of a burning town and lie to men who burned widows in their shops.

"I never saw you," Tam said flatly. "I've never seen you. Go."

There was no time for anything else. No time to warn the widow's ghost-shop, no time to pull the ledgers with clients' names written in them—names that would hang every one of them if found. Felix grabbed the ledger anyway, shoved it into the stove, watched the pages curl and blacken, names going up in flame one by one: Maret of the fish-guild, Old Sefa, the Harrow family, the widow Anselm. Each name a small betrayal or a small salvation, depending on which side of the fire you stood.

The back wall of the shop backed onto the tannery yard. Everyone in Ashmoor knew about the tannery tunnels—the old drainage ways from when the town had rendered things too foul to speak of, bricked at both ends and forgotten by everyone except smugglers, children, and Felix, who had mapped them during a slow winter out of habit and paranoia and had never once believed they'd need them.

Paranoia, it turned out, was just foresight that hadn't been tested yet.

Felix went through the curtain, through the storeroom, pried up the flagstone Tam had helped loosen years ago as a joke—"in case your dead clients get angry"—and dropped into the dark. The tunnel air hit like wet cloth: tannery-rot and river-slime and old lime. Above, muffled through stone and earth, Felix heard the shop door break open. Boots. Tam's voice, high and steady at once, performing ignorance.

Felix crawled.

The tunnels were low, hand-and-knees work, and the satchel caught on roots and brick. Twice Felix stopped, chest heaving, listening to the town above: shouts, glass breaking, the roar of a second fire taking hold somewhere near the market square. Ashmoor was not a large place. Ashmoor would burn fast.

In the pocket against their chest, the fingerbones had begun—very faintly—to warm.

"Oh no," Felix said aloud, to the dark, to the bones, to whatever was listening. "No. Not now. I don't have time for you."

The warmth spread anyway, deliberate as a hand closing around theirs, and a voice scraped through the back of Felix's skull like a boot heel on gravel.

*Boy.* Vessa Marrow had called everyone boy—men, women, the harbormaster, once a bishop. *You're running the wrong way.*

"I'm running the only way."

*You're running toward the river gate. There are soldiers at the river gate. I can smell them—no, I can't smell, I'm a handful of knuckles, but I remember how sieges smell, and that's what's up there.* The voice sharpened. *Left at the next junction. The old sluice. It comes up behind the salt sheds.*

"How do you know the tunnels?"

A laugh, dry as bone dust. *I drew the maps that got them bricked, boy. Where do you think you learned to hide?*

Felix turned left. The passage narrowed, then opened into a junction where three ways met, and by the faint glow leaking from the satchel—Vessa's own map-bones, the ones carved with passes and roads, lighting up like a lamp in answer to their owner's voice—Felix saw the marks scratched into the brick a century ago. A crown with a slash through it. An arrow. A smuggler's sign Felix had never been able to read until now, when it was lit by the dead queen's own relics.

*She used these,* Felix thought. *She ran these same crawls.*

Above, Ashmoor burned. Felix thought of Tam standing in the doorway, lying badly or well. Of Maret, who'd bought a message from her drowned husband last spring. Of the widow Anselm, who might already be ash, who had sat in Felix's shop holding a rib bone to her ear like a seashell, hearing her boy say *run, Mother, run south, don't look back.*

Felix had taken her coin. Had given her a dead boy's voice and a living warning and had kept, all the while, the one thing that mattered: their own safety. Their neutrality. The careful, cowardly architecture of a life built so that no side could claim Felix Ravensworth, so that when the kingdom collapsed around them, the carver would still be standing in an unburned shop, invisible, untouched, alive.

It had always been an illusion. Felix saw that now with terrible clarity, crawling through filth while a town paid for their gift. Neutrality was a luxury purchased with other people's risk. Every client who'd walked out of the shop carrying a glowing sigil had carried a piece of the danger Felix had refused to hold. The widow had died—or would die—with Felix's mark in her house. Tam was lying to inquisitors right now because Felix had never once told him the truth.

*I can hear you thinking,* Vessa said. *It's loud. Stop it.*

"People are dying up there."

*People are always dying. The question is whether you die usefully or stupidly. Right now you're on track for stupidly, so keep crawling.*

Felix kept crawling. The sluice passage climbed, narrowed, ended in a wooden hatch crusted with a century of salt. Beyond it, daylight in thin blades. Felix put their shoulder to the wood, and it gave with a crack that sounded, in the burning town's din, like nothing at all.

They came up behind the salt sheds, in the strip of weeds between the sheds and the sea wall, and the wind brought the smoke straight at them: thick, black, greasy with tar. From here Felix could see the harbor road, and the gray cloaks moving along it in pairs, and a line of townsfolk kneeling on the cobbles with soldiers standing over them. Among the kneeling figures: Maret. Old Sefa. Tam, blood on his face, held up by two soldiers while a figure in a gray traveling cloak leaned close to him, questioning.

An inquisitor. Even at this distance, the office showed—in the way the soldiers deferred, in the stillness of the figure, in the small leather case open at their belt. Felix had seen inquisitors once before, years ago, at a distance, in the capital, before Thornfell fell. They had seemed almost gentle then. Nothing about this one looked gentle.

Tam's head came up. For one awful moment his eyes swept the weed-strip, the salt sheds, the gap in the world where Felix crouched—and Felix was sure, absolutely sure, that Tam saw them. Saw them and looked away, deliberately, turning his face back to the inquisitor and shaking his head.

*He's buying you time,* Vessa said quietly. *Again. How many times does that make?*

"One," Felix said. "One is enough."

*Then make it count, boy. South road's watched. Cliffs are watched—you can see the lanterns on the headland path. Which leaves—*

"The high trail." Felix's mouth was dry. "Through the fells. Nobody watches the fells. Nobody goes there."

*Nobody sane,* Vessa agreed. *Which is why you'll be fine. Move. Before that inquisitor decides your friend's face is more interesting broken.*

Felix moved—up through the gorse behind the sheds, along the goat track that clung to the sea wall, away from the smoke and the kneeling neighbors and the life that had taken ten years to build and ten minutes to lose. At the top of the bluff Felix stopped once, unable not to, and looked back.

Ashmoor burned prettily, the way small towns do, all its sins and kindnesses rendered down to orange light and black columns. The shop was gone—he could see the roof fall even from here, a soft collapse, a puff of sparks. Somewhere in that collapse were forty pieces of carved bone, glowing through the ruins, refusing to go dark even now. Refusing to let the gods' attention pass over this town, this coast, this carver.

*They know your hands,* the dead boy had said.

Felix pressed a hand to the pocket where Vessa's bones lay, and felt—not warmth this time, but something worse. Attention. The sense, sudden and total, of being seen from underneath the world, from below the ground and beyond the sky, by things that had slept a hundred years and were now, all of them, turning their faces toward one small figure on a cliff path.

*Run,* Vessa said, and for the first time since she'd woken, the smuggler-queen sounded afraid. *Boy, run. They're not just watching you anymore. They're calling.*

Behind Felix, Ashmoor burned. Ahead, the fells rose black against a sky that had begun, very faintly, at the highest point where the stars should have been, to glow gold.

Felix ran.
