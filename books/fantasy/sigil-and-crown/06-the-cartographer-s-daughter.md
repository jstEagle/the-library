## 6. The Cartographer's Daughter

Wren had been watching the stranger for half a day before she decided to make her move.

Not because the stranger was dangerous—though anyone climbing the Shepherd's Stair alone in late autumn was either dangerous or a fool, and Wren had learned to charge both the same rates. She watched because the stranger was *wrong*. Wrong in small ways that added up to something Wren couldn't name yet, like a map with one river drawn where another should be.

For one thing, they walked like someone listening. Everyone on the high trails listened a little—you learned fast or you fell—but this one stopped at the cairns. Every cairn, every waymarker, every weathered stone stacked by hands long dead, and stood there with their head tilted as if waiting for an answer. Once, near the third switchback, Wren saw them press two fingers to a cairn's top stone and hold them there, eyes closed, lips moving.

Talking to rocks. Wonderful. A mad pilgrim. Those paid worst of all.

For another thing, the stranger carried a satchel like it was a sleeping child. Not slung carelessly across the back the way climbers carried rope and iron, but hugged against the chest whenever the wind gusted, one arm curled around it even while scrambling over scree. Wren had grown up reading what people carried. That satchel mattered more than the person carrying it. Which meant whoever—or whatever—was inside it was worth robbing, if Wren had been the robbing sort.

She wasn't. Mostly. Her mother had been the robbing sort, before the passes ate her, and Wren had promised herself she'd be better at it: take payment honestly, never from the desperate, always leave people their boots.

So she waited until the trail narrowed above the Grey Linn waterfall, where the path ran along a shelf barely wide enough for one, with a drop into white water on the left and a wall of wet granite on the right. There was no way past her. There never was. That was the whole trick of guiding: you didn't need to be strong or clever, you just needed to know where the mountain got narrow.

She stepped out from behind the boulder she'd been warming herself against and put her hand on her knife—not drawn, just resting there, a courtesy—and said, "Toll."

The stranger startled badly. Actually jumped, one boot skidding on wet stone, arms pinwheeling, and caught themselves against the granite with a crack of knuckles. The satchel swung and they snatched it against their body with both hands before they'd even steadied their feet.

Wren revised her assessment downward. Mad pilgrim, then. Possibly armed with nothing but prayers.

"Toll," she said again, louder, in case the wind had eaten it. "This is my mother's trail. Wren Marrow's daughter keeps the Stair. You want to pass, you pay."

The stranger looked at her. They were younger than she'd guessed from a distance—maybe thirty, maybe less, with soot still ground into the creases of their knuckles and a burn-blister healing along one jaw. Ashmoor skin. Ashmoor smell, too, faint under the woodsmoke: tallow and lye. Someone who'd come out of the burning town through the tannery ways, which meant someone who'd known those ways, which meant someone Ashmoor had trusted.

Their eyes went to her face, then to her knife, then—strangest of all—to her hands.

"You said Marrow," the stranger said. Their voice was rough, like it hadn't been used in days.

"Wren Marrow. My mother was Sera Marrow, cartographer. She drew every pass between here and the Salt Teeth." Wren lifted her chin. "You might have heard of her."

Something moved across the stranger's face—too quick to read, gone before it landed. "Sera Marrow," they repeated. "Yes. I've—" A pause. A recalculation, visible as weather. "I've heard the name."

"Everyone who climbs has heard the name. Her maps are the only reason anyone's ever come down again." Wren held out her free hand, palm up. "Toll is three silver, or its weight in food, or news worth hearing. Your choice. But you're not getting past this shelf without me anyway, because the bridge below the Linn went out in the spring melt and the ford will kill you, and the only other crossing is a scramble you'll break your neck trying alone. So really, the toll is cheap."

The stranger kept looking at her hands. It was beginning to feel like an insult.

"Do I have something on them?" Wren demanded, turning them over. Ink stains, rope burns, a healing cut across the palm from a bad anchor placement last week. Nothing interesting.

"No," the stranger said softly. "You have her hands. Hers were broader in the fingers, but the—" They stopped themselves, jaw tightening, as if they'd bitten down on the rest of the sentence.

The wind came up the gorge and shoved between them, cold enough to make Wren's eyes water. The stranger stood there hugging their satchel, ash-marked, bleeding a little from the knuckles they'd split on the rock, and looking at Wren like she was a page torn out of a book they'd thought burned.

Wren had a bad feeling. Wren had learned to trust her bad feelings the way other guides trusted rope.

"You knew my mother," she said.

"I know *of* her," the stranger said, too fast.

"Liar." Wren stepped closer, and now the knife did come partway out of its sheath, not threatening exactly, just making a point. "Nobody looks at a stranger's hands like that unless they've held them. Nobody says 'hers were broader in the fingers' about someone they heard of. So here's how this goes now. Either you tell me who you are and how you knew my mother, or you turn around and climb back down to whatever's chasing you, because something *is* chasing you—I found your cold camp two valleys back, and I found the rider sign behind it, and riders in armor don't come up the Stair for the views."

The stranger flinched at that. Confirmed everything.

"They're not mine to explain," the stranger said finally. "And neither am I. If I tell you who I am, you'll do the smart thing, which is walk away and let them take me."

"Try me. I'm not smart. Ask anyone."

That got the ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "My name is Felix."

"Felix what?"

"Just Felix. For now." The stranger's grip shifted on the satchel. "The toll. You wanted a toll. I can pay silver." They fumbled one-handed at their coat, produced a small worn purse, and shook coins into their palm—three silver, old ones, minted with a king's profile that hadn't meant anything in years. Thornfell coin. Coin from a country that officially didn't exist anymore, spent quietly in border towns by people who couldn't quite stop believing in it.

Wren didn't take it.

"Keep your silver," she said. "Here's my new price. You tell me what's in the satchel, and why the Regent's riders want you, and what you said to that cairn at the third switchback. All three, or no crossing."

Felix Ravensworth—for Wren would learn the name later, though they never offered it—looked at the drop beside them, then back up the trail the way they'd come, where the armored riders were presumably gaining, then at Wren's outstretched hand.

"You won't believe me," they said.

"I believed my mother when she told me the mountain gods used to answer her maps. I was raised on impossible things. Try."

* * *

They talked on the move, because Felix kept glancing back down-trail and Wren refused to stand still while soldiers climbed toward them. She led them off the shelf onto the goat track that switchbacked up through the pines, narrow and root-laced, the kind of path that made strong men weep and goats yawn.

"The satchel," Felix said, breathing hard between words—they climbed like a scholar, all heart and no technique, and Wren slowed without deciding to, matching her pace to theirs the way she'd have matched it to a client paying full rate—"contains bones. Finger bones. They belonged to Vessa Marrow."

Wren stopped dead on the trail.

A pinecone dropped somewhere upslope. Water muttered in the gorge below. Felix stood two steps above her, watching her face do whatever it was doing, which Wren suspected was nothing good.

"That's not funny," she said.

"It isn't a joke."

"Vessa Marrow died a hundred years ago. Vessa Marrow is a story. My mother told stories about her—her own great-great-aunt or some such, the smuggler-queen who mapped every pass out of Thornfell and then vanished at the border with her whole crew. There's a song. It's a *bad* song. Children sing it wrong."

"I know the song," Felix said. "It gets the ending wrong. She didn't vanish. She died at the Threshold, and the door wouldn't open for her, and her crew scattered, and her bones—" Felix's hand settled on the satchel, gently, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "Her bones ended up with a bone-carver in Ashmoor who could hear the dead speak. Me. I hear them. That's what I do, that's what I did, quietly, for years, carving messages into bone so the dead could talk to the living who missed them. And three days ago Vessa woke up fully for the first time since I bought her finger bones off a grave-robber, and she told me the gods are waking, and they know my hands, and the only way out of this country is a door that officially doesn't exist, and I have to find it carrying her."

Wren sat down on a root. Not gracefully. Her legs simply decided the conversation required sitting and executed the decision without consulting her.

"You hear dead people," she said.

"Yes."

"And the dead smuggler-queen in your bag talks to you."

"When she feels like it. Mostly she insults me."

"What's she saying right now?"

Felix tilted their head—that same listening tilt from the cairns—and after a moment their mouth twitched. "She says you have the family stubbornness and that your mother broke her ankle on the Kettle Pass in the Year of Salt and never mapped it right afterward, and that if you're going to sit on a root you should check it for adders first."

Wren was on her feet so fast the world spun. "What did you—did she say that? Did *she* say that? Word for word?"

"She also wants me to tell you that your mother's survey marks are still legible on the north face of the Widow's Tooth, and that she was prouder of you than she ever managed to say out loud, and that she watched you bury her maps in the iron box under the hearthstone and she wants you to dig them up, because you'll need them."

Wren turned away. The gorge roared below. She scrubbed her sleeve across her face once, hard, and pretended the wind had done it.

Her mother had been dead eleven months. Fallen on the very pass she'd mapped best, which everyone said was fitting and which Wren thought was the cruelest joke the mountains had ever played. And now some ash-covered stranger with a bag of hundred-year-old knucklebones was standing on her mother's trail telling her things no living person knew.

"You could have gotten all that from her papers," Wren said, not turning around. Her voice came out sanded flat. "If you robbed our house. If you tortured it out of someone."

"Did she have a scar on her left thumb?" Felix asked quietly. "From a chisel slip, the winter she taught herself lettering?"

Wren's throat closed. She nodded, then remembered Felix couldn't see her, and said, "Yes."

"So do I. Chisel slip. Same trade, nearly. When she spoke to me just now, she didn't speak to me, Wren. She spoke through me. Dead voices need living hands sometimes, and mine were closest, and she—" Felix exhaled. "She's been trying to reach you for eleven months. The mountains are loud and you were far away and grief makes the living almost as hard to hear as the dead. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it came this way."

Wren stood there with the wind pushing at her back and let herself cry for exactly as long as it took to count ten breaths, which was a discipline her mother had taught her for altitude sickness and which worked nearly as well for this.

Then she wiped her face properly, turned around, and said, "Right. New arrangement."

* * *

They made the high camp in the last light, in the lee of a split boulder that Wren's mother had named the Two Sisters because it looked like hunched shoulders sharing a secret. Wren built the fire small and shielded—riders might see smoke—and cooked barley and dried mutton into a gluey stew that Felix ate like a starving man, which he apparently was.

"So the Regent's riders," she said. "Why you?"

Felix fed a stick to the fire. "Because the gods are waking. After a hundred years of silence, prayers are being answered again—a drowned child walked out of a river alive in some village, that kind of thing. The Regent wants to control that. Own it. Weaponize it. And the waking gods have apparently marked me as their—" a wry twist of the mouth "—doorkeeper. His inquisitors want me alive. What they'd do with me after, I try not to picture."

"And you're running to this Threshold. The door out of Thornfell."

"Not running." Felix glanced at the satchel, propped against the stone like a seated guest. "Being summoned, Vessa says. The door can't be found except by someone carrying her bones and a god's attention. I have both. Lucky me."

"And what happens when you find it?"

Felix was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "I don't know," they said at last. "Vessa knows more than she's telling. Gods always want something. Doors always cost something. But there are people coming behind me, Wren—villages that burned, families with nowhere left inside the borders. If there's a way out of Thornfell, a real one, they deserve to find it. Even if I don't much like what it costs me personally."

There it was. Wren had been circling it all afternoon—the wrongness she couldn't name—and now she had it. The stranger wasn't wrong because they were mad or marked or hunted. The stranger was wrong because they were *kind*, out here, alone, hunted, with nothing, and kindness on the high passes was the most dangerous cargo a person could carry. It got people killed. It had gotten her mother killed, in the end—gone back down into the valley for a stranded wool-trader's kid against her own rule about weather, and the mountain had collected.

"You should turn people away," Wren said. "It's kinder. You lead refugees up a mountain in autumn with soldiers behind you, you'll get them all killed, and you'll die feeling responsible, and none of it will have mattered."

"Probably," Felix agreed.

Wren stared. "That's it? 'Probably'?"

"I've already run from one burning town and left people behind. I'm not going to get good at it." Felix looked into the fire. "I'm not asking you to come with me. I'm asking you to guide me as far as the Kettle Pass junction. Three days. Then you go home to your iron box of maps and your life, and I go on to whatever this is."

Wren poked the stew. Thought about the iron box under the hearthstone, and her mother's voice saying *dig them up*, and the eleven months of silence she'd carried like a stone in her chest that had, in the space of one afternoon, developed a hairline crack.

"Three days' guiding is four silver," she said. "Plus hazard rate, plus the fact that you're being chased by the government, which my mother always billed extra for."

"I have five silver and a dead queen."

"Then we're even." Wren lay back against the boulder and pulled her blanket to her chin. "Sleep. We move at first light, and we move fast, because those riders will reach the burnt bridge by tomorrow noon and after that we want altitude between us and them. And Felix?"

"Mm."

"If my mother's maps say the Threshold route goes through the Kettle Pass, then the Kettle Pass is where we're going. But if you're lying to me—if any of this is a story you carved yourself—" she let him see the knife, resting against her hip, friendly as a sleeping animal, "—the mountain has a lot of places to leave a body."

Felix smiled, tired and real. "Vessa says you're definitely Sera's daughter."

"Damn right I am."

Wren closed her eyes. Sleep came slow, and when it came, she dreamed of her mother's hands—broad-fingered, ink-stained, scarred—steady on a pen, drawing a road that Wren had never seen, toward a door at the edge of the map, and beyond the door, faint as breath on glass, a light like the one now banked low in their little fire.

She woke once, deep in the night, to a sound like many voices murmuring in the wind off the icefields. Felix sat awake against the boulder, fire dead, head tilted, listening. In the dark, faint as embers, the sigils carved into the strap of the satchel were glowing.

Far below, tiny as sparks, torches were moving up the Shepherd's Stair.

The riders had reached the mountain early.
