## 7. Roads That No Longer Exist

They climbed for three days before Felix dared to stop.

The trail out of Ashmoor's burning skirts wound up through pine and scree, following a smuggler's path that existed on no map because maps could be seized. Wren had led them the first night, moving through the dark like someone who had been born knowing where the ground would betray them, and then vanished at dawn with a promise to catch up that Felix half-believed. The refugees strung out behind Felix in a ragged line: eleven of them now, maybe twelve—a tanner with burned hands, two sisters from the river villages, an old man who had been a toll-keeper on a road that no longer legally existed, and children. There were always children.

Felix counted them the way they counted everything now. Not out of hope. Out of inventory.

On the third evening they reached a hollow beneath an overhang of granite where the wind couldn't reach, and Felix made the decision they had been avoiding since Ashmoor: it was time to talk to Vessa properly. Not in fragments snatched between footsteps. Not in the sullen silence she'd maintained since the tunnels. Properly.

Felix built a small fire—risk be damned—and set the satchel open beside it.

* * *

The fingerbones lay on a square of black silk, five slender segments arranged in the order Vessa herself had insisted upon, decades ago, when she'd still spoken only in dreams. They were yellowed as old ivory, each one etched with a single character so fine Felix needed firelight and squinting to read them. Together the characters spelled nothing. Apart, they spelled everything. That was very like her.

"Vessa Marrow," Felix said quietly. "Queen of the Unmapped Roads. Smuggler of the Salt Coast. I need you awake."

The bones did nothing, which was answer enough. When Vessa wanted to sleep, entire armies could not have woken her; when she wanted attention, she made sure you regretted asking.

"I need to know why the gods are waking." Felix fed a stick to the fire. "I need to know why they marked me. And I need to know what you're not telling me, because you've been sulking since the tannery and you have never once sulked without a reason."

Silence. Wind moved through the pines above the overhang, a long exhalation.

"I carried you out of a burning town," Felix said. "I left people behind for you. Maren is probably dead. The Kessels are probably dead. So you will talk to me, or I will bury you somewhere beautiful and walk away, and we will both find out how much of my luck was actually yours."

That did it.

Warmth bloomed up through the granite, sudden as fever. The fire bent sideways though there was no wind, and the air above the fingerbones shimmered, and then she was there—the way she always appeared, assembled out of shadow and heat and the stubborn refusal to be forgotten. A woman of perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, dark-haired, wearing a coat Felix knew had once been real and now remembered being real only imperfectly, its buttons shifting between brass and bone depending on how hard she concentrated. Her eyes were the worst part. They had died sharp, and death had not dulled them.

"You threaten my bones," Vessa said, "and you wonder why I don't confide in you."

"You don't confide in me because you're a smuggler."

"I was a queen."

"You were a smuggler with better branding."

Something flickered across her ghost-face that might have been approval. She drifted closer to the fire, or rather the fire leaned toward her, flames tilting like courtiers. Below them, the refugees slept in their huddle of blankets, and Felix kept one ear tuned to their breathing even while speaking with the dead. That was the trade. That had always been the trade.

"The gods are waking," Felix said. "The boy warned me. The sigils glow without being carved. Inquisitors burned my life down looking for me. Explain it."

Vessa looked into the fire for a long moment. Ghosts didn't need to think—they remembered thinking—but she performed the gestures anyway, and Felix had learned over the years that when Vessa performed hesitation, it meant the truth was expensive.

"When I was alive," she said finally, "I mapped every pass out of Thornfell. You know this. It's the whole reason you carry my fingers around in a bag like a relic."

"It's the whole reason you let me."

"Hush. Let me tell it my way, for once." She settled cross-legged by the fire, which was absurd, because ghosts cast no weight, but the firelight passed through her and threw no shadow and somehow that made her more solid, not less. "There were forty-one roads out of this country when I started. Mountain passes, salt caves, a ford under the lake that only ran dry in drought years, one tunnel through the roots of the Greyfell that the dwarrows dug and abandoned and that smelled like the inside of a promise. I found them all. I walked every one of them, and I wrote them down, and I sold passage to anyone fleeing the crown wars, and I got rich and famous and eventually dead, in roughly that order."

"And?"

"And there was a forty-second road." Her voice dropped, and the fire dimmed to match it. "I never walked it. I never sold it. Because it wasn't a road, Felix. It was a door."

The word landed in the hollow like a stone dropped down a well. Felix waited. Somewhere below, one of the children murmured in sleep, and Felix's hand moved instinctively toward the knife at their belt before settling again.

"The Threshold," Vessa said. "You've heard the name. Everyone has heard the name; that's the point of a name that means nothing. Fishermen say the Threshold is beyond the ice fields. Priests say it's a metaphor. My own people said it was a story I invented to charge double for the dangerous passes." She smiled, thin and bitter. "It was real. A door out of Thornfell—not out of its borders, out of its *existence*. Do you understand what I'm saying? Not a way past the boundary stones. A way past the erasure itself. The Regent didn't just conquer this country. He unmade it. He paid priests to unsay its name, bought historians to burn its records, bribed cartographers until Thornfell stopped appearing on maps. And when a country stops existing—" her ghost-hands spread wide, "—everything in it starts forgetting what it is. Roads forget where they go. Names forget whom they belong to. People start to fade at the edges, like ink in rain. You've seen it. Don't lie to me, carver. You've seen it in your clients' faces. Half the dead who come to your table can't remember their own surnames anymore."

Felix's throat tightened. They had seen it. They saw it every day—the dead arriving thinner than they should be, their voices fraying, their memories full of holes shaped exactly like the country that had been deleted around them. Felix had told themselves it was simply what happened to the dead over time. Everything faded. That was the trade's first law.

"But the Threshold," Vessa said, "was cut before the erasure began. By the old gods, back when they still bothered with doors. It doesn't lead anywhere in this world—it leads *out*, to wherever things go when they refuse to be forgotten. And it cannot be found by searching, because the country's erasure consumed it along with everything else. You cannot map a road to a place that has agreed not to exist."

"Then how do you find it?"

"You don't." Vessa turned those terrible dead eyes on Felix, and for the first time since Felix had known her—in dreams, in fragments, in the slow decades of carrying her bones—she looked afraid. "It finds you. It opens only for someone it chooses. Someone carrying both halves of the key."

"What halves?"

"One of them is me." She lifted her chin, defiant. "My bones. Not because I'm special, whatever you've decided to believe about your little relic collection. Because I died at the Threshold. A hundred years ago. I found it, Felix—I found the actual door, in a valley in the high mountains, standing alone in a meadow like the last tooth in an old mouth. And it wouldn't let me through."

"Why not?"

"Because the door demands a price, and the price is everything you are." Her voice cracked, and the fire guttered. "It opens only for someone willing to give up their name entirely. Their history. Their legend. Every road they ever walked, every person they ever loved, every song sung about them—all of it, surrendered, unrecorded, erased. Become no one, and the door becomes yours. And I stood in front of it with forty-one roads behind me and a name that half this country whispered like a prayer, and I thought: *what is a door worth if I walk through it as nobody? Who will remember the roads? Who will lead the next ones out?*" She laughed, and it was a horrible sound, dry as bone dust. "So I refused. And the door closed. And I went back down the mountain to keep smuggling, and I told myself I'd return someday when I was ready, and then the crown wars came, and I died in a cellar in Harrowgate with a knife between my ribs, and my name was already starting to fray at the edges because the erasure doesn't care whether you're alive or dead."

The fire popped. Neither of them moved.

"So that's half the key," Felix said slowly. "Your bones. Your failure."

"My bones. My *refusal*. They're the same thing, carver—that's the lesson I paid a century to learn. And the other half—" she pointed one insubstantial finger at Felix, and the gesture carried weight despite having none, "—is a god's attention. The Threshold answers only to the waking gods, because they're the ones who cut it. It needs their eyes on whoever stands before it. Which brings us to the question you actually came here to ask, and which I've been avoiding because the answer makes my marrow ache."

"Ask it anyway."

Vessa rose. The firelight streamed through her like water through a net.

"The gods aren't waking randomly. Nothing about this is random. For a hundred years they slept because a sleeping god can't be conscripted—the Regent tried, in the early years, before he settled for erasing them instead. But something changed. Something called them back. And they opened their eyes, all of them, everywhere, and do you know what every single one of them did first?" Her voice was very quiet now, pitched for Felix alone. "They looked at you."

"The boy's warning—"

"*The gods are waking and they know your hands.* Yes. And the sigils glowing unasked. And the drowned child in Vance's report walking out of a river with your maker's mark clutched in its fist—you didn't know I heard about that, but ghosts gossip, carver, we're worse than fishwives. Every sign points the same direction. This isn't a hunt, Felix. You're not prey. You're not even a fugitive."

The cold that moved through Felix then had nothing to do with the mountain night.

"They're calling you home," Vessa said. "Every hidden road in this country bends toward the Threshold, and every god that wakes turns its face toward you, because you are the one carrying my bones, and my bones are the key, and their attention is the lock turning. All those years you spent staying invisible, carving your quiet little messages, hiding behind artisan respectability—and the whole time you were being *kept*. Preserved. Held safe like a letter waiting for its reader. The gods didn't mark you when they woke. They marked you long ago. They've been watching you your whole life, waiting for the country to rot far enough that someone would finally need the door."

Felix stood up. Their legs felt distant, borrowed. "No."

"Yes."

"No. I'm a bone-carver. I hear dead people and I charge fair rates. That's all I am. That's all I've *made* myself."

"That," Vessa said, with something almost gentle, "is precisely what someone being summoned would say."

Below them, the refugees stirred in their sleep. One of the sisters rolled toward the warmth of another body. The old toll-keeper muttered a name in his dream—his son's, Felix guessed, or his own, worn smooth by repetition. Eleven lives, twelve, strung out behind Felix like beads on a cord because Felix had walked uphill and they had followed, and Felix had never promised anything, and they had followed anyway.

"If you're right," Felix said, "then this isn't a flight. It's a procession. I'm not running from the Regent's inquisitors—I'm walking a route the gods laid out, and everyone behind me is walking it too, and none of us chose it."

"Now you understand why I sulked."

Felix laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "What happens when we arrive?"

Vessa was silent for so long that Felix thought she had withdrawn. Then she said, softly: "You stand in front of a door that demands your name, your history, your self. Everything. And you decide whether you are the kind of person who pays it." Her dead eyes held Felix's living ones. "I wasn't. I chose my legend over the door, and look what my legend became: five fingers in a bag. Don't follow me, carver. Whatever else you do—whatever the gods ask—don't follow me."

* * *

Felix did not sleep. They sat at the mouth of the overhang with the satchel against their ribs and watched the stars wheel over the peaks, doing arithmetic they hated.

Eleven refugees. One guide who might return. One ghost with a century of regret. And somewhere below, closing fast along the low roads, an inquisitor named Sera Vance with a bone sigil bearing Felix's mark and a warrant written in a language that officially did not include the country it described.

If Vessa was right—if the waking was a summons—then every step Felix took toward the high passes was a step the gods had already anticipated. The salt shrine ahead, where Wren swore there was shelter. The Pass of Names beyond it. The valley where the Threshold had stood a hundred years ago, in a meadow that might not exist anymore either. The whole route unfurled in Felix's mind like a map drawn by someone else's hand, and Felix realized with a lurch that they had been following it since Ashmoor without ever choosing it—uphill, always uphill, toward the oldest part of the sky.

Behind them, a pebble clicked on scree.

Felix was on their feet with the knife drawn before thought caught up, and then relaxed by degrees: a small figure in a patched cloak, breath steaming, climbing the last stretch of trail with the easy stride of someone who owned every mountain she'd ever insulted.

Wren stopped at the edge of the firelight, hands visible, grinning.

"Told you I'd catch up," she said. Then her grin faltered, because Wren missed nothing, and Felix's face must have shown what the night had put there. "What happened? What did the bones say?"

Felix looked back down the mountain, at the dark country that no longer existed, holding its breath around them.

"We're not running," Felix said. "We never were. Come sit down. There's something you need to hear—and then you need to decide, right now, tonight, whether you're coming with me to the end of the road or going home while home is still a direction that exists."

Wren's grin returned, slower this time, and sharper.

"I told you already, carver," she said, dropping cross-legged by the fire. "I don't have a home. I have a route. Where does yours end?"
