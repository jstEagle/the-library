## 8. The Regent's Bargain

The palace of Thornfell still called itself a palace, which was the first lie Sera Vance had to swallow every morning. It had been one once—white limestone and banners and a king who held court in the long gallery—but now it was a fortress with pretensions. The banners were gone, burned for warmth during the first winter of the Regency. The windows on the south face had been bricked over after the third assassination attempt. What remained was grey stone, grey uniforms, and the Regent himself at the end of the map room, small against all that space.

He was studying a map of the eastern ranges when Vance entered. He didn't look up.

"Tell me about the child."

"The child, Your Grace?"

"Don't make me ask twice. You've been in Marrowfield four days. You filed nothing. I read everything that crosses my desk, Inquisitor, including the things that don't." He straightened. "A drowned boy walked out of the river with his mother's name in his mouth. The village prayed to a god nobody has worshipped since before my grandfather's regency. And you stood in the mud where it happened and wrote me *nothing*."

Vance kept her hands folded behind her back, parade rest, the posture she'd learned at fifteen in the Cadre school at Coldharbour, where they taught you that your body belonged to the crown before your soul did. "The initial report was inconclusive. I wanted to verify before wasting Your Grace's time."

"And?"

"And there is something in Marrowfield. Something real." She said it flatly, because he punished embellishment worse than he punished bad news. "The child's lungs were full of river water when they pulled him out. Three hours later he coughed it up on the bank and asked for supper. I examined him myself. Whatever happened to him did not happen through medicine or trickery. There was a bone sigil recovered from the shrine upstream—a maker's mark, a carver's signature pressed into the rib. Someone made that prayer *work*. Someone gave the old god a handle to hold."

The Regent crossed the room slowly, his boots loud on the flagstones. Up close he smelled of ink and lamp oil; he worked eighteen hours a day and slept badly, if the reports of his household were true. A man running on will alone. She had admired that about him once. She noticed, distantly, that she wasn't sure she still did.

"A bone-carver," he said.

"Yes, Your Grace."

"In Ashmoor?"

She hadn't told anyone that yet. The intelligence had come to her in a tavern conversation, half-drunk tanner's gossip, and she'd sat on it for two days while she verified. She watched his face now and understood she'd been expected to sit on it—that the withholding was itself a test, and passing it meant he already knew the answer. "Ashmoor," she confirmed.

"Mm." He turned back to the map. His finger traced the mountain spine of the country, the passes, the smuggler roads that appeared on no official chart but lived in the memory of every family that had ever needed one. "Do you know what I was doing when the gods went silent, Inquisitor?"

"No, Your Grace."

"I was nine years old. My father—the old King's brother—took me hunting in the western hills, and we came upon a shrine. Small thing. Moss-grown. A stone mouth carved into a hillside, and a bowl before it where travelers left coins. My father put a coin in the bowl and said a prayer for a good hunt, the way you'd toss salt over your shoulder. Habit. Nothing more." The Regent's voice stayed level, conversational, which was how she knew to be afraid. "The coin vanished. Not fell through a crack—vanished. And the stone mouth exhaled. Warm air. Like breath. Like something on the other side had leaned close to see who'd knocked."

"What did you do?"

"My father laughed and dragged me away. Said the stories were true after all, and wasn't that fine, we'd tell your mother." He tapped the map, on the hills, once. "Two weeks later the silence began. Every god in Thornfell stopped answering, all at once, like doors closing down a long corridor. Priests starved their orders trying to fast and pray their way back into favor. Whole villages emigrated. And everyone agreed, eventually, that it had been a mercy. Cleaner without them. No more blood in the shrine bowls. No more harvest rites where the wrong crop failed and someone had to be blamed." He smiled without warmth. "I have spent thirty years believing the silence was permanent. And then a dead child sits up in the shallows of the Marrow, and asks for supper."

Vance waited. This was the part where the shape of her commission would become clear, and she had learned long ago not to guess ahead of her superiors.

"I want him alive," the Regent said. "The carver. The one whose hands the gods know—I've read your field notes, don't look surprised, you write beautifully. Bring this person to me breathing, unspoiled, with their hands intact. A title comes with success. Land. The Wardenry of the Eastern Marches, if you want it—it's vacant, and it pays better than the Cadre ever will. A house with windows that face the sun."

"That is... generous, Your Grace."

"It is precise." He faced her fully now, and she saw the thing in his eyes that she had always chosen not to name: not ambition exactly, not even hunger. *Ownership.* The look of a man cataloguing assets. "You understand why it must be alive. Dead, the carver is a curiosity. Alive, the carver is an instrument. Think about what you described—a sigil that lets a god take hold. That is not superstition, Inquisitor. That is *interface*. Whoever holds the maker holds the mechanism. And whoever holds the mechanism holds the waking gods the way a falconer holds a hawk—not by loving it, but by knowing where the jesses tie."

The words landed in her stomach like stones dropped down a well. She heard herself say, "And if the gods cannot be held?"

"Everything can be held." He said it simply, without bravado, and that was what chilled her. "Everything that lives has a weight limit. Everything that answers has a name it answers to. We find the name. We set the weight. That is governance, Sera. That is all governance has ever been." He waved a hand, dismissal forming. "You leave for Ashmoor tomorrow. Take six men, no more—we don't want a procession frightening the fish. Quiet entry. Quiet exit. I want the carver in this room within the fortnight."

Vance bowed. At the door, his voice caught her again.

"One more thing." He was looking back at the map, at the mountains. "The child in Marrowfield. When it looked at you—what did you see?"

She thought of it. She had been trying not to think of it for four days, filling the hours with procedure and paperwork and the careful cataloguing of a bone rib into an evidence box lined with wool. But the memory was patient.

"He looked at me," she said, "like I was the one who'd been drowning."

* * *

Her quarters in the barracks annex were narrow and clean, one window, a cot, a writing desk. She had lived out of such rooms for eleven years and had never once thought of them as anything but sufficient. Tonight she sat at the desk with the evidence box open in front of her and did not touch it.

The rib lay in its bed of wool, pale as a sliver of moon. The maker's mark was cut deep and confident: a small bird, wings folded, above three vertical strokes. She had queried the guild registers in Coldharbour before leaving Marrowfield. Bone-carvers licensed their marks; it was a respectable trade, artisan work, the kind of craft the Regency tolerated because it produced rosaries and chess pieces and buttons. The mark matched a license issued nineteen years ago to one F. Ravensworth, resident of Ashmoor, occupation listed as *carver, devotional and ornamental*.

Nineteen years of quiet. Nineteen years of carving whatever paid. And then the gods woke up and reached, apparently, for those specific hands.

She took the rib out and turned it under the lamp. Up close she could see the tool-work: fine chisels, patient grain-reading, the kind of skill that took decades and could not be faked. And beneath the maker's mark, almost invisible unless the light raked across it, a second layer of cutting—sigils, dozens of them, packed tight as script on a page. Prayers, maybe. Or instructions. Or a language the dead spoke fluently and the living had forgotten.

"You're a letter," she murmured to it. "Someone sent a letter to a god, and a child stood up out of a river."

She should have felt triumph. This was the work, wasn't it? Eleven years in the Cadre, five as Inquisitor, and the commissions had always been the same in essence: find the thing that frightens the crown, and put it in a box. Smugglers. Seditionists. A prophet in the southern valleys who'd gathered four hundred followers by preaching that the Regency was a wound that hadn't been allowed to heal. She had boxed them all. She had been *good* at boxing them. The Regent trusted her precisely because she did not flinch, did not editorialize, did not stand in the rain of other people's tragedies wondering about her soul.

But the boy's eyes.

He had been sitting on the riverbank wrapped in his mother's shawl, blue-lipped, shaking, alive in a way that broke every law of bodies. And when Vance crouched in front of him with her credentials and her questions, he had looked up at her—and there had been no fear in his face. That was the wrongness of it. Children feared the Cadre the way they feared deep water; it was taught in the cradle. This boy had looked at her with the wide, terrible calm of the returned, as though he knew something about her that she didn't, as though the river had shown him the bottom of her too.

"*Like I was the one who'd been drowning.*"

She set the rib down. Her hands were steady—they were always steady—but there was a tremor somewhere underneath the steadiness, in the place where certainty used to live.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing. The Regent had said *instrument*, and she had nodded, because nodding was the job. But instruments were objects, and the carver was a person—somebody who'd spent nineteen years making quiet, honest things in a border town, whose worst crime might be a talent they never asked for. And the gods themselves. The Regent spoke of jesses and weights, but she had stood in the shrine at Marrowfield and felt what he had felt at nine years old and had spent thirty years pretending not to feel: that the old gods were not hawks. They were weather. They were the deep water under the ice. You did not own a thing like that. You survived it, or you fed it, or you built your house elsewhere—and the Regent, who owned everything else, had decided he owned this too, and he had sent *her*, because she had never yet told him no.

There was a knock. Her sergeant, Brandt, young and competent, with the kind of face that believed what authority told it. "Orders confirmed for dawn, ma'am? Six riders, Ashmoor?"

"Dawn," she said. "Yes."

"Ma'am." He hesitated. "They're saying in the mess that this one's different. That the gods are really coming back. Is that true?"

She looked at him—at the open, waiting face of a soldier who still thought the crown's word and the truth were the same object—and heard herself give the answer she had given a hundred times, the smooth one, the professional one.

"When we have the facts, Sergeant, you'll have them too."

He left. She sat alone with the lamp and the rib and the map of the eastern ranges she'd drawn from memory, and she found her pen moving before she'd decided to move it—sketching, in the margin, the little folded-winged bird of the maker's mark. Beneath it she wrote, in the shorthand only she could read:

*If the crown owns the gods, the crown owns the drowning. Ask: who is saved? Who is spent?*

Then, below that, smaller, as if written by someone else:

*The boy asked for supper.*

Vance closed the book. Outside, the wind came down off the mountains carrying the smell of snow, and somewhere east of her, past the dark hills and the sleeping passes, a town called Ashmoor lay along its river with its lamps going out one by one—lamps, workshops, a carver's bench, hands that gods knew by name. By dawn she would be riding toward it with six men and a warrant.

She told herself it was just another box to fill.

For the first time in eleven years, she didn't quite believe her own voice.
