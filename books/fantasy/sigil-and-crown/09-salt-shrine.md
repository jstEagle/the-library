## 9. Salt Shrine

The storm found them an hour before the shrine did.

Wren smelled it first—salt on wind that had no business carrying salt this far inland—and then the light changed, the gray afternoon going the color of old pewter, and Felix stopped walking so suddenly that Tammero, the tanner's boy with the burned hands, walked straight into their back.

"Storm," Felix said. Not a question. A verdict.

"I know," Wren said, because she did too. She'd grown up reading weather off the high passes the way other people read letters. "There's a cut in the cliff face about half a mile on. Old smuggler cache, maybe. Or nothing."

"It's not nothing." Vessa's voice came thin and sharp out of the satchel at Felix's hip, the fingerbones rattling like dice in a gambler's fist. Wren still hadn't gotten used to that—the way the dead woman spoke through bone, her voice arriving a half-second after you saw the satchel twitch. "There was a shrine on this stretch of road. Salt-cut into the cliff. My people used it when I ran the passes."

"A shrine to what?" Tammero asked.

"To paying tolls," Vessa said, and said nothing else.

The company—eleven of them now, since the Aldous family had joined two days back with their ox and their silence—picked up speed without being told. Fear moved them faster than any order could have. Wren counted heads out of habit: eleven refugees, one bone-carver who wasn't quite a person anymore, and herself, who was starting to wonder which category she belonged to.

She'd been following Felix Ravensworth for six days now, ever since she'd caught them on the trail above Ashmoor's ashes and demanded passage money they didn't have. She still hadn't been paid. What she'd gotten instead was stranger: a place in a column of the displaced, walking roads that officially did not exist, toward a door that might not either. Some nights she thought she'd been robbed. Most nights she thought she'd been chosen, though by whom, and for what, she couldn't have said.

The cut in the cliff appeared exactly where she'd guessed it would—a seam of shadow in the rock face, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall. The ice had built itself into a wall over the years, but someone had broken through it recently. Boot prints. More than one set.

"Others are sheltering here," Wren said.

"Were," Felix corrected quietly, kneeling to read the snow. "Two days old. They went on."

"In this?" Tammero gestured at the darkening sky.

"People walk into storms when what's behind them is worse," Felix said, and Wren heard something under the words—not pity, exactly. Recognition.

They broke the ice wider and filed inside, and Wren understood immediately why Vessa's smugglers had kept it secret. The shrine opened inward like a throat. The passage narrowed, then widened again into a chamber tall enough to stand in, its walls carved floor to ceiling with channels—grooves cut in spirals and weirs, like the inside of an enormous instrument. And everywhere, salt. White crusts bloomed from the stone like frost that had decided to stay forever. Water seeped somewhere in the dark, a sound like slow whispering, and where it touched the walls it left fresh salt behind, growing, always growing.

Wren touched a spiral groove and pulled her fingers back wet. "It's alive."

"It's a god," Felix said. "A small one. But they're all alive, now."

That was the thing Wren was still learning about the world since the gods had started answering prayers again: everything had to be re-sorted. Rivers were not just rivers. Shrines were not just ruins. And the quiet person carving bone by the fire at night was not just a craftsman.

Felix set the company to arranging themselves—dry corner for the Aldous baby, fire ring well away from the salt walls, watches posted—and then stood a long moment facing the deepest part of the chamber, where the whispering water gathered in a black pool no wider than a wagon wheel.

"You knew this place," Wren said, coming up beside them. It wasn't a question either. She was learning to ask those less.

"Vessa did." Felix lifted the satchel. Inside, the fingerbones had gone restless, clicking against each other like teeth. "She says the river god here took toll from every runner who used the pass. Safe crossing, dry shelter—one memory per head. Cheap, she says. Fair, even." A pause. "She also says she never paid. She stole past it instead, up the outside of the cliff in worse weather than this. She was very proud of that."

"And now?"

"Now there are eleven of us, and one of us is ninety years old, and one of us is four months old." Felix began unbuttoning their coat. "We pay."

* * *

Wren watched from ten feet back, close enough to see everything and far enough to pretend she hadn't.

Felix knelt at the edge of the black pool and did something with their hands—drew a sigil in the air, or maybe just in the salt dust, Wren couldn't tell—and the whispering in the walls stopped all at once. The silence was worse than the sound. It was the silence of something turning its attention toward you.

Then the pool rose.

Not much. An inch, maybe two, water climbing against its own nature, gathering itself upward into a shape that was almost a figure—tall, narrow, featureless except for two hollows where eyes would go if eyes were the kind of thing rivers bothered with. It had no voice. Instead the chamber spoke for it: every drop of seeping water, every salt crystal ticking as it grew, arranged themselves into words that arrived inside Wren's skull rather than her ears.

**TOLL.**

"One crossing," Felix said. Their voice was steady. Their hands, Wren noticed, were not. "Eleven souls. Shelter tonight, safe road tomorrow, and we trouble your water no further."

**ELEVEN,** the god said, tasting the number. **CHEAP FLESH. DEAR PASSAGE.**

"We can offer memories. That's the old rate, Vessa says."

Something moved in the water-shape—amusement, or hunger; Wren genuinely could not tell the difference. **THE BONE-WOMAN NEVER PAID. HER DEBT ACCRUES STILL. A CENTURY OF INTEREST.**

Felix's jaw tightened. "Her debt died with her body."

**DEBTS DO NOT DIE. THEY WAIT. LIKE ME.** The shape leaned closer, water straining against gravity, and the hollow eye-fixes fixed on Felix with a weight Wren felt physically, like a hand pressing down between her shoulder blades. **BUT YOU. YOU ARE INTERESTING, DOORKEEPER.**

"Don't call me that."

**THE OTHERS CALL YOU THAT IN THEIR PRAYERS. I HEAR ALL PRAYERS SPOKEN NEAR WATER. YOU ARE THE ONE THE OLD ONES MARKED. THE ONE WHOSE HANDS THEY KNOW.** The whispering swelled, and Wren realized the whole cliff was resonating, salt singing against stone. **FOR SUCH A ONE, THE TOLL IS NOT CHEAP.**

"Name it."

**A MEMORY. BUT MINE TO CHOOSE.**

And here was the thing Wren would turn over for nights afterward, the thing that made her afraid of Felix Ravensworth more than she was afraid of the Regent's inquisitors, more than she was afraid of the storm: Felix didn't hesitate. Didn't bargain, didn't flinch, didn't do any of the things a person does when a god announces it will reach into your skull and take what it likes.

"Yes," Felix said.

**YOU DO NOT ASK WHAT I WILL TAKE?**

"You'd take it anyway if I asked wrong. And—" Felix's voice caught, just barely, a hairline crack in good masonry. "And I think you'll choose fairly. Small gods are honest. It's only the big ones that lie."

The water-shape went very still. Then it laughed—or the chamber laughed, salt crackling, drips stuttering into something like rhythm—and Wren felt the attention swing from Felix to her, to Tammero, to the sleeping baby in the corner, weighing, cataloguing, deciding.

**FAIRLY, THEN.** The shape sank lower, until it was level with Felix's kneeling form, close enough that spray touched their face. **THE FACE OF THE WOMAN WHO MADE YOU.**

"No," Wren said, before she knew she was speaking.

Both of them turned. The god's attention landed on her like cold water poured down her collar.

**THE GUIDE SPEAKS.**

"She doesn't speak for me," Felix said quietly. "But she's right to be angry. Go on. Take it."

**IT IS ALREADY TAKEN,** the god said. **I ONLY NAMED IT SO YOU WOULD KNOW WHAT YOU LOST.**

And Felix made a sound.

Wren had heard people grieve before—she'd buried a father, watched her mother go quiet as a closed door—but she had never heard anything like the noise Felix made in that moment. It was small. It was barely anything. A single exhale shaped like a word that never got finished, *Ma-* and then nothing, because there was nothing left to finish it with. Felix's hands went to their own face, pressing their palms against their cheeks as if checking whether the face was still there, and Wren understood with horrible clarity that Felix was trying to remember something and finding smooth blank stone where the memory should have been.

"What did she look like?" Felix whispered. To the god. To the chamber. To no one. "What did she—I can't—I know I loved her, I know it the way I know my own hands, but I can't see—"

**THAT IS WHAT A MEMORY IS,** the god said, and for the first time its voice was almost gentle, which was somehow the worst thing yet. **NOT INFORMATION. SIGHT. YOU KEPT THE LOVE. I TOOK ONLY THE LOOKING.**

"That's not mercy."

**NO,** the god agreed. **IT IS ACCOUNTING.**

The water-shape dissolved. The pool went flat and black and ordinary, and the whispering resumed in the walls, and the salt went on growing, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere in the dark, water dripped once, twice, keeping time.

Felix stayed kneeling at the pool's edge for a long time.

* * *

Later, when the fire was burning low and most of the company slept, Wren found Felix sitting apart, sharpening a carving knife that didn't need sharpening. The motion was steady, rhythmic, mechanical—the work of hands while the mind went elsewhere. Or nowhere. Wren wondered what it was like, reaching for your mother's face and finding nothing. She decided she never wanted to know, and knew she probably would someday. Everyone lost things. The gods had just made loss itemized now.

"You didn't have to do that alone," Wren said.

"There was nothing to share out. One memory, one payer."

"You could have refused. Found another way around. Climbed the outside of the cliff like your queen did."

"Vessa climbed alone, young and arrogant, and lost two runners doing it." The knife paused. "I'm not alone anymore. That changes the math."

Wren sat down across the fire from them. Up close, in the low light, Felix looked older than she'd first guessed and younger than she'd feared—just a tired person with salt dust on their sleeves and a hole in their head where a face used to be.

"What was her name?" Wren asked. "Your mother. You'd keep the name, wouldn't you? Names aren't faces."

Felix was quiet long enough that Wren thought they wouldn't answer.

"Maren," they said finally. "Maren Ravensworth. She liked—"

The knife stopped.

"I don't know what she liked," Felix said. "I know her name and I know that I loved her and I know that she's why I carve bones the way I do, her grip, her angle, I can still feel her hands guiding mine, isn't that strange? The lesson survived. Only the teacher's face is gone." A breath that wanted to be a laugh and failed. "The god was precise. I'll give it that."

"Will you get it back?" Tammero asked from his bedroll, and Wren shot him a look, but Felix shook their head.

"Gods don't refund."

"But the Threshold," Tammero pressed, sitting up. "The door out of Thornfell. People say—Vessa says—it gives things back. Second chances. That's why everyone's walking toward it, isn't it? Not just to leave. To undo."

Wren watched Felix carefully. Watched the stillness come over them, the particular stillness of someone holding a truth they weren't ready to say aloud.

"Maybe," Felix said, which was not a yes, and was not a no, and fooled nobody.

In the satchel, the fingerbones stirred. Vessa's voice came out low and strange, stripped of its usual bite. "Ask her what she remembers of the Threshold's toll, Felix. Ask her what she's been avoiding telling you since Ashmoor."

"Vessa," Felix warned.

"Ask me," the dead queen said, "or ask the next god we meet. But one of these days, carver, you'll have to hear what the door costs. And I am so very tired of being the only one who knows."

The fire popped. Outside, the storm hammered the ice curtain over the shrine's mouth, and the salt walls grew another invisible fraction of an inch, and somewhere far below them, water whispered its patient accounting.

Felix looked at the satchel for a long moment. Then at Wren. Then at the fire.

"Not tonight," they said.

But Wren saw their hands shaking around the knife, and she thought: *tonight, tomorrow, it doesn't matter. Whatever Vessa knows, it's already waiting for us down the road.* And for the first time since she'd joined this doomed pilgrimage, Wren was afraid not of what was chasing them—

but of what they were walking toward.
