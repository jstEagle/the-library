## 10. The Company of the Missing

They came out of the smoke like moths.

Felix saw them first as movement on the ridgeline behind—dark shapes against a sky the color of wet ash—and for one foolish moment hoped it was wolves. Wolves could be waited out. Wolves didn't ask questions.

Wren spotted them too, from her perch on the fallen pine where she'd been chewing a strip of dried venison and pretending not to watch Felix sleep. She spat. "That's people."

"I can see that."

"That's *a lot* of people." She hopped down, hand drifting to the knife at her hip out of habit rather than intent. "Where's your god now?"

Felix didn't answer. The sigils in their satchel had been warm all morning, humming faintly against their ribs like a second heartbeat, and they had learned over the past weeks what that warmth meant: something was coming, and the old powers wanted them awake for it.

The shapes resolved into figures. A dozen at first, then more strung out along the trail behind them like beads off a snapped cord—families, mostly. A man carrying an old woman on his back. Two children leading a limping goat. A young soldier with his uniform burned off at the sleeves, which meant he'd walked out of a fire the Regent's men had set, which meant he'd run from his own side.

They stopped twenty paces away, a ragged crescent of faces, and every one of those faces turned to Felix with the same expression. Not hope exactly. Hope was too clean a word for what lived in eyes that had watched villages burn. It was closer to hunger—the kind that comes after you've starved so long you've stopped believing food exists, but your body hasn't gotten the message.

"You're the bone-carver," said the man with the old woman on his back. He said it the way other men said *you're the doctor* or *you're the priest*. "From Ashmoor way. They say you talk to the dead."

"They say wrong," Felix said. "I carve trinkets."

"The dead boy in Harrow Fen," the soldier said quietly. "After the flood took him. His mother swears he spoke through a piece of carved rib. Said her name. Said—" he faltered, then finished anyway, because grief makes men honest, "—said she shouldn't wait up for him anymore."

Felix remembered that carving. Three years ago. A mother's coin, a mother's grief, a few careful words passed between worlds. They had thought nothing of it at the time. That was the whole trick of staying invisible: do small kindnesses, take small payments, never let the work grow a reputation.

And now here was the reputation, walking up a mountain trail with a goat.

"Whatever I was," Felix said, "Ashmoor's ash now. Same as your villages, I'd guess. I'm nobody. I'm going nowhere useful."

The old woman on the man's back lifted her head. Her face was a topography of wrinkles, and her voice came out dry as seed husks. "You're going up," she said. "Nobody goes up. Up is the passes, and the passes are death, unless somebody knows the way." Her clouded eyes fixed on Felix with terrible precision. "And things have been *answering* lately. Down in the low country, we prayed into the dark for sixty years and got silence. Now my granddaughter prays for rain and gets rain. My neighbor prayed his mule out of a ravine." She paused. "The gods are waking. And waking gods don't follow nobodies."

*They know your hands.* The boy's voice, months ago, coming through the bone wrong—not grief but warning. Felix had spent every day since trying to prove him a liar.

Behind them, Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "Told you," she murmured. "Witnesses are a curse."

*

They did not turn the refugees away.

Felix told themselves, that first night, that this was temporary—that by morning they'd point the company toward the southern trade road, the one that still technically existed, and wash their hands of it. The southern road led to amnesty camps. Amnesty camps led to the Regent's registers, and the registers led to names written down and names written down led, sooner or later, to inquisitors. But that was the refugees' choice to make, not Felix's. Felix owed them nothing. Felix had never promised anything.

"You keep saying that," Vessa said, from the satchel, as Felix sat apart from the campfire feeding sticks to the flames. "Never promised anything. Never asked them to come. As if a promise is the only rope that binds a person."

"You're dead. You don't get opinions about binding."

"I'm dead and *free*, dear, which is more than I can say for you." The ghost's voice had that edge it got when she was enjoying herself, which was most of the time, which was one of the many unbearable things about her. "Look at them. Go on. Look."

Felix looked. The firelight caught maybe forty faces now—more had drifted in through the evening, drawn by smoke and rumor the way everything in Thornfell seemed drawn to Felix lately whether Felix willed it or not. The children were already asleep in a heap like puppies. The soldier—his name was Dain, he'd offered it twice, unprompted—was standing watch without being asked, because soldiers couldn't help it. The old woman sat closest to the flames, wrapped in everything she owned, which was one blanket and a coat.

"They think you're leading them somewhere," Vessa said. "Somewhere safe. Somewhere *out*."

"There is no somewhere out. There's a Threshold that might be a story, at the end of roads that officially don't exist, findable only by—" Felix stopped.

"By someone carrying my bones," Vessa finished, "and a god's attention. Yes. We've established this. So here's my question, carver, and think carefully before you answer, because I've been dead a century and I've had time to get good at questions." The satchel shifted slightly, the fingerbones inside rearranging themselves the way she did when she leaned forward. "If there's even a chance the door is real—if there's a chance these people can walk out of a country that's been erased from every map and every ledger—do you honestly intend to send them south to the amnesty camps instead? To be counted. Catalogued. Kept."

Felix fed another stick to the fire. Sparks climbed and died.

"No," they said finally.

"There it is."

"But I'm not promising them anything either."

"Oh, *obviously* not," Vessa said. "Heaven forbid."

*

The days found a shape after that, though Felix refused to call it leadership.

It went like this: each morning, Felix would walk the line of the company and note who was failing. The man whose hands shook too badly to hold a flint. The girl of perhaps nine who hadn't spoken since her village. The goat, who hated everyone. Felix would adjust the pace without announcing it, redistribute weight from the weakest backs to the strongest through some fiction about balance, and position themselves beside whoever looked most likely to sit down in the snow and stay there.

"You're doing the thing," Wren observed on the fourth day, watching Felix trade rations with Dain so the old woman would get the soft bread.

"What thing."

"The shepherd thing. You know, when you pretend you're just wandering in the same direction as the sheep."

"I am *wandering in the same direction as the sheep*."

"Mm." Wren chewed on a pine needle, her version of deep thought. "My mother guided caravans. Real ones, with contracts and tolls. She used to say the difference between a guide and a liar is that a guide tells you how bad the road is *before* you're on it." She looked sideways at Felix. "So tell them how bad the road is."

"And watch them give up? Half of them wouldn't survive knowing what's ahead."

"Then half of them deserve to choose." Wren said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You don't get to carry everyone's hope in your pocket and also decide they're too fragile to spend it. That's not kindness. That's just another kind of hoarding."

Felix wanted to argue. The argument was right there, fully formed: *I never asked for any of this. I was a bone-carver. I kept my head down for thirty years and the sky fell on me anyway.* But the girl who hadn't spoken since her village had started following Felix at a distance of exactly six paces, close enough to feel safe, far enough to flee, and when Felix stopped, she stopped; when Felix walked, she walked. And last night, when Felix had paused at a stream to drink, she had crouched beside them and—wordlessly, eyes down—placed a smooth grey pebble in their palm. An offering. The kind children make to things they think are holy.

Felix had kept the pebble. It was in their pocket now, and they were aware of it the way they were aware of the sigils' warmth.

That night, around the fire, the old woman called Marta caught Felix's sleeve as they passed.

"Bone-carver. Do a job for me."

"I don't take commissions anymore."

"It's not a commission. It's a favor." She pressed something into Felix's hand: a knucklebone, yellowed with age, worn smooth at the joints. "My husband's. Forty years gone. I never had anyone to ask before—never had the coin, never had the nerve. I don't want to talk to him. I just want to know if he made it wherever he was going. One word. *Yes* or *no*. That's all."

Every instinct Felix had honed across three decades screamed the same instruction: *refuse, deflect, disappear.* Every carved word was a thread, and threads accumulated, and threads were what inquisitors followed.

But the woman was looking at Felix with her clouded eyes, and around her the firelight lay gentle on forty sleeping faces, and the sigils against Felix's ribs pulsed once, slow, like a heart saying *yes.*

Felix closed their hand around the knucklebone. "One word," they said. "Tonight, after they're asleep."

Marta nodded and turned away, satisfied, as if this were a normal transaction between normal people in a normal country—as if the world had not ended and been quietly replaced with a stranger, harder one. And Felix stood there holding a dead man's bone and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the end of exhaustion, that neutrality had died in Ashmoor along with everything else. There was no more staying invisible. There was only choosing what to be visible *for*.

*

The word came at midnight, and it was *yes.*

Marta wept without sound, the way old people do, folding the knucklebone back into her shirt like returning a letter to its envelope. Then she did something worse than weeping. She went down the line of sleepers and whispered, and by dawn half the company knew—not rumors now, but certainty, witnessed—that the bone-carver spoke truly with the dead.

They began leaving things by Felix's bedroll. Not payment. Offerings. A carved wooden bird. A lock of hair tied with red thread. Notes folded small: *ask my father if he forgave me. Ask my sister if she suffered. Ask if there's a door, ask if there's a door, ask if there's a door.*

Felix read none of them aloud. But they kept every note, pressed flat in the satchel beside Vessa's bones, until the ghost complained she was being buried alive in paper.

"You realize what you've become," she said one gray morning as the company wound upward through scree fields, the peaks ahead white and indifferent. "A century ago I ran the largest smuggling operation in Thornfell's history. Do you know what my people called me? Not queen—that was the newspapers. They called me *the Ferrywoman.* Because I moved people from one side to the other, past every wall and ward the crown ever built." A dry laugh. "And now look. Look at you, trailing forty souls up a mountain toward a door that eats names. History doesn't repeat, carver. It just changes hands."

"I'm not you."

"No," Vessa agreed. "You're worse. I charged passage."

*

On the sixth day, the weather turned, and with it the company's luck.

Snow came sideways off the heights, fine as flour, erasing the trail within hours. Felix called the halt early—called it, Wren noted with insufferable satisfaction, *like a shepherd*—and they sheltered in the lee of a granite shoulder, backs to the stone, bodies packed together for warmth. The children in the middle. The goat, miraculously tolerated, in the middle too.

It was during that long white afternoon that Dain came to sit beside Felix, close enough to speak under the wind.

"I was at Thornholt," he said, without preamble. "When it burned."

Felix kept their eyes on the snow. Thornholt. Another name for the list of places that no longer existed.

"I wasn't ordered to burn it. I was ordered to *stand guard* while others burned it. There's a difference, they told us. A legal difference." His jaw worked. "There was a granary. People had locked themselves in. The captain said the fire would be merciful compared to what the inquisitors would do if they found god-touched inside." He stared at his hands. "I stood guard. I did my legal difference. And afterward I heard—" his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—afterward, for weeks, I heard singing. In my sleep. The kind of singing you hear from a burning building if you stand close enough and do nothing."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere above, snow sloughed off a ledge with a sound like a held breath released.

"Why are you telling me this?" Felix asked.

"Because you talk to the dead, and I need to know if they hate me. That's all. I don't need forgiveness. I need data." He laughed, horribly, at his own word. "The inquisitors taught us that word. Data. Dead people are data. Burned villages are data." He turned and looked at Felix directly, and his eyes were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of something. "So. Do they hate me?"

Felix thought of the boy's voice through the bone. Of the warnings. Of the dead of Thornfell, rising at the Pass of Names, demanding remembrance—not vengeance. Never vengeance. Only memory, always memory, as if hatred were a luxury the dead couldn't afford.

"They don't hate you," Felix said. "They're too busy asking to be remembered. That's the whole of what they want. All of them. Every one I've ever spoken to." They hesitated, then gave him the truth like a coin given to a beggar, quickly, before shame could intervene. "Remember them, and you're square. Forget them, and you'll hear that singing for the rest of your life. That's the entire law of the dead. It fits on a coin."

Dain bowed his head. When he raised it again, something in his face had settled, the way sediment settles in shaken water. "Then I'll remember," he said. "Names and all. I've got a good memory. It's the only part of me the army didn't use."

He rose and went back to his watch, and Felix remained against the cold stone, understanding that something had shifted in the company's architecture—invisible yet structural, like a keystone turning. They had come to Felix as fugitives following a rumor. But rumor had become testimony, testimony had become trust, and trust, Felix realized with a sensation like vertigo, was a load-bearing thing. You could not unbuild it. If Felix faltered now, the whole arch came down on forty heads.

*I never promised anything,* Felix thought, one final time, and heard the thought for exactly what it was: the last brick of the old life, loosening.

*

They broke camp at first light into a world scoured clean and silent, and that was when Wren, ranging ahead as she always did, came running back down the trail with her breath smoking and her face carefully, deliberately blank—which, Felix had learned, was Wren's face for *very bad news delivered calmly.*

"Smoke," she said. "Behind us. Two valleys back and closing. Not cook-fires." She glanced at the strung-out line of refugees, at the children, at the goat. "Riders. A lot of riders. Moving fast for someone who knows these trails."

Felix turned and looked back the way they had come. The mountains stood blue and stacked against the pale sky, innocent as a painting, and somewhere in all that beauty, someone was climbing toward them with a warrant.

The sigils against Felix's ribs went hot as coals.

"Well," said Vessa, from the satchel, with grim relish. "Shepherd. Your flock has wolves."
