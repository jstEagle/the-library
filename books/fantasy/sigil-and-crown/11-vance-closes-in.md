## 11. Vance Closes In

The prisoner was fourteen.

Sera Vance knew it before the guard told her, before the boy was shoved into the interview room of the waystation at Cold Ford with his wrists bound in front of him and his eyes fixed on the floorboards as if they might open and swallow him whole. She had spent eleven years reading faces for the Inquisition, and this one said nothing so much as *I am trying to become furniture.*

"Name," she said, when the guards had gone.

Silence. The boy's jaw worked. His boots were wrong—mismatched, one a size too large, stuffed at the toe with rags. Refugees wore whatever the road gave them. Soldiers wore what the crown issued. She had learned to look at feet first; faces lied, feet rarely did.

"You're not in trouble," she said, which was a lie, and then, because lying to children sat poorly even on a tongue trained for it: "That is, your trouble depends entirely on how much truth you give me."

He looked up at that. Brown eyes, chapped lips, a scab across one eyebrow that hadn't been cleaned properly. Somewhere behind her, the stove ticked as the iron cooled. Outside, sleet ticked against the shutters in answer, the two sounds keeping imperfect time like a clockmaker losing his nerve.

"My mother said—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "My mother said if anyone official caught me, I should say I was going to my uncle's farm. In Lowmarsh."

"And where is your mother?"

The silence stretched long enough that he answered it himself, quietly: "Ashmoor. When it burned." His hands turned over in his lap, studying his own knuckles as though they belonged to someone more fortunate. "She said run east. Everyone said run east."

Vance pulled out the chair across from him and sat, unhurried, laying her file on the table between them but keeping one hand flat upon it, closed. Interrogation was a trade like any other. You never showed the whole inventory at once.

"What's your name?"

"Tamsin. Tamsin Corrigan."

"And you left Ashmoor with the other refugees. How many?"

"I don't know. A lot. It changed. People joined on the trails, people gave up." His gaze flicked to her hand on the file, away again. "People got taken. Like me."

"Taken by whom? My riders took you at the river crossing yesterday. Before that—who else wanted you?"

Tamsin Corrigan went very still, the stillness of prey that has heard something in the grass. Vance had seen that stillness in villagers questioned about shrines, in priests questioned about prayers, in a drowned child's mother questioned about miracles. It was becoming the national expression of Thornfell, this careful quiet. The Regent would have called it loyalty to the old ways. Vance was beginning to think it was simpler than that. It was the silence of people who had learned that every institution within reach of them was a mouth, and mouths ate.

"The god-speaker," he said at last. Barely above a whisper.

*There it is.* Her pulse did not quicken—eleven years had burned the quickening out of her—but her attention narrowed to a point, the way it did over a lock whose tumblers were finally moving. "Tell me about the god-speaker."

"They don't—" He wet his lips. "They don't do anything. That's the thing nobody understands. We heard the stories, same as everyone. That there's someone out here who talks to gods, who can curse you or bless you, who the Regent wants because they're dangerous. So when we found ourselves following them, we thought—we thought we'd sold ourselves to something terrible, honestly, but the alternative was staying south, and staying south was dying. And then it turns out the terrible thing just—" He made a vague, helpless gesture with bound hands. "Walks. All night, every night. Gives their own food to the ones who ran out first. Sits up till dawn carving little bones for people, and when you ask what the carvings are for, they say *names*. They say the dead of Thornfell shouldn't be nameless, not even the ones the kingdom erased, so somebody's got to remember them somewhere the Regent can't burn."

The stove ticked. The sleet ticked. Vance kept her face in the practiced neutral that prisoners read as either mercy or doom, depending on their guilt.

"This god-speaker," she said. "Describe them."

"Small," Tamsin said. "Quieter than anyone I've ever met. Hands always stained with bone dust. They wear their hood up but the wind keeps taking it down and you can see—they're young. Younger than the stories. There's grey in their hair anyway, from the mountain nights." He hesitated, and something moved through his face that Vance recognized and distrusted on principle: tenderness. "They have a satchel they never put down. Old leather. And sometimes they talk to it. Not crazy-talk. More like—arguing with someone they respect."

*Vessa Marrow's bones.* The report from the Salt Shrine lay in her file, third from the top. A minor river god bought off with a memory. Witnesses—this boy among them—describing the fugitive paying grief itself as toll. Vance had catalogued it all as evidence of power hoarded: a god-speaker bargaining, collecting, accumulating favor with things older than crowns. It was how she had framed it for the Regent's ministers, and how they had framed it for the Regent: a weapon loose in the mountains, needing only capture to be aimed.

Now a fourteen-year-old with mismatched boots was describing the weapon giving away its bread.

"How many soldiers has this person killed?" Vance asked. "Since Ashmoor. Since the flight began."

Tamsin blinked. "None. I mean—I've heard of none. There was an avalanche at the high pass, but Wren says that was the mountain's own doing, that the speaker refused some bargain rather than pay it with a life. People argue about whether that's true. But no one's died. Not one. Not even the soldiers chasing us—the speaker makes us hide from patrols instead of fighting them. Every time. Even when we outnumbered them."

"Every time."

"Every time." The boy frowned, puzzled by her emphasis, by whatever he saw cross her face. "Isn't that what you'd want? Isn't that the point? To catch them without blood?"

It was the point, yes. It had been the point since Cold Ford, since the resurrected child walked dripping out of the river with his mother's name in his mouth and his father's grief transfigured into something too bright to look at. Find the god-speaker, take them alive, deliver the miracle to the crown intact.

But sitting across from Tamsin Corrigan and his dead mother's eastward instructions, Vance found herself asking a question she had not authorized: *alive for what?*

* * *

She walked the perimeter after dark, as she always did, because a commander who slept behind her own walls without walking them deserved to be surprised someday. The waystation at Cold Ford was a squat stone barn of a place, three days' ride from the pass country, garrisoned by eight men she mostly trusted and provisioned for a war that wasn't happening anymore, because officially Thornfell didn't exist and couldn't be at war with anything, least of all its own people.

Officially. The word had begun to taste like ash in her mouth.

At the north wall she stopped, as she'd known she would, and looked toward the mountains. Cloud cover sat low; the peaks showed themselves only in glimpses, white shoulders shrugged against black sky. Somewhere up there, a small quiet person with bone-dust-stained hands was leading two hundred civilians through winter passes that maps had stopped acknowledging a century ago, pursued by an army that answered to a Regent who had signed the erasure of their own sibling from every record in the palace archives.

Vance had read that file too. She was perhaps the only living officer besides the Regent who had. *Ravensworth, F. — records sealed by decree, bloodline confirmed, refusal of rite documented.* She had assumed, reading it a year ago, that it explained everything: the god-speaker was a rejected heir, and rejected heirs sought thrones. Power seeking power. It fit the shape of every treason she had ever hunted.

It did not fit a woman—or a man, the witnesses disagreed, or perhaps simply declined to be either—who fed strangers first and carved the names of the forgotten into bone by firelight.

Her breath fogged and hung. Behind her, in the stable, Tamsin Corrigan slept under guard, scheduled for transport south at first light, scheduled to become a line item in a report, *detained refugee, origin Ashmoor, disposition pending.* Disposition pending. The Inquisition's coldest phrase, the drawer into which human beings were filed when no one could decide yet how best to use them.

Vance thought of the resurrected child again. She thought of him often now; that was the trouble. His name had been Ollin Peake. He had been underwater for the better part of an hour while his village prayed to a god whose name they'd had to dig out of their grandmothers' memories. When he came up the riverbank, alive, gasping, wrapped in weed and impossible light, the first thing he said was not *help* or *mother.* It was: *she said thank you for remembering her.* As if the god had spoken through him. As if being dead had been a door and something kind had stood on the other side of it.

And stamped near the shrine where the village had prayed, half-buried in river mud, a bone sigil bearing a maker's mark—a small stylized raven, wings folded, patient. Felix Ravensworth's mark. The mark that had started all of this, that had turned Vance's quiet commission into a hunt.

She had assumed the mark meant claim. Territory staked, worship directed, power gathered to a center. That was how cults worked; she'd dismantled four of them.

But what if the mark had never meant claim at all?

What if it meant *service*? What if the bone-carver hadn't been gathering the waking gods but answering them—not as their master, but as their doorkeeper? The distinction seemed small. It was not small. A master could be captured and commanded. A servant could only be followed, replaced, or killed—and killing the servant of returning gods was the sort of act whose consequences no minister's report could model.

And a doorkeeper, Vance thought, watching the mountains hold their cloud, does not hoard. A doorkeeper holds the door *open*.

Two hundred refugees. No deaths. No battles. Bread given away. Names remembered. A memory paid at a salt shrine so a river would let travelers pass—paid, the witnesses agreed, from the speaker's own stock of self. Who gave up their mother's face to buy safe passage for strangers? Thieves didn't. Tyrants didn't. Weapons certainly didn't.

Only shepherds did. And shepherds were not the threat the Regent believed he was hunting. Which meant the threat was something else, or there was no threat at all, and the entire apparatus of the hunt—the riders, the checkpoints, the boy sleeping in a cell with a *disposition pending*—was a machine built to crush the last decent thing left in a broken country, because decent things were uncontrollable, and the Regent had learned from his sibling's example that uncontrollable things must be erased.

Vance stood at the wall until the cold made her fingers stupid, and then she went inside, and lit her lamp, and unsealed her report case.

* * *

The report wrote itself in the space of an hour, and every sentence of it was false.

Not flagrantly—that was the craft of it. False reports failed by lying boldly; good ones lied by arrangement. She reported the river-crossing engagement, accurate in every particular: one refugee detained, identity confirmed as Tamsin Corrigan, fourteen, of Ashmoor. She reported the intelligence gained, and here she arranged the truth like flowers in a vase, each stem cut short:

*Subject's methods suggest consolidation of influence over refugee population through religious authority. Subject's movements indicate possible rendezvous with armed elements in the pass country. Recommend continued pursuit with reinforced escort; subject believed to be approaching the Threshold region, where containment options narrow.*

All of it defensible. None of it true. There were no armed elements—Wren's scouts had seen to it that there weren't, and besides, the god-speaker forbade fighting. There was no consolidation of influence; there was bread shared and names carved. And containment options narrowing was true only in the sense that all options narrowed as winter deepened, including the option of pretending this hunt served justice.

Why lie, then? Why not resign, refuse, defect openly, walk into the mountains and offer her sword to the shepherd?

Because defection was a door that locked behind you, and she had eight men at Cold Ford whose families lived in towns the Regent could burn on a signature. Because a falsified report bought time, and time was the currency the mountains ran on—every week of *reinforcements requested, weather delaying pursuit* was a week the refugees climbed further beyond reach. Because the Regent read reports, not consciences, and a conscience that announced itself got executed alongside its owner.

Betrayal, Vance thought, sealing the dispatch with her signet, pressing the wax until the raven sigil of the Inquisition sank clean into red. Betrayal was not always a knife in the back. Sometimes it was a lie shaped like diligence, sent south to buy strangers time to outrun their king.

She could not undo it. That was the nature of the thing—she felt the irreversibility of it in her chest like a swallowed coal. From tonight forward she was a traitor to the crown regardless of what she did next, because the report existed, because it would be filed, because some clerk in the capital would eventually notice that Inquisitor Vance's pursuit estimates had grown suspiciously generous with delay. There was no version of the future in which she came back from this.

The strange part was how light her hands felt.

She woke Tamsin Corrigan before dawn, against schedule, against protocol. The boy came awake the way hunted things come awake—instantly, silently, already braced. Vance crouched by his pallet and set a water skin and a pouch of dried fish beside him.

"The transport leaves at first light," she said. "You'll be on it as far as the mill bridge at Harrow Beck. The driver will stop there to water the horses. It takes eleven minutes. After that, you run east, and you don't stop running east until you find the company you were taken from."

Tamsin stared at her. "You're letting me go."

"I'm releasing a detainee whose detention served no investigative purpose," Vance said, and heard how it sounded, and let it sound that way, because the boy deserved a version of events he could carry without owing her gratitude. Gratitude was another chain. "Tell the god-speaker nothing about me. Tell them nothing at all. But tell them—" She stopped. Eleven years of interrogations, and she could not find the sentence. She started over. "Tell them the Inquisition's estimate of their position is wrong. Tell them the southern passes will be clear of patrols for nine days. Nine days. After that, I can't help anyone."

"Why?" Tamsin whispered. It was the question she'd been waiting for, the one she had no honest answer to, and so she gave him the only one she had.

"Because I watched a dead child come out of a river saying thank you," she said, "and I have decided to believe him."

She walked him out through the grey beginnings of morning, past the sleeping garrison, to the mill road where the transport waited with its lantern lit. The driver was hers, a silent man named Odo who owed her his life and asked no questions, which was its own kind of betrayal now, compounding like interest.

Tamsin paused at the wagon step. "They'll come through the valley," he blurted. "The god-speaker. Vessa's map—it ends at a meadow below the icefields. That's all I know. That's why I'm telling you, so you can—" He faltered, confused by his own impulse, by the strangeness of warning your captor where your shepherd was going. "So you won't come to the meadow with soldiers. Please. Whatever happens there, it isn't a battle. Don't make it one."

*The meadow below the icefields.* The Threshold region. The place every thread of the hunt converged on, the place the Regent's own strategists had flagged months ago as the probable terminus, the place where—if the old stories held—something was going to happen that no report could contain.

Vance looked north, where the sky was paling over the peaks, and made her decision the way she made all her decisions now: quickly, irreversibly, with her stomach full of coals.

"I'll be there," she said. "Alone."

Then she put the boy on the wagon, watched the lantern swing away east toward Harrow Beck and freedom and a mother's grave, and turned back to the waystation to write the second lie of her new life: a requisition for nine days of patrol suspension along the southern passes, citing weather, citing supply lines, citing anything but the truth, which was that Inquisitor Sera Vance had finally found the law's edge and stepped over it, and discovered on the far side not chaos—

—but snow, and mountains, and a small light burning somewhere ahead that she intended, whatever it cost, to keep in sight.
