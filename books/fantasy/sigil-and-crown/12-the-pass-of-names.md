## 12. The Pass of Names

The boundary stones came into view an hour past dawn, and they were wrong.

Felix had grown up hearing about Thornfell's markers—the waist-high pillars of grey granite that had once ringed the kingdom like a fence of teeth, each one carved with the crown's sigil and the names of the wardens who'd set it. You didn't see them anymore. The Regent's crews had spent the first years after the collapse pulling them down wherever they stood, hauling them off in ox-carts to be crushed for road-fill, because a country that officially did not exist could not be permitted to have edges.

But here at the high pass, where the carts couldn't climb, the stones still lay where they'd fallen. Dozens of them, tumbled down the slope like dropped dominoes, half-buried in scree and old snow. Moss on their flanks. Lichen eating the crowns.

And every single one of them was humming.

Felt it first through the satchel—Vessa's fingerbones shivering against Felix's hip like a struck bell that wouldn't stop. Then through their own hands, which had begun, over these weeks on the road, to ache the way weather aches before a storm. The company felt nothing. Wren felt nothing. The forty-one refugees strung out along the trail behind them—farmers, tanners, two sisters who ran a dye-house in Lowmark, an old ferryman named Col with a bad knee—all they saw was a pass full of broken rocks and, beyond it, the last stretch of mountain between them and whatever waited on the far side.

Felix saw the dead.

They rose out of the ground the way frost rises—everywhere at once, silent, without drama. One moment the scree was empty. The next it stood shoulder to shoulder with soldiers. Old ones. The rusted mail of Thornfell's last king, the boiled leather of the border levies, the unmarked tunics of men whose regiments had been dissolved so thoroughly that even their deaths were now classified as weather. They filled the pass from wall to wall, hundreds deep, and they did not move, and they did not speak, and their eyes—

Their eyes were the worst part. Not hollow. *Expectant.*

"Felix." Wren's voice, low, at their shoulder. She'd gone still in the particular way she went still when she'd decided running wasn't an option. "What is it? What's up there?"

"Dead," Felix said. "A lot of them."

"I don't see anything."

"You're not supposed to." Felix shifted the satchel around and dug out a knucklebone—one of Vessa's lesser relics, kept for exactly this kind of question. Pressed it flat between both palms. "Vessa. Wake up. I need you."

For three weeks now the smuggler-queen's ghost had been waking easier, speaking longer, complaining louder. She surfaced now like a diver breaking water, and Felix felt her attention snap across the pass and lock onto the standing dead, and heard her breath go out of her in a hiss that no living throat could make.

"Oh," Vessa said. "*Oh.* I know this pass. Ravensworth, get them back. Get everyone back off the trail right now."

"Why? What is this?"

"This is the toll gate." Her voice had lost its usual acid; underneath it, for the first time since Felix had carried her bones out of burning Ashmoor, there was fear. "The old kings never paid their soldiers in coin when the coffers ran dry. They paid them in *remembering*. Every levy, every garrison, every poor bastard who died holding a line nobody wrote down—they were promised their names would be said at the boundaries, every year, on the warden's rounds. It was the oldest law in Thornfell. Older than the crown." A pause. "Then the Regent erased the country, and the rounds stopped, and nobody has said those names in eleven years."

The dead hadn't moved. But the humming in the stones had deepened, and Felix understood, suddenly, what it was: not the stones at all. The stones were just anchors. It was the dead, humming. Hundreds of voices pitched below hearing, saying their own names over and over to themselves so they wouldn't lose them.

"They're going to let us pass if we pay," Felix said.

"They're going to *make* us pay," Vessa corrected. "There's a difference. And there's only one currency they take."

* * *

Felix walked the line of the company and told them the truth, because lying to frightened people on a mountainside seemed like the Regent's trade, not theirs.

"There are dead in the pass," they said. "Soldiers of the old kingdom. They won't hurt anyone—but they won't move until they're remembered. Until their names are spoken and kept. I can do that. It's my trade. But it will take all night, and we camp here, exposed, and anyone who wants to turn back should say so now."

Nobody turned back. That was the thing about the people who'd joined the trek since Salt Shrine—nobody among them had a home to return to. The dye-sisters, Maren and Odile, simply looked at each other and sat down and began unbuckling their packs. Old Col lowered himself onto a boulder with a grunt and said, "My brother marched with the Third Borderers. Died at Harrow Ford." He said it like a man laying a card on a table. "If you're keeping names, keeper, start with Tam."

The name landed in Felix's chest like a coal. Because that was the trap of it, the beautiful terrible trap: everyone in this company was carrying names. Everyone had someone the erasure had swallowed. The pass wasn't demanding payment from Felix alone. It was demanding it from all of them—and offering, in exchange, the one thing the Regent had made impossible.

Memory as toll. Memory as gift. On this mountain, the two were the same coin.

Wren appeared at Felix's elbow as the light went amber. She had her knife out and a length of antler tine, already whittled smooth.

"You'll need blanks," she said. "You can't carve fast enough on found bone. I've been cutting spares since the shrine." She held out a dozen pieces, pale and clean. "Don't argue. Your hands shake worse than mine when you're tired, and I've watched you hide it for two hundred miles."

Felix took the blanks. Their throat was doing something inconvenient. "Thank you, Wren."

"Don't thank me yet." She nodded up at the pass, where the dead stood motionless in the last of the sun, and her voice dropped. "How many names?"

Felix closed their eyes and listened the way they'd taught themselves to listen as a child in a house where listening was safer than speaking.

"Six hundred and forty," they said. "So far."

* * *

Night came down cold and enormous, stars in their thousands, and Felix set up at the foot of the fallen boundary stones with a fire, a blade, and a lifetime of small skill, and began.

It was work like nothing else in the world. To carry a name out of a dead man's mouth and into bone, you couldn't just hear it—you had to hold it whole, feel its shape the way a carver feels the shape waiting inside ivory, and then cut it true. Each name wanted different handling. Some came out clean as river stones. Others were cracked, half-eaten by eleven years of silence, and Felix had to sit with those, patient as a jeweler, coaxing the missing syllables back from the dark until the man or woman behind the name remembered themselves enough to hand it over complete.

Tam Col. Third Borderers. Harrow Ford. Felix carved it into the first blank while old Col watched, lips moving, and when Felix held the finished piece up to the firelight and read the name aloud, clear and formal, the way the old warden's rounds would have said it—Col broke. Just quietly, an old man weeping by a fire with forty strangers pretending not to notice, and the first soldier in the line above them turned his head, just slightly, like a man hearing music after a long deafness.

That was the rhythm of the night. Name by name. Bone by bone.

The company organized itself around Felix without being asked. Maren kept the fire fed. Odile sorted the finished pieces into rows on a spread blanket, each one face-up so the starlight caught the cuts. Wren carved blanks and sharpened the blade and, somewhere past midnight, began taking dictation—writing each name twice more, ink on paper, because as she put it, "Bone keeps, but paper travels." The refugees formed a quiet queue, each waiting their turn to give Felix a brother, a mother, a neighbor, a name they'd been carrying in secret for years with nowhere to put it down.

And Felix carved.

Hour three, and the cramp set into their knife hand, and they switched hands, because the dead didn't care which fingers held the blade.

Hour five, and the names started coming faster than the clients' requests—dead men stepping forward out of the ranks to offer their own names directly, since the living supply had gaps. A sergeant named Brackish Hale who'd held a bridge for nine hours and whose name no one living knew. Twin scouts, Ferren and Fallow, who'd frozen together on watch and been buried under one stone and therefore, they insisted, must share one piece of bone. Felix obliged. The knife sang.

Hour seven, and Felix stopped feeling their body entirely. This was the part no client ever saw, the private cost of the listening trade: the carver dissolving. With each name Felix gave away a little of the wall they'd built around themselves, and the dead poured through—not their memories, Felix couldn't hold those too, but their *weight*. Six hundred years of border wars settling onto one pair of shoulders, one pair of bleeding, bandaged hands. At some point Felix realized tears were running down their face and freezing in the wind and that they were still carving, that the carving had become less like work and more like breathing, and that they could not have stopped now to save their life.

Somewhere in that long dark hour, exhaustion transfigured itself. Felix had spent their whole life hiding—hiding the gift, hiding the hands, hiding behind artisan respectability in a town that burned anyway. Neutrality, Vessa called it. The illusion. But there was no neutrality in this firelight, no invisibility, no craft to hide behind. There was only a person with a knife and a pile of bone and six hundred and forty names, giving everything they had, and finding—to their astonishment—that they had more. The well kept refilling. The dead kept handing down buckets.

*Ritual*, Vessa murmured, near dawn. She'd been quiet for hours, watching. *This is what the rounds used to be. You're not paying a toll, Ravensworth. You're restoring a law.*

"Then let it be law again," Felix said, hoarse, and carved the next name.

* * *

Dawn came up grey-gold behind the eastern peaks, and Felix carved the last name with hands that no longer belonged to them, reading it aloud in a voice scraped down to the grain:

*Sella March, warden's daughter, fell at the Stone Gate, aged sixteen. Remembered.*

Silence.

Then, all along the pass, the dead bowed. Not dramatically—a slow, collective inclination, hundreds of heads, like wheat under wind. The humming in the boundary stones resolved, harmonized, and faded. And the soldiers of Thornfell sank back into their mountain the way frost leaves a window, unhurried, each one pausing a moment to look at the rows of bone on Odile's blanket—at their own names, kept, portable, alive—before they went down into the ground that had finally learned to say them.

The pass stood open.

Felix tried to stand and discovered their legs had opinions. Wren caught them under the arm—she was stronger than she looked, all wire—and lowered them back onto the fire-warmed stone.

"Sleep," she said. "We're not moving today. Look at them." She tipped her chin at the company, who were moving through the morning in a kind of hush, touching the bone-names on the blanket like relics, because that's what they were. "Half of them haven't slept either. They stayed up all night listening to you. All of us did." Her voice caught, very slightly, on something. "I wrote down every name. Six hundred and forty. I checked twice."

"You counted."

"Someone had to." Wren crouched, and for a long moment she studied Felix's face with that sharp, cataloguing gaze of hers—the guide's gaze, measuring a route. Except this time it wasn't the route she was measuring. It was Felix. Something moved behind her eyes, some conclusion almost reached and then deliberately set aside. "Whoever you were before Ashmoor," she said quietly, "you're not that person anymore. I hope you know that."

Felix was too exhausted to answer, and perhaps that was why they missed it—the flicker in her expression, the question she swallowed, the way her glance lingered a half-second too long on Felix's hands, as if checking them against a memory.

Because Felix, drifting down into sleep at the edge of the fire, had already noticed the other thing. The thing they'd been refusing to look at since midnight.

High above the pass, where the glacier clung to the peak like a pale hand, the ice had caught the dawn and was glowing. Not with sunlight. The sun came from the east. The light in the ice came from *beneath*, steady and blue-green and patient, and it had been brightening, degree by degree, all through the long night of the names—as though something vast under the mountain had been watching Felix work.

As though something had been counting, too. And had found the number to its liking.

In the satchel, Vessa's bones went cold.

"That," the smuggler-queen whispered, "is not one of ours."
