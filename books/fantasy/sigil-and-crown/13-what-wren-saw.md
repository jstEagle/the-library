## 13. What Wren Saw

Wren had learned to read snow the way her mother read maps: by what it refused to say.

A clean slope lied. A slope with a faint seam of wind-packed crust told the truth—that something had passed beneath it, that the mountain was holding its breath over a hollow. So when the company made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder and Wren went out to check the drifts for morning water-melt, she didn't think twice about following the seam where it curved away from camp, down into a fold of rock no one had bothered to scout.

That was how she found the cairn.

It wasn't much. A ring of stones the size of two fists, set with a precision that had nothing to do with trail-marking. Inside the ring, half-buried, lay a slab of bone—sheep femur, old, its surface crowded with carved sigils worn shallow by weather. And around it, pressed into the snow like a signature nobody wanted to claim, were boot prints. Felix's boots. She knew the uneven left heel; she'd watched it climb three passes.

The strange thing was the direction they came from. Not from camp. From *up*—from the high shelf above the fold, where nothing lived and nothing led, where the map Vessa's ghost muttered about at night said only one word: *palace road.*

Wren crouched. The sigils on the bone weren't the ones Felix carved for clients or for the dead of the pass. These were older. Formal. A border of interlocking keys ran around the edge, and in the center, cut deep enough to have been done slowly, deliberately, was a crest she'd seen exactly once before—on the broken seal of a tax ledger in a burned village, the Regent's mark, the crowned thorn.

Someone had tried to gouge it out. The cuts were frantic, deep as knife-work could go, but bone remembers. Under the right light—and the light under this granite shoulder was blue and honest—the crown still showed through, like a scar under skin.

Wren sat back on her heels and let the cold soak through her trousers while her mind did the thing it did on trails: laid out the route, checked it against everything she knew, and arrived somewhere she did not want to be.

Felix Ravensworth. Bone-carver. Ashmoor artisan. Fugitive who flinched from soldiers not like a smuggler flinches—calculating—but like someone who'd been *taught* to flinch, early, by experts. Someone who walked into the Salt Shrine and bargained with a river god in a voice that expected to be obeyed. Someone whose hands, when they thought no one watched, held themselves like a person raised to hold a scepter instead of a carving knife.

And someone who, every night since the Pass of Names, had gone off alone toward the high shelf. Toward the palace road.

Wren picked up the bone slab. It was lighter than it should have been, the way things get when they've been carried too long. She turned it over. On the back, in a different hand—a child's hand, round and careful—someone had scratched letters into the surface before the sigils covered them:

*RAVENSWORTH IS NOT MY NAME.*

* * *

She meant to confront them privately. That was the plan, anyway, formed in the hot little furnace of her anger as she climbed back toward camp: find Felix alone, put the bone in their hands, watch their face do whatever it did, and then decide whether she'd spent nine days leading refugees through frozen hell for a liar.

But the mountain had other plans, because the mountain always did.

The storm came down the western col an hour past dusk, fast and white and screaming, the kind of blow that erased trails and tents and judgment all at once. Wren heard it before anyone—the particular hush in the wind, the smell of iron—and got the company moving to the ice caves below the shelf just as the first gust flattened the cookfire. By full dark, forty-odd people were packed into a cavern mouth with their backs to the stone, watching the world outside turn into a single moving wall of white.

And Felix was missing.

"They went up," Marta the weaver said, jerking her chin at the shelf. "Before the storm. Said they'd be back by dark." Her voice had the flat calm of someone who had already decided the answer and was waiting for everyone else to catch up. "Nobody goes up there."

"Where does it go?" Wren asked, though she already knew.

"Nowhere," Marta said. "It's the old palace road. Hasn't been used since the kingdom died. Nothing up there but wind and—" she stopped, and her face closed, and Wren understood that Marta knew something too, or suspected it, and had chosen the same neutrality Felix had once chosen in Ashmoor. The complicity of staying quiet.

Wren looked at the wall of snow. Somewhere up there, in a whiteout on a dead road, was the person who had paid a river god the memory of their mother's face so the company could cross. Who had carved four hundred names into bone through a freezing night until their fingers bled. Who might also have been born behind palace walls, wearing a crown on their cradle-clothes.

Both things were true. That was the part that made her furious—not the lie itself, but that both things could be true at once.

She clipped on her rope, took a lantern, and went up.

* * *

The storm on the shelf was a living thing. It took the lantern light and shredded it, gave her ten feet of visibility and then five, then three. Wren had grown up in these mountains; she knew how to walk blind—weight low, pole forward, counting steps against the wind's rhythm. But the shelf was featureless, and the palace road, if it still existed under the snow, offered no shelter, and after twenty minutes she had to admit she was searching, not traveling.

Then the wind dropped. Not gradually—all at once, like a door closing, and the silence rang in her ears. In the sudden stillness she saw the light ahead: a soft gold glow, wrong for a lantern, steady as a hearth.

She followed it around a spur of black rock and found the shrine.

Not a shrine—a ruin. The bones of some old waystation, walls half-collapsed, roof open to the sky, and in its center a fire burning in a ring of stones without smoke, without crackle, flames standing perfectly straight in air that should have been howling. Above the fire, suspended in nothing, hung a dozen carved bones, turning slowly, each one lit from within by sigils glowing the color of honey.

And kneeling among them, bareheaded, hood thrown back, snow gathering on their shoulders like they'd stopped caring about their own body, was Felix.

They were talking. Not to Wren—to the bones. To the glow. Their voice came thin across the ruined floor, and Wren, trained by years of listening for travelers in bad weather, caught every word.

"—I know. I know you're awake now. I felt you in the pass, all of you, I *carved* you awake, didn't I?" A sound that was almost a laugh. "You want me to come home. You've wanted that since Ashmoor. Since before Ashmoor. Well, here I am. Look at me. Look at what's left."

The bones turned. The light brightened.

"No," Felix said, sharp as a blade coming out. "Don't call me that. I gave it up. I burned it. I stood in front of the blood rite with the knife in my hand and I *refused*, and when they erased me I thanked them for it. Whatever I was before, that child is dead, and you don't get to dig them up just because the kingdom finally fell far enough for you to wake—"

The light flared. Every sigil blazed. And in the glare, for half a heartbeat, Wren saw what the gods saw: not a bone-carver in a patched coat, but a figure in white and gold, young, crowned, standing in a hall she'd never seen while robed figures chanted words that peeled a name out of the world like bark off a tree—

Then it was gone. The light settled. Felix's shoulders shook once, twice.

"I'm not going back to being that," they said quietly. "Whatever happens at the Threshold. Find another doorkeeper."

Wren's boot scraped stone.

Felix went rigid. Then, without turning: "How long?"

"Long enough." Wren stepped into the ruin's shelter, and the wind found her immediately, screaming past the broken walls, but inside the ring of stones the air stayed warm and still, which was its own kind of answer about what kind of power Felix was carrying. She pulled the bone slab from her coat and held it out. "This was buried at the cairn below the shelf. Your maker's mark on the front. This on the back."

Felix looked at it the way a man looks at a snake he knows has already bitten him. They didn't take it.

"You went through my things," they said.

"You left your things in a hole in the mountain on a road that leads to the palace. That's not hiding, that's leaving a door open." Wren's voice cracked higher than she wanted. Nine days of cold and fear and watching Felix bleed for strangers came up in her all at once. "*Ravensworth is not my name.* Whose is it? What are you? Because I've been telling these people—Marta, the Aldous family, that kid Tam with the burned hands—I've been telling them you're one of us. That you're running from the Regent same as them. And if you're his *brother*—his sister—his *whatever you are*—"

"Sibling," Felix said. "I was his younger sibling. Before the erasure, there wasn't a word for what I chose to be, so I chose one after." They finally looked up, and their eyes in the firelight were terrible and tired. "Yes, Wren. I was born in the palace. Third child of the old king. I had a name with eleven syllables and a household of servants and a place in the succession, and on my sixteenth birthday they brought me into the rite-chamber to seal my blood into the crown's wards, because that's what royal blood is *for*—fuel. The kingdom ate its own children to keep its borders shut and its enemies out, and I was supposed to kneel and let it drink."

The fire popped—or didn't pop; the flames just shifted, and the sound happened somewhere else.

"And I said no." Felix's voice went very level, the voice of someone reciting something they'd rehearsed in the dark for thirty years. "Do you understand what that means? Everyone says no, at first. Everyone. The priests wait for it. There's a whole procedure. They explain your duty, they show you the wards failing, they bring in your mother to weep at you. My mother knelt on the stone and begged me, and I loved her more than anything alive, and I still said no. So they did the erasure. Unmade my name from every record, every memory they could reach with the crown's magic. My mother forgot my face within the hour. My brother—" a pause, a hairline fracture in the level voice, "—my brother signed the order himself. He was nineteen. He thought he was saving the kingdom. Maybe he was. And I walked out of the palace with nothing, not even a self, and I built one. Bone-carver. Ashmoor. Neutral. Invisible. Thirty years of being no one, and it *worked*, Wren, it worked right up until the gods woke up and remembered whose hands had refused them."

Wren stood very still. The bone slab was heavy in her hand.

"So the Regent hunting you—"

"He doesn't know what I am. If he knew, he wouldn't send inquisitors; he'd send an army, because I'm the heir he erased, and worse than that, I'm the proof the crown's magic can be *refused*." Felix laughed, and it was the worst sound Wren had ever heard from them. "He thinks he's chasing a god-speaker. He's chasing his own conscience with a knife."

"That's not the point." Wren heard her own voice go hard, the guide's voice, the one that kept clients alive. "The point is you let us believe. All of us. You watched us follow you and you never said—"

"What would you have done?" Felix rose, and the hanging bones stirred around them like startled birds. "If I'd told you on the mountain trail, day one? 'Come with me, I'm the Regent's erased sibling, the gods are calling me home'? Would you have taken passage money and walked away? Would Marta? Would Tam?" They spread their hands. "Or would you have done exactly what you're doing now—stood in a storm and demanded answers—except with forty terrified people watching, and half of them deciding I was the Regent's spy, and the other half deciding I was their new king?"

"I would have decided!" Wren shouted, and the word tore something loose in her chest. "That's the whole point! You don't get to decide for us! You don't get to carry our trust like it's toll money you paid fair and square! We followed you because we thought you were *us*!"

"I am us," Felix said. "That's what none of you understand. I've been us for thirty years. The palace child died in that rite-chamber. What's left is a carver who hears the dead and can't stop helping them, even when helping gets people killed." Their voice dropped. "Even when it got Ashmoor killed."

The wind screamed. Neither of them moved.

Wren looked down at the bone in her hand. At the gouged-out crown showing through like a scar. At the child's careful letters underneath. *Ravensworth is not my name.* Written by a sixteen-year-old who had just lost everything and was trying, with a stolen stylus, to build a new self out of two words.

Identity as chosen, not given. Her mother used to say that about the passes: *the mountain doesn't care what the king calls the road. It cares whether you can walk it.*

"You should have told me," Wren said. Quieter now. "Not them. Me. I found the cairn because I read the snow, Felix. I'm going to find everything else too. You need someone beside you who knows where the bodies are buried. Literally, apparently."

Felix blinked. Something in their face came unclenched, fractionally, like a fist remembering it could open.

"You're not leaving?"

"Oh, I'm furious," Wren said. "I'm going to be furious for approximately the entire rest of my life. But the storm's up, the company's in the caves, and the only person who can get us to the Threshold is a liar with royal blood and a bag of haunted fingerbones." She shoved the bone slab at them. "Take your scar. And Felix—"

"Yes?"

"When this is over, whatever happens at the Threshold—you tell them the truth yourself. All of them. Not me. Them. That's the price of me staying."

For a long moment Felix just looked at her, and the firelight moved on their face, and Wren thought she saw, underneath thirty years of practiced blankness, something young and relieved and terrified—the face of someone who had been carrying a secret so long they'd forgotten it was heavy until someone offered to help lift it.

"All right," Felix said. "That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's a bargain. Fairness is for kingdoms that exist."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped them. They tucked the bone slab inside their coat, over their heart, where the satchel of Vessa's fingerbones hung, and for a moment the two relics rested together—the refused crown and the queen who wouldn't surrender her name—and both glowed, softly, like they recognized each other.

Then the fire guttered.

Not naturally. It bent sideways, all at once, toward the north wall, and the hanging bones swung on invisible strings, and every sigil in the ruin shifted from honey-gold to a red so deep it was nearly black. Felix's head snapped up. Wren felt it too, then—a pressure in her ears, a taste like coins, the specific wrongness she'd learned at the Salt Shrine when the river god leaned close.

"What is it?" she whispered.

Felix's face had gone gray. "Vessa," they said. "Her bones are singing." They fumbled the satchel open, and the fingerbones inside were rattling against each other, chattering like teeth, and when Felix lifted them out they arranged themselves in the air—in midair, unsupported—into a shape Wren had never seen but understood instantly, because her mother had drawn it once, drunk, crying, pointing at a ridge line: the shape of the Threshold valley.

And across it, Vessa's ghostly voice, thin and urgent as wind through a keyhole, speaking words Wren could barely hear:

"*They know the route. They're ahead of you. Boy-king's soldiers at the valley mouth by dawn—and oh, darling, oh, you fool—they aren't coming for the refugees.*"

The bones collapsed. The fire went out.

In the sudden dark, Felix's voice came steady and cold, the palace voice, the one they must have sworn never to use again:

"Wake the company. We walk tonight."
