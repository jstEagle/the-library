## 14. The God Beneath the Ice

The pass narrowed to a throat of grey stone two days after the dead let us by, and the cold came down like a verdict.

Felix felt it first in the bones they carried—the satchel of Vessa Marrow's fingerbones going stiff against their hip, rattling faintly whenever the wind gusted, as if she were shivering inside her leather house. Then Wren stopped walking, planted her boots in the scree, and pointed up.

"There," she said. "That's where we turn."

Above them the mountain rose in terraces of ice, blue-white and ancient, and set into the face of it like a pupil in an eye was a cave mouth. Not carved. Not natural either. It simply *was*, the way a scar is—something the mountain had grown around rather than healed.

"That's not on any map," Felix said.

"I know." Wren pulled her hood back. Frost had gotten into her lashes; she looked older than seventeen, suddenly, the way people do when they're about to do something stupid and brave. "My mother told me never to go near it. Which means it matters. Everything my mother told me not to go near turned out to matter."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She sounds dead," Wren said flatly, "like most everyone's mother these days. Are we going or not?"

Vessa stirred against Felix's hip. The dead smuggler-queen had been quiet since the Pass of Names—quiet in the way of someone thinking hard—and now her voice came up through the bone with an edge Felix hadn't heard from her before.

*Turn around, carver.*

Felix went still. In three weeks of travel together, Vessa had never once said *turn around*. She was the one who'd dragged them out of Ashmoor burning, who'd mapped roads that no longer existed out of pure spite, who'd called every obstacle between here and the Threshold "a door with bad manners."

"You want us to go back?" Felix murmured, low enough that only the wind could hear.

*I want you to hear me before you're standing in front of it.* A pause. The bones ticked against each other. *There are gods and there are gods, carver. The ones waking in the valleys—they're young. Hundred-year sleepers, nothing more. They forgot themselves and now they remember, and mostly what they want is to be sung to. But that one—"*

The wind changed. It came down off the glacier in a single long exhalation, and it smelled of nothing at all—not snow, not stone, not the iron tang of high altitude. Nothing. As if the air itself had been scraped clean.

"—that one never slept," Vessa finished. "It closed its eyes and waited. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Wren was watching Felix talk to empty air with the expression she always wore during these conversations: impatience braided with hunger. She couldn't hear Vessa. Almost nobody could. But she could read the shape of it.

"The old one's in there," she said. "Isn't it."

"It might be."

"And it knows the way to the Threshold."

"Everything old knows the way somewhere," Felix said. "The question is what it charges for directions."

They stood in the wind a moment longer. Behind them, strung out along the trail like beads on a fraying cord, the company waited—forty-one souls now, since the Salt Shrine, since word had spread through the burned villages that the bone-carver was walking toward a door out of Thornfell. Forty-one people with frostbitten feet and hollow cheeks and the particular silence of those who have already lost everything except the decision to keep moving. Old Tam with his bad hip. The sisters Ferro, who hadn't spoken since their village. A boy named Pell who collected stones shaped like teeth.

Forty-one reasons to take any shortcut offered.

"All right," Felix said. "But we go carefully, and nobody promises anything."

*Carver,* Vessa said, and for once her voice wasn't sharp. It was almost gentle, which frightened Felix more than shouting would have. *When we get in there, whatever it offers you—and it will offer, it has been waiting a century to offer—you say nothing. You hear me? Nothing. Let me do the talking.*

"You can't talk. You're fingerbones."

*I'm very persuasive fingerbones.*

---

The cave swallowed sound the way the sea swallows light.

Inside, the ice thickened until it wasn't ice anymore but something older—glassy, faintly green, shot through with veins of frozen air like trapped breath. Their lantern made a small yellow island in all that blue-green dark, and Felix held it high and tried not to think about how far underground they were, how much mountain pressed down overhead, how the company waited outside in the wind trusting a bone-carver and a teenage guide and a bag of a dead woman's fingers.

Wren walked close. Too close for dignity, exactly right for safety.

"How deep does it go?" Felix asked.

"My mother said the shrine-priests used to bring offerings up here. Before the Silence." Wren's voice echoed strangely, coming back flattened, as if the cave were eating the edges off it. "Grain. Wool. Once a year. And they never went past the third turning. They left the offering and walked backward out, so they wouldn't see anything follow them."

"Cheerful folk, your shrine-priests."

"They survived a hundred years in the mountains. I'd call that cheerful enough."

They passed the third turning. Felix counted it without meaning to—a bend in the passage where the walls widened into a chamber, where the floor still bore a ring of flat stones worn smooth by centuries of kneeling. Empty now. Whatever grain and wool had been laid here a hundred years ago had long since become part of the mountain.

And past the third turning, the voice began.

It didn't come through the ears. Felix had spent a lifetime hearing the dead speak through bone and blood and the thin membrane between worlds, and this was not that. This came up through the soles of the boots, through the stone, through the marrow itself. When it spoke, Felix's own skeleton seemed to resonate, a tuning fork struck by something vast.

***CARVER.***

Not a question. An identification. The word rolled through Felix's chest and sat there humming.

"Yeah," Felix said aloud, because it seemed rude not to. "That's me."

Beside them, Wren had gone rigid. She couldn't hear it—not the words—but she could feel it; anyone could feel it; Felix suspected the refugees half a mile back down the trail could feel it. Her hand found Felix's sleeve and gripped.

***YOU BRING BONES THAT REMEMBER ROADS,*** the god said. ***AND A CHILD OF THE SERVING LINE. AND YOURSELF. THREE GIFTS WALK INTO MY HOUSE UNINVITED.***

"We weren't sure you were home," Felix said, and heard Vessa make a sound in the satchel that was either horror or laughter.

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere above them, deep in the glacier, something shifted—a groan of stressed ice, long and slow, like a sleeper rolling over.

***I HAVE BEEN HOME A LONG TIME,*** the god said. ***THAT IS THE TROUBLE WITH BEING ELDEST. EVERYONE ELSE GETS TO SLEEP. SOMEONE MUST STAY AWAKE AND KEEP THE DOORS COUNTED.***

"Doors," Felix said carefully. "You know about doors."

***I KNOW ABOUT THE THRESHOLD.*** The hum in Felix's bones deepened. ***I HELD ONE END OF IT WHEN THE WORLD WAS YOUNGER AND THE COUNTRY HAD NOT YET LEARNED TO DIE. I KNOW THE SHORT WAY, CARVER. THE WAY THAT DOES NOT WIND THROUGH VALLEYS AND SHRINES AND PASSES FULL OF HUNGRY DEAD. THE WAY THAT ARRIVES TOMORROW INSTEAD OF NEXT MONTH.***

Felix's heart did something complicated. Next month. That was the honest arithmetic they'd been carrying for weeks—next month of cold rations, next month of the Regent's riders sweeping the low country, next month of forty-one people getting thinner and more desperate while winter closed its fist. Vance was out there somewhere, turned coat or not yet, hunting or warning, and every day of delay was a day the Regent's noose drew tighter.

"What's the price?" Felix asked. Because there was always a price. The river god at the salt shrine had taught them that much, had taken the memory of their mother's face and given safe passage, and Felix had learned to stop pretending otherwise. Power collects. Always.

***THE CHILD,*** said the god beneath the ice.

The word fell into the chamber and lay there.

***THE GIRL WHO GUIDES YOU. HER LINE SERVED ME BEFORE THORNFELL HAD A NAME. HER MOTHER'S MOTHER'S MOTHER KNELT AT MY THIRD TURNING AND LEFT GRAIN AND WOOL AND, ONCE, IN A HARD YEAR, SOMETHING MORE. SHE IS OWED TO ME. DEBTS OUTLIVE KINGDOMS, CARVER. I HAVE ONLY BEEN WAITING FOR HER TO WALK UP MY OWN MOUNTAIN AND DELIVER HERSELF.***

"No," Wren said.

She said it before Felix could. Her voice cracked on the second syllable but she got the whole word out, both halves of it, standing in the green-dark with her chin up and her fists at her sides.

"I'm not owed to anybody. My mother got out. That's what getting out *means*—the debt dies with the leaving. That's the whole point of doors." Her breath fogged. "You can't collect on someone who already ran."

***CHILD,*** the god said, and there was something in the voice now that Felix recognized with a lurch—something almost fond, almost weary, the tone of an ancient thing that had heard every argument mortals ever invented and found most of them charming. ***I HAVE EATEN KINGS WHO SAID WHAT YOU JUST SAID.***

"Then eat me too," Wren said, "because I'm not walking into your ice."

"Wren." Felix's voice came out steadier than they felt. "Behind me. Now."

She didn't move. Felix loved her for it and was terrified of her for it, both at once, the way you love and fear a lit fuse.

***THERE,*** the god said softly. ***YOU SEE? SHE HAS COURAGE. COURAGE KEEPS WELL IN THE ICE. AND CONSIDER WHAT I OFFER IN EXCHANGE, CARVER. FORTY-ONE SOULS DELIVERED FROM THE REGENT'S HANDS BY TOMORROW'S DUSK. NO MORE SHRINES. NO MORE TOLLS. NO MORE NAMES CARVED FOR THE DEAD BY LANTERN-LIGHT WHILE THE LIVING FREEZE. ONE CHILD AGAINST ALL OF THEM. EVEN SHE, BRAVE AS SHE IS, WOULD CALL THAT ARITHMETIC MERCIFUL.***

And the terrible thing—the thing Felix would carry for the rest of whatever life remained to them—was that for one full breath, the arithmetic *was* merciful. One life weighed against forty-one. One girl who had volunteered onto this road, who knew the risks, who had said herself that mountains kill guides all the time. The scales barely tipped. Any reasonable person would tip them.

Vessa moved in the satchel. Felix felt her press up against the leather, felt the heat of her attention even though she was ten years dead and mostly fingerbones.

*Carver,* she said quietly. *Tell him about doors.*

Felix blinked. *What?*

*Tell him. He said he keeps count of doors. So tell him what a door is for.*

Felix took a breath. The cold air tasted of nothing, of scraped-clean nothing, and in that taste they found their footing.

"You said you hold one end of the Threshold," Felix said. "That you've kept the doors counted since the world was young."

***I HAVE.***

"Then you know what a door is. A door isn't a wall with a hole in it. A door is a promise. It says: anyone may pass who comes in good faith. Not the ones who pay most. Not the ones who are useful. Anyone. That's the whole covenant. That's why the Threshold opens at all—it's the last promise Thornfell ever made, and it hasn't been broken yet." Felix lifted the lantern higher. The green ice drank the light and gave back nothing. "If I hand you Wren—if I buy my forty-one with someone else's blood—then I'm not opening a door. I'm building a toll gate. And toll gates don't lead anywhere worth going."

Silence. Long enough that Felix heard their own heartbeat, heard Wren's ragged breathing, heard the glacier groan somewhere far above like a ship in weather.

***SENTIMENT,*** the god said at last. ***THE OLDEST COINAGE. IT SPENDS POORLY.***

"Maybe. But here's the other thing about doors." Felix set the lantern down on the worn stone of the offering ring, deliberately, slowly, the way you set down a weapon you intend to pick back up. "A door also says *no*. That's the part everyone forgets. A door that lets everything through isn't a door, it's a wound. So here's my answer, eldest one, keeper of counts: no. No trade. No child. No shortcut. We'll take the long way, the cold way, the way with hungry dead in it, because it's the way that doesn't cost anyone their self. And if that makes the arithmetic cruel—" Felix met the darkness where the god's attention pooled, unblinking, unblinking, "*then the arithmetic can learn to live with cruelty, same as the rest of us."

The cold deepened. Not gradually—all at once, like stepping through a wall. Wren gasped. The lantern flame shrank to a blue bead. And the god beneath the ice said, in a voice that shook grit from the ceiling:

***THEN THE PASS CLOSES BEHIND YOU, CARVER. NO ONE DESCENDS MY MOUNTAIN BY THIS ROAD AGAIN. LET US SEE HOW YOUR PROMISE WEATHERS WINTER.***

"Fine," Felix said.

***FINE?***

"You heard me. Close it. Seal the whole pass top to bottom. Because I'll tell you what I see, keeper of counts—I see a mountain that's been alone so long it's forgotten the difference between keeping a door and being a cage. And I'd rather bury the road than leave it open for the Regent's riders to follow us through." Felix picked the lantern back up. "Seal it. Please. With everything you have."

Another silence. Shorter. Something in it had changed register—curiosity, maybe, the first non-hungry emotion the god had shown.

***YOU ASK ME TO BURY YOUR OWN ESCAPE ROUTE.***

"I ask you to bury *everyone's* escape route. Including theirs." Felix tipped their head toward the trail, toward the forty-one waiting in the wind. "That's what a refusal costs, if you mean it. You don't get to decline a bargain and keep the exit."

For a moment—just a moment—Felix thought they felt it: something enormous turning over in the dark of the glacier, considering, the way a mountain considers weather. Then the god laughed. It was not a kind sound. It was not entirely unkind, either. It was the sound of something ancient discovering it could still be surprised.

***GO, THEN,*** it said. ***TAKE THE LONG WAY. AND CARVER—WHEN YOU STAND BEFORE THE THRESHOLD, AND IT ASKS ITS PRICE, AND IT WILL—REMEMBER THAT YOU KNEW HOW TO SAY NO ONCE.***

The ice began to sing.

It started as a vibration underfoot, then a crack like the world's largest knuckle popping, then a roar. Above them, miles of snowfield let go all at once. Felix grabbed Wren's wrist and ran, and behind them the passage filled with white thunder, the avalanche pouring down the mountainside in a river of powder and shattered blue glass, sealing the throat of the pass, burying the third turning, the offering ring, the cave mouth, the whole road under a hundred feet of fresh-frozen silence.

---

They dug themselves out of the drifts an hour later, coughing, half-buried, alive.

Below them on the trail, the company had watched the whole face of the mountain come down. They stood in a silent row, forty-one figures dusted white, staring up at the place where the pass had been. Old Tam took off his hat. Nobody asked questions. People who had lost a country to erasure knew what it looked like when a road simply stopped existing.

Wren spat snow and glared at Felix with an expression caught between fury and something else, something warmer that she was working hard to disguise.

"You buried our way out," she said.

"I buried *their* way in." Felix brushed ice from the satchel. Inside, Vessa's bones had gone quiet again—that thinking quiet, that listening quiet. "The Regent's riders won't come over that. Nobody will. We just became the only people in Thornfell who know the rest of the road."

"And the rest of the road is?"

Felix looked north, where the pass descended into a valley of grey rock and early dusk, toward country none of the maps remembered. Somewhere past it, the meadow. Somewhere past that, the door—or the place where a door should be, if doors still kept their promises.

"Longer," Felix said. "Colder. And completely ours."

Then, from the ridge above the new-made snowfield, a whistle cut the wind—two notes, sharp, deliberate. A signal. Felix knew it from a lifetime ago, from another life, from a palace they did not speak of.

Someone was up there. Someone who shouldn't be.

And they were waving.
