## 15. Inquisitor's Choice

The summons came at dawn, and it came wrong.

Vance had received a hundred orders from the Citadel in her eleven years of service, and every one of them had arrived the same way: sealed in grey wax, carried by a courier who would not meet her eyes, worded with the cold courtesy of an institution that did not apologize because it never needed to. This one was different. It was delivered by two men on black horses who wore no livery at all, and it was not signed by the Regent's secretary.

It was signed by the Regent himself.

*Inquisitor Sera Vance is relieved of her commission effective immediately. She is to surrender her seal, her warrant, and her person to the bearers of this letter for transport to the capital.*

Her person. Not her presence. Institutions asked for your presence when they wanted to question you. They asked for your *person* when they had already decided what you were.

The taller of the two men smiled at her from the doorway of the waystation room she'd rented in Lastbridge, and his smile was the worst part—not cruel, not gloating, just patient, the smile of a man who had done this forty times and been disappointed only twice. "No trouble, madam. The Lord Regent remembers your service fondly."

"I'm sure he does," Vance said. She was still in her shift, her inquisitor's coat hanging on the back of the chair where anyone could see it, her seal sitting on the table between them like a piece of evidence already entered into the record. "Give me a moment to dress."

"Of course." He didn't move out of the doorway. His companion drifted, casually, toward the window that overlooked the stable yard.

Vance dressed slowly, thinking. Her false report had gone south six weeks ago—the one claiming the god-speaker was dead, burned in the Ashmoor fire, bones unidentifiable. She'd written it in her own hand, sealed it with her own wax, and sent it with a courier she'd paid triple to ride through the night. It had bought Felix time. It had bought the refugees time. She'd told herself it was a calculated risk, a professional judgment: that a live god-speaker running loose through mountains full of waking gods was worth more to Thornfell—what was left of Thornfell—than whatever the Regent intended to do with one in a cage.

She'd known, even as she wrote it, that if the lie unraveled, it would unravel her with it.

Something had unraveled it. A deserter, maybe. One of the refugees she'd let slip through the checkpoint at Grey Ford. Or the Regent had simply stopped believing convenient reports, which was the occupational hazard of serving a man who'd built a kingdom on the premise that nothing inconvenient was true.

"You'll want your coat," the tall man said pleasantly. "It's cold in the capital this time of year."

"It's cold everywhere," Vance said, and put it on.

* * *

They made it four miles.

She'd counted on that much. The road east out of Lastbridge ran along the river for three miles of flat, open ground where any rider could see any other rider for half a mile in either direction, and then it climbed into the beech woods above the ford, where the trees closed in and the road narrowed and the sound of the water covered everything. She'd ridden this route a dozen times. She knew exactly where the sightlines died.

She also knew that executioners were not escorts, whatever letter they carried. Men who came for your *person* did not ride in formation. They rode close behind you, one on each side, slightly back, hands loose near their belts, and they talked to fill the silence because silence made them think about what they were going to do at the end of the road.

"So," the tall one said—he'd introduced himself as Master Corren, which meant nothing, since men like him went by whatever name the work required—"the god-speaker. You actually met them?"

"Once," Vance said. "Briefly."

"And? What are we chasing? Man, woman?"

"A bone-carver," Vance said. "Small. Quiet. The kind of person you pass in a market and forget before you reach the next stall."

Corren laughed. "That's what they said about the Salt King, and the Salt King drowned a garrison."

"The Salt King had an army. This one has a satchel."

"Mm." Corren's horse dropped back half a step, putting him squarely in her blind spot, and she felt rather than saw his hand leave his reins. "You know what I heard? I heard the god-speaker's got a whole column of refugees up there now. Two hundred souls, maybe more, walking single file through passes that aren't on any map. And I heard somebody in the Citadel falsified a report saying they were dead." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way a man talks about weather right up until he isn't. "Funny thing, reports. You can always tell the false ones. They're too clean. Real reports have doubts in them. Yours didn't have a single doubt, Inquisitor. Not one."

The trees closed overhead. The river roared below, white over the shallows of the ford.

"In my experience," Corren said, "a report without doubts means the writer wasn't looking. And a writer who wasn't looking—" 

Vance wheeled her horse off the road.

She'd planned it in the waystation while she dressed, in the flat, cold part of her mind that had never once failed her: not escape, because there was no escaping two riders once they were alerted, but delay, distance, and terrain. She drove her heels in and plunged down the bank toward the ford, and behind her she heard Corren shout and the crash of hooves in brush, and then she was in the water, thigh-deep, the current shoving at her horse's flanks, the far bank a wall of beech trunks rising into shadow.

A crossbow bolt took the water a foot to her left with a sound like a whip.

The second one hit her horse.

She felt it go—a shudder through the animal's whole body, a scream that was almost human—and then the forelegs folded and the river took them both, and Vance went under into cold so complete it erased thought. She came up gasping twenty feet downstream, clawed at a root, missed, caught it on the second grab, and hauled herself against the current toward the bank while bolts slapped the water around her and the world narrowed to the width of her own arms.

She got into the trees. She ran. Behind her, voices—one angry, one giving orders—and she ran until the voices were gone and the light was failing and her lungs were full of knives, and then she walked, and then she climbed, because there was only one direction left in all of Thornfell that was not the Regent's, and it was up.

* * *

She found the camp on the third night, and it found her first.

She'd followed the refugees' trail the way she'd been trained to follow anything: water sources, dung, cut firewood, the worn places where two hundred feet had passed in single file. But somewhere past the second ridge the trail had begun to change. Cut wood became broken wood—snapped, not sawn, as if the forest itself had handed it over. Streams they crossed ran briefly backward. And on the morning of the third day she'd crested a granite shoulder and seen, in the valley below, a line of people moving through the snow like a thread of smoke, and above them, riding the wind off the peaks, a sound she had no training for: a low, vast humming, like a struck bell that never finished ringing.

The gods. Close enough now to hear without trying.

She came down into the camp at dusk, walking openly, hands visible, no warrant and no weapon except the knife she'd kept because a woman alone in these mountains without a knife was simply dead. The sentries saw her a hundred yards out—young ones, hollow-cheeked, carrying farm tools sharpened into weapons—and by the time she reached the fire line there were thirty of them around her, and none of them looked afraid, which frightened her more than hatred would have.

"I'm looking for the god-speaker," she said.

A murmur went through the ring of faces. Someone spat. An old man with a carpenter's forearms raised a maul, and Vance stood very still and did not reach for her knife, because reaching for it would have ended the conversation, and she had come a very long way to have this conversation.

"Then you've wasted a walk," the old man said. "There's nothing here for the Regent's dogs."

"I'm not the Regent's anymore." She opened her coat, slowly, and turned so they could see her side, where the crossbow bolt had torn through fabric and flesh three days ago, bound now with strips of shirt gone brown and stiff. "This is what the Regent gives his own people now. I falsified a report to buy you time. Six weeks ago. Ask your guide—the girl, Wren—whether the checkpoint at Grey Ford opened or not."

Silence. Then a voice from the edge of the firelight, young and hard and unmistakably suspicious: "I remember Grey Ford. The gates were open. Nobody knew why." Wren pushed through the crowd, sixteen years old and built like a mountain goat, a bone-handled knife at her belt and frost in her lashes. She looked Vance up and down with the flat appraisal of someone who had learned young that authority was a costume anyone could wear. "You're the inquisitor. The one from the shrine at Salt Hollow. You questioned the ferryman about us."

"I did."

"He lied to you."

"He did," Vance said. "And I wrote down that he hadn't."

Wren's eyes narrowed. For a long moment the only sound was the fire and, beneath it, faint and constant, the hum from the peaks. Then Wren jerked her chin toward the largest tent at the camp's center. "Felix will want to see you. Probably to decide whether to kill you." A pause. "Don't make it easy for them. They hate that part."

* * *

Felix Ravensworth sat cross-legged by a brazier inside the tent, carving.

That was the first thing. Not a warlord, not a prophet, not the weapon the Regent imagined—just a small, spare figure with ink-stained fingers and a knife no bigger than a paring blade, working a curl of bone into something that might have been a bird. A leather satchel lay open beside them, and from it, faintly, came a voice like dry leaves: *"Well, well. The law walks in on its own legs. Vessa Marrow owes someone a drink."*

"Inquisitor," Felix said, without looking up. Their voice was quiet, level, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Or former Inquisitor, I suppose. Word travels strangely up here, but it travels."

"Sera Vance." She stayed standing. It seemed important not to sit, not to accept hospitality she hadn't earned. "Formerly of the Citadel. Currently of nowhere."

"That's a start." Felix set down the knife and looked up, and their eyes were the second thing—grey and steady and older than their face, the eyes of someone who had spent years listening to the dead and had learned their patience. "Grey Ford was you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Vance had rehearsed answers on the climb. Professional judgment. Strategic calculation. The value of a live asset versus a caged one. All of them true, and all of them lies, and she found she couldn't say any of them to this face.

"Because I watched a drowned child walk out of a river," she said. "At Millbrook, at the start of all this. I held the autopsy record in one hand and the living child in the other, and the child looked at me—" She stopped. Started again. "Everyone thinks the gods coming back is about power. The Regent thinks it's about power. I thought it was about power; it's why they hired me. But it isn't. It's about the fact that things that were buried are standing up and asking to be counted. And I have spent eleven years burying things for a man who wants the counting to stop." Her voice held. She was proud of that. "So. Grey Ford. And a false report. And now he's sent executioners after me, and after you, and I've come to tell you how he'll do it, because I know his methods better than anyone alive."

Felix studied her for a long moment. From the satchel, the dry-leaf voice snorted. *"Ask her the real question, carver. Ask her what she'll do when it costs her something worse than a wound."*

"Hush," Felix said—to the satchel, to a dead queen, as naturally as telling a dog to sit—and turned back to Vance. "Tell me about the trap."

"There's a pass ahead of you. The company can't have more than four days' travel left—I tracked you, I know your pace. The Regent has known roughly where you are since Grey Ford; my false report bought you the lead, nothing more. He's not chasing you anymore. He doesn't need to." She crouched, drew in the dirt with one finger: the valley, the ridgelines, the meadow beyond where every map agreed the Threshold should stand. "He's ahead of you. Three hundred soldiers moved up the eastern valleys under cover of the amnesty proclamation—free pardon, food, transport south, all the lies that work on starving people. They're building a corral. When your company comes down into the meadow, the exits close, and everyone in it either surrenders or starves on open ground in winter. And the soldiers have orders regarding you specifically." She met Felix's eyes. "Alive. The Regent wants the doorkeeper alive. What he intends to do with you alive is worse than anything he'd do to you dead. I've read the warrants. I helped draft the doctrine."

The brazier cracked. Somewhere outside, a child cried and was hushed.

"They'll be in position in five days," Vance said. "Maybe six, if the snow slows them. You cannot fight three hundred. You cannot outrun them with two hundred civilians and elders and children. And you cannot go around, because the only other passes are—" She hesitated.

"Sealed," Felix said quietly. "Yes. We know about the one under the ice. We paid for that knowledge." Something moved behind their eyes, some grief filed away to be felt later, and Vance recognized the discipline of it because she practiced it herself. "So the Regent has finally built a trap worthy of him. Everything funnels to the meadow. Nothing gets out."

"Not through any door he knows about."

Felix went very still. "But you think there's a door he doesn't know about."

"I think," Vance said, "that I have spent my career learning how a man like the Regent thinks, and the flaw in every trap he builds is the same: he only believes in things he can measure. Soldiers, grain, walls, names on lists. If there's a door out of this country that opens on something he can't measure, he won't have posted a single man near it. He'll have posted them near the roads instead." She straightened. "I don't know where your Threshold is. But I know where he isn't looking. And right now, that may be the same thing."

From the satchel came a low whistle, dry as wind through a keyhole. *"Oh, I like her. Carver, I officially withdraw my objection. Mostly."*

Felix looked at the satchel, then at Vance, and something in their expression shifted—the wariness settling, not vanishing, but making room for something else. "You understand what you're doing," they said. "If you warn us and we get through, there's no report clever enough to save you. There's no going back to the Citadel, no trial you can win, no version of this where you're forgiven. He'll hunt you for the rest of your life."

"He already is," Vance said. "I told you. Executioners. I killed one of their horses and embarrassed both of them, which he'll take worse than the false report." She allowed herself, for the first time in days, the ghost of a smile. "I've been the law in this country for eleven years, Ravensworth. It turns out the law was a costume anyone could wear. I'd like to find out what I am without it."

Outside, the hum from the peaks swelled—just slightly, just for a moment, like a bell acknowledging a new arrival—and every person in the camp paused mid-motion, listening, before going back to their fires.

Felix rose, small and unhurried, and extended an ink-stained hand.

"Then welcome to the hunted," they said. "We're few, we're tired, and we argue constantly. But we're honest about it, which is more than the kingdom ever was." Their grip was cool and firm. "Come to the fire. There's someone you should meet—a girl who knows every pass the Regent's maps forgot. Between her roads and your knowledge of where the soldiers stand, we might just find the one path he left open."

"And if there isn't one?" Vance asked.

Felix glanced toward the tent flap, toward the darkening peaks and the bell-sound rolling off them, and their face did something Vance had never once seen in eleven years of the Regent's service: it hoped, openly, without apology.

"Then we make one," Felix said. "The gods woke up for a reason, Inquisitor. I'm beginning to suspect the reason is standing right here."

Beyond the tent wall, the snow began to fall again—soft, thick, erasing the trail behind them and, perhaps, opening something ahead.
