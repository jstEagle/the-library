## 16. The Doorless Country

The valley opened before them on the ninth morning, and Felix knew it was wrong before anyone else did.

They had climbed out of the high passes at dawn, through fog that smelled of stone and old snow, and then the land simply let go—dropping away in long green shoulders into a bowl of meadow so wide it seemed the mountains had stepped back to make room for something. Wildflowers ran to the horizon in drifts of white and gold. Bees worked the clover. Somewhere a lark was singing, indifferent and bright.

It should have been beautiful. It was, in the way that an empty cradle is beautiful.

"Vessa," Felix said quietly, hand resting on the satchel where her bones rode against their hip. "This is it. Isn't it?"

Her voice came up through the bone, thin and strange. "This is it."

The company came down behind them in a strung-out line—forty-one souls now, though Felix counted them the way you count breaths, without letting yourself think about what it means when one stops. Farmers from the burned villages. A miller with his daughter asleep on his back. Old Tam, who had carried his wife's loom-shuttle four hundred miles because it was the only thing the fire left him. Wren walked near the front, scanning the meadow with a guide's eyes, and Felix watched her face do the thing faces do: the narrowing, the searching, the slow failure to find.

"There's no door," Wren said. She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but her hand went to the rope at her belt and gripped it like a railing. "Felix. There's no door."

"I know."

"There's nothing here. There's grass." She turned a slow circle, taking in the whole impossible emptiness of it—the flowers nodding, the lark still singing, the sky blue as a child's painting of sky. "Vessa's map says—"

"Vessa's map doesn't say anything anymore." Felix knelt and set the satchel down in the clover. Their hands were shaking, and they hated that, hated being seen shaking. "I've asked her every hour since we crossed the last ridge. She won't answer. Or she can't."

* * *

They made camp because there was nothing else to do. That was the terrible arithmetic of exhaustion: when hope fails, the body still demands its rituals. Fires were built. The miller's daughter woke and demanded bread. Someone found a spring, cold and clean, and for half an hour the meadow held the ordinary sounds of people feeding themselves, and Felix almost believed they might simply live here, in the doorless country, growing something.

Then Tam stood up by his fire and said, "Where is it?"

Nobody answered him. He didn't need them to. "We were told—" His voice cracked and he steadied it with fury, which was how Tam did everything now. "We were told there was a way out. A door. The smuggler-queen's road, the Threshold, all of it. I buried my wife under a cairn two passes back because I thought I'd see her again on the other side of a door. So where is it?"

"It was here," Felix said. They stood, and forty-one faces turned to them, and they felt each gaze like a weight hung from their skin. "I don't know what happened. The country was erased—its roads, its records, its names. I think... I think the erasure took the door too. A door is a name. A threshold has a word written over it. And Thornfell's word was unmade, and the door went with it."

"So we walked here," Tam said, "for nothing."

"You walked here because staying was death."

"Some of us would rather die at home than starve in a field."

He wasn't the only one thinking it. Felix could see it moving through the camp like weather—the miller glancing south, the brothers from Harrow Vale already whispering with their heads together. The Regent had been generous lately, in the way of men who have won: amnesty for all who return and kneel. Pardon, and bread, and your village rebuilt around your obedience. It was not nothing. Hunger makes amnesty smell sweet.

By full dark, nine of them had packed their things.

Wren came to Felix's fire with her arms crossed and her jaw set. "The Harrow Vale brothers are going. Tam too. The miller says he'll decide at dawn." She sat down hard on the ground across from Felix, suddenly looking fifteen instead of whatever ancient thing the mountains had made her. "Say something to them."

"What would I say? That I know another way?" Felix laughed, and it came out ugly. "I don't. Vessa's gone quiet. The gods led us here and there's nothing here. I've got nothing, Wren. I'm a bone-carver who talked to dead people, and now even the dead people aren't talking."

"That's not true."

"It's—"

"She's talking right now." Wren nodded at the satchel. "Has been for an hour. You just stopped listening, because listening means hearing what she has to say, and you're afraid it's bad news."

Felix stared at her. Then they picked up the satchel, walked out beyond the fires into the dark of the meadow, and knelt in the clover with the fingerbones cupped in their palms.

* * *

"Vessa."

Nothing. Wind. The enormous indifferent dark, stars scattered overhead like spilled salt.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry I stopped carrying you properly, I'm sorry I treated you like a map instead of a woman, I'm sorry—" Their voice broke. "Talk to me. Even if it's the worst news in the world. Talk to me."

And Vessa Marrow, smuggler-queen of Thornfell, dead a hundred years, spoke through her own fingerbones in a voice like a knife being drawn slowly:

"You're kneeling in the wrong place, carver."

Felix looked up. The meadow stretched away in every direction, silvered by starlight, empty as a page before writing. "There's nowhere else. This is the valley. Your map ended here."

"My map never showed a place. How many times did I tell you that? Roads, rivers, passes—I drew those to keep fools like you moving. But the last mark, the one at the center of everything?" Her laugh was dry as moth wings. "That mark was always me, Felix. The Threshold isn't lost. The Threshold was refused."

"What are you saying?"

"A hundred years ago I stood in this valley with three hundred souls behind me and a door in front of me, real as rain, singing at the seams. And the door asked its price. Not gold. Not blood. It wanted my name—everything I was, every road I'd ever run, every legend they told about me in the taverns. Walk through as no one, or don't walk through at all." The ghost's voice dropped to nearly nothing. "I was Vessa Marrow. Queen of the smugglers, terror of the passes, the woman whose name alone opened locks. I couldn't do it. So the door closed, and the country died behind me, and I've been carrying that weight in a bag of fingerbones ever since, waiting for someone with less love for their own name than I had."

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "You're saying someone has to give up everything. Their name. Their history. Their self. To open a door that isn't there anymore because you wouldn't."

"I'm saying the door was never consumed by the erasure." The wind moved through the clover, and it sounded almost gentle. "The door is waiting to be *become*. That's why the gods called you, carver. Why they marked your hands. Not because you're a doorkeeper." A pause, and in it Felix heard a century of grief. "Because you're the only one who might be willing to be the door."

Felix knelt in the meadow for a long time after Vessa went quiet. Behind them, the fires of forty-one people flickered. Nine were leaving at dawn. Maybe more. Maybe all of them.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, in the roots of the mountains and under the floor of the world, something vast began to stir—a chorus of old gods waking all at once, drawn to this empty valley like moths to a candle that hadn't been lit yet.

Felix stood, brushed the clover from their knees, and walked back toward the firelight to tell them the price.
