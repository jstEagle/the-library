## 1. The Road That Remembers

The blizzard came down off the high shoulder of Thornfell like something with a grudge, and Mara Quintana let it.

That was the trick of it. Anyone could walk into a storm. The craft was in knowing which storms to fight and which to let pass over you like a rumor. She stood at the head of the caravan, six mules and eleven nervous men strung out behind her on a rope-line, and she let the wind scream at her face until her cheeks went numb, and beneath the screaming she listened for the storm's true voice underneath.

It was tired. That was all. Storms got tired the way everything did, and this one had been pushing itself up the pass since dawn with nothing left in it but habit. She could feel its edges fraying, the way you'd feel a loose thread on a sleeve if you were fool enough to reach for it.

She was not fool enough to reach for it. Not here. Not with eleven pairs of eyes behind her, any one of which might notice that the snow bent around their little column just slightly wrong, that the worst gusts seemed to lose interest three feet before they arrived, that the road ahead of them stayed findable when every other road on the mountain had been eaten white.

So she did what she always did. She walked. She read the mountain with her eyes instead of her skin — the lean of the drifts, the whistle-tone of wind through the notch ahead, the particular grey of the light that meant the squall would break within the hour. Ordinary knowledge. Guide's knowledge. The kind of thing a woman could know without anyone asking how.

"Quintana." Doss Hallow's voice, roughened by cold, from somewhere back along the line. "You sure about this cut?"

"I'm sure."

"The last guide said the notch floods by midwinter."

"The last guide also lost two mules and a client's entire cargo of brandy," Mara said, not turning around. "I've never lost anything on Thornfell. Ask anyone."

It was true, and it was the only reputation she allowed herself: reliable, unremarkable, slightly expensive. Seven years she had been walking these passes, and in seven years she had built herself into exactly nothing — no history anyone could trace past Lowmarket, no name that meant anything outside the valley, no gift anyone had ever seen. A guide who knew the mountains. That was all. Being no one was a discipline, same as any other, and she practiced it daily the way a swordsman practiced cuts.

The wind gusted again, hard enough to make the lead mule plant its feet and bawl, and Mara put her gloved hand against its neck and murmured something wordless, and — carefully, gently, without letting so much as a flake bend where it shouldn't — she took a thread of the storm's exhaustion and pulled it loose. Just a little. Just enough. The gust died between one heartbeat and the next, and the mule sighed and walked on, and nobody behind her noticed a thing except that the weather had, fortunately, eased.

*Good girl,* she told herself. *Nobody saw.*

Seven years of *nobody saw.* She sometimes wondered what she'd do if someone ever did.

* * *

They made the notch an hour past noon, and the squall broke the way she'd promised it would, and by the time they came down into the sheltered fold below Ravenstair the men were joking again, slapping each other's shoulders, calling her the best coin they'd spent all season. Mara took the praise the way she took everything: with a nod, and a price list, and no part of herself attached.

She was checking the mules' hooves for ice-balls when young Ferro came running up from the rear of the column, breath ragged, face the color of tallow.

"Mara. There's a man. Back there, off the trail — half in the drift. I thought he was a rock at first, but—"

She was moving before he finished.

The stranger lay crumpled against a granite outcrop maybe forty yards off the track, buried to the waist in blown snow, one arm flung out as if he'd been reaching for the road when the cold took him. He wore a courier's oilskin, travel-stained, and his boots were good boots, and neither fact explained what he was doing forty yards off the trail in a blizzard on the worst pass in Thornfell.

Mara knelt and brushed the snow from his face. Frost had welded his beard to his collar. His lips were the blue of deep ice. But when she pressed two fingers under his jaw, she found it — faint, stubborn, absurdly slow, but there.

"He's alive," she called back. "Barely. Get a litter rigged. We'll take him down to the Waystone Inn."

Doss came up frowning, snow crunching. "We're paid to deliver wool, not corpses that haven't finished dying. Man's half gone anyway. Could be a criminal. Could be carrying sickness."

"Could be," Mara agreed, already working the man's arm free of the drift. And then she stopped.

His hand — the hand she'd uncovered, frozen stiff, fingers curled — had something clenched inside it. And across the back of that fist, inked or branded, there was a mark she recognized.

Not the mark itself. She didn't know the mark. But she knew the *style* of it — the tight geometric interlock, the deliberate lines, the work of someone trained in sigil-craft. Her order's style. Or something close enough to make her palm itch inside her glove, where her own scar sat hidden and silent as it had been for seven years.

She closed the stranger's fingers back over whatever he held and said nothing about the mark to anyone.

"Litter," she repeated. "Now. If he dies we dump him at the inn and let the priests argue about him. If he lives, he owes us board. Either way we're not leaving a man to freeze when we don't have to."

Doss looked at her a long moment. "You're soft, Quintana."

"I'm expensive," she said. "There's a difference. Move."

* * *

The Waystone Inn sat in the fold of the valley like a stone dropped in a cup, one lamp burning yellow in its window, smoke standing straight up from its chimney in the dead air. By the time they carried the stranger inside, the caravan men had rebuilt their cheer, and the innkeeper's wife was already melting snow for broth, and everything had the warm, ordinary shape of an evening that would end with dice and bad wine.

Mara sat apart from it, as she always did, at the corner table with her back to the wall and her face to the door. Old habit. She ate slowly. She watched the stranger laid out on a pallet near the hearth, watched the innkeeper's wife chafe his hands, watched the frost recede from his beard by degrees.

And she felt the itch in her palm getting worse.

She'd felt it first on the pass, a faint heat like a coal held too near the skin. Now, as the evening deepened, it had become a burn — low, steady, insistent, the way her scar always behaved when something of her old life came near. Sigils answered sigils. That was the first law they taught you, back before they taught you everything else. Her scarred palm hadn't spoken in seven years, and tonight it was speaking clearly enough that she had to press it flat against the tabletop to keep from looking at it.

Near midnight, the inn quieting, she crossed the room to the pallet.

The stranger's breathing had steadied somewhat. His color was still wrong. She crouched beside him, and after a moment his eyelids dragged themselves open — pale grey eyes, unfocused, swimming — and fixed on her face with sudden, terrible urgency.

His mouth worked. No sound. She leaned closer.

His hand came up — shaking, impossibly heavy, dragging itself across the blanket like something underwater — and caught her wrist. The grip of a drowning man. And then his other hand pressed something into her palm, folding her fingers over it, forcing her scarred hand closed around—

A map. Folded small, stiff with old creases, and wet.

Wet because it was soaked in blood. His blood, she realized dimly; there was a dark stain spreading on the blanket beneath his side that the innkeeper's wife had missed, a wound under the oilskin that the cold had been holding shut and warmth was now opening.

"The vault," the stranger whispered, and his voice was a ruin, barely air at all. "It wakes. You have to—" His eyes rolled toward her right hand, the closed one, and something like recognition flickered through the drowning of them. "You're the—"

He never finished. His grip slackened. His chest rose once, twice, shallowly — and stopped.

Mara knelt there a long moment, the blood-soaked map folded inside her fist, listening to the fire pop and the wind test the shutters, while behind her the innkeeper's wife began to hum again over her dishes, unaware.

She should call out. Raise the alarm. Let someone else take charge of the body and the questions and the inevitable search of his effects.

Instead she opened her hand just far enough to look.

The map was drawn on something that wasn't quite paper and wasn't quite skin, and the ink moved. Slowly, lazily, like things drifting in deep water. Lines that shifted when she wasn't looking directly at them. And at its center, rendered in that same tight geometric style as the mark on the dead man's fist, was a symbol she knew intimately, because it lived burned into her own palm:

An ember-sigil.

Her scar answered it like a struck bell. Heat lanced up her arm, and she bit down on a gasp, and for one vertiginous second the fire in the hearth leaned toward her — actually *leaned*, flames bending sideways against the draft — before she clamped down on everything, all of it, the whole seven years of careful nothing, and the fire straightened, and no one saw.

No one ever saw.

She tucked the map away inside her coat, next to her heart, where it beat against her ribs like a second pulse. Then she stood, arranged her face into competent blankness, and turned to tell the innkeeper's wife that their guest had died.

Which was when she heard the horses.

Hoofbeats on the frozen track outside, more than one, coming fast through the dead-cold night. Mara moved to the window, parted the curtain a finger's width, and looked out at riders dismounting in the yard below — five of them, cloaked in travelling grey, moving with the unhurried precision of people who had followed something a very long way and were not accustomed to losing it.

The leader glanced up, and even across the dark yard, even through warped glass, Mara could have sworn his eyes found hers through the gap in the curtain.

Her palm burned. The map pulsed against her ribs like a captured bird.

Being no one, she thought, had just gotten a great deal harder.
