## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

The stranger didn't die in the pass. That was the first strange thing about him.

Everything frozen on the Thornfell high route died in the pass. Mara had pulled eleven bodies out of the snow in seven years—smugglers who'd misjudged a storm, a tax collector with more pride than sense, once a bride running from something no one asked about. The mountain kept what it killed. You wrapped them, you said the words if you knew any, and you let the ice have them until spring made the question someone else's.

But this man had crawled.

She found him forty paces below the treeline, at the edge of the waystation's lantern-glow, where the packed snow showed a drag-mark like a wound across the white. He'd torn his gloves off somewhere behind him—she passed them, half-buried, stiff as shingles—and he'd dug his bare hands into the drifts to keep moving after his arms stopped working. The snow told the whole story to anyone fluent in it: a man whose body had quit a mile back while the rest of him refused to agree.

Mara crouched over him and did the arithmetic she always did. Weight, exposure, time. His lips were grey. His breathing came in long, rattling pulls, like a bellows full of grit. By every measure she trusted, he was already a corpse that hadn't heard the news.

By every measure except the one in her palm, which had begun to burn.

The ember-sigil flared under her mitten—hot, insistent, the way it did when storms gathered over the eastern peaks, the way it never, ever did for anything else. She snatched her hand back and looked at the man properly for the first time.

He was nobody's courier. Couriers dressed warm and moved fast and carried nothing worth stealing on their bodies, because everything worth stealing rode in their heads or their blood. This man wore a merchant's coat, good wool, city-cut, soaked through and glazed with ice. And stitched inside the collar, half-hidden by frost, was a sigil she recognized because she'd spent seven years making sure she never saw it again: the coiled-grey knot of the Stormcaller Order.

Her stomach dropped through the mountain.

*No,* she thought. *No, no.* She glanced up the trail, at the white silence, at the caravan she could still hear below her—Bren shouting at the mules, Pip's laugh carrying thin and bright through the cold. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Seven years of careful, deliberate invisibility, and the world had just deposited an Order courier at her feet like a debt come due.

She should leave him. That was the smart move, the move that kept her nameless. Turn back, tell Bren there was a body by the treeline, let the mountain do what mountains did. In the spring, some other guide would find him and wonder, and wonder would never reach Mara Quintana because Mara Quintana did not exist.

Her hand burned.

"Damn you," she said—to the sigil, to the mountain, to whatever instinct in her refused to let a man freeze when she could kneel right here—and hauled him up by the collar.

* * *

The Waystone Inn sat in the fold of the pass like a stone dropped in a cupped palm, all black timber and slate roof furred with rime. It served smugglers, muleteers, and the occasional lost pilgrim, which was to say it served anyone who paid and asked nothing of its neighbors. Mara had been drinking there for seven years under the name *Corren*, quiet Corren, forgettable Corren, the guide who spoke maybe forty words a day and remembered every one of yours.

Getting the stranger inside without being seen was the first problem. Getting him inside *at all* was the second.

Old Sefa kept the inn, wide as a door and twice as hard to move, with forearms like ship rope and eyes that missed nothing on her own ground. When Mara dragged the stranger around to the wood-door and grunted the specific double-knock that meant *trouble, not law*, Sefa opened it before the third knock landed.

"Who's dead?"

"Might not be."

"That's not what I asked." But she took the other shoulder without being told, and together they got him down into the cellar where Sefa kept her illegal brandy and her legal grudges, laying him on the long table between the barrels. Upstairs, the caravan's evening noise went on—boots, dice, Bren's voice arguing freight prices—and the ordinary world continued, oblivious, blessedly unaware.

Sefa peeled back the frozen coat. Underneath, the man's shirt was soaked dark—not meltwater. Blood. A lot of it, from a wound low on his side that someone had bound with field-dressing gone brown and stiff. Frostbite had taken his fingers to the first knuckle; they looked like wax.

"He'll die," Sefa said.

"Probably."

"Then why are we doing this?"

Because my hand won't stop burning, Mara almost said, and didn't. "Because whoever cut him might come looking, and I'd rather hear them coming than find them standing over us."

That was true. It just wasn't the whole truth, and Sefa knew it, and mercifully Sefa was a woman who'd built a life on not asking for wholes.

They worked. Hot bricks wrapped in cloth against his core. Brandy, melted snow, the bitter willowbark tea Sefa brewed for dying muleteers. Mara cut away the ruined dressing and repacked the wound, and the man surfaced once, eyes rolling white, and moaned a word that might have been *vault* or might have been nothing.

Then, near midnight, when Sefa had gone up to feed the fire and keep the taproom honest, his eyes opened all the way.

They were brown, ordinary, terrified. They fixed on Mara with the terrible focus of a man spending his last coin.

"You," he rasped. "Storm-touched."

Her whole body went still. "You're delirious."

"I know the feel of it." A wet, clicking laugh. "Grew up around your kind. Before." His hand—the ruined one—crept across the table toward her, and she watched it come the way you watch a spider cross a floor. "I've been looking for you since Lowmarket. Followed the burn."

"The burn?"

"In you." His fingers closed on her wrist with shocking strength, the grip of a dying man, which is to say the grip of someone with nothing left to lose. "They gave me a job, girl. Last job. Get the map north. Don't let them take it back." His breath hitched, whistled. "They're coming. Grey riders. Three days behind me. Maybe two now."

"Let go of me."

"They can't open it." The words tumbled now, falling over each other. "Nobody can open it. Only—" and his eyes went to her right hand, deliberately, precisely, and everything in Mara went cold past freezing. "Only the marked. The order burned the key into somebody's hand seven years ago and then lost her, and Veyra has been looking ever since, and now the vault is waking up, and Kessrin knows, and everyone is coming, and I am so tired."

The room tilted. Seven years of careful, patient nothing—of *Corren*, of forty words a day, of walking through blizzards and smoothing them flat so no one ever saw—collapsed into a single point of light behind her sternum.

"What map?" she heard herself say. Stupid. Fatal. Asking questions was how you became part of a story.

His free hand fumbled at his belt, at a pouch sewn flat against the small of his back, and came out with leather wrapped around something stiff. Blood had soaked through the wrapping in a shape like wings. He pressed it into her hands—both her hands, folding her scarred fingers over it—and the ember-sigil flared so hot she gasped.

"It shows the way," he whispered. "Under the mountain. It only shows the way to the marked. Burn it if you're smart. Burn it and run south and never—" A cough took him, deep and tearing, and when it released, something in him released too. He sagged. His grip loosened. "The vault wakes," he breathed, and the words came out reverent and horrified at once, a prayer and a warning wearing the same mouth. "God help the one who holds the key. God help *you*."

And then he was gone. Not dramatically. Just a slow settling, like snow off a branch, and the terrible bellows-breath stilled, and the cellar was quiet except for the tick of the cooling stove and, distantly, Pip laughing upstairs about something.

Mara stood with a dead man's blood drying on her palms and the wrapped bundle heavy as sin between them.

* * *

She should have burned it. She had the fire right there—Sefa's stove ticking down to coals, heat enough to take leather and paper both. She stood over it for a long moment, the bundle in her hands, and told herself every sensible thing.

Burn it. Run south. Be no one again. It takes years to build a nothing, but you know how, you've done it, start tonight.

Instead she unwrapped it.

The map inside was not paper. It was hide of some kind, thin and pale as onion skin, and the ink on it was wrong—dark red-brown, and it *moved*, slowly, the way oil moves on water. Lines crawled and resettled. Mountains shifted their shoulders by fractions. And in the center, beneath a rendering of the Thornfell range so accurate it made her skin prickle, a single mark pulsed faintly, in rhythm, she realized with dawning horror, with the burn in her palm.

Not a mark on the map. A mark matching the map. Or the map matching her. She couldn't tell which direction the correspondence ran, and that frightened her more than the riders, more than the words *Veyra has been looking*.

She read what she could. A route, fragmentary, shifting—a descent beneath the roots of Thornfell itself, through places her own smuggler maps marked with the old dwarven glyph for *sealed*. Distances measured in something other than miles. And along the bottom margin, in handwriting that changed style three times as though written by different people across centuries, a single repeated phrase:

*It must not wake hungry.*

The sigil on her hand pulsed. On the map, the central mark answered. Pulse. Answer. Pulse. Answer. Like two hearts learning to beat together.

Mara rewrapped the bundle with shaking hands.

* * *

Upstairs, the taproom was loud and golden and utterly ignorant, and she loved it and hated it in the same breath. Bren sat by the big hearth arguing freight rates with a wool merchant, his beard frosted with brandy-foam, his voice booming the way it did when he felt rich. Pip perched on a stool beside him, twelve years old and all elbows, palming nuts off the bar when she thought Sefa wasn't looking. The twins—Sef and Illo, passing through with a load of untaxed silk—were fleecing a pair of trappers at dice with the cheerful shamelessness of weather.

Seven years, Mara thought. Seven years of these people knowing her as Corren, the quiet one, good in a storm, keeps to himself. Seven years of Bren calling her *lad* and slipping her extra pay because she never argued about routes. Seven years of Pip trailing her around campsites asking questions Mara deflected with shrugs.

None of them knew her name. That had been the point. That had been the *achievement*.

She sat in the corner with a beer she didn't drink and understood, with the clarity that comes in the hour after a death, that it was over. Not because the courier had named her—he hadn't, couldn't have, knew nothing but the burn. Because the grey riders were coming, and grey riders asked questions, and questions were a net, and somewhere in that net her thread waited. The Order had been looking for the marked one for seven years. The marked one had a habit, a face, a route, an inn. Anonymity was a wall, and walls fall from one breach.

She thought about leaving tonight. Slipping out the wood-door, south, alone, the old plan, the good plan.

Then Pip caught her eye across the room and grinned, gap-toothed, and waved her over with both arms, and Mara found—to her fury—that her feet didn't even pretend to consider the door.

* * *

They came at dusk the next day.

Three of them, riding into the inn-yard through falling snow, and Mara knew them before she saw the grey wool: the horses were too calm. No animal stays that calm in mountain weather unless something about its rider makes stillness safer than fear. They dismounted in unison. They stamped the snow off in unison. They walked into Sefa's taproom in unison, and the room's noise dipped the way a candle dips in wind.

Grey wool, travel-worn. Hoods back. Two men and a woman, and the woman led—mid-fifties, iron hair cropped short, a face like a folded letter. Her eyes went around the taproom once, unhurried, cataloguing. Mara, in her corner, kept her gaze on her beer and her breathing even and her right hand curled loose and empty on the table, and prayed to no god in particular that a certain dead man in the cellar had been wrong about following the burn.

"Travelers," the woman said. Not loudly. She didn't need to. "We're looking for a man. Merchant's coat, city-cut. Came through the high pass, likely wounded. Likely dead by now." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat. "He stole from the Order. We only want what he took."

Murmurs, head-shakes, the beautiful useless noise of people who genuinely knew nothing. Bren, bless his enormous heart, leaned back in his chair and said, "High pass in this season? Whatever he stole, the mountain's taken it back," and got a grunt of laughter from the trappers.

The woman smiled thinly. Let her gaze travel again. And this time it snagged.

On Mara.

Not on her face. Lower. On her right hand, resting on the table, and Mara realized—one heartbeat too late—that she'd unconsciously been rubbing the heel of her palm against the wood, the way she did when the sigil ached, the way she'd done for seven years without ever once being seen doing it.

The woman's eyes came up to Mara's face. Held there.

"Your hand troubles you," she said. "Guide?"

"Old break," Mara said. Forty words a day. Quiet Corren. "Bad winter, years back. It aches ahead of weather."

"Mm," the woman said. "Ahead of weather." Something moved behind her eyes—not recognition yet, but the *search* for it, a hound catching a thread of scent and not yet sure of the wind. She crossed the room slowly, unhurried, and the taproom held its breath around her, and Mara sat very still and calculated distances to the door, to the kitchen, to the cellar stair, and hated that she was calculating at all.

The woman stopped at her table. Close enough that Mara could smell road dust and something faintly electrical, like the air before lightning.

"Aches ahead of weather," the woman repeated, softly. "That's a curious talent, for a guide."

"Long way down from the Order's towers," Mara said, bored, rough-edged, *lad*-voiced. "We get all kinds of aches up here."

For a moment—three heartbeats, four—the woman simply looked at her. Then she reached into her coat, and Mara's whole body coiled, and instead of steel the woman drew out a small card of stiff grey paper and set it on the table between them.

"If a man passes through—or a body, or a rumor—send word to the garrison at Lowmarket. There's payment in it. Honest payment." She tapped the card once. "The Order pays its debts, guide. All of them."

She turned. At the doorway she paused, half-turned back, and said, to the room, to the storm outside, perhaps to Mara alone:

"We'll be at the garrison. We don't leave the mountains until what's ours is found." And then, quieter, almost gentle, almost kind, the worst thing she could possibly have said: "*Nothing stays buried forever.*"

The door shut behind them. The taproom exhaled.

Mara looked down at the grey card on her table, at her own hand beside it, at the faint heat rising through the scarred skin like a coal refusing to die. Out the frost-furred window, three riders moved off down the valley toward Lowmarket, unhurried, patient, certain.

In the cellar below her feet lay a dead man and a map that moved. In her palm, the pulse-answering-pulse continued, steady as a second heartbeat, keeping time with something far beneath the mountain that was, the courier had sworn, waking up.

*Burn it and run south,* the dead man had said.

Mara finished her beer, pocketed the card, and went down the cellar stairs to sit with the body, because it was suddenly, terribly clear that the mountain had finally found her—and that running was no longer one of the roads out.
