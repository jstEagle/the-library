## 4. Knights in Grey Wool

The rain found Ser Aldric Vane before the town did.

It came down in grey sheets off the Thornfell foothills, turning the road to porridge and his cloak to a wet stone draped across his shoulders. His horse — a bay gelding named Corben who had opinions about weather and shared them freely through his ears — picked his way down the last mile of switchbacks with the resigned patience of an animal who had learned that complaining changed nothing.

Below, in a bowl of mist where two rivers met, sat Lowmarket: border town, smuggler's den, and by every report Aldric had read on the ride north, the last place the courier had been seen alive.

He reined up at the crest of the hill and let himself look at it for a moment. He'd learned that habit from his old commander, Ser Hallam, dead now eight years: *look at a place before you enter it, boy. A town tells you what it is from a distance, if you're willing to listen.* Lowmarket told him it was tired. Smoke rose crooked from too many chimneys, the walls were more suggestion than defense, and the gate — such as it was — stood open because closing it would have been an admission that anything here was worth stealing.

Aldric touched his heels to Corben's flanks and rode down into the rain.

* * *

The orders were folded against his breastplate, next to his heart, which he suspected was someone's idea of poetry. He had read them so many times on the road that he could recite them with his eyes closed, and sometimes did, in the dark hours before dawn when the fire burned low and the words seemed less like ink and more like a weight settling onto his chest.

*By command of the Order of the Quiet Storm, and under seal of Archon Veyra: Ser Aldric Vane is charged to recover a certain document taken from the custody of the Order by one Merric Doss, courier. The bearer of said document, if found, is to be detained and questioned. If said bearer bears the mark —*

He always stopped there. Not because he couldn't finish it. Because finishing it out loud, even alone, even to his horse, made it real in a way the reading never quite managed.

*— the mark of the ember-sigil, he or she is to be executed without trial, and the body given to fire.*

Without trial. He'd been a knight of the Order for nineteen years, sworn at fifteen with his hand on a storm-glass and his knees in wet grass, and in all that time he had never once been asked to kill a person who hadn't raised a weapon first. He had killed men. He wasn't proud of all of them, but they had been men with swords, men who chose. This order asked him to kill someone for a scar.

*If marked,* the parchment said, in Veyra's narrow, patient hand, as though she were instructing him on the proper storage of grain.

He presented himself at the town's single inn — the Drowned Lantern, according to its sign, which depicted a lantern drowning, which Aldric felt was honest advertising — just as the light was failing. The common room was warm and smelled of wet wool, tallow, and stew that had been simmering since sometime last week. A dozen heads turned when he entered. They turned back when they saw the grey wool traveling cloak rather than plate; a lone rider in plain clothes was nobody's problem, in a town like this. That was the point of the grey wool. It was why the Order dressed its knights in it for work like this. *We are quiet,* the wool said. *We are not your concern.*

He took a seat near the hearth, ordered stew and whatever passed for ale, and began to listen.

* * *

Listening was the part of the work Aldric was actually good at, though no one at the Order had ever thought to praise him for it. Swordwork they praised. Riding they praised. But listening — sitting still in a room full of strangers and letting their talk wash over you until the shape of the truth emerged from it like a bone from a riverbank — that was considered a servant's skill, a spymaster's trick, beneath the dignity of a knight.

Ser Hallam had taught him otherwise. *Every room is a confession waiting to happen,* the old man used to say. *People can't help talking around a secret. Your job is to be furniture.*

So Aldric was furniture. He ate his stew, drank his thin ale, and listened.

The courier's name was Merric Doss, and the room remembered him fondly. He'd come through eleven days ago, half-frozen already, asking after a guide over the high pass — specifically, they said, a guide who could get a man over Thornfell in winter, which everyone agreed was either madness or desperation, and probably both. The innkeeper, a broad woman with forearms like hams, remembered him best.

"Paid double for a guide," she said to another patron, close enough that Aldric caught every word without turning his head. "And when nobody'd take the job, he paid triple. There's one guide takes winter crossings. Quintana. Mara Quintana. She was up at the pass that week — came down three days after the storm broke."

"And did he hire her?"

The innkeeper shrugged. "She never says yes to anyone. But he left the morning she came down. Headed east along the river road." She paused, wiping a mug. "Found him yet?"

"Found *what* of him?" the other man said, and laughed. "Shepherd up on Coldmere says there's a frozen man on the high trail. Or what's left of one. Birds got at him."

Aldric kept his eyes on his bowl and his breathing even, and inside his chest something cold and heavy settled into place beside the folded orders.

Eleven days. The courier had died on the pass eleven days ago, and the map — the document the Order wanted back so badly it had sent a knight across four hundred miles of bad road — had been on his body, or near it, for eleven days. And the only guide in the region who'd been anywhere near him was a woman named Quintana.

*If said bearer bears the mark.*

He finished his ale, paid with Order coin that drew no comment, and went upstairs to a room with a bed narrower than his shoulders and a window overlooking the muddy street. He sat on the bed in the dark, armor off, sword across his knees, and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning came grey and unhurried. Aldric spent it being furniture again.

He walked the market square while traders argued over tarps and fishmongers shouted prices that would have gotten them arrested in any civilized city. He bought an apple he didn't want from a girl who looked at his sword-belt and decided not to shortchange him. He heard the name Quintana twice more — once from a carter complaining that the good guides were all mad or dead, once from two dockhands arguing about whether the storm on the pass last month had been natural.

"Weren't natural," the younger one insisted. "My cousin was up at the mine. Said the blizzard just — stopped. Like somebody shut a door on it. Right over the trail, clear sky in the middle of a whiteout."

"Storms stop," the older one said.

"Not like that."

Aldric walked on, chewing the apple he didn't want, and thought about the reports he'd memorized in the Order's archive at Cael Sarn. *Exiled storm-caller. Refused binding. Marked with the sigil at severance, per custom.* Seven years in the Thornfells, guiding smugglers, keeping her head down. The file had been thin, because the Order's interest in exiles was thin. Exiles were supposed to be finished. Exiles were supposed to fade.

But the file had contained one line, underlined in Veyra's hand, that Aldric had read a dozen times on the road and understood only now, standing in a muddy market square smelling of fish:

*The sigil cannot be transferred. It burns until the bearer dies. If the bearer lives, the key lives.*

Somewhere in this town, or on that mountain, a woman with a burned palm was carrying the only key to a door the Order had spent centuries pretending didn't exist. And Aldric's orders, folded against his heart, told him exactly what to do about it.

He threw the apple core to a goat and went looking for the stables, because thinking was easier with his hands busy, and Corben needed shoeing anyway.

* * *

He saw her for the first time that afternoon, and afterward he could never fully explain to himself why it mattered — why a glimpse of a stranger in a rain-soaked street lodged in him like a splinter.

She came out of the chandlery across the square with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a canvas-wrapped bundle under her arm, moving with the particular economy of someone who has carried heavy things in bad places for a long time. Dark hair, rain-flattened. A oilskin coat the color of slate. Nothing about her announced itself; she moved through the crowd the way water moves through stones, present everywhere, noticed nowhere.

It was the way she looked at the sky that stopped him.

Everyone else in Lowmarket hunched against the drizzle or ignored it. She lifted her face to it — just for a moment, just a breath — the way a musician might touch a string to check the tuning. Her eyes tracked the clouds moving in off the western peaks, and something in her expression was so private, so unguarded, that Aldric felt like a trespasser just for witnessing it. Then her gaze swept the square — quick, professional, cataloguing exits and faces — and snagged, for half a heartbeat, on him.

On him. In a crowd of forty people, in a town where he was nobody, her eyes found the one man watching her and rested there for exactly long enough to measure him. He watched her decide what he was. He watched her decide he wasn't worth running from. And then she crossed the square and was gone down an alley between a cooper's shop and a shrine to some river god whose statue wore a necklace of real freshwater pearls, stolen or donated, impossible to say.

Aldric stood in the rain with his heart going strangely hard against his ribs.

*That's her,* he thought. Not *that's the target*, not *that's the bearer*. *That's her.* As if he knew her. As if some part of him had recognized something — the checking of the sky, maybe, or the way she'd measured him and dismissed him, the way a person does when they've spent years being hunted and have grown very, very good at knowing which hunters matter.

He had hunted people before. Fugitive priests, deserters, a hedge-mage who'd sold binding-work to bandits. It had never troubled him much; the Order's justice was blunt but it was justice, and the people he'd brought in had mostly earned what came to them.

This was different. This was a woman buying rope.

* * *

He reported nothing, because there was no one to report to — the nearest Order waystation was six days south, and Veyra had made it clear he was to work alone. *Quietly,* her letter had said. *The vault must not become a story. Stories bring armies, and armies bring ruin.* So he sat in the Drowned Lantern that evening, nursing ale, and turned the problem over like a stone with something living underneath it.

Option one: approach her openly. Identify himself, demand the map, take her south for judgment. She would run, or fight, or both, and she was a storm-caller, and he was one man in grey wool with a sword. The arithmetic was poor.

Option two: watch her. Confirm the sigil, confirm the map, then act with certainty instead of suspicion. Slower. Safer. The sensible course.

Option three.

Option three was the one he kept circling and refusing to name, the one that lived in the space between *execute without trial* and *she was buying rope*. Option three was the question a younger Aldric would never have dared ask and an older Aldric, he suspected, would ask whether he wanted to or not: *what if the Order is wrong?*

He was still turning the stone over when the door banged open and the cold came in wearing Kessrin's colors.

There were six of them, soldiers in black-and-rust livery, mud to the thighs and the particular swagger of men far from their officers. The common room went quiet the way a forest goes quiet. Their sergeant — a heavy man with a beard braided with wire — planted himself in front of the innkeeper and smiled with all the warmth of a drawn blade.

"We're looking for a woman," he said. "Guide. Goes by Quintana. Works the passes." He laid a purse on the bar, and the coins inside it clinked with the sound of silver, real silver, more than a guide earned in a year. "Warlord's coin for whoever tells us where she sleeps."

Aldric did not move. He had spent nineteen years learning to keep his face still, and he kept it still now, but behind it his mind had gone sharp and cold and fast, because everything had just changed. Kessrin's soldiers. Here. Hunting the same woman, hours — perhaps minutes — behind him. Whatever game the Order was playing with maps and vaults and marks, the warlord was playing it faster and with worse rules, and the woman with the rope over her shoulder was caught between them like a rabbit between hawks.

The innkeeper looked at the purse. Looked at the sergeant. Around the room, Aldric saw, other eyes were doing arithmetic of their own — and one pair of eyes, near the door, belonged to a lean young man in a drover's coat who had slipped out of his seat the moment the soldiers entered and was already easing toward the back.

Toward the alley. Toward the chandlery district. Toward wherever word traveled fastest to the woman who checked the sky.

The sergeant's smile widened. "Somebody knows something," he said. "Somebody always does."

Aldric finished his ale, set down his cup, and stood — slowly, stretching, a tired traveler calling it a night. He climbed the stairs to his narrow room, barred the door, and sat on the bed with his sword across his knees, listening to the soldiers' laughter rise from below and the rain thicken against the window.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he would find her first. Tomorrow he would look her in the eye and ask his questions, and she would lie, and he would decide — with the orders against his heart and whatever conscience he had left — what to do about the answers.

Through the wall, muffled by plaster and rain, he heard the sergeant ask the innkeeper one more question, almost idly, the way a man asks when he already suspects the answer:

"Anyone else been asking after her? Anyone at all?"

And the innkeeper, bless her ham-sized heart, said, "Just travelers," and Aldric closed his eyes and began, quietly, to plan.
