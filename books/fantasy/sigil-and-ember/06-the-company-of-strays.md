## 6. The Company of Strays

The waystation at Hollowmere had once been a shrine. You could still see it in the bones of the place—the arched windows, now shuttered with salt-bleached planks, and the stone font in the corner where travelers had once washed the road from their hands. Someone had filled it with kindling. That, more than anything, told Mara what kind of people ran it now.

She paid for two nights and a room with no questions asked, which cost double what rooms usually cost, because rooms without questions always did. Pip was asleep against her shoulder before they'd even climbed the stairs, the girl's breath sour with exhaustion and the cheap honey-cakes Mara had bought her at the last crossroads just to stop the crying.

*Just to stop the crying*, Mara thought, climbing. *That's how it starts.*

Three days out of Lowmarket, three days of moving only at night, sleeping in culverts, teaching Pip to walk soft and breathe quiet when riders passed on the high road. Three days of the map pressed flat against her ribs inside her shirt, warm as a second heartbeat, and her palm burning under its wrappings like a coal that refused to die down.

Bren's face kept surfacing when she closed her eyes. Not the way he'd looked when they took him—shoved into the wagon with his wrists lashed, spitting curses with blood in his teeth—but the way he'd looked seven years ago, on her first run through Thornfell, when she'd frozen halfway across a scree field and he'd waited without comment until her legs remembered how to move. *You'll do, storm-girl,* he'd said. *Long as you don't get us killed.* He'd never called her anything else. Storm-girl. Like it was a joke. Like he'd known all along and decided it didn't matter.

It had mattered. It had gotten him taken.

Mara laid Pip on the narrow bed, pulled off the girl's boots, and stood a long moment watching her breathe. Then she went back down to the common room, because hiding upstairs would feel too much like hiding, and hiding was what had killed Bren.

The common room was half-full and entirely hostile. Waystation folk never liked strangers, and strangers who arrived alone with a child and paid in southern coin liked them less. Mara took a table near the wall, ordered stew she didn't want, and began doing what she did best: being no one. Shoulders down. Eyes on the bowl. A woman tired from the road, nothing behind her face but miles.

She lasted about ten minutes before the twins found her.

"You're the one who came in with the kid," said the one on her left, dropping into the chair uninvited. He had a lean, hungry face and dice tattooed on his knuckles, one through five on the left hand, and when Mara glanced at his right she saw six through two. Twins, then. The other one slid into the seat across from her, identical except for a scar splitting his eyebrow.

"I'm Sef," said the left one. "This is Illo."

"Nobody," said Mara.

"Nobody," Illo repeated, delighted. "See, Sef, I told you. Everybody's nobody till you learn their price."

"We watched you come in," Sef said. "You checked the exits, the roofline, and the man by the fire who's wearing a sword under his coat. In that order. That's not nobody. That's somebody running."

"Or somebody careful."

"Same thing, out here." Sef spun a coin across his knuckles, five-one-two-three-four, smooth as water. "Here's our situation, Nobody. We were working the Lowmarket tables three weeks ago. Good work. Quiet work. Then Kessrin's greycoats came through torching everything south of the tanneries, and every soul we owed money to or who owed money to us scattered like pigeons. Now we're going east over Thornfell with nothing but our charm"—Illo snorted—"and we heard there's a guide who knows the passes better than the mountain does."

Mara kept her eyes on her stew. "Heard from who?"

"The kind of people who talk in cellars." Illo leaned forward. "They say she's expensive, she's reliable, and she doesn't ask names. They also say she's been quiet since Lowmarket burned. Which means either she's dead, or she's sitting right here eating bad stew and pretending she can't hear us."

The silence stretched. Mara weighed it the way she weighed everything now: risk against need. She couldn't take Pip over the high passes alone—not with Kessrin's riders sweeping the valleys, not with the order's hunters certainly already sniffing after the courier's map. Two gamblers were useless in a snowfield and worse in a fight. But useless men drew less attention than armed ones, and dice-players knew how to read a room, and a room was exactly what she needed read for the next hundred miles.

"East side of Thornfell," she said finally. "I take you as far as the Cinderford bridge. You don't ask my name, you don't touch my gear, and if I say walk, you walk."

Sef grinned and offered his hand. She ignored it.

"And the kid?" Illo asked, softer than she expected.

"Comes with me. Always."

"Didn't think otherwise." He shrugged. "Anybody who checks the roofline first isn't leaving a child behind. We'll take the front room. Loud, stupid, visible. You take the back. Deal?"

It was, Mara admitted privately, a good deal. She hated how good it felt to make one—to sit across a table from people who wanted something from her that wasn't her blood or her sigil, and to say yes.

*That's how it starts,* she thought again, and pushed the bowl away.

* * *

The dwarf came at midnight.

Mara woke to the sound of the common room door and lay listening, hand curled around the knife under her pillow, counting footsteps. Heavy ones. Deliberate. Not trying to be quiet, which meant either drunk or confident, and the tread was too steady for drunk.

She was down the stairs with her blade palmed before the stranger reached the fire. He was short, broad as a door, with a grey beard braided into two iron rings and a mason's forearms scarred white with old chisel cuts. He carried a sledgehammer over one shoulder like a walking stick and a bedroll like an afterthought. He looked at her knife the way a man looks at weather.

"Put that away, lass. If I wanted trouble I'd have brought my own."

"Who are you?"

"Torv." He said it like a place name. "Stone-mason. Late of the deep roads." He nodded toward the mountains' black silhouette through the shutters. "Heading under Thornfell, same as everyone with sense is heading over it this season. Heard there was a guide."

"Everyone's heard there's a guide."

"Aye, well." He lowered himself onto the bench by the fire with a sound like a rockslide settling. "Everyone's also heard the greycoats burned Lowmarket looking for something. And everyone's heard the courier what carried that something froze dead on the pass with his fingers clutching empty air." His eyes, pale as quartz, settled on her with the mild patience of a man who had spent forty years waiting for stone to tell him its business. "Empty air's an interesting thing, lass. Leaves a shape behind."

Mara's mouth went dry. Her wrapped palm throbbed.

"I don't know what you mean."

"No. Didn't expect you would." He held his hands to the fire. "Here's mine, then, plain as I can lay it. Them tunnels under Thornfell? My folk cut 'em. My grandfather's grandfather cut 'em, and my folk have been closing 'em up for three generations so folk like Kessrin can't use 'em. But there's ways through still. Ways only a mason knows. And I'm thinking whoever's carrying what the greycoats want might be needing a way through that doesn't show on any map Kessrin's got."

"And why would you help someone like that?"

Torv turned his quartz-pale eyes on her again, and for a moment the mildness dropped and she saw something old and tired underneath.

"Because Kessrin's army marched through the Cinder Vale last month," he said, "and used my cousin's meeting-hall for target practice with the folk still inside. So." He shrugged, mountains moving. "Some things you close up. Some things you open. Mason's choice. I'm choosing."

Mara looked at him a long time. Everything in her that had survived seven years of exile screamed to send him away—too sharp, too knowing, too many strings. But Bren's face surfaced again, and Pip asleep upstairs, and the simple arithmetic of the road ahead: two gamblers, one child, one hunted woman, and four hundred miles of winter mountain between them and anywhere safe.

"Sunrise," she said. "Back room. Bring your hammer."

Torv smiled for the first time, and it transformed his whole granite face. "Knew you'd see reason, lass. Us practical folk got to stick together."

* * *

The priest found them at dawn, which Mara would later decide was the most priestly thing about her.

They were gathered in the back room—Mara with the map spread on the table despite every instinct shrieking against it, Torv frowning over the tunnel routes marked in the shifting ink, the twins arguing cheerfully about whose fault it would be when they all died—when the knock came. Three raps, precise, unhurried.

The woman who entered was fifty-ish, silver-streaked hair cropped short, dressed in travelling leathers with the particular neatness of someone who had once worn vestments and could not quite stop arranging them. Around her neck hung a wooden pendant shaped like a stylized storm-cloud, and when she saw it wasn't fooling anyone, she took it off and pocketed it with the air of a woman discarding an old argument.

"Odessa," she said. "Formerly of the Order of the Quiet Sky. Formerly. Before the former part, I kept records." Her gaze went to the map on the table, and stopped, and something moved behind her eyes that Mara couldn't name—something between recognition and grief. "May I sit?"

"How did you find us?" Mara asked.

"I've been following you since Lowmarket. Or rather, following the hole you left. Everywhere you go, people stop talking. It's very distinctive." Odessa sat without waiting for permission. "I was the Order's vault-keeper for eleven years. Records, seals, catalogues of things that should never have been written down. I left twenty years ago because I read too much of what I was keeping." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, folded on the table, had gone white-knuckled. "And now someone has died carrying a map to the place I spent eleven years pretending didn't exist, and a warlord is burning cities to find it, and I would very much like to know what's happening before it happens to me."

The room had gone quiet. Even the twins had stopped arguing.

"You know what this is," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"I know what it leads to." Odessa's eyes lifted to hers, and there it was again—that flicker, that grief. "And I know that the Order will have sent hunters after it. And I know that whatever you're planning, you won't survive it alone, and neither will the child."

Mara looked around the table. The gambler twins, watching her with the shrewd attention they probably thought they hid. The dwarf mason, arms crossed, nodding slowly like a man hearing confirmation of a survey. The defrocked priest with her storm-cloud pendant in her pocket and her secrets worn like armor.

Strays, all of them. People the world had chewed up and spat out, drifting toward the same fire because the night was cold and the alternatives were worse.

*I should leave tonight,* Mara thought. *Alone. Take Pip and vanish the way I've always vanished. That's what staying alive looks like. That's what keeping people safe looks like.*

But Bren hadn't been safe. Bren had been invisible right up until the greycoats kicked his door in, and anonymity hadn't saved him, and being no one hadn't saved the courier frozen on the pass, and it wouldn't save Pip, and it wouldn't save Mara herself—not with the sigil burning on her hand and the map warm against her ribs and the word *vault* echoing through everything like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

"Rules," she said, and every head turned. "We move at dusk and sleep in daylight. Nobody uses real names on the road—I'm Mara, that's all you get. Pip stays in the middle of the column, always. And when I say a decision's made, it's made. I've buried people who argued with me in bad weather."

"Charming," said Illo.

"She'll do," said Torv.

Odessa simply studied her, that unreadable grief still moving somewhere behind her eyes. "One condition of mine," she said. "When we reach wherever this map leads—if we reach it—you let me see it first. Before anyone. Before you."

Mara should have refused. Every instinct said refuse; priests had agendas the way rivers had currents.

But the map's ink had shifted twice during the night, lines crawling like insects, and her palm had burned hot enough to wake her, and she was beginning to suspect that whatever waited beneath Thornfell was not going to care about her rules.

"Done," she said.

Sef produced a battered deck of cards from somewhere inside his coat and began dealing solitaire onto the table, grinning. "Well then. Company of strays it is. Somebody pour the priest a drink—she looks like she needs it."

Outside, the wind had begun to rise, coming down off the peaks in long, low moans that rattled the shutters. Mara felt it in her teeth, in the old places, the way she always did—a faint answering hum beneath her skin, like a struck bell remembering its note.

She looked at her wrapped palm and tried to feel nothing at all.

At dusk they would walk into the mountains together, five strays and a sleeping child, toward a vault full of storms and a past that had finally caught up with her. Mara told herself she was using them. Shelter, cover, extra blades on the road—nothing more.

Around the fire that evening, Illo taught Pip a cheating trick with three walnut shells, and the girl laughed for the first time since Lowmarket burned, bright and startled, like a bird surprising itself into song. Torv whittled. Sef lost money to his own brother. Odessa watched them all with an expression Mara refused, firmly, to call hope.

Warmth spread through the room like spilled wine.

Mara sat at the edge of it, outside the circle of light, and did not trust it one bit.

And far to the north, past the shuttered windows, past the black shoulders of Thornfell, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky—once, twice—and stopped.

Her palm burned like a brand.

Something had heard her laughing.
