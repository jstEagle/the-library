## 7. Storms Answer to No One

They made the ford at dusk, and Mara knew before they reached it that something was wrong.

The birds had gone quiet an hour back—not the ordinary hush of evening, but a silence with edges, the kind that came when men moved through the trees trying not to be heard. She'd spent seven years reading the Thornfell's moods the way other people read faces, and right now the mountain was holding its breath.

She said nothing at first. She told herself she was being careful, cautious, the way she'd always been. But the truth was uglier: some small, cowardly part of her wanted one more hour of being nobody, and so she let the company walk on toward the water while every instinct screamed *turn around*.

The waystation behind them was half a day gone now—Odessa's prayers muttered over a cracked altar, Torv grumbling about dwarven stonework that had outlived its masons, the twins cheating each other at cards while Pip watched with sharp eyes and sharper questions. A strange little fire of people. Mara had caught herself laughing twice in two days, and both times it had frightened her more than Kessrin's soldiers.

The river ran high with snowmelt, grey-green and loud. The ford's stones showed as dark teeth just beneath the surface.

"Hold up," Torv said suddenly, raising a fist. The dwarf stood a head shorter than anyone but Pip, and he heard the earth the way Mara heard weather. "Horses. Upstream. Moving quiet."

Illo's hand went to her knife. Sef swore softly and started gathering the cards he'd been shuffling as they walked, because even now, apparently, he wasn't going to abandon a full deck.

"How many?" Odessa asked. The defrocked priest had a way of asking terrible questions in a perfectly calm voice, as if she were inquiring after the price of bread.

"Too many to count quiet," Torv said. "Six? Eight?"

Mara closed her eyes and listened past the river, past the wind, into the pressure of the air itself. Storms left fingerprints on the world—static in the hair, weight in the chest—and so did large groups of armed men, in their own way. She felt them now, upstream and closing, fanning out through the pines with the unhurried confidence of soldiers who believed their quarry was already dead.

"Kessrin's riders," she said. "They've been tracking us since Lowmarket."

"You know this how?" Illo asked.

"I know it." Mara opened her eyes. Pip was looking at her, and the girl's gaze was too perceptive for her own good. Bren's daughter had been asking questions all week—the map, the burned quarter, why soldiers shouted Mara's description at checkpoints. *A woman, dark-haired, walks like she owns the road.* Mara had answered none of them.

"We cross fast and scatter into the scree," Torv said. "Dwarven ground above the treeline. Horses are useless there."

"They'll have riders on the far bank too," Mara said quietly. "It's what I'd do."

Everyone looked at her.

"It's what I'd do," she repeated, and hated herself for saying it.

* * *

They never made the water.

The first arrow came out of the pines and buried itself in Torv's pack, close enough that the dwarf staggered. Then the trees gave up pretending, and riders crashed down through the undergrowth in a loose crescent—eight of them, nine, ten, torches burning despite the dusk, black-and-crimson sashes snapping. Kessrin's colors. Kessrin's wolf-toothed sergeant in front, the same man who'd put a boot through Bren's door.

"There!" someone shouted. "The woman! Take the woman alive!"

*Alive.* The word went through Mara colder than the arrow could have. They knew what she was. Or they thought they did—they knew she mattered, which amounted to the same thing.

"Scatter!" Torv roared, and the company broke.

Mara grabbed Pip's wrist and pulled her toward the rocks at the river's edge. Behind them, Sef and Illo split in opposite directions with the easy choreography of twins who'd been running from trouble since birth. Odessa didn't run at all; she turned to face the riders with her empty hands raised, murmuring something that might have been prayer or might have been profanity, and a horse shied away from her as if it had seen something underneath her skin.

An arrow hissed past Mara's ear. Another took the ground at Pip's feet.

The girl stumbled. Mara caught her, dragged her behind a boulder the size of a cart, and pressed her down into the wet stone. "Stay. Don't move. Don't look."

Pip looked. Of course she looked. She was Bren's daughter.

The riders were converging, cutting off the scree slope, cutting off the ford, herding them against the river with the smooth patience of men who had done this many times. The sergeant rode at the center, torch held high, and his eyes found Mara across fifty feet of darkening air.

"The map," he called. "And the marked woman. Give us both and the rest of you walk away. My lord Kessrin keeps his bargains."

"He burns towns," Pip whispered. "I saw him keep that bargain."

Mara's heart was slamming. Her palm—the sigil, the ember-colored scar she'd kept hidden under wool and leather for seven years—had begun to burn. Not the dull ache it carried near the map, but a live heat, climbing her wrist like water running backward.

She knew what that meant. She'd known since the pass, since the blizzard she'd secretly smoothed flat for the smuggler caravan, since every small cheat of weather she'd allowed herself over seven years of careful invisibility. The storm in her blood recognized what was coming. It always had better instincts than she did.

"Mara," Pip said. "What's wrong with your hand?"

"Nothing."

It was glowing. Faintly, through the leather of her glove, a thread of orange light like a coal breathing.

Across the river, the sergeant saw it too. His torch dipped. His mouth opened.

Mara had seven years of practice at being no one. She had gotten very good at it—good enough to forget, most days, that there had ever been anything else inside her. She had told herself the hiding was wisdom. She had told herself it was penance. She had told herself so many stories about the smallness of her life that she'd almost believed safety meant disappearing.

But Pip was crouched at her feet with an arrow hole in her sleeve, and Torv was bleeding from a graze along his jaw, and Odessa stood unarmed in front of armed men, and somewhere in the dark the twins were running with dogs or arrows behind them, and every single one of them was in this because of her.

Because she had been no one, and no one had cost them everything.

The sergeant raised his hand. Ten bows came up as one.

"No," Mara said.

She said it quietly, and the river heard her.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe it to herself and fail. What she remembered was not a decision. It was a door opening that she had nailed shut so long ago she'd forgotten where the hinges were.

The pressure in the air reversed. Every rider felt it—she saw it hit them, saw horses plant their feet and roll their eyes, saw the sergeant's torch gutter sideways though there was no wind yet. The world went still the way it does before hail, a held-breath stillness, and then Mara reached up—not with her hands, with everything she was—and pulled the sky down.

The cloud came off the mountaintop like a wave off a reef. It hadn't existed thirty seconds before; she made it out of the river's breath and the cold coming down from the peaks, and it boiled together above the riders, black-bellied, lit from within by a flicker that had nothing to do with lightning yet.

Then the first bolt fell.

It struck the ground between two riders and the world went white. Thunder didn't follow so much as detonate, a sound she felt in her teeth, in the stone under her palms, in Pip's small gasp beside her. Rain came down all at once, a wall of it, fat and cold and driven sideways, and the river seemed to rise to meet it, spray whipping up until the whole ford was a grey roaring chaos.

Mara walked out from behind the boulder.

She hadn't decided to do that either. Her body simply moved, and the storm moved with her, and for the first time in seven years she stopped fighting the thing she was.

Lightning walked ahead of her steps, striking where she looked, precise as fingers. A rider's torch burst apart. Two horses screamed and bolted downstream, dumping their riders into the shallows. The sergeant's formation—which had been a disciplined crescent—was suddenly ten separate men fighting animals that wanted nothing on earth except to be anywhere else.

*Mine,* the storm said, in the old language of pressure and charge, the language the order had taught her and then tried to chain. *Say the word.*

And oh, the word was right there. She could feel it waiting behind her teeth like a hot coin: the call that would fill the whole valley, that would bring down the icefields, that would turn this river into a flood and these soldiers into flotsam. Seven years of swallowing that word, and now it sat on her tongue tasting of freedom.

For one long, suspended heartbeat, Mara Quintana stood in the middle of her own storm and understood exactly why her order had feared her.

Then she saw the sergeant dragging himself out of the river, coughing, alive. Saw a young rider—barely older than Pip—clinging to a root on the far bank, white-faced, his bow floating away. Saw Odessa standing untouched in the rain with her arms spread, looking up at the storm with an expression Mara couldn't read and didn't have time to.

*Not anger,* she reminded herself, the way she'd reminded herself a thousand times at her trial. *Never anger.*

She squeezed the storm instead of opening it. Reined it in the way you'd rein a horse at a gallop—hard, costly, muscles screaming. The lightning thinned. The rain slackened from a wall to a curtain to a drizzle. The black cloud unraveled overhead, and the last thing it did before it died was throw one final, theatrical bolt into the empty scree slope, scattering the reserve riders she hadn't even known were there.

Then it was gone, and the night was just night again, dripping and steaming, and Mara stood alone in the middle of the ford with her glove burned open and her sigil bare to the sky, glowing like a dying coal.

Ten riders fled. She counted them go—one limping, one dragged, one weeping with rage or terror, she couldn't tell. The sergeant was the last to turn his horse. He looked back at her across the ruined ford, and he didn't look afraid.

He looked like a man memorizing a face.

* * *

They found each other by degrees in the aftermath, the way survivors do. Sef first, soaked to the ribs and grinning like a madman, with Illo appearing out of the dark a minute later wearing the expression of someone determined never to admit she'd been scared. Then Torv, jaw crusted with blood, carrying Pip on his shoulders because the girl's ankle had turned on the rocks. Odessa last, walking out of the treeline dry—or drier than any of them, which Mara noticed and filed away among the thousand small mysteries the priest carried.

Nobody spoke for a while. The fire they finally dared to build, tucked deep in a hollow above the ford, crackled into a silence nobody seemed to know how to break.

It was Pip who broke it.

"You called the storm." The girl was wrapped in Torv's cloak, her ankle propped on a stone, staring at Mara with an intensity that would have been rude in anyone without her courage. "Out of the sky. Like the stories. Like the *order* stories."

"Pip," Torv rumbled.

"No. I watched my father get dragged away by soldiers and nobody did anything, and then she—" Pip's voice cracked, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything. "She pulled lightning out of the *sky*, and everyone's going to sit here and pretend we're talking about the weather?"

Mara looked into the fire. Her palm lay open in her lap, the sigil raw and visible, still faintly warm. She'd stopped hiding it. There didn't seem to be much point anymore.

"Yes," she said. "I called it."

The confession landed heavier than she expected. Saying it aloud made it real in a way seven years of secrecy never had. Somewhere downstream, Kessrin's riders were carrying a story toward their warlord: a woman at the ford, a marked hand, a storm that obeyed her. By morning the story would be in three garrisons. By week's end it would be in a hundred mouths, growing teeth as it traveled.

And it wouldn't stop with Kessrin. That was the part that sat in her stomach like swallowed ice. The order had hunters. The order had *always* had hunters—for deserters, for apostates, for the unbound. And there was no description in the world more precise than the one she'd just written for them in fire across the sky.

"You saved us," Odessa said quietly. All eyes turned to her. The priest was watching Mara with that unreadable expression again, and there was something in it now that might have been grief. "You should know what that costs. Not the power. Power is cheap. The *hiding*. You've lost it, child, and you will not get it back. Whatever comes next comes to you openly."

"I know."

"Do you?" Odessa leaned forward, firelight carving the hollows of her face. "Do you understand that the people hunting you will stop being men with orders and start being something worse? Do you understand that some of them loved you once?"

Mara's breath caught. "What did you say?"

But Odessa only shook her head and looked into the flames, and whatever she knew stayed behind her eyes, locked up with all the rest.

Sef cleared his throat. "So," he said, with the desperate brightness of a man steering a conversation away from a cliff edge, "our plan was to sneak across the mountains unnoticed. As a group containing a legendary storm-witch, a defrocked priest, a fugitive dwarf, and a child. In retrospect, our planning may have had flaws."

"Speak for yourself," Illo said. "My plan was to take the map and sell it in Lowmarket. Also flawed. Everything's flawed. Lowmarket's ash."

"We need a new plan," Torv said. "One that starts with the truth that every soldier, spy, and priest between here and the coast now knows exactly what travels with us." He looked at Mara, and his gruff face did something complicated—anger and respect wrestling to a draw. "You should have told us."

"Yes," Mara said.

"You should have told us *before* the arrows."

"Yes."

Torv held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, heavily, and that was the end of it. That was the whole trial. Mara felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to name.

Pip scooted closer, cloak and all, until her shoulder pressed against Mara's arm. "Does it hurt?" the girl asked, nodding at the sigil. "When you use it?"

"Always has."

"Will you teach me?"

Mara laughed—a short, startled sound, the third laugh in three days, and this one didn't frighten her at all. "No, little one. Pray you never need to learn."

She should have felt triumphant. Ten riders broken, her people alive, the storm answering her call after seven years of silence like a dog that had never once forgotten its name. Exhilaration was still in her blood; she could feel it singing in her fingertips, in the cooling sigil, in the taste of ozone that wouldn't leave her tongue.

But underneath the singing, cold and rising, was the other thing. The fear. Because exhilaration faded, and fear kept. Somewhere south, a warlord was learning her name. Somewhere north, in towers she'd sworn never to think about, bells might already be ringing.

And Odessa had asked if she understood that some of the people coming for her had loved her once.

Mara stared into the fire and found she couldn't make herself ask what the priest meant. Some doors, she knew now, didn't stay nailed shut forever.

High above the hollow, past the drifting smoke, the clouds were clearing—and through the gap, far to the north over the jagged spine of Thornfell, the sky flickered. Once. Twice. Silent, distant, wrong.

Storm-light where no storm should be.

Torv saw it too. The dwarf went very still, and when he spoke, his voice was low.

"That's no weather," he said. "That's a signal."
