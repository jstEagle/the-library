## 10. Beneath the Thornfell Roots

The door was hidden behind a waterfall of scree on the north face of the Thornfell, and it looked like nothing at all — a slab of granite with a crack down the middle that might have been lightning damage or might have been a seam. Torv stood before it in the grey pre-dawn light, breath steaming, and put his broad palm flat against the stone.

"Been shut since my grandfather's grandfather," he said. "Maybe longer. The clans sealed the deep roads when the last of the vault-work was done." He paused. "You understand what I'm saying. The tunnels we're about to walk were dug by the same hands that built the thing you're carrying us toward."

"I know," Mara said.

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway."

Torv grunted, which from him was as good as a blessing, and began to sing.

It wasn't much of a song — three low notes, repeated, more vibration than melody — but the stone answered. The crack widened with a grinding sigh, dust sifting down like flour, until there was a gap just wide enough for one person sideways. Cold air breathed out of it, old air, air that hadn't touched the sky in centuries. It smelled of iron and deep water and something faintly sweet, like embers long dead.

Pip peered into the dark with the particular delight of an eleven-year-old being offered exactly the wrong kind of adventure. "It's a mouth," she said happily. "The mountain's a mouth."

"That's a comforting thought," Sef said dryly. "Thank you for sharing it."

Mara went first.

* * *

For the first hour they walked in single file behind her torch, down a passage cut so precisely that the walls still showed the chisel-marks of dwarven masons dead a thousand years. The floor sloped gently downward. Somewhere below them water moved — a sound like breathing, slow and vast.

Mara kept her left hand closed. Even here, even this far from the map's coordinates, the sigil had begun to warm. Not burning yet. Warming. Like a hand laid over hers in greeting.

She didn't tell anyone. She told herself it was because there'd be time to worry later, which was a lie she'd been telling herself for seven years, and she knew it, and she told it anyway.

Behind her: the shuffle of boots, Odessa murmuring a prayer under her breath that sounded less like devotion and more like argument, Illo counting her steps out loud because silence made her twitchy, Aldric at the rear with his sword drawn and his grey wool cloak already greyer with tunnel dust. Bren's daughter between Sef and Torv, where everyone had silently agreed she belonged.

"You okay?" Sef asked Pip, and Mara heard the girl answer something cheerful, and heard Sef laugh, and felt something in her chest pull tight and strange.

That was the trouble with people. They got behind you in the dark and you started listening for their footsteps, started keeping count, started caring whether all six sets kept moving. Anonymity never demanded anything like that. Anonymity never woke up screaming.

"Left fork coming," Torv called from the middle. "Take the left. Right goes down to the drowned levels and I don't fancy swimming."

"How do you know any of this?" Odessa asked. "You said yourself the deep roads have been sealed since—"

"My clan kept the songs." His voice went quieter, almost embarrassed. "Not the reasons. Just the songs. Turn-by-turn, verse by verse. We thought they were only work-songs. Then your courier friend came through Lowmarket asking after Ember Vault, and my mother sat me down and sang me every verse twice, and told me to remember."

Mara glanced back at him. In the torchlight his craggy face looked older than it had a week ago. "Your mother knew what the songs were for?"

"She knew they'd matter to somebody, someday. That's how the clans think. A thousand years is a short memory if you're stone." He tapped the wall beside him with two knuckles, almost affectionate. "Take the left."

* * *

They camped in a chamber where the ceiling opened into a natural chimney, and through some crack too distant to see, a thread of daylight came down and pooled on the floor like spilled honey. It was the only light. They didn't dare a fire — smoke traveled in tunnels; Torv said stone remembered smoke longer than it remembered voices.

So they ate cold sausage and hard bread in a wedge of borrowed sun, and Mara found herself watching them instead of watching the dark, which was its own kind of dangerous.

Illo had fallen asleep mid-sentence, head tipped against Sef's shoulder, her cards still fanned in her fingers. Sef hadn't moved. He'd been holding still for two hours now, arm going numb probably, so his sister could sleep. Odessa had produced a needle and was mending Pip's coat hem, muttering scripture at the torn seam like she could shame it whole. Aldric stood watch at the passage mouth, and when Pip padded over to ask him if knights ever got bored, he crouched down to her height to answer, and stayed crouched, and let her braid a bit of tunnel-grass into his sword-belt without complaint.

Nobody had asked Mara to hold anything together. Nobody had asked her anything at all. They had simply arranged themselves around her like she was a hearth.

She hated how much she wanted it.

"The sigil's warmer," Odessa said quietly, beside her. Not looking at her. Working her needle. Of course the defrocked priest noticed everything; noticing was the only sacrament she had left.

"A little."

"Warmer means closer."

"Closer means everything wants us dead faster." Mara turned her palm toward the honeyed light. The ember-mark glowed faintly through the skin, orange as coals under ash. "I keep thinking about burning the map. We could go north. Over the Thornfell, past the border, start over somewhere the word 'vault' means nothing. Pip would grow up. Torv could build houses. You could—" She stopped. "It's not nothing, what we'd be leaving behind. I know that now. But it isn't everything either."

Odessa bit her thread and tied it off. "No," she said. "It isn't everything. But it's enough to make liars of us, running. And I've been a liar a long time, and I'll tell you the truth about lying: eventually you run out of road, and everything you buried is standing right there, waiting to be introduced." She packed away the needle. "Better to walk toward it while your friends can still see your face."

"That's very wise."

"I'm also a drunk and a fraud, so weigh it accordingly."

Mara laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound that echoed off the ancient stone — and realized she couldn't remember the last time. Before the exile. Maybe before the trial.

Above them, far up the chimney, rocks clattered.

Everyone was awake instantly, no words needed. Sef gathered Illo. Aldric drew steel without a whisper of sound. Torv pressed his ear to the wall like a man listening for a heartbeat, and his face did something Mara had never seen it do.

It went afraid.

"They're in the upper galleries," he said. "More than one party." He held up fingers. "Boots — heavy, armored, marching in step. That's soldiers. Kessrin's." Then, softer, worse: "And something else. No boots. Bare feet, or soft-soled, spread wide across the rock like—" He shook his head. "Like they're listening through the stone itself."

Storm-callers didn't wear boots in the field. Storm-callers ran silent and barefoot so they could feel the weather in the ground.

"Hunters," Mara said. Her own voice sounded far away. "My order's hunters. They found the tunnels."

"How?" Aldric demanded. "This entrance was sealed for centuries—"

"Because they're not following us." Mara closed her fist over the glowing sigil. "They're following *it*. The mark. It's a leash, remember? Wherever I go, they can feel it pulling." She looked around the little pool of daylight, at the five faces turned toward her — frightened, tired, and worst of all, trusting. "I led them straight to you. All of you. Every mile since Lowmarket."

Silence. Mara braced for it — the recrimination, the arithmetic of survival, someone saying what was obvious: *leave her, save yourselves.* She'd rehearsed the speech herself. She'd been rehearsing it since the inn, honestly. Since the frozen man in the pass.

It was Pip who broke the silence, tugging at Torv's sleeve. "Then we go where the leash doesn't reach. Right? There has to be a place like that. You know all the verses."

Torv looked down at her for a long moment. Then at Mara.

"There's the Sunken Stair," he said slowly. "Deep level. Old lava tube — the songs say it runs *through* the mountain's heart, through rock so hot and dense the order's listeners can't read it. Too loud. Too much fire in the stone." His jaw worked. "It's also half-collapsed, flooded in places, and the songs call it the Road of Regret for a reason. People who take it don't all come out."

"People who stay here definitely don't come out," Sef said. He nudged his sleeping sister awake with his boot. "Illo. Up. We're doing something stupid."

"We're always doing something stupid," Illo mumbled, already shuffling her cards. "Odds?"

"Slightly terrible."

"Then I'm in."

Aldric sheathed his sword and looked at Mara, and there was no accusation in his face at all, which somehow hurt more than accusation would have. "You didn't lead them to us," he said quietly. "You carried us out of Lowmarket on your back while they burned it. Whatever pulls them after you — that's their sin, not yours." A pause. "But if you want to spare us, the offer's noted, and refused. Permanently. Write it down."

Something cracked open in Mara's chest and she couldn't look at any of them for a moment, so she busied herself coiling rope, and her eyes stung, and she blamed the dust.

"To the Sunken Stair," she said finally. "Torv — lead."

* * *

The deep roads went down the way grief goes down: gradually, then all at once.

By the third day the worked stone gave way to raw tunnel, and the raw tunnel gave way to heat. The walls sweated. Veins of something luminous — Torv called it fireglass — threaded the rock and threw a dim red glow over everything, so they doused the torches and walked through the mountain's own bloodlight, shadows huge and wavering. The air tasted of sulfur. Sweat soaked through wool and leather. Pip rode on Torv's shoulders, listless with heat, and Odessa rationed the water drop by drop, counting each one aloud so no one could pretend not to notice the arithmetic getting worse.

They passed a collapsed gallery where bones jutted from the rubble — not dwarven bones, too long, too wrong-jointed — and nobody asked, and Torv sang a single low note at the stones as they crossed, the way you'd nod to a stranger at a funeral.

On the fourth day the floor ended.

Beyond it, the tunnel continued — visible by fireglass-light as a smooth glassy slope plunging into darkness, the walls fused to mirror-smoothness by some ancient cataclysm. At the bottom, far below, water gleamed black.

"The Stair," Torv said. "What's left of it."

"It's a slide," Pip said.

"It's a coffin with ambitions," Sef said.

They built a harness from rope and Illo's gambling winnings — literally; her silver belt-buckles made the best anchors, and she surrendered them with theatrical mourning. One at a time. Torv first, alone, to test the descent; his silhouette shrank down the glass slope and his voice came back up sounding tiny and satisfied: "Bottom's solid. Water's knee-deep. Come on, then, before I get lonely."

Odessa went second, praying the whole way down, loudly, in three languages.

Sef and Illo insisted on Pip between them, all three roped together, and watching them descend — the twins bracing the girl on either side, laughing at her shrieks as they picked up speed, controlling the fall with heels and rope and sheer practiced nerve — Mara felt the warmth of the sigil flare sharp against her ribs, and ignored it, and kept her eyes on the child until the child reached the bottom alive.

Which left her and Aldric at the top.

"You next," she said.

"After you."

"Aldric."

"Mara." He said her name like it cost him something, like he was still surprised he was allowed to spend it. "Seven years you've gone first into every dark place, because going first meant no one could see you flinch. I've watched you do it since Lowmarket. So." He stepped aside and gestured to the slope, courteous as a courtier. "Go first. And I'll be right behind you, close enough to catch you, which is more than anyone's offered you in seven years, I'd wager."

She stared at him. The fireglass painted him red as any ember.

"You're insufferable," she said.

"So I'm told."

She went. The slope took her and the world became speed and red light and the rope burning her gloved palms, and she heard his boots behind hers, close, exactly as promised, and at the bottom his hand caught her elbow before her balance failed, steady as bedrock.

"See?" he said. "Nothing to it."

Her heart was hammering and she was fairly sure it wasn't the slide.

* * *

They were an hour along the flooded bottom stretch — thigh-deep, wading in black water that steamed faintly, Pip perched again on shoulders, everyone's voice dropped to whispers that the tunnel drank greedily — when Mara felt it.

Not heard. Felt. Through the soles of her boots, through the water, through the stone.

A pulse. Rhythmic. Patient. Like footsteps taken barefoot on rock miles above, each one placed with a listener's care. And beneath it, threading down through the mountain's dense hot heart, a thin bright line of attention — finding her, touching the sigil, *pulling.*

She stopped dead. The water lapped. Behind her, Aldric's hand found her shoulder.

"They've reached the deep roads," she whispered.

"Yes," said a voice from ahead, out of the dark, and it was a voice she knew, and it stopped her heart colder than the water ever could. Calm. Dry. Familiar as her own reflection. "We reached them two days ago, Mara. You really should stop listening to the stone and start listening to what's inside it."

Torv's hand closed around a rock. Odessa's breath went ragged. The fireglass veins along the walls pulsed once, twice, brighter — and in that sudden red bloom, fifty paces ahead, standing on a shelf of stone above the black water, silhouetted against the mountain's burning blood—

Veyra's hunters. A dozen of them. Silent, barefoot, waiting.

And above them all, smiling down with seven years of patience in her face, the woman who had once held Mara's hands while the sigil burned into them.

"Hello, little storm," Archon Veyra said. "We need to talk."
