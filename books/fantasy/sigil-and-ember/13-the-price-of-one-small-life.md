## 13. The Price of One Small Life

The river called the Weep ran shallow and grey through the gorge below Ravenmoor, and Kessrin's men had chosen the crossing well—a stone bridge no wider than a cart, cliffs on either side, nowhere to run that wasn't down.

Mara stood on the near bank with the map pressed flat against her ribs beneath her coat, and Pip's hand locked in hers, and the taste of a night without sleep in her mouth.

"He'll bring her out," Aldric said beside her. He said it the way he said everything now—quietly, like a man laying stones across a stream. "Kessrin wants his prize more than he wants a dead child. Dead children make bad bargains."

"Dead children make excellent leverage," Odessa murmured from behind them. She had not slept either. None of them had. The fire at the waystation had burned down to ash while they argued about whether to answer Kessrin's messenger at all, and by the time dawn came, the argument had answered itself: Pip had been taken from the outhouse while Torv was watching the road, taken clean and quiet as a knife from a sheath, and there was nothing left to argue about.

Mara flexed her right hand inside its glove. The sigil burned like a coal wrapped in wool. It had been burning for three days now, ever since the tunnels, ever since the vault gate had flared under her palm and the horizon had gone the color of a bruise. She wondered if Kessrin could feel it too, wherever he stood. She wondered if it sang to him the way it sometimes sang to her.

"Don't do anything stupid," Sef said.

She turned. He was leaning against the bridge's first pillar, arms crossed, wearing the lazy half-smile he wore over dice and daggers and everything else in between. His twin stood a pace away, not smiling at all. Illo never smiled when Sef was about to do something, which Mara had learned meant Illo always knew first.

"I'm not going to do anything stupid," Mara said.

"You're going to trade the end of the world for one kid." He shrugged. "Which is stupid. Which is also why I'm here."

"Sef—"

"Relax." He pushed off the pillar and straightened his collar with two fingers, a gambler dressing for a table where he already knew the dealer. "Kessrin's men will count us before they count anything else. Six against however many they brought. They'll be watching your hands, the knight's sword, the priest's sermons. Nobody watches the twins. That's not stupidity, Quintana. That's arithmetic."

Mara looked at Illo. Illo looked at the river.

"It's a bad table," Illo said finally. "But he's played worse."

* * *

They came across the bridge at midmorning: eight soldiers in Kessrin's rust-and-iron colors, pikes shouldered, and between them a small figure in an oversized coat with her wrists bound in front of her and her chin up like she was leading the procession instead of being dragged through it.

Pip.

Something in Mara's chest tore loose and would not go back where it belonged. Seven years she had kept herself small. Seven years of being no one, of walking past burning villages because stopping meant dying, of telling herself that safety was a room with only one chair in it. And then Bren's laugh in the smoke of Lowmarket, and Pip's cold little hand finding hers in the dark, and now this—the whole architecture of her exile collapsed into a girl of eleven standing on a bridge with rope on her wrists.

The soldier in front lifted a gauntleted hand. "Lord Kessrin's terms. The marked woman walks halfway. Alone. She gives the map to Captain Dree, there." He nodded at a lean man with a crossbow slung across his back. "The girl walks the other way. No one crosses the middle of the bridge but the two of you. If anyone follows, if anyone so much as breathes wrong—" he gestured lazily at the cliff tops, where Mara could see, now that she looked, the glint of glass and steel. Crossbows. A dozen at least, maybe more.

"And if I refuse?" Mara asked.

"Then we keep the girl and come back for you tomorrow, and the day after, until there's nothing left of your company to bargain with." The soldier smiled under his helm. "Lord Kessrin is patient. He says you'll learn to be."

Mara pulled off her glove. She held up her bare hand, let the sigil show itself to all of them—black ember-lines coiled in her palm, faintly glowing, like a brand that hadn't finished deciding what shape it wanted to be. She heard the soldiers shift. Even at forty paces, even across running water, they knew what it was. Stories traveled faster than armies.

"The map for the girl," she said. "Nothing else changes hands."

"That's the terms exactly." The soldier turned and barked an order, and the line parted, and Pip came stumbling forward three steps before a hand on her shoulder stopped her at the bridge's midpoint.

"Mara." Pip's voice carried thin and clear over the water. She wasn't crying. Bren's daughter, through and through. "Don't give them anything. I can run fast."

"I know you can," Mara said, and stepped onto the bridge.

* * *

The stone was slick with spray. Mara counted her steps the way she counted everything now—out of habit, out of the old smuggler's discipline—and at twelve steps she stopped, ten feet short of the midpoint, and waited.

Captain Dree crossed to meet her. Up close he was younger than his crossbow made him look, with a wispy attempt at a beard and eyes that kept flicking to her open palm.

"The map," he said.

"Girl first."

"She walks when I have it."

"Then we stand here until the river freezes." Mara kept her voice level. Beneath her fear, beneath the sigil's heat, there was a strange calm settling over her, the calm she'd felt once before, years ago, standing in the order's amphitheater with storms screaming overhead. Some part of her had already done this arithmetic. Some part of her had known since the messenger arrived that every road from this bridge led somewhere terrible.

Dree's jaw worked. Then he whistled, low, and on the far bank a soldier cut Pip's wrist-bonds and shoved her forward.

Pip walked. Head up, shoulders back, eleven years old and furious at the world. She passed the midpoint. She passed Dree. She was six steps away, five, four—

Mara pulled the map from her coat.

It was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and she did it anyway: the folded leather she held out was the courier's oilskin wrapping, blank inside, the real map sewn into the lining of her coat where it had been since the waystation. It would buy thirty seconds. Maybe less. Thirty seconds was enough for Pip to reach the rocks; thirty seconds was enough for Mara to decide whether she could kill eight men and a captain before a dozen crossbows on the clifftops decided it for her.

Dree grabbed the wrapping, tore it open, and his face went through surprise and understanding in the space of a heartbeat.

He was fast. She'd give him that. The crossbow came off his back and up in one motion, and the bolt took Mara high in the left shoulder and spun her half around, and the world went white and ringing, and through the white she heard Pip scream her name.

And she heard Sef say, cheerfully, terribly: "Hey! Over here!"

* * *

Later, Mara would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. Everyone would have their own version. This much was true:

Sef had crossed the bridge behind her. Not at the midpoint, not slowly—he'd come along the outer edge, cat-footed, the way he moved toward any table where the odds were long, and when Dree raised the crossbow Sef had thrown himself between the bolt and the girl who wasn't even his to protect.

No. That wasn't true either. The bolt hit Mara first. Sef's throw came after—a knife, spinning, taking Dree through the throat—and then the clifftops bloomed with crossbow-fire and the morning filled with the sound of it, and Sef grabbed Pip by the collar and the back of her coat both and simply threw her, threw her bodily off the bridge's edge onto the rocks below where Illo was already waiting with his arms out, and then he turned back toward Mara with that same lazy smile starting—

—and the second volley came down like weather.

Three bolts found him. Mara saw it from her knees on the wet stone, her own shoulder pouring heat, saw him rock backward with his arms spread, almost like a man greeting rain. He sat down on the bridge as though tired. He looked at his chest with mild professional interest, the way he looked at a losing hand.

"Well," Sef said. "That's a fold."

Mara crawled to him. She didn't remember deciding to crawl; her body simply went, dragging her ruined shoulder across the stone, and she got a hand under his head and held it, and his blood came warm through her fingers, faster than blood should come.

"Shut up," she said. "Shut up, press here, we'll get you—Torv carries people, he carries beams, he can carry you—"

"Quintana." His hand came up and caught her wrist, and even now his grip was a gambler's grip, light and sure. "Listen. The middle of the bridge. You were supposed to stay at the middle of the bridge."

"You were supposed to stay on the bank."

"Yeah." He coughed, and the cough cost him something visible. "Bad habit. Chasing long shots." His eyes drifted past her, found Pip on the rocks below, alive, screaming, held down by Illo's arms. Something in his face unclenched. "There she goes. Told you. Arithmetic."

"Sef—"

"Hey." His voice dropped, and the smile finally fell off it, and underneath the smile he was just a young man from Lowmarket with ink stains on his fingers from marking cards. "Tell Illo it wasn't his fault. Tell him—" A breath. "Tell him he gets the good boots."

His hand slipped off her wrist.

Mara held him anyway, and shouted anyway, for Torv, for Odessa, for anyone, for the storm that lived in her bones—but the storm didn't come. It never came when she begged. It only ever came when she stopped asking, and she could not stop asking, could not stop, because Sef was cold under her hands and the river kept talking its grey indifferent talk and somewhere below Pip was screaming her name again and again and again.

The soldiers didn't advance. They didn't need to. They'd gotten what they came to measure: how much the marked woman would bleed for one small life. When Mara finally looked up, through the haze, through the rain that had begun to fall without her calling it, the far bank was emptying. Captain Dree lay where Sef's knife had put him. The rest were withdrawing, unhurried, carrying their pikes like men whose work was done.

They left the body of their own captain where he fell. Kessrin's arithmetic had no column for the dead.

* * *

They buried Sef at dusk above the gorge, where the wind came through the pines and made a sound like shuffling cards.

Illo dug most of the grave himself and would let no one help. Torv had carried the body up from the bridge in his arms, all the way, refusing the litter twice, and now he stood at the graveside with his great hands hanging empty at his sides, looking smaller than Mara had ever seen him. Odessa spoke the old rites, the ones from before her defrocking, in a language none of them knew but all of them needed. Aldric stood at attention in full armor, helmet under his arm, a knight honoring a thief, and did not move for the entire hour.

Pip did not cry. That was the worst of it. She stood with her fists clenched and her jaw set and her eyes dry and burning, and when the earth went in she whispered, "I told you I could run fast," to nobody, to the grave, to the world.

It was after, around the fire that no one wanted but everyone huddled toward, that the family broke.

"You gave him a blank," Illo said.

Mara looked up. He sat across the flames with Sef's boots—Sef's good boots—in his lap, holding them like something he'd forgotten how to put down.

"The map," Illo said. "You handed Dree a blank. You planned it. You planned to cheat a man with a crossbow at ten feet with a child on the line, and my brother paid your stake." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it. It stayed flat and careful, a man counting losses. "He followed you onto that bridge because you taught him it was worth it. Because you looked at one kid and decided she was worth more than sense."

"Illo." Odessa's voice, low.

"No. Let her hear it." He leaned forward. Firelight caught the wet on his face, the only tears he would allow. "We pulled you out of a snowdrift seven days ago. We fed you. We walked you into a hole in the ground because you said it mattered. And in four days I've watched you get Bren taken, get us hunted, and now—" His throat closed. He looked down at the boots. "Now this."

"Illo, that's enough." Aldric, quietly.

"It isn't enough. Nothing's enough." But he subsided, folding in on himself around the boots.

"It was my fault," Pip said suddenly, from the dark beyond the firelight. Everyone turned. She stood at the edge of the circle, small and rigid. "If I hadn't been taken—"

"You stop that right now." Torv's voice, gravel and iron. He crossed to her and crouched, which for Torv was an act of devotion. "Child. Look at me. Men with crossbows chose to loose them. That sin sits on their side of the river, and no one else's. Not yours." A pause. His voice roughened. "Not hers, either."

"Not hers?" Illo laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Torv, she hesitated. At the exchange. I saw it. One breath—one breath where she couldn't decide—and that breath is where Sef died. Her softness got him killed as sure as the bolts did."

"And her softness is why any of us are still breathing!" Torv surged up. "Who pulled you out of the flood at Graywater? Who talked the toll-wardens down at Cindermoot when you two were cheating them blind? Who sat up three nights with Pip after Lowmarket burned? Softness, boy. You've been living on it for a week and crying about the price."

"Enough." Mara stood.

Her shoulder screamed. She ignored it. The sigil in her palm had gone hot enough that she could smell her own glove scorching, and she realized distantly that the fire had begun to lean toward her, every flame in the ring bending like grass in wind, and she made herself breathe, made herself let it go.

"I don't regret it," she said. "Understand me. All of you. If I stood on that bridge a thousand times, I trade myself for Pip a thousand times. That's not softness. That's the only thing I've ever been sure of." Her voice cracked and she let it. "But Illo's right about one thing. My hesitation cost seconds, and seconds are blood. That's mine to carry. I won't put it down and I won't share it."

Silence. The fire crackled and straightened, released from her.

"So what now?" Illo asked at last, hollow. "We just keep walking? Toward the thing that wants her hand? With Kessrin behind us and your old sisters ahead of us?"

"Yes," Mara said.

"Why?"

Because Sef chased long shots. Because Bren would have. Because the alternative was a room with one chair, and she had lived in that room for seven years and it had saved no one. But what she said was simpler, and truer:

"Because the map leads somewhere, and I'm starting to think it doesn't lead where any of us think it does."

She turned to Odessa. The old priest had been silent through all of it, hands folded, face unreadable, watching Mara with an expression she couldn't name—grief, yes, but underneath the grief something older and more afraid. Guilt, maybe. The look of a woman watching a debt come due.

"Odessa," Mara said. "When we reach the vault. Whatever's there. Whatever the sigil really is. You're going to tell me the truth. All of it. Tonight, if you can. Because I've buried enough lies for one lifetime."

The fire popped. Wind moved through the pines over Sef's grave.

Odessa closed her eyes, and when she opened them, Mara saw that the decision had already been made—had perhaps been made decades ago, in another life, by a woman with a different name.

"Not tonight," Odessa said quietly. "Tomorrow. On the road. There are things you need to see first." She reached into her robe and drew out something Mara had never seen her wear: a small iron key on a cord, black with age, shaped like a coiled storm.

"And Mara," she added, as the fire guttered low, "when I tell you—who burned the sigil into your hand, and why—I need you to remember that some of us bury things to keep them safe. Not to keep them hidden."

Above the gorge, far to the north, the sky flickered without thunder. Once. Twice. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The sigil in Mara's palm answered, and for the first time, it felt less like a key than like a leash—and she could no longer tell which end of it she was holding.
