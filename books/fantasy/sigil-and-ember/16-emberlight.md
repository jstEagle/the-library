## 16. Emberlight

The mountain held its breath.

Mara felt it in the stone beneath her boots, in the way the wind had gone strange and thin through the pass, as if the whole of Thornfell were leaning in to listen for what came at dawn. Tomorrow they would hold the tunnel mouth against an army and an order and a warlord with a god's leash in his fist. Tonight there was only firelight, and the company gathered around it like moths who had decided, finally, that burning was worth it.

She sat apart from them, as she always did, on a shelf of granite overlooking the vault road. Below, the outer gate of the Ember Vault glowed faintly through the rock — not light exactly, more like heat remembered by stone, a warmth that had no source and no end. Her palm answered it. It always answered now.

The sigil burned.

Not the old ache she'd carried for seven years, the low smolder she'd learned to ignore the way you ignore a bad tooth. This was alive. The ember-lines in her skin pulsed in time with something vast and slow beneath the mountain, a heartbeat the size of a sleeping god, and every pulse sent pain lancing up her arm to the elbow. She'd stopped pretending it didn't hurt somewhere around noon. She'd stopped hiding her hand entirely about an hour ago. There seemed little point anymore. Tomorrow everyone would see what she was, or what she'd been made into.

"You're supposed to be resting."

Aldric's voice, behind her. She didn't turn. She'd known he would come; he always came, eventually, wearing that expression of a man who had sworn an oath to kill her and then misplaced the oath somewhere between his duty and his heart.

"So are you," she said. "Knights need sleep too."

"Ex-knights." He lowered himself onto the granite beside her, armor off, grey wool and travel leathers, looking less like the man sent to execute her and more like something softer and more dangerous. "I burned my orders, remember. I'm nobody's anything now."

"Liar. You're Torv's pack-mule and Pip's climbing frame and Odessa's favorite argument. That's plenty of somebodies."

He huffed — almost a laugh. In the firelight below, Sef's twin Illo was dealing cards to a circle that included a defrocked priest, a dwarf mason, and a nine-year-old girl who cheated shamelessly. The sound of their arguing drifted up the slope, thin and bright against the dark.

Aldric reached for her hand.

He did it without asking, which was new, and which she let happen, which was newer. He turned her palm toward the firelight, careful as a man handling a wounded bird, and Mara watched him look at the sigil — the branching lines of ember-gold sunk into her skin, glowing now, actually glowing, throwing faint orange light up onto both their faces.

"It's worse," he said.

"It's louder."

"Mara."

"I know." She flexed her fingers. The light moved with them, beautiful and awful, like holding a coal that loved her. "It's worse. Happy?"

"No." His jaw tightened. He dug into his satchel one-handed, never letting go of her, and produced a small clay pot and a strip of clean linen. "Odessa gave me this. Comfrey and something she wouldn't name. She said it draws heat out of a burn. She also said—" he paused, and something flickered across his face, "—she said to tell you she's sorry. For what she buried. For how long she let you carry it alone."

Mara looked out at the dark. "Tell her I'm still deciding whether to forgive her."

"She said you'd say that. She said to tell you she'll wait."

* * *

He worked in silence, and the silence was its own kind of confession.

Aldric Vane had been raised in a knightly order that valued three things: precision, obedience, and the proper care of one's hands. So he cleaned the sigil-hand the way he'd once cleaned his own sword after a battle — thoroughly, gently, without flinching when it hurt her, though he winced every time she did. The salve was cool going on and then warm, drawing the worst of the fire down out of her skin. When he wrapped the linen, he left her fingers free, because he'd noticed — of course he'd noticed — that she hated being unable to move them.

"My mother was a healer," he said, tying off the bandage. "Before I was taken for training. I used to watch her do this. Village burns, scythes slipping, children who'd grabbed a kettle. She had a way of talking while she worked so you'd forget to be afraid." A pause. "I never learned the talking part."

"You're doing fine."

"I'm doing badly. I've been trying to find the words since the shrine, and I keep—" He stopped. Started again. "At the shrine, when I drew on you. Do you remember what you said?"

"You mean before or after you pointed a sword at me?"

"After."

Mara closed her eyes, remembering. The little roadside shrine, moss-eaten, its god long forgotten. Aldric with his blade drawn and his orders in his breast pocket and his hands shaking — she hadn't understood then why they shook. And her, exhausted, hunted, holding out the bloodstained map like a gambler showing a losing hand, saying: *Read it. If you still want to kill me after you understand what this is, then do it.*

"You told me to read it first," he said. "Nobody in my entire life had ever asked me to think before obeying. Not my father. Not my order. Not my king." He turned her bandaged hand over in both of his, studying it like scripture. "You handed a condemned woman's map to her executioner and trusted him with it. I have been trying to deserve that trust ever since, and I want you to know I intend to keep deserving it tomorrow, when it gets hard."

"It's going to get very hard tomorrow."

"Yes."

"They might all die. We might all die."

"Yes."

"And you're sitting here wrapping my hand."

His thumb moved over her knuckles, slow. "Where else would I be?"

The question landed in her chest like a dropped stone hitting still water. Mara Quintana had spent seven years perfecting the art of being no one, of needing nothing, of walking away before anyone could walk away from her. She had a whole architecture built for solitude, load-bearing walls of silence, and this man kept putting his shoulder against them without even noticing they were there.

"Aldric." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "There's something I should tell you. Before tomorrow. In case."

"In case," he agreed quietly.

"The storm offered me something. Back in the vault, when we touched the gate. It spoke — or it did whatever storms do instead of speaking. It offered to make me matter. To make all of it — the exile, the hiding, the years — worth something. Unlimited power, if I opened fully to it. Became its vessel completely." She watched his face. "And the terrible thing, the thing I need you to know, is that for one moment I wanted it. Not the power. The *mattering*. Seven years of being no one, and here was something offering to make me the most someone who ever lived."

"What did you say?"

"I said no. But wanting it shames me."

"Don't let it." He said it simply, without performance, and she believed him because Aldric lied badly and knew it. "I spent ten years wanting my father's approval. He died before I earned it. Wanting things isn't the sin, Mara. It's what you refuse to trade for them that tells you who you are." His grip tightened fractionally. "You refused. With everything on the line, you refused. I've seen knights with lifetimes of honor fail that test."

Below them, Pip shrieked with laughter — she'd clearly won a hand she shouldn't have won, or lost one on purpose to make Sef crow too soon. Torv's rumbling bass rolled up the slope like distant thunder, and Mara realized she was smiling.

"When I was on trial," she said, "seven years ago, Archon Veyra asked me one question before the sentence. She asked: *If your gift could save ten thousand lives by binding one storm into a weapon, would you refuse?* And I said yes, I'd refuse, and she looked at me like I'd failed some final test, and maybe I had, by her measure." She turned her hand in Aldric's, feeling the bandage, feeling the heat beneath it, patient and enormous. "But I've been thinking about that question all day. Because tomorrow I might call a storm bigger than any weapon the order ever dreamed of. And the difference isn't the size of it. The difference is that I'll be calling it *for* these people, not *with* them as targets. That's the whole difference. That's everything."

"That's why you'll win," Aldric said.

"Or die."

"Or die," he agreed. "But you'll win. I've decided. I'm very good at deciding things; it was most of my training."

She laughed — really laughed, for the first time in days, and the sound surprised her coming out of her own mouth, rusty and unguarded. Aldric watched her laugh with an expression she'd only ever seen him wear once before, at the shrine, right before he fed his orders to the flames.

"Mara." His voice had changed. Lower. Careful. "There's something I should tell you too. In case."

The wind moved through the pass above them, carrying the smell of far-off snow and nearer smoke. Beneath the mountain, the slow vast heartbeat pulsed, and her palm pulsed with it, and none of it mattered as much as the next ten seconds.

"I was sent to kill you," he said. "You know that. What you don't know is that I hesitated the first time I saw you — before the courier, before the map, before any of it. In Lowmarket, at the well. You were buying apples and haggling like your life depended on it, and you laughed at something the fruit-seller said, and I stood there like an idiot thinking: *there's a person who has already decided who she is.* I'd never met anyone like that. I'd spent my whole life being told who I was." He exhaled. "So when the orders came, I told myself the hesitation was tactical. Then I told myself it was doubt. It took me until the shrine to name it honestly."

"Name it, then," Mara said softly. "In case."

He looked at her. Firelight and sigil-light, gold and ember, moving on his face.

"I love you," Aldric Vane said, plainly, the way he did everything once he'd finally decided — no hedging, no retreat, no clever escape route left open. "Not the way knights love causes or kings love kingdoms. Plainly. I love you, and I am telling you now because if tomorrow takes either of us, I will not have it said that I died with this unsaid. That's all. That's the whole of it. You don't have to—"

"Aldric."

"—say anything, I know the timing is—"

"*Aldric.*" She took his face in her bandaged hand, heat bleeding through the linen, and kissed him.

The mountain went on breathing beneath them. The company argued over cards. Somewhere far below, a god stirred in its chains. And on a shelf of granite above it all, two exiles held onto each other with the particular ferocity of people who had each, separately, spent years believing they would die alone — and had been wrong.

When she pulled back, her eyes were wet and she was smiling and she didn't try to hide either thing.

"There," she said. "Now it's said."

"Now it's said," he agreed, hoarse.

* * *

They went down to the fire together, and the company made room without making a point of it, which was its own kind of grace.

Illo dealt them in. Odessa sat across the flames with her defrocked priest's hands folded, watching Mara with an expression that was apology and hope braided together, and when their eyes met, Odessa inclined her head — a small motion, a vow renewed. *I'll wait,* she'd said. Mara nodded back. Not forgiveness yet. But a door, unlocked.

Torv was mending a crack in his hammer-haft with wire and patience, muttering dwarven engineering curses. "Tomorrow," he said around the wire in his teeth, "I bring the pass down behind us. Sealed in. All of us. No retreat for anyone — them or us." He spat the wire free and looked around the fire, at each face in turn. "I want to hear no complaints about that. I chose this company. I don't build things to fall down, and I don't stand in tunnels with people I'd leave behind."

"Sentimental old rock," said Sef.

"Gambling fool," said Torv, with tremendous affection.

Pip crawled into the space between Mara and Aldric, uninvited, entitled, nine years old and carrying a wooden practice knife Bren had given her a lifetime ago. She leaned against Mara's side and yawned enormously.

"Are you scared?" Pip asked, with the directness of children and the deafness of children to their own cruelty.

Mara considered lying. Then she considered what Bren would have wanted her to say, and what Pip deserved to hear.

"Yes," she said. "Terrified. But I'll tell you a secret about fear. It's just love with nowhere to go yet. Tomorrow it goes somewhere."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. "Then I must love a lot of people," she concluded, "because I'm extremely scared," and fell asleep against Mara's ribs before anyone could answer.

The fire settled. Illo quietly collected the cards. Sef draped his cloak over the twins' shared bedroll and lay staring at the stars with an expression Mara had learned to read as grief for his brother's death, worn openly now instead of drowned in dice. Odessa began, very softly, to sing — an old hymn, one the order had sung before Mara's time, except Odessa sang it differently, slower, the way you sing a lament rather than a creed. Torv's hammer-hand stilled. Even the wind seemed to listen.

Aldric's arm came around Mara's shoulders. Against her hip, Pip breathed slow and deep. Across the fire, her strange, impossible, found family kept vigil in their separate silences, all of them awake, all of them choosing to stay.

Mara looked at her bandaged palm, glowing faintly through the linen, pulsing with the heartbeat under the world.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *Whatever you are, whatever I was made to be — you don't get them. They're mine.*

Far to the south, past the sealed pass, past the dark horizons, torches were moving. Hundreds of them. Kessrin's vanguard had entered the valley, and among the columns, at their head, rode figures in storm-grey robes whose leader lifted her face to the mountain and smiled.

Archon Veyra had come home.

And deep beneath Thornfell, the Ember Vault heard her arrive — and began, at last, to wake.
