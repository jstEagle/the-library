## 20. Aftermath of Ash

Aldric Vane had dug men out of rubble before. He'd done it at Cael Ford, when the bridge came down with forty of his company still on it. He'd done it in the war, before the war had a name, hauling bodies from the smoke and learning the difference between a man who was dead and a man who was only sleeping under his stones.

He told himself that difference now, over and over, as he climbed the shattered throat of the Thornfell pass.

The mountain was wrong.

That was the only word for it. The peak he had known as a grey wall against the sky — the wall he'd crossed twice a year on patrol, unremarkable, eternal — was broken open like a split loaf. The vault's gate was gone. Half the ridge above it was gone. Where Kessrin's army had been camped there was nothing but a scoured bowl of glassed rock, smooth and pale, still warm through his boots even hours later. The storm that had done it had come and gone in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and Aldric had been flat on his back in a tunnel mouth with Torv's arm across his chest, watching lightning move *upward*, out of the mountain, into a sky that tore open like wet paper.

Then silence. Then snow. Then nothing.

"Mara." His voice was ash in his throat. "Mara!"

The tunnel behind him groaned. Somewhere deep, stone settled into its new shape, and Aldric flinched at every sound, half expecting the whole mountain to finish what it had started. But Torv had collapsed the pass deliberately, sealing the army out — and sealing all of them *in* — and the old dwarf's work held. It would hold. Dwarven masonry did not do anything else.

He climbed anyway. Over the fall line. Into the killing field.

* * *

He found Kessrin first, or what was left of him.

The warlord had been close to the gate when it broke — close enough to touch it, maybe, close enough to have seen what came out. Now there was a shape fused into the rock of the slope, blackened and twisted, armor melted into the posture of a man reaching forward. Reaching for something he'd wanted his whole life and had never understood.

Aldric stood over him for a long moment.

He had sworn an oath to a kingdom that sent him here with orders to kill a woman for the crime of existing near a map. He had burned those orders at a roadside shrine because a woman with a scarred hand had shown him the truth and he'd found, to his lasting surprise, that he still had a conscience under all the wool and steel. And now the man who might have burned the world open lay dead by a power no army could have answered, and no one would ever know. No banners would fly over this. No histories would record it. The war had simply failed to happen, quietly, in a place where no one went.

Kessrin's soldiers — the ones outside the pass, the ones sealed on the far side — would wake tomorrow to a mountain that had eaten their warlord and a campaign with no object left in it. They would go home. Men always went home eventually.

"Should've stayed a smuggler," Aldric told the corpse, and meant it almost kindly, and walked on.

* * *

The sigil-light led him.

He hadn't understood it until later — wouldn't understand it until much later, sitting in Lowmarket with Odessa's maps spread out and the priest talking in her careful, guilt-heavy way about leashes and vessels. But in the moment, climbing the broken slope with the light dying, he saw it plainly: a faint ember-glow beneath the snowfield where the vault's mouth had been, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart.

Or dying one.

He dug with his hands. He'd lost his sword somewhere in the tunnel crush — Torv's collapse had taken it, along with nearly everything else — so it was gauntlets first, then bare fingers, tearing through rock dust and snow and shards of something that had been stone and was now neither. The cold bit down to the bone and he didn't care. At Cael Ford he'd pulled eleven living men from the rubble and he remembered every one of their faces, and he refused — *refused* — to add Mara Quintana's face to the other list, the longer one, the one he kept in the locked room at the back of himself where he stored all the people duty had cost him.

His fingers hit fabric. Wool, torn, singed at the edge.

"Mara."

She was curled on her side, the way you curl when you've fallen and haven't had the strength to arrange yourself better. Her hair was grey with rock dust. There was blood at her temple, dried to a dark crust, and more of it on her lip, and when he brushed the debris from her face she didn't stir, and for three full seconds — he counted them, they were the longest of his life — Aldric Vane believed she was dead.

Then her chest moved. Shallow. Slow. But moving.

"*Mara.*"

Her eyes opened halfway. Unfocused. She looked at him the way you look at something in a dream, without recognition, without alarm, and then her gaze drifted past his shoulder to the broken sky, and something in her expression changed — not pain, exactly. Something worse than pain. Recognition.

"It's quiet," she whispered.

"I know." He got an arm under her shoulders. "Don't talk. I'm getting you out of here."

"No, I mean—" She coughed, and the cough wracked her whole small body, and he held her through it. "It's quiet *in here*. In my head. There was always— since I was six years old, there was always weather in me, Aldric, always pressure, always the storm wanting out, and now it's just—" She pressed a hand to her sternum, confused, like a woman checking for a missing limb. "It's quiet."

And then he saw her palm.

He'd seen the sigil a hundred times by now. In the shrine's candlelight, by firelight at the waystation, once in the tunnels when it flared so hot she'd cried out and Torv had thought the ceiling was coming down. It had always seemed alive — that was the horror of it, the beauty of it. A spiral of ember-red lines burned into her skin, shifting when storms gathered, glowing when the vault called, warm to the touch even in blizzards. Odessa said it was a leash. Mara had said it was a brand. Either way, it had *burned*. Right up until the moment she'd reached into the heart of the mountain and unmade a god.

Now it was grey.

Not faded. Not scarred-over. Grey — dull as cold iron, inert as any ordinary scar, the lines crisp and permanent and utterly dead. Aldric took her hand in both of his, careful of the burns, and felt nothing. No heat. No hum. Skin. Just skin.

"Oh," Mara said softly, looking at it. "Oh. It's gone."

"Mara—"

"It's gone." A strange sound came out of her, half laugh, half something else entirely. "Seven years I hid this thing. Seven years I wrapped it and gloved it and slept with my fist closed so no one would see it, and I used to dream about cutting it off. Did you know that? I used to lie awake and think, if I just cut it off, I'm free, I'm nobody, I'm safe." Her voice cracked. "And now it's just a scar. And I'd give *anything*—"

The rest broke apart. She turned her face into his shoulder and shook, and Aldric held her, kneeling in the ruin of a mountain with a dead god's grave smoking around them, and understood that he was holding a woman grieving herself.

You didn't argue with grief like that. You didn't say *it's alright, it's better this way, you saved everyone.* He knew better. He'd buried a knighthood once — handed his commission to a superior officer and watched twenty years of identity get folded into a drawer, and everyone had told him it was for the best, and he'd wanted to kill every one of them for saying it.

So he just held her while the snow came down soft and clean over the ashes, and after a long time her shaking slowed, and after a longer time she said, muffled against his shoulder:

"The others?"

"Alive. All of them. Torv sealed us in on purpose — mad old bastard planned the whole collapse, had charge-marks set in the tunnel walls for days, apparently. Pip's fine. Illo broke two ribs and won't stop talking about it. Sef—" He stopped.

Mara lifted her head.

"Sef died buying Pip's escape," Aldric said quietly. "Before the vault. Before all this. You know that."

"I know. I keep forgetting. Isn't that awful? I keep turning around to tell him something and he's not—" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a smear of dust. "Grief is stupid. It doesn't file things properly."

"No," Aldric said. "It doesn't."

* * *

Getting her down the mountain took the rest of the day.

She could walk, mostly — she insisted on walking, waving off his offers to carry her with a ferocity that told him more about how bad she felt than any complaint would have. Twice she stumbled and he caught her. Once she stopped altogether, staring at a stretch of glassed rock where Kessrin's vanguard had been, and Aldric waited beside her without speaking until she was ready to move again.

They passed the place where the vault gate had stood. Or rather, where it hadn't. There was a crater, and at the bottom of the crater a smooth basin of melted stone, and in the center of the basin a single shape: a ring of fused metal, vast, warped, half-buried. All that remained of the door the order had built to cage a storm.

Odessa would want to see it. Odessa would probably want to spend the rest of her life studying it, penance by scholarship. But not today. Today the defrocked priest was at the tunnel mouth with Pip asleep against her shoulder, and when Aldric and Mara came stumbling down into the lamplight, the whole company surged up at once.

Pip got there first. The girl hit Mara like a small cannonball and Mara caught her, staggered, went down to one knee with the child wrapped around her neck, and Aldric heard the sound she made — not words, just a sound, raw and cracked — and decided some things weren't for looking at directly.

Torv clasped his forearm hard enough to bruise. "Told ye the roof'd hold."

"You told me the roof would hold *if I stood exactly where you put me.*"

"Aye. And did ye stand where I put ye?"

Illo, propped against a pack with two splinted ribs and an expression of profound grievance, said, "You look terrible, knight."

"So do you."

"Yes, but I look terrible *heroically*. Two ribs. Tell her, Torv. Two ribs."

Odessa came last. She stood in front of Mara for a long moment, hands folded, and whatever she was going to say — and Aldric suspected it was a great deal; the woman had decades of confession backed up behind her teeth — she swallowed all of it and simply took Mara's scarred hand in both of hers and bowed her head over it, the way priests bowed over relics.

Mara let her. After a moment she said, very gently, "It's just a scar now, Odessa."

"No," Odessa said. "It isn't. Nothing that's carried that much is ever *just* anything." She straightened. "But we'll speak of it when you've slept. When everyone has slept. There are years I owe you in words, and none of them should be spent standing in a tunnel."

* * *

They made camp in the waystation chamber, the deepest one Torv trusted, and built a fire from the emergency stores the dwarf had cached there years ago with the grim foresight of a man who expected mountains to betray him. Someone produced food. Someone else produced, impossibly, a bottle of plum brandy from the wreckage of Illo's pack, and Illo held it aloft like a trophy and declared it medicinal.

They drank to Sef. That was Pip's idea — the girl stood up, too big for her grief and refusing to grow around it yet, and said, "We drink to Sef. Because he was annoying and he cheated at cards and he bought me ten seconds and I'm going to be alive for a really long time so those ten seconds are going to last basically forever." And then she sat down fast, and Illo put an arm around her, and nobody said anything clever, which was the correct response.

Later, when the fire had burned low and the others slept in a sprawl of blankets and limbs, Aldric sat beside Mara against the tunnel wall. She was clean now — Torv had water heated, and someone had found her a spare shirt — and the bandage on her temple made her look younger, and stranger than that, calmer. Like a rope that had been taut for seven years had finally been cut.

"I keep waiting for it to feel different," she said. "Being nobody again. That was the whole point, wasn't it? Be no one, stay safe. I got what I wanted."

"And?"

"And I don't know yet. Ask me in spring." She turned her grey-sigiled palm up, studied it. "Aldric. What happens to us now? To you?"

He considered the question honestly. It deserved honesty. "I imagine the order will disperse. Without the vault, without Veyra—" Veyra, who had gone into the storm and become it and been unmade by it; they had agreed, wordlessly, not to discuss her tonight — "without either of those, there's nothing left to hold them together. Hunters without a quarry. Some of them will follow Odessa, maybe. She knows more of the truth than any of them ever did."

"And your kingdom? Your orders? Someone sent you to kill me."

"My kingdom thinks I'm dead, most likely. Buried in Torv's pass along with everyone else." He shrugged. "There's a version of me that reports back. Walks into the capital, tells them the vault is destroyed, the warlord is dead, the threat is ended. Gets a medal. Maybe gets his commission back."

"Do you want that?"

He looked at her. Firelight, dust, the bandage, the tired eyes that had held a god's attention and survived it. He thought about the shrine where he'd burned his orders, and how easy it had been, and how he'd told himself it was conscience when part of it — most of it, if he was honest, and she had taught him honest — had been her.

"No," he said.

Mara smiled. It was small and exhausted and it reached all the way to her eyes, and Aldric Vane, twenty years a soldier, felt it land somewhere no blade had ever reached.

"Good," she said. "Because Pip's already told Torv you're carrying the heavy packs on the way down. Apparently it was discussed at length while you were digging me out."

"Of course it was."

"She says you're slow but reliable. Like a mule."

"A mule."

"Her words. I'd fight her on it, but she's had a very hard month." Mara tipped her head back against the stone and closed her eyes. For a while he thought she'd fallen asleep. Then, barely audible: "It really is quiet in my head. I thought I'd hate it. I think I might learn to love it."

Outside, far away, thunder rolled — some natural storm, leagues off, moving east over the lowlands toward the sea. Ordinary weather. Weather that owed them nothing and asked nothing in return.

Mara's grey scar twitched. Just once. Just faintly — a ghost of warmth, a memory of heat, there and gone before Aldric could be sure he'd seen it.

Her eyes opened. She stared at her palm.

Neither of them said anything. Outside, the thunder rolled on toward the coast, and the fire burned low, and the mountain held its silence around them like a secret keeping itself.

But Mara did not close her eyes again for a long time.
