## 22. Sigil and Ember

The thaw came late that year, the way it always did in Thornfell, arriving not as a season but as a slow negotiation between winter and everything that wanted to live.

Mara felt it first in her feet. Seven years of walking these passes had taught her to read snow the way sailors read water, and by the time the meltwater started talking in the gullies—thin, bright, insistent—she was already planning routes. Not escape routes, for once. The other kind. The kind that ended somewhere.

"You're smiling at the mud," Aldric said behind her.

"I'm smiling because the mud means the pass to Lowmarket opens in a week."

"The mud means my boots are ruined."

"Your boots were ruined when you bought them. You have terrible taste in boots."

He had, she'd noticed, stopped arguing with her about most things around midwinter. It had happened so gradually she couldn't name the day it changed, only that somewhere between the rubble of the vault and the first hard frost, Ser Aldric Vane—knight of an order that no longer existed, man of orders burned and oaths rewritten—had become simply *Aldric*, the way a sword becomes a plowshare without ever quite losing its edge.

They stood together on the shoulder of the high pass where she had once walked alone through a blizzard, calming the sky with small secret mercies while pretending to be no one. The mountain remembered her. She could feel it in the way the wind came off the crags—not hostile, not exactly welcoming either, but familiar, like a dog too old to bark who still lifts its head when you come home.

Her palm ached.

Not burned. Not flared. Just a low grey ache, deep in the scar tissue where the sigil had lived, the way an old break aches before rain. She'd learned its new language over the winter: it spoke only of distant weather now, storms gathering over the Sea of Glass or rolling down from the northern ice, hundreds of miles away, none of them hers. A weathered stone where a key used to be.

She found she didn't mind. It was, at least, honest. The ache said *there is weather in the world*, and nothing more. It no longer said *you are the door*.

"Pip's going to fall in the meltwater," Aldric observed.

Below them on the trail, Pip was doing exactly what Mara had done at fifteen: walking the treacherous edge of the snowmelt torrent with her arms out, daring it, testing whether the world would catch her if she slipped. Torv bellowed something paternal and profane from twenty yards back, hauling a mason's sledge full of timber for Lowmarket's rebuilding, and Odessa walked beside him with her hood down, letting the thin mountain sun touch her face for the first time in decades, as far as anyone knew.

Sef and Illo were arguing about dice. They were always arguing about dice. But Illo's voice had lost the brittle brightness it carried after Sef died, or rather—it hadn't lost anything, Mara thought. It had just made room for the grief instead of running ahead of it. That was what this company did, apparently. Made room.

"They're slow," Aldric said.

"They're careful."

"They're *slow*, Mara. We could be at the shrine by noon and you know it." He said it lightly, but she heard the thing underneath, the same thing he'd been carrying since Emberlight, since the night before the battle when he'd tended her burning hand with oil and linen and told her, plainly, in the shadow of the end of the world, what he felt. He'd never taken it back. Neither had she. Some things, once said into the dark with a storm coming, stay said.

"We'll get there," she said. "We've got nowhere we have to be. That's rather the point."

* * *

The shrine sat in a fold of the mountain like a cupped hand.

It had been a storm-caller waypoint once—one of dozens scattered through Thornfell, places where her order's pilgrims had rested and left offerings of iron and rainwater. After her exile she'd used it as shelter a dozen times, always alone, always sleeping with her back to the wall and her marked hand curled shut like a fistful of secrets.

Now there was a fire pit someone had built properly, ringed with flat stones Torv had dressed himself, and a lean-to against the old wall, and Pip's collection of interesting rocks arranged on the altar slab with no reverence whatsoever. Someone had hung a wind chime made of broken sword-blades—Aldric's old order's steel, melted and reforged into something that sang instead of cut.

Mara stood in the doorway and looked at all of it and felt something in her chest come loose that had been tight for seven years.

Home wasn't a place. She'd known that since the vault, since she'd called the storm down through every bond she'd forged and let the company anchor her—Odessa's steady voice, Torv's grip on her shoulder, Pip's small fierce hand in hers, Aldric's heartbeat under her palm. She'd known it intellectually, the way you know the sum of a sum. But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different weathers entirely.

"You're doing it again," Aldric said.

"Doing what?"

"Standing in doorways looking surprised."

"I spent seven years being no one," she said. "It turns out being someone takes practice."

He came to stand beside her, close enough that his shoulder touched hers. Below, the company straggled up the switchbacks—Torv's sledge creaking, Odessa laughing at something, which was still strange enough that Mara turned to watch it every time, like a comet.

"Veyra's trial is next month," Aldric said quietly. "In Lowmarket. Odessa's been asked to speak."

"I know."

"She asked if you would too."

Mara watched the wind move the grass on the south slope, green coming up through last year's dead gold like a second chance. "I don't know what I'd say."

"You'd say the truth. You're irritatingly good at that."

"The truth is that I loved her once, and she offered me the whole sky, and I said no, and I still don't fully understand why saying no cost so much—or why it was right anyway." She flexed her scarred hand, watching the pale sigil-scars go silver in the light. "Maybe I'd just say that. Maybe that's enough."

"It will be," he said. "Whatever you say. It'll be enough because you'll be saying it as a free woman in front of people who love you, and Veyra will hear it from inside a cage, and that's the whole difference between you two, isn't it? She needed to own the storm. You just needed—" he gestured, awkwardly, at the shrine, the trail, the stragglers below, "—this."

* * *

Pip reached the shrine first, because Pip reached everything first, breathless and triumphant, and slammed into Mara's middle with the force of a girl who had decided, sometime during the worst year of both their lives, that Mara Quintana was hers and that was final.

"Told you I'd beat Aldric up the switchback."

"He let you win."

"He absolutely did not," Aldric said gravely, "and I demand a rematch."

That evening they cooked barley and goat over the fire Torv built, and Odessa told a story about the order's founding that was probably forty percent true, and Illo dealt cards on a flat stone and cheated everyone cheerfully, and nobody mentioned the vault, or Kessrin, or the war that hadn't happened because six people and one stubborn knight had held a tunnel mouth long enough for a woman to call down a storm made out of love.

Later, when the others slept, Mara walked out onto the ledge above the shrine.

The sky was enormous and clean, washed by spring, salted with stars. Far to the north, past the horizon, she could feel it—a pressure, a gathering, thunder building over some distant sea. Her palm ached with it, faint and grey and far away.

Once, that ache had meant *run*. Once, every flicker of the sigil had been a countdown to discovery, exile, death. Now it just meant the world was turning, weathering, alive. Storms would gather somewhere forever. None of them needed her. And she—she looked back at the firelight spilling from the shrine, at Torv's snore and Pip's blanket-burrow and Aldric sitting up keeping watch because he still kept watch even though there was nothing left to guard against—

She smiled.

It was a small smile, private, the kind she used to spend on empty passes with no witness but the wind. But it wasn't lonely anymore. That was the difference. That was the whole seven-year difference, folded into the curve of a mouth.

The storm gathered far away, and went its own way, and she let it.

Behind her, Aldric's footsteps came soft on the stone, and he wrapped his cloak around them both against the mountain air, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left that needed saying, and the chime of broken swords sang softly in the dark, and the pass—the road that remembered her—held them all the way home.
