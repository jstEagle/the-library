## 1. The Charm-Seller's Hour

The lamps of Vellumgate came on one by one as the sun went down, and Edmund Ravensworth counted them the way other men counted money.

There were forty-two between his stall at the mouth of Cooper's Lane and the corner of Sable Street, though only thirty-seven worked on any given night. The five dead ones had been dead for years—the Ministry of Seals did not repair what it could not tax, and lamplighters' guild rates had doubled since the outlawry. He had learned the number in his first winter behind the charm-stall, when counting was the only thing that kept his hands from shaking, and he had never quite stopped. Forty-two lamps. Thirty-seven lit. A city of ninety thousand souls going dark by increments, and no one but a charm-seller who noticed.

He arranged his wares with the care of a man setting a table for guests he both wanted and feared. Copper bracelets, green with honest age. Knotted red thread sold by the span. Dried lavender in twistings of paper. Horse brasses polished until they threw back the lamplight like small dull suns. And beneath all of it—invisible to any eye that did not already know how to look—a second inventory, quieter and infinitely more dangerous: three luck-charms with true workings knotted into their cores, two fever-binders stitched into the linings of ordinary sachets, and a single finding-stone disguised as nothing more remarkable than a pretty river pebble.

Nine years he had kept this stall. Nine years of selling superstition to the superstitious and hiding the real thing underneath like a coin sewn into a coat hem. It was not a living, exactly. It was a life shaped precisely to the size of a keyhole—small enough to pass through, too small to be worth the effort of opening anything wider.

"Still open?"

Edmund looked up without lifting his head, an old habit. The voice belonged to Tam Wickerly, a junior clerk from the Ministry's own records office, which was either very good or very bad depending on which way the evening decided to go. Tam was twenty-four, soft around the middle, and sweating despite the October chill. His eyes went everywhere and settled nowhere: the stall, the lane, the dark window above the chandler's shop, his own hands.

"For you, clerk, always." Edmund smiled the smile he wore like an apron—plain, serviceable, concealing everything beneath. "Trouble sleeping again?"

"It's not—" Tam stopped. Started again. "It's the examinations. Promotion boards next month. There are men who'd cut their mothers' throats for a records post with window-light in it, and I'm—I'm nobody, Edmund. I'm the son of a cooper from Cooper's Lane itself, and everyone knows it."

"And you want luck."

"I want *not to fail*," Tam said, with the raw honesty of a man too frightened to lie well. "Is there a difference?"

*Yes,* Edmund thought. *Luck is weather. Failure is a door. I sell you weather because the doors are mine to worry about.* But he said none of it. Instead he reached beneath the counter with the unhurried motion of long practice and brought up the tray of red thread, letting Tam's eye catch on it while his fingers found the third knot from the left—the one with the working inside.

"A span of thread," Edmund said. "Tie it round your left wrist on the morning of your examination. Red for courage, clerk. Everyone knows that much."

Tam laughed, high and thin. "Everyone knows that much," he agreed. "And does everyone know what else there is?"

Edmund held the man's gaze and let the silence do its work. This was the delicate hour of every sale, the moment on the knife's edge where a charm-seller learned whether his customer wanted comfort or wanted magic—and in Vellumgate, wanting magic out loud could get a person fitted for a gray hood before the soup went cold.

"I sell thread," Edmund said gently. "Thread, and bracelets, and dried flowers that smell nice. If a man ties my thread about his wrist and goes into his examination feeling lucky—" he shrugged, spreading his hands, the picture of a humble tradesman, "—then perhaps he writes a steadier hand than he otherwise would. Perhaps he remembers things he'd have fumbled. The Ministry calls that fraud, if they catch you saying it. I call it confidence, and confidence isn't against the law. Yet."

Something in Tam's shoulders came down half an inch. "Yet," he repeated, and almost smiled.

Edmond slid the span of thread across the counter, working knotted into its heart: a simple thing, old craft, his mother's craft—a tightening of fortune's weave, no more. Enough to tip a nervous man toward his better self. Not enough to light a candle or turn a lock or do any of the thousand small wonders that had once made hedge-mages welcome at every farmstead kitchen between here and the sea. The Ministry had seen to that. The Ministry had seen to nearly everything.

"That'll be four coppers."

Tam paid six and didn't wait for change. He wound the thread into his pocket, glanced up and down Cooper's Lane once more—that glance again, that terrible hunted glance—and hurried off toward Sable Street with his collar turned up, a small frightened man carrying a small unlawful hope through a city built to crush both.

Edmund watched him go and felt the familiar weight settle onto his chest, the one that lived there always now, patient as furniture. Somewhere in the Ministry's records, Tam Wickerly existed in triplicate: birth, residence, employment. Somewhere in the Ministry's ledgers, a purchase of red thread from a stall in Cooper's Lane might someday be noted by a clerk who noticed too much. Safety in Vellumgate was not a possession. It was a loan, called due without warning, and Edmund had spent nine years making his payments in invisibility and praying the note never came due.

He was still watching the empty end of the lane when the patrol came around the corner.

* * *

They moved the way patrols always moved in the evening hours—too slow, deliberately, so that shopkeeps and stall-keepers had time to see them coming and adjust themselves accordingly. Four sweepers in gray-and-black livery, seal-staves in hand, the white wax sigils on the staves' heads catching the lamplight like little moons. At their head walked Inspector Dree, whom Edmund knew by sight the way mice know the silhouette of an owl.

Dree was not cruel. That was the worst of her. Cruelty could be predicted, hated, survived. Dree was simply thorough, a woman doing her job with the placid competence of someone stacking crates, and her job was the finding of magic where magic was forbidden, which was to say everywhere, which was to say nowhere, if everyone was careful enough. She had closed eleven stalls in the charm-quarter over the past three years. She had taken a midwife from Tanner's Row whose crime, so far as anyone ever proved, was humming the old birthing-chants while she worked. She had once stopped directly in front of Edmund's stall, picked up a horse brass, turned it over twice, and set it down again, and Edmund had stood there smiling his apron-smile while his heart performed gymnastics worthy of a temple acrobat.

Tonight she slowed. Of course she slowed. She always slowed; it was part of the theater that kept the quarter docile.

"Evening, Ravensworth." She did not consult the slate of licensed vendors she carried; she knew his name. That was its own small terror. "Anything new?"

"Dried lavender's fresh in from the hill farms," Edmund said. "Good year for it. Cool summer."

"Mm." Her gaze traveled the stall with the flat patience of a fisherman watching still water. It touched the copper bracelets. The knotwork. The trays of thread. Edmund felt each touch of her attention like a fingertip laid briefly on his skin, and he kept his breathing even and his face pleasant and thought, with practiced discipline, about absolutely nothing at all. Emptiness. Lavender. The price of copper. A man could be taught to do this, the way a man could be taught to hold his breath. Nine years of practice, and it never got easy; it only got smoother.

"Selling well?" Dree asked.

"Autumn's kind to us. People like charms when the nights come early. Buttons and ribbons, mostly, whatever I can dress them up as." He gestured at the thread. "Courage, love, luck. All the same knot, really. Just different words on the paper wrapping."

Something flickered at the corner of Dree's mouth—not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "Honest work," she said, and Edmund could not tell whether it was praise or a test or simply the sound a woman makes when she has decided, for tonight, to move along. "Keep the lamp lit till nine, Ravensworth. Quarter curfew's been moved up. There've been... irregularities."

"Irregularities," Edmund echoed carefully.

"Nothing that concerns a thread-seller." She was already turning, signaling her sweepers onward with two fingers. Then, pausing: "Though it seems to concern somebody. Three houses searched on Sable Street this afternoon. Records people." She said it lightly, tossing it away, but Edmund heard the hook buried in the bait. "Clerks, mostly."

She walked on. The gray-and-black livery swallowed the corner of Sable Street and was gone.

Edmund stood behind his stall with his hands flat on the counter and did not move for the space of ten full breaths. Tam Wickerly. Records people. Clerks. The word *irregularities* sat in his ears like a stone dropped into a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom.

It meant nothing, he told himself. It meant a stolen ledger, a forged permit, one of the hundred small corruptions that flourished in any ministry like mushrooms in a cellar. Tam had bought red thread. Thousands bought red thread. The thread was thread. The knot was a knot. No sweeper's stave would ever warm at the touch of it unless—

Unless someone told them where to look. Unless a name surfaced somewhere it should not have. Unless the careful, keyhole-sized shape of his life shifted by a finger's width and let the cold in.

He began packing the stall. His movements were steady, economical—he took a certain grim pride in that—but his mind ran ahead of his hands, down the familiar corridors of contingency. The false-bottomed chest under the floorboards, with its true inventory wrapped in oilcloth. The root-cellar of the chandler upstairs, who owed him more than money and asked no questions. The road east out of Vellumgate that he had walked in his mind perhaps a thousand times and never once with his feet.

Nine years, and the plan was worn smooth as a river stone from handling. That was the loneliness of it, he thought, folding the awning cloth. Not the absence of people—Cooper's Lane had plenty of people, warm ones, kind ones, Mrs. Aldery who brought him onion soup on Thursdays, the chandler's daughters who waved to him from the window. It was the absence of anyone who knew. Every friendship he had was a friendship with the shopkeeper, the thread-seller, the pleasant man with the lavender. The self beneath—the boy who had been taught knots by firelight, who had once, before he understood what it cost, made a lame sparrow fly—that self had no one. It sat alone under the floorboards with the oilcloth-wrapped inventory and waited, as it had waited for nine years, for either freedom or the knock at the door.

His mother had told him: *Be small, Edmun. Be plain. Be forgettable.* She had said it the last night he saw her, kneeling in front of him with her hands on his shoulders, and there had been something in her face he was too young to read then and had spent his whole life since failing to forget. *Small and plain and forgotten, and you will live.*

He had gotten very good at it.

He doused his lamp at nine exactly, as instructed, because obedience was another kind of camouflage. The remaining lamps of Cooper's Lane burned down toward Sable Street, thirty-six of them now—one had gone out since sundown, guttered, unattended, unmourned—and Edmund stood a moment in the doorway of the chandler's shop where he lodged, looking back at his stall, his neat trays, his harmless wares, the whole modest architecture of his hidden life.

Tomorrow he would unpack it all again. Tomorrow Tam Wickerly would tie red thread to his wrist, and Mrs. Aldery would bring soup, and Inspector Dree would walk her rounds, and the loan of safety would roll over for one more night, as it had rolled over for three thousand nights running.

He was halfway up the stairs when he heard the knock at the chandler's door below—soft, apologetic, utterly wrong for the hour—and heard old Master Chell shuffle to answer it, and heard a voice ask, politely, in the tone of a woman stacking crates:

"Forgive the lateness. We're looking for a Mr. Ravensworth. The charm-seller."

Edmund did not move on the stairs. In the dark, his heart hammered against nine years of careful, borrowed quiet—and somewhere across the city, though he could not have known it yet, a book was waiting with his true name written in it, beside tomorrow's date, beside the name of a stranger he had never met.

The knock came again.
