## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The rain had been falling on Vellumgate for three days without conviction, a thin grey drizzle that never quite committed to washing anything clean. It beaded on the awning of Quill & Daughters and dripped in a slow metronome onto the cobbles, and Edmund Ravensworth counted the drops the way other men counted sheep, because sleep had become a country he could no longer afford to visit.

He had not told anyone about the dream.

Three nights running now, the same one: his mother's hands, cupped around something small and burning, her voice saying *forget your name* in a language he did not know and yet understood perfectly. He had woken each time with the taste of smoke in his mouth and his left palm itching, deep beneath the skin, in a place no salve could reach.

*Charm-sellers don't dream,* he reminded himself, hunching his shoulders against the wet as he slipped through the shop's side door. *Charm-sellers are ordinary men who sell ordinary trinkets to ordinary people, and ordinary people sleep like stones.*

The bell above the door gave its small brass cough. The smell of the shop folded around him — old paper, beeswax, lamp oil, and beneath it all that faint mineral scent of stone that has been rained on for a hundred years. Tamsin Quill kept her establishment the way a spider keeps a web: every strand deliberate, every corner holding something caught and precious.

She was not at the counter. That was the first wrongness.

Tamsin was always at the counter by the second bell of morning, small and sharp-faced behind her spectacles, mending book spines with thread pulled from the hem of her own coat when she ran out of proper linen. Edmund had known her for eleven years — longer than he had known most of his own lies — and in eleven years she had missed her post exactly twice, both times for funerals.

"Back here," came her voice, low and roughened, from the depths of the rare-books room.

He hung his dripping coat on the peg, out of habit rather than hope of dryness, and followed the sound past shelves that leaned together like conspirators. The rare-books room sat at the very back of the shop, windowless, lit by a single oil lamp whose flame burned steady and green-tinged — Tamsin's one indulgence, a charm-lamp she'd bought off him years ago and which he pretended, for both their sakes, was merely well-made glasswork.

She was standing at the reading table, and she had spread a cloth over it. Not the good cloth for customers — the plain grey wool she used only for things she considered serious. On it lay a book.

Edmund stopped in the doorway.

It was a large volume, folio-sized, bound in leather gone the colour of dried blood, its corners worn round as river stones. The spine had been broken and badly resewn at some point in its long life, and the whole thing gave off an aura of having survived something — fire, flood, confiscation, he couldn't have said which. But it was not the book itself that made his mouth go dry.

It was the way Tamsin was looking at it. She looked at rare books the way a jeweler looks at stones, with professional hunger. This was different. She looked at this one the way people look at graves.

"Tamsin," he said carefully. "You look like you've seen a ghost with outstanding debts."

"I've seen worse." She didn't smile. Tamsin always smiled at his jokes; it was part of the architecture of their friendship, load-bearing. "Come here, Edmund. And close the door."

He closed the door. The latch clicked, soft as a held breath.

* * *

"A scavenger brought it up from the Sump last week," she said, while he crossed the room at a pace he tried to keep casual and failed. "Dredging the old archive collapse — the Ministry wing that went down in the flood year, before either of us was born. They've been pulling things out for months. Mostly rotted ledgers. Tax records." Her mouth twisted. "The Ministry of Seals, drowning in its own paperwork. There's a justice in that."

"And this?"

"This came up wrapped in oilcloth inside a lead box." She turned the book toward him, and the lamplight slid across its cover, where letters tooled into the leather had been scraped away — deliberately, savagely, though the ghost-shapes of them still caught the light if you knew how to look. He knew how to look. It was practically the whole of his trade.

"The title's been effaced," he said.

"Everything's been effaced. Names, places, dates — there's a whole section near the front where someone went through with a blade and a pot of ink like they were killing insects." She pushed her spectacles up her nose. "But whoever did the killing got bored, or interrupted, or died. Because the back third is untouched."

Edmund reached out — then paused, hand hovering. Old instinct. You didn't touch strange books bare-handed; you didn't touch them at all until you'd watched them for a while and made sure they weren't watching back.

"It's just paper," Tamsin said quietly. "I've had it three days. Whatever it was going to do, it would have done it."

That was meant to reassure him. It did not, particularly, but he let his fingers rest on the cover anyway. Cool leather, slightly tacky with age. Nothing stirred. The itch in his left palm flared once, sharply, and subsided, like a dog turning over in its sleep.

He opened the book.

The early pages were exactly as she'd described — a massacre of blacked-out text, lines drowned in ink so old it had gone brown and brittle, margins scarred by knife-work. Someone had wanted this book unread so badly they'd been willing to destroy half of it. He turned page after page of murdered prose, and the dread that had been sitting quietly in his chest since the door closed began, slowly, to stand up.

Then the killings stopped, and the surviving text began.

It was written in a fine, slanted hand, the ink faded to the colour of weak tea, and the script was old-fashioned but legible — the kind of formal bookhand they taught in scriptoriums a century ago, before the Sealing, when there were still scriptoriums to teach it. Edmund read the first full line, and then read it again, because the first time through, his eyes had refused to accept it.

*In the year the hearth grows cold, the ravens return to the garden.*

"Prophecy," he said. His voice came out flat, which took effort. "Someone's century-old poetry."

"Read on."

*Two names shall stand at the hinge of the door,* the next line ran. *One who hides, and one who was cast out. Between them the covenant sleeps or wakes.*

"Covenant," Edmund repeated. The word landed somewhere in him and rang, faintly, like a bell struck in an empty house. He didn't know why. He had never heard it applied to anything but marriage contracts and guild charters. "That's a word, certainly. People write words."

"Edmund." Tamsin's voice had gone very gentle, and gentleness from Tamsin was more alarming than shouting. "Turn to the dated entries."

The back third of the book was organized differently from the front — less flowing verse, more ledger. Dates, set down in that same slanted hand, one after another down the years, each with a line or two of commentary beside it. Some entries were terse: *the third seal cracks; the west road floods.* Some were longer. All of them were old. He let his eye run down the column, dates marching backward into the last century, and told himself he was only curious, only a bookseller's friend admiring an antique, only a charm-seller with nothing in his history worth—

His finger stopped.

The date was written in the same slanted hand as everything else, faded tea-brown, unmistakably a century old. Unmistakably, undeniably, put there by a pen dipped and dried long before Edmund Ravensworth was born, long before his mother was born, in a city that had since outlawed the very idea that such a pen might write true things.

The date was tonight.

Not approximately. Not the right season, the right month, some numerological near-miss he could laugh off over a cup of Tamsin's terrible chicory. Tonight. The day, the month, the year, rendered in the old style and translated without effort by some cold clerk in the back of his mind.

And beside it, two names.

The first name was his.

Not *Aldous Finch*, the name he wore like a borrowed coat, the charm-seller of Copperlane with his licence papers and his rent receipts and his careful, forgettable face. Not the name on his forged papers or the name the neighbors used or the name he signed on the little tags he tied to luck-charms.

*Edmund Ravensworth.* Written out whole. A name he had not spoken aloud in twenty-two years. A name he had buried with his mother, in the only grave he'd ever dared dig — the one inside himself.

The floor did not literally tilt. Edmund was distantly aware of that. The floor remained a perfectly serviceable arrangement of old boards, and the lamp continued to burn its steady green flame, and the rain went on ticking against a roof somewhere above them. But some deeper floor — the one he had spent two decades laying plank by plank, the hidden life built level and true and load-bearing — that floor gave way all at once, silently, the way ice gives way under a walker who has measured every step.

He heard himself make a sound. Small. Involuntary.

"Edmund." Tamsin's hand was on his arm. When had she moved? "Sit down. Sit down before you fall down."

He sat. The chair received him. He was aware of these facts the way a drowning man is aware of the color of the water.

"That's—" His voice cracked and he started again. "That's my name."

"I know."

"You *know*?" The word came out louder than he intended, and he strangled it, glancing at the door, at the walls, at the world beyond the walls where Ministry patrols walked their rounds with seal-rods humming against their hips. "How could you possibly know that? Nobody knows that. Nobody *alive* knows that."

"Nobody alive wrote this book, either." Tamsin crouched beside the chair, and her eyes behind the spectacles were fierce and frightened in equal measure. "I didn't know, Edmund. I swear it on my daughters' graves. I found the entry yesterday evening and I've been sick with it ever since, trying to decide whether to burn the thing or show you. I nearly burned it." She laughed, a horrible small sound. "I actually had it in the stove. And then I thought — if it's real, burning it won't unwrite it. And if it's false, then someone went to extraordinary lengths to write your name in a book a hundred years dead, and either way you need to see it. Either way, it's already about you."

He looked back down at the page. The second name waited below his, patient as a knife on a table.

*Isolde Vane.*

He didn't know it. The name meant nothing — a stranger, exactly as foreign to him as his own name had suddenly become. Below the names, the slanted hand had added a single line of commentary, and Edmund read it four times before the words would sit still long enough to mean anything:

*On this night the hidden heir stands at the threshold. What he chooses, the realms inherit.*

"Hidden heir," he whispered.

"Do you know what it means?"

"No." Yes. No. Something in him that predated his memory, that lived below the sealed cellar of his childhood, had begun to hammer on the door, and he was pressing his whole weight against it, because if that door opened he did not know what would come out, and he had built his entire life on the certainty that nothing was down there at all. "I'm a hedge-charmer, Tamsin. I sell fever-eases and fortune-knots to clerks who want their wives to stop being angry at them. My greatest crime is an unlicensed warming-stone. There's nothing to inherit. There's nothing *hidden*. My mother sold buttons."

"Your mother," Tamsin said, "sold buttons in a city where your real name could get you hanged, and raised a son who flinches at patrol-whistles before he hears them, and you're telling me there's nothing hidden?"

He had no answer. The rain ticked. The lamp burned.

* * *

They talked for a long time after that, or Tamsin talked and Edmund nodded in the right places, which amounted to the same thing. She wanted to keep the book — hide it in the false bottom of the map chest, where she'd kept contraband through two purges. He heard himself argue the opposite: burn it, drown it, give it back to the Sump. Neither of them believed their own position. A prophecy you'd read could not be unread; a name you'd seen could not be unseen.

What he did not say — what he could not make his mouth form, even alone with the one person in Vellumgate he trusted — was the thing underneath everything, the thing making his hands shake as he buttoned his coat to leave:

*Someone knew. A hundred years ago, someone knew I would exist. Which means the hiding worked, and the hiding failed, in the same breath. Which means the life I built was never mine. It was assigned. I have been living inside someone else's sentence.*

He stepped out of Quill & Daughters into the drizzle feeling like a man who has just learned the ground is a ceiling.

The street was doing what streets do — carts, umbrellas, a fishwife arguing with a gull, two Ministry patrollers at the far corner checking a baker's seal-licence with the bored efficiency of men near the end of a shift. Ordinary. Safe. The whole rotten architecture of Vellumgate holding up its sky of grey law, and Edmund walked through it and felt, for the first time in eleven years, visible. Not watched, exactly. Worse than watched. *Accounted for.* As though somewhere in a century-old book his place had already been marked, and tonight's date circled, and all he had to do was arrive.

He turned toward home through Copperlane's crooked lanes, and it was only halfway there, passing the shuttered mouth of Chandler's Wynd, that he noticed the itch in his left palm had returned — deeper now, insistent, almost rhythmic. Like a pulse. Like knocking.

And it was then, in the same breath, that he saw the man across the lane: tall, hooded despite the drizzle, standing far too still amid the flow of the crowd. Not a patroller — no seal-rod, no tabard. Just watching. Watching *him*, with the particular patience of someone who has read ahead in the book and is waiting, quite comfortably, for the date to arrive.

The stranger stepped out of the doorway and began, unhurried, to cross the street.
