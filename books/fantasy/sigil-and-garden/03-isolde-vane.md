## 3. Isolde Vane

Edmund did not sleep.

He sat in the back room of his shop with the prophecy book open on his knees and a single candle burning down between his fingers, and he read the line again for the fortieth time, as though the words might tire of being true.

*On the eve of the Frostfair moon, the Raven wakes. Edmund Ravensworth and Isolde Vane shall stand at the broken door.*

His name. Not *Edmund Marsh*, the name painted on his shopfront in careful, unremarkable letters. Not the alias he had worn for eleven years like a second skin, oiled and maintained and never once allowed to crack. His *true* name, written in faded iron-gall ink by a hand that had been dust in the ground before Edmund's grandmother learned to walk.

The candle guttered. Outside, Vellumgate settled into its frost-bound hush—the groan of ice on the canal, the far-off clank of the night-watch's seal-lanterns, that particular silence a city makes when it is pretending not to be afraid of itself.

*Isolde Vane.* He turned the name over. It meant nothing to him. No charm-seller he knew had ever spoken it; no whisper in the quarter, no name scrawled on the Ministry's wanted-broadsheets that papered the market pillars. A stranger, then. A stranger who had been bound to him in ink a hundred years before either of them drew breath.

And tonight was the eve of the Frostfair moon. He had checked the almanac three times, hoping to be wrong about that most ordinary of facts.

The book lay heavy on his thighs, its vellum cool as river stones. He should burn it. That was the sensible thing, the survival instinct of eleven years speaking plainly: burn the book, sweep the ash, open the shop at dawn and sell a widow a charm against rheumatism and go on being no one. The Ministry did not come for no one. The Ministry came for names, and his name was sitting in his lap glowing like an ember in a hayrick.

But his hands would not close the cover. There was a terrible gravity to it, the pull of a thing that had waited a century to be read, and Edmund—who had spent his whole adult life pretending he felt nothing deeper than commerce—found himself weeping without quite noticing, two hot lines down his face, because someone, somewhere, once, had known what he was and written it down as though it mattered. As though *he* mattered. Not the charm-seller. The heir.

He heard the knock at ten past midnight.

Three soft raps, spaced too evenly. Customers knocked in fives and sevens, hurried and human. Three even raps was cadence. Was drill.

Edmund closed the book and did not move. Through the gap beneath the door to the shopfront he could see lantern-light sliding across the floorboards—white light, hard-edged, none of the warm amber a watchman carried. Seal-light. The kind that burned without flame and left no wax, the kind the Ministry issued to men who were permitted to be certain of themselves.

"Shopkeeper." A voice through the wood, pleasant and flat. "Ministry of Seals. We'd like a word about your inventory."

There was no version of this where they went away. Edmund set the book into the loose brick behind the flour barrel with hands that shook only a little, tamped the mortar dust back over it, and stood. He took stock of himself the way he took stock of his wares: coat, plain; face, forgettable; fear, filed under later. Eleven years of practice folded around him like a familiar coat.

He opened the door with a shopkeeper's apologetic smile already fitted in place.

Four of them. Three in grey seal-cloaks with the Ministry's interlocked rings stitched at the collar, and one in front—a lean man with a clerk's ink-stains and a hunter's eyes, holding a strip of pale parchment that shimmered faintly. A truth-strip. It would taste the air of a room and darken near unregistered magic, near old magic, near *him*.

"Edmund Marsh," the man said, reading from a slate. "Charm-trader, licensed, third district. We've had a report."

"A report," Edmund repeated mildly. "I'm licensed. You can see the seal on my—"

"We've had a report about your *name*." The man smiled. It was the smile of someone who has already enjoyed this conversation in his head several times. "Your real one."

The floor did not fall away. Edmund had expected it to—that vertigo from the bookseller's back room—but instead something colder happened: everything went very clear and very quiet, the way the world goes just before a roof beam gives. Somewhere behind his ribs, the small banked coal of his hidden magic stirred and pressed against its cage, wanting out, wanting to burn the truth-strip black, wanting to do anything but stand here and be reasonable.

"Sir," Edmund said carefully, "I don't know what you've been told, but—"

"Bring the strip closer," the hunter said to one of the others, almost lazily. "Let's see if the room agrees with him."

The seal-man stepped forward, parchment extended—and the lantern above the door burst.

Not dimmed. *Burst*, glass spraying inward in a bloom of white sparks, and in the same instant every seal-lantern on the street died at once, and the dark came down over the shopfront like a dropped curtain. Edmund heard the seal-men shout, heard steel leave sheaths, heard his own heart slam once against the cage of his magic—

—and then a hand closed on his wrist from inside the dark, iron-firm, and a voice at his ear said: "Back room. Now. Unless you'd rather be arrested."

He went. Later he would be ashamed of how quickly he went.

* * *

She moved through his own shop better than he did, in the dark, pulling him past the flour barrel and the curtain and into the back room, and when the seal-men came crashing through the shopfront thirty seconds later, splintering his display cases and trampling eleven years of careful anonymity under their boots, the back window was already open and Edmund was already dropping into the frozen alley behind it, following the silhouette of a woman who ran like water finding a downhill grade.

They went through Vellumgate the way smugglers and cats go through Vellumgate: the cooperage yard, the low arch where the canal wall had crumbled, the rookery stairs with their missing steps memorized by every child who'd ever needed to disappear. Behind them, whistles rose and knotted together—three short, one long. Cordon call. They were cutting off the district.

"They'll have the bridges," the woman said, not breathing hard, which offended Edmund obscurely, since his lungs were full of knives. "Not the ice. Nobody official walks the ice."

"The ice is a foot thick and cracking all winter," Edmund managed.

"So step where I step." She dropped over the canal wall without breaking stride, and after one appalled heartbeat Edmund followed, boots skidding on black glass, the whole city tilted sideways above them, lamplit windows like a constellation fallen into the water. She moved along the frozen canal in long confident strides, choosing her path by some sense he couldn't see, until the whistles faded behind the warehouses and she turned up a drainage stair into the tannery district, where the smell covered all other traces, including, presumably, magical ones.

Only there, in the lee of a bark-shed, did she stop and turn and look at him properly for the first time.

She was perhaps thirty, sharp-featured, with the pallor of someone who had spent years indoors or underground. Her hair was cropped short and impractically, defiantly red. And on both her forearms, where her sleeves were pushed up, her skin was marked—inked, scarred, *written*—with sigils in a dense, deliberate script that hurt slightly to look at, the way a bright sky hurts after a dark room. Edmund recognized maybe one glyph in twenty, and that one from a stolen book he'd been afraid to finish.

"You're bleeding," she said.

He looked down. His palm was sliced from the canal wall. He hadn't noticed. "Who are you?"

"You know," she said. "You read the book."

The cold went through him then, all at once, the delayed price of the last hour. "How do you know about the book?"

"Because I've spent four years looking for it, and you found it in an afternoon." Something moved across her face—not quite anger. Envy, maybe, or grief wearing envy's coat. "Isolde Vane," she said. "Which you already knew. Which is why you're looking at me like I'm the trap and you're the bait."

"Are you?"

"If I were working for the Ministry, charm-seller, you'd be in irons and I'd be drinking their wine." She pulled something from her coat: a strip of parchment, blank, and pricked her thumb with a needle from her collar, and wrote a single character in blood. The sigil flared—a brief, clean gold light, shaped like a key seen through deep water—and Edmund felt it in his teeth, in the coal behind his ribs, which leaned toward it like a plant toward sun. Real magic. Unhidden. He couldn't remember the last time he'd seen it done openly. He couldn't remember if he ever had.

"That proves nothing," he said, "except that you're powerful and reckless."

"It proves I'm a sigil-keeper. Third circle, before they took my circles away." She wrapped her thumb in a scrap of cloth, unhurried. "It also proves I just spent enough craft to light a beacon for anyone hunting me, to convince a paranoid hedge-mage I'm not the people who are actually hunting him. So let's skip ahead. You're Edmund Ravensworth."

"Don't say that name on the street."

"There is no street safe enough to say it in, and no street dangerous enough to unsay it. That's rather the point." Her eyes were very green and very tired. "Come inside. There's a cellar under the bark-shed. I'll tell you what the book didn't, and you can decide whether to hate me in comfort."

* * *

In the cellar, among barrels of tanning lime, by the light of a candle she shielded with a sigil that ate the glow and gave back only warmth, Isolde Vane told him about the Raven Covenant.

She told it plainly, without theater, which somehow made it worse.

"Before the Ministry, before the outlawry, there was an exchange. Mortal magic and dragon-fire, bound in a covenant older than either kingdom. Our side fed the fire—every charm worked, every spell sung, a portion of its strength flowed down the root-roads to the dragon-realms. Their side blessed us back. That's why crops grew in the old days. Why fires burned steady. Why the land—" she paused, chose her word— "*breathed*. The covenant kept the exchange balanced. It had keepers. Sigil-keepers, sworn to maintain the terms. My family held the office for nine generations."

"And then?" Edmund asked, though he was beginning to guess, and the guessing made him sick.

"Then a hundred years ago a group of mortal magicians decided the exchange was a tax. They broke the covenant, sealed the root-roads, and founded the Ministry of Seals to make sure no one could ever reopen the account. Outlawing magic wasn't piety, Ravensworth. It was theft. They didn't ban the power—they *hoarded* it. Every charm confiscated since, every hedge-witch burned, every child born with the gift and registered and 'sealed'—it all flows into their vaults. And the dragons' realms, cut off, have been dying slowly ever since. The failing harvests. The fires that gutter. The winters that bite deeper every year. Everyone thinks the world is getting worse. The world is *starving*."

Edmund sat with that. The candle's borrowed warmth touched his face and gave no light.

"What does that have to do with me? I sell luck-charms. Half of them don't work."

"Your family made the covenant." She said it gently, which was the cruelest way to say it. "The Raven Covenant. Your bloodline were the first signatories—the mortal anchor of the whole exchange. When the Ministry broke it, they hunted the Raven line to extinction. Almost." She watched him. "Someone got a child out. Hid him in Vellumgate, buried his name, scarred over his birthright so thoroughly that he grew up thinking he was nothing but a clever boy with a knack for charms."

"I would know," Edmund said. His voice came out strange, thin. "If I had—I'd *know*—"

"Would you?" Isolde asked softly. "Do you remember your mother?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was: barely. A kitchen. A song hummed off-key. Hands pressing something into his palm—warm, warm, unnaturally warm—and a voice saying *don't show anyone, never show anyone, promise me.* He had thought, all these years, that the memory was simply grief-blurred. That everyone's earliest years dissolved like that.

"She sealed you herself," Isolde said. "To save your life. Whatever she used, it was strong enough to hold for thirty years." She hesitated, and for the first time since the canal she looked uncertain. "Strong enough that I can't undo it. But there are those who can. If you want to know what you are."

The coal behind Edmund's ribs pressed against its cage, and for one dizzying moment he could almost hear it—not sound, but pressure, a want older than his fear. His whole life he had told himself the hiding was a choice. Careful, sensible Edmund, choosing invisibility. And underneath, all along, a locked door he'd mistaken for a wall.

"And the prophecy," he said. "Tonight's date. My name and yours. What happens tonight, Isolde?"

"Nothing has to happen tonight," she said—which was, he would learn, the way liars and prophets both begin their most important sentences. "The date marks a threshold, not an event. The door stands open for one night only, once a century. Tonight, the covenant *can* be reached. Touched. Perhaps tested." She met his eyes. "And the Ministry knows it too. Why do you think they came for you within hours of that book surfacing? Someone is watching the thresholds very closely indeed."

Above them, muffled by floors and frost-hardened earth, a whistle sounded. Then another. Then a third, closer, and boots on the street outside—many boots, moving in that unhurried coordinated rhythm that meant a cordon closing, not a patrol passing.

Isolde snuffed the candle with a pinch and a whispered syllable, and in the sudden dark her voice came low and steady:

"They found us faster than I hoped, which means I was right about everything, which means you're going to have to trust me, Edmund Ravensworth, whether you like it or not."

He could not see her in the blackness. He could see, perfectly, the glint of the Ministry's seal-lanterns sliding under the bark-shed door.

"All right," Edmund said—to her, to the dark, to the century-old ink drying on his name. "Show me the way out."

* * *

Interlude — what Isolde did not say

Later, much later, when he slept and she kept the watch, Isolde Vane took out the copy of the page she had carried for four years and read it again by touch, needing no light.

*Edmund Ravensworth and Isolde Vane shall stand at the broken door.*

The book named two names. The book did not say that only one of them would be able to pay what the door demanded. The book did not say what she had done, nine years ago, the last time a threshold opened—the reason her circles were taken, the reason her name was struck from the keepers' rolls, the reason she had sworn, alone in the ruins of her family's office, that if the door ever opened again she would not fail it twice.

She looked at the sleeping hedge-mage, this man who had hidden so long he'd forgotten he was hiding, whose bloodline was written into the bones of the world, and she felt the old vow settle over her like armor settling onto old scars.

One of them would pay. She had already chosen. She would simply have to make sure he never found out in time to argue.

Outside, the seal-lanterns swept the street, and Isolde Vane put the page away, and began, quietly, to draw the sigils that would take them both out of Vellumgate alive.
