## 4. What the Ministry Keeps

The Ministry of Seals did not look like a place where stolen things were kept. It looked like a bank, or a courthouse, or any other building that had been designed to make a person feel small and in the wrong before they'd done anything at all.

Edmund stood across the square from it in the blue hour before dawn, his collar up, his breath fogging, and studied the façade the way he'd once studied lock mechanisms—looking for the seam, the flaw, the honest weakness in an dishonest face. Pale granite, quarried from somewhere north and cold. Columns thick as old oaks. Above the doors, carved deep enough to survive a century of rain, the Ministry's motto: *ORDER IS THE FIRST MERCY.*

"Charming," Isolde murmured beside him. She had a way of speaking that made even two words sound like a verdict. "They put mercy first so no one asks what came second."

"And what came second?"

She didn't answer. Her eyes were on the doors, and there was something in her stillness that he was learning to read—not calm, but compression, like a spring held under a flat hand. She had been a sigil-keeper once. Whatever this building had taken from her, she had walked past these doors before as someone else entirely.

"The archive is below," she said at last. "Three levels down. The public registry occupies the ground floor—deeds, licenses, seal-registrations for lawful charm-wrights. Anyone can request a document." A thin smile. "Anyone who fills out the forms in triplicate and waits six weeks."

"We don't have six weeks."

"No. We have until the night the prophecy named, which is tomorrow, and until Seraphel's people stop chasing the wrong man through Vellumgate's lower quarters, which may be sooner than either of us would like." She turned to him. In the pre-dawn grey her face looked carved, all planes and shadow. "So we go tonight, through the way I know. And you do exactly what I tell you, exactly when I tell you, because the archive is not guarded by men, Edmund. Men can be bribed, distracted, pitied. The archive is guarded by seals."

He'd heard the word all his life, worn smooth by official use—the Ministry of Seals, seal-registrars, sealed documents—but hearing it from her mouth stripped the varnish off. These were not wax stamps and ribbons. These were the working edges of magic, turned inward, made into teeth.

"What kind of seals?"

"The kind my order used to forge." Something moved behind her eyes, too fast to name. "Before my order was dissolved for asking inconvenient questions about where confiscated magic went."

*Where confiscated magic went.* He turned the phrase over as they crossed the square, keeping to the shadow of the colonnade, past a pair of Ministry clerks unlocking the great doors for the day shift. Ordinary people going to ordinary work. That was the horror of it, he thought—that the machine needed only ordinary hands to keep turning.

* * *

Isolde's way in was a drainage culvert.

Of course it was, Edmund thought, waist-deep in water that smelled of tallow-rendering and something older, something mineral and strange, like the inside of a bell. The culvert ran beneath the Ministry's western foundation, and according to Isolde it had been built—deliberately, quietly—by the sigil-keepers themselves, a hundred years ago, when the Ministry was young and the keepers still believed they could watch it honestly from within.

"They built their own back door into the house of law," she said, wading ahead with a hooded lantern held low. "Arrogant of them. Lucky for us."

"You were one of them."

"I was trained by the last of them." Her voice bounced off wet stone. "I never served. I was expelled before my keeping." A pause, long enough for three steps of sloshing water. "For forging a seal I wasn't authorized to forge. To read a document I wasn't authorized to read."

"Did you find what you were looking for?"

"I found out how much there was left to look for." She stopped. Ahead, the culvert branched, and the air changed—colder, drier, carrying a faint hum that Edmund felt in his molars before he heard it with his ears. "We're here. From this point, everything you feel is real. Don't trust your instincts. They'll lie to you beautifully."

She set the lantern down and drew from her pack a strip of pale leather, covered edge to edge in inked characters that seemed to crawl when he wasn't looking directly at them. She pressed it against the culvert wall, spoke four syllables that hurt slightly to hear, and the wall—there was no other way to describe it—*agreed*. A section of stone the size of a door dimmed, softened, became passable, like a membrane remembering it had once been skin.

"That seal will hold the passage open for one hour," she said. "After that, it closes and forgets us. So we move quickly, we touch nothing without my leave, and if I say run, you run and do not ask why. Understood?"

"Understood."

"One more thing." She caught his sleeve. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "In there, you're going to feel something. Every hedge-mage does, the first time they come near hoarded power. It will feel like coming home. It will feel like your whole life you've been thirsty and here, finally, is water. It is not water. It is bait. Do you understand me, Edmund?"

He thought of his little shop, the luck-charms and fever-knots, the true magic folded small beneath the trinkets like a knife wrapped in cloth. He thought of thirty years of being careful.

"Yes," he said. "Bait."

* * *

The archive did not look like a vault. It looked like a library that had been designed by someone who hated libraries.

Shelves rose into darkness beyond the reach of Isolde's lantern, iron-framed, ranked in perfect rows, and every shelf was full—not of books, though there were books, but of *objects*. Sealed jars. Caged lamps. Boxes banded in lead. Musical instruments with their strings wound in wire. A child's wooden horse, its painted eye covered by a scrap of parchment inscribed with a sigil Edmund recognized from his mother's few surviving possessions: *containment, gentle.*

And underneath it all, beneath the sight and the silence, ran the hum. It was louder here, or perhaps it was simply that he could no longer pretend it was his imagination. Power. Vast, layered, patient power, stacked like grain in a tithe-barn.

"Oh," Edmund said. It was not a word; it was a wound opening.

"Say nothing yet," Isolde whispered. "Look first. Grieve after."

She led him down an aisle marked with a brass plate: *COVENANT DOCUMENTS — RESTRICTED — SEAL OF THE SECOND ORDER REQUIRED.* At its end stood a reading desk, and above the desk, suspended in a globe of glass, hung a seal of light that turned slowly, shedding no warmth. Isolde unrolled another leather strip, older, cracked with age, its sigils faded almost to memory.

"This is my master's work," she said quietly. "Forged the year before they burned him. It should pass." She hesitated, and for the first time since he'd known her, her hands were not steady. "It has never been tested. If it fails, we have perhaps ten seconds before the whole floor wakes."

"And then?"

"Then you learn whether you can outrun a building."

She pressed the leather to the desk. The faded sigils drank the light of the hanging seal—and held. The globe flickered, considered, and dimmed to a sullen ember, satisfied or fooled, Edmund couldn't tell which and decided not to care.

A drawer opened itself at Isolde's touch. Inside lay documents bound in grey vellum, and Edmund reached for them with hands that had begun, without his permission, to shake.

The first was the covenant itself. Or rather—a certified copy, stamped and countersigned, but the original text, the words as they had been sworn. He read standing up, because he suspected that if he sat he might not stand again.

*Let the mortal realm and the dragon-realms be bound in exchange eternal,* he read. *What mortal hands kindle shall feed the fire that blesses the land. What dragon-fire blesses shall feed the hands that kindle. Neither realm shall hoard. Neither realm shall hunger while the other feasts. This is the balance, and the balance is the mercy.*

*Order is the first mercy.* The motto over the doors. They had taken half a sentence and built an empire on it.

"There's more," Isolde said. Her voice had gone very quiet, the voice of someone standing at the edge of a drop. "Read the marginalia. The registrar's notes."

The margins were dense with a clerk's precise hand, dated across decades. Edmund read, and the shaking in his hands stopped, replaced by something colder and far more dangerous.

*Year 12: Confiscation of unregistered workings proceeds. Storage question unresolved—objects retain charge indefinitely. Recommend study.*

*Year 19: First successful transfer of stored charge to Ministry seal-work. The wards on this building now draw from the hoard. Cost savings considerable.*

*Year 31: Note from the Lord Registrar: the common belief that outlawed magic is destroyed upon confiscation is useful and should be encouraged.*

*Year 44: The hoard now powers the city's boundary-seals, the Registrar's private workings, and the Minister's—*

The entry stopped. Not faded—*stopped*, cut off mid-word, and beneath it, in different ink, a single line:

*This page reviewed and amended by order of the Second Seal. The original entry is retained in the deep archive. Access denied.*

"They didn't ban magic," Edmund said. His own voice sounded foreign to him, flattened, as if heard through a wall. "They nationalized it. They've been feeding on it for a hundred years. Every charm they've ever confiscated, every hedge-mage they've burned or broken or driven underground—it all went into *this*. Into their walls. Their wards. Their—" He stopped, because the next thought arrived fully formed and terrible: *their Minister.*

"Not just that." Isolde had pulled a ledger from the bottom of the drawer, and her face in the lantern light was the color of paper. "Look at the intake rolls. By district. By year."

He looked. The rolls listed seizures, thousands of them, itemized with bureaucratic tenderness. Luck-charms. Fever-knots. Hearth-blessings. Rain-songs. And beside each entry, a valuation, and beside each valuation, a destination code. Almost none of the codes read *destroyed.*

One entry near the middle of the page stopped his heart. Dated forty-one years ago. District: Vellumgate, Charm-Quarter. Item: *bloodline sigil, Raven line, active bearer deceased, minor heir unaccounted for.* Disposition: *sigil scarred over in situ per Standing Order Nine. Heir remanded to civilian life. Monitoring discontinued Year 3.*

*Scarred over.* Someone had sealed his magic before he was old enough to know he had any. Someone had stood over a sleeping child and closed a door inside him, and written it down, and filed it, and gone to lunch.

"Edmund." Isolde's hand was on his arm. "Edmund. Breathe."

"I'm breathing." He found that his anger had passed through fear and out the other side into a terrible clarity, a cold bright room where everything was visible. All those years of hiding, of folding himself small, of selling luck to frightened people while the Ministry sat on a mountain of stolen fire—they hadn't been enforcing safety. They'd been *farming* him. Farming all of them. Keeping the fields fallow so the crop belonged to one barn.

"The crops fail," he said slowly. "The fires gutter. The dragons are dying. Because the exchange was broken—not by accident, not by war. By theft. Deliberate, patient, century-long theft, dressed up as law." He looked at Isolde. "Your order asked where the magic went."

"My order asked where the magic went," she agreed, "and was dissolved for it. My master was burned for it. And I—" the crack in her voice was small but real, "—I spent twenty years telling myself exile was survival. It was surrender with better manners."

Above them, faintly, through floors of stone, came the sound of the Ministry waking—doors, voices, the day's machinery beginning to turn. Their hour was bleeding away.

But Edmund found he could not move. He stood in the aisle with the child's wooden horse at his shoulder, its painted eye blindfolded in containment-script, and he understood suddenly, completely, why the prophecy had put his name beside tomorrow's date. Not fate. Arithmetic. The hoard was nearly full. The land was nearly dead. The balance was coming due, and the debt had his family's name on it.

"Isolde." His voice was steady now, and that steadiness frightened him more than any trembling had. "Standing Order Nine. Who signed it? Who gave the order to scar a child?"

She took the ledger back, traced the column with one finger, and went very still.

"The authorization signature," she said, "is the same hand that appears throughout these rolls. The same hand that amended the Year 44 entry. It's not a registrar's mark." She looked up at him, and in her eyes he saw the drop she was standing at the edge of. "It's the Minister's. Lady Seraphel signed your sealing personally, Edmund. Forty-one years ago. She knew what you were before you did."

Somewhere above them, a bell began to ring—not the hour, but the alarm, thin and urgent, rolling down through the stone. Isolde snuffed the lantern with a pinch of her fingers.

"Change of plans," she breathed. "We don't leave the way we came."
