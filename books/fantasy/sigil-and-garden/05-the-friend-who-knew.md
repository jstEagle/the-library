## 5. The Friend Who Knew

The rain came to Vellumgate the way debt came—slowly at first, then all at once, and never where you wanted it. Edmund watched it silver the cobbles of Chandler's Row from behind his shop window, one hand resting on a jar of luck-charms that were, tonight, only glass and wire and wishful thinking. He had not worked true magic in three days. Not since the book. Not since his name.

His hands still remembered how.

That was the trouble with hiding a thing for twenty years: it didn't die. It waited in the fingers like an old song, humming under the skin whenever he let his guard slip. He'd caught himself warming a customer's tea with a thought on Tuesday, and had spent the rest of the afternoon scrubbing counters that were already clean, as if penance could be done with lye water.

The bell over his door rang at half past seven, and Corwin Ash came in shaking rain off his coat like a wet dog, all elbows and apology.

"Closed," Edmund said, though he hadn't turned the sign.

"You're never closed. You're *open* in a way that's technically closed." Corwin dropped onto the stool by the counter, the one he'd claimed so long ago it might as well have been mortgaged. He was thirty now, same as Edmund, but the years had dealt with them differently. Where Edmund had learned to fold himself small, Corwin had grown outward—broader shoulders, a better coat, a laugh that filled rooms whether they wanted filling or not. "I brought bread. The good kind, from Marla's, before the queue. Which means I love you more than I love anyone else alive, including myself."

"Low bar."

"Astoundingly low bar." He set the loaf on the counter between them like a treaty document. Then he looked around the shop, and something in his looking was different—slower, more deliberate, the way a man reads a room he's already memorized. "Quiet night?"

"Rain keeps people home."

"Mm." Corwin tore the bread, handed him the larger piece without appearing to notice he'd done it. He'd been doing that since they were nine years old, since the winter Edmund's mother died and Corwin's family had quietly absorbed him into their suppers, their Sundays, their life. Twenty years of larger pieces. Twenty years of not noticing.

Edmund ate, and watched the rain, and felt the prophecy-book's weight sitting in his chest where no book actually was. *Edmund Ravensworth.* His true name, the one buried with his mother, inked in a century-old hand beside tonight's date. Beside another name too: *Isolde Vane*, who had appeared out of the Ministry's own shadow three nights ago with a knife, a warning, and a story about dragons and covenants that should have been madness and wasn't.

He had told no one. He had especially not told Corwin, because telling Corwin would make it real, and because Corwin was the last unpoisoned thing in his life—the friend-shaped proof that Edmund Hale, charm-seller, was a man someone would miss.

But Isolde had said the Ministry was closing in. She had said names were surfacing in old records, that snatch-squads had been reassigned from smugglers to *heirs*. And she had said, in her flat, unsentimental way, that a man alone in a city is a man already caught.

So here was Corwin, dripping on his stool, breaking bread, and here was Edmund about to do the most dangerous magic he'd attempted in twenty years: he was going to tell the truth.

"Corwin."

"Hm?"

"If I told you something—if I told you something enormous—and asked you to sit on it, could you?"

Corwin chewed. Swallowed. For a moment his face did something complicated, a whole weather system passing behind his eyes, and then it settled into the familiar grin. "Depends. Is it about a woman? Because if it's about a woman I reserve the right to be insufferable about it first and discreet after."

"It's not about a woman."

"Pity." But the grin stayed a half-second too long, and Edmund saw beneath it, the way you see the current under ice—a flicker of strain, of calculation, there and gone. Or maybe he imagined it. Maybe fear made everyone look like strangers. He thought of Isolde saying, *Trust is just a slower form of betrayal,* and hated her a little for putting the sentence in his head.

He got up, checked the door bolt, drew the blind. Small rituals. Then he reached under the counter and brought out the thing he'd sworn he'd destroyed years ago: a plain iron nail, unremarkable except that it hummed faintly against his palm like a struck tuning fork.

"Watch," Edmund said, and let go of the hiding.

It had been so long since he'd allowed himself even this much. The power rose through him like water finding an old channel—warm, familiar, *his*, whatever Isolde said about whose it truly was. The nail lifted off his palm. It hung in the lamplight, turning slowly, and frost feathered across its surface in patterns too regular for weather, spirals within spirals, a script older than the city.

Corwin had stopped breathing.

The nail drifted between them, patient as a moon. In its frosted curves Edmund could almost read the old shapes—the covenant-script Isolde had scratched into his tabletop while explaining bloodlines and bound realms, the sigil of the ravens that his mother had once pressed into his skin with a kiss and a whisper: *never show anyone, my heart, never, never.*

He closed his fist. The nail dropped. The frost melted.

"That," Corwin said finally, very quietly, "is a hanging offense."

"Yes."

"You've been doing—that—for how long?"

"My whole life. Properly, since I was twelve." Edmund sat back down. His hands weren't steady, so he folded them. "You remember when my mother died. You remember what she was like before—the herbs she sold, the things people whispered. It wasn't herbs, Corwin. We come from somewhere else. Somewhere the Ministry erased. And I think—" His voice snagged. He started again. "I think they're coming for me. And I don't know why yet, not fully, but I found my name in a book that was written before either of us was born, next to a date that was three days ago, and everything I've built—this shop, this street, this *life*—it's paper over a hole, and the wind's found the corner of it."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere down the row, a cart rattled past, and both of them flinched at it, and pretended they hadn't.

Corwin looked at the nail on the counter. At Edmund. At the nail again. And Edmund watched his oldest friend's face with the terrible new attention that betrayal teaches you retroactively, cataloguing every twitch, every blink, hunting for the moment the arithmetic changed.

"You should burn that," Corwin said at last. "The nail. Everything. If they're really—Edmund, if what you're saying is true, then the smartest thing you can do is disappear. Tonight. There are boats out of Greyharbor, there's the coast road—"

"I'm not running."

"Why not?" It came out sharper than Corwin probably meant it, sharp enough that he heard it himself and softened. "Edmund. Listen to me. Whatever this is—dragons, prophecies, whatever nonsense that woman fed you—it's bigger than you. Bigger than both of us. You can't fight the Ministry. Nobody fights the Ministry. People *survive* the Ministry, and the ones who survive are the ones who stay small and quiet and *boring*." He was leaning forward now, urgent, gripping the counter's edge. "You've stayed boring for twenty years. Don't throw it away three days after some stranger shows up with a story."

"She didn't feed me a story. She saved my life. There were four of them, Corwin, in grey coats, with seal-irons, and they knew my face."

Something crossed Corwin's expression then—guilt, unmistakably guilt, a flash of it like a fish turning under dark water—and Edmund's stomach went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"Knew your face," Corwin repeated carefully.

"Their captain called me by name. My *real* name." Edmund held his gaze. "How would they know my real name, Corwin? I've been Edmund Hale for twenty years. My mother took that name to her grave. There are perhaps three people alive who ever knew otherwise, and two of them are dead."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain thickened.

"Maybe the book," Corwin said. "You said there was a book. Maybe they found the same book."

"Maybe." Edmund let the word sit there, thin and unconvinced, and watched his friend not quite meet his eyes. "Corwin. Look at me."

And Corwin looked at him—God help them both, he looked at him, with those honest brown eyes that had never once lied to Edmund across twenty years of scraped knees and stolen apples and funerals—and for one suspended moment Edmund saw the whole shape of it, the trap and the tragedy of it, a man standing on a bridge between two things he loved with the water rising on both sides.

Then Corwin shifted on the stool, and his coat swung open, and the lamplight caught metal.

A pin. On the left lapel, where a man wore his allegiances. Silver-grey, octagonal, worked with a design Edmund had seen ten thousand times on ten thousand doors and documents and prison carts without ever truly seeing it: a closed eye above a closed hand.

The seal of the Ministry of Seals.

Not the tin badge of an informer. Not the brass of a junior clerk. Silver. A *seat*-holder's pin, or near enough to one.

The floor of the world tilted, gently, the way it had tilted in the bookshop when he'd read his own name. Edmund found that his body kept working—kept breathing, kept a neutral pleasant expression on his face—while somewhere underneath, quietly and completely, something broke.

"When," he said. His voice sounded fine. Distant, but fine. "When did you join them?"

Corwin's hand came up halfway toward the pin—an instinctive gesture, covering it, hiding it, which was itself an answer—and then fell. "Three months ago."

"Three months." Three months. Before the book. Before Isolde. Before any of it. While they'd drunk together at the Drowned Bell, while Corwin had helped him re-shingle the roof, while he'd sat on exactly that stool eating exactly that bread—Corwin had been wearing the enemy under his collar like a second heartbeat. "The promotion. The 'clerkship in the Chancery.' That was—"

"It's not what you think."

"Tell me what it is, then." Edmund stood. He hadn't decided to stand; his legs had simply concluded the conversation ahead of him. "Tell me what a man does for the Ministry of Seals for three months and doesn't mention to his best friend. Tell me what they pay, Corwin. What's the going rate these days for a seat at the table?"

"It's not like that." Corwin was up too, hands out, palms showing, the universal language of *don't*. "Edmund, listen—I took it to be *inside*. You think I wanted it? They've been watching this quarter for a year. Watching charm-sellers, hedge-folk, everyone. Someone was going to be their man on Chandler's Row, and I thought—if it has to be someone, let it be someone who loves you. Let it be me."

"And has it worked? Has loving me changed anything they've done?"

The question landed like a stone in still water, and the ripples went out and out, and Corwin had no answer for any of them. He opened his mouth. Closed it. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing, roughened at the edges.

"There's a list."

The rain filled the pause.

"What list?"

"Names. Old names, dug out of the archives—records nobody's touched in a hundred years. They're calling it the Reclamation. Seraphel's own project, straight from the Minister's office." Corwin's eyes were wet, or the lamplight made them look wet. "Your name is on it, Edmund. It was on it before you ever saw that book. That's why the squad came. That's why they knew your face. I didn't—I swear to you, I didn't give them anything, but I *knew*, I've known for weeks, and I kept telling myself I'd warn you when there was time, when I'd found a way to take you off it—"

"*Weeks.*"

"They're moving tomorrow night. A sweep of the whole quarter. Everyone on the list goes to the Hall of Seals for 'questioning,' and nobody comes back from questioning, we both know that—" He grabbed Edmund's sleeve, and Edmund let him, because some animal part of him still trusted those hands even now, even with the silver eye glinting on the coat above them. "Run. Tonight. I mean it. Forget the boats, forget the roads—they'll watch the roads. Go out through the Undercroft, the old ways, and don't stop until you're past the walls. Please. Please, Edmund."

Edmund looked down at the hand on his sleeve. At the bread, abandoned on the counter, torn open, going cold. At the little iron nail lying in its puddle of meltwater, harmless now, just iron.

Twenty years of larger pieces. Twenty years of a friend shaped like a brother. And underneath it all, three months—or longer, how much longer, how long does a man wear a secret before it wears him—of belonging to the thing that hunted him.

"You should go," Edmund said quietly.

"Edmund—"

"Before I say something we can't take back. Go home, Corwin. Sleep in your Ministry bed. And tomorrow, when they ask you where you were tonight, you tell them you were nowhere, you saw nothing, you know nothing." He peeled the hand from his sleeve, gently, the way you'd remove a bandage. "Because if you cover for me, that's treason. And if you report me, that's—you. And I find I can't make you choose tonight. I don't trust you to choose right, and I don't want to watch you choose wrong."

Corwin stood in the middle of the shop with the rain loud behind him and his face coming apart by degrees, the grin long gone, the boy from the winter suppers surfacing through the man like a drowning swimmer. "There's a third thing," he said hoarsely. "There's always a third thing."

"Then find it fast."

At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and didn't turn around. "The woman," he said. "Vane. If she's mixed up in this—be careful. She's not what she says she is. None of them are."

"How would you know what she is?"

Corwin's shoulders rose and fell once. "Because her name's on the list too," he said, and pulled the door open, and the rain took him.

* * *

Edmund sat alone for a long time after the bell stopped swinging.

He lit no lamp he didn't need. He packed nothing—he couldn't pack, packing was a confession to any watcher—but he moved through the shop touching things: the jars of false charms, the counter worn smooth by his father-in-trade's ghost, the stool where Corwin sat. Grief, he discovered, felt exactly like inventory.

Isolde would be waiting at the tannery on Mote Street, as arranged, impatient and armed and probably right about everything. He should go now. Every minute was a brick in the wall forming around the quarter.

But first he stood at the window, in the dark, and looked out at Chandler's Row glistening under the lamps—at Marla's bakery, dark now; at the Drowned Bell, where someone was laughing; at the whole small warm organism of a street that had never known his real name and loved him anyway.

Tomorrow night, Corwin had said. A sweep. Everyone on the list.

Everyone.

Marla bought luck-charms. So did the widow Prentice, and old Tam with his bad knee, and a dozen others who'd never done a day of magic in their lives but liked the idea of being lucky. Their names wouldn't be on any list. Lists didn't need names when a sweep was a net, and nets caught whatever swam.

Edmund's hand curled slowly shut around the iron nail until the edges bit.

He had spent twenty years learning to be invisible, and he had believed—in the way a man believes the loan will never be called in—that invisibility was safety. Now he understood it for what it was: rent paid to a landlord who burned houses. His hiding had not protected the street. It had merely guaranteed that when the fire came, no one on Chandler's Row would know there was a man among them with water in his hands.

He put on his coat. He blew out the lamp. And Edmund Hale, charm-seller, stepped out into the rain for the last time, carrying his true name through the dark toward Mote Street—while behind him, three streets away, a lantern burned late in a Chancery window, and a man sat at a desk with a blank report form and a pen that would not move, and outside his door the corridor filled, quietly, with the sound of grey coats.
