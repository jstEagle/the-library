## 6. Down the Root-Road

Edmund had lived in Vellumgate for nineteen years and had never once seen the city from behind. From the rooftops of the charm-quarter you saw the river, the Ministry spires, the smoke of a thousand chimneys—all the face the city showed its people. But crouched in the drainage culvert beneath Tanner's Row, looking back through the grate at the lamplit streets he was abandoning, Edmund saw Vellumgate's spine: the long ridge of the old city wall buried under centuries of building, running away north like the back of some enormous sleeping animal.

"They'll check the river gates first," Isolde whispered beside him. "Then the coach yards. Then they'll start on the sewers." She said it without drama, the way she seemed to say everything—as though fear were simply another tool to be kept sharp and used correctly.

"Then we should be quick."

"We should be quiet." She pressed two fingers to his lips before he could argue, then pointed down the culvert, where the water ran shallow and cold over stones that were not quite stones. He'd noticed it already and told himself he hadn't: the rocks lining this stretch of tunnel were too regular, too smoothly curved, their surfaces worn in patterns like the grain of wood. Like grain, in fact. Like the growth rings of something that had once been alive.

"The root-road," Isolde said, reading his expression with something close to satisfaction. "You've felt it, haven't you? Every time you walked over the old quarter, near the tanners' guildhall. A hum in your teeth."

He had. He'd assumed it was the tannery machinery.

"Vellumgate didn't grow up on empty ground," she went on, already wading into the water. "It grew up on top of the roads the dragons made. Not roads for feet—for *sap*. For fire moving slow, the way blood moves slow. The Ministry paved them because paving is forgetting. But stone cracks, and things remember." She glanced back at him, her face half-lit by the grate-light above. "Keep your hand on the wall. The road will know what you are before I can explain it."

"What I am," Edmund repeated. His name—his true name, Ravensworth, written in ash a century before his birth—still sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. "And what is that, exactly? You still haven't told me plainly."

"No," she agreed. "Because you wouldn't believe me in daylight, standing on cobbles, with your little shop and your little life around you convincing you otherwise." She started forward, boots splashing softly. "Walk first. Believe after."

The culvert bent twice, dropped through a fall of broken brick, and opened without warning into darkness so complete that Edmund's eyes reported nothing at all—not blackness but absence, as though he had been closed like a book. Behind them, faintly, came the sound of a whistle. Ministry signal, three notes rising.

Isolde froze. So did Edmund—and in the freezing, he felt it: a pulse under his palm where it rested against the curved wall. Slow. Deep. Patient beyond all human patience.

The root-road had noticed him.

"Hand stays on the wall," Isolde murmured. "Always on the wall. It's older than oaths and it keeps them better than the Ministry ever did. Touch it, and it counts you as traveling, not trespassing." A pause. "Mostly."

They walked. The dark did not lift, but it stopped being empty; Edmund began to sense the tunnel's shape the way a blind man senses a room, by pressure and echo and the behavior of his own breath. The passage widened. The ceiling rose until his outstretched hand found only air, and the wall itself grew smoother, warmer, faintly ridged in rings wide enough that he could have lain across one. Somewhere ahead—or perhaps everywhere ahead—the air moved with a current that smelled of loam and rain on hot stone.

"How far does it go?"

"To the borderlands. Two days walking, if the road allows it." Isolde's voice had changed in the dark—looser, almost young. "It allowed me, once. When I was seventeen and the Sigil-Keepers' Hall cast me out, I walked this road back toward the city and it let me go no farther than the third ring. It knew I was returning to serve the wrong master." A short laugh, dry as ash. "Roads have opinions. That's the thing nobody tells you about the old world. Everything has opinions. Stone, rivers, doors. We built Vellumgate to be a city of things with *no* opinions—things that just sit there and obey. No wonder it feels dead. It's not dead. It's silenced."

Edmund thought of his shop. Of the luck-charms and hearth-blessings and love-knots he sold at three coppers each, each one carrying a thread of true working hidden under harmless craft, each sale an act of courage so small it looked exactly like cowardice. Nineteen years of being a thing with no opinions.

"I used to think I was hiding my magic," he said slowly. "From the Ministry. But most days I think I was hiding it from myself. If I never used it properly, never let it grow, then it could stay small enough to deny."

"And now?"

"Now there's a book with my name in it and a date that's today, and a woman who fights Ministry snatch-crews like she's swatting flies, telling me to put my hand on a dragon-road." He laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, echoing strange and large in the dark. "Now I'm asking it to hurry."

As if in answer, warmth bloomed under his palm. Not much. A degree or two, no more than a sun-struck windowsill. But Edmund had spent nineteen years learning to read magic the way a miser reads coin, and he knew what he felt. The road had answered. Something vast and half-asleep, somewhere along the miles of buried sap-vein, had turned its attention toward a frightened charm-seller and said, in whatever language roots speak:

*I know you.*

He walked faster, and did not let go of the wall.

* * *

Isolde had lied about the road knowing her.

Not entirely—it had known her, had refused her passage once, and the memory of that refusal still stung like a slap from a parent. But she had not told Edmund the rest: that the road had also *recognized* her sigil-work when she touched it tonight, had pulsed with something that might, in a less disciplined mind, be called welcome. Keeper-trained hands were known to these veins. Her order had tended them, before the Ministry burned the Hall and salted its foundations.

She kept that to herself. Trust was a currency she'd learned to spend carefully, and she'd already spent more on Edmund Ravensworth in three days than in the six years since her exile. It wasn't sentiment. It was arithmetic: the prophecy named them together, the covenant needed a Raven heir, and this nervous, self-deprecying man with ink stains on his fingers was the last of that line whether he liked it or not. She needed him functional. Fear made people stupid; wonder made them brave. So she would feed him wonder, carefully measured, and keep her own history locked where it belonged.

But the borderlands tested her resolve, because the borderlands were not careful.

They surfaced near dawn, through a seam between two leaning stones in a hillside overgrown with broom and gorse, and Isolde climbed out into air that hit her like a memory. Clean air. Cold, but alive-cold, the cold of a living body rather than a corpse. In Vellumgate even winter smelled of coal and sewage and the Ministry's seal-smoke. Here the dawn smelled of wet grass, distant peat, and—faintly, unmistakably, impossibly—

Smoke. Sweet smoke. Hearthwood burning the way hearthwood was meant to burn.

"Do you smell it?" she asked, and hated how eager her voice came out.

Edmund stood beside her, blinking in the grey light, his hair plastered flat by the tunnel damp, looking younger and more lost than any heir of an ancient covenant had a right to look. "Smoke? There's a farmstead maybe. People live out here."

"Not farm smoke. Listen—" She took his wrist and held his palm up, open, the way Brannoc had taught her twenty years ago in a courtyard full of students who were all dead now. "Feel. Not with your skin. With whatever's under your skin that you've spent nineteen years sitting on."

For a long moment nothing. Isolde watched his face go through skepticism, concentration, embarrassment—and then the embarrassment fell away all at once, replaced by something naked and unguarded that made her look quickly elsewhere, because it was too private to witness.

"Oh," Edmund breathed. "*Oh.*"

"There," she said quietly. "There it is."

"It's everywhere." He turned in a slow circle, arm still raised as if feeling rain. "It's thin. Threadbare. Like—like a blanket washed too many times. But it's *everywhere*. In Vellumgate there are pockets, corners, my cellar, the crypt under St. Verity's—but here it's not hiding. It's just... lying out in the open, all over the fields, and it's *starving*, Isolde. Why is it starving?"

And there it was: the question she had carried across six years and half the realm, the question that had gotten her order murdered, and here was a hedge-mage who'd never read a covenant text arriving at it in four minutes of standing in a field.

"Because it's fed by exchange," she said. "That was the whole design. Mortal magic flows outward, feeds the dragon-realms; dragon-fire flows back, blesses soil and flame and childbirth and harvest. A circulation. Blood in a body." She knelt and pressed her own hand flat against the turf, letting herself feel what she already knew: the thin, wheezing trickle of returning fire, barely a pulse. "The Ministry broke the exchange. They dammed the mortal side—all that confiscated power in their vaults, all those sealed mages—and they told everyone the dragons died naturally, faded away, that outlawing magic was mercy. It wasn't mercy. It was theft. And everything downstream of a theft starves."

Edmund lowered his arm. He was staring out over the valley below them—the borderlands proper, patchwork fields and wild moor climbing toward blue-grey hills, a river stitching silver through it all—and Isolde watched him see it twice. Once with his eyes, and once with whatever new sense had cracked open in the dark of the root-road.

"The crops," he said at last, very quietly. "My mother's family came from farming stock, out past the Marches. She used to say the wheat got thinner every year of her childhood, and everyone blamed weather, blight, taxes. Everyone always blames taxes." His jaw tightened. "Fires gutter. My neighbor Marta's stove—she swears it eats wood twice as fast as her mother's did. Candles burn crooked. Children born small. We built a whole civilization of explanations."

"People prefer explanations to truths that require anything of them," Isolde said. "The Ministry understands that better than it understands seals." She stood, brushing earth from her knees. "Come on. We're visible on this skyline, and the road's exit won't stay unwatched forever."

"Where are we going?"

"North. To the Emberhold." She hesitated, then gave him the truth entire, because he'd earned a piece of it and because lies, she was learning, weighed heavier every year. "The last dragon-fire still burns there. Low. Guttering. Tended by a warden who was old when my teachers' teachers were children. If your name is truly in the prophecy—if the Raven Covenant is truly reborn through you—then it begins there. At the hearth. Where all of it began."

Edmund was silent for three steps, four, five. Then: "And if I'm not? If the book is wrong, or forged, or means someone else?"

"Then you'll finally get your ordinary life back," Isolde said, "and I'll have led a charm-seller on a very long walk for nothing, and we'll both be embarrassed."

He laughed again—that startled, rusty sound—and she found, to her mild alarm, that she'd timed the joke to make it happen. Six years of discipline, undone by a man with ink on his fingers.

They walked north, along hedgerows silvered with rime, while behind them the sun came up over the ruined exchange of two worlds, and the land breathed its thin, patient breath.

* * *

By midday Edmund had stopped counting marvels and started keeping them, the way a man might pocket interesting stones.

A stream that ran warm and smelled faintly of iron and, when he trailed his fingers in it, hummed against his skin like a struck string. A stand of birches whose leaves turned—there and gone, surely a trick of light—toward him as he passed, the way sunflowers track the sun. An old boundary marker, a squat pillar carved with spirals, where Isolde stopped and laid her palm and murmured something in a language with too few vowels, and the carved spirals glowed briefly gold, and she pretended she hadn't seen him see it.

"You could teach me that," he said, catching up to her on the moor path.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because your power isn't mine, and borrowing my forms will strangle yours before it learns to walk." She said it briskly, but he caught the flicker underneath—something guarded, something that flinched. He filed it away. He was learning that Isolde Vane had a room in her with the door shut, and that pressing on the door made her dangerous.

Instead he asked the question he'd been turning over since the field at dawn. "The exchange. If mortal magic feeds the dragons, and the dragons feed the land—what happens if the fire dies completely? Not gutters. Dies."

Isolde walked several paces before answering, and when she did, her voice had the flat evenness of someone reciting something learned by heart under terrible conditions.

"Soil stops accepting seed within a season. Fires won't catch from flint—you can strike all day; the spark simply won't take. Wells go bitter. Beasts miscarry. People don't die quickly." She looked at him. "People die slowly, and badly, and they invent explanations. Taxes. Weather. Blight. Gods' displeasure. And then, eventually, they die."

"And the Ministry knows this."

"The Ministry wrote the terms of surrender themselves, sixty years ago. They know exactly what they broke and what it costs. They've decided the cost is acceptable, provided it lands mostly on people like the ones who buy your charms." Her bitterness showed through, quick as a knife-flash, then sheathed again. "Which is why the prophecy matters, Edmund. Not because books of prophecy are reliable—they're mostly wishful thinking bound in calfskin. But because somewhere in the Ministry's vaults is a hoard of stolen fire big enough to restart the exchange, and somewhere in your blood is the key that was cut for that lock. The book just happens to agree with the arithmetic."

"A key," Edmund said. "You keep describing me as a key. Keys don't get a vote."

"No," Isolde said, and slowed, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse—regret, maybe, or apology arriving early. "Keys don't. That's rather the problem."

The wind came off the moor then, hard and clean, bending the heather in silver waves, and in the wind Edmund heard—or imagined he heard, though the hair on his arms insisted otherwise—a note underneath the whistle of it. Deep. Rhythmic. Like breathing. Like something enormous, miles north, drawing breath slowly in a stone room, and letting it out again, and holding on.

He stopped walking.

"Tell me you hear that," he said.

Isolde had gone still too, her head tilted, her face unreadable in the way he was coming to recognize as her version of strong feeling. The wind poured past them both, carrying the low, vast, patient rhythm.

"That's the Emberhold," she said at last. "Or rather—that's what's left inside the Emberhold. We're still a day out. You shouldn't be able to hear it yet." A pause. "Your hearing it means the bloodline's waking. Which means the seal on you is weaker than I thought, which means—"

She stopped. Edmund watched the calculation move through her like weather.

"Means what?" he asked. "Isolde. Means *what*?"

"Means we need to reach Brannoc before it wakes fully," she said, and started walking again, faster now, her cloak snapping in the wind. "Unsealed power with no training is a candle in a powder store. Come on, Ravensworth. Your inheritance is impatient."

Edmund stood a moment longer in the singing wind, listening to the great slow heartbeat of the last fire in the world, feeling it answer something in his chest beat for beat—and for the first time since he'd read his name written in ash, he was not afraid.

Then he ran to catch up with her, northward, toward the breathing dark.
