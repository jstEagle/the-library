## 7. The Guttering Hearth

Isolde felt the Emberhold before she saw it.

It came as a change in the air itself—a thinning of the Ministry's deadness, the way a deafened room changes when someone, far away, begins to play an instrument. For eleven days they had walked the root-road in silence so complete it had begun to feel less like quiet and more like absence, the hush of a house where something had been carried out and never returned. Now, as the road climbed through the last stand of black pines toward the shoulder of the mountain, Isolde stopped mid-stride and pressed her palm flat against the trunk of the nearest tree.

Bark under her fingers. Sap beneath the bark. And beneath the sap—faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall—warmth.

"What is it?" Edmund asked. He had learned to read her stillness by now, if not its meaning. Eleven days on the road will do that: teach you the shape of a person's silences before you know their words.

"The land's breathing again," she said. "Here. Only here." She pulled her hand back as though the tree had burned her—which, in a sense, she supposed it had. Everything warm was dangerous now. Everything alive reminded her what she'd lost. "We're close."

Edmund looked up the slope, where the pines thinned into grey scree and the sky hung low and colourless, the colour of pewter left out too long. He had been quiet for most of the journey—not sullen, never that, but careful, the way he was careful with everything, hoarding his words like coins he might need later. Isolde had decided somewhere around the fourth day that this was simply how hedge-mages survived in Vellumgate: they made themselves small and stayed small until smallness became a kind of armour. She had once been the same. She wondered, sometimes, whether she would have been, if her fall had been gentler—if disgrace could be worn lightly instead of seeping into the bone.

"Will there be anyone?" he asked. "At the hold?"

"One person. If we're fortunate." She started walking again, boots crunching on frost-stiffened needles. "Brannoc is not a man who enjoys fortune. Or visitors. Or questions." She glanced sideways at him. "He will test you. I want you prepared for that."

"Test me how? I don't even know what I'm supposed to be yet. You've told me stories, Isolde. The Raven Covenant. My bloodline. My mother's name written in your order's rolls like a line from a ledger. But stories aren't proof."

"No," she agreed. "Proof is proof. And Brannoc keeps it."

* * *

The Emberhold revealed itself all at once, the way old things do—as though it had been waiting behind the landscape's curtain for the right moment to step forward. One switchback more, and the pines fell away, and there it was: a fortress built not upon the mountain but out of it, its walls the same dark stone as the peak itself, so that at first glance there was nothing to see at all. Only when the low sun caught the eastern face did the windows show themselves—narrow slits glowing amber, a hundred of them, climbing the rockface like the lit windows of some impossible vertical city.

"It's inhabited," Edmund said, wonder cracking his voice open.

"It was. Once. Now—" Isolde counted the lit windows and felt her stomach drop. Three. Perhaps four. A century ago there had been two hundred keepers here, sigil-smiths and fire-wardens, an entire order devoted to tending the exchange between realms. Her grandmother had trained in these halls. Her grandmother had spoken of the Emberhold the way other people spoke of paradise.

Now three windows burned. That was all the world had left to spend.

The gate stood open, which frightened her more than a closed one would have. Closed gates meant caution; open gates meant either confidence or surrender, and she did not know which she'd find. Beyond the threshold, a courtyard opened onto the mountain itself, and at the courtyard's centre—

She heard Edmund's breath catch beside her, and understood why.

The hearth was vast. There was no other word for it. It was less a fireplace than an architecture, a circular basin of black ironstone thirty feet across, ringed by steps worn smooth by centuries of keeper-feet, and in its bowl lay fire—but fire unlike any Isolde had seen since her exile began. It did not leap. It breathed. Long, slow exhalations of gold and deep ember-red rolled across the coals and sank again, rhythmic, patient, ancient. The heat that rose from it was not the sharp dry heat of woodsmoke but something richer, heavier, carrying scents that had no names in the mortal tongue—oranges and hot metal and rain on distant stone—and beneath it all ran a sound so low that Isolde felt it in her sternum rather than heard it with her ears.

A heartbeat. The heartbeat of a realm.

But it was slow. Terribly slow. She remembered the fire of her childhood visits—her grandmother lifting her up to see, the flames standing tall and proud as banners—and compared it to this guttering, laboured respiration, each breath longer than the last, as though the fire were falling asleep and no one dared wake it.

Beside the hearth, stirring something in a copper pot no larger than a kettle—the absurd domesticity of it nearly undid her—stood the oldest man Isolde had ever seen.

* * *

Brannoc did not look up when they entered. He finished whatever he was doing with his pot—she caught the smell of nettle and pine-resin, medicine or tea or both—poured two cups anyway, and only then turned his head, unhurried, as though visitors arrived every day and always precisely when expected.

His eyes found Isolde first. They were pale, almost colourless, set in a face seamed and browned like old leather, and they held the particular patience of someone who has outlived everyone entitled to hurry him.

"Isolde Vane," he said. His voice was gravel over deep water. "The Vane family always did come home eventually. Usually too late." His gaze slid past her to Edmund, and everything in him changed—not dramatically, nothing so legible as surprise, but Isolde saw his shoulders settle, saw his weight shift to the balls of his feet, saw the warden emerge through the shell of the old man the way a blade emerges through a worn scabbard. "And you've brought me a raven."

"You can see—" Edmund began.

"I can see what's written on people," Brannoc said shortly. "It's the whole of my trade, boy. Sit down. Both of you. Drink that. It tastes like punishment and works like mercy." He lowered himself onto a bench carved from a single slab of mountain stone, and Isolde noticed for the first time the cane leaning against it—black ironwood, banded in silver, and along its length, sigils. Old ones. True ones. Her hands ached with wanting them.

She sat. She drank. It tasted like punishment and worked like mercy.

"Now," Brannoc said, folding his knotted hands, "you'll tell me why you've come, and then you'll tell me why you've lied about why you've come, and we'll get on from there."

Isolde set down her cup. "Warden. I've brought the heir of the Raven Covenant. Edmund Ravensworth, son of Maerwen Ravensworth, who—"

"Maerwen's son." Something moved behind the pale eyes—grief, perhaps, or its fossil. "Yes. She passed through here the winter before you were born. Frightened girl. Powerful beyond her knowing, and running from people who knew it better than she did." He turned to Edmund fully now, and Isolde watched the younger man straighten under that gaze like a plant finding light. "You'll want to know if you're what she says you are. So let's stop talking and find out. Give me your hand."

Edmund hesitated—one heartbeat, no more—and gave it.

Brannoc turned the palm upward, cradled it in both of his own gnarled hands, and bowed his head. His lips moved without sound. The air above the hearth seemed to lean inward. Isolde felt the pressure build the way it built before a storm, that ache behind the eyes, that taste of copper—

And then Brannoc went rigid.

His hands clamped around Edmund's wrist hard enough that Isolde saw the tendons stand out in the old man's forearms. His breath came ragged. When he raised his head, his colourless eyes were wet.

"Scarred," he whispered. "Oh, you poor child. Scarred over. Sealed."

"What?" Edmund tried to pull his hand back; Brannoc held fast. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Brannoc said, and his voice had dropped into something terrible and gentle, "that someone took your magic away from you. Not dampened it. Not taught you to hide it. Took it. Cut the sigil out of you and grew scar tissue over the wound, neat as a mason's work." He turned Edmund's palm toward the firelight, and Isolde leaned close, and there it was—invisible to any ordinary eye, but under Brannoc's grip and the hearth's strange light, surfacing like a drowned body rising slowly through water: a mark on Edmund's palm, or rather the ghost of a mark, a sigil rendered in the pale silver of healed flesh where every other line of his life had been inked in red.

The raven-sigil. The covenant-mark of the Raven bloodline. Sealed shut.

"I don't understand," Edmund said, and his voice was very small. "I'm a mage. I sell charms. I do magic. Small magic, but—"

"Sparks," Brannoc said. "Embers off a fire that's been banked. Whatever they left you, they left you just enough to survive on. Just enough that your blood wouldn't turn on itself and kill you. But the rest of it—" He shook his head slowly. "The rest of it is buried under that scar like a river under a landslide. And it's been there a long time, boy. This isn't fresh work. This is a child's wound."

Isolde watched Edmund's face and knew, before he spoke, exactly which question was forming—because it was the same one that had formed in her own mind the moment she'd first touched the prophecy book, the same cold arithmetic that every hidden thing forces upon its hider.

"When?" he asked quietly. "How old was I?"

Brannoc met his eyes. "Four years old. Perhaps five."

The silence that followed was the loudest Isolde had ever heard. Even the great hearth seemed to hold its breath, its golden exhalations slowing further, further, as though the fire itself were listening.

"My mother," Edmund said at last. Each word came out carefully placed, like stones across deep water. "My mother hid me in Vellumgate. You said so yourself—she was running. Was it her?" His voice cracked on the second word and he mastered it. "Did she seal me? Did she take it away herself?"

"She may have ordered it done," Brannoc said gently. "I doubt she wielded the knife. A sealing of that quality—that permanence—took a master sigil-keeper." His gaze flicked to Isolde, brief and unreadable, and Isolde felt the old shame rise in her throat like bile. "There were few enough of those left. Fewer every year."

"And she never told me." Edmund withdrew his hand at last, cradling it against his chest as though it belonged to a stranger. "Twenty-nine years. I lived in that city for twenty-nine years thinking I was hiding something small. Thinking I was lucky to have escaped notice. And the whole time—" He laughed, one short, broken sound with no humour in it whatsoever. "The whole time the thing they were hiding wasn't my magic. It was *me*. I was the treasure. I was the thing worth sealing away in a box."

"Your mother saved your life," Isolde said. "Whatever else she did—"

"You don't know that." He turned on her, and for the first time since the alley outside the bookshop, since the snatch-squad and the sigils flaring blue in the dark, Isolde saw real anger in him—not fear wearing anger's clothes, but anger itself, clean and burning. "Neither of us knows that. Maybe she saved my life. Maybe she stole it. Maybe those are the same act and maybe they're not, and I'll never know, because she's dead, and whoever cut this—" he thrust the pale palm toward them both— "is dead or vanished or sitting in a Ministry office counting confiscated fire, and I am the only living soul who has to *be* the answer to that question."

The echo of his voice came back off the ironstone and died.

Then, slowly, Edmund looked at his own hand. Turned it over. Looked at the ghost-sigil shining faintly in the firelight, and Isolde watched the anger drain out of him and leave something worse behind: grief. Not the grief of loss—he had lost his mother years ago, had grieved her properly, had laid flowers on a pauper's grave in Vellumgate every autumn—but the stranger grief of discovery. Grief for a self he had never met. Somewhere inside him, sealed under a mason-neat scar, lived a four-year-old boy who had known what he was, whose blood had sung with the covenant, whose palm had borne the raven unhidden—and that boy had been put away in the dark before he could even remember his own name for it, and had grown up as a stranger in his own skin, selling luck-charms to nervous clerks, mistaking his prison for his personality.

"I don't remember anything," Edmund said. "Do you understand? There's nothing to mourn because there's nothing to remember. It's like being told I had a brother who died before I was born. I can feel sad about it. But it isn't mine. The sadness isn't even mine—it belongs to that boy, and he can't feel it anymore, and neither can I, really. We're just two people sharing a body and a scar."

Brannoc rose, slow as a tree growing, and laid one hand on Edmund's shoulder. Up close, Isolde could see how ancient the warden truly was—the sigils inked along his own forearms had faded to the grey-blue of old bruises, and his skin hung loose at the jaw, and yet his grip, when it closed, was iron.

"That grief is yours," the old man said. "Take it. It's the truest thing you own. And tomorrow—" he squeezed, gentle and merciless at once— "tomorrow we talk about undoing it."

Edmund's head came up sharply. "Undoing it? Can it be undone?"

"Aye. Unsealing is possible. I've done it twice in three hundred years." Brannoc released him and stepped back toward the hearth, and the firelight carved his shadow huge against the far wall. "But I'll warn you what I warned the others, and what no one believes until it happens: what's under a scar that old doesn't come back the way it left. Power kept prisoner thirty years learns things in the dark. Learns hunger. It comes up wild and starving and it will know your name before you know its temper." He picked up his poker—iron, older than kingdoms, its tip shaped like a raven's beak—and stirred the great fire, and the coals answered with a sound like a sigh, like something turning over in its sleep.

"So sleep tonight," Brannoc said, not turning around. "Both of you. Sleep well, eat well, say your goodbyes to the people you were yesterday. Because tomorrow morning, boy, I cut that scar open—and whatever comes out of you when I do..."

He looked back over his shoulder, and the firelight made his colourless eyes blaze like the last two lit windows in a dark city.

"...we had best hope it remembers it's a raven, and not a wolf."
