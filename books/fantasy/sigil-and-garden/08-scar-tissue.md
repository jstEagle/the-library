## 8. Scar Tissue

The hearth-room of the Emberhold smelled of iron and old smoke, and Edmund lay on a slab of black stone that had been worn smooth by centuries of other bodies.

He knew that last part because Brannoc told him. The warden said it the way he said everything—flatly, without comfort, as though kindness were a currency he'd stopped carrying decades ago. "Every heir of the Raven Covenant has bled on this stone," he said, grinding something in a mortar with hands like tree roots. "You'll be in good company."

"I'd rather be in no company at all," Edmund said. His voice came out thinner than he wanted it to.

"Most of them said the same." Brannoc tipped the mortar's contents—a paste the color of dried blood and smelling of hot copper—onto a cloth of woven grey. "Isolde. Hold his shoulders. When it comes back, it comes back all at once. Like a river through a broken dam. He will want to fight it. Do not let him fight it."

"And if I do fight it?" Edmund asked.

Brannoc looked down at him for the first time since they'd entered the room. His eyes were pale as frost on slate, and very old, and not unkind—but not kind either. Something past kindness. Something like grief that had been lived in so long it had become furniture.

"Then you die fighting yourself," he said. "Which is the worst way. Lie still."

Edmund lay still.

The hearth burned low behind him—he could feel its heat against his shoulder blades even through his shirt, a warmth that was somehow also a presence, an attention. The dragon-fire. He had seen it only hours ago for the first time: a flame the color of dawn caught in amber, burning without wood, breathing without lungs, guttering now toward its death. It had looked at him. He was almost certain it had looked at him.

Isolde knelt beside the slab. Her hands settled on his shoulders—light, precise, the hands of someone who had spent her life handling things that could detonate. She did not look at him. She was looking at the sigil scar over his heart, the one Brannoc had found when he pressed his thumb to Edmund's chest and gone very quiet, the way men go quiet before bad news.

"It will hurt," she said. Not a question. A courtesy. The last one he'd get.

"Yes," Edmund said. "Do it anyway."

* * *

It began as cold.

That surprised him. He had braced for fire—for heat, for burning, for everything the word *unsealing* promised—and instead there was a line of winter drawn across his chest, thin as a wire, as though someone were unzipping him from sternum to navel with a blade made of January.

Then the wire became a seam, and the seam became a wound, and the wound remembered what it was.

* * *

*Rain. He is six years old, maybe seven, and the rain is coming down in ropes, and his mother is running.*

*Not walking. Running, through an alley behind the tanneries where the water runs silver with waste and the smell is so thick he can taste it, and she has his wrist in her grip hard enough to leave marks, and she keeps looking back over her shoulder at nothing he can see.*

*"Mama, you're hurting me—"*

*"Be quiet, Edmund. Be quiet and count your steps. Count them for me."*

*So he counts. One-two-three-four, splashing, five-six-seven, and somewhere around twenty she stops so suddenly he slams into her back, and she turns and crouches and takes his face in both her hands, and her eyes are wild and wet and shining with something that isn't rain.*

*"Listen to me. Whatever happens next, whatever you see—you are Edmund Marsh. You sell charms. Little ones. Luck-charms, love-charms, nothing that matters. You have never been anything else. Say it."*

*"I'm Edmund Marsh—"*

*"Say you've never been anything else."*

*"I've never been anything else."*

*She kisses his forehead then, and her lips are burning hot, and he feels it go *through* him—through skin, through bone, into some deep room inside himself he didn't know he had—and everything in that room goes dark. Every candle snuffed at once. A door closing on a house he will spend thirty years forgetting he ever lived in.*

*And his mother steps back from him, and she is crying, and she says, "Good boy. Now forget me too, as fast as you can," and she walks out into the rain toward the lanterns and the boots and the voices shouting words he doesn't understand yet but will someday—*seal him, seal the bloodline, burn the sigil*—and Edmund Marsh, age six, stands alone in a tanner's alley and begins, obediently, to forget.*

* * *

Edmund screamed.

He heard it from a long way off, the way you hear your own voice underwater. Isolde's hands were iron on his shoulders; Brannoc's voice was somewhere above him, saying words in a language that predated Vellumgate, that predated perhaps language itself, and each syllable landed on the scar tissue over his heart like a chisel-blow.

The scar was opening. He could feel it—not metaphorically, not in the dreamlike way of memory, but physically, actually, the sealed sigil peeling back layer by layer like bark off a living tree. And beneath it—

Beneath it was everything.

* * *

*A different night. Older. He is seventeen and doesn't know why he can't sleep, why his chest feels like a locked room with something pacing inside it, why the charm-seller's stall he inherited from the woman who died calling herself his aunt feels wrong in ways he can't name.*

*There's a girl in the market who dropped her basket. Eggs rolling everywhere in the mud, and everyone stepping around her, and Edmund—without thinking, without deciding—crouches and touches one cracked egg and thinks, absurdly, *be whole*, and the egg is whole. Unbroken. Warm in his palm.*

*He stares at it. The girl stares at it. Somewhere down the lane a Ministry whistle sounds, two notes, the patrol-call, and Edmund's hand closes around the egg and he walks away very fast and does not eat eggs for a month.*

*But that night, lying awake, he does it again. Holds his hand over the stub of a candle and thinks *light*, and the wick catches without flint or match, a small gold tongue of flame that bends toward him like a plant toward sun. And the locked room in his chest goes silent—the pacing thing lies down, presses itself against the door, listening—and Edmund feels, for three full seconds, more alive than he has ever felt in his life.*

*Then terror arrives, right on schedule, and he pinches out the flame with his fingers and lies in the dark shaking, whispering the litany his mother gave him: *I am Edmund Marsh. I sell charms. Nothing that matters. Nothing that matters. Nothing that—**

*Except it did matter. It mattered enormously. And some buried, starved animal part of him knew it, and wanted it, and hated him for starving it.*

* * *

"He's surfacing," Isolde's voice, far away. "Edmund. Edmund—don't fight it. Let it come up."

"I'm not—" he gasped, and discovered he was. Discovered his whole body was arched off the stone, every muscle locked, fighting the thing rising in him the way a drowning man fights the surface—as if air itself might be the enemy.

Because it hurt. Gods, it hurt. But underneath the hurt, worse than the hurt, was the *returning*. Thirty years of power coming home to a house that had forgotten its own rooms. It poured up through the opened scar like floodwater through a cellar door, and every year of it carried its own weather: the six-year-old's terror in the rain, the seventeen-year-old's candle-flame and shame, the charm-seller's decades of tiny magics smuggled under honest work, luck-charms that were real, love-charms that were real, all of it fed drop by drop from a well he'd been told was empty.

He had never been hiding magic. He had been *bleeding* it, all these years, a slow leak he'd mistaken for his own smallness.

"No," Edmund heard himself say. "No, stop, put it back—"

"There is no putting it back," Brannoc said. "Boy. Look at the fire."

He couldn't. He was writhing. Isolde leaned her whole weight onto his shoulders and put her mouth close to his ear, and her voice came through the roaring like a rope thrown into floodwater:

"Look at the fire, Edmund. It's dying. It's been dying for a hundred years, waiting for you. *Look at it.*"

He looked.

* * *

The dragon-fire stood in its hearth like a prisoner at a window.

Up close—or as close as he could see it, twisted sideways on the stone, tears streaming into his ears—it was not one flame but many, layered, a depth of burning that went down further than fire should go. And it was guttering. With every breath it drew smaller, and with every breath Edmund felt the drawing in his own chest, a sympathetic contraction, as though the fire and the newly opened room inside him were two ends of the same bellows.

And the fire saw him.

There was no other word for it. The light bent toward him—not heat, not wind, but *attention*, vast and ancient and unspeakably tired, focusing on the boy on the slab the way a dying man focuses on the face of his son.

*Oh,* Edmund thought, in the wordless language underneath thought. *Oh, you know me.*

The fire flared.

Just once. Just a hand's width taller, gold going briefly to white, and in that flare every candle-snuffed room inside Edmund blazed open at once, and the power that came home was not a trickle anymore but a *torrent*, and he understood why Brannoc had said *like a river through a broken dam*, and he understood why they'd held his shoulders, and he understood, finally, with the clarity of the nearly destroyed, why his mother had sealed him.

Because this would have killed a child. This was killing-adjacent even now.

* * *

*One last memory, and it comes not as rain or candlelight but as a single sentence, spoken in a voice he hasn't heard in thirty years, in a room he cannot place, on a night he cannot date—because it must be from before, from the before-time, from the life that ended in a tanner's alley.*

*His mother, kneeling, holding his small hands in hers. His hands are marked. He can't see how, in the memory, but he knows—they are marked, faint lines on the palms and between the fingers, a pattern like branches, like wings folded, like a bird drawn in ink that hasn't dried.*

*"This is yours," she is saying. "They'll tell you it's a curse. They'll tell you it's nothing at all. Both are lies. It's a covenant, my love—it's a promise your family made to keep the world warm. And promises are heavy, and I am so sorry, but I cannot carry it for you. I can only hide it until you're strong enough to choose."*

*Her thumb traces the mark on his palm. It tingles like a limb waking up.*

*"Choose," she says. "When the time comes. Promise me you'll choose."*

* * *

Edmund came back to himself on the floor of the Emberhold, on his hands and knees, because at some point he had rolled off the slab and no one had stopped him. The black stone was slick with sweat. The copper-smelling paste had burned a ring into the fabric of his shirt, and through the burnt gap he could see his own chest—and the sigil.

It was beautiful. That was the obscene part. A raven in flight, rendered in raised silver-white scarring, wings spread wide across his sternum, and along the edges of every feather ran lines of faint gold light that pulsed in time with his heartbeat. In time, he realized, with the hearth-fire's flicker. The same rhythm. The same breath.

His hands were shaking. He lifted one and turned it over, and the old memory rose to meet the sight: branches, wings, wet ink. The mark had been there all along, hidden under thirty years of ordinary calluses, ordinary dirt, ordinary work.

Power moved in him like a second pulse. It was enormous. It was terrifying. It did not feel like the little magics he'd sold in Vellumgate, the careful thimblefuls siphoned off the leak—it felt like standing at the edge of a sea he'd been told was a puddle. Every shadow in the room leaned toward him. The embers in the hearth rearranged themselves when he breathed. He could feel the stone under his hands thinking its slow mineral thoughts; he could feel Isolde's heartbeat where her hands still gripped his shoulders, quick and controlled; he could feel, distantly, miles away, the city of Vellumgate like a knot of cold iron in the world's fabric, and threaded through it, hundreds of small warm lights—charm-folk, hedge-witches, people like him, hiding, leaking, starving.

He could feel them *all*.

"Oh," he whispered. His voice came out wrecked, scraped raw from screaming. "Oh, gods. It was never small. Any of it. None of it was ever small."

"No," Brannoc said. The old man crouched in front of him, and for the first time since Edmund had met him, something moved in the frost-pale eyes that might, in a younger man, have been called tenderness. "It was never small. You were made small. There's a difference, and the difference is a crime, and the ones who committed it sit in a marble archive in Vellumgate counting what they stole." He rose, joints cracking like green wood. "How do you feel?"

Edmund considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty.

"Like I've been given back a limb I didn't know I'd lost," he said. "And like the limb hates me. And like I could burn down a mountain. And like if I try to hold all of it I'll come apart at the seams." He laughed—a strange, ragged sound, half sob. "All of those. At once."

"That's normal," Isolde said quietly. She released his shoulders at last and sat back on her heels, and he saw that her hands were trembling too, fine tremors she was pretending weren't happening. "The first days are the worst. Your power remembers being caged. It will test you. It will push. Some of what it pushes you to do will feel like *you* feeling it, and it won't be, entirely, and learning to tell the difference is the work of years." She paused. "We don't have years."

"How long do we have?" Edmund asked.

Brannoc answered, turning toward the hearth, where the great flame had sunk again after its single flare—smaller now than before, as if the effort of reaching for its heir had cost it dearly. The light it cast was the color of the last hour of dusk.

"The fire had strength left for one reaching," the warden said. "It spent it on you. What remains will gutter out within days—three, perhaps four, if the nights stay mild. When it dies, the covenant dies with it, and everything the covenant held together begins to come apart." He looked back at Edmund, and the firelight made his shadow enormous on the wall behind him, winged and vast. "Rest tonight. Eat, if you can. Tomorrow we read the terms of the old bargain, and tomorrow after that, we begin teaching you what your mother never got the chance to."

Edmund nodded. Then, because the question had been building in him since the tanner's alley surfaced, since the rain and the running and the burning kiss on his forehead:

"Brannoc. My mother. The night she sealed me—she walked toward the Ministry lanterns. Toward the patrols." His throat tightened. "What happened to her?"

The old warden was quiet for a long moment. The hearth-fire ticked and shrank.

"She was brought to the archive," he said at last. "Sealed bearers are valuable. They're kept." His pale eyes did not soften. "Whether anything that could still be called *your mother* is kept with her—I cannot say. I have not been beyond these walls in sixty years."

Edmund knelt on the cold stone with a raven blazing on his chest and a sea of power moving in him like weather, and found that the terror and the exhilaration had both gone suddenly quiet, replaced by something simpler and colder and far more useful.

Three days, Brannoc had said. Four at most.

Edmund intended to need fewer than that.
