## 9. Terms of the Old Bargain

Isolde Vane had spent eleven years learning to read what the Ministry wanted no one to read, and she had learned it in the worst possible school: their own.

They had not exiled her for ignorance. That was the bitter joke of her disgrace—that she had been *good*. A sigil-keeper of the third rank, youngest ever appointed to the Office of Seals, with a memory like a vault and hands steady enough to inscribe a binding-writ on a grain of rice. She had been good at her work right up until the day she read too carefully, and saw that the seals she was keeping were not locks on magic but locks *made of* it—confiscated power hammered flat and stacked into law.

Now she sat in the Emberhold's scriptorium with the covenant's true text open before her, and the old vertigo came back, familiar as an ache in an amputated limb.

The book was not a book, exactly. Brannoc had called it the *Root-Text*, and it was bound in something that was not leather—pale, faintly warm, veined like a leaf—and its pages were not paper but thin sheets of pressed ash that had never quite finished being burned. The letters did not sit on the surface. They lived in it, rising and sinking with some slow respiration, so that reading required patience and a kind of surrender: you could not seize the text; you could only wait for it to surface, the way one waits for fish.

Edmund sat across from her, hunched over his half of the spread pages, his lips moving slightly. He read like a charm-seller, she thought—quickly, hungrily, hunting for the useful line—and she found herself watching him more than the text. Three days since Brannoc had unsealed him, and he still flinched at his own shadow when it moved wrong. His power came off him now like heat off a forge-stone, uncontrolled, apologetic. Candles leaned toward him when he entered a room. The hearth-fire at the center of the Emberhold brightened when he laughed and guttered when he grieved, and he had not yet noticed either thing, which told Isolde more about the depth of his sealing than any scar could.

"Here," Edmund said suddenly. "This passage. It repeats—the same phrase, three times down the margin." He turned the page toward her, careful not to touch the ash-skin with bare fingers. Brannoc had been very clear about that. "It says—well, it says *the raven feeds the wyrm and the wyrm warms the raven*, but there's a second layer under it. Look at how the strokes double back."

Isolde looked. And felt the floor of everything tilt, gently, the way it had eleven years ago.

"It's a palimpsest," she said quietly. "No—it's worse than a palimpsest. It's a *contract*. The surface text is the covenant's poetry. The under-text is its terms."

Brannoc had gone to fetch water—or so he claimed; Isolde suspected the old warden simply knew when two people needed to be alone with a terrible document—and the scriptorium was theirs alone, lit by the strange amber light that seeped through the Emberhold's high windows, light that had passed through dragon-fire on its way in and carried a memory of it. Dust hung in that light without falling. Isolde had stopped finding this beautiful sometime around yesterday, when she'd realized the dust wasn't suspended—it was *listening*.

"Read me the under-text," Edmund said. "Your eyes are better trained for this than mine. I sold charms; I didn't keep archives."

"You sold charms," Isolde said, "that were real. Don't undersell yourself. Under-text work is just reading what someone tried to hide, and you did that every day of your life in Vellumgate." She drew a breath and bent over the page, letting her trained sight go soft, the way she'd been taught—*look through the writ, not at it*—and the doubled strokes resolved into a second hand, older, plainer, merciless in its clarity.

She read aloud, slowly, translating the archaic formulae into plain speech as she went, because she wanted Edmund to hear it plain. Some things deserved to be heard plain.

"*Article the First.* Let there be two fires: the fire of mortal making, which is magic, and the fire of elder burning, which is dragon-flame. Neither shall be hoarded. Neither shall be starved. What one realm gives, the other returns, sevenfold turned, season upon season."

She paused. In the amber light, Edmund had gone very still.

"*Article the Second.* The Raven Covenant binds the bloodline of Ravensworth to the exchange. Their sigil is the hinge. Through it flows the tithe of mortal magic into the elder fire, and through it returns the blessing: rain in season, seed that wakes, hearth-flame that cannot be extinguished by storm or malice while the covenant stands."

"Ravensworth," Edmund repeated. "Not Ravensworth-the-name-my-mother-gave-me. Ravensworth the *office*."

"The name was the office," Isolde said. "Or the office was the name. With covenants it's usually both." She kept reading, because stopping would have been mercy and the text did not offer mercy and neither, she decided, should she—not to him, and not to herself.

"*Article the Third.* Should either party withhold its fire, the other shall diminish. Should the mortal realms hoard their magic, the dragons starve, and the land loses its blessing: crops fail, fires gutter, rivers forget their courses. Should the dragons withhold, mortal magic sickens and turns inward, feeding on its bearer." Her voice caught on the next clause, because she had seen that sickness, had *administered* to its victims, had called it corruption and sedition and written the reports herself. "Should either party break the exchange entirely, both realms begin to unravel—but slowly, over a hundred years, so that the breaking may be hidden, and the hoarding justified, and no single generation need see the whole of the theft."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. Somewhere below them, the last dragon-fire breathed in its stone hearth, low and labored, and Isolde thought: *a hundred years*. The Ministry's founding charter was ninety-seven years old.

"They didn't outlaw magic," Edmund said at last. His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man carrying something fragile across ice. "They *collected* it."

"They nationalized it," Isolde said. "Which is the same crime wearing a uniform." She turned the page, and the under-text continued, and she made herself continue with it. "*Article the Fourth.* The keeper of the seal shall serve the exchange, not either party. The keeper's charge is balance, and the keeper who serves a throne instead of the balance commits the one treason the covenant names unforgivable—" 

She stopped.

Edmund looked up. "What?"

"Nothing," Isolde said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and after a moment she gave him the truth because she was tired of carrying it alone and because, some treacherous part of her noted, he had earned it by not looking away. "The unforgivable treason has a punishment attached. It's not death. Death would be simple. The punishment is *sealing*—the traitor's own power taken and held in trust until the covenant is restored, to be returned only when the balance is mended." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle as the ash-pages. "Eleven years ago I found irregularities in the Office of Seals. Ledgers that didn't ledger. Sigils in the deep archive that matched no writ on file. I asked my superior where the confiscated power went, and three days later I was sealed, stripped of rank, and put out of Vellumgate with a mark on my record that said *doctrinal instability*."

"Doctrinal instability," Edmund repeated softly.

"I asked questions about the terms," Isolde said. "That's all doctrine is, in the end—who owes whom, and how much. I read Article Four before I ever knew it existed, and the Ministry sealed me for it." She closed her eyes. "I've spent eleven years thinking I was disgraced. I wasn't disgraced. I was *confiscated*. I'm sitting here as evidence."

* * *

Brannoc returned with the water and the particular slowness of a man who had been listening at doors for longer than most nations had existed. He set the jug down among them, lowered himself onto the third stool with a sound like a ship taking ground, and said, "You've reached the Fourth Article, then."

"You knew," Isolde said. Not a question.

"I've known for sixty years, girl. I've known since the last keeper came up the root-road with her belly full of child and her sigil already scarred, begging an old man to hide the Root-Text where Ministry eyes couldn't find it." The warden's face was a landscape of old burns and older patience. "She left the boy in Vellumgate. She left the book with me. She left the truth with neither, because the truth was the one thing the Ministry could torture out of anyone, and she meant for the boy to live small and live long." He looked at Edmund, and Isolde watched the young man bear it—bear being loved and lied to in the same gaze. "Hiding was the kindest lie I ever helped tell. Doesn't make it less a lie."

"Why show us now?" Edmund asked. "Why not let me stay a charm-seller? The covenant's been broken for a century. One more life wouldn't—"

"One more life is exactly what it would take," Brannoc said. "The fire's down to embers, boy. Embers don't wait for convenient generations. You were always going to find the book or the book was going to find you—prophecy is just the covenant remembering itself out loud." He tapped the ash-page with one scarred finger, gently, the way one taps a sleeping animal. "And there's more. Read the Fifth Article, keeper. You've earned the reading of it."

Isolde bent again to the under-text. The fifth article was shorter than the others, and written in a different hand—newer, though still a century old, and she recognized the style of it with a lurch: it was the hand of a sigil-keeper, formal and exact, the hand she herself had been trained to write in. The last keeper had amended the covenant's text with her own testimony, and testimony in a binding-writ was not commentary. It was *clause*.

"*Article the Fifth, appended by the hand of Maren Ravensworth, last lawful keeper:* I write this in the year the Ministry completed its great collection. They believe they have won. They hold the confiscated fire in the deep archive beneath their seat and call it *the Public Trust*, and they feed upon it in secret, and they age slowly, and they tell themselves they are stewarding what they steal. Let the record show: stolen fire does not bless. Hoarded flame gives heat but not warmth. The Ministry's magistrates will live long lives and feel no joy in them, and their gardens will grow, and their bread will rise, and nothing they touch will ever quite come alive, because blessing cannot be stockpiled—it exists only in the giving." 

Isolde's throat tightened. She had met those magistrates. She had *been* junior to them. She remembered Lady Seraphel's hands, unlined past all reason, and the way the Minister's smile reached her eyes and somehow did not warm them, and she had filed that observation under *reverence* for eleven years and it had been *diagnosis* all along.

"There's a final line," she managed. "It's addressed—to a reader. To *you*, I think." She read it aloud:

"*To my child, if the covenant remembers you: they will tell you your magic is dangerous. It is. They will tell you it must be controlled. It must. But hear the terms, little raven: the danger was never yours alone. Every soul in Vellumgate who bought a luck-charm, every hedge-witch who whispered a fever down, every child who made a candle flicker for the fun of it—they were all paying the tithe without knowing it, small coins into a great fire, and the fire blessed them back whether they believed or not. The Ministry did not take your power. They took your* membership. *Restore the exchange, and even the smallest gift becomes part of the oldest bargain. That is what power is for. Not to keep. To pass.*"

Edmund had put his head in his hands. Isolde watched his shoulders shake once, twice, and then go still with the effortful calm of a man deciding not to weep in front of witnesses, and she looked away to give him the privacy of her averted eyes, and in looking away she saw the hearth below through the scriptorium's grate-floor: the ember-bed pulsing, faintly, in rhythm with his breathing.

Even grieving, he fed it. Even unknowing.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the light moved, and the dust listened, and Isolde built the whole architecture of the crime in her mind the way she had once built binding-writs: clause by clause, load-bearing piece by load-bearing piece.

The Ministry had not merely hoarded magic. That would have been robbery, and robbery, however vast, was comprehensible—a crime with a shape, punishable in principle. No. What the Ministry had done was subtler and far worse. They had rewritten the *story*. They had taken a covenant—an exchange, a marriage between realms, a bargain with obligations running both directions—and recast it as a *threat*. Magic became dangerous. Dragons became monsters. The keepers became cultists. And the confiscation became *protection*, and the protection became law, and the law became the air everyone breathed, until a century later a charm-seller in Vellumgate hid his gifts like a disease and thanked his stars no one caught him doing good.

"They framed it," Isolde said. She was pacing now, unable to sit, the Root-Text's phrases chasing each other in her skull. "The whole outlawry. It's not policy, it's *laundering*. They stole a river and convinced everyone downstream that the river was the flood."

"And it worked," Edmund said. He had recovered his composure, mostly, though his eyes were red-rimmed. "It worked on me. My mother died hiding what she was, and I grew up ashamed of her instead of angry for her. I spent thirty years apologizing for existing." He laughed, short and hard. "Do you know what I thought when I first read my name in that prophecy book? Not *what does it mean*. I thought *who saw me*. I thought *how long do I have before they come*. Eleven years of your exile, thirty years of mine, and the first instinct of both of us was fear. That's what they built. That's the real architecture."

"Yes," Isolde said. "And that's why the terms matter more than the treasure." She stopped pacing and faced him across the ash-pages, and she spoke with the precision she had once reserved for binding-writs, because this was a binding-writ, in its way—one drawn on the two of them. "Understand what we're holding, Edmund. If we restore the covenant, we're not restoring *magic*. Magic never left; it's in their vaults, going stale. We're restoring the *terms*. The obligation. The idea that power runs in circuits, not piles—that it's a verb, not a possession. And the moment that idea is loose in the world again, every magistrate in the Ministry of Seals becomes what they actually are: not lawmakers. Thieves with excellent stationery."

"That sounds like a declaration of war."

"It is. It has been since before we were born. We've only just been handed the treaty that proves whose signature started it." She gathered the pages, squared them, and felt the strange warmth of the ash against her palms—warmth with a pulse in it, faint and patient. "Seraphel will not negotiate. You don't negotiate with the person whose entire legitimacy rests on your silence. When she learns the Root-Text survives—and she will learn, she has spent a century making sure nothing survives without her permission—she'll come for it, and for the hearth, and for you. Not because you're a threat *yet*. Because you're a precedent."

Edmund was quiet a moment. Then: "My friend Corwin joined the Ministry last spring. He thinks he's reforming it from inside." His jaw worked. "I've been telling myself that. Reforming it from inside."

Isolde looked at him—at the loyalty in him, stubborn and unexamined, aimed at a man who wore a Ministry seal on his coat—and she thought of her own superiors, whom she had loved once, whose approval had been the architecture of her whole young life, and she said, gently, because gentleness was also a discipline: "Then he'll have a choice to make soon. Everyone does. The terms are back in the world, Edmund. That's what terms do. They *demand*."

Below them, the ember-bed pulsed. Above them, the dust went on listening. And far to the south, though none of them could know it yet, a rider in Ministry grey was already on the road, carrying a warrant with a name on it that Corwin Hale had signed without reading closely enough—and the name on the warrant was Edmund's.
