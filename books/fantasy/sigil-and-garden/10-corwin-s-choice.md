## 10. Corwin's Choice

The warrant weighed less than a letter.

Corwin Ashby had carried it for three days, folded into the inner pocket of his coat where it lay against his ribs like a coal that would not cool. He had read it so many times he could recite it with his eyes closed—*By order of the Ministry of Seals, the bearer is empowered to locate and detain one Edmund Ravensworth, also known as Edmund Fenwick, charm-seller of Copperlane*—and each recitation made the words stranger, as though repetition were slowly translating them into a language he refused to speak.

He had signed it himself. That was the part no one else knew. The clerk had offered him a fresh copy to countersign, and Corwin had taken the pen, and his hand had not shaken, and that was the most frightening thing of all: how steady his hand had been.

Now he stood at the edge of the borderlands where the Ministry's writ went thin as morning mist, and the road beneath his boots was not a road at all but a seam in the earth, a root-road, older than Vellumgate, older than the Ministry, running between hills that had never learned to be afraid. The air tasted different here. Sweeter. There was something under the taste, something like iron and something like honey, and when he breathed deep his chest ached in a way he could not name.

He knew why they had sent him. Not because he was their best tracker—he wasn't; that honor belonged to men with colder hands. They sent him because Edmund trusted him. Because twenty years of shared bread and borrowed coats and one long childhood in Copperlane's crooked lanes had built a door into Edmund's heart, and the Ministry had simply asked Corwin to walk through it and lock it behind him.

And he had said yes. He had said yes because they offered a seat—a real seat, a chair with his name on a brass plate, a voice in the room where the seals were made—and because he had spent his whole life watching other men be listened to while he was merely liked. Liked. The word had begun to curdle in him over the years. *Corwin's good company. Corwin's reliable. Corwin will see the joke.* Reliable Corwin, who sold insurance against fire in a city where the real fires were all bureaucratic. Reliable Corwin, whose father had died owing rent, whose mother had died owing nothing only because she'd died before winter.

They had promised him a seat at the table, and he had sold them a friend, and now he had walked three days into the wild country to collect what he'd sold.

The root-road bent around a hillside dense with hawthorn, and beyond it Corwin saw smoke—not the greasy black of city chimneys but a thin grey thread rising from a hollow in the stones, and beneath it, faint as memory, a glow like the last coals of an enormous hearth.

He should have signaled. He had a whistle in his pocket, bone-carved, keyed to summon the sweepers waiting two valleys back. All he had to do was raise it to his lips and blow, and forty men in grey would come over the hills, and Edmund Ravensworth would go into a Ministry wagon, and Corwin Ashby would go into history—or at least into an office with a brass plate.

His hand found the whistle. His fingers closed around it.

He did not blow it.

* * *

Edmund saw him first. Of course he did. Corwin had never once managed to surprise him, not even as boys, when they'd played at ambush in the tannery yards and Edmund would turn around mid-game and say, *You breathe too loud, Win.*

Now Edmund turned from the hearth-fire where he'd been crouching, and his face did exactly what Corwin had feared it would do: it lit. For one unguarded moment there was pure gladness there, the old gladness, the kind that didn't check pockets or count knives—and then it shuttered, and Corwin watched his friend understand everything in the space of a breath: the coat, cut in the Ministry style; the warrant-shaped weight in the pocket; the absence of the whistle's sound.

"Win," Edmund said quietly.

"Edmund." His voice came out wrong. Too formal. He tried again. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive." Edmund rose slowly. Behind him, near the mouth of the hollow, a woman stood with her arms folded—dark-haired, sharp-eyed, with the stillness of someone who had already decided how she would kill him if it came to that. Isolde Vane. He'd seen her name in the briefing folio. *Disgraced sigil-keeper. Armed. Do not approach alone.* He had approached alone anyway, and he was beginning to suspect that every choice he'd made in the past week had been some species of walking alone toward a thing that would hurt.

An old man sat by the fire, ancient and broad, with hands like tree roots and eyes that had gone the color of banked embers. He did not rise. He simply looked at Corwin the way a mason looks at a wall that has begun to lean.

"You came up the root-road," the old man said. "Alone. On foot." Something in his voice was almost pitying. "They always send the one who's loved."

"I'm not—" Corwin started, and stopped, because the denial was so obviously false that finishing it would have been its own confession.

Edmund came closer. Close enough that Corwin could see the changes in him: a new scar along the jaw, a steadiness in the shoulders that hadn't been there before, and underneath it all a kind of brightness, as though somewhere inside him a lamp had been relit after years of dark. "How did you find me, Win?"

"You know how."

"I want to hear you say it."

"The charm-sellers' network. The waystation at Marlow's Cross keeps a ledger." Corwin heard himself reciting the report he'd rehearsed, and hated the sound of it, and kept going anyway, because the momentum of lying was easier than the stillness of truth. "I followed the ledger south."

"That's the route," Edmund said. "That's not the answer."

The wind moved through the hawthorn behind them, and far off, down in the valleys, Corwin could just make out the haze of Vellumgate, a smudge on the horizon like a bruise. Somewhere in that smudge were forty sweepers and a Minister who had smiled at him across a desk of black oak and said, *Mr. Ashby, we find that loyalty is best rewarded quickly, while it's still warm.*

"I have a warrant," he said. It came out flat, dead, absurd. He pulled it out anyway, held it up between two fingers like a dead bird. "For your detention. Under the Sealing Acts. It names you as Edmund Ravensworth, heir of—" He faltered. "It uses the word *covenant*. I didn't know what that meant until I read it. I still don't, entirely."

Isolde Vane shifted forward, and the old warden's ember-colored eyes narrowed, but Edmund raised a hand—*wait*—and stepped nearer still, close enough that Corwin could smell woodsmoke and cold air on him, close enough to see that his friend's hands were trembling very slightly, which meant Edmund was more afraid than he was showing, which meant Edmund was still Edmund.

"So arrest me," Edmund said softly.

"What?"

"Arrest me. That's what you came to do. I'm standing right here, Win. No sigils drawn, no wards up. One man, one woman, one old warden, and a fire that's nearly out." He spread his arms. "Do it. Blow your whistle. Call your sweepers. Take me back in a wagon and collect your seat."

Every instinct Corwin had trained into himself over three days of marching—the instincts of ambition, which are mostly the instincts of fear wearing better clothes—screamed at him to do exactly that. And then Edmund lowered his arms, and his voice changed, dropped into the register he'd used since they were nine years old, the one reserved for sick birds and broken promises and the night Corwin's father died:

"But first tell me one thing, and tell me true, because you've never once lied to me and I don't think you can start now. When you signed that paper—when you gave them my name—did you know what they'd do with it? Did you know about the charm-quarter? What happens to the people who bought luck-charms off me? The clerk with the bad knee. The widow who buys a blessing every solstice. Do you know what the Ministry does with people like that, Win?"

Corwin's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

Because he did know. He'd sat in the briefing and heard the phrase *comprehensive sweep, Copperlane district, following confirmation of target location*, and he had understood, in the part of himself he kept locked and fed and never looked at, exactly what *comprehensive* meant. He had understood and he had signed anyway, because the seat was right there, warm and waiting, and because he had told himself the oldest lie in the Ministry's catalogue: *someone will do it regardless; better it be done gently, by a friend, than cruelly by a stranger.*

Gently. As though friendship were a form of anesthesia.

"They'll burn it," he heard himself say. His voice sounded far away, like someone else confessing through him. "The whole quarter. Seraphel—the Minister—she's not doing a sweep, Edmund. She's doing a purge. Every charm-seller, every buyer, every ledger, every name. Anyone who ever touched small magic. They've been waiting for a justification and I—" His throat closed. He forced it open. "I'm the justification. Finding you confirms the network exists. My signature is the match they strike with."

The silence that followed was the longest of his life. The dragon-fire behind Edmund guttered and breathed, guttered and breathed, and Corwin realized dimly that he could feel it now, here in this place—feel it the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall. The land itself had a pulse, faint and failing, and the Ministry had spent a century pretending the pulse wasn't there.

"You can still stop it," Edmund said.

"No." Corwin laughed, and it was a horrible sound, wet and short. "No, I can't. You don't understand what she is. What she has. I've seen it—I wasn't supposed to, but I've seen the thing under her glove. It isn't a seal, Edmund, it's a *sigil*, a real one, old ones, and it burns. She burned a man in the archive corridor for misfiling a folio. She did it with her bare hand and everyone applauded." He dragged a breath. "If I defect—if I stand in front of you and refuse—they don't just take the quarter. They take me, and then they take everyone I've ever eaten dinner with, starting with my sister, and they do it slow, and they do it with paperwork, and by the time anyone notices, the paperwork will be perfect."

"Then don't defect," Edmund said. "Just warn us. Walk away. Go home, Win. Go home and be ordinary and hate yourself privately like the rest of us used to."

"I can't walk away either." It burst out of him before he could dress it up. "Don't you see? That's the trap. That's the whole design of it. She doesn't want my soul, she wants my *signature*—she wants me complicit enough that going back is worse than going forward. And it worked. God help me, it worked. I signed, and now every road out runs through the people I love." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm telling you what's coming so you can run, and then I'm going to stand here and let you decide whether to arrest *me*, because fair's fair, and I think—I think I'd let you."

* * *

Later, Corwin would remember the next hour in fragments, the way one remembers a fever.

He remembered Isolde Vane circling him slowly, reading him like a document, and saying at last, "He's not lying. He's worse than lying. He believes every word, and he'll still go back." And Corwin, stung, unable to deny it.

He remembered sitting by the dying fire while Edmund told him everything—the prophecy book, the covenant, the theft at the heart of the Ministry, the truth that magic had not been outlawed for safety but hoarded for appetite. He remembered laughing at first, the reflexive laugh of a practical man hearing a fairy tale, and then remembering the burn-scarred corridor, and the Minister's gloved hand, and the way fires in Vellumgate had guttered these last years, and crops failing in districts that had never failed, and the laugh dying in his throat as a hundred small wrongnesses rearranged themselves into one enormous shape.

He remembered Brannoc, the old warden, saying quietly: "Your friend is not your enemy, boy. Your enemy is the bargain you made with your own hunger. We all sign it. Some of us get the chance to tear it up."

And he remembered the moment at the end, when the light was going gold and low over the hills, and Edmund walked him to the edge of the hollow, and they stood together like two boys at the end of a lane that no longer existed.

"Come with us," Edmund said. "Not to fight. Just—don't go back to her. Stay here. Be nobody with us. It's the best thing I've found in thirty years."

And Corwin felt the offer go through him like a blade made of warmth, and he wanted it—God, he wanted it, wanted it with a purity that frightened him more than Seraphel ever had. He thought of mornings without warrants. Of a life where his hands stayed clean because they stayed empty.

"I can't," he whispered. "My sister, Edmund. And—" He stopped. Started again. "And because if I run, I'll spend the rest of my life knowing I ran, and she'll spend hers knowing exactly how much pressure it takes to bend me. She keeps files on everyone. I'd rather keep my spine and lose everything else than learn what I'm worth to myself after that."

Edmund looked at him for a long time. There was no forgiveness in his face yet, and no hatred either—something harder and more patient, something being weighed.

"Then go back," Edmund said finally. "Go back, and sit at her table, and eat her food, and smile her smiles. But you'll leave me one thing before you go."

"Anything."

"When the purge comes—and it's coming within days, isn't it?"

"Three days. Maybe four. She moves at moonset; she thinks moonlight flatters her."

"When it comes, you'll be in that room. And whatever she orders, whatever they put in front of you—you'll find one family. One. In Copperlane, in the crowd, anywhere. And you'll get them out. Quietly, however you have to. Not because it balances anything—it won't, nothing will—but because it's the first stone of whatever you build afterward, and you *will* build something afterward, Win, because you're too stubborn and too decent to do otherwise. That's the price of walking away from me today. One family. Swear it."

Corwin's eyes burned. He nodded, and then, because a nod was too small, he said it aloud: "One family. On our fathers' graves."

"On our fathers' graves," Edmund echoed, and gripped his shoulder once, hard, the old grip, and let go.

Corwin walked back down the root-road with the whistle unused in his pocket and the warrant unsigned in his heart, and at the crest of the hill he turned, and Edmund was still standing there, small against the vast failing glow of the hearth below, raising one hand—not farewell, Corwin thought, but *remember*.

He remembered. He would remember for the rest of his life, which he was beginning to understand might be shorter than he'd planned.

Three days later, at moonset, the sweepers came down into Copperlane with torches and sealing-irons, and Corwin Ashby stood in the war-room beside Lady Seraphel's chair, wearing his new Ministry grey, and began—quietly, carefully, with the steady hand that had never once shaken—to look for one family to save.

Behind him, on the great map of Vellumgate, the Minister's finger rested on the charm-quarter like a spider on a web, and her voice, when it came, was soft as ash falling:

"Begin."
