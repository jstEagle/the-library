## 11. The Purge of Vellumgate

They saw the smoke before they heard anything at all.

Edmund had been walking since dawn, following Isolde up a shepherd's track that switchbacked through gorse and grey stone, and his mind had been full of small things—the ache in his boots, the way the borderland air tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet, the memory of Corwin's face when he'd lied to it two nights ago. He was so busy being tired that he almost didn't notice the sky over Vellumgate had turned the color of an old bruise.

"Isolde." He stopped on the path. "Look."

She turned. For a long moment she said nothing, and her silence told him everything before the wind did.

The wind brought the smell next—burnt tallow, scorched paper, something sweeter underneath that he refused, for three whole seconds, to name. Then the sound arrived, thin with distance: bells. Not the chapel bells that marked the hours. These were the Ministry's alarm-bells, the ones rung in a pattern every child in Vellumgate learned before they learned their letters, the pattern that meant *stay indoors, bar your door, ask no questions.*

"They're burning the charm-quarter," Edmund said. His voice came out flat, as if someone else were speaking through him.

Isolde shaded her eyes. "Sweepers don't burn unless they've been given leave to take everyone. That's not a raid. That's a purge."

He started down the hill.

Her hand closed on his arm—not gently—and held him there with a strength that surprised him every time he forgot what she was. "You go back there, you die. Or worse, you're taken, and they open you like a ledger looking for names, and everyone you ever sold a charm to dies with you."

"I have to *see* it."

"You'll see it fine from here. Better than fine. You'll see all of it, which is exactly what you need and exactly what you'll wish you hadn't." But she let go of his arm, and she came down the slope with him to the outcrop where the gorse grew short and the whole bowl of the city lay open below them, and together they watched Edmund Ravensworth's life be set on fire.

* * *

From the hills, Vellumgate looked like a map he had once drawn of himself.

He knew every roof in the charm-quarter by heart. The crooked lane called Tallow Row where Mrs. Hesk kept her stall of knotted threads and whispered blessings over babies' cribs for a copper apiece. The shuttered tea-house above the millrace where old Fenwick read fortunes in the dregs and always, always lied kindly about what he found. The narrow stair behind the chandler's that led to his own rented room, its window painted over with milk-glass so his candlelight wouldn't show, its floorboards hiding the loose stone under which he'd kept his mother's sigil-ring until Isolde made him wear it again.

Smoke rose from all of it now, in columns so straight and orderly they might have been planted.

The Ministry worked in rows. That was the thing he couldn't look away from, the thing that would come back to him later in the dark: how *orderly* it was. Sweepers in their grey lacquered coats moved up Tallow Row in pairs, methodical as clerks, dragging families out into the street while others went through the shops behind them. Wagons waited at both ends of the quarter. Not prison wagons—he could see that even from this distance, could see the shape of them, tall-sided and curtained, the wagons the Ministry called *receptacles* in its printed notices, as if people were water to be contained rather than spilled.

A charm-buyer of his, a nervous clerk named Pell who had once paid double for a luck-charm because he was afraid of losing his post—Edmund watched a pair of sweepers walk him out of the counting-house where he worked, hands bound with seal-cord, and put him in one of those wagons. Pell had never done anything but want to feel lucky. Pell had never been anything but afraid.

"There," Isolde said quietly, pointing east along the river. "See the light that isn't fire?"

He saw it. A pale radiance moving through the smoke, drifting from wagon to wagon, and when it touched the curtained sides the curtains glowed faintly, like lamps being filled.

"They're not taking them to cells," Edmund said. The understanding arrived cold and complete, the way understanding always did, too late to be any use. "They're harvesting them. Every scrap of belief, every little working those charms ever held—it goes into the wagons, and the wagons go to the archive, and it feeds—" He stopped. His throat had closed.

"It feeds the hoard," Isolde finished. "And the hoard feeds the Minister. This is what I tried to tell you the night we met. The outlawing of magic was never about safety. It was about supply." She was watching him more than the city; he felt her gaze on the side of his face like a hand. "I'm sorry. I know some of these people are yours."

"Some of them bought charms off me for twenty years." His voice cracked on the number. "Mrs. Hesk used to slip me bread when my mother first brought me to the city and we had nothing. Fenwick taught me cards. Pell—" He laughed, or something near it, ugly and small. "Pell asked me last month if he should stop coming. Said it felt dangerous lately. I told him the Ministry only cared about real magic. I told him a luck-knot was beneath their notice."

"You believed that when you said it."

"That's not the same as being right." He crouched down on the cold rock, arms wrapped around his knees, and made himself keep watching, because he owed them that much. He owed every one of them the witness of his eyes. "I spent twenty years learning how to be invisible. How to hide the true working under the trinket, how to smile like a fraud so no one would guess I wasn't one. And it worked. It worked beautifully. I was so safe, Isolde. I was the safest man in Vellumgate."

"And?"

"And everyone around me wasn't." The words came out of him like something lanced. "Invisibility isn't free. Somebody pays for it. I just never had to be the somebody, because there was always someone poorer, someone louder, someone whose little magics were a little less hidden than mine. Mrs. Hesk never hid hers. She thought blessing babies was holy work and hiding it would be a sin. So she stood in the open her whole life, and today the Ministry came for her, and I was up here learning to feel guilty on a nice clean rock."

Below them, a roof collapsed—a toy-sized sound, a soft rush of sparks—and the smoke plume bent and thickened. Somewhere in the streets, very faintly, people were singing. He recognized it after a moment: the old charm-folk hymn, the one about the hearth that cannot be put out. They sang it raggedly, frightened, defiant, and the sweepers let them sing, because singing wasn't against the law yet.

It would be soon. Everything would be soon.

* * *

They stayed on the outcrop until dark.

Not because Edmund wanted to—he begged Isolde twice to move on, once in words and once by simply standing and starting down the far side of the hill—but because she refused, and because some part of him knew she was right. Anyone fleeing the city tonight would be stopped on every road. The root-road entrance they meant to use lay half a day further north, and the Ministry's riders would be watching the obvious ways out for days. So they made themselves small among the rocks, wrapped in Isolde's grey traveling cloak, and watched the fires burn down through the evening like a slow sunset in the wrong part of the sky.

At some point she spoke, low, without turning her head. "Tell me about your mother."

"Why?"

"Because you're going to spend the whole night blaming yourself otherwise, and I need you functional tomorrow. Guilt is a luxury on the road. Speak it out or bury it deep, but choose one."

So he spoke it out, in fragments, watching the embers of Tallow Row pulse like a dying coal. How his mother had come to Vellumgate with a boy of six and a scarred-over secret, how she'd sold mending-spells disguised as needlework, how she'd taught him that the deepest magic hides inside the most ordinary gesture. How she died plain and quiet, of a fever any hedge-healer could have broken, except neither of them had dared work the healing openly and the discreet ones cost silver they didn't have. How he'd stood at her bedside doing the working anyway, badly, secretly, too late, and how he'd decided somewhere in that grief that invisibility was survival—that the safest life was the smallest one.

"She gave up everything to hide me," he said. "Sealed my own power before I could walk, Brannoc says. Gave up her standing, her name, her covenant—everything—so I could sell luck-knots in a city that hates what we are. And I told myself that was wisdom. That she'd want me to stay hidden. Stay safe." He shook his head slowly. "But she didn't hide me so I could watch other people burn, Isolde. She hid me so I'd *live*. There's a difference, and I think I only just found the edge of it."

Isolde was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had an odd roughness he hadn't heard from her before. "I was a keeper of seals for eleven years. Do you know what I did, the day they stripped my rank? I signed an order. One order, authorizing a collection sweep in the river-districts. Small. Routine. I told myself it was law, and law was balance, and balance was my whole faith." Her jaw tightened. "Three hundred people went into receptacles that night. I never even learned their names. So understand me, Edmund Ravensworth: I am not the person to absolve you, and you are not the person to absolve me. We're just two people who stayed comfortable too long, and the bill has come due, and the only question left worth asking is whether we pay it forward or pass it on."

He looked at her—at the hard profile cut against the firelight below, at the sigil-keeper's ring she still wore though it had cost her everything—and felt something shift in his chest, some load redistributing its weight.

"Pay it forward," he said.

"Yes." She pulled the cloak tighter around them both. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we go north, and you're going to learn what your blood can actually do, and I promise you—" a ghost of grim humor touched her mouth, "—you're going to miss the days when your biggest problem was guilt."

* * *

He dreamed of fire, but not the Ministry's fire.

In the dream he stood in the charm-quarter and it was whole again, lamplit and ordinary, and Mrs. Hesk was at her stall knotting threads, and she looked up at him and said, quite calmly, "You always were the invisible one, Edmund. Even as a boy. Your mother used to say you could stand in a room and vanish." Then the dream-fire came—not sweeping, not orderly, but rising out of the ground itself, out of the roots beneath the streets, and it was warm instead of cruel, and it spoke in a voice like a great slow heartbeat: *the hearth remembers what the city forgets.*

He woke before dawn with the sigil-ring hot on his finger.

Not warm. Hot—hot enough that he yanked his hand out of the cloak and shook it, and in the grey pre-light he saw the old scarred lines across his palm glowing faintly through the sealed tissue, like coals breathing under ash. Isolde was already awake, already watching, and her expression when she saw his hand was one he'd learn to know well in the weeks ahead: hope and fear sharing the same face.

"The fire's calling you," she said. "Or what's left of it. Brannoc warned me it might, once you crossed the borderlands." She stood, shouldering her pack, and looked north along the ridge where the land climbed toward wild country and the Emberhold beyond. "We've lost time sitting here. Days, maybe. If Seraphel has burned the charm-quarter, she's not cleansing the city—she's flushing. She knows where the prophecy points. She knows the fire is guttering. And she knows the last Raven is running toward it."

Edmund rose. Below, Vellumgate smoldered under a lid of smoke, and he made himself look at it one last time—not the city he'd hidden in, but the people he'd hidden among, the ones in the wagons, the ones in the ashes, the ones who had sung while it burned. He carried their names north with him as best he could, though he'd never known most of them. Perhaps that was the point. Perhaps belonging to people didn't require knowing them; perhaps it only required refusing, finally, to be invisible while they suffered.

"Then we don't run anymore," he said. "We walk. Straight at it."

Isolde smiled then, thin and fierce, and turned up the mountain road, and Edmund followed—out of the shadow of the smoke, into the high country where the old magic still breathed, toward the hearth that remembered, and the warden who would show him, painfully, everything his mother had buried.
