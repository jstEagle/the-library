## 12. Sigilcraft

The Emberhold's training yard had once been a dragon's courtyard, Brannoc said, back when the mountain was a mountain and not merely a shell around a fire. The flagstones were wide as doors, fitted so precisely that rain could find no seam, and each one bore the ghost of a sigil worn nearly smooth by centuries of clawed feet. Edmund felt them through his boots even now—a faint hum, like a tuning fork struck long ago and never quite allowed to fall silent.

He stood in the center of the yard at dawn, breath steaming, watching Isolde cross the stones toward him. She moved differently here than she had in Vellumgate. In the city she had been all sharp angles and lowered eyes, a woman practicing invisibility as diligently as he did. Here, in the borderlands where magic still breathed through the ground like warmth through floorboards, she walked like someone remembering her own name.

"Today we begin properly," she said. She carried no book, no chalk, no tools—only a strip of linen wrapped around her left palm. "You've been drawing sigils since you were a child, I imagine. Little ones. Luck-charms, finding-spells, the small domestic grammar of hedge-magic."

"I sold a charm last week that would keep moths out of a wool coat," Edmund said. "It worked for eleven years."

"And how much of yourself went into it?"

He considered. "A thread. Less than a thread. A suggestion of a thread."

"That's why it lasted." She stopped an arm's length away. "A covenant-sigil is not a suggestion. It is a vow made visible. When you draw one, you are not asking the fire to listen—you are telling it what you will give, and it will hold you to every word. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means I should be careful with my promises."

"It means," Isolde said quietly, "that the sigil does not distinguish between what you mean to offer and what you're afraid you might offer. It takes the whole truth of you. So before we draw anything, you need to know your own whole truth. Most people don't. Most people spend their lives negotiating with themselves and calling the result honesty."

Edmund looked down at his hands. Three days ago, Brannoc's unsealing had peeled back the scar tissue over his power—the work of some Ministry sealer, or perhaps something older, done the night his mother hid him in Vellumgate—and what lay beneath was still raw. He could feel it now constantly, a second pulse beneath his own, warm and vast and impatient, like a horse that had spent twenty years in a stable and remembered open country.

"It's loud," he said. "Is it supposed to be this loud?"

"No." Isolde's gaze sharpened. "It shouldn't be loud at all. Power that has been sealed for decades should wake slowly, like a limb regaining feeling. Yours woke like a dam breaking. Which tells me the seal wasn't protecting you from your power, Edmund. It was protecting your power from being noticed—or protecting everyone else from what your bloodline can do when it's hungry."

"Hungry?"

"You'll see." She unwound the linen from her palm, revealing a faded scar across the heel of her hand—an old burn, deliberate, sigil-shaped. "First lesson. Grounding. Before any covenant-work, you anchor yourself to something that isn't you. A stone. A tree. In the old days, the hearth itself. You give the power somewhere to go besides into whatever's in front of you." Her mouth twisted. "I learned this lesson badly. Hence the scar."

"What happened?"

"I was nineteen, brilliant, and certain. I drew a binding-sigil on a man who'd stolen from our chapter-house. I anchored nothing. The sigil took my anger as part of the vow, because my anger *was* part of the vow whether I admitted it or not, and it burned straight through the intent and into him." She rewrapped her hand with careful, practiced motions. "He lived. But I resigned my keeping within the year. That's the story they tell about me in the Ministry's version—that Isolde Vane maimed a thief and fled justice. The true version is worse. I didn't flee justice. I fled myself."

The wind came down off the crags carrying the smell of stone and old snow. Somewhere below them, deep in the mountain's heart, the dragon-fire guttered in its hearth, and Edmund felt its weakness like a toothache—he couldn't stop feeling it now, the great slow failing of the thing that kept both realms alive.

"Ground me, then," he said.

* * *

They began with the simplest sigil in the covenant-grammar: *holding*. Not binding, Isolde stressed—binding seized, holding supported. She scratched the pattern into the dirt between two flagstones with a stick: three interlocking arcs around a central point, each arc open at the bottom, "so the held thing can always leave if it chooses. That's the difference between us and the Ministry, Edmund. Their seals close. Ours stay open."

"Now you," she said. "Draw it. And while you draw it, think of something you want to keep safe. Something small. Don't reach for anything grand—not yet, not ever, if I have my way. Think of a cup of water. A bird's egg. Think of holding, not having."

Edmund knelt and took up the stick. His hand trembled slightly—not with fear, he realized, but with eagerness, and that frightened him more than fear would have. The power under his skin leaned toward the half-drawn sigil the way a dog leans toward a door it knows opens outward.

He drew the first arc. Warmth flooded up his arm, sweet and enormous, and the arc in the dirt glowed faintly gold.

"Good," Isolde said. "Slower. You're not carving, you're speaking."

Second arc. The glow brightened. The hum in the flagstones rose to meet him, and the dragon-fire far below seemed to lean toward him too, a vast attention turning his way, and Edmund thought, unbidden: *it wants me. All of it wants me.* The thought arrived wearing pleasure like a mask, and he hated how good it felt.

Third arc. He closed it over the central point—

"Stop." Isolde's hand closed on his wrist.

But the sigil was already finishing itself. The third arc curled upward out of the dirt without his hand, drawn by something in him that had decided the lesson was over, that had heard *think of something you want to keep safe* and answered with a hunger dressed as devotion. The golden light snapped taut like a wire. The loose stones at the yard's edge lifted, trembling. The air pressure changed; Edmund's ears popped. And the holding-sigil, meant for a cup of water, an egg, a small and humble safety, reached outward and *took hold*—of Isolde.

She made a sound he had never heard from her: short, involuntary, startled. The light wrapped her wrist, her arm, her chest, and pulled—not hard, not yet, but with the patient terrible strength of something that intended to keep. Her feet slid an inch across the flagstones. Two inches.

"Edmund." Her voice was level. Too level. "Let go."

"I'm not—" His hands were empty. The stick had fallen. He wasn't doing anything, and that was the horror of it: the sigil was doing him. "I don't know how—"

"Anchor. Find the stone. Any stone. Give it somewhere else to want."

He dropped his palm flat against the nearest flagstone and pushed everything into it—all the heat, all the reaching, all that eager golden wanting—and the flagstone drank it with a sound like a sigh, and the light around Isolde unraveled thread by thread and died.

She stood very still in the sudden quiet, rubbing her wrist where the light had gripped it. Under the linen wrap, he saw, her burned scar had gone white.

"I'm sorry," Edmund said. "I'm sorry—I didn't—I was thinking of the egg, I swear, I was thinking exactly what you said—"

"I know." She flexed her fingers, testing. "That's what I've been trying to tell you. Your power doesn't take orders, Edmund. It takes *dictation*. Whatever is deepest in you, it writes down in full. And something in you—" she met his eyes, and there was no accusation in her face, which was somehow worse than accusation, only a terrible gentleness—"something in you has been locked in the dark for thirty years, and it has decided that it will never again hold anything loosely. Not a cup of water. Not an egg. Nothing it loves gets to be let-go-able ever again."

The words went through him like cold water through cloth. Because he knew what that something was. It was the five-year-old in a Vellumgate cellar while his mother's seal closed over him like a lid, the child who had learned that everything loved gets taken, who had grown into a man who sold luck-charms and owned nothing, loved no one, kept no ledger of attachments because ledgers get confiscated.

"I could have hurt you," he said.

"Yes."

"I could have—" He couldn't finish it. His hands were shaking now, properly shaking, and he shoved them under his arms like a boy.

Isolde crouched, picked up the stick, and smoothed the dead sigil from the dirt with her boot. Then she sat down on the flagstone beside him, which she had never done, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"When I trained," she said at last, "there was a saying in the chapter-house. *The sigil is a mirror with teeth.* You look into it to see yourself truly, and it bites whatever lie it finds. Every keeper learns their own lie eventually. Mine was that control was the same as virtue—that if I just held myself tightly enough, I'd never become what I feared." She turned her scarred palm up toward the pale sun. "Your lie is different. Yours is that if you hold nothing, you can lose nothing. And your power has read that lie right through, Edmund, and it is trying to protect you from it in the worst possible way. It holds everything. Because you won't."

"So what do I do?"

"The hardest thing in the covenant-grammar." She smiled, and it was a tired, real smile. "You practice letting go. On purpose. Small things first. We'll spend weeks on sigils whose entire purpose is to release—to bless a thing and then give it its freedom. You will be terrible at it. You may cry. I did."

"And until then? Until I'm not dangerous?"

Isolde was quiet for a moment. Below them, the fire guttered, and the whole mountain seemed to lean inward around its failing heart.

"Until then," she said, "you stay close enough that I can stop you, and honest enough to warn me. That's the old bargain, Edmund. That's what the covenant *was*, underneath all the law and the language—people agreeing to be each other's anchors. The Ministry told Vellumgate that magic was too dangerous to trust to ordinary hands. They were wrong about the magic. But they understood something true about the danger: that no one survives it alone. They just built the wrong answer. Their answer was to take the power away from everyone and give it to no one but themselves." She stood, brushing dirt from her knees, and offered him her unscarred hand. "Our answer is to take the risk together."

He took her hand and let her pull him up. Her grip was strong and warm and briefly, deliberately tight—a keeper's grip, sealing a promise.

"Again," she said. "From the beginning. Slower this time. And Edmund—when it starts to feel good, that's when you tell me. Promise me that. The moment it feels good instead of frightening, say it out loud."

"Why?"

"Because that's how it got Seraphel." The name dropped between them like a stone into a well. "She started as a keeper too, you know. The best of her year. And one day the power stopped frightening her, and she mistook that for mastery, and forty years later she's drinking dragon-fire in a tower while the world dies around her." Isolde's eyes held his, steady and unflinching. "The moment it feels good, you tell me. Swear it."

Edmund looked at her hand, at the scar hidden under the linen, at the flagstones that had drunk his hunger without complaint, and understood that he was not swearing an oath to her. He was swearing one to himself, in front of a witness, which was the only kind that held.

"I swear it," he said. "The moment it feels good."

* * *

They trained through the morning and into the afternoon, and the lessons came slowly, and mostly they failed, and it was better than he'd expected to fail at something honestly. By late afternoon he could draw a releasing-sigil that let go of a pebble without trying to keep a piece of it, which Isolde called "the first honest thing your power has done in thirty years," and he laughed—actually laughed—until the sound surprised them both.

It was near dusk, the light going amber across the courtyard, when Brannoc appeared in the archway. The old warden moved like weather, slow and total, and today there was something in his face that made the laughter die in Edmund's throat.

"Warden?" Isolde said.

Brannoc's ancient eyes went from Edmund to Isolde and back, and when he spoke, his voice was gravel over a grave.

"There's smoke on the western road," he said. "And someone coming up it who carries a sigil I sealed myself, sixty years ago, on a young keeper who swore she'd never use it again." He paused. "She's brought soldiers."

Edmund felt the power under his skin rise toward the news like a dog scenting meat, eager and hot and already deciding, and he caught Isolde's eye across the dying light—and told her, out loud, the moment it felt good.
