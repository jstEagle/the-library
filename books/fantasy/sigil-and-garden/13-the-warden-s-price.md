## 13. The Warden's Price

Edmund woke before dawn to the sound of Brannoc singing.

It was not a song with words, or if it had words once, they had worn away like the lettering on old gravestones. It was a low, cracked drone that rose and fell with the breathing of the hearth, and Edmund lay in his narrow cot in the Emberhold's east gallery and listened until the melody and the fire became one thing in his ears—a single vast animal, sleeping and sighing.

Three days since the unsealing. Three days since Brannoc had pressed his thumbs into the scar over Edmund's heart and torn open a wound that had been healing wrong for twenty years. His magic was back, all of it, and it did not sit quietly in him the way it once had—before he'd known what it was, before he'd learned to hide it beneath charm-craft and misdirection. Now it moved through him like a river that remembered being dammed. Even at rest, even lying still in the dark, he could feel it pressing against the inside of his skin, asking *what next*, asking *how much*, asking questions he didn't know how to answer.

He rose and dressed by touch. The Emberhold kept its own hours; the corridors lit themselves faintly where dragon-fire ran in veins through the walls, a warm amber glow like light through honey. He followed it down toward the hearth-hall, because he always did now, the way a man might visit a sick relative every morning just to check the breathing.

Isolde was already there.

She sat cross-legged on the stone floor before the great hearth, her sigil-keeper's satchel open beside her, copying something onto a slate. The firelight caught the grey at her temples and turned it copper. She did not look up when he came in, but she said, "He's been at it since midnight. Louder than usual."

"Is that bad?"

"I don't know." She set down her chalk and finally looked at him, and there was something in her face he couldn't read—something held very carefully behind the eyes, the way she held everything. "I think he's deciding how to tell us something."

Brannoc stood at the hearth's edge, an old man so bent and weathered he seemed less built than grown, like a tree that had come up through a crack in the floor and simply never stopped. His hands were black to the wrist with ash that no water had ever washed away. He sang, and the fire answered him—or rather, the fire *was* him, Edmund thought; there was no longer any clear line between the warden and the flame he tended.

The last dragon-fire in the world burned low in that hearth. Not out. Never quite out. But low, the way a candle burns in its final hour, the flame gone small and blue at the base, clinging to the wick.

Edmund felt it in his chest, in the reopened place where his sigil lived. It felt like listening to someone breathe who might stop.

"Sit," Brannoc said, without turning. "Both of you. What I have to say is best said sitting down, though you won't stay seated long."

They sat. Isolde folded her hands in her lap with the deliberate calm of a woman preparing for bad news she intended to survive professionally. Edmund found he could not manage even that much composure; his knee bounced until he stilled it with his hand.

"The covenant," Brannoc began, "is not a wall. I know Isolde has told you it is a binding, a treaty written in sigil-work between the mortal realm and the dragon-realms. That is true as far as it goes. But a wall stands on its own. A covenant does not. A covenant is more like—" he searched, his gnarled fingers opening and closing— "like a marriage. It must be fed. Attended. Spoken to. For six hundred years, the Raven Covenant was tended by your line, Edmund Ravensworth, and the dragons' fire flowed into your blood, and your blood's magic flowed back into the fire, and both realms grew fat and green on the exchange."

"And then the Ministry broke it," Edmund said.

"They broke the *exchange*. They could not break the covenant itself, because the covenant is not theirs to break. It lives in the fire, and the fire does not die of neglect quickly. It dies slowly. It has been dying for a hundred years, and it is nearly done." Brannoc turned at last, and his eyes were pale as wood-ash, and kind, which made what was in them worse. "You have read the terms Isolde recovered from the Ministry archive. You know what was stolen. But there is a page they did not keep, because they did not understand it, because it was never written down. It was passed mouth to mouth along your bloodline, father to child, and your mother died before she could pass it to you."

The fire sighed. Edmund's sigil ached in sympathy, a dull throb like a tooth.

"So tell me," he said.

Brannoc lowered himself onto the hearthstone beside them, joints cracking like green wood in a blaze. "To reforge the covenant—to wake the exchange again, to let the fire flow back into the land and the land's magic back into the fire—the rite requires a life-bound sigil. One bearer. One person of the old blood, who takes the covenant into themselves entire, and holds it, and feeds it, forever."

"Forever," Isolde repeated flatly. "Define forever."

"As long as the bearer lives, the covenant lives. And here is the price, girl, so listen: the sigil that binds the covenant consumes everything else the bearer carries. Every scrap of their own magic. Their gift, their craft, their connection to the realms—all of it goes into the fire, all at once, and does not come back. The bearer becomes the covenant's keeper and nothing else. No spellwork. No charmcraft. No feeling of the dragon-realms at the edge of the mind. They live out their days as any ordinary mortal, tending a fire only they can see, until they die, and the covenant passes to another willing hand—if one can be found."

The silence after that was enormous. The hearth filled it with its small, struggling breath.

Edmund heard the words. He understood each of them individually. Together they formed a shape he refused, for several long seconds, to look at directly.

"You're telling me," he said slowly, "that to save both realms, someone has to give up their magic. Permanently. That the price of the reforging is—"

"Everything you just got back," Brannoc said gently. "Aye. I'm sorry, lad. I'd have found a kinder way to say it if one existed. There isn't."

* * *

Isolde watched Edmund's face do something terrible and private.

She had spent eleven years learning to read people—it was half of what sigil-keeping was, understanding what a soul was willing to pay—and so she saw it all: the hope that leapt up first, absurd and bright, *there must be another way*; then the arithmetic beginning behind his eyes, cold and fast; then the moment the arithmetic finished, and something in him went quiet and settled, like a house whose owner has decided to sell.

She knew that settling. She had done it herself, three nights ago, alone in the archive-gallery with Brannoc's oldest book open on her knees, reading the rite's requirements by candlelight while the two men slept.

She had known for three days. She had said nothing.

Because here was the truth she had carried since Vellumgate, since the night the Ministry stripped her rank and burned her sigils off her forearms and put her out of the city she had served since she was nineteen: her magic had already been taken from her once. The scars on her arms were smooth and silver and dead. Whatever remained in her was small, banked, a cookfire compared to what Edmund carried—what the unsealing had revealed in him, raw and vast and frightening, a wellspring the Ministry's theft had never managed to reach because his mother had hidden it too deep.

And so the logic arranged itself in her mind with terrible neatness, night after night, while she pretended to sleep: *the rite will take what little I have left. It will take everything of his. Let me pay with coins; he would pay with the whole treasury. Let it be me.*

She loved him. She had not said it and did not intend to, not yet, maybe not ever, because saying it would make this harder and it was already going to be hard enough. But love, she had learned, was not mostly a feeling. It was a discipline. It was deciding, over and over, what you were willing to lose so that someone else would not have to.

"Is there truly no other way?" Edmund was asking Brannoc. "No substitution, no—some other source of power to feed the rite?"

"You could try feeding it stolen fire," Brannoc said. "Ministry hoard-fire, the kind Lady Seraphel carries in her flesh. It would burn hot and fast and it would poison the covenant at the root. You'd trade a dying realm for a corrupted one. I've seen what hoard-fire does to living things." He looked into the hearth. "So has the fire."

"Then it's one of us." Edmund said it lightly, testing the weight of it, the way you test ice before committing your full step. "One of us takes the rite."

"It must be one of the old blood," Brannoc said. "The Raven line. Which means, lad—"

"I know what it means." Edmund stood. He walked to the hearth and stood looking into the small blue-hearted flame, and Isolde watched his shoulders, watched the way his magic rose in him like a tide answering some call she couldn't hear. "I need to think. I need to—I need to walk."

"Walk, then," Brannoc said. "The mountain doesn't mind."

Edmund left. His footsteps faded up the corridor toward the high gallery where the root-road door stood open to the wind.

Isolde remained seated, hands folded, spine straight, every inch the disgraced keeper holding form together through sheer professionalism.

Brannoc looked at her for a long time. His ash-black fingers drummed once on the hearthstone.

"You've known three days," he said.

"Yes."

"And you haven't told him."

"No."

"Why not?"

She met the old man's eyes. "Because he'd argue. Because he'd make it a contest, and he'd win, because he's got a hundred times the power I have and he'd insist that meant he owed a hundred times the debt. Because—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "Because he only just got it back, Warden. Twenty years sealed. Twenty years being less than he was, hiding in a city that hates what he is, selling luck-charms to clerks. He only just learned what his mother died protecting. And you're telling me the price of saving everything is that he gives it up *again*, permanently, having had it for barely a week. I won't let that be the shape of his life. I've already lost mine. I know the weight of it. I can carry it."

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. The fire crackled, small and stubborn.

"When I took this post," he said at last, "eighty years ago, my predecessor told me the hardest part of keeping a fire is not the burning. It's watching folk you love decide to walk into it for reasons they believe are selfless, when really they're only afraid." He rose, creaking. "You're afraid, girl. Fear makes fine-sounding arguments. Mind it doesn't make your choices for you."

He shuffled out toward the ash-yard, leaving her alone with the hearth.

Isolde looked into the flame for a long time. Then she opened her satchel, took out the slate she'd been writing on before dawn, and read her own handwriting there—the draft of the life-bound sigil, every stroke mapped, every sequence memorized, ready.

*I'll tell him tomorrow,* she lied to herself, as she had lied for three days. *Tomorrow, when he's had time to accept it.*

* * *

Edmund walked the high ridge above the Emberhold until the sun cleared the eastern peaks and the shadow of the mountain withdrew down the slopes like a tide going out.

From up here he could see the borderlands spread wide and broken-green: the failing fields, the streams running thin, the forests where the trees grew twisted because whatever blessed their growing had been starving for a hundred years. This was what the covenant's death looked like from altitude. From the ground it looked like a clerk buying a luck-charm because his crops failed and his children coughed and the world had gone quietly, persistently wrong.

His magic surged in him, eager, and he understood suddenly why it was so eager. It wasn't restless. It was *homesick*. All that power had spent twenty years locked behind scar tissue, and now it could feel the dying fire below, the starving land, and it wanted—desperately, achingly—to pour itself out and fix it. To feed. To be spent.

That was the nature of the Raven gift, he realized. Not hoarding. Not rule. Spending. His whole line had been built to be poured out.

Which meant the choice Brannoc had laid before him was not actually a choice between keeping his power and saving the world. It was a choice between doing the one thing his blood was *for*, or refusing, out of—what? Attachment? Fear of emptiness?

He laughed, alone on the ridge, and the laugh came out wrong.

Because he also knew—he knew with the same certainty he knew his own name—that Isolde was planning to take the rite herself. He had seen it in her face that morning, that careful held-back thing behind her eyes. He had watched her for weeks now; he knew her silences the way he knew the streets of Vellumgate. She had decided. She was waiting for the right moment to announce it, believing he would accept it, believing her sacrifice was the smaller one, the reasonable one, the one he ought to be grateful for.

She was wrong. And he could not tell her she was wrong without revealing that he intended to beat her to it.

The symmetry of it almost undid him. Two people standing on either side of the same hearth, each secretly reaching for the same burning coal, each convinced they were sparing the other.

*Let her,* whispered a voice in him that sounded like comfort and was really just fear. *Let her do it. She's already lost most of her magic. It costs her less.*

But that was a lie too, and he knew it, because he had seen what the Ministry's stripping had done to her—not the scars, but the way she touched sigils now, with her fingertips only, the way a pianist with ruined hands touches keys. It cost her everything, every day. She simply never said so.

He turned back toward the Emberhold, and the wind coming down off the peaks smelled, faintly, impossibly, of smoke and roses—the scent the fire gave off when it was dreaming, Brannoc said, of the old days when dragons had flown.

By the time he reached the hearth-hall, he had made his decision, and he had made it the way he made all his decisions now: completely, silently, and without telling anyone.

He would learn the rite. He would perform it. And he would do it before Isolde could stop him.

He found her in the gallery, copying sigil-diagrams by lamplight, and asked, casually, casually, "Teach me the covenant-forms tomorrow? All of them. I want to understand the whole structure before we decide anything."

She looked up, and smiled, and agreed, and neither of them saw the other's lie, because each was holding their own in front of it like a shield.

Below them, in the dark of the hearth, the last dragon-fire breathed, and waited, and began—very slightly, so slightly that neither the warden nor the keeper nor the heir noticed—to gutter.
