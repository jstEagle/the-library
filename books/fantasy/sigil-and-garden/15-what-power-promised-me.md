## 15. What Power Promised Me

The dream began, as it always did now, with the smell of bread.

Not the flat, ash-tinged loaves of Vellumgate's charm-quarter, but real bread—crusted gold, torn open so the steam rose in a white ribbon through a kitchen window. Edmund stood in his mother's kitchen on Candlemaker's Row, though he had not stood there since he was nine years old. The copper pots hung where they had always hung. The blue jug with the chipped spout sat on the sill, holding wildflowers that should have been out of season.

And she was at the table.

His mother looked up from her mending the way she always had—one eyebrow raised, needle paused mid-stitch, as if she'd known he was coming and had simply been waiting for him to stop pretending otherwise. Her hair was dark, ungrayed. Her hands were whole. In the dream, no one had ever come for her in the night; no one had ever sealed her sigil into silence and taken her away to a Ministry cell whose records had been burned before Edmund was old enough to ask questions.

"You're late," she said. "The kettle's been on twice."

"I've been busy," Edmund said, and heard how young his voice sounded, how unguarded. "Saving the world, mostly."

"Mm." She bit her thread and went back to her mending. "Sit down, then. You're thin. You were always thin when you lied to me about eating."

He sat. The chair knew him. That was the thing about dreams—the furniture remembered you even when you had forgotten yourself. He put his hands flat on the worn oak of the table and felt the grain of it, every scratch he'd made with a butter knife at four years old, carving what he'd insisted was a dragon and what everyone could see was a duck.

Outside the window, Vellumgate remade itself while he watched. The Ministry's black banners came down from the Spire like shed skins. The seal-lamps along the canal brightened instead of dimming, because there was nothing left to fear in the light. Charm-stalls lined the Row again—not his furtive little table of luck-knots and fever-charms, but proper stalls, proper craft, sigils worked openly in silver and riverstone. A woman across the street turned water into small dancing shapes above her palm just to make a child laugh, and no patrol came, and no door splintered, and no one screamed.

"There," his mother said softly. "That's what it was meant to be. Before they broke it."

"I know."

"Do you?" She set down her mending. Under the table, her hand found his—the same gesture, the exact pressure, that she had used the last night he saw her, pressing something small and warm into his palm. *Hide this. Hide you.* "Because I remember a boy who wanted exactly this. Who used to practice sigils in the flour and cry when I wiped them away. This isn't their promise, Edmund. It's yours. They only stole the shape of it."

"That's what frightens me," he said. The kitchen was growing warmer, or he was. Something under his breastbone pulsed like a second heart—the returned power, raw and enormous, answering the dream the way a dog answers its name. "It doesn't feel like temptation. Temptation is supposed to feel wrong. This feels like *coming home*."

His mother smiled, and it was the saddest smile he had ever seen her wear, because part of him understood, even asleep, that his mother had been dead for twenty years and this kitchen existed nowhere but in the hollow of him that Seraphel's offer had poured itself into.

"Then look closer," she said. "Look at who isn't here."

He looked. The kitchen was warm, the city was free, the bread was real—and the chair across from his mother sat empty. Isolde's chair. Brannoc's chair. Corwin's chair, with its broken rung he'd never fixed. The charm-folk who bought his luck-knots because hope was cheaper than bread. None of them were in the remade Vellumgate. The dream had given him everything except anyone to share it with.

"The fire needs feeding," his mother said, rising, and her hand passed through his shoulder like light through water. "You already know which fire I mean."

* * *

He woke in the Emberhold's guest cell with salt on his face and dawn-gray light leaking through the arrow-slit, and lay still for a long time, listening to the mountain breathe.

That was the strangest gift of the unsealing—he could hear it now. The Emberhold was not merely built on the root-road network; it was grown into it, stone and root and dragon-bone fused into one slow living architecture, and with his power restored he could feel the whole vast body of it beneath him: the deep galleries, the buried aqueducts, and far below, faint as a pulse at a wrist, the hearth-fire burning low.

Faint. Fainter than yesterday. He didn't need Brannoc's grim arithmetic to know it. Every morning the pulse came back a little weaker, a little slower, like a heart deciding whether the next beat was worth the effort.

Edmund dressed and climbed the spiral stair to the hearth-hall, and found it empty except for Isolde, who sat cross-legged on the cold stones before the great hearth, her back to him, her hands loose in her lap. The last dragon-fire burned in the ancient basin—a flame unlike any other, deep red shot through with veins of gold, casting shadows that moved a half-second behind the things that made them. Even guttering, even reduced to what Brannoc called *a candle-end of the old blaze*, it was the most alive thing Edmund had ever seen.

Isolde didn't turn. "You talked in your sleep."

"Did I."

"You said 'the kettle's been on twice.'" Now she glanced over her shoulder, and there was something careful in her face, something she was holding very still. "And you said your mother's name."

Edmund lowered himself onto the stones beside her. The heat of the dying fire reached his knees and stopped, as if it hadn't the strength to reach further. "Seraphel got inside my head yesterday. Or tried to. She left something behind."

"She left an offer."

"You heard?"

"I heard enough." Isolde's jaw tightened. "Rule beside her. Magic without limit. Vellumgate remade in your image instead of hers. She's been making that speech for thirty years, Edmund. She's polished it like a jewel. The terrible thing is that she means every word of it—that's what makes her dangerous. Liars you can guard against. Sincere people offering you everything you ever wanted—" She shook her head. "Those you have to survive."

"And how do I do that?"

Isolde was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flattened, stripped of its usual dry armor, laid bare the way she almost never let it be.

"I'll tell you how I know," she said. "Because I took the offer once. Not hers. My master's."

Edmund turned to look at her. In all the weeks since the prophecy book, since the snatch-squad, since the root-road and the Emberhold and the long bitter lessons in covenant-sigils, Isolde had told him what she *was*—disgraced sigil-keeper, exiled, the last trained keeper of the old order—but never, in any detail, *why*. He had assumed disgrace meant failure. He had never imagined it meant this.

"I was nineteen," she said, eyes on the fire. "Brilliant, they told me. Youngest keeper ever sworn to the Deep Archive. And my master—Keeper Aldous Vane, my father's brother, the man who taught me every sigil I know—came to me one night with a proposal. The Ministry was consolidating. Confiscated magic was pouring into the vaults faster than it could be catalogued. And Aldous said: we are the keepers. We are the ones who understand what power is, what it costs, what it's *for*. Shouldn't it be held by people like us? People who would use it well?"

"Let me guess," Edmund said quietly. "You believed him."

"I *wanted* to believe him. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." Her hands curled in her lap. "I helped him. For two years, I helped him siphon confiscated sigils out of the official vaults into our private ones. And I told myself the story he gave me—we're saving it, we're protecting it, we'll restore it when the time is right. And do you know what I discovered, at the end? That the story is the trap. Not the power. The power just sits there, humming, waiting. It's the *story* that eats you. Because the story lets you keep believing you're the exception. Every person who has ever hoarded power believed they were the exception, Edmund. Seraphel believes it right now, down in her vault of stolen fire, and she believes it with her whole sincere heart."

"What happened? With Aldous?"

"He decided I was a liability. Or perhaps he decided I was competition—I honestly don't know which would have hurt more. He sealed my keepership, burned my name from the rolls, and sent me into exile with a warning that if I ever touched a covenant-sigil again, he'd hand me to the Ministry himself." She laughed, short and without humor. "So yes. I am disgraced. But I wasn't disgraced for failing. I was disgraced for finally understanding what I'd become and trying to stop."

The fire shifted in its basin. One of the golden veins guttered out, and Edmund felt the loss somewhere behind his ribs, the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects it.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

"Because yesterday, when Seraphel made her offer, I watched your face." Isolde finally turned to him fully, and her eyes were fierce and wet and unashamed. "And I saw the exact expression I wore at nineteen, listening to Aldous. That hungry, homesick *yes*. And I could have let you work through it alone, the way no one worked through it with me. But I'm asking you—no, I'm telling you, keeper to heir—look at what the promise actually contains. Not the picture. The *contents*. Seraphel offers you Vellumgate remade. Does she offer you the people in it? Does she offer you Corwin, who sold you and wept doing it? Does she offer you the clerk who bought your luck-charm, the widow on Candlemaker's Row, Brannoc's grave? No. She offers you a city-shaped throne with everyone you love standing outside it, looking up."

Edmund thought of the dream. The empty chairs. His mother's voice: *look at who isn't here.*

"She showed me my mother," he said. His voice cracked on the word and he let it. "Alive. In the old kitchen. The whole city freed, everything undone, everything mended. And it was perfect, Isolde. It was so perfect I woke up crying. And the only thing wrong with it—the only thing—was that none of you were in it. I noticed. Some part of me noticed even while I was drowning in it. What does that mean?"

"It means the promise is a forgery," Isolde said, "and you are a better authenticator than you think. Power's promises are always beautifully drawn and always missing the living parts. Your mother isn't in Seraphel's future, Edmund. Neither is anyone. Only the shape of you, seated high, holding fire, alone." She reached out and took his hand—the same gesture, he realized with a jolt, that his dream-mother had made, the same pressure, warm and grounding and real in a way no dream could counterfeit. "Your mother hid you in Vellumgate. She spent her freedom, maybe her life, buying you an ordinary existence among ordinary people. Seraphel is offering to unspend it. To tell you it was all a waste, all those years of hiding, all that smallness. Don't let her insult her that way."

Edmund bowed his head over their joined hands. The power in him stirred, wanting to answer her words with flame, with grandeur, with some magnificent gesture—and he held it, the way you hold a dog that wants to lunge, and felt it slowly settle, sulking, into warmth.

"When I was a boy," he said at last, "my mother told me the Raven Covenant wasn't about power at all. Everyone thinks covenants are about power—who has it, who gives it up. But she said a covenant is just a promise you make with your whole self, so you can't wriggle out of it later. So the other party can sleep at night." He looked up. "Seraphel makes promises with other people's selves. That's the whole difference, isn't it? That's the entire war."

"Yes," Isolde said. "That's the entire war."

Above the hearth, the old stone lintel bore carvings worn nearly smooth—dragons and men facing one another across a shared flame, and between them, small and central, a single carved raven with its wings spread wide. Edmund studied it for a long moment.

"One more day," he said quietly. "The fire has maybe one more day in it. We should spend tonight learning the terms of the rite properly. All of them. Whatever it asks."

Isolde's grip on his hand tightened—just slightly, just for a heartbeat, in a way he was too exhausted to read correctly. "All of them," she agreed. "Whatever it asks."

They rose together and walked toward the archive stair, and neither of them spoke again, and each of them carried the same secret down into the dark: *it will be me. It has to be me. And they will never know until it's done.*

Behind them, the last dragon-fire burned on, patient and dying, waiting to learn which of them loved it enough to leave it everything.
