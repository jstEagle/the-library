## 17. Ashes and Names

The Emberhold had never been quiet, Edmund realized—not in all the days he'd known it. Even at its lowest, the hearth had murmured, a sound like a sleeping animal's breath, like wind moving through a house's bones. Now that sound was failing. He could count the gaps between the fire's exhalations the way one counts a dying man's breaths, and each gap grew longer than the last.

He sat on the cold stone steps before the hearth with his hands open on his knees and watched the light die by degrees. The great chamber that Brannoc had kept for—no one knew how long, perhaps longer than Vellumgate itself—was dimming into shadow. The dragon-carvings along the walls, once warm to the touch, had gone cool as river stones. Somewhere above, Isolde moved through the upper galleries, her footsteps quick and purposeful, doing whatever a sigil-keeper does when the thing she keeps is slipping through her fingers.

And somewhere below, in the warden's old cell, Corwin Ashgrave knelt beside the body of the oldest creature either of them had ever known.

Edmund had not gone down there. Not yet. He had told himself he needed an hour, then half an hour, then that he would go when the fire did this or that—but the truth was simpler and uglier: he could not yet look at his oldest friend without wanting to put his hands around his throat, and he could not yet look at him without remembering a boy in a Vellumgate alleyway sharing stolen plums, and both of those men were Corwin, and Edmund did not know how to hold them in the same gaze.

So he watched the fire gutter, and he counted.

* * *

When he finally descended, the stair seemed longer than it had been before. It wound down past niches where warden after warden had been laid to rest—or so Isolde believed; the shapes beneath the stone shrouds were too old and too still to be anything else—and the air grew colder with every turn, until Edmund's breath showed faintly silver.

Corwin was exactly where Edmund had known he would be: kneeling, hatless, his Ministry coat folded neatly beside him as though removing it were some small penance. His hands hung between his knees. He had not touched Brannoc. Perhaps he felt he had no right to touch anything here anymore.

Brannoc lay on the low stone bier where they had dragged him after the fighting ended, and the fighting had been brief and terrible and mostly over by the time Edmund reached the top of the stairs. The old warden had stood in the doorway of the hearth-chamber with nothing but an iron poker and eight hundred years of stubbornness, and he had held the line long enough—long enough for Isolde to bar the inner door, long enough for Edmund to drag Corwin out of the crush of Ministry sweepers who had very nearly trampled the man they'd come to use. A crossbow bolt had taken him through the chest. He had died looking at the fire, which was, Edmund thought, the only deathbed Brannoc would ever have wanted.

"Edmund." Corwin's voice came out cracked. He didn't rise. "I've been sitting here trying to find words that aren't filth, and I can't. There aren't any."

"No," Edmund said. "There aren't."

He lowered himself onto the bench against the far wall, an arm's length away but a world apart, and looked at his friend properly for the first time since the doors had burst open. Corwin had a bruise blooming along his jaw where a sweeper's gauntlet had caught him—friendly fire, or perhaps not so friendly; Seraphel's people did not suffer hesitation gladly. His eyes were rimmed red. His hands, those careful clerk's hands that had sorted charms and counted coins beside Edmund for twenty years, were shaking.

"You knew," Edmund said. It wasn't a question. "Not just tonight. You knew when I showed you the prophecy book. You knew when I asked you—God, Corwin, I *asked* you—if there was anything strange at the Ministry, any talk of old covenants, and you looked me in the eye and said no."

"I said no," Corwin whispered.

"Why?"

The question hung in the cold air. Corwin bowed his head, and for a long moment the only sound was the distant, arrhythmic pulse of the dying fire filtering down through the stone.

"Because they offered me a seat," he said finally. "An actual seat. Third Chair of the Registry. My name read aloud in the Minister's hall. Do you know what my father was? A tallow-render. Do you know what I was supposed to be? A tallow-render. And there was Lady Seraphel in her gray silk saying *Master Ashgrave, we believe in useful men*, and I—" His breath hitched. "I told myself it was nothing. That you'd be fine. That they only wanted to ask you questions. I sold you for a chair, Edmund, and I told myself it was furniture."

Edmund laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

"Furniture," he repeated. "I hid under floorboards when I was six years old because sweepers came for my mother. I have spent thirty years being small. Being forgettable. Being *nobody from the charm-quarter*, smiling at clerks and selling luck to frightened people while praying every night that nobody would ever say my true name aloud. And my truest friend—" His voice broke and he let it. There was no dignity left to protect. "My truest friend was the one holding the list."

"I know." Corwin's shoulders shook. "I know what I am. I've known since the moment the seal left my hand and I saw your face in that bookshop window. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I don't—I couldn't bear it if you forgave me quickly. That would mean it was cheap."

Edmund looked at him then—at the ruined man kneeling in the dark beside the warden he had helped kill—and something moved in Edmund's chest that was not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but was at least its cousin. Pity, perhaps. Or recognition. Because he had spent this whole terrible week learning how power whispers, how it finds the hungry place in a person and speaks to it in their own voice, and he had felt that whisper himself. In the archive, with the hoarded magic singing behind the seals. At the hearth, when his unsealed power had roared up through him like a flood and he had thought, for one shameful instant, *I could take everything back.*

He was not better than Corwin. He had only been poorer, and poverty had made caution of him.

"Get up," Edmund said.

Corwin raised his head.

"Get up, Corwin. I'm not going to hit you, though God knows I've imagined it four different ways since sundown. And I'm not going to tell you it's all right, because it isn't and it won't be for a long time, if ever. But I need you to listen, because we have hours. Maybe less. And you're going to help me spend them."

* * *

They climbed back up together, and the chamber above was darker now. The hearth-fire had shrunk to a knot of coals no larger than a fist, pulsing slow and red, and the shadows it threw were thin and trembling. Isolde stood before it with her sleeves pushed back and chalk dust to her elbows, and when she turned, Edmund saw she had been crying without any fuss about it—the silent kind of crying, the kind done on a schedule, because there was work.

"The terms are degrading," she said, without preamble, because grief had burned the ceremony out of her. "The covenant-sigils are unraveling from the outside in. When the last thread goes, the exchange stops entirely. No dragon-fire blessing the land, no mortal magic feeding the realms. The borderlands go first—we've already seen it, the blight, the gutted fires—and then it spreads. Vellumgate. Everything."

"How long?" Edmund asked.

"With the fire at this level?" She looked at the coals, and her jaw tightened. "Dawn. Perhaps a little after. The rite needs the fire alive to reforge through. Dead fire is just ash."

"Dawn," Corwin echoed. He had stopped at the edge of the light, unwilling to come closer to the hearth, as though the fire might judge him. Perhaps it would. "And Seraphel?"

"Gathering everything she has," Isolde said flatly. "She lost face tonight—a warden dead, two fugitives uncaptured, and her stolen sigil tasted real dragon-fire for the first time in decades and found it stronger than her hoard. She'll come back at dawn with the full weight of the Ministry, and she'll come personally, because pride is the one hunger she's never learned to manage." She glanced at Corwin, and her eyes were flint. "You know her better than we do. Confirm it."

"She'll come herself," Corwin said quietly. "And she'll bring the Purge companies. Whatever's left of the charm-quarter will burn tonight whether or not she needs it to. She'll call it reprisal."

The words landed in Edmund's stomach like stones. The charm-quarter. Lamplight Lane and the Soldered Kettle and old Marta's candle-shop and the nervous clerk who had bought a luck-charm a lifetime ago, a week ago, a hundred years ago—all of it, all of them, swept into Seraphel's bonfire because Edmund Ravensworth had run.

"They're already gathering," Corwin went on. "I heard the orders before I—before I came back. Wardens on every bridge. Anyone caught selling, buying, *carrying*—" He stopped. Swallowed. "There are hundreds of them, Edmund. Charm-folk. Most of them don't even have real magic. They sell knots and candles and prayers with the power scrubbed out, and they'll be taken anyway, because Seraphel needs a crowd to frighten."

"And you know this," Isolde said, "because an hour ago you were wearing their colors."

"Yes." Corwin did not flinch from it. Edmund gave him that much credit—he didn't dress it up, didn't reach for excuses. "Yes. I was one of theirs tonight. I led them to this door. I'll wear that until I die, and I should."

The silence stretched. The coals pulsed. Edmund walked slowly to the hearth and stood before it, feeling the heat—or what remained of it—against his shins, and inside him the returned power stirred and leaned toward the dying flame like a plant toward light. It wanted to pour out. It wanted, with a terrifying eagerness, to give itself away. He had spent a week learning to fear that eagerness, and now, standing at the edge of losing everything, he understood it at last. Power that has been sealed for thirty years knows what it is for. It had always known. It was he who had forgotten.

But that was for the next hours. That was for the rite. First—

He turned to Corwin.

"There are hundreds of charm-folk," Edmund said, "and they're frightened, and frightened people stand still. They stand still and they get taken, and Seraphel burns them to make a point, and the point works because nobody ever tells them there's another option." He crossed the chamber, took his oldest friend by the shoulders, and felt Corwin tremble under his hands. "You're going to be the other option."

"Me?" Corwin's laugh was hollow. "Edmund, no one in the quarter will listen to me. I'm the man who wore the gray coat. By tomorrow night they'll have songs about me, and none of them will be kind."

"They'll listen," Edmund said, "because you're the only man in Vellumgate who can walk into the Purge lines and not be arrested. Your seal still works, doesn't it? Your credentials. Your *name*. You can get through the wardens on the bridges."

Corwin stared at him. "You want me to go back."

"I want you to do the hardest thing anyone's asked of you in your whole ambitious life. I want you to walk into the charm-quarter ahead of the sweepers and tell every soul on Lamplight Lane to get out. The root-road mouth behind Marta's shop—it opens, Isolde says, for anyone carrying honest intent. Tell them that. Tell them the Emberhold will shelter them. Tell them—" Edmund's grip tightened. "Tell them Edmund the charm-seller sent you, and that he's sorry it took him thirty years to stop hiding."

"They won't believe it. Why would they believe anything I say?"

"Because you're going to tell them the truth," Edmund said. "All of it. That you sold me out. That you led the Ministry here. That the warden of this place is dead because of you. You're going to stand in front of frightened people and confess, and then you're going to tell them where the road is, and some of them will spit at you and some of them will run the other way, and a few—enough, I think—will follow you, because a man confessing is the one voice a frightened crowd believes." He released Corwin's shoulders and stepped back. "That's your penance, Corwin. Not kneeling next to a dead warden in the dark. Saving the people you helped condemn. And I promise you nothing about how it ends. I don't know if it works. I don't know if they come. I don't even know if we're alive at dawn. But grace isn't a guarantee. It's just a door, and you either walk through it or you don't."

For a moment Corwin simply looked at him, and Edmund watched something move across his friend's face—the ambition dying, or being killed, or perhaps only being set down for the first time in years, heavy and familiar, like a pack taken off at the end of a long road.

"Marta's shop," Corwin said hoarsely. "Behind the candle racks. There's a loose flagstone. I used to hide there when we were boys and your mother pretended not to see us steal her matches." A broken almost-smile. "You remember."

"I remember everything," Edmund said. "That's the curse of being invisible. You watch."

Corwin bent and picked up his Ministry coat, and Edmund braced himself—but instead of putting it on, Corwin folded it once more, deliberately, and laid it across the bench. Beneath it he wore a plain wool shirt, quarter-folk cloth, the kind he hadn't dressed in since they were young. He took the Ministry seal from his breast pocket and turned it over in his fingers—a disk of gray metal, cold-looking even in the ember-light—and then, with a care that looked almost ceremonial, he set it on the stone floor and stepped on it. The crack it made was small. It sounded enormous.

"For the record," Corwin said, "in case anyone asks later. I resigned."

"Nobody's keeping records," Isolde said dryly, "except us. And we'll keep that."

He went to the outer door, the one Brannoc had died defending, and paused with his hand on the iron ring. He didn't turn around. Edmund was glad of it; neither of them could have survived the look.

"Edmund. If the rite needs—whatever it needs—" Corwin's voice failed and restarted. "Don't let her pay it alone. Whatever it costs. Don't let Isolde pay it alone."

"We'll argue about it," Edmund said. "It's what we're best at."

And then the door opened on the blue-black of the borderland night, and Corwin Ashgrave went out alone down the mountain path toward the burning city, walking, Edmund noted, faster and faster, until he was nearly running—as though the door of grace might close behind him if he gave it time to think.

* * *

The chamber felt emptier with him gone, though it was hard to say how a room could grow emptier when it had already lost its keeper. Edmund stood a long while at the hearth. The coals breathed. The gaps between breaths lengthened.

Isolde came to stand beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither spoke. Above them, through the smoke-hole, the stars were coming out—cold, indifferent, beautiful.

"He'll fail somewhere," Isolde said at last. Not cruelly. Just honestly, the way she was honest about everything, which was the thing Edmund had come to love in her and the thing that most often made him want to throw things. "Some part of it. He'll freeze at a bridge, or lose his nerve in front of a crowd, or save forty souls when there were sixty. Men who've lived for ambition don't shed it in one night. It comes back on them like a tide."

"I know," Edmund said.

"And you sent him anyway."

"I sent him anyway." Edmund watched the coals pulse. "Because grace that waits for certainty isn't grace, Isolde. It's just accounting. I'm not absolving him. I'm giving him a door and trusting him to be worth the giving. That's all any of us get to do." He glanced at her, and found her watching him with an expression he couldn't read—something tender and troubled at once. "What?"

"Nothing." But she looked away too quickly, and her hand went briefly to her sleeve, to the pocket where Edmund had seen her slip a scrap of parchment earlier that evening—covered, when the wind had lifted it, in covenant-script he was only beginning to learn how to read. Something in the way she guarded it made his stomach tighten, though he couldn't have said why.

"What's the rite require?" he asked. "Truly. Not the version you give to keep people calm. The true text."

Isolde was quiet for three pulses of the dying fire. Four. Five.

"At dawn," she said, "I'll show you everything. I swear it. But tonight I need to prepare, and you need to sleep, because whatever happens at first light, you'll want your hands steady." She met his eyes, and hers were steady and warm and hiding something, and Edmund loved her, and did not trust her, and understood in that moment that these two things could live in the same heart without contradiction. "Sleep, Edmund. Dawn is coming fast."

He went. But at the foot of the stair he looked back once—back at Isolde standing alone before the guttering hearth, her silhouette thin against the last red light, her hand closed tight around whatever was written on that hidden page—and the dread that settled over him had nothing to do with Seraphel, or the Ministry, or the unraveling worlds.

It had to do with the way she wouldn't look at the fire. As if she already knew what it was going to cost.
