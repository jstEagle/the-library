## 18. The Reforging

The Emberhold had never felt so quiet.

Edmund stood before the hearth with the covenant-text unrolled across his knees, reading the final clause for the fourth time because the first three readings had refused to mean what they plainly said. The old script was dense as briarwork, Isolde's translations inked in the margins in her narrow, precise hand, and still the words would not soften: *One bearer. One binding. The sigil given entire—fire for blood, blood for fire—and the giver shall walk henceforth empty.*

Empty. He turned the word over like a coin he didn't recognize. All his life he had been hiding what he was, folding his power down small enough to fit inside a charm-seller's stall, and now that it had been torn back open—raw, enormous, singing in his veins like a struck bell—he understood at last what Brannoc's unsealing had truly cost him. It hadn't given him back himself. It had shown him the size of the thing he might lose.

Behind him, Isolde moved through the preparations she'd been making since dawn. Chalk lines refreshed on the flagstones. The seven anchor-stones of the old covenant set in their worn sockets around the hearth, each one carved with half a sigil that reached toward its twin across the circle. She worked with the economy of long habit, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her forearms bare and mapped with the faded keeper's marks that had earned her exile from her own order.

She had barely slept. Neither of them had. And neither of them had spoken, in all those sleepless hours, about who would stand in the center of the circle when the rite began.

*Because we both already know,* Edmund thought, and his stomach clenched around the knowledge like a fist. *We both decided days ago. We just haven't told each other.*

He watched her kneel at the eastern stone, tracing its half-sigil with one fingertip, murmuring the waking-words under her breath, and something in his chest went tight and hot. She had told him once—drunk on root-road honey-wine, laughing at her own expense—that sigil-keepers were taught to hold nothing back, that a mark given halfway was worse than no mark at all. She lived by that. Which meant that whatever she intended, she intended wholly.

And so did he.

"Isolde." His voice came out rougher than he meant it to. "When the rite starts—the sequence you showed me. The bearer stands at the heart-stone and speaks their bloodline sigil into the flame."

"Mm." She didn't look up. "That's the shape of it."

"And the bearer is me. That was settled when we found my name in the book. The Raven line. The last heir."

Now she looked up, and her eyes were steady and gray and utterly unreadable, which was how he knew she was lying. "Settled," she agreed. "Though the text says *a bearer of the covenant*, not specifically—"

"It doesn't say *anyone*, Isolde." He rose, the scroll sliding from his knees. "It says the heir's sigil or the keeper's seal. I read your margin note. I can read your handwriting better than you think."

For a moment the only sound was the fire behind them, guttering low, breathing like an old animal. Then Isolde got to her feet, unhurried, brushing chalk dust from her skirt, and Edmund saw her compose herself the way she composed a sigil—one careful stroke at a time.

"Then let's not waste our last calm hours arguing about a hypothetical," she said.

"It isn't hypothetical."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

* *

Isolde had known this argument was coming for three days, and she had rehearsed it in the dark of the wardroom while Edmund slept badly ten feet away, and none of her rehearsals survived contact with his face right now—that stubborn, wounded, *seeing* expression, the one that made her feel flayed and known and furious all at once.

"You're going to tell me it has to be you," Edmund said. "Because you're the trained one. Because you've spent your whole life preparing to give yourself to the covenant. Because your order broke its oaths and this is how you redeem them. Am I close?"

"Every word true," she said. "Shall I go on? Because you're about to say it has to be *you*. Because it's your bloodline that broke the exchange. Because your ancestor signed the theft into law and your magic is stolen goods and only returning it makes you clean." She took a breath. "And then we'll both be standing here having discovered, with great surprise, that we each intend to burn ourselves hollow, and neither of us willing to let the other do it."

"So what's your solution?" His voice cracked upward. "Draw lots? Arm wrestle?"

"My solution," Isolde said quietly, "is that I've been a keeper for twenty-two years, Edmund. I have held dying fires and sealed breaches and carried marks that would have killed an untrained mage. The rite will try to take more than we offer—it always does. It needs hands that know how to let go *slowly*, in the right order, or the fire takes everything near it too. You've had your power back for a month."

"And you've been carrying guilt for twenty-two years," he shot back. "You want to make it a trade. Your redemption for my inheritance. That's not sacrifice, Isolde, that's bookkeeping."

The words landed harder than he could have known—harder than he did know, because he couldn't see the night she'd sat in the ruined chapter-house of her order, holding her severed seal-cord, promising herself she'd find some work large enough to balance what she'd failed to stop. He watched her flinch anyway, watched her jaw set, and hated himself a little and kept going regardless, because the alternative was letting her step into that fire.

"I won't allow it," he said.

"Allow it?" Something dangerous kindled in her voice. "Edmund Ravensworth, you have known me eight weeks."

"Long enough."

"Not nearly. If it were long enough, you'd know that I don't *allow* things either. You'd know I walked out of the Scriptorium of Veils with my marks stripped and my name struck from every register on the continent, and I survived it, and I am the only living soul who has performed even a fragment of this rite and lived. When the Ministry comes—and they are coming, Corwin's warning bought us hours at most—I need you *whole*. Not ash. Not an empty man standing in a circle he can't defend. You are the last Raven. If the covenant fails tonight, your line is the seed of the next one. You don't get to spend that."

"And if it succeeds," Edmund said, "you'll be the keeper of a reborn covenant, and I'll be—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I'll be nobody again. A charm-seller. Less than that. Ordinary."

"Yes," Isolde said. "Ordinary. Alive. Free." Her voice gentled against her will. "I have seen what you look like when you use your power, Edmund. Like a man carrying a live coal in his bare hand, afraid to drop it and afraid to hold it. I have watched you flinch from your own reflection since the unsealing. Don't tell me the magic is home to you. It's a wound that learned to sing."

"That's not yours to decide."

"No," she said. "But the rite's requirements are written in a language I speak and you don't, and I'm telling you: it will accept me cleanly. With you, it will fight. And a fighting rite, with Seraphel bearing a stolen dragon-sigil somewhere on the mountain road, is how everyone dies instead of one of us."

* *

Edmund turned away from her because staying would have meant shouting, and shouting would have meant saying things that couldn't be unsaid, and there was no time left for unsayable things. Outside the narrow windows the sky over the borderlands had gone the color of pewter, and far off, down the root-road, he thought he could smell smoke—or imagined he could; fear manufactured smells now.

His magic stirred restlessly under his skin, answering his anger the way it always did now, too eagerly, echoing something hungry. He clamped down on it. *This,* he thought, *is exactly what she means.* The power felt less like an inheritance than an occupant. It had been sealed away so long that when Brannoc opened the scar, everything came back at once—his mother's midnight lessons, the taste of starlight on the tongue, the vast patient presence of the dragon-realms pressing against the thin wall of the world—all of it crammed into a body that had learned to live without it.

And yet.

He thought of the hearth-fire, guttering. Of the fields past the borderlands coming up gray-stalked and fruitless. Of Vellumgate, where candles burned blue and bread refused to rise and children were born without the small bright talents that had once threaded through every street. He thought of the purge, and the charm-quarter burning, and Mrs. Aldous the candlewoman being dragged away screaming his name because he had stayed invisible too long.

Somebody had to pay for all of it. The covenant demanded a life-bound sigil, whole and entire. There were two of them standing in this room, and only one of them was going to walk out of the rite still themselves.

*Let it be me,* he thought, with a ferocity that surprised him. *Let it finally, for once in my cowardly hidden life, be me.*

"Isolde," he said, turning back. "Show me the opening sequence again. All of it. If we're doing this, we do it perfectly."

She studied him for a long moment, and he watched suspicion and relief war openly across her face—suspicion winning briefly when he asked her to repeat the bearer's invocation twice—but in the end she knelt at the heart-stone and began to teach, and Edmund memorized everything, and said nothing about any of it meaning what she feared it meant.

* *

By dusk the circle was ready, and it was beautiful in the way that old true things are beautiful: seven anchor-stones humming faintly, chalk lines drinking lamplight, the covenant-text weighted open at the final page. The hearth-fire had been fed to the edge of what it could bear—a waist-high bloom of ember-light that threw their shadows huge against the vaulted stone.

Isolde took her place at the eastern stone. Keeper's position. She began the waking-words, her voice dropping into the deep cadence of the old tongue, and the anchor-stones began to answer, one by one, a slow bell-tone rising out of each.

Edmund waited until the third stone sang.

Then he stepped off his mark at the western stone, crossed the circle in four strides, and stood at the heart-stone with his palm flat against it, and spoke his bloodline sigil aloud.

The effect was immediate and catastrophic. Every stone in the circle changed its tone. The fire roared up three feet and went white at the core. Isolde's voice broke off mid-syllable, and the silence afterward was the loudest thing Edmund had ever heard.

"What," she said, very precisely, "are you *doing*."

"Starting the rite." His heart was slamming but his voice held, and beneath the terror he felt his power surge up joyfully, terribly eager, recognizing its own ending and rushing toward it like a river toward a fall. "You were right about everything, Isolde. About the sequence, about the fighting, about all of it. So I listened. I learned it properly. But the choice was never yours to make, and it was never mine to give away. It's my blood in the bargain. My ancestor's theft. My debt."

"The rite needs a keeper's seal to *anchor*—"

"Then anchor it from where you stand." He was dimly aware that he was smiling, that his eyes were wet, that the heart-stone was warming under his palm like a living thing waking. "Keep the circle. Keep me steady. That's the keeper's work, isn't it? Holding the line while someone else crosses it."

"Don't you dare," Isolde said, and her composure cracked clean through, and Edmund saw—not the exiled sigil-keeper, not the strategist, but the woman underneath, the one who had lost an order and a name and had planned, in secret, to lose herself last of all. "Don't you *dare* do this alone. I was going to— I decided before we ever left the root-road, I was always going to—"

"I know," Edmund said softly. "So was I. That's why we argued. Two people planning the same funeral."

The fire climbed. The stones sang. And Edmund closed his eyes and began to pour himself out.

It didn't hurt, at first. It felt like exhaling after thirty years of held breath—power streaming out of him along the bloodline sigil, into the stone, into the fire, and the fire *taking* it, greedy and grateful, climbing from white toward a color that had no name, gold shot through with green, the color the dragon-realms must have burned before anyone built a wall between worlds. He felt his mother's lessons go. He felt the starlight-taste go. He felt the vast presences beyond the wall turn toward him, ancient and starving, and begin to wake.

He was so far into the giving that he almost didn't hear Isolde shout.

* *

Isolde felt the rite turn true under her hands and knew, with a clarity that arrived like ice water, that she was going to lose him—not to death, which she could have fought, but to ordinariness, to emptiness, to the exact fate she had claimed for herself. And grief and fury rose in her together, and she did the only thing her training allowed: she anchored. She fed the circle everything it needed, held the tones true, kept the fire from turning on the man feeding it.

Which was why she felt the wrongness before she heard anything.

The eastern anchor-stone shivered. Not with the rite's music—with something else, a vibration from outside the mountain, from the root-road, from the world. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling. The lamplight bent sideways as though the air itself had leaned.

Then the doors of the Emberhold blew inward.

They came through the smoke in ranks—Ministry sweepers in gray lacquer, warded shields locked shoulder to shoulder—and ahead of them, unhurried as a queen entering her own hall, walked Lady Seraphel. The stolen dragon-sigil on her throat was blazing, and Edmund's poured-out fire seemed to bend toward it, toward her, like iron toward a lodestone.

"Well," said the Minister of Seals, surveying the blazing hearth, the singing stones, the hedge-mage standing empty-handed at the heart of it all. "Don't stop on my account. I've traveled rather a long way to watch the ending."
