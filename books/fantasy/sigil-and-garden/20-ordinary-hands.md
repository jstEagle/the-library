## 20. Ordinary Hands

Edmund woke to the sound of fire.

Not the roar of it—that had come and gone in the night like a tide, filling the Emberhold's great hearth until the stones themselves seemed to sing—but the ordinary, domestic sound of a fire burning well. The pop of resin in seasoned wood. The soft collapse of a log settling into its own embers. A sound he had heard ten thousand times in ten thousand rooms, and never once listened to.

He lay still on the narrow cot in the warden's chamber and tried, out of habit older than memory, to reach for the realms.

Nothing.

It was not silence exactly. Silence implied an edge, a boundary where sound stopped and something else began. This was more like waking from a fever to find the world had always been this shape—flat, singular, closed—and every sense of depth he'd ever carried had been the illness. He reached the way he had reached since childhood, that inward turning of attention toward the thin places where dragon-fire bled through into mortal soil, and found only Edmund: his own heartbeat, his own breath, the ache in his hands where the sigil-carving knife had bitten deep.

He opened his eyes.

The chamber was small and round, built into the Emberhold's shoulder, its single window framing a wedge of grey morning sky. Someone had laid a blanket over him that he did not remember pulling up. Someone had set a basin of water on the stool beside the cot, and a clean cloth folded beside it, and a cup of something dark and steaming that smelled of bitterroot and honey—Brannoc's recipe, though Brannoc was three days dead now, burned to ash in the doorway he had refused to yield.

Edmund sat up slowly, the way old men sit up, testing each joint for treachery. His hands were bandaged. Under the linen, the lines of the Raven sigil he had carved into his own palms were scabbed and furious, but when he flexed his fingers they obeyed him, plain flesh and bone and tendon doing what plain flesh does. No light leaked from the cuts. No warmth gathered there beyond the warmth of healing.

*Ordinary hands,* he thought, and waited for the grief to arrive.

It came, but not the way he expected. Not as a wave. As a hollow—a space in his chest shaped exactly like everything he had given away, aching the way an amputated limb aches, present in its absence. He could feel the shape of the covenant now the way you feel a tooth after the tooth is pulled: with the tongue of the soul, probing the gap, unable to stop probing.

And beneath the hollow, lighter than he had any right to be—

He got up. His legs held. He crossed to the window and looked out over the borderlands, and for the first time in his life he saw them without seeing through them.

That was the thing no one told you about magic. It stood between you and the world. Every hedge-mage knew it in his bones without knowing he knew it: the way power made everything double. Look at a hill and see the hill, yes, but also the ley-vein running under it, the old blessing soaked into its roots, the dragon-road that had once crossed its spine. Look at a stranger and see the stranger, but also the flicker of their aura, the signature of their craft or their emptiness. You never got the world alone. You never got the world *first.*

Now Edmund looked at the hills and saw hills. Brown and green and grey in the early light, wet with last night's rain, utterly unremarkable, utterly themselves. A hawk turned circles over the valley with nothing beneath its flight but air. Somewhere downslope a stream was talking to itself over stones, and the sound was only a sound, and it was the most beautiful thing he had heard in years precisely because it asked nothing of him, promised nothing, meant nothing beyond water moving downhill toward the low country.

He laughed. It came out cracked and startled, half sob, and he pressed his bandaged hand against his mouth to hold the rest of it in, and failed, and let it out anyway—laughing alone in a dead warden's chamber while the fire he had reforged with his own blood roared gently in the hearth below.

*Strangely light.* That was the phrase Isolde would use later, when he finally found words for it. Strangely light, like a man who has carried a pack so long he keeps reaching for straps that are no longer there, and stumbles at their absence, and then walks on amazed at how far his legs will carry him.

* * *

Isolde was in the hearth-hall.

She sat on the stone bench before the great fireplace, wrapped in Brannoc's spare cloak—it hung off her shoulders like a sail furled wrong—and she was watching the fire with an expression Edmund had never seen her wear. He had known her angry, contemptuous, afraid, determined, tender in the fierce grudging way she permitted herself tenderness. He had never seen her simply *watching*, the way a person watches something they love and cannot fix.

The dragon-fire filled the hearth from floor to lintel. It was not flame as Vellumgate understood flame. It moved slow, like something breathing, and its color shifted as he watched—gold to rose to a green deep as deep water—and where it touched the blackened hearthstone, the stone drank the light and gave back warmth instead of shadow. Carved into the hearthstone's face, still raw-edged, still weeping faint traces of his blood into the grain, the Raven sigil blazed softly with each swell of the fire, dimming and brightening like a heart at rest.

His name was written in fire now. It would burn there as long as the fire burned, which was to say, as long as either realm endured.

"You're supposed to be resting," Isolde said, without turning around.

"I've been resting for two days. I woke up and couldn't feel my own feet, so I figured I'd better check whether I still owned them."

"They're yours." She still didn't turn. "Everything's yours now. That's rather the point."

He crossed the hall slowly. The fire's heat reached him at six paces, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck. Up close he could see what the night's work had cost her too—she had ash in her hair, real ash, grey streaking the black, and there were burns along her forearms where Seraphel's stolen fire had licked out at her in the final moments. She had bound them herself, badly, the way people bind their own wounds when there is no one they trust enough to ask.

"Let me see your arms," Edmund said.

"It's nothing."

"Isolde."

She sighed—the long-suffering sigh of a woman conceding a battle she had already lost—and held out her arms. He sat beside her on the bench and unwound the scorched wrappings and cleaned the burns with the basin water he'd brought down, working in silence, the way you tend a wound you have tended before. Her skin was hot under his fingers. Ordinary heat. He could no longer feel the sigil-work knotted beneath it, the keeper's marks that ran from her wrists to her shoulders like a script only fire could read, and the not-feeling of them was its own strange intimacy: he touched her arms and felt only Isolde, the woman, the whole of her, nothing hidden behind the craft.

"The fire is strong," she said quietly, while he worked. "Stronger than it's been in a hundred years. Brannoc said—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Brannoc used to say it guttered because it was lonely. That fire isn't fuel, Edmund. It's relationship. It burns on what is given to it. And for a century, no one gave it anything. Everyone just took." She looked at her bandaged arms, at his hands rewrapping them. "Last night someone gave it everything."

"Heartfelt," Edmund said. "You should write it down. Prophecies seem to run in the family these days."

That earned him half a smile, gone as quickly as it came. She pulled her arms back and re-clasped her hands in her lap, and for a while they sat together and watched the fire breathe.

"I can't feel it," Edmund said at last. "Any of it. I reached this morning, first thing, before I even opened my eyes. Habit." He shook his head. "There's nothing there, Isolde. There's no *there* there. The realms might as well be a story someone told me." He paused. "I keep waiting for it to be worse than it is."

"And is it?"

"No." He laughed again, softer this time, bewildered by his own answer. "That's the mad part. I spent thirty years hiding, starving the little magic I had, burying it under charm-craft and misdirection, terrified of the day someone would take it from me. And now someone has—me, myself, all of it—and I feel..." He searched for the word and found it waiting, patient as the stream outside. "*Light.*"

Isolde studied him for a long moment. In the firelight her eyes were the same green as the fire's deepest register, and he understood suddenly why the old texts called the keeper's sight a burden: she was looking at him with eyes that could still see both worlds, and he wondered what she saw. A man hollowed out? A man freed?

"Grief isn't done with you," she said. "Don't mistake the shock for peace. It'll come back around. Grief always makes another pass."

"I know." He did know. He had buried a mother; he understood the rhythm of it, how loss circled like the hawk over the valley, striking when you least guarded the sky. "But Isolde—I need to tell you what it's like right now, while it's true, before the second pass comes. Because I don't think anyone's ever written it down, and someone should."

She waited.

"When I had magic," he said, "I was never entirely *in* the room. Part of me was always listening downward, or outward, or backward—to the ley under the floor, to the wards on the door, to the past pressing on the present. Even hiding was a kind of attention. Thirty years of holding still, of being a held breath." He spread his hands, bandaged, plain, astonishingly light. "This morning I watched a hawk for ten minutes and thought about nothing except the hawk. Do you understand? Nothing. Just the hawk. I have never in my life thought about only one thing at a time. I didn't know it was allowed."

Something moved across Isolde's face—not quite envy, not quite fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing described the country she had been exiled from, and wondering, despite herself, despite everything, what the air was like there.

"You'll want it back," she said. "One day. Everyone does. The texts are full of bearers who regretted the giving."

"Maybe." He wouldn't insult her by pretending certainty. He had seen himself clearly enough in the rite's mirror-light to know his own hunger, the Ministry-shaped appetite that lived in every mage and that Seraphel had merely fed until it ate her. The hunger didn't die when the power did. It just went quiet, the way a dog goes quiet, and waited. "Maybe. But if I do—if one day I catch myself reaching for it, resenting the fire, resenting *you* for keeping what I gave—then I'm asking you to remind me of this morning. Of the hawk. Of how it felt to be light. Promise me that."

"That's a heavy promise to put on a woman who has to watch you make it."

"It's the only kind worth making."

She looked at him a long time. Then she nodded, once, the way she nodded over terms of a bargain—keeper's nod, covenant nod—and he knew the promise was entered somewhere in whatever ledger she kept now behind her eyes.

* * *

They buried Brannoc at noon.

There was no body to bury—Seraphel's fire had taken even that, leaving only the scorch-shadow of an old man standing in a doorway, arms wide, that the rain was slowly washing from the stone—but they buried his staff, and his ring of hearth-keys, and the small worn book of tending-notes he had kept for sixty years, in the warden's garden behind the Emberhold, where the soil was black and rich and smelled, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke.

Edmund dug the grave himself. He insisted. Isolde stood by with the staff across her arms and let him, because she understood that some griefs must be worked with the back and the hands or they curdle into something worse. The digging was hard and slow and it hurt his carved palms abominably, and he was grateful for every spadeful, because pain in ordinary hands was a language he could speak fluently now, and grief needed a language.

When the grave was filled they stood over it in the wind, and Isolde spoke the keeper's words—old ones, older than the Ministry, older than Vellumgate—in a voice that the fire seemed to lean toward through the walls behind them. Edmund didn't know the words. He listened the way you listen to rain: not to understand, but to be inside something larger than yourself.

"...and the hearth remembers its keeper," Isolde finished, "and the keeper's keeping passes to the fire, and the fire gives it back to the land, and the land holds it for those who come after. Rest, Brannoc Ashwarden. Your watch is ended. Ours begins."

"Ours," Edmund echoed, when the words had blown away.

"You're staying," Isolde said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. It was the tone of a woman setting a term on the table and waiting to see if it would be accepted.

"For now." He wiped the spade and leaned it against the wall. "Someone has to teach me the names of the trees. I've lived next to the borderlands my whole life and I couldn't tell an ash from an alder if my supper depended on it."

"That's a poor reason to stay."

"It's an excellent reason to stay. The best reasons always look poor from the outside." He looked at her. "What about you? When do you go back?"

"Soon. There's a covenant to rebuild, and rebuilding starts in Vellumgate—with the charm-folk, with Corwin's frightened congregation, with the archive itself. The Ministry's hoard doesn't vanish because Seraphel is dust. Someone has to decide, lawfully, openly, what happens to a century of stolen fire." She grimaced. "Ledgers. Negotiations. Committees. The unglamorous machinery of keeping."

"Gods preserve you."

"Gods preserve us both," she said dryly, "though I notice they've outsourced the actual work." Then her voice gentled, which it did rarely and never by accident. "Come with me. To Vellumgate. When I go."

Edmund was quiet for a moment. Below them, through the Emberhold's ancient walls, the dragon-fire breathed, and he could not feel it, and the not-feeling was a hollow, and the hollow was getting easier to carry, the way a scar gets easier.

"Not yet," he said. "Soon. But not yet. I spent thirty years being invisible in that city, Isolde. I think I need to learn how to be visible somewhere smaller first. Somewhere the fire knows my name and everyone else is willing to let me earn theirs." He smiled ruefully. "Besides. You'll be back and forth. Keepers travel. It's in the job description—I read it, in the archive, page four hundred and something."

"Page four hundred and twelve," Isolde said. "You would remember the page number."

"I have an ordinary mind now. It hoards trivia because it can't hoard power."

She laughed—really laughed, short and surprised out of her—and the sound went through the hollow in his chest and did not fill it, but warmed it, the way the hearth warmed the stone: not restoring what was lost, but making the loss livable.

They walked back inside together, out of the wind, and the fire rose to meet them, gold and rose and deep-water green, and Edmund held his bare hands out to it—his ordinary hands, his emptied hands, the hands that had saved two worlds by giving up the only thing that had ever made him special—and felt only warmth.

Only warmth. Only warmth, and the weight of nothing, and the first unhurried afternoon of the rest of his life stretching out before him like unmapped country.

Outside, far to the west, a rider was climbing the border road toward the Emberhold, leading a second horse, and on the second horse were strapped bundles wrapped in oilcloth, and the bundles were full of books.

But that, as Edmund would say later, is getting ahead of the story.
