## 21. Vellumgate, Unsealed

The first thing Corwin Ashby learned about penance was that it smelled like lime and sawdust.

He stood in the middle of Charmers' Row with a hammer in his hand and a bucket of mortar at his feet, watching the morning fog lift off the ruins of the quarter he had helped burn. The Ministry sweepers had been thorough—of course they had; thoroughness had been the whole point—but they had not been complete. Fire always left something. A doorframe here, a cellar stair there, a chimney standing like a gravestone over a house that no longer existed. The people of Vellumgate were learning to build around what remained.

"Mind the lintel," said Widow Sarey, hauling a basket of kiln-bricks past him on her hip. "It's original. My grandmother hung that lintel when the Row was nothing but three stalls and an argument."

Corwin shifted his ladder two feet to the left. "Yes, ma'am."

"You'll want to say more than yes, ma'am, if you mean to be forgiven," she said, not unkindly, not kindly either. "But I suppose it's a start."

He supposed it was.

Four months since the Emberhold. Four months since he had led Seraphel's column up the mountain road with his Ministry seal in his fist and his stomach full of ice, telling himself all the way that he would find some way to warn them, some way to turn it aside at the last moment—and then hadn't, because there was never a last moment, only this one and then the next one, each demanding a courage he kept promising to have later. Brannoc had died because of that arithmetic. Edmund's old life had burned for it. And Corwin had come down from the hills afterward carrying a letter in Edmund's handwriting—not absolution, just truth—and found that the truth weighed more than any sentence the courts could have given him.

The courts, in the end, had given him almost nothing. That was the strange part. When the covenant reforged itself in dragon-fire and Lady Seraphel collapsed into ash-dust on the hearthstone of her own stolen power, the Ministry of Seals had not so much fallen as evaporated. Its seals went dark overnight. Its archives opened—or rather, were opened, by crowds who found shelves upon shelves of confiscated charms, bottled blessings, sealed sigils, whole lifetimes of magic hoarded under the banner of law. The magistrates who remained had spent four months doing the only thing left to them: giving it back. House by house. Name by name. Ledger by ledger.

Corwin had volunteered for the restitution office on the second day. Nobody asked him to. That was rather the point.

"Master Ashby." A voice behind him, dry as old paper. He turned and bowed slightly to Magistrate Ondrell, who had once signed the orders for the purge and now spent her mornings signing their reversals. She looked, if anything, more tired than guilty. Guilt at least had energy to it.

"The lintel stays," Corwin said. "I've marked the new frame to fit around it."

"Mm." She surveyed the Row with the expression of a woman reading a confession she'd written herself. "The Vane woman sent word. Keeper Isolde, I should say. She'll walk the Row tomorrow, to lay the new warding-stones properly. Lawfully, I mean. She asks that the charm-folk be told."

"I'll tell them."

"She also asked—" Ondrell paused, and Corwin watched her choose her words with the care of someone defusing something. "She asked whether you'd be present. She said the stones take easier when they're laid by hands that know both sides of the seal."

Both sides of the seal. There it was again, the thing everyone in Vellumgate had decided to do with him: use him. Not punish him, not forgive him—use him, which was harder and better and worse. His knowledge of Ministry craft was now a public tool, pried out of him in deposition after deposition until the archives themselves had been mapped from his memory. Every secret he had kept, he now gave away daily, free of charge, to anyone who asked. It was astonishing how much lighter he felt and how much heavier.

"I'll be present," he said.

Ondrell nodded and moved on down the Row, and Corwin picked up his hammer and went back to work on the frame of Sarey's granddaughter's shop, where a luck-charm seller had once operated out of a back room and paid rent in small kindnesses. He worked until his shoulders burned and the fog burned off entirely, and at midday he ate bread and pickles on an overturned crate while children chased each other between the half-built walls, playing at dragons. They roared. They fell down laughing. One of them wore a paper sigil pinned to his shirt—a raven, crudely drawn—which every child in Vellumgate seemed to own these days, sold by street vendors alongside candied nuts.

The Raven Covenant. It was on banners now, and ballads, most of them terrible. Corwin had heard six versions of the ballad of Edmund Ravensworth, and in five of them Edmund died gloriously, and in the sixth he married a dragon. The real Edmund had become a cartographer, of all things, and lived above a map shop near the Weavers' Bridge, and had sent Corwin exactly one letter since the Emberhold: short, plain, without cruelty and without warmth, containing a list of the charm-sellers taken in the purge whose families might still live within reach of Vellumgate. *Find them,* the letter said. *That will do more than apologies.* So Corwin had found eleven families in four months. Two had slammed doors in his face. One had thrown a cup. Eight had listened, and of those eight, perhaps three would ever speak to him again, and he counted those three the way a miser counts coins.

*That will do more than apologies.* It did not do more. But it did *something*, and something, Corwin had learned, was the only currency redemption traded in. It did not come as a pardon. It came as sawdust.

* * *

In the afternoon, a girl of about nine appeared at the edge of the worksite and stood watching him with the fixed, fearless stare of children and cats.

"You're the one who burned the Row," she said.

Corwin set down his trowel. "I'm the one building it now."

"That's what my mum says. She says you're doing your penance." She came closer, hands in the pockets of an oversized coat. On her lapel, a raven sigil of tin, polished bright. "Did you really know him? Before? Edmund?"

"I did. Since we were boys smaller than you."

"What was he like?"

Corwin considered the question honestly, which was the only way he permitted himself to consider anything anymore. "Afraid," he said. "All the time. He was the best-hidden man in Vellumgate, and hiding is a kind of fear. And kind, underneath it, in a way he thought was a liability. He used to sell luck-charms to clerks who couldn't afford them and pretend he'd charged them full price."

The girl frowned. "Why pretend?"

"So they wouldn't feel small."

"Oh," she said, in the tone of someone filing away an important fact about the world. Then: "My mum bought a finding-charm from him once. Before. It found our cat. The cat had been dead two days, though. It found the cat anyway." She looked up at him. "Was that a good charm or a bad one?"

Corwin thought about it. "I think it was a true one. It just didn't flinch from the answer."

She nodded seriously, satisfied, and ran off toward the bridge, roaring softly to herself, and Corwin sat on his crate and let the grief come, because it still did that sometimes, ambushed him in ordinary moments—the dead cat, the clerk, the boy he'd known before ambition taught him that friendship was a resource. He had sold Edmund for a Ministry seat, but the terrible truth, the one he made himself look at every day, was that he had never even wanted the seat very much. He had wanted to matter. And the Ministry had understood, better than he did himself, that a man who wants to matter can be handed anything at all, provided it comes with a title.

Now he mattered in the only way that stuck: eight families found, one lintel preserved, three coins in the miser's count.

* * *

Toward evening, word moved down the Row faster than any crier: the keeper had come early. Isolde Vane walked the ruins in a grey traveling cloak, unaccompanied, stopping here and there to kneel and press her palm flat against the scorched ground as though listening for a pulse beneath it. People gathered at a respectful distance, the way they'd gathered for Seraphel once, though the resemblance ended there. Where the Minister had demanded awe, Isolde seemed embarrassed by attention. She had the look of someone perpetually late for an appointment with work.

Corwin approached when she waved him over—she had seen him, of course; keepers saw everything, that was rather the job description now.

"Magistrate Ondrell said you wanted me at the warding," he said.

"I want your hands, not your presence." She held out a satchel. Inside were seven stones, each the size of a fist, carved with the covenant-raven in a style older than the Ministry, older than the outlawry. "These are anchor-stones for the Row's new ward. The old one was a suppression-net—Ministry work, fed from confiscated power, designed to smother anything true. What replaces it has to be laid by someone who understands suppression from the inside." Her eyes were steady and without malice, which somehow made it harder. "You sealed things for them. You know how seals lie. Help me make sure these don't."

He took the satchel. The stones were warm. That startled him—he hadn't touched openly living magic in years, not outside the restitution vaults, and warmth felt indecent, like hearing laughter in a sickroom.

"Where does the power come from?" he asked. "For the ward. The Ministry hoarded everything."

"Not everything." She turned toward the far end of the Row, where the fog was returning off the river, silver-grey and soft. "The covenant flows both ways, Corwin. Dragon-fire blesses the land; mortal craft feeds it back. It's a circulation, not a treasury. Seraphel dammed the river and called the reservoir law. We're just reopening the channel." She glanced at him sidelong. "Edmund reopened it. With everything he had. All we're doing is keeping the ditches clear."

They laid the stones together through the last hour of light. Isolde sang under her breath—an old cadence, sigil-cant, half prayer and half instruction—and Corwin set each stone into its bed of mortar at the points she indicated, seven points for the seven gates of the old quarter. At the fourth stone, his hands shook. At the fifth, they stopped shaking. He could feel the ward taking shape the way you can feel a house becoming home: not dramatic, structural. Something that would hold.

At the seventh stone, Isolde straightened and wiped her palms on her cloak. "Done," she said. Then, quieter: "You laid them well."

"They're not the first things I've laid for the Ministry."

"No. But they're the first things you've laid for anyone else." She shouldered her empty satchel. "Edmund sends a message, by the way. He's mapping the root-roads—all of them, every branch, so no road is ever forgotten enough to be lost again. He says the eastern fork surfaces inside the city, under the old archive. He says—" and here she paused, and Corwin braced, "—he says you'd be the right person to walk it with him when he surveys it. Next month. If you're willing."

Corwin looked at the seventh stone, at the raven carved into it, wings spread over the small bright flame cut into its heart.

"He knows I'd go anywhere he asked," Corwin said. "That was the problem, once. I'd go anywhere anyone important asked. He's testing whether the difference has sunk in."

"Is it sunk in?"

"It's sunk in deep enough to hurt. That's usually how you know."

Isolde almost smiled. Almost. "Next month, then." She walked off down the Row toward the river gate, and the folk of the charm-quarter parted for her and closed behind her, and the new ward woke with a sound nobody heard but everybody felt—a settling, like a long-held breath finally released.

That night Corwin climbed the half-built scaffolding of Sarey's shop and sat with his back against the preserved lintel, and looked out over Vellumgate. Lamps were lit in windows where lamps had been forbidden to burn certain colors. Somewhere a fiddler played one of the terrible ballads, and got the words wrong, and nobody minded. On the horizon, faint as breath on glass, the mountains glowed the way mountains are not supposed to glow—a thread of ember-light along their spine, the dragon-fire visible again after a century of dark, blessing the land whether or not anyone believed in it.

Redemption, Corwin thought, was a root-road. It didn't surface where you expected. It ran underground a long way, through dark, and you walked it not because you could see the end but because somewhere ahead, someone had left a lamp burning and a map with your name penciled beside a date.

Next month. The eastern fork. Edmund.

He took out his knife and, by lamplight, began carving his initials into the underside of the old lintel—small, humble, where only the building would know—when his eye caught something already scratched there in the ancient wood, faded nearly to nothing, in a hand he knew better than his own.

His mother's hand.

And beside it, a date.
