## 1. The Date in the Margin

The chapel at Greyfen Pass had lost its roof sometime before Gideon's grandfather learned to walk, and what remained of it had decided, apparently, to lose the rest on its own schedule. Three walls of mossy stone, a doorway with no door, and a bell tower that leaned like a drunk telling a secret. Gideon had sheltered in worse. He'd once spent a whole night inside a dead giant's ribcage, and the ribs had been draftier.

He shrugged off his pack, set it against the altar — which was less an altar now than a table with ambitions — and began the small ritual of every smuggler's evening: inventory, concealment, dinner.

"Let's see," he murmured to himself, unbuckling the pack's false bottom. "Twelve luck-charms, three of them actually lucky. A fertility talisman I am never speaking of again. Two ward-stones chipped enough that they'll ward nothing bigger than a soup pot." He held one up to the last grey light coming through the empty window frames. "And the good stuff."

The good stuff was six vials of bottled hearthlight, warm as a kitten and worth more than the horse he'd sold two valleys back. Bottled hearthlight was illegal above the snowline for reasons nobody could adequately explain to him, which usually meant the reasons were embarrassing to somebody important. Gideon's entire philosophy of life fit neatly inside that observation.

He was halfway through a heel of bread and a wedge of cheese so hard it might legally have been considered a building material when he heard footsteps on the gravel outside.

Not heavy steps. Not the measured clank of Thornfell border wardens, who walked like they were trying to nail their boots to the ground with each stride. These were light, quick, deliberate — someone used to walking quietly but not ashamed of it.

Gideon did what any sensible man did. He put out the fire, picked up his knife, and stepped into the shadow behind the leaning bell tower with the practiced ease of a man who had been hiding from things since before he could write.

The figure in the doorway was a young woman, hooded, mud to the knees, carrying a satchel the size and approximate shape of a small angry dog. She looked around the chapel with the air of someone checking whether a room still matched her memory of it, then her gaze went directly — unnervingly directly — to the shadows where Gideon stood.

"You're the charm-runner," she said. "Gideon Blackwood."

"That depends entirely on who's asking," said the shadow, "and what they're offering, and how quickly they can be talked out of whatever they came for."

"My name is Wren." She pulled back her hood. She was younger than he'd guessed — twenty, maybe twenty-two, with a wind-burned face and eyes the flat grey of slate that had been rained on a long time. "I've been looking for you for eleven days."

"Well," Gideon said, deciding the shadow game was lost and stepping into view, "I hope you enjoyed them. Most people find me much faster. I'm told I have a memorable face." It wasn't true. His face was the kind you forgot while looking at it, a professional asset he maintained through aggressive blandness. "But I don't sell to people who find me. I sell to people I find. House rule."

"I'm not here to buy charms."

"Nobody's ever here to buy charms. Then something happens and suddenly everybody's here to buy charms." He returned to his seat on the fallen lintel and resumed his cheese with dignity. "So what are you here for?"

She sat down across from him without being invited, which he found both rude and slightly admirable, and unslung the satchel. From it she drew a book.

Now, Gideon had seen a lot of books in his line of work. Grimoires, mostly, or things pretending to be grimoires, which were usually just cookbooks with dramatic covers. This was neither. It was big — the kind of big that meant it had been written by someone who expected to be taken seriously or expected never to be moved again — bound in leather gone dark and soft with age, its corners worn round as river stones.

"A ledger," Wren said, setting it on the stone between them. "Written over a hundred years ago."

"I'm thrilled for it."

"It's a book of prophecies."

Gideon sighed, which was a sound he made often enough that friends had learned to recognize it as a complete sentence meaning *here we go*. "Look. I appreciate the enthusiasm, truly. But I've met prophecy before. There was a woman in Lowmarsh who read my future in the drippings of a tallow candle and charged me four coppers for it. She said I'd come into great fortune beside water. Two days later I fell in a creek and lost my boots. Technically she was right, but I don't feel enriched."

"This isn't candle wax." Wren opened the book with the care of someone handling a sleeping animal. The pages were dense with handwriting in three different hands — Gideon could see where one script gave way to another, then another, generations of keepers adding to the record. And threaded through the prose ran columns of dates, names, and small annotations in red. "Every entry is dated. Every date has proven correct. The flood at Millbrook, the year the bees died, the fall of the Marrow Bridge — all written down decades before they happened. My order kept this book for a century and checked it against the world every single day."

"And where's your order now?"

She didn't answer immediately, and something in her silence made Gideon stop chewing.

"Burned out," she said finally, lightly, the way people say things when they've practiced saying them lightly. "Two years ago. I'm the last keeper. Which is why I'm here, sitting in a ruin, talking to a smuggler, instead of somewhere warm doing literally anything else."

"All right," Gideon said slowly. "Ledger. Prophecies. Dead order. Last keeper. You've got atmosphere, I'll give you that. What does it say about me? Let me guess — I'm the chosen one. I'm going to unite the shards of some broken crown and defeat a darkness. Do I get a sword? Everyone always gets a sword. I'd want a good one. Nothing ornamental."

Wren turned pages. Her fingers knew exactly where to go, the way his knew the false bottom of his own pack, and he felt a small cold thread of recognition: *this book is her whole life.*

"Tonight's date," she said.

"It's not night yet."

"The date of tonight." She turned the book toward him and tapped a line with one grimy finger. "Here."

The page was dense, but she'd found the place the way you find your own name shouted in a crowd. There, in the margin beside a block of cramped prophecy-text, in ink faded to brown:

*The ninth of First Thaw. Blackwood comes to the high chapel. The debt wakes.*

Gideon looked at it for a long moment.

Then he laughed.

It was a good laugh — warm, easy, the laugh of a man who had survived a lot by refusing to take things seriously. "Blackwood," he said. "That's fantastic. Do you know how many Blackwoods there are in these mountains? Blackwoods are like stones. My father had six brothers and every one of them bred like rabbits. Some cousin of mine comes to a ruined chapel every other week — half of us are smugglers, we all use the same passes—"

"Gideon," Wren said. "Your name is written down. In a book that has never once been wrong. Written before your grandfather was born."

"So were floods. Floods happen. People visit chapels. It's coincidence with calligraphy." He leaned forward, tapping the page. "Besides, look at the rest of it. 'The debt wakes.' Debt? Whose debt? I owe eleven coppers to a ferryman named Doss and I fully intend to continue owing them until Doss dies, at which point the debt dies with him, that's the beautiful thing about ferryman debts—"

"*Your* debt," Wren said quietly.

The fire had been out for ten minutes, but the chapel seemed to get colder anyway. Gideon noticed, distantly, that the light through the empty windows had changed — lower, bluer, the light of evening arriving early, though the season was wrong for early evenings.

"What's that supposed to mean?" he asked, and heard his own voice do something it rarely did: lose its polish. "What debt? I told you, I'm careful with money. Careless with everything else, but money—"

"Not money." She was watching him with those rain-slate eyes, and there was no satisfaction in her face, none of the smugness of a person winning an argument. If anything she looked sorry. That was the worst part. "I don't know what kind of debt. The ledger doesn't explain itself, it never explains itself, that's the whole curse of keeping it. It says what happens. Never why." She turned the page, and there was more text below, and Gideon found himself reading it upside down before he could stop himself, the way you read your own name on a bill of sale.

*Blackwood comes to the high chapel. The debt wakes. What was given before breath shall be asked after it. The pale court keeps its accounts, and the account is old, and the account is due.*

"'Pale court,'" Gideon repeated. "That sounds like a legal term. Is that a legal term? I know a magistrate in Hollowbridge who'd love that phrase."

"They're real too," Wren said. "The pale ones. You'd know them if you saw them. Everyone does. Something in the back of the head knows."

Something in the back of Gideon's head chose that moment to shiver, which he elected to ignore.

"Right," he said, standing up, because standing up felt necessary, felt like a thing a free man would do. "Here's what's going to happen. You're going to close your spooky book. I'm going to finish my cheese. Tomorrow morning we part ways, and you go be haunted somewhere south of here, where the winters are polite. Because I have six vials of contraband hearthlight to move over the pass before the thaw roads close, and I do not have time to be a paragraph in somebody's margins."

"The thaw roads closed three days ago," Wren said. "You haven't heard? The weather's turned wrong. It shouldn't snow for another month, but the shepherds on the south face say—"

Outside, something whispered.

Both of them went still. It wasn't wind. Wind pushes; this drifted, soft as ash settling, and it caught the blue light as it came through the roofless nave — small, slow, white flakes turning in the dead-still air.

Snow. On the ninth of First Thaw, when the mountain should have been dripping and the streams should have been running loud with meltwater.

Gideon watched it fall through the empty window frame, and the laughing feeling he'd been carrying all evening — the armor of it, the warmth of it — went quiet in his chest, the way a fire goes quiet when someone opens a door somewhere far away.

"That's not right," he said. His voice sounded smaller in the stone room than it had a minute ago. "That's — the thaw's early this year. It's been warm for weeks. I crossed a river yesterday that was practically gossiping, it was so loud with melt."

"I know," Wren said. She hadn't looked surprised. That was somehow worse. She simply reached out and turned the ledger another page, and her finger came to rest on a line near the bottom, small, almost an afterthought, in the oldest hand of all:

*When the snow falls out of season, the Court counts its coin.*

The flakes drifted past the window. One landed on the sill and did not melt.

Gideon looked at it for a long time. He thought about his mother, whom he did not remember, and the word *debt*, and the way the pale figures of children's stories were always described — courteously, everyone always insisted on that word, *courteous*, like it was the most frightening thing about them, which of course it was.

"Eleven days," he said at last. "You looked for me for eleven days. Why not just send a letter? 'Dear Mr. Blackwood, terrible news regarding your soul, please respond by post.'"

"Would you have come?"

"No."

"There it is." Almost, almost, she smiled. It was a tired smile, the smile of someone who had rehearsed this conversation alone for years and was relieved beyond words to finally be having it badly instead. "For what it's worth, I hoped you'd laugh. The ledger says the Blackwood laughs first. Right there." She pointed. "'He will not believe, and his disbelief will be a mercy, briefly.'"

"A mercy," Gideon said. "Briefly." He looked out at the snow falling silent and unhurried between the chapel walls, filling the roofless nave like light falling into a well, and felt the last of the evening's humor drain away into a cold, clear, unwelcome clarity. "How long is briefly?"

Wren closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

"The snow started," she said, "and I'd guess we have until moonrise."

* * *

They sat in the ruined chapel while the snow thickened, and Gideon told himself he was only staying because the pass would be impassable in a whiteout and leaving would be idiotic. It was a reasonable explanation. It was even mostly true.

But his eyes kept going to the ledger, and to Wren's hand resting on it, steady as a compass needle, and to the window where the out-of-season snow piled on a sill that should have been running with meltwater — and the uneasiness in him grew roots, quietly, in the dark, the way uneasiness does.

"Tell me about the debt," he said finally. "All of it. Start from the beginning."

"The beginning's not in the book," Wren said. "The beginning is in a cottage two valleys east, with a hedge-witch who owes you an explanation."

"I don't have a hedge-witch," Gideon said. "I had one. I left. Eleven years ago, and I've been very careful never to go back."

"Yes," Wren said softly. "The ledger mentions that too."

Outside, far down the pass, something pale began to move through the trees.
