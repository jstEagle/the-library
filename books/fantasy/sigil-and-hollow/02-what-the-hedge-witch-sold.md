## 2. What the Hedge-Witch Sold

The pale figures came over the pass at dusk, and they did not leave footprints in the snow.

Gideon noticed it the way a smuggler notices most things that will ruin his evening: late, and all at once. He had stopped to re-tie a bootlace on the switchback above the tree line — a lie he told himself; really he'd stopped because his chest was doing something unpleasant and tight ever since he'd read that margin in Wren's ledger — and when he straightened up, there they were. Three of them. Far below on the white road, moving without hurry, without breath-plume, without any of the small honest messes that living bodies make.

They were too tall. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that their faces kept *settling*, like water finding its level, like masks deciding whose face to be today.

"Right," Gideon said aloud, to nobody, because talking to himself was one of the few habits from his apprenticeship he'd never managed to shake. "That's not weather."

He did what any sensible man would do with a soul that was possibly owed to somebody: he ran.

* * *

Maren's cottage sat in a fold of the mountain where the pines grew thick enough to swallow sound, three days' hard walk from the pass if you took smugglers' cuts and didn't sleep. Gideon took them and didn't. By the time he hammered on her door — hammering being the only language they still shared — his hands were shaking and the sky had begun, quietly and obscenely, to snow.

In midsummer.

The flakes were fat and slow and wrong, falling through air warm enough that they should have been rain. They landed on his sleeves and stayed whole, each one perfect as a coin struck by some meticulous god.

The door opened before his third knock. Maren Osgood stood in the gap looking exactly as she had seven years ago, which was to say: small, gray-stubbled chin, eyes like two wet river stones, and an expression that suggested she had been expecting trouble for so long that its arrival was almost a relief.

"You," she said.

"Me," Gideon agreed. "Lovely to see you too. May I come in before the impossible snow gets me?"

She looked past him at the road, at the snow, at something behind him that he couldn't see but felt anyway, like a draft from a door left open in winter. Then she stepped aside.

"Mind the cat."

There was no cat. There had never been a cat. It was a test she'd run on him since he was nine years old, and he answered it the way he always had: "Your cat's imaginary, you old fraud." Something in her shoulders loosened half an inch, and he understood, with a lurch, that she had genuinely been checking whether he was still himself.

Which raised the question of what else might have knocked.

* * *

The cottage smelled of rosemary, tallow, and the particular metallic tang of charms worked badly by desperate people — contraband work, the kind Gideon himself carried over the passes in false-bottomed crates. She lit the fire without striking anything, which she'd never done when he was young, and he filed that away under *things my former master has learned to be afraid of.*

He told her everything. The chapel. The girl with the ledger. His own name written in a dead woman's cipher beside tonight's date. And then the figures on the pass, walking without footprints, faces settling like silt.

Maren listened with her hands wrapped around a cup she never drank from. When he finished, she was quiet long enough that the fire got nervous and spat.

"Say something," Gideon said finally. "You're the one who taught me that knowledge delayed is knowledge decayed. Your words. I've been quoting you smugly for years."

"I taught you a great many things," Maren said. "Some of them were even true." She set the cup down. "Gideon. Do you remember why you ran?"

He laughed, because the alternative was something worse. "I remember I was seventeen and insufferable, and you told me my charm-work was 'adequate,' which from you was practically a marriage proposal, and I decided I deserved better than adequate. I went over the pass with forty silver marks and a stolen coat. It's not a mystery, Maren. It's just a Tuesday."

"It isn't." Her voice had gone very gentle, the way it did when she was about to tell a farmer his cow was beyond saving. "You ran because I made you run. I told you your work was adequate when it was brilliant. I picked fights. I gave you reasons. Seven years ago, I needed you gone from this valley before Midwinter's Eve, and I needed you gone angry, because angry men don't look back."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wrong snow kept falling, patient as arithmetic.

"Why?" Gideon said. It came out smaller than he intended.

"Because tonight is Midwinter's Eve," Maren said, "and the Hollow Court beneath Thornfell collects its debts on Midwinter's Eve. And the debt they were coming to collect—" she looked at him then, directly, and he wished to every merciful power that she hadn't— "was you."

* * *

She told it plainly, because Maren had never learned how to soften anything. He'd hated that about her once. Now he clung to it like a rail in a rockslide.

His mother had come to her in the last month of the pregnancy. Gideon had known almost nothing about her — a name, Sera Blackwood, a face he could no longer hold steady in memory, the fact of her absence the way you know the shape of a missing tooth. Maren filled in the rest in a flat voice while the fire burned low.

Sera had been running too. From what, she'd never fully say — debts, a man, a name that meant something bad in the low country. She'd been starving, alone, weeks from anywhere, and she'd come to the hedge-witch's door with frostbite on her fingers and terror in her mouth.

And she'd been offered a bargain. Not by Maren — Maren had merely been the broker, the local hand, the one who knew how to draw the circle and speak the old courtesies. The offer had come from below. From the court under Thornfell, which in those years had still sent envoys up instead of collectors.

"They wanted a soul," Maren said. "Unborn ones are best. Unclaimed. A soul that has never chosen anything yet belongs wholly to whoever holds the paper." Her hands were steady around the cup; her voice wasn't, quite. "In exchange, safe passage. Warmth. A life. She'd have died otherwise, Gideon. You'd both have died. I've told myself that every night for thirty-one years, and some nights it even helps."

"And she said yes." His own voice sounded like someone else's. Someone reasonable. Someone standing slightly outside his body taking notes.

"She wept for a day first. Then she said yes. Then she made me promise that when the debt came due, I'd get you out of the valley before they arrived. That was the one clause she bargained for — not her own safety. Yours. She wanted you to have time. Time to live somewhere they couldn't reach." Maren's mouth twisted. "So I spent seventeen years teaching you everything I knew, and then I spent one very good week making sure you'd hate me enough to leave."

Gideon sat with that. Outside, the summer snow ticked against the window like fingernails.

"The ledger," he said at last. "The date. Tonight. Why now? Thirty-one years — why collect now?"

"Because the king is dying." Maren said it the way you'd report a change in the weather, but her knuckles had gone white around the cup. "The court has been hungry for decades — you'll have seen the signs, even running contraband. Villages losing whole years. Old men forgetting their children's names. The crown under the mountain feeds on mortal memory, and the wells are running dry. They've been calling in old paper. Every debt, every bargain, everything anyone ever signed." She finally drank, grimacing at whatever cold dregs remained. "Yours is simply the oldest uncollected debt on their books. The founding stock, you might say."

"My mother sold me before I was born," Gideon said slowly, testing each word like a rotten floorboard, "and you helped her do it, and then you spent seventeen years raising me as cover, and then you threw me out to save my life, and you let me spend seven years thinking our whole history was about whether my charm-work was *adequate*."

"Yes," said Maren.

"That is the single most hedge-witch thing I have ever heard."

Something cracked in her face then — not quite a smile, not quite the opposite. "You may shout at me now. I've had thirty-one years to prepare for it. I imagine you'll need at least ten minutes."

He didn't shout. That surprised them both. What he did was stand up and walk to the window and watch the impossible snow fall, and feel his entire life quietly rearrange itself around a new center of gravity.

Every restless year. Every night he'd woken in some waystation attic with the absolute conviction that he'd forgotten something vital, some appointment, some obligation — he'd blamed the roads, the drink, the company he kept. Every time a stranger had looked at him a beat too long, or a priest had flinched from his handshake, or a fortune-teller in Vell's Crossing had taken one look at his palm, gone gray, and refunded his coin unprompted. He had built a self out of those splinters: *Gideon Blackwood, man with no roots, no home, nothing owed and nothing owing.* Free.

And the freedom itself had been the cage. Bought and paid for before he drew breath. Even his running had been scheduled.

"I'm not angry at her," he heard himself say, and found it was true, which was somehow worse. "Gods help me, Maren, I'm not even angry at you. I'm angry at—" he gestured at the window, at the mountain, at the whole patient architecture of the world— "*the paperwork.*"

"That's healthy," Maren offered.

"It's not. It's the least healthy thing I've ever felt. Anger at paperwork can't be punched."

"No," she agreed. "It generally can't."

* * *

It was full dark when he saw them coming up the lane.

Three shapes, pale as the snow that shouldn't exist, drifting between the pines without bending a single branch. They moved the way smoke moves when it's being polite about it. And though they made no sound, Gideon felt them the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — a soft, total attention settling over the cottage, like a hand closing around a candle flame.

"Maren," he said. "Company."

She was already moving, snuffing candles with her fingers, pulling a cloth off a bundle he'd assumed was ordinary laundry. Underneath: salt, iron filings, a ledger of her own, dog-eared and swollen with damp.

"How much time?"

"Minutes. Maybe less." She pressed the bundle into his arms. "Back door. There's a smuggler's cut behind the woodshed — you know it, you used it at fourteen to sneak to the fair, don't pretend you didn't, I counted the missing pies."

"Maren—"

"There is a girl," she said, fast and low, gripping his wrist with a strength that belied her size. "The ledger-keeper. If she showed you your name, then she's real, and real prophecy-keepers are rarer than honest tax collectors. Find her. Whatever she knows, it's more than I do, and I know enough to be terrified, which is saying something." She pushed him toward the back of the cottage. "Go."

"Not without—"

"Gideon." For one moment the old master looked out of her face, the woman who'd taught him to read sigils and skin rabbits and lie convincingly to customs men, and her eyes were wet river stones no longer. "Thirty-one years ago I helped sell your soul because a frightened girl asked me to and I thought the price was worth paying. Tonight I am buying back what I can. Let me. It's the only charm I've got left that works."

Outside, the front gate creaked — though no wind blew — and a voice called out, courteous and clear and utterly wrong, with a warmth in it like a hearth in a house where nobody has ever lived:

"Hedge-witch. We know the debtor is within. Open, and this remains a matter of accounting rather than... surgery."

Maren squared her shoulders, tucked her chin, and turned toward the front door with the expression of a woman about to be extremely rude to something ancient.

"Through the back," she whispered. "Run, boy. And Gideon — whatever they tell you down there, whatever they show you — *you are not the sum of what was signed over you.* Remember that. It's the one lesson I couldn't teach you until now."

The knock came. The door did not wait to be opened.

Gideon ran into the dark with salt in one fist and thirty-one years of lies burning in his chest — and behind him, through the walls, he heard his master greet the collectors with impeccable, weaponized courtesy:

"Good evening. You're early. Do come in — mind the cat."
