## 3. The Collectors

The knock came at the wrong hour — the small black hour past midnight when even wolves have the decency to sleep — and it was polite.

That was the first wrong thing about it. Three knocks, evenly spaced, soft as a knuckle on a grandmother's door. Nobody knocked like that in the Thornfell passes. Smugglers scratched twice and waited. Tax collectors didn't knock at all. And the pale figures that had been following Gideon down the mountain since dusk — the ones he'd been pretending were shadows and then pretending were weather — would not have knocked. They'd have simply been inside already, the way cold gets inside.

Maren set down her mortar. She had been grinding something bitter-smelling since they'd arrived an hour ago, her hands steady, her face doing the thing old hedge-witches' faces did: absolutely nothing.

"Sit by the fire," she said. "Don't answer it."

"I wasn't going to answer it," Gideon whispered. "I'm a professional."

"You're a boy who runs charms over frozen passes and calls it a profession." But there was no bite in it. Her eyes stayed on the door.

The second knock came. Same three beats. Same terrible courtesy.

And then a voice, muffled through the oak, warm as mulled wine: "Mistress Maren of the hedgerow. We know you're awake. Forgive the hour. We are not ourselves at night — none of us — but we couldn't wait until morning, and morning, I'm afraid, is rather beside the point now."

Gideon's skin did something he didn't authorize. It crawled.

He knew voices. Twenty years of smuggler camps and back-alley markets had given him an ear for them — who was lying, who was desperate, who was dangerous. This voice was kind. That was what made his palms sweat. It was kind the way a snare is smooth.

"Maren," he breathed. "What is that?"

She didn't answer him. She crossed to the hearth instead, took down the iron poker, and held it loosely, the way you hold something you don't believe will help but feel foolish without. "Come in, then," she said to the door, loud enough. "You always do."

The door opened without being opened. Gideon never saw a hand touch it. It simply swung inward on its hinges with a creak of complaint, and the night leaned in — and with it, three figures.

They looked almost right. That was the horror of them. They wore faces the way Gideon wore his coat: correctly, warmly, but you could tell it wasn't grown there. The one in front had the face of a farmer Gideon half-remembered from the valley villages, ruddy and broad-nosed, except the smile sat too high on the cheeks, pinned like a brooch. Its joints bent in the wrong order when it stepped over the threshold — knee first, then ankle, a suggestion of a joint where no joint should be — so that its walking looked like a memory of walking performed by someone who had only ever had it described.

"Thank you," it said, inclining its head to Maren with genuine grace. "You've kept the cottage lovely. Rowan wood. You still favor rowan." It said this the way a man compliments a hostess on flowers, and Gideon understood, with a lurch, that it wasn't a compliment. It was inventory.

The other two stood behind it in the doorway, letting the snow swirl around their borrowed shoulders without melting. One wore the face of a young woman, pretty and forgettable. The other wore the face of a child of perhaps ten, and Gideon found he could not look at that one directly, the way you can't look at the sun or your own worst thought.

"We come regarding an account," the first one said. It produced, from nowhere sensible, a ledger of its own — slim, bound in something pale, and Gideon's stomach dropped because he knew that binding. He'd carried enough contraband grimoires to know calfskin from vellum from *other*. "An old account. Long dormant. Tonight it wakes."

"His name is Gideon Blackwood," Maren said flatly. "If that's what you've come for, you've wasted a walk. There's nothing owed on him. His mother's debt died with—"

"Debts don't die, mistress." The courtier smiled its pinned smile. "That is rather the whole architecture of the thing. Debts outlive the debtor, the creditor, the currency, and occasionally the civilization. Your hedge-craft teaches you herbs die back in winter and return in spring. Ours teaches the same of obligations." It turned its head toward Gideon — all the way around, a few degrees past comfortable, though the face stayed pointed at Maren a moment longer, which was somehow worse. "But we are being rude. Introductions. We are called Collectors tonight. Other nights, other names. We find it polite to match the occasion."

Gideon discovered he was standing. He had no memory of standing. His legs had made a decision without consulting him, which, thinking about it later, he would realize was the whole theme of his life.

"Whatever she owes," he said, "or whoever owed it — I'll hear it. Not her."

"Oh, *there* he is." The courtier's delight seemed real, which frightened Gideon more than anything else in the room. "The runaway. The pass-runner. Do you know, we have watched you for years? Every charm you carried over Thornfell, every toll you dodged, every winter you survived by wit and stubbornness — we kept the books current. You are a fascinating entry, Gideon Blackwood. Most souls in our keeping sit quietly in lamps and dream. Yours has been *out* all this time. Running about. Spending itself." It sighed with something like affection. "Reckless. Wonderful. Terrible for the interest."

"The interest," Gideon repeated.

"Compound," said the courtier apologetically.

* * *

It went, in the end, the way these things apparently always go: gently, which is to say unforgivably.

The courtier explained while its companions arranged themselves around the cottage with unhurried grace, touching things — the drying herbs, the jars of tincture, the chipped blue cup Maren had drunk from every morning of Gideon's apprenticeship — and each touched thing seemed to become slightly more theirs and slightly less hers, though nothing visibly changed.

The account, it said, was opened nineteen years before Gideon's birth. A woman named Liss Blackwood, fleeing something it did not specify and Gideon could not ask about because his throat had closed, came to the Hollow Court beneath Thornfell and asked for passage out of the mountain's reach. Passage was granted. Passage always has a price. She had no gold worth having and no years worth taking — she was young and poor and pregnant — so she offered the only unencumbered asset she possessed.

"She offered you," the courtier said, not unkindly. "Unborn, unbaptized, unwritten. A blank soul is rare, you understand. Most arrive in the world already promised — to gods, to families, to grief. Yours was clean. Clean souls appreciate beautifully."

"You're saying my mother sold me before I was born," Gideon said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Inside, something enormous and quiet was collapsing, the way snow loads collapse off a roof — no drama, just a sudden absence where weight used to be.

"We are saying the transaction occurred. What she felt, what she feared, what she told herself on the road south — those pages are not ours to read." The courtier closed its pale ledger. "What is ours is the account. And accounts, mistress Maren, accrue. Nineteen years of dormancy. The principal comes due at moonrise tonight — the soul of Gideon Blackwood, delivered below. The interest, however—" it spread its hands, and its fingers had too many knuckles, "—the interest we may collect now."

"No," Maren said.

"Mistress—"

"*No.*" She planted herself between the courtier and Gideon, poker raised, and Gideon felt a fierce, useless love for her that nearly buckled his knees. Twenty years since he'd run from her cot in the middle of the night with her best grimoire under his arm and a note that said *sorry, don't follow*, and here she was, sixty-some years old, facing down a thing wearing a dead farmer's face with nothing but iron and stubbornness. "Take mine instead. I'm old. My memories are mostly weather and regret. Take the lot."

"That is generous," the courtier said, "and noted, and accepted."

"What?" Gideon said. "No — wait —"

"Interest must be paid in kind," the courtier said, turning to Maren with its pinned smile, and Gideon saw too late that he'd been managed, that his offer had been the opening the thing had waited for, that courtesy was just teeth wearing gloves. "Yours are freely offered. Freely given payment carries no taint. His would have to be taken, and taken things sour the books." It tilted its head. "You taught him hedge-craft, mistress. Surely you know: the willing gift outweighs the stolen prize. In our ledgers, yours is worth ten of his."

"Don't listen to it," Gideon said. "Maren. *Maren.*"

But she was already looking at him, and her face had finally stopped doing nothing. It was doing everything at once — apology, defiance, and underneath both, a tenderness so plain it embarrassed him to witness it.

"You ran," she said. "Best thing you ever did. Worst manners I ever suffered." Her mouth twitched. "I kept your cot made. Foolish habit. Don't you dare feel guilty about it, guilt attracts collectors."

"Maren—"

"Look at me, boy. Not at them. At me." Her eyes were brown and sharp and exactly as he remembered them from the mornings she'd thumped his head over his cipher-work. "Whatever happens next — whatever they say you are, whatever the book says, whatever you find down there — you are not a *debt*. You hear me? Debts are what people owe. You are what you *do*. Now stand still and let an old woman settle up."

The courtier raised one too-knuckled hand and touched her forehead, feather-light, almost tender.

And Maren's memories left her.

There was no light show. No scream. She simply became quieter, from the outside in — her grip on the poker loosened, her shoulders settled, the fierce bright thing behind her eyes dimmed like a lamp turned low to save oil. When the courtier withdrew its hand, she blinked at the room with mild, pleasant confusion, the way you wake in an unfamiliar bed.

"Oh," she said softly. "Hello. Are you the physician? I feel as though I've forgotten something. Something important." She frowned, groping. "There was a boy. Wasn't there a boy?"

"There was a boy," the courtier agreed gravely. "He sends his regards."

"He sounds like a nice boy." She smiled at the empty air where Gideon stood, and looked straight through him, and Gideon learned in that moment that there are worse things than death, and one of them is being forgotten kindly. "I hope he writes."

* * *

They marked him after that.

The child-faced one did it — approached Gideon with that small borrowed face tilted up at him, and pressed two fingers to the back of his left hand. Cold went in. Not the cold of snow; the cold of a cellar, of a locked drawer, of somewhere things are *kept*. When the fingers withdrew, a sigil sat in his skin like frost-flower under glass: fine silver lines, a circle broken by a descending line, delicate as embroidery.

"Moonrise," the courtier said. "We will come for you wherever you stand. There is no door in Thornfell we cannot open, no distance we cannot cross, no bargain that voids a signed instrument. These are not threats, Master Blackwood. They are merely the terms, restated for clarity. We find most distress comes from ambiguity." It gathered its companions with a gesture. At the door it paused, courteous to the last. "For what it is worth — and it is worth little — we hope you run. The ones who run make such better reading."

Then the night folded around them and they were gone, and the door swung shut on its own, and the cottage was very quiet except for the fire and the sound of Gideon breathing like a man whose lungs had just been repossessed.

Maren was humming by the hearth. Some old tune. She patted the chair across from her, hospitable, vacant. "Sit, dear. You look half-frozen. Can I get you tea? I have tea. Or... soup?" She peered into her own pantry like a stranger. "I appear to have soup."

Gideon sat because his legs quit again. He put his head in his hands and laughed, once, horribly, because the alternative was making a sound he'd never be able to stop making.

"Soup would be great," he said, because what else was there. "Thanks."

* * *

The window banged open twenty minutes later, letting in a whirl of snow and a girl.

She came through it like she owned it — boots first, ledger clutched to her chest, hair full of flakes, entirely ignoring the perfectly good door. She landed, straightened, took stock of the room in one sweep, and fixed on Gideon with eyes the color of wet slate.

"Gideon Blackwood," she said. "Good. You're still marked and not yet collected. We have maybe two hours. Less if the moon clears the ridge early."

Gideon stared at her. She was perhaps his age, wiry, wind-scoured, dressed in layer upon practical layer, and she looked at him with the particular expression of someone who has rehearsed a meeting her entire life and is furious that it's going badly.

"Do I know you?" he said.

"No. Yes. It's complicated. I know *of* you. Extensively." She held up the book under her arm — old, cracked, swollen with centuries of marginalia. "This is the Ledger of Thornfell. It has been kept by my order for four hundred years. It names dates, debts, and doors. Last night it named tonight, and it named you." She flipped it open to a ribboned page and thrust it at him. "Read it."

He read it. The script was cramped and ancient, but the date leapt out at him in fresh ink that should have dried a century ago: *this night*. Beside it, in the same impossible freshness: *the Blackwood debt comes home. The sold son descends or is dragged.*

"Charming," Gideon said. "It rhymes."

"It doesn't rhyme."

"It rhymes in my heart." He pushed the book back at her. "Look — whoever you are — I'm sorry, genuinely, this clearly matters enormously to you, but I have a plan, and the plan is *run*. South, over the Sere Pass, down into the lowlands where whatever those things are, they aren't. By dawn I'll be three valleys gone and this will be a very strange story I tell in taverns."

"There is no south," the girl said flatly. "The Collectors told you the truth about one thing: there is no door they cannot open. I have spent thirty years — my whole life — studying what they are. There is exactly one exception. One door in all of Thornfell older than the Court itself, predating the mountain's bargain, sealed by a law even they must honor." She tapped the ledger. "And it only opens for a sold soul. Willingly. From the outside."

Gideon looked at her. Then at Maren, who was humming and offering the girl soup. Then at the sigil on his hand, faintly glowing now, like frost catching lamplight — a clock ticking in his skin.

"Let me guess," he said. "The door wants *me*."

"The door has been waiting for you since before you were born," the girl said. Then, softer, with the first crack in her certainty: "So have I."

Outside, the clouds began, grudgingly, to tear. Moonlight spilled down the white slopes like something spilled on purpose.

Gideon sighed the sigh of a man whose life had never once belonged to him and who was, at long last, about to do something about it.

"All right," he said. "Where's this door?"

"Under the falls," said the girl. "And we need to leave *now*."
