## 4. Wren of the Ledger

The tunnels beneath the Thornfell passes smelled of lamp oil, wet stone, and forty years of men who had never once washed their coats.

Wren knew this because she had memorized the tunnels before she ever walked them. She had memorized everything — every junction, every deadfall, every smuggler's chalk mark and what it meant when turned upside down. The ledger had taught her that preparation was a form of prayer. You could not know the future, but you could know the ground it would happen on.

She had not prepared for Gideon Blackwood to be like *this*.

"Stop," she said, for the third time in an hour.

He stopped, which was something. He also sighed, which was not. "Let me guess. The ceiling."

"The ceiling," Wren confirmed, pointing up at the hairline crack running along the left-hand wall where the old timbering had shifted. "It's holding. It's been holding since before my grandmother was born. But if you walk under it dragging your pack like a man clearing a path through a wheat field, you'll bring down enough rubble to bury us both, and I will be extremely annoyed, because I have spent twenty-two years getting to this point and I refuse to die under a rockslide caused by *carelessness*."

Gideon looked at the ceiling. He looked at his pack. He looked at her with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he resented.

"You've been counting," he said. "How many times I've nearly killed us."

"Eleven."

"Eleven."

"Twelve, if we count the way you held the lantern."

"I held the lantern *normally*."

"You held it like a man who has never once considered that light can be seen from a distance." Wren took the lantern from him — gently, she told herself, gently, though her fingers may have been somewhat less than gentle — and hung it from the hook some long-dead smuggler had driven into the seam of the stone. "There. Now it lights the tunnel instead of advertising us to whatever is still following us."

"Nothing's following us." He said it too quickly, and then, hearing himself say it too quickly, added: "The courtiers marked me at moonrise. They don't chase. They said so. Very politely."

"They're polite right up until they aren't." Wren shouldered her own satchel and started down the tunnel at a pace calculated to be fast enough to make progress and slow enough to let him keep up without admitting she was waiting for him. "My order kept records of collectors going back four hundred years. Politeness was their signature. They'd knock. They'd apologize. One account describes a courtier helping an old woman finish her mending before taking her memories, because it seemed unkind to interrupt."

"And then?"

"And then it took sixty years of her life and left her thinking she was nineteen." Wren ducked under a low beam. "Politeness isn't mercy, Gideon. It's just good manners about appetite."

Behind her, silence. Then footsteps, careful now, avoiding the cracked section of ceiling without being told. Wren felt something small and warm kindle in her chest and sternly extinguished it. Trust was a debt, and she had learned young that debts were how everything went wrong.

* * *

They made camp in a side gallery where the smugglers had once stored brandy — she could still smell it in the wood of the racks, ghost-sweet — and Wren built a fire the size of two cupped hands, because heat drew attention and attention drew teeth.

Gideon sat across from it, turning something over in his fingers. A charm, she realized. One of the contraband ones he'd been running over the passes. A luck-knot, cheaply made, the kind farmwives bought to hang over doorways.

"You were good at this," she said, before she could stop herself. "The smuggling. Maren's cottage — you read the wards on her door faster than I did, and I've studied ward-craft since I was six."

"I was fast because I was scared." He said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "You get quick when every stranger might be a collector and every locked box might be yours." He tossed the luck-knot into the fire, where it flared green and died. "That's the whole trick of me, prophecy-keeper. Fear and fast hands. Your book picked a real hero."

"The book didn't pick anyone." Wren pulled the ledger out of its oilcloth wrapping and set it on her knees. In the firelight the cover looked like ordinary calfskin, scuffed and water-stained, worth perhaps three coppers to a dealer in old books. "It recorded. There's a difference. A river doesn't choose the shape of its valley."

"That's a very comforting thing to hear from someone who's spent her whole life preparing me for a death I didn't agree to."

"I never said—"

"Chapter nine," Gideon said. "Or eleven. Whichever one you went white reading and then hid from me by walking very fast and talking about drainage."

Wren closed her mouth. Opened it again. Closed it.

He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time since the chapel he looked at her properly — not as the strange woman with the book, but as a person, which was frankly more unnerving. "I'm not stupid, Wren. I've been running contraband through these mountains for six years. I know what it looks like when someone's carrying more than they've declared." He nodded at the ledger. "So declare it."

The fire popped. Somewhere far above, wind moved over the pass with a sound like a great slow breath.

"My name isn't Wren of anywhere," she said finally. "I mean — it is now. But I was born in the Cloister of the Turning Page, in the high valley past Larkspur Ford. Have you heard of it?"

"No."

"Nobody has. That was rather the point." She traced the edge of the ledger's cover with one finger. "We were prophecy-keepers. Not fortune-tellers — we didn't read palms or cast bones, and if you ask me whether you'll find love by spring I will hit you with this book. We kept records. The founding sisters learned a cipher that let them write down things before they happened. Not everything. Not everyone. Just... knots in the thread. Moments where the world narrows down to a single road."

"And I'm a knot."

"You're the knot." She opened the ledger to the page she had known by heart since she was nine years old and had first deciphered it, crouched in the cloister scriptorium with cold fingers and a candle stub, feeling the whole shape of her life settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. She turned it toward him. "Read your own name. Read the date."

He read. His jaw tightened. "Tonight. That's tonight."

"Yes."

"And this word after it. *Hollow.* That's—"

"The Court. Yes." Wren watched his face do several things at once, none of them easy. "The entry was written a hundred and twelve years ago by Sister Ondile, the last of the founders. It names a soul sold before birth, reclaimed at midwinter, and — there's more, but the rest is in cipher layers I haven't finished breaking. What I can tell you is that the entry doesn't end where you think it ends. There are conditions. Choices. The sisters were very clear that prophecy isn't a sentence, it's a *map*, and maps show more than one road."

"Then show me the other roads."

"I'm working on it." It came out sharper than she intended, and she made herself soften it. "I'm working on it. The cipher nests. Each layer needs a key, and the keys are... expensive. Memories, mostly. Freely given. The sisters believed truth shouldn't come cheap."

Gideon stared into the little fire for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet in a different way. "You said *we*. The prophecy-keepers. And then you said *last*. Back at the chapel you said you were the last."

Wren's hands went still on the ledger.

"There were forty-one of us when I was born," she said. "Forty-one keepers, and the cloister, and the library, and the bees Sister Anthea kept, and the bell that rang for study hours. I was the youngest by fifteen years. They called me the little margin, because I lived in the edges of everyone else's pages." She smiled, and was distantly surprised to find it didn't hurt as much as usual. Maybe telling it to a stranger helped. Maybe telling it to *this* stranger did. "The collectors came in autumn, eight years ago. Not for prophecies — the Court doesn't fear the future, it fears being *remembered*. They took the sisters' memories first, politely, one cottage at a time, and then they burned the library, and then—" She stopped. Started again. "Sister Ondile put the ledger in my arms and told me to run, and gave me one instruction. *Find the name. Walk with the name. Whatever he says, whatever he does, do not let him face the Hollow Court alone.*"

Gideon was silent for a while.

"So you found me," he said. "Eight years later. In a ruined chapel. By luck?"

"By record. The ledger notes where the named one tends to shelter in bad weather — the sisters tracked you longer than you'd find comfortable. You've slept in that chapel eleven times in six years. You leave offerings at the broken shrine even though you claim not to believe in anything. You always take the eastern pass in winter because the western one reminds you of somewhere you used to live." She hesitated. "And you whistle when you think no one's listening. Badly. The same four notes."

His ears went red. "That's surveillance."

"That's *preparation*." Wren heard her own voice rise and didn't quite manage to lower it. "Do you know what I did for eight years? I learned the tunnels. I learned the cipher. I learned which villages would hide me and which would sell me. I practiced conversations with you. *With you*, specifically — I'd stand in the cloister orchard and rehearse what I'd say when I finally found the man from the page, and in every version you were brave, or at least curious, or at *minimum* willing to listen for five minutes before calling it nonsense. Do you know what you actually did? You laughed. You looked at a hundred-year-old ledger with your own name in it and you *laughed* and asked if I was selling indulgences."

"I was having a bad week!"

"You were having a bad *decade*, I read all about it, that's not the point—" She caught herself, breathing hard, and pressed her lips together. The fire had shrunk to embers. In the dark of the brandy cellar, Gideon Blackwood was looking at her with an expression she couldn't parse, and she hated that she couldn't parse it, because she had rehearsed every possible conversation except the one where she lost her temper first.

"You rehearsed talking to me," he said slowly. "For eight years."

"In an orchard."

"With the bees?"

"The bees were good listeners."

Something happened to his face then — a crack, a twitch at the corner of his mouth, and then he was laughing, actually laughing, low and helpless and slightly hysterical, the laugh of a man whose week had contained soul-debt and memory-eating monsters and was apparently ending in a smuggler's cellar being yelled at about bees. Wren held out against it for approximately four seconds before the laugh caught her too, and then they were both sitting by the dying fire in the dark under the mountain, laughing at absolutely nothing that was funny, until it hurt.

"Oh, that's grim," Gideon wheezed at last, wiping his eyes. "That's genuinely grim. I'm sorry. About the laughing. At the chapel."

"You're not sorry."

"I'm a little sorry." He poked the embers back to life. "It's just — you spent eight years preparing for me, and I spent six years running from everything, and neither of us got what we ordered." He glanced sideways at her. "What were the rehearsals like? The imagined me. Was he better company?"

"He listened," Wren said. "Imagined-you was an excellent listener. Imagined-you also carried the lantern correctly."

"I'm going to hear about that lantern for the rest of my life, aren't I."

"Almost certainly."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had been fed and banked and fed again — Wren woke to the sound of Gideon talking in his sleep. Not words. Just a name, repeated, soft and cracked: *Maren. Maren.* The old woman's emptied face floated up behind Wren's eyes, the polite wrong-jointed things standing in her kitchen doorway, and she lay in the dark and felt the weight of the ledger beside her like a sleeping animal.

He hadn't asked yet what the final seal required. He would. He was quicker than he pretended.

And she still didn't know what she would say.

Near dawn, half-dreaming, she heard him stir, and heard him say — awake this time, quietly, to the ceiling or to her or to nobody:

"The eastern pass. You said it reminds me of somewhere I used to live." A pause. "It does. Don't put that in the book."

"It's already in the book," Wren murmured, and heard him huff, and felt — fragile as frost on a morning window, already half expecting to melt — the first small weight of trust settle between them.

Above them, far above, the snow kept falling out of season, and somewhere below them, something ancient began to count the hours until moonrise.
