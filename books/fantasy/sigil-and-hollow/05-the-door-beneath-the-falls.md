## 5. The Door Beneath the Falls

The smugglers' map Wren carried was drawn on skin.

Not human skin — she'd been very quick to clarify that, somewhere around the second hour of their flight through the tunnels, when Gideon had made a face at it. Goatskin, or something goat-adjacent. The distinction had not improved his appetite for the dried plums she'd offered him after, but he'd eaten them anyway, because a man running from wrong-jointed things in borrowed faces learns not to be particular about his meals.

"Left here," Wren said, holding the map up to the light of her hooded lantern. "Then down."

"That's not a direction."

"It's absolutely a direction." She tapped the parchment where a smudge of ink indicated what might have been stairs, or might have been a very ambitious crack. "Everything under Thornfell is left and then down. That's why nobody finds anything. They all insist on going right."

Gideon leaned against the tunnel wall, which was damp in a way that suggested it was thinking about becoming mud. Above them — impossibly far above, through rock and frost and one extremely startled badger they'd passed an hour ago — the pass lay buried under snow that had no business falling in late autumn. Snow that fell out of season meant collectors. Collectors meant moonrise. Moonrise meant him, presumably, wrapped up with a ribbon.

"How much farther?" he asked.

"To the door? Half a night's walk. To understanding the door?" Wren tucked the map away. "I've spent thirty years on that part. I'll let you know if I get there."

They walked. The tunnels were old smuggler roads, cut smooth in places by hands generations dead and rough in places where the mountain itself had opinions. Gideon knew these roads the way you know a scar — he'd run contraband charms over the passes for three years now, and the tunnels were where a man went when the passes had soldiers on them. He'd slept in this stretch once, back when he still bothered with names for the caves. The Hollowway. The Kettle. The Long Stair that wasn't long at all if you didn't stop to complain about it.

But he'd never gone this deep. There was a rule among smugglers: below the third stratum, don't. Not because of monsters, exactly. Because the air got strange, and men who slept too far down woke up homesick for places they'd never been.

He hadn't told Wren about the rule. He suspected she'd only point out that he was already homesick for a home that had sold him before he drew breath, so really, what was the harm.

* * *

The waterfall announced itself as a sound first — a low, grinding hush, like the mountain breathing through its teeth. Then the tunnel opened into a vaulted cavern, and there it was.

Frozen mid-fall. A whole river caught in the act of throwing itself off a cliff, every drop and curl of it turned to blue glass, so that the water seemed less stopped than *listening*, hanging in the air like a held breath. Lantern light moved across it and the ice answered in slow colors, green and violet, like something dreaming behind a window.

"Well," Gideon said. "That's showing off."

"The smugglers called it the Hush," Wren said quietly. "The ledgers say the water hasn't fallen in four hundred years. It froze the night the Hollow Court sealed itself in." She walked to the base of the fall and stood looking up, and the colored light moved over her face and made her look younger and older at once. "The door is behind it."

"Behind the ice?"

"Behind the mountain the ice is part of." She glanced back at him, and there was something careful in her expression, the way people are careful when they've rehearsed a sentence and hope you won't notice. "The smugglers' door isn't smuggler work, Gideon. It's older than the tunnels. Older than the mountain, maybe. The ledger says it was here when Thornfell was just a hill that hadn't decided to be dramatic yet."

"And it opens how?"

Wren didn't answer. She lifted the lantern instead and picked along the frozen pool at the fall's base, careful as a woman walking on eggs, until she reached the cliff face beside the cascade. There she crouched and brushed frost from the stone with her sleeve.

A sigil was carved there.

It wasn't large — smaller than his two hands spread together — but Gideon felt it before he properly saw it, the way you feel a draft from a door you can't find. Rings of interlocking lines, sharp as script and flowing as water, arranged around a hollow center. And in the center, a shape.

His stomach dropped through the floor of the world, which he was beginning to suspect was a thing his stomach did professionally now.

It was the mark from his palm. The one that had appeared three nights ago, faint as a bruise made of light, the night he'd read the date in the margin of Wren's ledger and laughed and laughed and then stopped laughing. The mark he'd told himself was ink. Then a burn. Then, briefly, in a moment of real optimism, a rash.

"That," he said. His voice came out flat and reasonable, which took effort. "That's my hand."

"It's your soul's shape," Wren said. "There's a difference. Come here and I'll show you."

"Wren."

"Gideon."

"I did not agree to be a key."

"No," she said gently, "you agreed to nothing at all. That's rather the entire problem we're here to fix."

He should have argued. Some part of him noted, distantly, that arguing was available, that a man could simply turn around and walk back up the Long Stair and out into the snow and take his chances with the collectors, and that this option remained open right up until it didn't. But his feet were already carrying him toward the cliff face, and he let them, because — and this was the thought that scared him more than any courtier — he wanted to see.

Wren held the lantern high. Up close the sigil resolved into finer detail: the rings weren't decorative. They were words. Thousands of them, spiraling inward, written in a hand so small and sure it looked grown rather than carved. He could read perhaps one word in twenty, fragments of an alphabet Maren had taught him in another life — *given, freely, kept, returned* —

And in the center, the hollow. A space shaped exactly like the mark on his palm, exactly like — he understood with a slow cold certainty — the shape of the thing that had been scooped out of him before he was born and filed away in a treasury beneath a dying king.

"It only opens for a sold soul," Wren said softly. "That's what the ledger says. The ones who built it wanted to make sure the door could never be used by the court. Never by the free. Only someone with a claim already against them could walk in and make a new one." She looked at him. "Only someone who has nothing left to lose except everything."

"That's the worst sentence anyone has ever said to me, and last week a man with a heron's face told me my bones smelled interesting."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference, and it's mostly that savoring comes with the full knowledge that what comes next will be terrible." He crouched beside her anyway. The sigil waited. Of course it waited. It had probably been waiting four hundred years; it could wait while a man had a small crisis.

Because here was the thing he couldn't say out loud, the thing sitting in his chest like a stone swallowed sideways:

Every choice he'd ever been proud of was suddenly suspect.

Running from Maren — he'd always told himself that was the day he became his own man, the day he spat on hedge-magic and struck out alone. But had he run because he chose to, or because something in him, some hollow place shaped like a debt, had been steering him all along? Taking up contraband work on the passes, keeping to the high roads, never staying anywhere longer than a season — he'd called it freedom. Now it looked like a leash with a great deal of slack. Even tonight. Even coming here. Had Wren found him, or had he simply arrived where the book said he would, on time, like a train?

"Your face is doing something complicated," Wren observed.

"I'm renegotiating my relationship with my own biography."

"Ah." She sat back on her heels. "That happened to me when I was nine. I found out the ledger named a keeper who would die, and I worked out it was me. I cried for a day, and then Aunt Sable made me dinner, and then I decided that if the story was going to use me, I'd at least do the reading myself." She shrugged. "It doesn't get better. But it gets *yours*, a little. Knowing is yours."

"That is genuinely the most comforting bleak thing I've ever heard."

"I practice."

Gideon looked at his hand. He turned it over slowly, palm up, and there it was in the lantern light — the sigil in miniature, faint as frost on a window, waiting with the same patience as its big sibling in the stone.

"If I put my hand on it," he said, "what happens?"

"The ledger says the door knows its own. It reads you. If you're the one it was made for—" She paused. "There's more, actually. There's a line about the reading. It shows the door's opinion of you. Most people who ever touched it, according to the record, saw their own lives laid out plain, every choice, every reason. The builders believed you shouldn't go into the dark without knowing what you're carrying."

"So it's an audit."

"A sacred audit."

"Wonderful. I'll bring receipts."

But he was stalling again, and worse, Wren knew it, and worse than that, some stubborn coal in him was tired of stalling. Three days ago he'd been a man with a route and a purse and a plan to winter in Vael's Crossing. Now he was a name in a dead woman's ledger, a debt come due, a key with legs. Everything he'd believed about himself had been taken apart and spread out on the ground like a smuggler's pack at a checkpoint.

Fine. Let them look. He'd survived checkpoints.

He pressed his palm to the stone.

* * *

The cold went through him like a bell being rung.

Not pain. Something stranger — a vast attention, patient and enormous, turning over in its sleep. The rings of writing lit one after another, word by word, spiraling inward, and with each ring Gideon felt something lift out of him and hold itself up to the light.

He saw his mother. Not her face — he'd never known it — but the shape of her leaving, the weight of her decision, the way fear walks in a body that has made a bargain it cannot take back. He saw Maren's kitchen, the smell of burnt rosemary and lamp oil, the argument that ended everything, and underneath the argument, so old he'd forgotten it was there, the true reason he'd flinched from her: because she had looked at him sometimes with guilt, and guilt looks like disappointment when you're young.

He saw the passes, every crossing, every winter he'd chosen the high road alone. He saw that he'd done it partly from pride and partly from habit and partly — this one stung — because some quiet voice had always whispered that roads leading down toward Thornfell were roads he shouldn't take. And he'd obeyed that whisper for years without ever once asking who was whispering.

*Even your rebellion was routed,* said the door's silence, which was not unkind. It simply stated things.

The last ring lit. The center hollow glowed.

And then, because apparently the door had a sense of theater, it showed him one thing more.

He saw himself standing where he stood now. He saw himself pull his hand back. He saw the tunnel close, the sigil go dark, the ledger end unfinished. He saw himself walking out of this story entirely — alive, uncollected, free in the way a coin is free when it rolls into a gutter. Nobody would blame him. Nobody would even know.

And the vision asked him, without words, in the language of held breath and falling water:

*Is that yours? Or is that theirs too? Is even your walking-away a road somebody else built?*

Gideon breathed out. His breath hung in the freezing air between him and the door.

"No," he said aloud. His voice cracked on it, and he let it. "If I leave now, it's because they wrote me as a coward, and I'm done performing other people's handwriting."

The mountain, if mountains can be satisfied, sounded satisfied.

Stone ground against stone. Behind the frozen waterfall, seams of light split the cliff face, and a section of rock the size of a barn door swung inward on hinges older than kingdoms, exhaling air that smelled of deep places and green things and, faintly, absurdly, of spring.

Wren stood beside him. When he looked at her, she was smiling — not triumphant, not relieved. Something quieter. Proud, maybe, in the way of someone who has watched a seed do exactly what the book said it would and is delighted anyway.

"You chose," she said.

"I chose." He flexed his fingers. His palm ached pleasantly, like a hand after good work. "First time in my life, possibly. I'm given to understand it's addictive."

"Terribly. There's no cure. People have been known to choose breakfast." She hoisted her lantern and looked into the dark doorway, and her smile settled into something steadier. "It goes down from here. Through the spring-stratum first — the ledger says the oldest layers remember warmth. Then summer. Then autumn. The court sits in the winter at the heart."

"Down through the seasons, backwards." Gideon peered past her into the opening, where the dark was total and patient and, he noticed with resignation, almost certainly full of teeth. Somewhere in the deep, something shifted — a sound like a great many small feet, or one enormous thing practicing being many.

"Wren," he said. "If I'd pulled my hand away. Would you have stopped me?"

She considered it honestly, head tilted, lantern light catching the frost in her lashes. "No. I'd have grieved the ledger and let you go. That's the whole point of you, Gideon. You're the first person in this story who gets to pick."

"Hm." He stepped up to the threshold, warm air rising past his face like a tide going out. "In that case — after you."

"Oh, absolutely not. You're the key. Keys go first."

"That's not how keys work."

"It's exactly how keys work. I've read extensively."

And arguing about it, warmly, stupidly, both of them refusing to cross first — that, he thought, stepping down into the dark with her laughter echoing off walls that hadn't heard any in four hundred years — *that* felt like something no one had written for him either.

Below them, far down in the dark, something heard the door open.

And began, unhurriedly, to climb.
