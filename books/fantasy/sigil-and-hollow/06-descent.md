## 6. Descent

The door beneath the falls closed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

Gideon stood in the dark and listened to the mountain settle around them — stone shifting against stone, water moving somewhere far below, and beneath it all a low hum he felt in his teeth more than heard. The sigil-mark on his palm had gone quiet since the door opened, but it wasn't asleep. It was *waiting*. He knew that feeling. It was how a trap waited.

"Congratulations," Wren said into the blackness. "You're the first person in three hundred years to open that door."

"I'd rather have been the second."

"The second person would have had to watch you do it first, so no. You're stuck with it." There was a rustle of cloth, a strike of flint, and then her lantern bloomed to life, throwing their shadows huge and wavering up the walls of a stairwell that spiraled down into the dark like the inside of a shell. "The ledger says the mountain runs backward. Older as you go deeper. Autumn first, then summer, then spring."

"That's not how seasons work."

"You're a smuggler who just opened a pre-human door with his hand. I'd stop lecturing the universe about how things work."

He couldn't argue with that, so he started walking instead.

* * *

The autumn stratum announced itself by smell before anything else — woodsmoke and rotting apples and wet leaves, all of it impossibly far underground. Then the lantern light caught the walls and Gideon stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into his back.

The cavern around them was a forest in miniature. Trees grew from the stone itself, their roots drinking from veins of glowing amber sap, their leaves a riot of red and gold and brown. And falling — actually falling, drifting down through air that shouldn't have had weather in it — were leaves. They landed on the path, on Gideon's shoulders, in Wren's hair, and where they touched the ground they sank into the stone like rain into soil.

"Don't eat anything," Wren said, in the tone of someone reading a warning aloud.

"I wasn't going to eat anything."

"You were looking at the apples."

"I was *noticing* the apples. Noticing is free." But he kept his hands in his pockets as they walked under branches heavy with fruit that glowed faintly, like embers wrapped in skin. "How deep does this go?"

"The ledger gives measurements." She patted the book where it rode against her chest in its oilcloth wrapping. "But the ledger also describes this level as 'a hall of gold trees,' which whoever wrote that clearly never saw actual gold, so I'm taking everything with a grain of salt."

"A prophecy-keeper who doubts her own book."

"A prophecy-keeper who's read the whole thing twice." Her voice did something complicated there — went light on top and heavy underneath — but before he could ask about it she pointed ahead. "Look."

The path descended through the autumn forest toward a wall of stone, and set into the wall was a doorway. Not built. *Grown* — two trees leaning together over an arch, their trunks fused, their branches woven into a lattice that had been trained, deliberately, patiently, into the shape of a gate. Someone had made this. Someone had wanted people to pass through it.

Above the arch, carved into the living wood in letters that wept slow beads of amber, was a line of script Gideon didn't recognize.

Wren did. She was already pulling out the ledger, cross-referencing, her lips moving. "It says: *All that falls is gathered. All that is gathered may rise.*"

"Cheerful."

"It's an instruction, not a comfort. Look at the letters — they're the same root-forms as the cipher in the margins. This is the teaching language. Everything down here that wants to be understood will be written in it." She looked up at him, and the lantern light turned her eyes the color of strong tea. "Which means you need to learn it. Tonight, ideally. Before something down here offers us a contract."

"Why me? You're the keeper."

"And you're the one whose hand opens doors." She said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "The ledger names you, Gideon. Whatever's written down here, some part of it is going to be about you. Better you can read it yourself than take my word for it."

He thought about arguing. He was good at arguing; it was most of what smuggling negotiations were. But she was right, and they both knew it, and there was a particular misery in being right at someone when you were both tired and the dark was full of glowing apples.

"Fine," he said. "Teach me."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the grown gate, because Wren insisted that thresholds were neutral ground — "the ledger's very clear, nothing hunts across a threshold, it's considered rude" — and because Gideon's legs had opinions about more stairs.

She taught the way she did everything: precisely, relentlessly, and with an underlying current of delight she probably didn't know was visible. The cipher, it turned out, wasn't a substitution code. It was older than that, a way of writing where each symbol was a small picture of what it meant — fall, gather, rise, debt, gift, hollow, crown — layered and rotated until the meanings braided together.

"It's a language for writing things that are true whether you read them forward or backward," she said, scratching symbols in the dust with a stick. "That's why the Court uses it. Contracts. Debts. Things that have to hold from both ends."

"Show me the one for debt again."

She drew it: a circle with a bite taken out of it, and inside the bite, a smaller shape like a flame. "Debt. Something owed, held by the thing that took the bite." Then she drew another symbol next to it, a flame standing alone. "And that's soul. Or lamp. Same word, down here. Which tells you something about how they think."

Gideon looked at the little flame in the dust for longer than he meant to. His soul was down here somewhere. A lamp in a treasury. He tried to feel something about that — terror, mostly, he supposed, or grief — but what he actually felt was a strange, stubborn curiosity, like a man staring at a locked box that had his name on it.

"All right," he said. "Your turn."

"Turn for what?"

"You've been carrying that book your whole life. You know languages and laws and how rude it is to hunt across a threshold. But you've never been underground, have you? Never slept rough. Never walked a road where the danger doesn't announce itself."

Wren sat back. "I've read extensively—"

"Reading isn't knowing." He got up, crossed to the cavern wall, and put his lantern close to a seam in the rock where the amber glow didn't reach. "See this? Dust smoothed at the bottom, scuff marks above. Something wide and low comes through here regularly. If we'd camped in front of that gap instead of beside it, we'd have found out what it is around the time it found us." He moved along the wall, touching things as he went, and she followed, watching. "Cracks that breathe are tunnels. Cracks that don't are just cracks. Water that flows silent is deep; water that gurgles is shallow enough to trust. And if you ever see a cave that's too tidy — bones stacked, floor swept — you don't go in. Tidy means somebody lives there, and anything that sweeps its floor down here wants something from you."

"That's grim."

"That's Thornfell passes. My master used to say—" He stopped. The sentence had come out before he could catch it, and now it lay between them, Maren-shaped.

Wren picked it up gently. "Your master taught you well."

"My master sold me." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. It had been two days since Maren confessed, and the truth still hadn't settled into a shape he could carry easily. "Or my mother did. Maren just... knew. And let me run around the mountains for ten years with a hole in me I couldn't name."

"She took you in anyway. After."

"After. Yes." He crouched and drew the flame-symbol in the dust again, then rubbed it out. "She was a bad teacher and a worse liar and she fed me every day for six years. I don't know what to do with any of that."

"You don't have to do anything with it yet," Wren said. "That's the trick nobody tells you. Feelings keep. They're the one thing down here that keeps without a contract."

He looked at her — this woman who had spent her whole life memorizing a stranger's death date, who quoted a book the way other people prayed, who had never once in her life slept rough until two days ago and was now sitting in a magical autumn forest teaching dead languages by lantern light — and felt the first thin thread of something that wasn't quite trust yet but was at least willing to be mistaken for it.

"Get some sleep," he said. "I'll take first watch. And Wren?"

"Mm?"

"When something wide and low comes through that gap tonight, wake me before it gets to the apples."

* * *

*(Wren)*

Nothing wide and low came through the gap, though something *sang* around the third hour of her watch — a sound like a wineglass mourning — and Gideon, woken by her elbow, listened with his eyes shut and pronounced it "rock settling, or a banshee, fifty-fifty," and went back to sleep, which she found either deeply reassuring or deeply infuriating and hadn't decided which by morning.

The morning, such as it was — the lantern-hours down here obeyed no sun — brought them to the gate, and through it, and down.

The summer stratum hit them like walking into an open oven door. Heat rolled up the stairwell, thick with the smell of cut grass and hot stone and something sweeter underneath, like honey left in the sun. The cavern opened into a vast chamber where the ceiling was lost in golden haze and wheat — actual wheat, tall and heavy-headed — grew in terraces spiraling down toward a lake of light that might have been water or might have been molten glass.

"It's harvest," Wren breathed. "Underground. In winter. Above us right now Thornfell is buried in snow and down here it's—"

"Older," Gideon said. He'd gone still in that way he did, she was learning — the stillness of a man reading a room for exits, except now he was reading a season. "Autumn above, summer below. Running backward. Every step down is a step earlier." He glanced at her sideways. "You realize what's at the bottom, then."

Spring. The beginning. Whatever the mountain had been before the Court had gotten its hands in it.

They crossed the summer country carefully. Gideon taught her the wheat was safe to touch but the light-lake was not ("anything that bright down here is either a mirror or a mouth"), and she taught him the harvest-symbols carved into the terrace walls — *plenty, portion, tithe* — and they argued cheerfully about which of them was more useful, a debate that remained unresolved and, she suspected, was designed to remain unresolved forever.

It was at the edge of the light-lake that she saw the first courtier of the descent.

It stood among the wheat at a distance, wearing the shape of a tall man in fine clothes, and it did not move, and it did not approach, and after a long moment it simply wasn't there anymore, the way a figure in the corner of your eye isn't there when you turn.

"They're watching us," she whispered.

"Course they are. We're two uninvited guests walking through their pantry." Gideon's voice was easy but his hand had found the knife at his belt, the one he'd told her was for opening crates and would definitely, definitely never be needed for anything else. "Keep walking. Don't run. Nothing chases what isn't afraid of it."

"Is that true?"

"No idea. But it sounds true, and down here sounding true is half the battle."

Despite herself — despite the heat and the watching shapes and the ledger weighing on her chest like a second heart — Wren laughed. Actually laughed, out loud, in the summer stratum of the Hollow Court, and saw Gideon grin in response, quick and crooked, and thought: *oh no.* Because the ledger had taught her everything about the road ahead except what it would be like to walk it with someone who made the dark feel navigable.

She buried the thought and kept walking.

* * *

*(Gideon)*

The spring stratum was the strangest yet, and Gideon had stopped being surprised by that somewhere around the singing wineglass hour.

Here the cavern was small and close and soft, walls furred with moss that glowed a gentle green, air cool and sweet as a cellar in April. Snowdrops grew in the crevices. Actual snowdrops. Somewhere nearby, water dripped in a rhythm so regular it was almost a heartbeat, and the hum he'd felt in his teeth since the door had changed pitch — higher now, younger, like the mountain itself was remembering being new.

"This is the oldest part," Wren said quietly, "and it feels like the youngest. That's the whole point, isn't it? They built their winter on top of a spring."

"Built a house on a grave, more like." But even he could hear there was no conviction in it. The place was doing something to him — loosening something. He crouched and touched the moss and it was warm, faintly, like a sleeping animal, and the sigil on his palm pulsed once in answer, and for a moment he felt the whole mountain around him the way you feel a crowd in a dark room: present, breathing, waiting.

Then the moss-light flickered, and down the passage ahead came a sound he knew from a hundred bad nights on the passes — the click of claws on stone, deliberate, unhurried.

He moved before thinking, one arm out to push Wren behind him, the other drawing the crate-knife. "Tidy rules," he murmured. "What do you see?"

"Nothing. The light won't reach that far."

"Then we don't wait for it to." He backed them both toward a side crevice he'd already marked — crack that breathes, therefore tunnel — and they slipped into it just as something long and pale flowed past the passage mouth, all wrong joints and borrowed courtesy, its head turning slowly, slowly, like a man reading a room.

It passed. It paused. It passed on.

In the breathing dark of the side tunnel, pressed shoulder to shoulder, Wren exhaled against his ear. "You said nothing hunts across a threshold."

"I said *nothing* says that. I never believed it."

Ahead of them, down the tunnel, the darkness gave way to a distant glow — not green, not gold, but the cold silver of lamplight, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of many voices trading, haggling, laughing.

A market. Underground, in the heart of the mountain, a market.

Wren's hand found his sleeve. "The ledger calls this the next stratum," she whispered. "*The Market of Small Debts.* It says—" she stopped, and he heard her swallow. "It says everyone there traded something to get warm. And it says we'll find someone you know."

Gideon looked down at the sigil on his palm, then toward the silver light, and felt the last of his laughter go out like a lamp pinched between fingers.

"Then let's go find out who I'm not allowed to forget," he said, and stepped toward the light.
