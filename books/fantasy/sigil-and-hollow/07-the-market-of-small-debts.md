## 7. The Market of Small Debts

The tunnel opened without warning, and Gideon walked out of the dark into a market.

He stopped so abruptly that Wren nearly collided with his back. "What—"

"Shh." Wren peered past his shoulder. "I know. I remember the first time."

"You've *been* here?"

"Once. With my mother. To look. Only to look." Her voice had gone thin and careful, the way it did when she was holding something down. "Come on. If we stand gawking at the mouth of the passage, someone will gawk back."

Gideon gawked anyway, just for a moment longer, because he could not help it.

The cavern was vast — a cathedral hollowed out of the mountain's ribs, its ceiling lost somewhere above in a haze of pale light that came from no torch he could see. The glow drifted up from below, from veins of something luminous running through the stone floor like frost caught mid-melt, and by that strange cold light the whole space glittered: hundreds of stalls built of black wood and white bone-lattice, hung with lanterns of colored glass, strung with banners that rippled in an air current he couldn't feel on his skin.

And it was warm here. That was the first thing that made his skin crawl, once he noticed it. Above them, Thornfell wore winter like a second skin; the passes had been snow-choked since harvest. But down here the air sat soft and mild as late spring, and every mortal face in the crowd wore the loose, comfortable smile of someone who had forgotten what cold felt like.

Courtiers moved among the stalls. They were easier to bear at a distance, Gideon found — at this remove they almost looked human, tall figures in layered grays and whites, their faces smooth and pleasant until the light shifted and you saw the joints sit wrong, the elbows bending where elbows shouldn't, the smiles a half-inch too wide for the skulls beneath them. They drifted between the stalls like shopkeepers' assistants, courteous, attentive, patient as debt collectors on the first of the month.

Because that was what they were.

"Look," Wren murmured, steering him toward a stall with a striped awning. "Watch how it works. You'll want to understand before you spend anything."

"I'm not spending anything. I don't have anything worth—" He stopped. He had exactly one thing worth taking, down here. His hand went to his chest without asking his permission.

"I know," Wren said quietly. "Keep your palm closed and your name in your teeth. Come on."

The stall she'd chosen sold warmth itself — or rather, sold the memory of it. A courtier with a kind, forgettable face stood behind a counter of polished ice, and before it, a farmer in a moth-eaten coat was counting coins onto the surface. Not coins, Gideon realized as they drew closer. Coins would have been simpler. The man was counting out small grey pebbles, and each pebble, when it left his fingers, seemed to take something of him with it — his shoulders dropped a little, his hair looked a shade lighter, his eyes went duller by one careful degree.

"What are those?" Gideon whispered.

"Years," said Wren. "Watch."

The courtier swept the pebbles into a silk pouch with a gesture of genuine courtesy. "Seven years of summer," it said, and its voice was warm as bread. "A fair trade. Your knees will thank you."

The farmer laughed — actually laughed — and flexed his hands like a man relieved of a weight. "It's only sitting by the fire in July, is all," he said to no one, to everyone. "Only the smell of cut grass after rain. I won't miss it. Who misses that?"

*Who misses that,* Gideon thought, and felt sick.

They moved on. At the next stall, a young woman traded her name — not the sound of it, Gideon understood somehow, but the whole weight of it, everything the name meant, twenty-two years of being called *Liese* by people who loved her. She walked away from the stall smiling, unburdened, and when Wren gently asked her the way to the falls-gate, the woman looked at them both with polite blankness and said, "Oh, I couldn't say, I'm sure. Are we acquainted?"

At the next, an old soldier sold his first kiss. The courtier received it in cupped hands like a moth, and the soldier stood there afterward turning the absence over in his mind, frowning faintly, touching his own lips as though checking whether they still worked.

"They're happy," Wren said. It wasn't a defense; it was an observation, delivered flatly, because she knew Gideon needed it said plainly or he'd start shouting. "That's the worst part. Nobody's dragged here. The mountain keeps the winter above us, and down here there's warmth, and food, and music — listen."

He listened. Under the murmur of commerce there *was* music, a fiddler in a corner playing something lilting and sweet, and dancers, and children chasing each other between the stalls, laughing. Mortals living in the belly of the thing that owned them, trading themselves away piece by piece for the privilege of staying comfortable while the world above froze.

"It's a good bargain, if you never look at the ledger," Wren said. "Warmth now, in exchange for whatever you can spare. And nobody thinks they're sparing anything important. That's the trick of it. Nobody ever thinks the small things matter until they're gone."

"The courtiers feed on it," Gideon said. "Maren told me. Memory keeps them alive. Keeps the crown alive."

"Keeps everything down here alive." Wren nodded toward the luminous veins in the floor. "Every light in this cavern is paid for. Every warm breath. Someone's July. Someone's mother's voice saying their name." She pulled her ledger from her satchel and held it against her chest like a shield. "We should keep moving. The king's hall is beyond the far arch. We only came through the market because—"

"Because there's no other way," Gideon finished.

"No other way," she agreed.

* * *

It was Wren who spotted him first, though she didn't know why the big man by the pie-stall mattered.

"Gideon," she said. "That fellow keeps looking over. Does he know you?"

Gideon followed her gaze and forgot how to breathe.

Doss. It was Doss — broad as a barn door, ginger-bearded, with the crooked grin Gideon had spent three years sharing watch-fires and bad whiskey with. Doss, who'd taught him which passes the wardens watched and which they'd grown lazy about. Doss, who'd once carried him four miles down a scree slope with a busted ankle, complaining the entire way that Gideon weighed as much as a sack of wet turnips and sang worse than one. Doss, who owed him eleven crowns and had promised, laughing, to pay it back with interest the next time they met.

Doss was selling pies. Small ones, steaming, golden-crusted, and the line of customers in front of his stall was three deep, and he was calling out prices in a cheerful barker's voice, and none of it — not one flicker of it — touched Gideon as recognition. Doss looked straight at him twice and saw a stranger both times.

"Doss!" Gideon said, and his voice cracked on it, and he didn't care.

The big man turned. "Evenin', friend! Pie? Best in the deep dark, and I'll swear that on my gran's grave, wherever she is." The grin was the same grin. The eyes were not. There was nothing behind them looking for Gideon. Nothing at all.

"Do you—" Gideon gripped the edge of the stall. "Doss. Blackwater job. Two winters back. The pass under Raven Tor, the wardens' sled, you and me and Fen and—"

"Funny," Doss said pleasantly, "everyone says that sort of thing. Everyone's got a story about knowing old Doss." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Can't help ye, friend. Don't remember a one of 'em. Comes of selling, see." He tapped his temple cheerfully. "Sold a lot of years, me. Sold the names too — mine, mostly, and anyone attached to 'em. Can't keep hold of a man if you've sold the rope he was tied to you with." He winked. "Cheaper that way, some say. Lighter. I'm the lightest man in this whole market, ask anybody."

"How much?" Gideon heard himself ask. "How many years did you sell?"

Doss considered, genuinely counting, and Gideon watched him fail to arrive anywhere. "Couldn't say. Couldn't say what they were, neither. That's the beauty of it! You don't miss what you can't remember missing." He leaned across the counter, conspiratorial, warm. "Look, friend — you've got a hungry look, and not the pie kind. First time below? It shows. So take advice from a light man: don't count your goods too careful. The heavy things are the ones what drag you down."

"Doss." Gideon's hands were shaking. "Eleven crowns. You owe me eleven crowns."

"Ha! Wish I did, friend. Wish I remembered owing anyone anything — sounds like a right adventure." He pressed a pie into Gideon's hands, refusing payment with a wave. "On the house. For the story. I like a man with a story about me, even if I can't have it myself."

And he turned back to his next customer, still grinning, already gone.

Gideon stood there holding the pie like it might bite him. It smelled wonderful. That was the obscenity of it — it smelled *wonderful*, baked by a man who had sold himself hollow and didn't know what he'd been, didn't know who he'd carried down a scree slope, didn't know he owed anyone eleven crowns or anything else, because he had sold the owing along with everything else.

Wren touched his elbow. "Gideon."

"He doesn't know me." His voice came out wrong. "Three years. He carried me off that slope with his own back, and he doesn't know me, and he's *happy*. He's happy and light and he's selling pies in hell and humming."

"That's what they buy with, the warmth down here." Wren's voice was very quiet. "This is what the crown eats. Not souls whole — not at first. It eats the small debts first. The little pieces people can spare. And then one day a man looks at his oldest friend and sees a stranger, and he can't even be sad about it, because he sold the sadness too."

Gideon set the pie down on the counter. Very carefully, the way you put down something breakable. Then he turned and looked at the market — really looked, past the lanterns and the music and the dancing children, at the grey pebbles changing hands, the names vanishing, the kisses going into silk pouches, the courtiers gliding through it all with their courteous wrong-jointed patience, harvesting. The luminous veins in the floor pulsed faintly, fed, content.

Above them, snow fell on Thornfell. Out of season, endless, hungry. Down here, everyone was warm.

Everyone was warm because they were being eaten.

"Every soul in this mountain was sold," Gideon said. It wasn't a question anymore. It was arithmetic. "Every single one. And every lamp in the king's treasury — Wren, those aren't strangers. Some of them are people like Doss, who walked in the front door and started paying. And the ones in the treasury are people like *me* — sold before they could refuse."

"Yes," said Wren.

"And the crown keeps the winter falling, and the winter keeps them trading, and the trading keeps the crown fed." He could see the whole wheel of it now, turning, turning, greased with Julys and first kisses and mothers' voices. "It doesn't end. It isn't *meant* to end. It's meant to run forever, and everyone down here calls that survival."

Wren was watching him with an expression he hadn't seen on her before — something like hope wearing armor. "That's the first time you've sounded like the person in my ledger," she said. "The one who does something about it."

"Don't get sentimental. I still think your book is mad." But he said it absently, because he was looking at Doss again — at the great hollow cheerful shell of him, humming as he handed pies across the counter, and something in Gideon's chest had gone hard and bright as a coal.

Anger, he realized. Not the hot flaring kind that burned out on the road. This was the other kind, the slow kind, the kind that kept. The kind you could carry a long way down into a dark place and still have left when you arrived.

"Eleven crowns," he said softly. "Plus interest."

"Hm?" said Wren.

"Nothing." He squared his shoulders and turned toward the far arch, where the light changed and the king waited. "Let's go meet the man who owns my soul. I want to see his books."

Behind them, Doss called out cheerfully to the crowd, "Pies! Warm as July, friends! Warm as July!"

And Gideon walked on without looking back, carrying the cold with him on purpose, because someone in this mountain ought to.

At the far arch, flanked by two courtiers whose smiles did not reach any part of them that mattered, a herald in bone-white livery raised a silver horn to its lips — and spoke Gideon's name.

Not the name Gideon used. The older one. The true one.

The one his mother had sold.
