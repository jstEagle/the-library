## 8. The King Who Is Dying

The throne room had no ceiling. That was the first thing Gideon noticed, and the last thing he wanted to think about, because where a ceiling should have been there was only darkness going up and up, and snow falling through it — slow, silent, upside-down snow that rose from the floor instead of sinking from the sky.

"Don't stare at it," Wren murmured at his elbow. "The courtiers say it's rude."

"The courtiers can say whatever they like. They're the ones with the extra elbows."

"Lower your voice," she said, in the tone of someone who had spent her whole life preparing for this moment and was now watching it be handled by a man who'd once tried to sell her a luck charm made of painted acorns. "And stop fidgeting. You look like a smuggler."

"I am a smuggler."

"You're an audience. Act like one."

They walked the length of the hall together, boots ringing on stone that rang back a half-second too late, as though the floor were remembering each step after it happened. Courtiers lined the way — pale, courteous things in borrowed faces, bowing at angles joints shouldn't allow. One of them smiled at Gideon with Maren's neighbor's mouth, and he fixed his eyes firmly on the middle distance and pretended very hard to be somewhere else.

The throne itself was carved from ice, or something pretending badly to be ice, and upon it sat the Hollow King.

Gideon had expected — well. He wasn't sure what he'd expected. Something vast. Something with teeth. Instead the king looked *thin*. Translucent as old glass, you could see the dark of the throne through him, could see faint lights drifting inside his chest like lamps sunk in deep water. His crown was missing. Where it should have sat, his brow was bare and cracked like drought-earth, and he held himself with the careful stillness of a man who has learned that moving costs more than he can afford.

He looked, Gideon thought with a lurch of unwelcome pity, like someone's grandfather three days before the end, propped up for one last family dinner.

"You came." The king's voice was quiet. It carried anyway, the way cold does. "Most debtors run. I respect that, actually. Running shows sense."

"I ran for six years," Gideon said. "It got me here."

"Yes. Debts are like that." The king gestured — a small motion, costing him visibly — to a space before the throne. Two chairs waited there. Actual chairs, which somehow unsettled Gideon more than anything else so far. "Sit. This will take a while, and I have very little while left."

Wren sat. Gideon stayed standing out of pure stubbornness, then sat too because his knees were shaking and pride is no excuse for falling over in front of royalty.

"You know why you're here," the king said.

"My soul. My mother sold it before I was born. Tonight it comes due." Gideon heard his own voice go flat and hard. "Say it plainly. Everyone else has."

"It sits in my treasury," the king said. "A small lamp, burning low, among several thousand others. I could show it to you. I choose not to, because you would try to touch it, and the wards would peel the skin from your hands, and then we would both be embarrassed."

"That's comforting."

"Nothing about me is comforting. I am a dying king presiding over a dying season, and I called you here not to collect you, boy, but to warn you." He leaned forward, and the lights inside his chest drifted closer together, dimming. "Your soul is in danger. Not from me. From my chancellor."

"Vesperine," Wren breathed.

"Vesperine." The king pronounced the name the way other men spit. "My courtiers feed on mortal memory — years, names, first kisses, the smell of bread — small debts, small meals. But a whole soul, unclaimed and ripe? A soul like yours, freshly owed, never nibbled?" His translucent hand closed on the arm of the throne. "That is not a meal to them. That is a feast. And Vesperine intends to serve it at midwinter, to the hungriest of them, and let the long winter finish falling over Thornfell while they gorge themselves and forget there was ever anything above the snow."

Gideon found his voice had gone somewhere without him. "You're telling me my own soul is bait."

"I'm telling you that you are a candle in a room full of starving moths, and the moth who organized the moths has plans." The king almost smiled. It was a terrible thing, that almost. "I am offering you a bargain."

"Of course you are. Nothing down here is free. Believe me, I've checked the prices twice."

"Good. A suspicious debtor lives longer." The king straightened, and for one moment the light in his chest flared, and Gideon saw how vast he must once have been — a mountain wearing a man like a coat. "Bring me the crown-sigil. Vesperine stole it from my brow while I slept, ninety years ago. Without it, I cannot hold the winter. With it returned, I can keep Thornfell's world turning — spring above, thaw, rivers running. And you—" the hollow eyes fixed on him— "go free. Your lamp walks out of my treasury and into your chest, and the debt is burned, and no courtier may touch you again. Sworn on the mountain itself."

Silence, except for the rising snow.

"And if I refuse?" Gideon asked.

"Then at moonrise on midwinter night, Vesperine feeds your soul to her favorites, the winter falls forever, and everything you've ever done — every charm smuggled over the passes, every corner cut, every narrow escape — ends up meaning nothing at all." The king settled back. "I don't offer this bargain because I'm kind. I offer it because I'm desperate. Desperate kings make honest offers; we haven't the strength left to lie elaborately."

"He could be lying anyway," Gideon said to Wren, quietly.

"He could," Wren agreed. She had the ledger open across her knees and was reading it with the fierce concentration of a woman checking arithmetic. "But the ledger says the same thing. Chapter of the Crown, verse nine: *the thief returns what was taken, and the debtor walks out by the door he came in.*"

"The ledger also says somebody dies opening a seal," Gideon muttered. "I've decided to treat the book as a rumor with good posture."

Wren elbowed him. The king watched them both with an expression Gideon couldn't read — something old and tired and, absurdly, fond.

"There is one more thing," the king said. "Vesperine will come to you. Before the feast. She always talks first. She'll offer you everything you ever wanted, and she'll be sincere, which is the worst part. Refuse nothing outright — refusal delights her. Simply... remember which of us told you the truth about your mother."

Gideon went still. "What do you know about my mother?"

"Only what the debt records." For a heartbeat the king looked, impossibly, sorry. "She paid a great price, that woman. Larger than she understood. Larger than anyone understood." He waved one glassy hand toward the far doors. "Go. Rest. The feast summons will find you whether you want finding or not. And boy—" 

Gideon paused at the foot of the throne.

"—when you meet her, count her fingers. The chancellor keeps her hands hidden. There's a reason."

* * *

They were shown to a guest chamber that was either generous hospitality or a beautifully appointed trap, and Gideon genuinely could not tell the difference, which he suspected was the point. The bed was soft. The fire burned warm without any wood. The window showed the rising snow and, far above, a crack of starlight that might have been real sky.

Wren sat cross-legged on the floor with the ledger, writing in cipher by lamplight.

"She didn't deny it," Gideon said, after a while.

"Who?"

"The ledger. The prophecy-keepers wrote all this down a century ago — my name, tonight's date, the treasury, the crown. Somebody knew. Somebody always knows, and nobody tells the poor fool whose soul's been pawned until it's too late to do anything but march." He dropped onto the bed and stared at the ceiling that wasn't there. "Why do you people even keep the book?"

Wren didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice was careful. "Because someone has to remember what's coming. Even when it's terrible. Especially then."

"That's not remembering. That's sentencing."

"No." She looked up, and the lamplight caught the ink stains on her fingers. "Sentencing is when you decide it happens *to* someone. Remembering is just... leaving a lantern lit so they can see the road." A pause. "Even if the road goes somewhere awful."

Gideon snorted, but softly. "You rehearsed that."

"For twenty years," she admitted, and the corner of her mouth twitched. "You're the first person I've ever gotten to say it to."

Something loosened in his chest, just slightly. He lay back and stared into the dark where the ceiling should be, listening to snow fall upward, doing sums he didn't like.

A gift from a dying king. A door out, if he stole one thing and brought it home. Simple. Clean. The kind of deal he'd have taken over a card table without blinking.

Which was exactly why he didn't believe it.

Nobody gives a man his soul back for one errand. Nobody holds a kingdom together for ninety years and then hands the fix to the first smuggler who wanders in with a ledger and a grudge. Somewhere in the bargain there was a hook, and Gideon Blackwood had spent his whole life finding hooks in gifts, and he'd be damned — properly, officially damned, apparently, with paperwork — before he'd swallow this one whole.

The trouble, he thought as sleep finally took him, was figuring out whether the hook belonged to the king...

or to whoever had written chapter nine.

Outside the door, soft as a secret, something began counting its many-jointed way along the corridor.
