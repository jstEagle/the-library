## 9. Chancellor Vesperine

The invitation came the way a knife comes: quietly, and from behind.

Gideon had barely returned to his borrowed chamber in the guest wing of the Hollow Court — a room carved from pale stone that might once have been coral, if coral grew underground and dreamed of winter — when he found a second door where the first had been. It hadn't been there an hour ago. He was fairly sure of that. In this place he was no longer sure of anything except Wren's grip on his sleeve and the ledger's weight against his ribs.

The new door was tall and narrow, framed in silver frost that did not melt, and it stood slightly ajar as though the room beyond were embarrassed to be found.

A card lay on the threshold. Actual cardstock, cream-colored, with handwriting so elegant it looked embarrassed by its own beauty:

*Lord Blackwood — the Chancellor requests the pleasure of your company. No guards. No tricks. Tea.*

"Lord," Gideon said aloud, to nobody. "That's generous."

He considered his options, which were: open the mysterious door, or stand in a haunted bedroom arguing with stationery until moonrise. He'd done the second one before, back in Maren's cottage, and all it had earned him was a stiff neck and a soul-debt.

He opened the door.

* * *

The room beyond was wrong in the gentlest possible way. It was warm. That was the first offense. Everything else in the mountain had been cold in the way of things that had forgotten warmth was ever an option, but here a fire burned in a hearth of black glass, and the light it threw was gold instead of blue, and there were books — real books, leather-spined, dog-eared — stacked on every surface like the room had been caught mid-argument about how to organize them.

And there was tea. A service for two on a low table, steam rising, smelling of honey and something herbal and faintly familiar — rosemary, maybe, or the memory of rosemary.

Seated beside it was a woman who looked, at first glance, entirely ordinary. Silver-streaked dark hair pinned up carelessly. A dress the color of deep water. Hands folded in her lap like a grandmother waiting for gossip.

It was the second glance that cost him. Her joints bent a half-beat too smoothly, the way water bends, the way nothing with bones should. And her eyes — her eyes were the exact gray-white of the glacier above Thornfell, and they held the same patient certainty that spring would never come.

"Chancellor Vesperine," she said, and her voice was warm too, which somehow made it worse. "You must be Gideon. Though I suppose I should say *Lord* Blackwood now. Debts of your magnitude confer a certain rank here. Didn't Maren ever mention that?"

"She mentioned," Gideon said, staying by the door, "that you people eat memories. So forgive me if I don't sit down and put my head anywhere near that teapot."

Vesperine laughed. It was a good laugh. That was the worst part. It was the laugh of someone who had heard a thousand jokes and genuinely liked most of them.

"The tea is only tea. Chamomile, honey, a sprig of something I brought from the surface two hundred years ago and have been regretting ever since — it never took properly to the climate." She poured a cup anyway and set it across from her, an invitation without pressure. "Sit or stand, Lord Blackwood. But do listen. You've had a long day of being told what you owe. I thought you might like, just once, to hear what you're owed."

Gideon didn't move. "Everyone down here keeps offering me deals. Your king wants me to fetch his shiny rock. The courtiers want to eat me at a feast. What's your angle?"

"My angle." She smiled, and for a moment the glacial eyes warmed toward something almost human. "My angle is that I am the only person in this mountain who has ever actually been you."

* * *

She let that sit while the fire crackled. Gideon found, to his irritation, that his feet carried him closer — not sitting, not yet, but near enough to feel the fire's heat on his shins, and stars, he'd missed heat. He hadn't realized how cold he'd been since the pass until warmth touched him again, and something in his chest unclenched by a single desperate degree.

"You were sold before you were born," Vesperine said. "Your mother traded your soul for her life and her freedom, and she ran north, and she never once looked back — well. Once. There was one look back, at a frozen river crossing, and she spent the rest of her years pretending it hadn't happened. Am I close?"

"How could you possibly—"

"I read ledgers. It's my job. Yours is in the treasury, third shelf, under the lamps marked 'inherited.'" She said it lightly, the way one mentions a coat checked at a door. "But that's not what I mean. I mean I know what it is to find out, mid-life, that the ground under you was never yours. That your whole story has a co-author you never met, and he signs his work in other people's blood."

"You're not mortal," Gideon said. "Wren says none of you are. You're courtiers. You eat memories."

"I was mortal." Something flickered across her face — fast, but not fast enough. "I was the first. Before me there was no debt-system, no market, no court. There was a starving king under a freezing mountain and a bargain he made with one desperate family, and then another, and then another, until the bargains learned to walk and talk and call themselves a government." She lifted her own cup, inhaled the steam, did not drink. Courtiers, Gideon remembered, couldn't really taste anything anymore. They could only remember tasting. "So yes. I know exactly what it is to be sold, Gideon Blackwood. I've simply had longer to get over it than you will live."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, through walls of stone, came the sound of the court going about its evening — a music like wind through keyholes, a laughter with too many harmonics in it.

"And the deal?" Gideon said finally. "There's always a deal."

"There is always a deal." Vesperine set down her cup and leaned forward, and for the first time the chancellor fell away and Gideon saw what sat underneath: something old and tired and terribly, terribly reasonable. "Here is mine. Walk away. Not tonight — take a week, take a month, enjoy our hospitality, such as it is. Then take the crown-sigil — not for the king, he's dying and he knows it, and everything he told you today was a dying man rearranging furniture. Take it for yourself. Put it on. And walk out of this mountain free, rich, and untouchable, with a hundred lifetimes of warmth banked against any winter the world cares to send."

"And the souls?" Gideon asked, though his mouth had gone dry. "The treasury full of them? The smugglers and farmers and children who got sold like I did?"

"They stay."

"Just like that."

"Just like that." She spread her hands, an honest gesture, and that was the trap of it — honesty was so much more convincing than lies. "They stay sold, because they have always been sold, because their sale is the fire that warms the crown, and the crown is the only thing standing between Thornfell and a winter that does not end. You've seen the snow, Gideon. It's falling out of season already. Do you know why?"

He didn't answer. He suspected she'd tell him anyway.

"Because the king is dying," she said softly, "and the crown is hungry, and a hungry crown reaches further. Every year the winters come earlier. Every year the snow climbs lower down the passes. In fifty years, perhaps sixty, the ice takes the lowlands. In a century, the ports. The world doesn't freeze because we're cruel, Lord Blackwood. The world freezes because we exist, and we intend to go on existing, and somebody has to pay for that." She smiled, small and terrible. "The only question on the table tonight is whether that somebody is everyone — or whether it's the few thousand souls already in the vault, who frankly stopped noticing they were missing years ago."

* * *

"That's a lie," Gideon said. But his voice came out uncertain, and Vesperine heard it, and her smile gentled rather than sharpened, which was infinitely more frightening.

"Is it? Let me show you something, and you can decide."

She didn't touch him. She didn't need to. She simply turned her palm upward, and the firelight bent toward it, and the air above her hand filled with images the way still water fills with sky.

Gideon saw a cottage. Not Maren's crooked hovel — a real home, stone and timber, smoke rising straight from the chimney into a summer sky. He saw himself in it, older, broad through the shoulders, laughing at something. A woman stood in the doorway behind him with flour on her hands and his own brown eyes, and she was saying something that made him laugh harder, and the sound of it reached him across the vision like warmth reaching through a wall.

He saw himself behind a market stall — no, owning the market, three stalls, then a shopfront, then a warehouse with his name painted over the door in letters he recognized from his own hand. Honest roads. Honest coin. Smugglers tipping their hats to him not because he ran contraband past them but because he'd built the road they all traveled, and everyone knew his name, and nobody said it with a warrant attached.

He saw a daughter. That was the one that broke something loose in his chest. Small, fierce, dark-haired, riding on his shoulders and shouting directions to nowhere, and he was obeying every one of them, and the sun was out, and it was *warm*, and in the whole wide vision there was not one single flake of snow.

"This never happened," he whispered.

"No," Vesperine agreed. "But it could have. If your mother hadn't run. If Maren hadn't lied to you. If you hadn't fled down a mountain with stolen charms in your coat the night before your debt came due." She closed her hand, and the visions folded away like letters. "Every choice you've made since you were seventeen has been made inside a cage someone else built. I'm offering you the door out of it. All it costs is other people's chains."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you'll spend the rest of your very short mortal life being brave and right and poor, and the winter will come anyway, and in a hundred years some other wretch will be sitting in that chair listening to some other chancellor make some other offer, and the world will be forty years colder." She refilled his untouched tea, unasked, unhurried. "Bravery is lovely, Gideon. I've watched six centuries of it march through these halls. It always ends the same way: loudly, briefly, and with no measurable effect on the temperature."

* * *

He should have said no then. Some part of him, the part that had laughed off Wren's ledger in a ruined chapel, the part that had survived ten years of hedge-witch discipline and smuggler's luck, knew exactly what this was — the softest voice in the mountain telling the sweetest lie, dressed in chamomile and firelight.

But he looked at the tea. He looked at the fire. He thought about the daughter on his shoulders, and the sun, and the word *warm*, and he understood — with a clarity that felt like falling — that Vesperine wasn't offering him power.

She was offering him a childhood. The one he'd never gotten. The one that had been sold before he drew his first breath.

And gods help him, he wanted it. He wanted it the way the frozen want fire, stupidly, wholly, with no thought for the burning after.

"What happens to them," he said quietly, "the ones in the vault. If I take the crown and leave them. Do they suffer?"

Vesperine studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her answer honestly, which was either mercy or the finest cruelty he'd yet encountered.

"No," she said. "That's the tragedy of it. They sleep. They dream warm dreams. They never know they were ever anything but asleep. Only you would know, Gideon. Only you would carry it. And you'd carry it comfortably, in a warm house, with flour-handed women and daughters on your shoulders, and in twenty years you wouldn't even remember why the sight of a lamp made you sad." She rose, smoothing her dress, and the glacial eyes held his without apology. "That is my offer. No guards. No tricks. Think on it. The feast is in three nights; give me your answer before then."

She moved toward the impossible door, then paused.

"One more thing, since you'll hear it eventually and I'd rather you heard it from me." Her voice stayed pleasant. Her eyes did not. "Your little prophecy-keeper. Wren. Ask her sometime what the ledger says happens to the keeper when the final seal opens. Ask her why she's never once, in all your conversations, told you what she's planning to do." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "She's known since she was a child, you know. Just like I did. We sold children learn early what we're for."

The door closed behind her, and the frost along its frame bloomed once, like a sigh, and faded.

Gideon stood alone in the warm room with the cooling tea and the dying fire, and the vision of the daughter on his shoulders hung behind his eyes like an afterimage of a sun he'd never see.

Then he picked up the teacup, sniffed it — chamomile, honey, nothing else, probably — drank it in one defiant swallow because it was warm and he was tired of being cold, and went to find Wren.

Because if there was one thing a lifetime of running contraband past armed men had taught him, it was this: when someone tells you a secret about your friend to keep you compliant, the first thing you do is go ask your friend.

And if the answer was the one Vesperine wanted him to hear —

Well. Then he'd just have to be brave and right and poor, loudly, briefly, and see about that temperature.
