## 10. The Treasury of Souls

Wren had spent her whole life learning to read a book that hated her.

The ledger was not cruel, exactly. It was simply honest in the way of old things — it recorded what would be and did not care how you felt about it. She had grown up tracing its cramped cipher by candlelight, memorizing dates like prayers, and she had learned early that the worst entries were never the dramatic ones. The dramatic ones at least gave you something to do. It was the small annotations that kept her up at night. *He will laugh when he should not,* the ledger had said of Gideon Blackwood, years before she met him. *He will trust too slowly and forgive too quickly. He will go down because he cannot bear to be sent.*

She had thought, reading that last line as a girl of twelve, that it sounded terribly sad.

Now, crouched behind a pillar of black glass while two courtiers drifted past on their wrong-jointed legs, Wren thought: *he went down because I asked him to, and he made a joke about it, and somehow that is worse.*

"Stop fidgeting," Gideon whispered beside her.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You're doing the thing with your thumb. The counting thing."

She looked down. Her thumb was indeed moving against her forefinger, ticking through the ledger's cadences without her permission. "It's how I think," she said, with as much dignity as a person could manage while flattened against cold glass in a stolen courtier's cloak.

"It's how you panic," he said. "I've seen you do it three times since we came down here. Once before the market, once after the market, and once when that little servant-thing offered us soup."

"The soup had eyes in it."

"The soup had *pearls* in it. Eyes would have been more honest." He peered around the pillar, and the faint light of the treasury antechamber caught his face — sharp-boned, tired, and wearing an expression Wren was beginning to catalog the way she cataloged dates. This one meant trouble. Most of his expressions meant trouble. "There. Do you see it?"

She saw it.

The treasury doors were not doors at all, or not only. They were a wall of ambered light, floor to vaulted ceiling, and behind the light hung — there was no other word for it — lamps. Thousands upon thousands of them, small and round and glowing, arranged in rows that receded into darkness deeper than the mountain had any right to be. Each one burned with its own color, its own rhythm, its own stubborn little flicker. They looked, Wren thought, like a city seen from very far away on a clear night. Like every window in every home where someone was waiting up.

Souls. Sold souls, harvested and shelved, each one still burning because souls, apparently, were too obstinate to do anything else.

"There's a ward-line across the threshold," she said, forcing her voice steady. "See how the light bends? It doesn't touch the floor. Nothing crosses that line unless it's been claimed."

"Then we'll be unclaimed," Gideon said. "We're nobody's. Well." A crooked half-smile. "Technically I'm somebody's. That's rather the whole problem."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"Gideon."

"Wren." He said her name the way he said most things — like he was testing whether it would hold weight. Then, softer, without the joke anywhere in it: "My soul's in there. Somewhere among all those lights, there's one that's mine, and it's been sitting on a shelf for twenty-six years while I ran contraband charms through passes I didn't even know why I couldn't stand. Don't tell me not to be funny about it. Funny is the only thing keeping me from putting my hand through that wall right now and consequences be damned."

Wren opened her mouth to argue — and closed it again, because the ledger had taught her something useful after all, which was that some people could not be argued out of a thing, only walked alongside into it.

"Fine," she said. "But we do this my way. Slowly. The wards here are older than the market, older than the feast-hall. Old wards don't just alarm, they *remember*. Touch one wrong and the whole treasury will know your name, your face, and probably your mother's maiden name, and it will tell everyone."

"Charming. And my way?"

"Your way involves consequences be damned."

"...Fair."

* * *

They waited for the changing of the shelf-watch — the courtiers who tended the treasury moved on a rhythm, Wren had discovered, and the rhythm was the closest thing the Hollow Court had to honesty. Everything else down here performed courtesy. The watch-schedule alone simply *was*, indifferent and regular as breathing.

When the drifting shapes folded themselves away into a side gallery, Wren led Gideon along the wall to a seam in the amber light where two ward-lines crossed and left a gap no wider than a handspan. She'd found it an hour ago by holding the ledger open and watching which way its pages trembled. The book had been made below, once; it remembered the architecture even if it refused to say so outright.

"In," she breathed. "Quickly. Don't touch anything that hums."

"Everything down here hums."

"Don't touch anything that hums *at you*."

They slipped through the gap into warmth.

That was the thing Wren hadn't expected — the warmth. After the endless winter-strata above and the cold courtesy of the court, the treasury was almost tender, air thick and golden as honey, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and rain-on-stone and a hundred other things she had no names for. Home-smells. A thousand homes' worth, leaking gently out of a thousand small flames.

Gideon had stopped walking.

He stood in the aisle between the shelves with his head tipped back, and for once he wasn't funny at all. His throat worked. His hand rose halfway toward the nearest lamp and stopped, hovering, not quite daring.

"They're all still lit," he said quietly. "All of them. Nobody ever put them out."

"No," Wren said. "They can't be put out. Only given back."

He turned a slow circle, taking in the impossible depth of the place. "How many?"

The ledger knew. Of course the ledger knew; it had a column for it, updated in a cipher-hand so fine she needed a magnifying lens. But she said the number softly, the way you spoke near sleeping things. "Eleven thousand, four hundred and six."

"And mine's one of them."

"One of them."

He started down the aisle, and Wren followed, and she watched him search — really search, leaning close to lamp after lamp, studying each flame the way a man studies faces in a crowd for someone he loves. Some lamps burned white, some gold, one deep river-green. A few guttered low, old sales, old griefs. One, near the end of a row, flared bright orange whenever Gideon passed, and he paused, frowning at it.

"Do you feel that?"

"Yes," Wren said. "They know their own. That one belonged to someone from your valley, maybe. Or someone who loved someone from it. Souls are sentimental. It's their whole job."

"Huh." He touched the shelf edge — carefully, wood and not flame — and moved on.

And then he stopped so suddenly she nearly walked into his back.

Three shelves up, set slightly apart from its neighbors as though the shelf itself were reluctant to hold it, burned a small lamp the color of a struck match. Not gold. Not white. Orange-gold, fierce, restless, the flame bending sideways against the still air as if it were trying to leave. Trying, Wren realized with a lurch, to reach him.

"Oh," Gideon said. Just that. Just *oh*, in a voice gone young.

"Gideon—"

"That's me." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "That's — Wren, that's *me*. Twenty-six years and it's still — look at it, it hasn't even dimmed, it's been sitting on a shelf in a mountain and it's still—" His hand came up.

"*Wait.*"

He didn't wait. Of course he didn't wait. The ledger had promised she would fail to stop him at least twice tonight, and this was the first failure arriving precisely on schedule.

His fingers closed around the lamp's warm glass —

— and the treasury woke up.

* * *

Later, Wren would try to describe the sound and fail. It was not a bell and not a scream but something between: a single vast note that came out of the walls, out of the floor, out of the amber doors themselves, and every lamp in eleven thousand four hundred and six flickered at once, and the light bent toward Gideon like ten thousand eyes opening.

The ward-lines blazed up across the aisles, silver-white and hungry, writing themselves in the air faster than any cipher Wren knew — and she knew *all* the ciphers, that was supposed to be the one thing she was for.

"Put it down," she hissed.

"It's *mine*—"

"PUT IT DOWN OR WE'RE BOTH NAMES ON A SHELF!"

Something in her voice got through where sense couldn't. His fingers loosened. The lamp settled back onto its shelf — and the flame reached after him, straining, and Wren felt something in her chest tear a little at the sight of it, a soul reaching for its own body and being left behind *again*.

The great note faded. The ward-lines hung in the air, quivering, unfinished — waiting, she understood with a cold drop in her stomach, for confirmation. For a second verdict. The treasury was deciding whether they were thieves or merely lost.

Then, from far off in the dark reaches of the shelves, came footsteps. Wrong-jointed, courteous, unhurried.

"Shelf-watch," Wren whispered. "Early. The wards called them. Gideon, we have maybe forty counts."

"Which way?"

"Not the way we came. They'll seal the gap first." Her mind was racing, thumb flying through ledger-cadences, and then — absurdly, wonderfully — the answer arrived not from the ledger at all but from Gideon himself, from something he'd said days ago on the pass, complaining: *smuggler logic, love — when the front door grows teeth, find the chimney.*

"Lamps need tending," she said. "Tended things need a service door. There — behind the green one, see how the shelf doesn't meet the wall? Move."

They moved. The gap behind the river-green lamp was narrow, unwarded, and smelled of dust and old oil, and it let them out into a maintenance gallery barely wide enough for one — which meant they went single file, pressed chest to stone, while the shelf-watch glided past the mouth of the gallery close enough that Wren could hear the soft wet click of borrowed faces settling.

Gideon's hand found hers in the dark. She didn't pull away. She decided, in the interest of efficiency, that she simply hadn't gotten around to it yet.

They climbed. The gallery spiraled up and away from the treasury, through rock that grew warmer and then cooler and then strange, until at last they spilled out through a rusted grate into a disused storeroom stacked with centuries of the court's leavings — broken masques, moth-eaten banners, chairs built for spines that bent the wrong way. Safe. Probably safe. As safe as anywhere got down here.

Gideon sat down hard on the floor among the ruined chairs and put his face in his hands.

"I touched it," he said. "I finally find it after twenty-six years and I get one second and then I have to put it *back*."

Wren sat down beside him. Close enough that their shoulders touched. She was discovering she liked that, and filed the discovery away next to several others she wasn't ready to examine.

"You found it," she corrected gently. "That's not nothing. Do you know how many people down here don't even know their lamps exist? You know yours. You know its color, its shelf, the way it leans toward you. That's not nothing, Gideon. That's a map."

"A map to a thing I can't carry out." He dropped his hands and looked at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and desperately hopeful all at once, and Wren thought: *ah. There it is. The exact expression the ledger never managed to capture in its dry little margin-notes.* "Tell me there's a way. You're the prophecy person. Tell me the book has a way."

So she told him the truth, which was the hardest thing the book had ever asked of her.

"The lamps can't leave," she said. "Not smuggled, not stolen, not carried. The mountain holds them the way a lung holds breath — take one out and the whole body knows, and the whole body closes. We proved that tonight, unfortunately, expensively." She pulled the ledger from inside her cloak and laid it open between them, turning to the page she had been avoiding for three days. The cipher there was dense, ancient, annotated in a hand she recognized as her own grandmother's. "But listen. Listen properly. The lamps can't be carried out. They can only be freed from within the crown itself."

"The crown-sigil." He frowned. "The king's bargain. Vesperine's little trinket."

"It's not a trinket. It's the lock and the key and the vault door, all one thing. Every debt in this mountain runs through it. Which means every lamp is tethered to it — and a tether can be cut, but only at the anchor." She traced the cipher line with her finger. "*From within,* it says. From inside the crown. Whoever holds the sigil at the moment of unbinding doesn't just free one soul. They free all of them. Eleven thousand, four hundred and six, all at once, like — like reversed snowfall."

Silence. Somewhere far above them, muffled by miles of stone, the false winter wind moaned against the mountain's skin.

"All of them," Gideon repeated.

"All of them."

"Including mine."

"Including yours."

He stared at the page. And then — because he was Gideon, because the ledger had known he would be — he started to grin. It began crooked and grew until it was incandescent, and Wren felt her own mouth betraying her, curving helplessly upward in answer.

"So all I have to do," he said, "is steal the most guarded object in the Hollow Court, from the chancellor who has already tried to buy me twice, while the entire courtier class wants my soul as a midnight snack, and then break it in a way that frees every soul in the mountain including mine, presumably without killing us, and walk out."

"When you say it like that it sounds difficult."

"When I say it like that it sounds *insane*."

"Yes," Wren agreed warmly. "Welcome to prophecy. It's mostly insane with occasional snacks."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound bouncing off the broken masques — and for a moment the storeroom felt less like a hiding place and more like the beginning of something. Hope, revised. Not destroyed. Rewritten in a harder, better hand.

Then Wren looked back down at the ledger, at the next line of her grandmother's cipher, the one beneath the good news, and the warmth drained out of her all at once.

Because there was another requirement. The final seal had conditions, and the first condition was written plainly enough that even Gideon could have read it: *a freely given memory of home, surrendered willingly.*

And beneath that, in her grandmother's smaller, sadder hand, the annotation Wren had been carrying like a stone since childhood:

*The keeper opens the last seal. The keeper does not survive the opening.*

"Gideon," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "there's something you should know about the final seal."

He turned. Outside, far off, the court bells began to toll — and not the ordinary hours. The summons-toll. The feast-toll.

Someone, somewhere above them, had just invited them to dinner.
