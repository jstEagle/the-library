## 11. What Wren Buried

The spring-stratum smelled like a lie.

That was the first thing Wren thought when they crawled out of the narrow throat of rock and into the cavern beyond: *this is too kind to be true.* Green light filtered down through the ceiling in long watery ribbons, as though the mountain had remembered sunlight and couldn't quite let it go. Moss carpeted the floor in soft grey-green drifts. A stream ran somewhere nearby, chuckling to itself over stones that had never seen frost. After the autumn stratum's dying orchards and the summer stratum's heavy, honeyed heat, this place felt like an apology the mountain was making for everything above and below it.

"It's not real," Gideon said, echoing her thought. He was still catching his breath, hands braced on his knees, hair plastered to his forehead with sweat. "None of this is real."

"Real enough to sleep on." Wren pressed her palm into the moss. It gave like a featherbed. Somewhere overhead, water dripped in a slow rhythm — *drip, drip* — that she suspected the mountain had invented specifically to lull intruders into false security. "The courtiers don't come here. Vesperine said the spring-stratum is 'sentimental.' She said it the way you'd say *infected*."

"She says everything like that." Gideon lowered himself onto the moss with a groan that suggested every bone in his body had filed a complaint. "Sentimental. Infected. *Gideon Blackwood, how lovely, we've been expecting you since before your mother met your father.*" He shuddered. "I'm going to hear that voice in my sleep for the rest of my life."

"Rest of your life," Wren repeated, and then heard herself, and busied herself unpacking their supplies so she wouldn't have to look at him.

They had been below for three days now, or what she estimated were days; time in the mountain moved like syrup, pooling strangely in the deep places. Three days of smuggler tunnels and backward seasons. Three days of teaching him the ledger's cipher by firelight while he taught her which shadows were just shadows and which ones had teeth. Three days since the treasury, since the wards had screamed around him like a nest of disturbed hornets, since they'd learned the truth that had rearranged everything:

*The lamps cannot be carried out. They can only be freed from within the crown itself.*

And one other truth. The one she'd been carrying alone since she was seven years old, tucked beneath her ribs like a stone she'd swallowed and never passed.

She set out the last of the bread. It had gone stale in the way of bread bought from the Market of Small Debts — slightly wrong, slightly sweet, as if it had been paid for with something better than coin. Gideon ate his share without complaint. He complained about almost everything else — the tunnels, the temperature, the courtiers' manners, the way the ledger's cipher made his eyes cross — but he ate without complaint, which told her he understood hunger in the old way, the road way, the way she'd only ever read about.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you organize our supplies very carefully and don't look at me." He tipped his head back against the cavern wall. In the green light he looked younger than he was, and more tired, and — she hated that she noticed this — rather less insufferable than when they'd met. "You've done it twice since the treasury. Once on the stairs, once when I asked if you were all right. So either you're hiding an injury, or you're working up to something."

"I could be hiding an injury *and* working up to something."

"That would be efficient of you."

Wren folded the empty cloth that had held the bread into a neat square, then unfolded it, then folded it again. Above them the water dripped its patient rhythm. *Drip. Drip.* Like a countdown. Like a heartbeat that wasn't hers.

"Gideon," she said. "When I was seven years old, my grandmother sat me down in front of the ledger and taught me to read the page with tonight's date on it. Not the date — the date hadn't happened yet. But the entry. Your name. And underneath your name, two lines of cipher." Her fingers had gone still on the cloth. "One line said *the sold soul descends*. That was you. Easy enough."

"And the other?"

Wren looked at him. Really looked — at the smuggler's scars on his knuckles, the hedge-charm he still wore on a cord around his neck because Maren had given it to him even after he'd run away, the stubborn set of his jaw that she had learned meant he was frightened and refusing to show it.

"The other line said *the keeper opens the seal*," she said. "And keepers open seals the way lamps burn oil, Gideon. All the way down. To the end of the wick."

* * *

He didn't say anything for a long moment. The green light shifted overhead, those impossible ribbons brightening and dimming as though the cavern were breathing.

"No," Gideon said finally.

"Yes."

"No, that's — no. That's a translation problem. Cipher is ambiguous. Half those glyphs have three meanings each; you told me so yourself. You told me the word for *door* and the word for *wound* are nearly identical—"

"They're not nearly identical, they're exactly identical, that's the whole point of the cipher, everything costs something—" Wren stopped, dragged in a breath. She had rehearsed this conversation for seventeen years. She had rehearsed it in the voice of calm authority, in the voice of gentle sorrow, in the voice of a soldier delivering hard news. What came out instead was her own voice, small and cracking: "I've known since I was seven. I've had my whole life to get used to it. You've had thirty seconds. Take longer if you need."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That." He gestured at her, at all of her, with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "The thing where you hand me my grief pre-wrapped like it's yours to parcel out. You've known since you were *seven*? Seven-year-olds know bedtime and whether there'll be jam on their bread. They don't know they're going to die in a hole under a mountain."

"This one did." Wren heard how flat her voice had gone and couldn't help it. Some things, once flattened, stayed flat. "My grandmother didn't tell me gently, if that helps. She told me the way she told me everything — as a fact of the trade. *The book names a keeper, and the keeper pays the toll, and that is why we keep the book.* She said it while shelling peas. I remember thinking the peas sounded very loud."

Gideon made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't hurt so much. "Peas."

"There were a lot of them. Shelling takes hours. I had time to ask questions." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I asked if there was any way around it. She said the ledger doesn't record ways around things, only things. I asked what would happen if nobody opened the seal. She went quiet for a long time, and then she said, *then the winter finishes falling, and everyone above us freezes, and the debt goes unpaid forever, and debts that go unpaid forever grow teeth.* I asked what she meant. She wouldn't say. I think she meant the crown finds another way. I think she meant it always finds another way."

"So your grandmother raised you as a sacrifice."

"My grandmother raised me as a *keeper*." The correction came out sharper than she intended, and Wren winced at herself. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference, or the last nineteen years were — " She stopped again. Pressed her lips together. Started over, quieter. "Every keeper before me kept the ledger and waited for the name to appear. I got the name when I was seven. Do you know what that's like? Everyone else in my line got decades of not-knowing. I got to grow up alongside it. I learned to write my letters copying out your name. I practiced the cipher on your entry until I could read it in the dark. When other children had imaginary friends, I had a *date*."

"A date," Gideon repeated faintly.

"Tonight's date. Well — the night of the collection. Which turned out to be tonight, because the ledger is never early and never late." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle as old ice. "I used to imagine you. Did you know that? Before I met you. I imagined what sort of person would be named in the book. I decided you'd be tall. You're not tall. I decided you'd be brave. You're — well. You climbed down a frozen waterfall because a stranger told you to, so perhaps. I decided you'd be angry, and that part was right. You were furious from the first minute. You laughed at the ledger like it had insulted your mother."

"It *did* insult my mother. Sort of. It revealed that my mother sold me before I was born, which is arguably worse." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Wren. Look at me."

She looked.

"You should have told me," he said. "Not on the mountainside, fine. Not at Maren's cottage, fine. But somewhere between the waterfall and the treasury, somewhere in three days of tunnels where I trusted you with my life about forty separate times — you should have told me that the plan ends with you dead."

"The plan doesn't end with me dead. The plan ends with the souls freed and the winter broken. My death is a *step* in the plan. A middle step. There are several steps after it."

"That is not comforting in the way you seem to think it is."

"I wasn't going for comforting. I was going for accurate."

"Wren." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and his voice dropped into something low and fierce that she hadn't heard from him before — not anger, exactly, or not only. Something underneath the anger, older and warmer. "When I found out my soul was sold, do you know what the worst part was? Not the selling. The *deciding*. Someone decided, before I existed, what my life was for. My mother chose, and Maren chose to hide it, and the Court chose to collect, and every single one of them treated me like a thing with a purpose instead of a person with a choice." His jaw worked. "And now you're doing the same thing. You decided when you were seven what your death was for, and you've been walking toward it ever since, and you didn't think I deserved a vote."

"That's not — it's not the same."

"It's exactly the same."

"It's *my* death!"

"And it's my — " He stopped. Blinked. Seemed to surprise himself with whatever he'd been about to say. Wren waited, heart doing something complicated against her ribs, but he only shook his head and tried again. "It's my seal. It's my soul in that lamp. If anyone opens anything, it'll be because we both decided, together, with full information, like two people who trust each other. Which means you owe me the information. All of it. Starting now. Starting with why you never ran."

The question landed harder than any accusation could have. Wren looked away, at the green light, at the little stream making its cheerful counterfeit of a brook.

"Where would I run?" she said. "The ledger named me the day it named you. My grandmother checked. Twice. Running doesn't unname you, it just means you die somewhere colder, with the winter unfinished, and everyone above ground frozen anyway." She hugged her knees tighter. "Besides. Who would I be, if I weren't the keeper? I've never been anything else. I don't have a before. You have a whole runaway life you built out of spite — bad roads and worse company and contraband charms, a self you made yourself even if it turns out someone else held the deed. I have a book and a duty and a grave with my name already carved on the inside of my own skull." She smiled, and knew it was a terrible smile. "Running requires somewhere to run *to*, Gideon. I never had one. Until — "

She stopped. Too late. The word had already escaped, trailing its meaning behind it like a torn hem.

Gideon went very still. "Until?" he said carefully.

"Until recently," Wren said, with tremendous dignity, "there was a cave with a man in it who complains about cipher."

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. The stream chuckled. The green light breathed. Somewhere far above, muffled by strata and stone, the mountain went on being enormous and hungry and doomed.

Then Gideon said, "All right. New plan."

"We don't have a new plan. We barely have the old plan."

"We have the bones of a new plan. Step one: nobody dies. Step two: we figure out the rest on the way." He held up a hand before she could object. "Before you say the ledger doesn't record ways around things — I've spent ten years as a smuggler, Wren. Every route I ever ran was a way around something somebody official swore was impassable. Borders, wards, tariffs, curses. There is *always* a way around. The officials just can't see it, because they wrote the rules and rules make people blind." His eyes had lit with the particular gleam she'd learned to dread and rely on in equal measure. "Your ledger records things. Fine. Ledgers also have margins."

Wren stared at him. "Margins."

"Margins! Footnotes! Blank space!" He tapped the satchel where the ledger rode, wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping animal. "A book that predicts everything still has to leave room for everything it can't predict, or the pages wouldn't fit in the world. Your grandmother read you the lines. Did she ever once read you the spaces between the lines? Because I've been looking at that book for three days, and the cipher isn't the only writing in it. There are marks. Little ones. Faint ones. Down the edges of the pages, like — like someone was keeping a second, smaller ledger in the gaps."

Wren's mouth went dry. "Those are just — ink bleed. Age spots. The binding—"

"Wren."

"They're *age spots*, Gideon."

"When we get the book out, I want you to look at them properly. In good light. With the eyes of a woman who has spent her whole life reading that book and maybe, just maybe, never once read what was written beside it." He settled back against the wall, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had won an argument and knew it and was being gracious about it, which was somehow more infuriating than gloating. "Because here's what I think. I think your grandmother shelled a lot of peas in her life. And I think a woman who spends that many hours with a prophetic ledger might have written some things down that she didn't want her seven-year-old granddaughter to find. Things like *how to survive the opening*. Or *what the seal actually takes*. Or *where the keeper's line hid the trick the Court never learned*."

"You're speculating."

"I'm smuggling. It's what I do. You take a thing everyone believes is solid and you find the gap in it and you move goods through the gap. Right now the solid thing is a prophecy, and the gap is a margin, and the goods are your life." He shrugged. "Also I refuse to plan around your funeral. It's bad for morale. Mine specifically."

Something in Wren's chest cracked — not painfully, or not only painfully. More the way ice cracks on a river in the first thaw: a sound that means the season has turned whether the winter likes it or not. She realized she was crying, quietly, without having decided to, and that Gideon was pretending very hard not to notice, and that she loved him a little for the pretending.

"If you're wrong," she managed, "if the margins are just age spots and the seal takes what it takes—"

"Then we'll know we tried everything. And I'll spend whatever time we have left being extremely annoying about alternatives." He reached over and took the folded bread-cloth out of her hands, unfolding it, refolding it badly, deliberately, until she snatched it back. "There. Now your hands have something to do that isn't grieving. We'll start with the margins at first light. Or what passes for light down here. Meanwhile — " he nodded upward, toward the distant, muffled world — "we should sleep. Tomorrow we go see a chancellor about a crown, and I intend to be insufferably well-rested for it."

Wren lay back on the moss. It was warm, treacherously warm, the mountain's kindness doing its work. Above her the water dripped its slow count. *Drip. Drip.*

"Gideon," she whispered, when his breathing had gone long and even. "If the margins really do say something — if she left something — what do I call her? In my head, all these years, I've just called her *she*. I never let myself call her grandmother. Grandmothers are people you get to keep."

The silence stretched. Then, drowsy and certain, from the edge of sleep:

"You call her whatever you want. That's the whole point of margins, Wren. Nobody gets to decide what you write there but you."

Above them, deep in the green dark, something in the spring-stratum shifted — a sound like a page turning, vast and slow, as though the mountain itself had begun, at last, to read.
