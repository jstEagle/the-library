## 12. The Courtiers' Feast

The invitation arrived the way everything arrived in the Hollow Court: without knocking.

Gideon woke in the guest chamber they'd assigned him — a room carved from pale stone that might once have been coral, if coral grew underground and dreamed of winter — to find a silver tray on the table where no servant had entered. On it lay a card of black ice that did not melt, letters rising through it like bubbles through deep water:

*You are summoned to the Midwinter Feast. Attendance is a courtesy. Refusal is also a courtesy, of a different kind.*

"Charming," Gideon said, and turned the card over. On the back, smaller: *Dress as you are. The Court finds honesty refreshing.*

"They find honesty *nutritious*," Wren said from the doorway. She had a ledger page spread across her knee and ink on her fingers, which meant she'd been decoding since before he woke, which meant she hadn't slept, which meant she was running on the particular ferocity of a woman who has decided sleep is for people with better odds. "I found it. The law. I *found* it."

She crossed the room and thrust the page at him. The cipher was half-translated, her neat annotations crowding the margins, but even half-translated Gideon could read the shape of it.

"*The Right of Challenge*," she said. "Older than Vesperine. Older than the king's treasury, maybe. Any mortal present at court may challenge any courtier for what that courtier holds — title, treasure, *throne* — provided the challenge is spoken publicly, before witnesses, and the terms are set by the challenged party." She tapped the page. "Provided, too, that the challenger stakes something of equal weight."

"Equal weight." Gideon looked at the card on the table. "And what do I have that weighs anything here?"

"You have a soul they're all planning to eat," Wren said. "That's not nothing. That's the whole menu."

He laughed — the short, disbelieving laugh that had been getting him through this mountain one horror at a time — and then stopped laughing, because she wasn't smiling.

"You're actually considering it."

"I'm considering surviving the feast first," Gideon said. "One catastrophe at a time. That's how I've run contraband over the Thornfell passes for six years. You don't fight the avalanche and the blizzard at once. You let them take turns."

"And when they don't take turns?"

He didn't have an answer for that. He pocketed the invitation instead, because it seemed like the kind of thing you wanted evidence of later, and followed her out into the corridors of the Court.

* * *

The feast hall had been a cathedral once. Gideon could tell by the bones of it — the ribbed vaults overhead, the rose window at the far end filled now not with glass but with packed snow that glowed faintly blue, like moonlight that had forgotten what it was for. Long tables of black wood ran the length of the floor, and the benches were full of courtiers.

They were beautiful, the way frost on a window is beautiful, and wrong the way frost is wrong. Their borrowed faces were handsome and slightly too smooth, as if someone had described a face to a sculptor who had never seen one. Their joints bent in small extra places. When they turned their heads, they turned them a degree or two past comfortable, and held there, watching.

And between them sat mortals.

Dozens of them. Men and women in fine clothes gone threadbare at the cuffs, faces slack with a comfort that had clearly cost them everything. They ate from plates of steaming food — real food, bread and roast meat and honeyed pears, the smell of it hitting Gideon's empty stomach like a fist — and laughed at jokes they seemed only half to hear, and leaned against their courtier hosts the way drunks lean against lampposts.

"There," Wren murmured. She nodded toward the high table, where a young man sat beside a courtier wearing the face of a gracious aunt. As they watched, the courtier took the young man's hand in both of hers, gently, tenderly, and pressed her lips to his knuckles.

The color went out of him.

Not his blood — his *color*. It drained from his hair first, brown to grey to white, then from his eyes, which went wide and startled and then soft, so soft, and he smiled as it happened, smiled like a man remembering something wonderful, and Gideon understood with a lurch that he *was* remembering something wonderful, was living it backward at terrible speed while the thing wearing his aunt's face drank it down.

When she released his hand, the young man sat blinking at the feast, at the candles, at his own hands, with the mild polite puzzlement of a stranger in his own life.

"What did she take?" Gideon asked. His voice came out flat.

Wren's jaw was tight. "Can't tell from here. Could be his wedding day. Could be his mother's voice. Could be every summer he ever had." She pulled her hood lower. "That's the trick of it. They never take what you'd miss most. They take what you wouldn't know to miss until it's gone."

A courtier drifted near — tall, in robes the color of deep water, its borrowed face a pleasant middle-aged man's — and inclined its head to them with perfect courtesy.

"The honored guests," it said. Its voice was warm. Its breath, when it spoke, made no fog in the cold air, though the hall was cold enough that Gideon's own breath bloomed white. "The chancellor asks that you be seated at her right hand. She would not wish you to miss the toasts."

"How kind," Gideon said.

"It is what we are known for," the courtier agreed, and glided away, joints bending in all the wrong quiet places.

They walked the length of the hall under more eyes than Gideon cared to count. He kept his hands loose at his sides and his face bored, the expression he wore at border checkpoints, and thought: *you've been watched by men with crossbows who wanted your pack. These are just taller. Just hungrier.* It helped less than he hoped.

Vesperine waited at the high table, and she was radiant tonight — some private joke of hers about radiance, Gideon suspected, given the king's translucence. Her gown was the red of embers going out. She gestured to the seats at her right hand with genuine-seeming delight.

"Gideon Blackwood. And the last keeper of the ledger." Her smile was lovely and exact. "You honor us."

"We were invited," Gideon said, sitting.

"We *summoned* you," Vesperine agreed pleasantly. "But I find the words are interchangeable, below. Everything here is a gift until it isn't. Do eat. The pears are excellent. They're grown in the summer stratum — you passed through it, I believe? Some of those very trees may have fed you already."

Gideon looked at the pears. He did not eat them.

* * *

The feast unfolded around him like a slow tide, and Gideon learned its grammar.

Toasts came first — the courtiers raised goblets of something dark and smoking and spoke names. Not the mortals' names. Older names, names in a language that made the candle flames lean. Then the courses, each brought by silent courtier servants, each accompanied by small ceremonies Gideon couldn't parse: a bell rung once; a cup poured out onto the stones; a moment where every courtier in the hall turned its head toward the glowing rose window in unison, held for three heartbeats, and turned back.

"What are they doing?" he whispered.

"Feeding the window," Wren whispered back. Her knuckles were white around her fork. "Or feeding *through* it. There's a whole liturgy in the ledger I haven't cracked. It's older than the debt-system. Whatever they were before they were collectors — this is left over from that."

Before Gideon could ask what she thought they'd been, the trumpets sounded — long notes on instruments made of ice, music like the memory of music — and the herald cried out that the Court would now receive its tribute.

They brought the tribute in on a litter.

She was an old woman, and she was carried gently, reverently, wrapped in furs, and set in a chair of state at the center of the floor. Her family — Gideon guessed family; two younger people with her same stubborn chin — stood behind her, holding her hands. Everyone in the hall rose. Even Vesperine rose.

"What is this?" Gideon breathed.

"A hundredth-year payment," Wren said. Her voice had gone thin. "Some bargains are paid across generations. A village above — I'd guess Hollowmere, or one of the shepherd holds — owes the Court a memory every hundred years. They choose their own. It's in the old contracts. *Voluntary*, the ledger says. That word again."

The old woman was smiling. That was the worst part. She was smiling up at the assembled court, serene as a bride, and she said, in a clear strong voice that carried to the vaults:

"I give the day my husband came home from the lowlands war. I give it freely, and gladly, and I ask the Court to remember: he was kind, and he was funny, and he whistled off-key, and I loved him."

The courtiers bowed their heads. For a moment — one strange, suspended moment — the whole hall felt almost like a church.

Then the chancellor's own herald stepped forward, and the ceremony curdled.

Because it wasn't the old woman's memory they took. It was the *family's*. The two younger people behind her chair — the courtiers flowed to them like spilled milk finding its level, and took, and took, and Gideon watched the son lose his father's face entirely, watched the daughter forget the sound of the whistle, watched them both stand afterward nodding politely at the old woman as though she were a kindly neighbor, while the old woman screamed — screamed with a grief that had nowhere to land, because the people she was grieving for had been hollowed out in front of her and no longer knew what they'd lost—

Gideon was on his feet. He didn't remember standing.

"Sit down," Wren hissed, grabbing his sleeve.

"That's not the bargain—"

"No," Wren said. "*Sit.* Look at Vesperine."

He looked. Vesperine was watching him. Had been watching him, he realized, since the old woman began to speak. Her expression was one of gentle, patient expectation, and her eyes moved — deliberately, visibly — from Gideon's face to the doors at the far end of the hall, and back.

*Fight,* her look said. Or else: *run.* Either would do. Either would be *useful*.

The hall had gone quiet. Every wrong-jointed head was turning toward him now, a slow ripple of attention, and Gideon understood that the insult had been engineered — the substitution, the theft, the old woman's scream — all of it laid out like bait in a snare, and he was standing in the middle of it with his heart hammering and every instinct screaming at him to do *something*.

Fight, and they'd have cause to strip him here and now. Flee, and they'd hunt him through the halls and call it justice, and Wren would be taken as his accomplice.

Or.

Or there was a third door. Wren had spent three nights hunched over the ledger by candlelight to cut it into the wall.

Gideon made himself breathe. Made himself think of border checkpoints, of avalanches taking turns. Fear was fuel; his master Maren had told him that once, back when he'd hated her, back before he'd understood she'd been trying to arm him. *Fear is fuel, boy. Burn it, don't drown in it.*

He straightened his coat. He walked — calmly, insolently calmly — to the center of the floor, past the weeping old woman, past the hollowed children, and stopped where every eye in the cathedral could see him.

"My lords and ladies of the Hollow Court," he said, and his voice, thank every smuggler's god, held steady. "I am Gideon Blackwood. Sold soul. Debtor. Tonight's collection." He let that sit. Let them taste it. "And I claim the Right of Challenge."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere in the vaults, ice creaked.

Vesperine's smile did not move, but something behind it did — a flicker, quick as a fish under pond-ice. "There is no such right," she said softly.

"There is," said Wren, rising from the high table with the ledger open in her arms, and her voice shook and she spoke anyway, and Gideon thought: *good for you,* and also: *we are absolutely going to die.* "Article nine of the Concord of Roots, older than the treasury, older than the crown's current bearer. Any mortal present at court may challenge any courtier for what that courtier holds. Publicly. Before witnesses." She turned the book around so the hall could see the ancient script, the sigils glowing faintly along the margin like embers. "It has never been revoked. It cannot be. Your own law binds you."

Murmurs ran through the courtiers — not voices exactly, but a sound like wind through a frozen forest, like many things agreeing at once. The old woman's family stared blankly. The king's empty throne at the head of the hall seemed, impossibly, to lean forward.

Vesperine descended from the dais slowly, unhurried, her ember-red gown whispering over the stones. She stopped an arm's length from Gideon and studied him the way a jeweler studies a stone she intends to cut.

"You understand the terms," she said. "As challenger, you stake something of equal weight to what you demand. And as the challenged—" her smile widened by a precise, terrible fraction, "—*I* set the terms of the contest."

"I know."

"You stake your soul, then. The very thing you came to reclaim. If you lose, it is forfeit — collected tonight, consumed at this table, before these witnesses. No treasury. No bargaining. Gone."

"Yes," Gideon said.

"And what do you imagine you can take from me that weighs a soul?"

Gideon looked at her — at the first sold child, at the keeper of the crown-sigil, at the architect of this evening's cruelty — and found, to his own surprise, that the fear had burned down to something clean.

"The crown-sigil," he said. "Your office. Your *throne*."

The frozen forest-sound of the courtiers rose sharply. Above, in the shadows of the vaults, something vast shifted its weight.

Vesperine was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed — genuinely, warmly, horribly — and extended her hand as though sealing a trade agreement.

"Witnessed," she called to the hall, and every courtier bowed. "Three days hence, in the presence of the king. I will set the terms, little smuggler, and you will find I set them *well*." Her grip, when he took it, was cold as the inside of a lock. She leaned close, and for him alone, she murmured: "You should have run."

"Yeah," Gideon said. "People keep telling me that."

Behind her, Wren clutched the ledger to her chest, and her eyes were bright with terror and something else — something that looked almost like hope — and Gideon grinned at her with blood still singing in his ears, because reckless courage, he was discovering, felt exactly like momentum, right up until the moment it felt like the drop beneath your feet.

Three days. He had three days to learn how to win a duel he didn't yet understand, against a creature who had been practicing for a thousand years.

Somewhere far above, the winter deepened.
