## 13. Terms of the Challenge

The law of challenge was read aloud three times, because the Hollow Court believed nothing existed until it had been said in triplicate.

Gideon stood on the dais of the feast-hall with his heart hammering somewhere up around his ears and tried to look like a man who had done this sort of thing before. He had not. The closest he'd come was an argument over a card game in a smuggler's den at Coldharrow, and that had ended with him being thrown into a snowbank, which he suspected was not how Vesperine intended to settle matters.

The old law came from a scroll so ancient it had to be unrolled by two courtiers wearing the borrowed faces of bored clerks. The king watched from his throne of grey glass, translucent as sea ice, his hands folded like a man waiting for a kettle to boil.

"*Let any soul sold to this Court,*" intoned the first clerk, "*challenge for that which is held against it.*"

"*Let the challenged name the terms,*" said the second.

"*And let the crown-sigil be the stake,*" they finished together, "*for there is nothing else worth having under this mountain.*"

Vesperine rose from her seat among the courtiers. Up close she was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful — all surface, and something patient underneath it. Her joints bent a half-beat wrong when she moved, and Gideon had learned, in the past few days, to stop pretending not to notice.

"You understand what you've done," she said quietly, as the clerks rolled up their scroll. "You've made yourself legible. Every truth you carry, I will read aloud before witnesses. And if you flinch — if one word of yours burns your tongue — you forfeit."

"I've been legible my whole life," Gideon said. "It's the first time anyone's offered to read me for free."

Something flickered across her face that might have been amusement, or might have been hunger deciding to dress up as amusement for the evening.

"The duel is set for the third hour past moonrise," said the king, and his voice was dry wind through a dead orchard. "Witnessed by me, and by the ledger-keeper." His hollow eyes turned toward Wren, standing at the hall's edge with her book clutched to her chest like a shield that had never once worked. "The Court does not fight its own chancellor without record."

Then the courtiers bowed — all of them at once, in that wrong-jointed way that made Gideon's skin crawl — and the feast resumed around them as though a man hadn't just wagered his soul against the engine of an entire kingdom.

* * *

Wren found him afterward in a side gallery hung with tapestries that depicted, as far as she could tell, the history of winter winning. She was pacing. She had been pacing since the feast, and the courtiers had learned to give her a wide berth, partly because of the book and partly because she kept mutting cipher fragments at them and they found prophecy unsettling even secondhand.

"You absolute *idiot*," she said, by way of greeting.

"Good evening to you too."

"You claimed the right of challenge off *one line* of cipher I translated twenty minutes before the feast. One line! I didn't even have time to tell you the rest of the passage!"

Gideon leaned against a pillar. "What's the rest of the passage?"

Wren stopped pacing. She opened the ledger to a page near the end — the ink there was older than anything else in the book, browned and cracked like autumn leaves — and read aloud:

"*The challenger who prevails shall hold the sigil. But the sigil cannot be broken from without. It opens only to a freely given memory of home, surrendered willingly at the crown's own heart.*"

The words settled over them both like snowfall.

"That's the final seal," Wren said. Her voice had gone very careful, the way it did when she was holding something down. "A memory of home. Given freely. Not stolen, not traded, not taken in payment — *given*. The Court can buy anything, Gideon. That's the whole architecture of the place. Debts, trades, interest. But a gift—" she tapped the page, "—a gift breaks the whole system. It's the one currency they can't mint."

Gideon stared at the tapestries. Winter winning, winter winning, winter winning again; whoever had woven these had not been subtle.

"So Vesperine knows this too," he said slowly. "She's had centuries to read it. Which means she knows exactly what I'm planning, and she let me claim the challenge anyway."

"Yes."

"And the king knows."

"The king," Wren said, "has been lying about what freedom costs since before either of us was born. I'd assume he knows everything."

They stood in silence for a moment. Somewhere far above them — so far it was more memory than sound — the winter over Thornfell creaked and deepened. Gideon had begun to feel it in his teeth, a pressure like the mountain itself grinding its jaw.

"A memory of home," he repeated. "I don't even know what mine would be. My mother's face — Maren gave me that back, but it's not *home*, is it? Home is..." He frowned. "Home is a kitchen. A specific kitchen. There was bread. Someone was singing, badly. I can't—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "It's like trying to remember a dream through someone else's eyes. Because it *was* someone else's. It was hers, before I was born. Everything I remember about home is secondhand."

Wren looked at him for a long moment, and then did something he wasn't expecting: she sat down on the cold stone floor, right there, and patted the space beside her.

"Sit. We're going to do this properly. If we're going to spend a memory, we should know which one we're spending."

He sat. The stone was bitterly cold through his trousers, but after the feast-hall's cloying warmth it felt honest.

"Tell me about the kitchen," she said.

"It's not much. Bread on the table. A window with frost on the inside of the glass. Someone singing — off-key, deliberately off-key, like they thought being bad at it was funny. And I think... I think there were two chairs. Just two. And one of them was mine."

"Whose voice was singing?"

"I don't know. That's the terrible part. I keep reaching for it and it slides. Sometimes I think it's my mother. Sometimes I think it's Maren, younger. Sometimes I think it's nobody at all, just a song that got left behind in a room where people were happy." He laughed, short and bitter. "That's what she took from me, isn't it? Not memories. The *ownership* of them. I've got furniture in my head that belongs to somebody else."

Wren was quiet. Then, softly: "My home was a scriptorium."

He glanced at her.

"I was raised by the keepers — well, keeper, singular, by the end. Brother Aldous. He smelled of lamp oil and onions, and he hummed while he copied, and every night he'd say 'one more page, little wren, one more page,' and every night I'd believe him." Her mouth curved. "I used to think I'd give anything to go back. Now I know exactly what the going rate would be, and it turns out I'd give almost nothing, because almost nothing is worth more than that kitchen."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said about onions."

"Don't make it weird, Gideon."

"I would never."

She elbowed him. It was, he noted, the first time she'd touched him without a purpose — no pulling him out of wards, no shoving him behind cover. Just an elbow, because he'd earned one. He decided he'd take it over most treasures he'd smuggled.

* * *

The hours before moonrise passed the way hours do before executions: quickly and horribly.

They worked in a storeroom the king had grudgingly granted them — "the Court extends hospitality to challengers," he had said, in the tone of a man extending a noose — surrounded by shelves of confiscated things. Years in stoppered bottles. Names folded into paper boats. A shelf of first kisses, each one labeled in spidery script, which Gideon refused to look at directly on principle.

Wren spread the ledger open and attacked the final seal's cipher with a stylus, muttering. Gideon sharpened a knife he wouldn't be allowed to use and pretended this was useful.

"Walk me through it again," he said. "The duel."

"No blades. No wards. Truths only. Each combatant speaks the other's true history — their real names, their real deeds, their real shames. Every true word spoken lands like a blow. Every lie—" she grimaced, "—burns the speaker. Vesperine has had five hundred years to learn everything about everyone. You've had three days."

"Cheerful. What are my advantages?"

"You're asking me?"

"You're the prophecy-keeper. Prophecy me."

Wren blew out a breath. "Your advantage is that you've stopped being ashamed of the things she'll say. I watched you at the feast. When the courtier mocked your mother, you didn't flinch. You got *angry*. Anger doesn't burn you in a duel of truths. Shame does." She looked up. "She'll try to find the shame. It's what she does. It's how she fed the Court for five hundred years — she finds the wound and presses until people sell her the bandage."

"And the disadvantage?"

"She knows a truth about you that you don't know yet. About your mother. About why she really ran." Wren hesitated. "Maren gave you the face and the reason. But reasons have layers, Gideon. Your mother didn't just run from something. She ran *toward* something. And I think Vesperine knows what."

The knife slipped in Gideon's grip and bit his thumb. He watched the blood bead up, absurdly red in all that grey.

"Wonderful," he said. "Anything else? Plague of frogs? Locusts?"

"One more thing." Wren pulled the ledger closer, and her voice dropped. "The seal requires a memory given *willingly*. Not under duress. Not as a bargain. If you walk into that crown's heart thinking of it as a price — as another debt — it won't open. It has to be a gift. Truly. The Court will test it. The mountain itself will test it."

"So I have to mean it."

"You have to mean it the way Brother Aldous meant it when he gave me the ledger. He knew it would cost him everything — the keepers who carried it before him died carrying it — and he handed it over anyway and said, 'One more page, little wren.'" Her throat worked. "He didn't call it a sacrifice. He called it a *continuation*. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

Gideon put down the knife. Outside the storeroom, the mountain groaned — a long, tectonic sound, like a giant turning over in sleep. Frost crept visibly along the doorframe, inching inward, and both of them watched it come.

"The winter's getting worse," he said.

"Faster now. Since you claimed the challenge, the Court's been... tightening. Like a fist." Wren traced the frost with one finger. "Above us, Thornfell's passes are buried. The villages are burning their furniture to stay warm. Every hour we spend down here, the crown eats harder to keep its strength for the duel." She looked up at him, and for once there was no cipher in her eyes, no keeper's calm — just a young woman who had spent her whole life preparing for a moment that was now close enough to touch. "We're running out of mountain, Gideon."

"Then we'd better win fast."

* * *

An hour before moonrise, a courtier came for them — wearing the face of a young man with kind eyes and a jaw that hinged slightly too wide. It announced, in a voice like a music box playing underwater, that the challenger was requested in the antechamber of the throne.

They followed it through corridors of grey glass. The frost followed *them*, Gideon noticed. It grew along the walls in their wake, delicate fern-patterns blooming and dying, and he couldn't decide whether the mountain was marking their path or mourning it.

In the antechamber, alone except for the dying king — who sat propped on his throne like laundry left out too long — Wren finally said what she'd been chewing on for hours.

"When you give the memory," she said, "you won't get to choose what's lost around it. Memories aren't bottles. They're threads. Pull one and the whole weave shifts." She hugged the ledger to her chest. "You could lose the kitchen entirely. Or lose everything *around* the kitchen — Maren, the cottage, the road out. You won't know until it's done."

"I know."

"And you're still—"

"Wren." He turned to face her. In the grey light of the antechamber, with frost feathering up behind him like wings he hadn't asked for, he smiled — tired, crooked, real. "Everything I ever owned was sold before I had it. My soul, my name, my future. This is the first time in my life I get to *give* something instead of having it taken. Do you understand? Whatever it costs — it's mine to pay. Nobody's ever let me pay for anything myself before."

Wren blinked rapidly. "That's a horrible thing to find liberating."

"I'm a hedge-witch's runaway turned smuggler. My standards are extremely low."

The king stirred on his throne. When he spoke, his voice was barely a whisper of wind, and for once there was no performance in it.

"Challenger." His hollow eyes fixed on Gideon. "A word before the duel, which your keeper must not hear."

Wren hesitated — then, with visible effort, withdrew to the far end of the antechamber, though Gideon noticed she positioned herself where she could watch his face.

The king leaned forward. Ice crackled along his arms.

"Vesperine will offer you mercy at some point during the duel," he whispered. "A moment of hesitation. A truth that seems kinder than cruelty. Do not take it. She has killed nine challengers in five hundred years, and eight of them died accepting her kindness."

"And the ninth?"

The king's smile was a crack in old glass.

"The ninth was me," he said. "A long time ago. Before I understood that the kindest thing she ever offers is the trap." His gaze slid past Gideon, toward the doors of the feast-hall, where the courtiers were already gathering like weather. "Moonrise is coming, son-of-the-sold-mother. Speak your truths clean. And whatever she shows you about the woman who bore you—"

The great doors began to open. Cold poured through them, and with it the sound of the Court assembling, hundreds of wrong-jointed bodies settling into seats with a rustle like dead leaves.

"—remember that she loved you enough to lie to the devil himself," the king finished. "Now go. And challenger — *win*. Because if you fall, the winter does not stop at Thornfell."

Gideon walked toward the doors. Behind him, Wren fell into step, the ledger open against her forearm, her stylus already moving as she checked the cipher one last time.

"For luck," she murmured, and pressed something small into his palm.

He looked down. It was a scrap of dried onion skin, curled and papery and utterly ridiculous.

"You're joking."

"Brother Aldous always said a keeper travels with her kitchen." Her chin was up, her eyes bright, her hand steady on the book. "One more page, Gideon. Let's go write it."

The doors closed behind them, and the duel of names began.
