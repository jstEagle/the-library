## 14. Maren's Last Gift

They brought Maren down on the third day after the challenge was set.

Gideon knew it before anyone told him. He felt it the way you feel a door open somewhere in a large house — a draft across the back of his neck, a wrongness in the air of the spring-stratum where he and Wren had been hiding among the fossil flowers and the warm pale stone. The courtiers did not announce things like ordinary heralds. They simply arranged the world so that you learned what they wanted you to learn, at the moment they wanted you to learn it, and hated them a little more for the courtesy.

A moth-winged servant with a face borrowed from a fisherman long dead found him at the edge of the underground lake and bowed with its knees bending backward.

"The chancellor presents her compliments," it said, in a voice like wet gravel poured slowly. "She invites the challenger to view an asset recently acquired."

"Asset," Gideon repeated.

"The word is hers." The creature's borrowed eyes were kind, which was somehow worse than cruelty. Kindness from something wearing a dead man's face was like honey on a hook. "She thought you might wish to see it before the feast."

He went to find Wren first. She was sitting cross-legged on a shelf of stone above the lake, the ledger open on her knees, her lips moving through the cipher the way other people prayed. She had been doing that for three days — reading, cross-referencing, hunting for anything about the duel of names that Vesperine might have left out. There were shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose coins in.

"They've taken someone," Gideon said.

Wren looked up. She didn't ask how he knew. Three days of partnership had burned most of the explanations out of their conversations, which Gideon privately considered the mark of any decent friendship: you stopped needing to say the obvious parts.

"Who?"

"Maren."

The ledger closed with a small, final sound, like a bird folding its wings. Wren stood. "It's leverage," she said. "Vesperine wants you angry. An angry challenger makes mistakes in the duel. Speaks carelessly. Lies without meaning to."

"I know what she wants."

"Knowing it doesn't stop it working." Wren slung the ledger into the satchel at her hip and came down the stone shelf with the sure-footedness she'd learned in a lifetime of caves. "So we go in knowing it twice as hard. Come on, then. Let's go be furious *carefully*."

* * *

They were escorted up through the strata by two courtiers who walked with the smooth gliding gait of things that had never quite mastered joints. Gideon counted the turns out of habit — smuggler's discipline, Maren's discipline, drilled into him over seven years of cold cottages and colder lessons — and realized halfway up that counting was pointless. The Hollow Court rearranged its own geography the way a sleeper shifts under blankets. He could have mapped every corridor and it would have moved anyway, patient and polite, to put him exactly where the court wanted him.

The audience chamber where Vesperine received them was small, which surprised him. He had expected grandeur — pillars, thrones, the architecture of menace. Instead there was a round room of black stone hung with tapestries so old their pictures had faded into weather, and a table, and chairs, and on the table a tea service in white porcelain, steaming.

Vesperine sat pouring with the serenity of a woman who had never once been interrupted in her life.

"Gideon Blackwood." She gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit. You'll want to be seated for this."

"Where is she?"

"All in good time." Vesperine's face was beautiful the way winter is beautiful — flawless, and built entirely out of taking things away. She filled a second cup and pushed it toward the empty chair. "Tea? It's brewed from snowmelt off the crown of the mountain. The last honest water in Thornfell. Everything below us has been recycled through the court's... appetites... for centuries."

"I'm not drinking anything you pour."

"Sensible." She drank her own, unhurried. "Then let's not pretend this is hospitality. It's negotiation. You've claimed the right of challenge, and the old law binds me to answer — I chose those laws myself, four hundred years ago, when I still believed rules would protect people like me. A mistake I've had a very long time to regret." She set down her cup. "But laws only bind behavior, Gideon. Not preparation. And I have prepared."

The doors opened.

Two courtiers brought Maren in between them, walking — that was the horror of it, she was *walking*, upright, unresisting — and settled her into a chair at the side of the room with the gentle efficiency of servants arranging a cushion. Then they stepped back and folded their hands, and waited, and there was nothing left in the room to do but look at her.

Gideon looked.

Maren Osgood had been the most stubbornly *present* person he had ever known. Seven years under her roof, and he could not remember a single time she had failed to notice something — a lie in his voice, a charm gone sour, a storm coming over the pass three days early. Her attention had been a physical force. She'd had a way of looking at you that made you feel like a specimen pinned under glass, and he had spent his whole runaway life afterward flinching from anyone who looked at him too closely, because no one else ever looked that hard, that honestly, that much like they intended to keep you alive whether you liked it or not.

The woman in the chair looked at nothing.

Her eyes were open. That was the worst part. They were open and aimed vaguely at the middle distance, and behind them there was simply — absence. Not sleep. Sleep has weather in it; dreams move under the surface like fish. This was a window with the glass painted black. Her hands lay in her lap, and even her hands were different — loose, unguarded, the hands of a woman who had forgotten she owned anything worth holding onto.

"What did you take?" His voice came out low and rough.

"Interest," Vesperine said. "On the debt your mother defaulted on. The court is thorough, if nothing else. Your master owed a century of memory for sheltering a sold soul and concealing the contract. We collected." She tilted her head. "Well. Most of it. I confess I skimmed. There wasn't much worth keeping — hedge-craft, mostly. Herb-lore. The names of birds. A great deal of irritation."

"You emptied her."

"I *paid* her, in a sense. She lived comfortably for seventy years on memories she never earned. Now the account balances." Vesperine smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the cruelest trick yet. "This is what victory costs, challenger. I want you to understand that before the duel. Look at her. Really look. Because if you take the crown-sigil from me — if you win — this is the shape of everything afterward. Every soul freed is a debt unpaid. Every debt unpaid is a courtier unfed. And every courtier unfed..." She spread her hands elegantly. "Winter. Above us, right now, the snow is falling deeper than it has fallen in nine hundred years. The king will tell you the crown holds it back. I am telling you the crown *is* it. Either way, the arithmetic does not care who is telling the truth. Someone pays. It has always been someone pays."

Maren turned her head slightly, following some movement none of them could see.

Gideon sat down. He hadn't decided to; his legs simply ended the argument. He sat across from Vesperine with his hands flat on the black table and stared at the woman who had raised him, and felt the anger he had carried since he was seventeen years old — the anger that had put a hundred miles between them, that had fueled every smuggled charm and every bitter night — turn over inside him like soil.

Because here was the thing. He had spent eleven years hating Maren Osgood. Properly hating her, with structure and foundations, the way you hate a house you grew up in. She had worked him like a mule and taught him like a weapon and loved him, when she bothered, like a chore she resented needing done. He had a whole catalogue of grievances, alphabetized by year.

And now the court had stripped all of it away, and what was left was just a small grey woman in a chair, breathing, waiting for nothing, and Gideon discovered that the hatred had been load-bearing. Take it out and the whole building of him sagged.

"She kept one thing," Vesperine said quietly.

He looked up.

"When my collectors went through her, they found a single memory warded — hedge-work, crude, but stubborn. Old stubbornness. The oldest kind." Vesperine studied him with those winter eyes. "They couldn't crack it without destroying the vessel, and the vessel is leverage, so it survived. One memory, sealed in a knot of craft so simple my scholars are embarrassed by it. Would you like to know what it is?"

"No," said Gideon.

"Yes, you would."

"It's a trap. Everything you hand me is a trap."

"Of course it is." Vesperine refilled her cup, serene. "But traps work both ways, challenger. Consider: I show you her last memory, and you spend the hours before the duel thinking of nothing else. Or you refuse, and spend them wondering. Either way, you walk into the duel of names carrying something I gave you." She rose, smoothing her robes. "I'll leave you alone with her. The law requires the challenger be granted counsel before combat, and I am nothing if not lawful. Take whatever time you need."

At the door she paused. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I did not order her taken. The king did. He wanted to test whether mercy or rage moves you faster." Her smile flickered, genuine and terrible. "I'm curious which it will be myself."

* * *

The chamber emptied. The courtiers withdrew. Even the tea service vanished, porcelain and all, the way things did down here — not removed but *unremembered*, as though the room had briefly imagined it and thought better.

Gideon crossed to Maren's chair and knelt in front of it, the way he used to kneel at her hearth when she was teaching him sigilwork, close enough to feel the heat and get rapped on the knuckles for leaning in.

"Maren."

Nothing. Her breath went in and out, slow as tide.

"Maren Osgood. You old goat. Your stew was terrible. You knew it was terrible. You served it anyway and called it character-building." Nothing moved behind her eyes. "You told me once that the difference between a witch and a hedge-witch was that a hedge-witch had learned to be useful to people who couldn't pay. You said it like it was the proudest sentence in the language. I laughed at you. I shouldn't have laughed at you."

The fire in the little grate — there was a fire, the court indulged itself in details — popped softly.

"I ran because of you," he said. "Eleven years, and half of why I never went home was that I couldn't decide whether I wanted to scream at you or—" His voice caught. He pushed through it. "Or sit at your stupid hearth and eat your terrible stew. Both. I wanted both, and I couldn't have both, so I took neither, and that's the most hedge-witch thing I've ever done, isn't it. Refusing to choose until the choosing got done to me."

He reached out and took her hand. It lay in his like something set down.

And her fingers curled.

Just slightly. Just enough. Gideon went absolutely still, his heart slamming, as Maren Osgood's painted-black eyes turned — slowly, with the effort of a millwheel starting — and focused on his face.

"*Boy,*" she said.

The voice was rust and gravel and it was *hers*, and Gideon's eyes stung so fast he had to blink hard to see through them.

"Maren—"

"Don't." Her grip tightened on his hand with shocking strength, the strength of the woman who had once dragged him out of a rockslide by the collar. "Don't waste it on sorry. Listen. I haven't got — it's like holding water, boy, I can feel the rest of me running out through my fingers, I've maybe a minute of being *me* before the knot slips. So listen."

"I'm listening."

"That memory they couldn't take." Her mouth twitched toward the ghost of her old crooked smile. "Warded it myself, forty years ago, when your mother first came to my door. In case. Hedge-witches keep receipts." She drew a breath, and her eyes — for one moment, one impossible moment — were sharp and present and full of grief. "You want to know why she sold you. Everyone wants to know why. Here's why, boy, and I'll only say it once, so hold it tight."

"Your mother didn't come to the Hollow Court to buy her escape." The words came slow, each one costing her. "She came to buy *yours*. There was a fever moving through Thornfell that winter — the red cough. You were six months old and already turning. I saw her carry you through my door blue-lipped and burning, and the courtiers were already at the village edge, collecting, and she knew — she *knew* — there was only one coin they'd take from a dying woman with nothing."

Gideon's throat had closed. "No."

"Aye." Maren's smile was fierce now, blazing through the ruin of her. "She didn't sell your soul to save herself. She sold it to buy you a *body that would live*. The contract was written so the debt fell due on your twenty-eighth year — adulthood, a life fully lived first. That was her price. That was her *term*, boy, negotiated on her deathbed with the coldest creatures under this mountain, and she held them to every word of it. You didn't get stolen. You got *bought time*. Twenty-eight years she bought you, with the only thing she had."

"But the grimoire—"

"Was written by men who only read the debit column." Her grip was failing; he could feel it, the strength ebbing like a tide going out. "She ran because staying meant watching you die. She ran because the bargain required her to live far enough away that the court couldn't collect interest on her grief. She ran because loving you and leaving you were the same act, and she knew you'd hate her for it, and she paid that too. Everything, boy. She paid *everything*."

"Maren—"

"One more thing." Her eyes were dimming, the millwheel slowing, but she fought it, jaw set, stubborn to the last grain. "The ward. On the memory. When it breaks — and it's breaking now — the memory passes to the next of blood. That's how hedge-craft works. Receipts transfer." Her crooked smile blazed one final time. "So catch, boy. Your mother's face. Her voice, singing the fever-song. The weight of you asleep on her shoulder. It's yours. It was always meant to be yours. Happy birthday, twenty-eight years late."

The knot broke.

Gideon felt it happen — not in his ears or his eyes but somewhere behind his sternum, a seam opening, and then it *poured* into him: a kitchen with frost ferns on the window, a woman with dark hair and laugh-lines and chapped hands, humming a tune in a minor key while a bundle of blankets squirmed against her collarbone; the smell of woodsmoke and milk; a voice saying, clear as the day it was spoken, *"Grow up strong, my heart. Grow up strong and don't you dare come find me."*

His mother's face.

He had it. He had it, and it was warm, and it hurt worse than anything the court had ever done to him, and he would not have traded it for the crown-sigil and every soul in the treasury combined.

When he could see again, Maren's hand lay quiet in his. Her eyes had gone back to the painted black. But her mouth was still curved, faintly, in that crooked ghost of a smile, and Gideon chose — freely, with no one watching — to believe that somewhere in the emptied dark, some smallest part of her knew she'd finally gotten through to him.

He laid her hand gently in her lap. Stood. Rolled his shoulders the way she'd taught him before every hard working day.

Above, faint through a thousand feet of mountain, thunder rolled — or something like thunder. The winter was deepening. The feast was coming. Vesperine was waiting with her truths like drawn knives, and somewhere past her stood the dying king and the crown and the choice of a lifetime.

Gideon wiped his face with his sleeve, once, briskly, the way Maren would have approved of.

"Right," he said to the empty room, to the small grey woman, to the mother whose face he carried now like a coal cupped in both hands. "Let's go win a duel."

The doors opened behind him, and the moth-winged servant bowed, backward-kneed and courteous, and said:

"The court will see the challenger now."
