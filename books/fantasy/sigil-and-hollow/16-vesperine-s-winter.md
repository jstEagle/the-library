## 16. Vesperine's Winter

The duel-ground was still smoking where Gideon's truths had landed.

Vesperine lay on her back on the obsidian floor of the feast-hall, and for a long moment she did nothing at all. It was, she reflected, the first time in four hundred years that she had been permitted to do nothing. The crown-sigil had rolled from her brow and come to rest against the leg of the king's throne, glowing faintly, patient as a debt. The courtiers had drawn back to the walls in a rustling ring of borrowed faces, watching her the way carrion birds watch a thing that might not be carrion after all.

She should have stood. She should have spoken some last elegant cruelty, gathered what remained of her power, and made the boy pay for every inch of his victory.

Instead she started laughing.

It came out of her like water out of a cracked jug — rusty, unlovely, unstoppable. She laughed until her borrowed ribs ached, until the nearest courtier took an alarmed step backward, until Gideon Blackwood himself came cautiously into her field of vision, sword still drawn, looking exactly like a man who had won a fight and did not know what to do with the room afterward.

"Are you dying?" he asked. "Is this a dying thing? Wren said you might do a dying thing."

"I am *defeated*," Vesperine said, "which is worse, and considerably funnier." She pushed herself up onto her elbows. Her form was flickering now — the beautiful chancellor's face slipping here and there into something older, thinner, a child's face worn over centuries like a coat too small. She let it slip. There seemed little point in holding it anymore. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone defeated me at anything?"

"I'm going to guess four hundred years."

"Four hundred and eleven," she said. "But who's counting." She sat up properly, wincing. Truths burned the speaker when they were lies; they also, it turned out, bruised the listener when they were true. She would carry the marks of his words for a while. Good. Let him see them. "You're supposed to take the sigil now, by the way. That's how this works. Victor claims the crown. I'd get up and hand it to you with proper ceremony, but my knees have stopped believing in me."

Gideon didn't move toward it. She had expected that too. He was, annoyingly, exactly as advertised — the ledger had promised her a stubborn man, and the ledger had delivered.

"You're going to tell me something now," he said. "Some new trap."

"Almost certainly," Vesperine agreed pleasantly. "But that doesn't mean it isn't also true. The two things are not mutually exclusive, whatever your prophecy-keeper tells you." She looked past him to where Wren stood at the edge of the courtiers' ring, ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, watching Vesperine with the particular hatred of someone who has spent her whole life being right about terrible things. "Come closer, keeper. You'll want to hear this. You'll want to check it against your book, and find, to your horror, that the book agrees with me."

Wren did not come closer. But she did not leave either, which was its own kind of answer.

Vesperine got to her feet — slowly, honestly, letting them see the cost of standing — and looked around the feast-hall one more time. The long tables. The cold candles. The courtiers with their wrong-jointed courtesy and their stolen faces. Four hundred years of her work, arranged just so.

"Do you know what I was," she said, "before any of this?"

"A liar," said Gideon.

"The first sold child," said Vesperine, at the same moment, and watched the words land on him like weather.

* * *

They gave her a chair. It was absurd — the victor of a duel of names, offering the defeated a chair — but Gideon did it without thinking, dragging one of the feast-benches close and saying "sit down before you fall down," and something in Vesperine's chest went sideways at the ordinariness of it. No one had offered her furniture in three centuries. Kings commanded. Courtiers flattered. Mortals begged. Nobody simply dragged over a bench.

She sat. She told them.

"I was born in a village at the mountain's foot, before Thornfell had a name, before the passes had roads. My mother sold me before my birth — does that sound familiar, Blackwood? — to a power older than the Hollow Court, because the winter was already coming even then. A true winter. Not weather. A hunger in the world's bones, moving south, eating everything green. The old kings fought it and lost. The old priests prayed at it and were eaten mid-prayer. And then something in the mountain learned a trick: that a soul could be made into fuel. That warmth could be *bought*, if you were willing to buy it with people."

"The crown," Wren said quietly.

"The crown," Vesperine agreed. "Or rather, what the crown holds. Thousands of souls, burning slow, pushing the long winter back year after year. Your soul is in there somewhere, Blackwood. Did you feel it? Little lamp, little flame. They burn so *prettily*."

"And the king," Gideon said. His voice had gone flat, careful. She recognized the tone; she had invented half its vocabulary. "He told me the winter was coming regardless. That the crown merely held it off."

"He told you what keeps you useful," Vesperine said. "Yes. He's very good at that. He taught me everything I know about lying, which is why I'm better at it than he is now — the student surpasses the master, et cetera, you may applaud later." She leaned forward. "Here is what your dying king did not tell you, hedge-thief. The long winter is not coming. The long winter is *here*. It has been here for four hundred years, held at bay by nothing but the burning of souls — and the crown does not push it back. The crown only decides *where it goes*. Every soul we feed it buys another spring for the valleys below. Another harvest. Another generation of children who grow up never knowing they live inside a furnace's shadow."

The hall was silent except for the small sounds of the courtiers shifting along the walls, hungry and attentive.

"So when you shattered nothing yet," Vesperine went on, "and when you stand there looking at me with your righteous face, planning — oh, don't think I can't see it, you have 'I'm going to break the wheel' written across you in letters a mile high — understand what breaking the wheel means. Four thousand lamps go out. The winter comes down off the peaks all at once. Not slowly. Not gently. All at once, like a held breath finally released. The valleys freeze in a day. The lowlands in a week. Everything your smuggler friends carry and sell and steal to survive — gone under white. Every soul in the treasury dies screaming, including yours, and the world above freezes with them." She smiled, and let the smile be tired instead of cruel, which cost her more than the duel had. "That is what freedom costs, Blackwood. Your king has been telling you it's free. He's been telling everyone it's free for four hundred years. *Freedom*, he calls it, when what he means is *someone else will pay*."

"That's not—" Wren started.

"Check your book," Vesperine said mildly. "Go on. The oldest pages. What did the first prophecy-keepers write about the winter? Not the court. Not the debt. The *winter*. What did they call it?"

Wren's hands tightened on the ledger. Vesperine watched her open it — watched her turn pages with increasing speed, back and back toward the beginning, and saw the exact moment the girl found it, because all the blood left her face at once.

"What," said Gideon. "What does it say."

"It says," Wren said, and her voice came out very small, "*the cold beneath the mountain shall be fed or it shall walk.*" She looked up. "It doesn't say anything about ending it. There's no — there's no page about ending it. I always thought that meant hope. That it meant we just hadn't found the answer yet." She closed the book slowly. "It might mean there isn't one."

"Oh, there's an answer," Vesperine said. "There's always an answer. The answer is arithmetic. Feed it, and it sleeps. Stop feeding it, and it wakes. The only question the crown has ever answered is *who burns*." She spread her hands — flickering, child-faced, ancient. "And I decided, four hundred years ago, that it would not be the world. Let it be the sold ones. Let it be us. We were sold anyway. At least this way our burning buys breakfasts. At least this way it buys *something*."

* * *

Gideon walked away from her then. Not far — just to the far side of the hall, where he stood with his back turned, doing the thing men did when the ground moved under their certainties and they didn't want witnesses to the wobbling.

Vesperine used the time to take stock of herself, which was grim. The duel had stripped her of most of her borrowed strength. Without the sigil, the courtiers' obedience was already fraying — she could feel them at the edges of her awareness, no longer kneeling in their minds, merely waiting, the way wolves wait. In an hour, perhaps two, they would remember that they had eaten chancellors before. She ought to flee while she could. She ought to bargain. She ought to do any of the ten thousand ruthless things that had kept her alive since she was a lamp in a dark place.

Instead she watched Gideon's shoulders and waited for him to turn around, and hated herself a little for hoping he'd ask the next question, and hated herself more for knowing exactly which question it would be.

He turned around. "You said the king created the debt-system."

"I said he perfected it," Vesperine corrected. "The hunger was here first. The trick of feeding it souls — yes, that was his. He was the first to learn it, and the last to admit what it cost, because admitting it would mean admitting that four hundred years of his reign were built on a lie he tells himself every morning: that he is the mountain's savior, and not its most successful parasite." She tilted her head. "Why do you ask?"

"Because he told me the winter would devour the world if the crown fell. And you're telling me the same thing. And you're both liars." He came back toward her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not righteousness, not fear. Arithmetic. *Her* arithmetic, turned around and pointed at her. "But you just told me the crown doesn't hold the winter back. It *aims* it. So which is it, Chancellor? Does the crown save the world, or does it spend the world?"

Ah. There it was.

Vesperine considered lying. It would have been easy; it was always easy; it was the one skill that had never abandoned her. But she was tired down to the wick, and there was a strange luxury in exhaustion — the permission to stop performing.

"Both," she said. "That's the genius of it. That's the horror of it. The crown feeds the hunger and aims the hunger, and because it aims it away from the living lands, it *is* salvation, in the way a dam is salvation — right up until you notice what the reservoir drowned." She rubbed her eyes with a child's small hands. "I have spent four hundred years telling myself the drowning was necessary. Do you know what happens to a person who spends four hundred years telling themselves a thing like that?"

"No," said Gideon. "What?"

"They get very good at it," Vesperine said. "And then a smuggler with a stolen grimoire beats them in front of the whole court, and they sit on a bench, and they find out the good-at-it was the only thing holding them together." She laughed again, briefly, without much humor in it. "Ask your questions, Blackwood. I can feel my courtiers remembering their teeth. We haven't got all night."

"One question." He crouched down to her level, which she suspected he did to everyone — masters, kings, dying things — and which she resented and appreciated in equal measure. "My mother. Sold me before I was born. You were sold before yours. Is there a version of this where I end up like you?"

Vesperine looked at him for a long moment. Around them, the courtiers had begun to drift inward, unhurried, faces flickering between their borrowed shapes and the jointless things beneath.

"Yes," she said honestly. "If you win. If you take the crown and keep it and rule wisely and burn souls carefully and tell yourself every morning that it's necessary — in four hundred years, you'll be me. Better dressed, perhaps. Worse singing voice." She stood, joints cracking. "And if you lose the crown to the courtiers, the winter walks, and everyone dies. And if you shatter it — well." She smiled at him, and for once there was nothing performed in it at all, only a very old tiredness and, buried deep, something that might once have been hope. "Then you'll be the first person in four centuries to find out what the truth costs. The king says it's cheap. I say it's everything. One of us is lying."

"Which one?"

"That," said Vesperine, "is the question you'll have to ask him yourself. And Blackwood —" she nodded toward the throne, where the old king sat translucent and silent, watching them with eyes like frost on a window, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle — "when you ask him, watch his hands. Liars keep their hands still. He hasn't moved them in four hundred years. Ask yourself why a dying man works so hard at seeming calm."

Behind her, the nearest courtier took a step forward, smiling with a face that belonged to a smuggler who had gone missing on the pass eleven winters ago.

"And now," Vesperine said, "if you'll excuse me, I believe I'm about to be interest."
