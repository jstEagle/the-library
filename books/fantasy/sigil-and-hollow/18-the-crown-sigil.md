## 18. The Crown-Sigil

The king's brow was cold as a riverbed stone, and lighter than it should have been.

Gideon had imagined this moment a hundred ways on the long climb up through the strata — triumphant, terrified, bloody. He had not imagined it would feel like lifting a dead leaf off a grave. The crown-sigil came away from the Hollow King's forehead with a sound like a sigh let out after centuries of holding its breath, and for one heartbeat the whole throne room was silent enough that Gideon could hear the snow falling somewhere far above them, through miles of mountain, out of season and out of patience.

Then the silence broke, and everything knelt.

It started at the edges of the hall. A courtier with a heron's neck and a borrowed merchant's face folded down onto the flagstones like wet paper. Then another. Then a dozen, then all of them, the whole glittering wrong-jointed court going down in a rustling wave, their borrowed faces pressed to the floor, their too-many fingers splayed flat. Vesperine's chancellor-captains, her feast-lords, the things that had hunted him across the pass and stripped Maren's memories like fruit off a branch — kneeling. Not because he commanded it. Because the thing in his hand did, and they could not tell the difference between the sigil and the man who held it.

"Oh," Gideon said. His voice came out very small in the enormous room. "Oh, that's — no."

The sigil sat in his palm like a circlet of black ice, thin as a coin, older than the mountain that had grown around it. It should have been heavy. It wasn't. That was the first lie it told him, and he recognized it as a lie only because Wren had spent three weeks teaching him to count the ledger's ciphers, and ciphers were lies with arithmetic in them.

*You could put it on,* said the hunger, in a voice that was not a voice so much as an absence of every other sound. *It would be so easy.*

Gideon had been offered easy things before. His mother had been offered an easy escape and taken it, and the bill had come due twenty-six years later in a ruined chapel with his own name written in the margin. Maren had been offered easy interest and paid it in memories until there was nothing left of her but courtesy. Easy, in the Hollow Court, was just the price tag turned around.

He knew all of this. He knew it the way you know a stove is hot.

And still his arm rose.

* * *

Here is what the sigil showed him, in the space between one heartbeat and the next:

Thornfell in summer. Real summer, not the spring-stratum's pale imitation — green to the treeline, the passes open, smuggler roads gone honest because there was no more need for smuggling when the mountain stopped eating people. Himself, older maybe, standing in a doorway with sawdust on his sleeves, building something instead of stealing it. And faces. Faces everywhere, whole and remembered: Maren laughing over her kettle, his mother's face — the one she'd given him in the flicker of her last kept memory — clear as a window, Wren beside him not because a ledger said she must be but because she'd chosen, freely, the way he'd chosen —

*All of it,* the sigil murmured. *Kept. Every memory. Nothing paid. You wear me, and I hold back the winter, and no one has to lose anything at all.*

"That," Gideon said aloud, to the empty air, to the kneeling court, to himself, "is the most beautiful lie anyone has ever told me."

But his fingers were curling around the sigil's edge, and the edge fit them perfectly, the way the door beneath the falls had fit his hand, the way every trap in his life had been tailored to him by people who'd known about him before he was born. The metal — if it was metal — was warming now. It wanted to be worn. It had been worn for so long that being held was, for it, a kind of thirst.

He thought of the treasury. Thousands of small burning lamps, each one a soul somebody had sold or had sold for them, each one waiting in the dark for a buyer who never came. If he wore the crown, the lamps stayed where they were. The system stayed. The market of small debts stayed, and the smugglers who'd forgotten their friends' names stayed forgetting, and some other child someday would get a date written beside their name in some other book, and the whole machinery would keep turning, warm and fed and eternal.

If he wore the crown, he would be the machinery.

And the terrible thing — the thing he would carry out of this room if he carried anything out of it — was that for three full heartbeats, he wanted to be.

Not for power. He'd never much wanted power; power got you hanged in the low country and eaten in the high ones. He wanted it because wearing the crown meant the debt stopped moving. No more bills arriving. No more margins with dates in them. He could stand still, finally, in a life where he'd been running since he was fourteen years old, and everything he loved would be *held*, held forever, held the way the winter held the mountain —

"Held," Gideon whispered. "That's what a cage is."

The courtiers stirred at the word, a ripple through the kneeling ranks, and somewhere among them he heard Vesperine's voice say, softly, almost kindly: "Yes."

* * *

She stood apart from the others, near the dais steps, and she had not knelt. Of course she hadn't. She was the first sold child; the sigil's hunger was her native tongue. Her borrowed face was composed, but her hands — her real hands, the wrong-jointed ones she didn't bother hiding anymore — were clasped tight, and Gideon realized with a lurch that she was afraid.

Not of him. For him. Or perhaps those were the same thing, down here.

"You see it now," she said. "Good. The king never let any of them see it. He wore it until it wore him, and he called that sacrifice, and we called it mercy, because what else do you call a cage when you've lived your whole life inside it?" She took one step closer, and the courtiers flinched in unison, a hundred borrowed faces wincing at once. "Put it on, Gideon Blackwood. Be kinder than he was. Feed them gently. Let the market close slowly. You could spend a century making this place gentler, and no one would ever have to break."

"And the world above?"

"The world above has survived a long winter already." Her voice did not waver, which was how he knew she was lying, or grieving, or both. "It would survive another."

"It wouldn't." He heard his own steadiness and was surprised by it. "You told me yourself. The crown's hunger *is* the winter. Every year it eats deeper. You're not offering me a kingdom, Vesperine. You're offering me the wolf's job."

"I'm offering you the only door that doesn't cost a piece of you." And there it was — the crack in her, the first sold child looking at the boy who might actually walk free, and wanting it so badly it looked like hatred. "Everyone who breaks the crown pays. You know the terms. You've read the ledger. Is that freedom? Trading one debt for another? At least mine comes with a roof."

Gideon looked down at the sigil.

It had gone quiet. That frightened him more than the hunger had. It lay in his palm, patient, certain, the way a fisherman is patient with a hooked fish that hasn't yet pulled. It didn't need to show him visions anymore. It had shown him the doorway, and it could feel his feet on the threshold, and it knew — the way Maren had known, the way the ledger had known, the way everyone in his life had always known — exactly what he was made of and exactly how much pressure that made.

His thumb moved toward the inner curve of the circlet.

One inch. Half an inch. The metal rose to meet him, eager, and he felt the first whisper of what wearing it would be — not pain, never pain, just *ease*, the whole weight of the world sliding off his shoulders and onto a band of black ice, command flowing into him like warmth into cold hands, the court rising at his merest thought, the winter folding itself obediently around his will —

"Gideon."

Wren's voice. From the doorway behind him, breathless, singed at the edges from the wards, alive — alive, which the ledger had promised otherwise, and here she was anyway, stubborn past all prophecy.

"Don't," she said.

* * *

Later — much later, in the spring-stratum, over burnt tea — she would ask him what he'd felt in that last moment, and he would try to explain it honestly, and fail, and settle for: "It felt like coming home. That's how I knew."

But in the moment itself there was no time for philosophy. There was only her voice, cracking slightly on the second syllable of his name, and the sigil's grip tightening on his hand like a lover who'd felt the bed shift, and the choice — his choice, the one thing in his entire sold and bartered and margin-annotated life that had never belonged to anyone else — narrowing to a single point of light.

He thought about his mother. Not her face — that memory was already promised elsewhere, already spoken for, already given. He thought about what Maren had told him: *she ran because staying meant watching you be sold slowly.* She hadn't saved him. But she'd tried to buy him time, and time was the only currency Gideon had ever really understood.

The crown offered him eternity. His mother had bought him years.

Years were enough. Years were everything. Years were the point.

"No," Gideon said.

And he opened his hand.

The sigil screamed. There was no other word for it — a soundless scream that bent the torchlight and made the kneeling courtiers cover their borrowed ears, a scream of pure offended appetite, because in all its long hungry existence nothing had ever held it and refused it. It clung to his palm with hooks he couldn't see. It poured visions faster, thicker — the green Thornfell, the sawdust, the faces, *everything kept, nothing lost* — and Gideon stood in the flood of it with tears freezing on his cheeks and said, again, quietly, the hardest word he had ever learned:

"No. I don't want everything kept. I want some things *given*."

The hooks loosened.

Just slightly. Just enough. And in that sliver of space, Wren reached him — crossing the throne room at a dead run, ledger under one arm, hair full of ash, absolutely furious — and closed both her hands over his fist around the sigil, so that they held it together, four hands, two heartbeats, one choice made twice.

"Took you long enough," she panted.

"You try refusing a magic crown sometime. See how chatty you stay."

"I heard you talking to it. You negotiate with everything. You'd haggle with a landslide."

"It's a flaw," Gideon agreed. His arm was shaking. Her hands were colder than the sigil. "Come on. Before it remembers it's stronger than I am."

They turned together toward the doors — toward the deep places, the crown's heart, the final seal that waited below with its terrible price — and behind them the court began, slowly, joint by joint, face by face, to rise.

Vesperine watched them go. She did not kneel, and she did not follow, and when Gideon glanced back at the threshold, he saw her standing alone among her risen courtiers with an expression he had no name for yet — though he would learn it soon, in the chapter of her story he had not yet read.

"Blackwood," she called after him.

He stopped. Wren's grip tightened on his sleeve.

"When you shatter it," Vesperine said, "and it asks you for the payment — and it will ask, it always asks — remember that it cannot take what is freely given. It can only receive it." Her borrowed face smiled, and for once the smile reached whatever lived underneath. "Make sure you mean it."

The doors of the throne room closed behind them like a held breath released, and the stairs went down, and down, and down, into the dark where the crown's heart waited — and where, Gideon now understood with a clarity that felt like cold water, something was going to have to be given, and meant, and lost.

He just didn't yet know whose it would be.
