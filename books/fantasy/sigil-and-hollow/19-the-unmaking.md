## 19. The Unmaking

The crown's heart was not a room so much as a wound.

Gideon felt it before he saw it — a pull behind his sternum, gentle and insistent, like a hand tugging the hem of his coat. The passage had narrowed to a corridor of black glass, and at its end hung the sigil itself: a circlet of pale metal, floating in nothing, turning slowly as though it had all the time in the world.

Which, he supposed, it did.

"It's smaller than I imagined," Wren said beside him. Her voice came out hushed, flattened by the strange acoustics of the place. "All that hunger, and it's the size of a hat."

"Most terrible things are," Gideon said. "My master's goat could have eaten a village's worth of curses and still looked disappointed about dinner."

That earned him half a smile — thin, but real. She'd been rationing her smiles since the spring-stratum, since she'd told him what the ledger demanded of her, and he'd learned not to waste them.

The sigil turned. And turned. And somewhere beneath the turning, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something *noticed* them.

"Gideon." Wren touched his sleeve. "Before we do this. I need you to know which memory I'm giving."

He looked at her. In the sigil's light her face was silvered, young and old at once, the way she always looked when she spoke of the ledger — like a woman carrying a book heavier than her body.

"You don't have to tell me," he said.

"I want to." She took a breath. "It's the morning my mother taught me the cipher. I couldn't have been more than five. She sat me on her knee and wrote my first name in the old letters, and she said, *'This is yours. No one can take this from you.'*" Wren's laugh cracked in the middle. "She was wrong about a great many things, but I'd like to keep believing that one. So I'm choosing it. If I have to lose something, I choose the moment I believed hardest. Let the seal crack on that."

"Why that one?" His throat was tight. "Why not something small? A meal. A rainy afternoon."

"Because it has to be freely given, and it has to cost." She met his eyes. "You taught me that, actually. Back in the tunnels, when you said smugglers' bargains only hold if both sides bleed a little. You were insufferable about it."

"I'm frequently insufferable. It's one of my better qualities."

"Gideon."

"I know." He took her hands. They were cold — everything down here was cold — but they held on like they meant it. "I know. Go on, then. And when it's done, I'll tell you your name back. All of it. Every letter."

For a moment she just breathed. Then she stepped forward into the sigil's light, lifted her chin the way she did before reciting from the ledger, and spoke:

"I give this willingly. No debt. No trade. A gift."

The crown-sigil flared.

And Wren made a sound he would hear for the rest of his life — not a scream, nothing so simple — a sound like a door opening in an empty house, like air rushing into a place that had been sealed for years. The light poured into her mouth and eyes and the palms of her raised hands, and above her head, visible for one impossible instant, a small shape rose: a memory, whole and shining, a woman's knee and a child's finger tracing letters of light.

Then it was gone. Taken into the seal.

The sigil shuddered. Across its surface, hairline fractures bloomed like frost on a windowpane.

One seal left.

* * *

Wren swayed. Gideon caught her before the dark glass floor could, and for a moment they stood there in the wreckage of the light, her weight against his chest, her breath coming fast and shallow.

"Do you still know your name?" he asked quietly.

"Wren." She tested it like a loose tooth. "Just... Wren. There was more. There was—" Her brow furrowed. "There was a morning. Someone was sitting—"

"Don't force it." He kept his voice steady even though nothing in him felt steady. "I told you I'd carry it. Later. When we're out. I'll tell you every letter of it, and you can decide whether to believe me."

"That's a terrible system."

"It's the only system I've got."

She laughed — wet, unsteady, but a laugh — and pushed herself upright. Above them, the sigil spun faster now, its fractures spreading, and the pull behind Gideon's sternum grew teeth.

"The last seal," Wren said. "It needs you."

"I gathered."

"It needs your choice. Freely made. That's what the ledger says — *the court cannot buy what is given away.*" She frowned. "But it doesn't say how. It doesn't say what form the choice takes."

"No," Gideon agreed. "Books are like that. My master's grimoires never told you where the beeswax was, either. Just *'anoint with wax'* and let you figure out the rest."

He stepped forward alone.

Up close, the sigil was worse than a hat. Up close he could see faces in the metal — or the suggestion of faces, the way clouds suggest things — thousands of them, pressed thin, turning with the turning. Souls. Lamps. Debts collected across centuries, each one filed and burning and *owned.*

And threaded through all of it, familiar as his own handwriting, was him. The piece of himself he'd been missing without ever knowing he was missing it. He could feel it now, the way you feel a tooth that's been pulled — the ache of a shape that should be full.

*Take it,* the sigil whispered, in a voice like his own mother's might have been. *Put it on. Everything comes back. Everything is yours. You'll never be sold again.*

"There it is," Gideon said aloud. "The hard sell."

"Gideon?" Wren's voice, sharp with alarm.

"It's fine. It's doing the thing Vesperine does. The thing the king does. Everyone down here wants to make you an offer." He raised his hand toward the spinning crown, palm open, and the old sigil-mark on his skin — the brand his mother's bargain had left there before he was born — began to glow. "Here's mine."

He thought of Maren's cottage, dim now in his memory, the smell of bread gone vague as a rumor. He thought of his mother's face, which Maren had given him and which he had already spent, in part, getting this far. He thought of the Market of Small Debts, of Tam the smuggler looking through him with polite blank eyes. He thought of a girl on a chapel step showing him a ledger with his name in it, and how he'd laughed, and how she hadn't, and how glad he was — *freely, gladly, no debt, no trade* — that she hadn't.

The court could buy memories. Years. Names. First kisses. It had bought his soul before he drew breath.

But it had never once, in all its long hungry centuries, managed to buy a choice. Choices were the one coin that only worked if you gave it away.

"I choose," Gideon said, "to break it."

He closed his fist around the crown-sigil.

* * *

From outside — from anywhere at all — it must have looked like nothing. A man standing in a dark place, holding a small bright thing.

Inside, it was the end of winter.

The sigil resisted. Of course it did; it had been resisting its own unmaking since before Thornfell had a name, feeding on ten thousand sold souls, projecting its hunger outward as frost and famine and calling the bill someone else's. It poured itself against Gideon's grip like floodwater against a door — offered him his mother's face, his master's voice, whole decades of his own life back, unspent, untouched —

*Freely given,* he thought, and squeezed harder. *You can't match it. You never learned how.*

The fractures spread. The faces in the metal opened their mouths — and did not scream. They *sighed.* Ten thousand sighs at once, the sound of a held breath finally released, and the crown-sigil came apart in his hands like ice on the first warm day of thaw.

Light went up through the mountain.

Not down. Not outward. *Up* — a thousand lamps igniting at once, souls streaming free through strata of stone and reversed season, autumn and summer and spring lit from below like lanterns rising off a dark sea. Gideon stood in the heart of it with empty hands and felt the ache behind his sternum fill — sudden, scalding, *whole* — his soul coming home to him after a lifetime of renting rooms in someone else's treasury.

He heard Wren shouting something joyful and wordless. He heard the mountain itself groan, long and low, like an old man standing up from a chair he'd sat in too long.

And then, everywhere at once, it began to snow upward.

* * *

Wren had seen wonders. She had spent her life with her nose in a prophetic ledger; wonder was practically her profession.

Nothing had prepared her for this.

The freed souls rose through the caverns like snowfall run backward — flakes of soft gold light drifting up past her, past the shattered vaults of the crown's heart, up through the market where mortals had traded their years for warmth, up through the frozen falls and the smuggler tunnels and the black throat of the mountain, toward the sky. Some of them paused as they passed her. One brushed her cheek, warm as a hand, and for half a heartbeat she smelled bread baking and heard a woman laughing and knew — *knew*, the way you know a thing you've lost and found again — that somewhere above, a smuggler named Tam was going to wake with a start and remember a friend he'd forgotten he had.

"Oh," she said. Just that. Just *oh.*

Beside her, Gideon stood with his fists still half-closed, watching the light go up, tears cutting clean lines through the grime on his face. He wasn't sobbing. He was smiling the way people smile at funerals that were also weddings — wrecked and radiant at once.

"Look at them go," he said hoarsely. "Every single one. Nobody's keeping the books anymore."

"Someone will have to write this down," Wren said, and then laughed at herself, because her ledger was upstairs in a ruined chapel, and every page of it had gone blank the moment the crown broke, and she had never in her life been so happy to be unemployed.

The last of the lamps rose past them. And in its wake, drifting slower than the rest, came one small light that did not rise.

It hovered at Wren's shoulder. Waiting.

She knew what it was before she understood it — the way you know rain is coming by the smell. The memory she'd given. Her mother's knee, the cipher, *this is yours, no one can take this from you.* The seal was broken. The price was paid. And the mountain, having taken what it needed, was handing the change back.

"Go on," she whispered to it.

The light settled against her chest like a bird coming home to roost, and the morning came flooding back — the cold kitchen, the flour on her mother's hands, the old letters glowing under her own small finger. Wren put her hand over her heart and laughed and cried in roughly equal measure.

"Well?" Gideon asked. "Still yours?"

"Still mine." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "No one could take it. Turns out even I couldn't, permanently."

"Funny thing about gifts," Gideon said. "They come back around."

Above them, far above, the mountain rumbled again — deeper now, structural, the sound of a great weight shifting. Dust sifted from the ceiling of the crown's heart. Somewhere overhead, she suspected, a very old translucent king was discovering what happened to a throne built on debts when every debt was called paid at once.

"We should probably be leaving," Wren said.

"We should probably be running," Gideon corrected, already moving, catching her hand as he went. "There's a difference. Leaving is dignified. Running is survivable."

They ran together, up through the springing light, while behind and below them the Hollow Court — hollow now in truth, hollowed clean through — let out its last long winter breath.

And the first snow of a true spring began to fall.
