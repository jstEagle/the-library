## 20. What He Chose to Keep

Gideon woke to the smell of snow and the sound of his own breathing, which was, he decided after some consideration, a very good thing to wake up to.

He was lying on the mountainside above Thornfell, on his back in a hollow of heather gone silver with frost, and the sky overhead was doing something it had no business doing in midwinter: lightening. Not the bruised grey of the endless winter — that flat, pressed-down grey that had hung over the passes for as long as anyone could remember — but an actual dawn, gold at the edges, with clouds moving through it like ships.

He lay there a while and took inventory.

That was what Maren had taught him first, back when he'd been a boy too stubborn to learn anything gently. *When you don't know where you are, take inventory. Hands, feet, breath, name.* He flexed his fingers. They worked. His ribs ached from where Vesperine's truths had struck him like thrown stones during the duel, but they were whole ribs. His breath came easy.

His name came last, and slower.

*Gideon Blackwood.*

It arrived, but it arrived alone. And that was when he understood, lying in the frost with the dawn coming up over Thornfell, what he had paid.

He sat up carefully, the way you sit up when you suspect the room might argue with you.

The memories were not gone so much as *hollowed*. He could feel the shapes of them, the way you can feel the shape of a tooth that isn't there anymore. His mother's face — he reached for it and found only warmth. A warmth shaped like a face, like arms, like a voice humming something he couldn't quite catch, but no features, no color, no name. He knew she had loved him. He could not have picked her out of a crowd now if the crowd were made entirely of her.

Maren's cottage was worse, or perhaps just sadder. He remembered the cottage the way you remember a house from a story someone told you once. There had been a crooked chimney. There had been herbs drying in bundles from the rafters. There had been a cat, probably — Maren seemed like a cat person, and something in him insisted on this with great confidence and zero evidence. But the door was blank in his memory. The garden had no flowers in it, or any flowers at all, whichever version was true.

And yet.

Here was the strange part, the part he kept turning over like a coin that shouldn't be legal tender: he did not feel robbed. He felt — he searched for the word, tested several, discarded them — *lighter*. Lighter by whole years. Lighter by the weight of every grudge he'd carried down that mountain, every night he'd spent hating a woman who sold him before he drew breath, every bitter mile of a runaway life built on the foundation of "she never wanted me."

She had wanted him. He knew that now, absolutely, the way you know your own heartbeat. She had traded his soul because the alternative was both their souls, and she had spent her life trying to buy it back one smuggled charm at a time. He didn't remember her face. He remembered — no, he *knew*, which was somehow better and worse — that her face had been worth running toward, all those years he'd spent running away.

"Ah," he said aloud, to the heather, because there was no one else. "So that's the price."

The mountain agreed with him by being beautiful about it.

* * *

Thornfell was thawing.

Not melting — nothing so vulgar. It was *thawing* the way a held breath releases. Snow slid from the pines in soft slumps. Water moved under the ice in the streams, audible now, chuckling to itself like a merchant counting honest coin. And everywhere around him, rising off the mountainside in the early light, souls.

There was no other word for them, though Gideon, who had spent three days among creatures for whom words were weapons, tried a few anyway. Lights? Too small. Spirits? Too churchy. They looked like snow falling upward — thousands upon thousands of pale bright motes streaming out of the broken crown-heart deep beneath them, pouring up through the rock itself, through the frozen falls, into the air above Thornfell, and then scattering.

Each one went somewhere. That was the thing that caught in his throat. They didn't drift; they *chose*. One streaked east toward the lowlands and vanished over the ridge. Three rose together in a slow spiral, as if reluctant to separate even now, then peeled off one by one toward three different horizons. Some sank back toward the valley towns below, and Gideon thought of the Market of Small Debts, of the hollow-eyed mortals who had traded their years for warmth, and hoped — uselessly, humanly hoped — that some part of those people would wake tonight remembering how to want things.

One lamp-soul hovered near him for a long moment. Close enough that he felt it against his cheek, warm as a hand, patient as an animal deciding whether to trust him. He thought: *I don't know you. I never got to know you.* And then it rose, and joined the others, and the mountainside filled with light going home.

"You're supposed to be unconscious," said Wren, behind him. "Heroically."

"I woke up. It happens."

"It ruins the tableau." She dropped down beside him in the heather, cross-legged, and he saw her properly for the first time since the crown's heart — since she had given her promised memory willingly and the final seal had opened like a lock that had only ever been waiting for permission. She looked *unburdened*. She also looked like a woman trying very hard not to check whether she still had everything.

"Well?" Gideon asked. "Is it still in there?"

"My memory?" She was quiet a moment. "Most of it. The ledger took its toll from the seal, not from me — the prophecy-keepers wrote it so. But—" She hesitated, and Gideon watched her reach into herself the way he had, and flinch at what she found missing. "I don't remember my mother teaching me the cipher anymore. I remember knowing the cipher. The knowing is there. The *her* is—" Her jaw set. "A shape where a person used to be."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." But she said it softly, and bumped her shoulder against his, and left it there, leaning, which was more apology than either of them had currency for. "You?"

"Hollowed out in all the right places." He held up his hand and studied the palm. Faint as frost on glass, the old sigil remained — the mark of the debt, the brand that had named him property. Except it didn't look like a brand now. It looked like a scar looks after it stops hurting: a record. *This happened. You survived it. It means something different than it did.* "I can't see my mother's face. Or Maren's cottage. Or—" he cast around, testing doors and finding some open, some shut — "the name of my first horse. Which apparently existed. I'm choosing to believe it was called Buttercup."

"No smuggler names a horse Buttercup."

"This smuggler did. I've decided. It's one of the few things left I get to decide."

Wren laughed — really laughed, a short surprised bark of it, like the laugh hadn't been oiled in years and needed the rust knocked off — and then pressed her sleeve to her eyes and pretended the wind was responsible.

Below them, the last of the lamps streamed up out of the mountain, and the Hollow Court, such as it had been, let them go.

* * *

They found Vesperine at the treeline.

She was standing at the edge of the thawing forest with her hands folded, watching the dawn like a woman reading a letter she'd waited a century to receive. Without the chancellor's silks she was smaller. Without the court behind her she was almost — ordinary. Her skin had taken on color overnight, mortal color, the pink-and-imperfection of a living body. Her joints bent the way joints were meant to bend, all of them, and Gideon realized with a lurch that this was the first time he'd seen her fully human-shaped, and that she must have been feeling every honest ache of it.

"You're alive," he said, because it seemed rude not to.

"Barely. Thoroughly. It's wonderful." She turned. Her eyes were still winter-pale, but the cold in them was just weather now, not intent. "Do you hate me?"

Gideon considered it honestly. "Yesterday, yes. This morning I can't find the reason. You were the first sold child. You held the winter back the only way anyone showed you how." He shrugged. "I'm lighter than I was. Apparently the price included my grudges."

"Some of mine stayed," she said. "But they're mortal-sized now. I can carry them." She looked up at the sky, where the last bright motes were vanishing southward. "A thousand years I fed the crown so the world wouldn't freeze, and the world was freezing *because of the crown*. Do you know what I'll do with the rest of my life, Gideon Blackwood? Anything. Anything at all. I've never once done anything at all."

"Start small," Wren advised. "The big freedoms go to people's heads."

Vesperine smiled — a real smile, crooked, unpracticed, all the more devastating for it — and inclined her head to them both, debtor to creditors, queen to nobody. Then she walked into the trees, into the dawn, unhurried, without hatred, without escort, carrying her grudges like a woman carrying firewood: heavily, willingly, home.

They watched until the forest took her.

"Do we follow the lamps down?" Wren asked. "To the valley?"

"In a bit." Gideon settled back into the heather. Above them the sky was fully awake now, blue at the top, gold at the rim, and somewhere downslope a stream was running loud and free and showing off. "First I want to sit here and miss things properly. It seems rude to skip it."

So they sat, two hollowed-out people on a thawing mountain, grieving on purpose, and it hurt, and it was good.

* * *

Near noon, Wren said the thing she'd been chewing on since dawn.

"The ledger's blank."

"I know. I saw."

"Not *empty*. Blank. Like the book is waiting." She pulled it from her pack — scorched at one corner, soaked in cavern water, older than the mountain it had prophesied inside of — and opened it to the page that had borne his name and tonight's date. Clean paper. No ink. No prophecy. For the first time in a hundred years, the future owed no one anything. "Every keeper before me died with pages left unread. I get to die with none. Do you understand what that means?"

"That you're unemployed?"

"That it's *over*, you absolute man. The keeping is finished. Whoever writes tomorrow writes it themselves." She closed the book with a gentleness that contradicted her tone entirely, and Gideon understood that she wasn't holding a tool anymore. She was holding a grave marker for everyone who had come before her, and a birthright she finally got to spend instead of serve.

"What will you do with it?" he asked. "The freedom."

"I haven't the faintest idea." She sounded delighted and terrified in equal measure, and Gideon recognized the condition. He was suffering from it himself. "What about you? The famous Gideon Blackwood, freed soul, breaker of crowns, former horse-owner. What does a man do the morning after he saves the world?"

"He goes and finds out whether his master is alive," Gideon said quietly, "and whether she remembers him."

The laughter went out of the morning, gently, like a candle cupped rather than blown.

"Maren gave you the last thing she had," Wren said. "Her one kept memory. Your mother's face, and why she ran. And the price of shattering the sigil took it."

"Took most of what I paid. But yes. That too." He looked at his marked palm. "She emptied herself for me twice. First when the collectors came, and once more when it mattered. And I can't remember her face, Wren. I can't remember the cottage, or her voice, or—" the cat, he almost said, *was there a cat* — "anything except that I loved her and she loved me, and that I forgive her. I kept the forgiveness. Of everything I could have chosen, I somehow kept the forgiveness."

Wren put the ledger away and stood, and offered him her hand, and pulled him up out of the heather.

"Then we go find her," she said. "And if she doesn't remember you, you introduce yourself. You're good at smuggling yourself into places. Consider it one final job."

"Breaking into my own master's affections. Worst contract I've ever taken."

"Terrible rates."

"No severance."

They started down the mountain together, through snow going soft and rotten and glorious underfoot, through water running everywhere, laughing at nothing, arguing about which road to take — Wren insisting on the eastern pass because the ledger's margins mentioned an inn with decent stew, Gideon refusing on principle to let a dead book plan his holidays — and above them the last of the freed souls streamed homeward over Thornfell, and the first true spring in living memory followed them down like something that had finally been given permission.

At the fork in the road, Gideon stopped. Just for a moment. He looked back up at the mountain that had held his soul in a treasury and his history in chains, and he touched the faint sigil on his palm — not a brand now, a reminder — and he chose, freely, the way he intended to keep choosing:

He turned downhill, and walked on.

Down, toward the valley. Toward a cottage with a crooked chimney and a woman who might look at him like a stranger. Toward whatever a man becomes when every step belongs to him.

The road was long, the day was warm, and for the first time in his life —

— Gideon Blackwood was going home with nothing but what he'd chosen to keep.
