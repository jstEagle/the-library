## 21. First Snow of Spring

The thaw began, as Wren would later tell anyone who asked, with a sound like a sigh.

She had been sitting on the mountainside above the falls, watching Gideon sleep off the unmaking, when the snow on the lower slopes went soft all at once. Not melting — softening, the way a fist relaxes. A century of Thornfell's winter loosened its grip in a single long exhale, and the sound it made was the sound of something enormous setting down a weight it had been carrying so long it had forgotten it was heavy.

Then the water moved.

The falls had been frozen for as long as anyone could remember — a wall of blue-green ice, beautiful and dead — but now a crack ran up its face like a lightning strike in slow motion, and behind the ice the river remembered itself. Water thundered free, throwing mist into air that smelled, impossibly, of mud and green things and the particular sweetness of a world waking up.

Wren laughed. She hadn't meant to. It just came out of her, startled and ragged, and she kept laughing until she was crying instead, and then she gave up trying to tell the difference.

Gideon woke somewhere in the middle of it, sat up groaning like an old man, and looked at the falls, and at the snowmelt running silver down every gully, and at Wren laughing and crying on a rock.

"Well," he said. "Either we won, or I'm still dreaming in that treasury."

"We won." Her voice cracked on it. She wiped her face with her sleeve, undignified and not caring. "We actually—"

"Give me a moment," Gideon said, "to be smug about it."

He tried to stand, discovered his legs had opinions, and sat back down. Wren watched him take inventory of himself — patting pockets, flexing hands, that small frown of a man checking whether everything he owned was still where he'd left it. She knew what he'd find missing. The ledger's last page, before it went blank, had shown her exactly what the crown would take from him: whole years, whole rooms of memory, gone like frost off a windowpane.

His mother's face. Maren's cottage. The name of the village where he was born.

He didn't say anything about it. He just finished the inventory, found himself acceptable, and gave Wren a lopsided grin that was maybe ten percent genuine and ninety percent stubbornness.

"So," he said. "Which road down?"

* * *

They buried the ledger three days later, beneath the falls, in the smugglers' door chamber where this whole disaster had properly begun.

Getting there required crossing the river, which was now a river again — loud, muscular, and deeply uninterested in being crossed. Gideon solved it the way he solved everything: by finding the one rotten plank bridge the smugglers had never bothered to record, testing it with a charm he absolutely should not have been using for bridge-testing, and daring Wren to have a better idea.

"I have several better ideas," Wren said. "All of them involve not dying."

"The plank's fine."

"The plank is *weeping*."

"The plank is structurally optimistic." He held out a hand. "Come on, prophecy-keeper. Trust me. It's what the book would have wanted."

"The book," Wren said darkly, tucking it under her arm, "wanted you dead in chapter nine. The book can want things without getting them."

The plank held. Barely. On the far side they climbed to the door beneath the falls, which stood open now — the sigil-mark faded to nothing, its purpose spent, just old stone and older dark. Behind it the tunnel breathed cool air that no longer felt like the mountain's hunger. It just felt like a cellar. Wren found that almost unbearably moving. Cellars were safe things. Cellars were ordinary.

Gideon cleared a patch of ground near the entrance with his boot, and Wren knelt and began, with great care, to dig.

"You don't have to do it like that," Gideon said after a moment.

"Like what?"

"Like it's a funeral. It's a book."

"It's a hundred years of my life." She kept digging. "It named you before you were born. It told me what I was for when I was six years old. Every night of my childhood I fell asleep reading it, looking for the page where I got to stop being alone." She paused, hands in the dirt. "It's a funeral."

Gideon was quiet for a while. When Wren glanced up, he was crouched beside her, helping, his long smuggler's fingers surprisingly gentle with the earth.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I'm glad it was wrong about some things."

"It wasn't wrong about anything. That's the problem. It was right about all of it — the debt, the door, the duel, the keeper who—" She stopped.

"Who lived," Gideon said. "Because you gave it a memory it didn't expect. Because you chose differently." He shrugged. "Turns out prophecy is just weather forecasting. Useful until someone builds a roof."

Wren laughed despite herself. "You cannot build a roof against fate, Gideon Blackwood."

"You can if the fate is made of debt and the roof is made of spite." He nodded at the ledger. "Bury it deep. If any courtier survived down there, I don't want them reading their own obituary."

They buried it together — wrapped in oilcloth, tucked into the earth where the falls' spray would keep the ground soft forever, beneath the roar of water that would run for a hundred more years because of what they'd done. Wren pressed her palm flat over the grave once, said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the blank pages hadn't already said, and stood up.

"So," Gideon said. "Roads."

* * *

They argued about roads for the entire climb out of the valley, which Wren privately considered the finest argument of her life, precisely because nothing depended on it.

"The east pass," Gideon said, "is faster."

"The east pass is a smuggler's road, which means it's illegal, which means you picked it out of habit."

"The east pass is *efficient*, which you'd know if you'd ever smuggled anything besides other people's destinies."

"My destiny-carrying was perfectly legal."

"Was it? Show me your papers."

"I'll show you the scar from where the mountain tried to eat us."

"That was the west pass," Gideon said. "Different mountain entirely. The east pass has *views*. Terraces. A hot spring. There's a woman at the top who sells chestnuts and doesn't ask questions, which after the last month is my primary criterion for human company."

"The north road goes through Lowmark," Wren said. "Lowmark has libraries."

"You've spent your whole life with a library strapped to your back and buried it two days ago. Take a holiday from books. Live a little."

"I am living a little. That's why I want a library."

They reached the ridge by late afternoon, and stopped arguing only because the view stole the words from both of them.

Thornfell Valley spread below, and it was *green*. Not the green of spring arriving on schedule — the reckless, show-off green of a place making up for lost time. Snow clung only to the highest peaks, honest winter doing honest work at its proper altitude. Down in the valley, smoke rose from chimneys in the villages, and fields that had lain fallow for generations were already black and wet and waiting. Somewhere a bell was ringing, probably for no reason at all except that bells deserved ringing.

"People are going to come up here eventually," Wren said. "Pilgrims. Scholars. They'll want to know what happened."

"They'll invent something better than the truth. Truth's always too strange to sell." Gideon squinted down the east trail. "Chestnut woman first. Then we'll let the scholars earn their keep."

"*We?*" Wren raised an eyebrow. "Since when is there a *we*?"

Gideon opened his mouth, closed it, and had the grace to look briefly uncertain — a look Wren intended to remember for the rest of her life and deploy whenever convenient.

"You walked into a mountain with me," he said finally. "Pulled me out of a warded treasury. Fought a duel-adjacent situation. Buried a holy book. I assumed we were—" he gestured vaguely between them "—a *situation*."

"A situation."

"A partnership. An arrangement. A—"

"I'm teasing you," Wren said. "Yes. We."

He recovered his composure with the dignity of a man pretending he'd never lost it. "Good. Because the chestnut woman gives discounts to couples. Probably. I haven't asked. I'm going to ask."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's my plan. You're welcome to propose a better one involving libraries."

And Wren — who had spent twenty-three years following a book, walking a road laid down before her birth, preparing a stranger for a death she believed was hers — found herself standing on a mountaintop in a world with no instructions left, and discovering that having no instructions felt like standing in warm rain.

There were a thousand roads. None of them were written anywhere. Nobody had sold her this one; nobody had bought it either. It was just hers.

"North," she said. "But through Lowmark *and* past the chestnuts. Both. In sequence. Like civilized people."

Gideon considered this with the gravity of a man reviewing treaty terms. "Counteroffer: chestnuts first, because I'm hungry, and libraries second, because you'll sulk otherwise."

"Accepted," Wren said, "because I will sulk otherwise."

They started down the east pass together, arguing companionably about whose boots would give out first (his), whose fault that would be (hers, for choosing the wet route along the river), and whether the chestnut woman would believe even one word of their story (no, and thank every open sky for it).

Behind them, the falls roared on, keeping watch over a blank book in soft ground. Ahead, the road dropped through melting snow toward terraces and hot springs and a woman who sold chestnuts and didn't ask questions.

Halfway down, Gideon stopped, frowned, and patted his coat pocket.

"What?" Wren said.

"Nothing. Just—" He pulled his hand out empty, shook his head, half-smiled. "For a second I thought I'd forgotten something important. But it's gone whatever it was, and I'm not." He rolled his shoulders like a man setting down luggage. "That's new."

Wren took his hand. It was calloused and cold and it fit hers the way the sigil had fit his palm once — perfectly, and by choice this time.

"Come on," she said. "The chestnuts are getting cold."

And they went down the mountain, unhurried, unwritten, into the first spring Thornfell had seen in a hundred years.
