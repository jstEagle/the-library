## 22. Sigil and Hollow

The trouble with honest roads, Gideon had discovered, was that they were longer.

Smuggling routes cut straight through the world — over passes no shepherd would name, through tunnels that smelled of other people's secrets. Honest roads went around. They stopped at every village with a shrine and a toll, they wandered, they trusted the bridges to hold. Gideon had spent the first month of his new life muttering about it, and Wren had spent that same month laughing at him, which he supposed was fair.

"You could take the pass," she said now, from her seat on the cart's bench beside him. "The one above Harrowmere. We'd save two days."

"The one above Harrowmere," Gideon said, "is where a man once tried to sell me a charm against wolves that turned out to be a dried turnip."

"And you've never forgiven the mountain for it."

"I've never forgiven *him*. The mountain was an accomplice."

She smiled and leaned into his shoulder, and the cart rolled on down the honest road toward Harrowmere, where the autumn fair was in its second day and where, if the rumors were true, someone owed somebody something that needed un-owing.

That was the trade now. It had started as a joke, almost — a scarred man who walked into debt-markets and walked out with the paper burned. But word traveled, as word does, and by midsummer travelers were telling stories in taverns about the man who bought back debts. Not forgave them. *Bought* them. He'd pay a lender three coppers on the shilling and then tear the note in half while the whole room watched, and when the debtor asked what he wanted in return, he'd say the same thing every time:

"Pass it on when you can afford it. That's all."

Nobody believed that was all. People rarely do. So the stories grew teeth — he was a fallen lord, he was a ghost, he was the Hollow King walking abroad in a mortal skin. Gideon let them talk. A reputation did half his work for him; lenders grew polite when he entered a room, the way dogs grow polite around thunder.

Only the sigil on his palm told the truth, and only to those who knew how to look: faint as frost on a windowpane, a small closed ring with a line through it. It didn't burn anymore. It didn't pull. On cold mornings it caught the light like breath on glass, and Gideon had learned to read it the way sailors read weather — not as a sentence, but as a sky.

* * *

Harrowmere's fair smelled of tallow, cinnamon, and wet wool, which Gideon privately considered the three smells of civilization. He left Wren at the pie stall — she had developed, over the months, a devotion to meat pies that bordered on the liturgical — and made his way to the debt-tent at the fair's edge, where a moneylender named Osk Vail held court behind a table of ledgers and a face like a closed door.

Vail was not the worst of his kind. Gideon had met worse below the mountain, wearing borrowed faces and courtesy like ill-fitting coats. But Vail held paper on eleven families in Harrowmere, and three of those notes were written in the old style — the style with a sigil pressed into the wax, the style that had come back into fashion lately, quietly, the way bad ideas always do.

"Blackwood." Vail didn't look up. "I wondered when you'd come. You're becoming expensive, you know. Every time you burn one of my notes, my other clients start asking why *they* still owe me."

"Terrible business model," Gideon agreed, sitting down across from him without being invited. "Lending money to people and expecting them to keep suffering for it."

"Lending is a kindness, in its way."

"So is a knife, if you use it to cut bread." Gideon laid three coins on the table. "The Aldern family. The widow Sess. And the cooper's boy — Tam, seventeen, signed for his dead father's debts because nobody told him he could refuse."

Vail finally looked up. His eyes were shrewd and tired and, Gideon thought, not entirely without conscience — just without exercise. "You know I have to charge you more than face value. My other clients will hear of it."

"Then charge me. I'll pay it." Gideon turned his hand over, palm up, and let the firelight catch the frost-line of the sigil. "But look at this first, and tell me honestly what you think it means."

Vail looked. Most people saw a scar. Lenders, who dealt their lives in marks and seals, saw more. Something moved behind Vail's tired eyes — recognition, or its cousin, fear.

"That's the old style," he said quietly. "That's the mark they used to press into soul-notes. Before my time. Before my grandfather's time."

"It came back," Gideon said. "It never really left. And here's what I want you to understand, Osk Vail, man to man, ledger to ledger: every time you press that seal into wax, somewhere under a mountain, something remembers how to breathe. There are doors that open from both sides. I've been through one. I don't recommend the trip, but I'll tell you this much for free — the things on the other side keep excellent books, and they never stop collecting."

He tore the three notes in half while Vail watched. Then he stood, tipped an imaginary hat, and added, "The interest on what I just paid you is simple. Next contract you write, write it plain. No sigils. No old style. If I ever see that seal in your wax again, I won't buy the note. I'll come for the book."

* * *

Wren was waiting outside the tent with two pies and the particular expression she wore when she'd been eavesdropping successfully.

"Three notes," she said. "You're getting soft. Last month it was five."

"Last month I hadn't seen the price of pies in Harrowmere." He took one. It was, frankly, transcendent. "Also, Vail isn't wicked. Just comfortable. Comfortable is harder to fight than wicked — wicked at least knows what it is."

"Is that wisdom?" Wren said. "Or indigestion?"

"Both. I'm a complete person now."

They walked the fair as the light went long and gold, past the shrine where an old woman was pinning ribbons for luck, past children selling roasted chestnuts out of a brazier that smoked like a small volcano. Gideon found himself doing what he always did now — reading the crowd for marks. Not victims. *Marks.* The hollow-eyed ones. The ones who flinched when a stranger touched their shoulder. The ones who'd traded something small and terrible to get through a hard winter and were carrying the receipt like a stone.

There was a girl by the well, maybe nineteen, staring at her own hands. When Gideon drifted close enough, he saw why: a faint gray ring around the fourth finger of her left hand, like a band of shadow sunk into the skin. A memory-debt. Small. Recent. Someone had taken a memory as collateral, and the girl kept trying to remember what she'd lost and couldn't, which was the cruelest part of the design.

"Wren," he said softly.

"I see it." Wren was already moving. She had a way with the hollow-eyed ones — she'd been raised among prophecies, after all, and knew better than anyone how it felt to have your life written by someone else's hand.

Her name was Isel. Her mother was sick, and a traveling charm-seller had offered a cure for the price of "one warm thing," and Isel, frightened and fifteen kinds of foolish with love, had given him her memory of her father singing. She couldn't describe the memory anymore. She just knew there was a shape missing, the way a mouth knows a tooth is gone.

Gideon sat with her on the well's edge and explained, gently, what he was. Not all of it — nobody ever got all of it — but enough. That he'd been marked once. That the mark could be turned. That some debts could be called due in the other direction.

"It won't be easy," Wren warned the girl. "And it won't be free. But it will be *yours*, and that matters more than easy."

Isel looked at Gideon's palm, at the frost-ring sigil, and asked the question everyone asked eventually, the one he'd answered so many times it had worn smooth:

"What did it cost you? To break yours?"

Gideon considered lying. He considered the soft version, the kind version. But Wren was watching, and Wren had taught him — slowly, over a hundred honest miles — that kindness built on lies was just another kind of lending.

"A great deal," he said. "I paid with memories of home. My mother's face. My master's kitchen. Whole years, gone like snow off a roof. Some days I reach for them and find nothing, and that nothing aches worse than any pain I've known." He turned his palm up again, letting her see the sigil. "But this isn't a brand anymore. It's a reminder. Everything I am now, I chose. Nobody sold it, nobody lent it, nobody holds the paper. And I would make that trade again tomorrow, and the day after, until my hands wore out."

Isel cried a little. Then she nodded, and Gideon began the work — the slow, careful turning of the mark, the calling-due-in-reverse, the craft he'd taught himself out of fragments of Maren's lessons and Wren's cipher and his own stubborn refusal to leave a debt standing anywhere he could reach it.

It took until moonrise. When it was done, Isel gasped — and then laughed, wet and startled, and hummed four bars of a song she couldn't name, and wept again, and hugged them both hard enough to crack ribs.

"Pass it on," Gideon told her. "When you can afford it. That's all."

* * *

They camped that night in a field outside the fair, under a sky rinsed clean by wind. Wren built the fire; Gideon laid out the blankets; between them they had assembled, over the months, a domesticity so modest it would have embarrassed a mouse, and neither of them would have traded it for a kingdom.

Which was, he reflected, precisely the point.

"Do you ever miss it?" Wren asked, later, when the fire had gone low. "The mountain. What you gave up."

Gideon reached for his mother's face and found, as always, the smooth nothing where it should have been. It hurt less these days. Or perhaps he'd simply grown strong enough to carry it.

"I miss faces I can't remember," he said. "That's a strange grief. But no — I don't miss the rest. Down there, everything I had was on loan. Up here—" he gestured at the fire, the field, her, "—everything's owned outright. Even the ache. Especially the ache."

Wren nodded, and for a while they watched the embers breathe.

Then she said, casually, in the voice she used when she absolutely was not being casual: "A peddler came through the fair today. Said there's trouble in the low country. Villages along the Graywater — folk waking up with gray rings on their fingers, memories gone, and no charm-seller within a hundred miles to have taken them. He said the rings appear in the night. Like frost."

Gideon sat up slowly.

"Frost," he repeated.

"Like frost," Wren confirmed. "And the peddler said — and I quote, because I made him repeat it twice — 'it's as if something old has remembered the way out.'"

The sigil on Gideon's palm caught the last ember-light, faint and cool and patient. Not burning. Not pulling. Just there — the way a compass needle is there, the way a key in your pocket is there when you pass a locked door.

Gideon looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at Wren, who was already reaching for the map.

"Well," he said. "How do you feel about dishonest roads?"

Wren grinned.

"I thought you'd never ask."
