## 2. Proclamation at Dusk

The bell in Grayhollow's market square had not rung in eleven years.

Lior Marchetti knew this because they had counted, in the way of people who keep tallies against forgetting. Eleven years since the plague scare that emptied the square for a season; eleven years of the bell hanging silent and green-throated with moss, a dull iron tongue in a wooden mouth. So when its voice cracked out across the town at dusk — three slow strikes, then silence, then three more — Lior's hands stopped moving over their map mid-stroke, and a bead of ink fell from the nib and bloomed like a wound across the river they'd been drawing.

They sat in the corner of the Drowned Goose with their back to the wall, as always, the good chair by the hearth that Marta the innkeep pretended was reserved for no one but which everyone knew belonged to the cartographer. Around them the tavern filled with the evening's usual tide: drovers smelling of rain and sheep, two wool merchants arguing over weights, old Tam Fletcher nursing his third cider and his first grievance. Ordinary sounds. The scrape of benches. The clink of crockery. The particular sigh of people settling into the belief that the day was done with them.

Then the bell rang again, and even Tam went quiet.

Lior wiped the nib, capped the ink, and did what they always did when something felt wrong: they listened past it. It was a habit so old it had worn grooves into them, the way water wears stone. Beneath the ordinary noise of the Goose there were other noises — the ones no one else heard, or heard only as a prickle at the neck. The land had lines in it, living lines, threads of something older than roads, and Lior could feel them the way a blind person feels warmth from a fire. Through the tavern floor, through Grayhollow's foundations, through the packed earth of the market square, those lines ran and braided and sang — faintly tonight, drowsily, like streams under ice.

And now, beneath the bell's dying echo, Lior felt something else. A tremor running along the nearest line, out from the north road, quick as a pulse of fear.

*Someone is coming,* the land said, in the wordless way it said everything. *Many someones.*

The tavern door opened on cold air and lamplight.

Three figures entered, and the room's warmth seemed to bend around them rather than welcome them. They wore the gray-and-crimson of crown service, travel-cloaked, boots muddy from the north road, and the one in front carried a staff of office topped with a brass bell — a crier's staff, though no crier Lior had ever seen wore a sword beneath his cloak the way this man clearly did. Behind him came two more, and it was the woman among them who made Lior's stomach tighten without knowing why. Tall, dark-haired, plain-faced in the way of people whose faces have been made deliberately forgettable, she moved through the crowded room the way a heron moves through reeds — unhurried, precise, disturbing nothing, missing nothing.

Lior knew her.

Not by name — they'd never exchanged names — but by shape, by the memory of last night burned bright and unwelcome behind their eyes. The stranger at the corner table. The one who had been watching when the sigils on Lior's map had glowed.

* * *

It had been such a small thing. That was the horror of it, Lior thought now, watching her scan the room. Such a small, stupid slip after thirty years of care.

Maps were Lior's trade and their camouflage both. Smugglers paid well for passes through Thornfell, and Thornfell paid better than honest work ever had, and the gift — the thing in Lior's blood, the quiet inheritance that let them read the mountains' hidden ways like handwriting — stayed safely invisible as long as the maps looked like the work of a very skilled, very lucky surveyor. Lucky. That was the word everyone used. *Marchetti has a knack for finding passes where there are none.* Knack. Luck. A talent for terrain.

Last night, tired and careless and pleased with a commission finished — a route map for the Corven brothers, all switchback and hidden ford, drawn in the deep hours when judgment thins — Lior had inked the final sigil-marks into the margin. Not the decorative flourishes real cartographers used, but the true marks, the ones that weren't drawing at all but listening: small characters that hummed against the paper's grain, that settled over the living lines beneath the ink like hands resting on a shoulder. And the candlelight had caught them, and they had glowed. Faintly. Softly. Green-gold, like foxfire under water.

For perhaps three heartbeats before Lior smothered the light with a palm and laughed it off — *old varnish catching the flame, happens all the time* — and the tavern had gone back to its drinking.

Everyone except the woman in the corner.

She hadn't stared. That was the worst part. She had simply watched, with the flat patient attention of someone confirming an entry already written in a ledger somewhere, and then she had finished her wine and left, and Lior had spent the walk home composing and discarding a dozen explanations, and had woken before dawn with their pack half-packed and their grandmother's journal heavy in their thoughts, and then had told themselves they were being foolish, paranoid, a cartographer spooked by candlelight —

— and now the bell was ringing, and she was here, and her eyes found Lior's across the room with the unhurried certainty of a hawk that has already chosen its branch.

* * *

"Citizens of Grayhollow." The crier's voice was trained, carrying, and utterly without warmth. He mounted the low platform by the hearth that served for announcements and the occasional fiddler, and the room turned to him the way a wound turns to salt. "By order of Aldric the Third, King of Caelmor and its marches, protector of the realm by grace and by warding: hear the king's proclamation."

He unrolled a scroll with a snap. Beside him, the dark-haired woman stood with her hands folded, watching the crowd, and Lior understood suddenly, viscerally, that she wasn't there for ceremony. She was counting. Marking faces. Doing whatever hunters did before hunts began.

"Let it be known throughout the realm," the crier read, "that certain persons bear within them the taint of wild gift — an unlicensed magic that corrupts the bearer and endangers the crown's warding rites. Let it be known that the Gift Hunt is declared. All bearers of gift shall present themselves for registration at any garrison, warden-house, or temple within thirty days."

A murmur ran through the room — confusion more than fear, yet. These were sheep-farmers and smugglers and innkeeps. Gifts were stories told about other places, other people. Witches were things that happened in Hollowmere, wherever that was, not here.

"Registration," the crier continued, "confers protection under the king's warding. Unregistered bearers, once discovered, shall be—" he paused, and Lior would remember forever how he paused, how his eyes flicked down to the scroll and up again with the mechanical efficiency of a man reading a shopping list, "—put to death. By fire, in public, that the realm may be cleansed. So it is written. So it is willed."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere near the door a mug slipped from a hand and shattered, and no one even flinched, because the sound of it was nothing beside the sound of thirty days becoming a number attached to their neighbors.

Marta, behind her bar, said softly, "Registered? Registered for *what*, exactly?"

"For the crown's warding rites," the crier said, smooth as oiled leather. "The king protects us all. The gifted serve that protection. It is an honor."

An honor. Lior's grandmother had died whispering about honors. Had pressed a journal into young Lior's hands and said, *they will call it an honor, little fox, and it will be a leash, and our blood wrote the leash, and someday you must understand what we did* — and Lior had been nine years old and had not understood, had carried the journal for twenty years unread because reading it meant admitting the glow in the margins was not a trick of varnish, and now the crier was saying *warding rites* in that same smooth voice and every hair on Lior's arms was standing.

Beside the crier, the dark-haired woman shifted her weight, almost imperceptibly, and Lior realized she was looking at them. Had been looking. Through the entire proclamation, while her partner read death aloud, she had been watching the cartographer in the good chair by the hearth, and there was something in her face that was not hunger and was not duty and was not anything Lior could name.

* * *

The crowd broke apart around the crown's men like water around stones. Questions were asked and answered with practiced blandness. Where does one register — the warden-house in Marrowford, two days' ride. What if you don't know if you're gifted — come anyway; the warders can tell. What does registration cost — nothing, only your name and your mark, freely given. *Freely given.* Lior bit the inside of their cheek until they tasted copper.

They should leave. Now, tonight, out the back, over the wall, into Thornfell where the lines ran thick and secret and only Lior could follow them. Every instinct that had kept them alive for thirty years screamed it.

But the woman was between them and the door, and she was walking over, and her partner had peeled off toward the bar to question Marta, and so Lior did the only thing left: they sat still, arranged their face into the pleasant blankness of a tradesman, and picked up their ruined map as if the evening were continuing as planned.

"You're the local surveyor." She stopped at their table. Up close her forgettable face resolved into details: a thin scar through one eyebrow, eyes the gray of slate before rain, a crown badge pinned inside her collar where it wouldn't catch light. "Marchetti."

"Lior Marchetti, at your service. Maps, routes, surveys. I hear the crown's roads are being remeasured — happy to quote rates."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite amusement. "I'm Serah Vane. Royal Hunter, third cohort." She didn't show credentials. Hunters apparently didn't need to. "You were in this room last night. Working late."

"I often do. Deadlines."

"Mm." Her gaze dropped to the map in Lior's hands — to the margin, where the ink stain bloomed over the river. To the place where, twelve hours ago, foxfire light had lived and died in the space of three heartbeats. "The varnish," she said quietly. "Does it happen all the time?"

Lior held her eyes. Their heartbeat was a drum in their throat, loud enough that they were half-afraid she could see the pulse jump. "Varnish?" They smiled, easy and empty. "I'm afraid I don't follow, Huntress. If you're asking whether my maps are honest — ask the Corven brothers. Ask anyone I've charted for. My reputation is the only thing I own."

"I know," Vane said. "That's what worries me."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The tavern noise swelled back around them — the desperate, too-loud chatter of people reassuring themselves that proclamations happened to other people — and inside that noise the two of them stood in a pocket of stillness, hunter and hunted, and Lior felt the land's lines beneath the floorboards trembling like plucked strings. Fear traveled those lines, they realized. Their own fear, going down into the earth, and the earth answering with a low, mournful hum that only they could hear.

*Run,* the lines said. *Run now.*

Vane's hand drifted toward her belt — toward the manacles hanging there, iron-gray, etched with fine suppressant script that Lior could actually *feel* from three feet away, a deadening pressure like a hand pressed over the mouth. Registration irons. For transport. For harvest, whispered the journal's ghost, though Lior still hadn't read far enough to know why those words surfaced.

And then she hesitated.

It was small. A hitch in the motion, barely a breath's worth. But Lior had spent a lifetime reading the land's smallest tells — the lean of a boulder, the lie of a fern, the hesitation of a stream before a drop — and they saw it in her the same way. Her fingers touched the manacles and stopped. Her slate eyes went distant for half a second, fixed on something that wasn't the tavern, wasn't Lior — something elsewhere, something remembered — and her jaw tightened as though she'd bitten down on words.

"You'll be registering, of course," she said, and her voice had changed. Flatter. Quieter. As though reciting a line she'd been handed and didn't entirely believe. "Thirty days. Everyone must."

"Of course," Lior said. "Bright and early."

Another pause. Then Vane reached out — slowly, deliberately, keeping her movements in full view — and turned Lior's ruined map ninety degrees on the table, squaring its edges with the wood, a gesture so absurdly domestic that Liar nearly laughed from sheer nerves. "Good maps matter," she said, almost to herself. "Even now. *Especially* now. People get lost, when things change." Her eyes came up and met Lior's, and in them was the flicker — the mercy, or the mistake, or whatever name history would eventually give it — brief as foxfire. "Get home safe, Marchetti. Mind the roads."

And she turned and walked away, cloak swinging, manacles silent at her belt, and collected her crier and her partner at the door, and led them out into the dusk.

* * *

Lior sat in the good chair for a long time after the door closed, listening to the tavern rebuild its fragile noise around the absence of the crown.

Around them, the talk had turned fever-bright: farmers vowing they'd never heard of such nonsense, merchants calculating which neighbors might be accused, old Tam declaring loudly that he'd known the king's family since before the warding and there wasn't a witch among them, never had been — and beneath it all, threaded through every voice, Lior could hear the fear doing its slow work, the way rot works through timber. Thirty days. People would inform. People always informed, when the alternative was wondering whether they'd be next.

Marta brought them a cider they hadn't ordered and set it down without a word, and her eyes said what her voice couldn't: *go, you fool, go now.*

Lior drank half of it, because their hands needed something to do, and then spread the ruined map flat and looked at the ink stain drowning the river, and made themselves think like a cartographer instead of a frightened animal. North road: patrolled now, certainly, the criers working village to village. West: open moor, visible for miles, and Vane had come from the west. South lay the lowlands and the capital, which was not fleeing but surrendering. Which left east — east into Thornfell itself, the high passes, the unmapped country where the living lines ran dense and singing and only a Marchetti could walk them without the mountain shrugging them off into some gorge with no bottom.

Home, in other words. Or the closest thing to it.

They rolled the map, slung their satchel, and left coin under the mug — too much coin, and Marta didn't try to give it back, which was its own kind of message — and paused at the tavern door with their hand on the latch.

Behind them, Tam Fletcher was saying, "...registered at Marrowford, that's all, it's nothing, we're honest folk—" and a drover answered, "Hollowmere registered, years back, whole village did, and no harm ever—" and someone shushed him, quickly, as though the name itself were now dangerous.

*Hollowmere.* The word snagged in Lior's chest like a fishhook. Grandmother's journal, third chapter, a page Lior had finally forced themselves to read two winters ago by candlelight with shaking hands: a list of villages, and beside each village a date, and beside each date a figure — *harvest yield* — written in their grandmother's careful, guilty hand. Hollowmere had been on the list. Registered. Protected. Yielded.

Outside, dusk had gone the color of cooling ash, and the first stars were pricking through above Thornfell's black shoulders. The land's lines rose to meet Lior as they stepped into the street — stronger here, away from the crowd, the night-side of the world humming its old secret song — and Lior stood in the empty square and let the mountain tell them the news: riders on the north road, six of them, moving fast toward Grayhollow. Riders on the south road too. The net drawing closed with the patience of all crown work.

Thirty days, the proclamation said. But the land said *tonight*, and the land had never once lied to a Marchetti.

Lior pulled their hood up, turned east, and walked toward the dark edge of town where the fields began — walking, for the last time in their life, out of a place where they had been safe, though they did not yet know it was the last time, and would not know until the smoke started.

Behind them, in the window of the Drowned Goose, a lamp guttered and steadied, and the bell hung silent over the square, its mossy tongue done ringing, its work finished: the whole valley now knew exactly who was free to hunt whom.
