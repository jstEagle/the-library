## 3. Ash on the Wind

The wind changed in the night.

Lior noticed it first as a wrongness in the lines — that was how they thought of it now, the land's hidden paths, the living threads beneath stone and root that only they could feel. For eleven years they had charted Thornfell by those threads, tracing them with fingertips pressed to granite, reading the mountain the way other folk read a page. The threads had moods. They sang when rain came. They went quiet and watchful when wolves moved through the high passes.

Tonight they tasted of smoke.

Lior sat up in their room above the Drowned Lantern, the tavern where they'd inked the map for the stranger three nights past, and pressed their palm flat against the floorboards. Beneath the wood, beneath the foundation stones, the deep line that ran from Thornfell's spine down toward the lowlands shivered like a plucked string. It carried something on it. Not sound exactly. Not scent. Something older than either — grief, maybe, or fear, moving along the land's veins the way blood moves through a body.

They told themselves it was a forest fire. Dry autumn, lightning in the peaks, such things happened. They told themselves this while dressing in the dark, while pulling on boots, while taking the stairs two at a time, and they did not believe a word of it.

The common room was empty except for Marta, the tavern-keeper's widow, banking the hearth for the night. She looked up when Lior came down, and whatever she saw in their face made her stop mid-motion, poker hovering over the coals.

"You felt it too," she said.

Lior froze. "Felt what?"

Marta set the poker down. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a door, with hands that had kneaded bread through two famines and buried a husband and a son. She was not a woman who startled easily, and she was not a woman who lied.

"The wind," she said. "It's been coming out of the south since midnight, and there's no fire in the south that should smell like that. My mother was from the lowlands. I know what a burn smells like when there are houses in it." Her jaw worked. "There are people in that smell."

*Registered gift-bearers,* Lior thought, and could not say aloud. *Hollowmere is south. Hollowmere is nothing but registered gift-bearers.* The village sat in a fold of the foothills where the magic ran shallow and gentle — healers, weather-singers, a woman who could coax orchards into bearing twice a year. Harmless gifts. Registered gifts. Names in the crown's books, addresses attached, in exchange for the promise — written right there in the royal proclamation, posted on every door in every market town — that registration meant protection.

*Register, and be shielded. Refuse, and be hunted.*

Lior had laughed at that proclamation, once, privately, bitterly. A shield with your name and address painted on it.

"I'm going to the crossroads," Lior said. "Refugees will come up the south road if—"

"If," Marta said. She was already reaching for her shawl. "I'll open the kitchen. There's barley enough for forty, if it comes to that." She paused at the foot of the stairs, and her voice dropped. "Lior Marchetti. Whatever you're carrying under all that quiet — now is not the night to be carrying it alone."

Lior looked at her. Three nights ago a stranger had watched sigils glow on wet ink, and Lior had spent every hour since waiting for hunters to kick the door in. But Marta had lived above this tavern for thirty years, and Lior had drunk her tea and mended her roof and never once asked what she knew.

"How long?" Lior asked.

"Since you fixed my son's leg, the winter he fell from the hayloft. Bone-knitting isn't cartography, child." She pulled the shawl around her shoulders. "Go to your crossroads. I'll have soup on when you're back."

* * *

The crossroads at Grayford was an hour's walk downhill from the Lantern, where the south road met the old drovers' track, and Lior felt it before they saw it: the crowd. Not because of torchlight or noise, though there was both, but because of what the crowd did to the land's lines. Two hundred frightened people trampling the same ground wove their terror into the earth like a stain. By the time Lior crested the last rise, their hands were shaking.

Grayford burned orange against the dark. Not the village itself — the village stood untouched, doors barred, shutters closed, its people watching from behind glass the way people watch a flood they've been spared. The orange came from cookfires and torches, dozens of them, and from the wagons. Wagons drawn up in the crossroads meadow in ragged rows, loaded with furniture and livestock and children, and standing among them, hollow-faced in the firelight, the survivors of Hollowmere.

Lior walked into it the way you walk into cold water.

The stories reached them in fragments, passed mouth to mouth through the crowd, each telling worse than the last. Crown riders, a dozen of them, wearing no livery anyone recognized until someone said *the gray cloaks, the ones with the eye stitched on*. They'd come at dusk, when everyone was gathered for evening meal. They hadn't fought. They hadn't even shouted. They'd simply gone house to house with the register in hand, checking names off, and then—

A woman with a burned shawl clutched around her shoulders kept saying *they knocked first*. That was the detail she returned to, over and over, as though politeness were the horror. *They knocked. They waited. Tam Weller answered his own door, he'd registered, he had his papers framed on the wall, and they — they just —*

She couldn't finish it. Another woman finished it for her, flat-voiced, staring at nothing: "They poured lamp oil through the windows and sealed the doors. The riders stood in the square and watched. One of them sang. Like a hymn."

Lior stood in the churned mud of the meadow and felt something in their chest close like a fist.

*Registered means protected.* Forty-three names in the crown's book at Hollowmere. Lior had never been there — smugglers avoided registered villages precisely because they were watched — but they knew the type. Good people who had believed the arithmetic. Who had traded their names for safety and gotten ash.

And underneath the grief, quieter and colder, a thought that felt like ice water down the spine: *your name is not yet in any book. Every day it stays out is a day bought with other people's burning.*

"Lior?"

Lior turned. Marta stood at their elbow with a lantern, having followed them down after all, or sent someone ahead — it didn't matter. What mattered was that she was looking past Lior's shoulder at the edge of the camp, at the shadow between two wagons, and saying, in the voice one uses for small animals caught in walls:

"There's a girl back there. Been sitting in the dark since they arrived. Won't eat, won't speak. The others won't go near her."

"Why won't they—"

"Because they say she called them down." Marta's lantern light trembled. "They say she dreamed it before it happened. Told the village elders a week past that fire was coming, that riders were coming, and nobody believed a child's nightmare — and then it came true, exact as she told it. So now they look at her like she lit the match herself."

Lior turned and looked into the dark between the wagons.

At first they saw nothing. Then the lantern's reach found a pair of eyes — enormous, dark, unblinking — set in a small smudged face. A girl, seven years old perhaps, maybe eight, sitting with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, wearing a man's coat far too big for her with the sleeves rolled in thick cuffs. Her hair was the color of wet straw, cut short and crooked, the cut of someone who'd done it themselves with a knife. She didn't flinch from the light. She just looked at Lior with the terrible patience of the very young who have seen something no one should see.

"What's your name?" Lior asked.

The girl said nothing. Her gaze slid past Lior's shoulder, to the south, toward the black line of hills where Hollowmere had been, and stayed there.

"She came in with the Corvath family," murmured a man nearby, one of the wagon-dwellers. "Found her walking the road alone at dawn, two days before the burn. Walking north, away from the village, already packed. When they asked why, she said—" He stopped, swallowed. "She said, *because tonight it burns, and I don't want to see it.* And then it burned, and she was right, and her own parents—" He shook his head. "Her parents put her out of the house, near as we can tell. Sent her away to save her, or to save themselves the shame of what she knew. Either way she's got no one."

Lior crouched down. Slowly, the way you'd approach a wounded bird, they lowered themselves to the girl's level, until the lantern light wasn't glaring and their face was level with hers.

"My name's Lior," they said. "I live up at the Drowned Lantern. I make maps."

Nothing. The dark eyes held theirs, and behind them Lior saw something that made their throat tighten: not emptiness. Calculation. A child weighing whether this adult was another person who would ask her to carry what she carried, or another person who would flinch away.

"The others think you did it," Lior said quietly. "That your dream caused it."

A tiny movement — not quite a nod.

"They're wrong," Lior said. "Dreams don't light fires. Men light fires. Dreams just see them coming, and that's not a curse, that's—" They stopped. Found the truth and said it plainly, because this child had earned plainness. "That's a gift. A hard one. The hardest one I know of. But it isn't yours to be ashamed of. It belongs to whoever used it badly, and that's the men with the gray cloaks, not you."

The girl's chin trembled. Just once. Then, in a voice so small it barely survived the wind:

"It doesn't always come true."

"No?"

"Sometimes I dream silly things. Dogs wearing hats. Rivers running backward." Her hands tightened on her knees. "But the bad ones always come true. Every single time. So I stopped telling anyone about the bad ones, but this time I couldn't — it was so loud, it woke me up screaming, and Mama made me tell the elders because she thought—" Her breath hitched. "She thought if they believed us, they could stop it. And they didn't believe, and it happened *exactly*, exactly like I dreamed, even the singing rider, even the part where the church bell rang thirteen times and bells can't do that, and now everyone looks at me like—"

She broke. All at once, the way small children break — total, immediate, without art. She pitched forward into Lior's chest and sobbed with her whole small body, fists knotting in Lior's jacket, and Lior held on and looked over her head at Marta, whose lantern hand had gone steady again, whose face had gone hard as river stone.

"Well," Marta said. "She'll want feeding. Come on, then, the both of you."

* * *

The kitchen of the Drowned Lantern was warm and yellow-lit, and the girl — Pip, she said finally, around her third bowl of barley soup, *just Pip, I don't remember the rest or maybe there wasn't any* — ate like a creature half-starved, which she was. Marta fussed and refilled and pretended not to watch, which fooled no one. And Lior sat across the table and felt the grief of the day slowly, grain by grain, hardening into something else.

It was not resolve yet. Resolve would come later, in the days after, when the shape of things grew clear. Tonight it was simpler and more stubborn than resolve. Tonight it was the sight of a child's knuckles white around a spoon, and the thought: *no one else looks at her like she's a curse. Not while I'm at this table.*

"Pip," Lior said, when the bowl was empty and the girl's eyelids were losing their war against exhaustion. "The dreams. The bad ones. Do you have them often?"

Pip's head came up sharply — the flinch of a child braced to be thrown out again — and Lior raised both hands, palms open.

"Not to send you away. The opposite. I need to know what's coming. Because I think—" They glanced at Marta, who gave a small nod, *tell her or don't, it's your road* — and decided. "Because I have a gift too. A quiet one. I can read the land, find paths that aren't on any map. And I think the men who burned Hollowmere would very much like to find me, and I'd rather know what's coming than not."

Pip stared at them. Then, slowly, wonderingly, the way a person tests ice:

"You have one? Really? Can I see it?"

Lior hesitated. Three nights ago, glowing sigils on wet ink had nearly cost them everything. Every instinct honed over eleven years of hiding said *never show, never tell, never let them see the gift move.*

But the world where those rules kept you safe had burned down with Hollowmere. Hiding had saved the registered families nothing. Their names in a book had saved them nothing. The old careful life was over; the only question left was what you built on the ashes.

Lior took the stub of charcoal from beside the hearth and, on the scrubbed wood of the table, drew a single mark — the old Marchetti wayfinding sigil, the one their grandmother had taught them, a small spiral crossed by a road-line. They laid two fingers beside it and let the gift breathe.

The sigil glowed. Faintly, softly, gold-green as foxfire, and as it glowed it *moved*, the spiral turning gently like a compass needle finding north, pointing — Lior realized with a chill — south. Toward the hills. Toward whatever came next.

Pip made a sound like a laugh and a cry sharing one throat. She leaned close, her nose nearly touching the glow, and the light played across her face and washed some of the ash-grief out of her eyes, just for a moment.

"It's pretty," she whispered. "It's like it's alive."

"It is alive," Lior said. "Everything the gift touches is. That's what they don't understand, the ones in the gray cloaks. You can't harvest a living thing and expect it to keep living." They smeared the sigil out with their thumb, and the light died, and the kitchen was just a kitchen again. "Pip. Listen to me. Tomorrow, or the next day, or however long it takes — riders are going to come asking questions in these parts. About refugees. About strangers. And when they come, you and I are going to be gone, up into Thornfell, where I know roads they don't. Do you understand?"

Pip nodded. Then she frowned, and her brow furrowed in a way that Lior would learn to dread and cherish in equal measure, the little crease that meant the dreams were speaking.

"They're already coming," she said.

The kitchen went still. Marta's ladle stopped mid-stir.

"What do you mean, love?" Marta asked carefully.

"The bad dream came back. While I was eating." Pip's voice had gone flat and distant, reciting. "It's short this time. That's how I know it's soon. The long ones take weeks. Short ones take days." She looked up at Lior, and her eyes were ancient. "A knight with no banner on his shield. He's riding toward us through the pass, and behind him there's a lady in a gray cloak who's crying under her hood, and neither of them knows why they're crying. And there's teeth." Her small hands curled. "There's a crown made of teeth, and it's laughing."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged in the wind that still smelled, faintly, of the south.

"That's tomorrow's problem," Marta said briskly, too brightly, gathering bowls. "Tonight everyone sleeps. Lior — the blue room. Extra blankets in the chest."

But Lior barely heard her. They were looking at the child who had dreamed a knight and a weeping hunter and a crown of teeth, and thinking of the land's lines shivering under the floorboards, and understanding — with the calm certainty of a mapmaker seeing a new road appear where no road had been — that the safe life was not merely ending.

It had ended. And whatever came walking out of that pass tomorrow, they would meet it not as a smuggler hiding a secret, but as something else entirely.

Someone with a family to protect.

"Come on, Pip," Lior said, lifting the lantern. "Let me show you where you'll sleep."
