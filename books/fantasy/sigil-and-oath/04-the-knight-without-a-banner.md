## 4. The Knight Without a Banner

The rain had been falling on Thornfell for three days, and Dain Corvath had stopped counting the hours since he'd last slept properly. Sleep came to him in shallow, guilty increments — the kind of sleep a man steals rather than earns — and each time he closed his eyes he saw the same thing: the eastern road out of Hollowmere, lit orange by the burning thatch behind him, and the faces of the people he had not saved.

He crouched beneath an overhang of granite above the smuggler's switchback, watching the trail below through the grey curtain of rain. His armor was gone. He'd buried it in a streambed forty miles south, plate by plate, like a man burying pieces of his own body, and now he wore oiled wool and a hood that smelled of sheep and pine tar. The sword remained. He could not bring himself to part with the sword, though he no longer felt entitled to carry it. It hung at his hip anyway, heavy as a verdict.

Below him, at the mouth of the pass where the trail narrowed between two walls of wet black stone, a small caravan was making camp.

Three figures. A lean wagon drawn by one mule that looked as tired of the mountains as any creature Dain had ever seen. A woman — or something that might be a woman; the hood shadowed everything but a sharp jaw and ink-stained fingers — kneeling by a cold fire ring, coaxing it back to life with a patience that spoke of long practice. A child, small and dark-haired, chasing something in the puddles. And a third figure standing apart, arms crossed, wearing the particular stillness Dain had learned to read in twenty years of soldiering: the stillness of a person listening to a country no one else could hear.

A scout's stillness.

Dain knew why they were here. He'd been tracking them for two days without quite admitting to himself that he was tracking them. The cartographer — because that was what she must be, the mapmaker the smugglers whispered about, the one who sold routes through Thornfell that no crown surveyor had ever found — was leading her little family north along the high passes. North, toward Grayford Bridge, toward the river valley, toward safety or whatever passed for it these days.

And three miles behind them, moving fast despite the rain, came Serah Vane.

* * *

Dain had known Vane for eleven years. He had trained beside her in the king's yard when she was all elbows and fury and a raw talent that made the old master-at-arms weep with something close to joy. He had stood next to her at coronations and executions, at sieges and parades, and he had believed — with the simple, unexamined faith of a man who had never yet been asked to choose — that she was the best of them. Sharper than Mallet, kinder than Kestrel, more honest than any of the silken officers who staffed the palace and called hunting a sacred duty.

Then came Hollowmere.

He remembered the orders arriving under royal seal, written in that beautiful, careful hand the chancellery used for its worst work: *Registered gift-bearers of Hollowmere to be collected for warding rites. Resistance to be met with fire.* He remembered reading it twice, looking for the loophole, the clause, the mercy. There wasn't one. There never was.

He remembered standing in the village square while the hunters went door to door, and an old woman with soil on her hands asking him — *him*, the king's shield-knight, the man whose oath was supposed to mean protection — whether it would hurt. And he remembered saying, because there was nothing else true left to say: "I don't know."

He had not raised his hand against his brothers-in-arms. That was the coward's arithmetic he'd performed that night, the compromise he told himself was principle: he would not fight them, but he would not help them either. He had walked into the smoke, found two children hiding in a root cellar whose mother had paid for their registration with everything she owned, and carried them out through the barley fields while Hollowmere burned behind him like a second sunset. He'd left them with a ferryman at the Marle with every coin he possessed and instructions to take them south, over the border, anywhere the crown's writ grew thin.

Then he had turned north into the mountains, because north was away, and away was the only direction left to a man who had broken his oath in the only way that mattered less than the ways he hadn't.

They would call it desertion. They already did. He'd heard the proclamation read by a crier in a valley town, his own name spoken in that flat civic voice alongside the words *traitor* and *hunt on sight*. Dain Corvath, who had taken thirty wounds in the king's service, who had once caught an assassin's blade meant for the royal throat with his bare forearm and borne the scar like a medal. Hunt on sight.

He found, examining himself honestly in the way sleepless men do, that he could not muster much grief over it. What surprised him was how little of him wanted to. The honor had died in Hollowmere square, somewhere between the old woman's question and the first scream. What remained was just a man with a sword and a debt he didn't know how to pay.

But Vane was coming up this trail after the cartographer, and Vane did not hesitate the way he had hesitated. Vane followed orders the way rivers follow gravity. And the cartographer down there, with her ink-stained fingers and her child and her quiet third companion, was walking straight into her hands.

Dain realized he had already decided. He'd decided two days ago, probably, when he'd first picked up their trail. Everything since had been the slow, elaborate business of a man talking himself into what he was going to do regardless.

He stood, rolled his shoulders against the cold, and started down the switchback.

* * *

The woman with the ink-stained fingers saw him before he reached the fire. Of course she did. She rose from the flames with a smoothness that wasn't startled so much as resigned, one hand drifting toward a knife at her belt, and the tall quiet one by the wagon turned with the unhurried precision of someone who had done violence before and expected to again.

"Easy," Dain said, and pulled back his hood, and let them see his face.

He watched recognition move across the cartographer's expression like weather. He was not an anonymous face, not anymore. The proclamations had seen to that — his name, his description, the reward that would keep a smuggler fed for a decade.

"You're the knight," she said. Her voice was low and even, an accent from the river towns, the vowels worn smooth. "Corvath. The one who wouldn't burn the village."

"The one who didn't stop it either." He kept his hands visible, open, away from the sword. Old habit from approaching frightened civilians; newer habit from approaching people who had every reason to fear the crown. "I'm not here for your coin and I'm not here to turn you in. I'm here because there's a hunter on your trail."

The tall one shifted. "We know about patrols. We've been dodging them since—"

"Not a patrol." Dain cut him off, gently. "Vane. Serah Vane. Three miles back, moving alone, which means she's confident, which means she's good, which means you should believe me when I tell you that confidence is earned. She tracked a ghost-smuggler through the Fenmarshes in winter when I was still young enough to think that was impressive. If she's on this trail, she will find you, and she will not hesitate."

The word *hesitate* landed strangely. He saw it land — saw the cartographer's eyes narrow, some private calculation flickering behind them, as though the word meant something different to her than it did to him. Later, much later, she would tell him about the tavern, and the stranger, and the moment mercy almost looked like a trap. But that evening he only registered that she was deciding something, weighing him on scales he couldn't see.

"Why?" she asked simply. "Why warn us?"

Because I burned my life down and got two children out and it wasn't enough. Because the old woman asked if it would hurt and I said I don't know. Because every night I dream the square is dry and I raise my sword against my brothers and wake up grateful for a cowardice I didn't manage.

What he said was: "Because I was the king's shield, and I failed the people I was shielded to protect. This is the only amends I know how to make."

The child appeared then, materializing out of the rain-shadow of the wagon with the uncanny silence of children who have learned that being noticed can be fatal. She was perhaps eight, thin as a wren, with dark hair plastered to her forehead and enormous eyes that fixed on Dain with an intensity that made his skin prickle. Not fear. Something worse. Recognition.

"You smell like smoke," she said.

"Pip—" the cartographer began.

"I dreamed you," the child went on, ignoring her, stepping closer, tilting her head back to look up at him. Rain dripped off her chin. "Before. In the bad dreams. You were carrying somebody and the sky was orange and you were crying but knights aren't supposed to cry so nobody saw."

The silence that followed was total except for the rain and the fire spitting. Dain felt something crack open in his chest, quietly, the way ice cracks on a river — no drama, just a structural failure, permanent.

"I don't know what she's talking about," the tall one said carefully.

"I do," Dain said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He crouched, slowly, bringing himself down to the child's level, and saw her flinch — a small, trained flinch, the flinch of a child who had learned what grown men's hands did — and it broke something else in him. "My name is Dain," he said. "I'm not going to hurt anyone. Not ever again. That's a promise, and I know promises are cheap right now, so I'll just have to spend the rest of my life making it expensive."

Pip studied him with those vast grave eyes. Then she nodded once, with the terrible authority of children, as though confirming a fact she'd already filed away, and went back to the puddles.

The cartographer exhaled. "Lior," she said. "Lior Marchetti. That's Wren—" a gesture at the tall one, who gave a nod that acknowledged nothing and promised less, "—and you've met Pip. You'd better sit, Ser Corvath. Tell us about Vane. Everything."

"It's just Dain now," he said. "There's no ser anymore. There hasn't been since Hollowmere."

"There's whatever you decide there is," Lior said, and gestured him toward the fire, and something in the plainness of it — no pity, no suspicion, just a chair offered at a table he hadn't earned — nearly undid him more thoroughly than the child's dreaming had.

* * *

He told them everything. It poured out of him with a shamelessness that alarmed him, the way water finds every crack in a hull once the seal is breached. Vane's habits, her methods, her blind spots — the way she always scouted the high ground at dusk, the way she trusted her nose for trails more than her eyes, the way she traveled light and fast and alone because she'd never yet needed backup. The bridge at Grayford, narrow, the only crossing for thirty miles, the obvious choke point where a smart hunter would wait rather than chase. The timing: Vane would reach the bridge by tomorrow midday, ahead of them, unless they turned east into the cavern country, which was suicide for anyone without a guide.

"Which is why you need me," Lior said, when he finished. Not a question. She was staring into the fire, and Dain saw her fingers moving absently against her knee, tracing shapes — sigils, he realized, though he didn't yet understand what they meant to her. "The caverns. I can get us through where Vane can't follow."

"And I can hold a bridge," Dain said. "If it comes to that."

Wren snorted softly from the shadows of the wagon. "One man against a royal hunter."

"One man who trained her," Dain said. "Who knows every form she favors and every mistake she makes when she's angry. Which, for what it's worth, is the only state I've ever seen her fight in that isn't controlled." He paused. "She's not evil. I want that understood. Whatever the crown has made her into, there was a person underneath it, and for eleven years I thought that person was the finest soldier I ever served beside. If there's a way through this that doesn't end with her dead, I'd prefer we find it."

Lior looked at him for a long moment across the fire. "You sound like a man talking about a sister."

"She is a sister. That's the whole rot of it. The crown doesn't ask you to stop loving people. It just asks you to burn them anyway, and calls your obedience virtue afterward." He heard his own bitterness and was surprised by it, by how much venom had accumulated in him without his noticing, filling him like floodwater filling a cellar.

Pip had crept back during the conversation and settled herself, uninvited, against Dain's side, as casually as if she'd known him all her life. He went very still. He could count on one hand the number of times anyone had leaned on him without wanting anything — and the child was warm and damp and breathing, and the weight of her against his ribs was doing something to him that he refused to examine in company.

"You're staying," Pip said. It wasn't a question either. Children and Lior Marchetti, apparently, conducted all their important conversations in declaratives.

"I'm escorting you to Grayford," Dain corrected gently. "Then I'm gone. Exile suits me. I'm not fit company for—"

"You're staying," Pip repeated, with the flat certainty of prophecy, and closed her eyes, and within moments was asleep against his shoulder, trusting him completely, instantly, in a way that no adult had trusted him since before Hollowmere.

Dain sat rigid, terrified of disturbing her, and looked helplessly across the fire at Lior, who wore the expression of someone watching a stray dog walk into a house and lie down by the hearth as though it had always lived there.

"She does that," Lior said. "Decides things about people."

"Is she usually right?"

Lior was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the wagon's canvas. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered in the high passes like an argument among giants.

"Ask me again tomorrow," Lior said. "After the bridge."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, in that blue-black hour when the rain finally thinned to mist and the mountains showed themselves in ragged silhouette. Dain took point without being asked; it was the one language he still spoke fluently, the grammar of rearguard and vanguard, of sightlines and fallback positions, and it felt like putting on a coat he'd thought he'd outgrown.

As they climbed toward the ridge road, he looked back once, down the trail they'd abandoned, toward the gray distance where Serah Vane was even now climbing toward a bridge where no one would meet her. He thought about her face as it had been at nineteen, laughing in the training yard with straw in her hair. He thought about the old woman's hands, and the root cellar, and the orange sky.

Two lives, he thought. The one where I kneel to the crown until they put my head on a spike for a mercy I already committed, and the one where I walk north with strangers who owe me nothing and somehow expect everything of me anyway.

Pip, half-asleep in the wagon, murmured something in her dreams. Dain caught only one word of it, carried back to him on the mountain wind:

"—teeth—"

He tightened his grip on the sword he no longer deserved to carry, and walked north.
