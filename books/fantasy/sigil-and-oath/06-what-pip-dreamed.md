## 6. What Pip Dreamed

Pip knew the cavern was dreaming before she did.

That was the trouble with sleeping in a place that had never been slept in before — the dark down here was old and hungry for company, and it crept into her head through her ears like water finding a crack in a jug. She dreamed of the mountain first: its roots going down and down, past stone, past water, into a black so deep it felt like standing at the top of a very long stair and knowing someone was climbing up.

Then the dream changed, the way dreams do when something else wants to be dreamed.

She was small again — smaller than now, small enough that the world was all hems and boot-tops — and she was standing in a great hall with pillars like the legs of giants. Candles burned in rows, hundreds of them, but their light didn't reach the ceiling. The ceiling just went up and up until it stopped being a ceiling and became a sky with no stars in it, only a darkness that breathed.

In front of her stood a man on a throne.

He wore a crown of gold worked into shapes like thorns, and he sat very straight, the way Mama had taught her to sit in the temple when the reeve came to count them. But his eyes were wrong. They were open, and they were looking at nothing, and behind them — behind them, deep inside his head where nobody should be able to see — Pip could see him.

The real one. The man under the crown.

He was screaming.

Not with his mouth. His mouth was closed and calm and kingly. He screamed the way you scream in a dream when you know you're shouting but no sound comes out, the way she'd screamed the night the hunters came to Hollowmere and the fires started and old Marta pulled her under a cart and put a hand over her mouth and said *don't make a sound, little bird, don't you dare make a sound.* That kind of scream. The kind that lives in your bones.

*Help me,* said the man inside the king, without any words at all. *Please. It's been so long. Please, I'm still in here.*

Pip tried to run to him. In dreams your legs are made of syrup and your feet belong to somebody else, and hers wouldn't move at all. And the thing wearing him turned its head — not the man's head, the *other* thing, the shape underneath the shape, which was all wrong angles and long patience — and it looked at her, and it smiled with the king's mouth.

*Little dreamer,* it said, and its voice was the sound a fire makes when it finds a full granary. *You see so clearly. Come closer.*

She woke up gasping, tangled in Dain's spare cloak, with the cave's cold pressing on her chest like a hand.

For a while she lay still and listened. Somewhere near the fire, Lior was turning over in their bedroll. Further off, Serah Vane sat watch; Pip could tell by the particular silence of her breathing, even and careful, like someone who had learned to sleep with one ear open. Dain snored the way big honest things snore, like a millstone rolling gently downhill.

Everyone was safe. Everyone was here. Nobody was screaming inside anybody.

But her hands were shaking, and they wanted to hold a stick of charcoal so badly that they shook harder without it, the way a dog's paws twitch when it runs in its sleep.

So Pip crawled out of the cloak, quiet as a mouse in a cathedral, and went to find the charcoal.

* * *

They kept their writing things wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Lior's pack, because paper was worth more than bread out here and charcoal was worth more than paper. Pip wasn't supposed to touch them. Lior had said so twice, kindly both times, the way you say things to children whose hands you don't yet trust.

But this was important. This was the most important thing there had ever been, maybe, and grown-ups were slow about important things because they spent all their time worrying about bread and patrols and whether Vane was going to murder everyone in the night. (Pip had decided Vane probably wasn't. Vane's dreams, when Pip caught the edges of them across the fire, smelled like smoke and guilt and a door she wished she'd opened sooner. Guilty people who wish about doors were mostly safe. It was the ones who dreamed tidy dreams you had to watch.)

She took one stick of charcoal and three sheets of the worst paper, the crumbly brown kind Lior used for practice, and carried them back to the firelight, and she knelt there in her shift with her knees going numb against the stone, and she drew what she had seen.

She was good at drawing. Mama had said so. Not pretty-good, not the kind where you draw flowers and everybody claps, but true-good: the kind where what comes out of your hand looks like the thing itself, so that looking at it later is almost like seeing it again. Pip had drawn the reeve's face once, two years ago, with the exact fold of his jowl and the exact wet shine of his little eyes, and Mama had laughed and then gone pale and burned it, though Pip hadn't understood why until much later.

She drew the hall first. Pillars, candle-rows, the ceiling that became a starless sky. Then the throne, and the man on it — the outside of him, gold crown, straight back, folded hands, a face like a coin left too long in a rich man's pocket.

Then she did the hard part, the part her fingers knew better than she did.

Inside the drawn man's head, she drew the other man. Small, curled up, mouth open in a scream with no sound coming out. She shaded around him very carefully, cross-hatching layer over layer, because the space between the real man and the false one was thick, thick as tar, thick as the dark under a bed, and the real man was buried in it up to his neck.

And around the whole head — around the outside of the king, ringing him like a fence rings a garden — she drew the marks.

She didn't know what they were. Her hand knew. That was the strange and terrible truth of it: her fingers moved and the marks came out fully formed, sharp-edged, certain, as if she were copying from a page held just behind the air. A spiral like a snail shell crossed by three bars. A triangle with an eye that wasn't an eye but a knot. Lines that hooked downward like roots, and lines that hooked upward like chains, and where they met, a symbol like a key that had been broken on purpose and soldered back together wrong.

She drew it seven times around the king's head, because seven was how many it was. She didn't decide that. Seven was simply correct, the way four legs are correct for a dog.

When she finished, the last mark still warm under her fingertip, she looked at what she'd made and felt cold all over, colder than the cave.

Because she knew the drawing was true. That was the curse of being her. Every dream she'd ever drawn true had come true, or had already been true, and the ones about the king had been coming every night for six nights now, getting closer each time, the way thunder gets closer.

On the first night, the man on the throne had been far away, at the end of a long corridor.

Last night, he had been close enough that she could see the tears drying on the inside of his face.

* * *

"You're supposed to be asleep."

Pip jumped so hard she smudged the nearest pillar. Lior crouched beside her, hair loose and eyes soft with sleep, and for a moment they just looked at the papers spread on the stone.

Pip braced herself to be told off. Instead Lior sat down slowly, folding their long body the way a heron folds, and picked up the sheet with the king on it, holding it close to the fire.

"Where did you see this?" Lior asked. Their voice was careful, the voice adults used when they were trying not to frighten you, which always frightened Pip more.

"In my dream." She hugged her knees. "Every night since the bridge. Since Grayford." Since Dain had come home bleeding and half-dead, she didn't say, because saying it might make it her fault, and she had a private terror that her dreams *were* things' fault somehow — that thinking about a thing called it, the way calling a dog's name called the dog. "There's a man inside the king. A real man. He's screaming. The thing that wears him keeps him like—" she searched for it, found it, hated it— "like a coat. Like a coat that's still alive."

Lior's hands had gone very still.

"And these marks?" They tapped the ring of symbols circling the drawn head. "Did you see these too?"

"My hand drew them." Pip shrugged, defensive. "I don't know what they are."

"I do," Lior said quietly.

Something in their voice made Pip look up. Lior wasn't looking at her anymore. They were looking at the sigils the way you look at a snake you thought was dead, and their face had gone the color of the ash that had drifted over Hollowmere's fields, the ash Pip still sometimes found in her hair.

"This is Marchetti work," Lior said, mostly to themselves. "This is — I've seen this hand. This is my grandmother's hand. These are binding sigils, the First Binding, the one from the journal, the one that—"

They stopped. Grown-ups always stopped right at the best part, like a storyteller who falls off a cliff mid-sentence.

"The one that what?" Pip demanded.

Lior looked at her for a long moment. Firelight moved in their eyes. When they finally spoke, their voice had a crack down the middle of it, thin as a hairline fracture in a bell.

"The one that put the thing in the king," they said. "Or rather — Pip, listen to me. My family built the cage. Generations ago. To trap something that would have eaten the world. And this—" they touched the ring of marks around the drawn king's head, and their finger trembled, just slightly, "—this is the lock. You've drawn the lock. From a dream."

"It's getting closer," Pip whispered. "The man inside. Every night he's closer to the surface. Last night he cried." She swallowed. "Tonight I think he's going to ask me something. And I think the thing that wears him knows I can see him, and I think it wants me to come *closer*."

The word dropped into the fire-silence like a stone into a well.

Lior gathered the drawings up with sudden, urgent care, all three sheets, squinting at the sigils as if they might rearrange themselves if unwatched. Then they did something Pip hadn't seen them do before, not once, not ever, not even after Grayford Bridge when Dain was bleeding and the hunter Mallet was screaming on the far bank.

Lior looked afraid.

"Pip," they said. "Tomorrow we go through the journal. All of it. Every page." They paused, and their jaw tightened. "I need to know exactly what my grandmother chained to that throne — and how to open the cage before it opens itself."

Far down the tunnel, in the dark where the firelight couldn't follow, something that was not the wind sighed against the stone — long, patient, and pleased — and Pip, who saw truly, understood that it had heard every word.
