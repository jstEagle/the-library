## 7. The Marchetti Inheritance

The waystation announced itself as a wound in the mountainside long before they reached it — a black gap in the pines where the trees refused to grow, and above it, the broken silhouette of a roof like a snapped wing.

Lior saw it first, of course. They always did.

"There," they said, and Dain grunted and adjusted Pip's weight on his shoulders. The child had fallen asleep an hour back, her cheek pressed against his gorget-less collarbone, one hand curled loosely in his hair. He carried her the way he seemed to carry everything now — carefully, as if he'd already broken too many things to risk another.

"Waystations on the old salt road went up every twenty miles," Lior said, picking their way over scree. "Gran used to talk about them. Said her mother had been born in one."

"Your grandmother was born in a ruin?"

"She was born before it was a ruin." Lior smiled despite themselves. "There's a whole century of Thornfell that only exists in her voice, Dain. Roads that aren't roads anymore. Names nobody uses. She talked about this place — *Cinderhold*, she called it — said the hearthstones were cut from a single piece of mountain and never went cold."

They came around the last shoulder of rock and stopped.

Cinderhold crouched against the cliff face like something that had tried to burrow into it and failed halfway. Its walls were granite gone grey with lichen, its doorway a tall arch without a door, and through that arch Lior could see the great cold hearth — a hearthstone wide enough to sleep on, split down the middle by some violence decades old. Snow had drifted through the broken roof and lay in the corners like unmade beds.

But the sigil-lines didn't pass it by.

That was what stopped Lior's breath. Every other dead thing in Thornfell — every abandoned mine and burned farmstead — sat outside the land's living veins, forgotten even by the magic that ran beneath everything. Cinderhold sat *on* them. Lior could feel it from thirty feet away: a knot of lines converging under the floor, thick as tree roots, humming faintly against the soles of their boots. Not flowing toward anything. Converging. Gathering.

Like a road built to bring travelers somewhere. Or to bring something *here*.

"Lior?" Dain had read their stillness correctly; he shifted Pip higher and put himself half between Lior and the dark archway. Old habits. "Trouble?"

"No trouble." Lior wet their lips. "Something old. Come on — we need walls tonight anyway, and I don't think anything living has been here in years."

*Nothing living,* they thought, stepping over the threshold. *But maybe something else.*

* * *

They made camp in the manner of people who had gotten good at it in three hard weeks: Dain cleared and checked the corners while Lior coaxed a small fire alive in the split hearthstone — which, Gran had been right about everything else, still held a ghost of warmth, a stubborn coal-deep memory that made the flames catch faster than they should have. Pip woke just enough to eat half a heel of bread and drink broth from the dented pot, then folded herself into the bedroll against Dain's side and was gone again, breathing slow.

"She dreamed last night," Dain said quietly, when her breathing had evened out. "Wouldn't say what. Woke up crying but wouldn't say why, either."

"She'll tell us when she can." Lior fed the fire a stick. "Dreams are heavy things to carry at her size."

"You talk about it like you know."

"I know enough about carrying things you can't put down." They hadn't meant it to come out sharp. Dain took it without flinching, the way he took most things, and looked back at the fire, and let it go. That was the strange grace of him, Lior thought. A man who had spent his life being told where to stand had learned, in exile, how to simply be near someone without demanding anything of them.

Later, when the fire had settled and Dain slept sitting up with his sword across his knees — he would never lie flat, Lior suspected, not for years — Lior took the lamp and went to search the waystation properly.

Not for supplies. Supplies had been the excuse. But from the moment they'd crossed the threshold, the sigil-lines under the floor had been tugging at Lior's awareness like a fisherman's line, patient and insistent, and there was a quality to the pull they recognized the way you recognize your own name spoken in a crowd.

It felt *familiar*. It felt like home.

The main room gave up little: rotted shelving, a rusted chain, the bones of a chair. But behind the hearth, where the stone had cracked along the split, there was a gap — and set into the gap, wedged deep by hands that had wanted it hidden but findable, a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth and waxed thread.

Lior knew before they touched it.

Their fingers knew. There was a tingling in the pads of them, the same prickling that came when sigils glowed under their pen, and when they drew the packet out and turned it over, the dust fell away from a mark pressed into the wax seal:

A circle, quartered by a vertical line, with three dots descending along the line like steps going down into the earth.

The Marchetti mark. Their mark. The same one they inked, small and disguised as a surveyor's flourish, into the corner of every map they'd ever sold.

Lior sat down on the cold hearthstone with the packet in their lap and did not move for a long time.

* * *

The oilcloth crackled like old leaves. Inside were two things: a bundle of maps, brittle and brown, drawn in a hand Lior knew almost better than their own — because it was the hand theirs had been taught to imitate, stroke by stroke, at a kitchen table in a house that no longer stood — and beneath the maps, a journal bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth.

The first page bore the full Marchetti sigil, large and unhidden, and beneath it, in that beloved, terrible handwriting:

*For whoever of my blood finds this. If you are reading it, then the roads have led you home, as they were always going to. We do not choose the paths. We only draw them.*

Lior read until the candle guttered, then lit another, and read on.

* * *

Her name had been Serafina Marchetti, and she called herself the last honest liar in Thornfell.

*I have spent my life making maps,* the journal began, in an entry dated sixty-three years past, *and every map I ever sold was false, and every map I kept was worse than false, because the truth it showed is the truth I would burn if burning it would help. But paper does not burn hot enough. Nothing burns hot enough. So I write instead, and I hide this where only our blood will look, and I pray to whatever gods are left after what my family did that you will forgive us, or failing that, that you will finish what we started.*

Lior's hands had begun to shake by the third page. They gripped the hearthstone's edge and made themselves keep reading.

Serafina wrote of her grandmother — Lior's great-great-grandmother, Alessa Marchetti — and of a time when the kings of the realm had not yet worn the crown that wore them back. She wrote of Vhalgorren without naming it; the name appeared nowhere, scratched out in every place it must have been written, gouged so fiercely through the vellum in one entry that it tore. But she described it. A hunger older than the mountains. A thing that had come up from beneath the world in the age of the first kingdoms and eaten its way through them — through their gift-bearers first, drinking their magic, and then through everyone else, because a realm drained of its gifted folk is a realm with no defenses left at all.

*Alessa was the greatest cartographer of her generation,* Serafina wrote, *but that is not why history will curse her, if it ever learns. History will curse her because she was the one who understood that the Devourer could not be killed. It could only be bound. And a binding of that size requires an anchor of flesh and a leash of blood, and Alessa had both.*

The king. Some young king whose name Serafina recorded only as *the vessel* — chosen, Lior gathered with a nausea that climbed their throat like floodwater, precisely because he was beloved, because his death would have broken the realm, because who would ever suspect that the crown's own warding rites were a feeding trough?

*She bound it into him,* Serafina wrote. *My grandmother bound a god into a living man and called it salvation, and perhaps it was, for a hundred years, for two hundred. The Devourer sleeps in the vessel and cannot wake, and the vessel reigns, and the realm endures. That is the story. Here is the rest of it.*

Here Lior set the journal down.

The fire had burned low. In the bedroll across the room, Pip murmured in her sleep and turned, and Dain's hand moved to her hair without his eyes opening. Lior watched them for a moment — the disgraced knight and the dreaming child, the family assembled out of wreckage — and felt the full weight of what they were about to read settle onto their chest like a stone laid there deliberately, by their own ancestors, four generations deep.

Because Serafina had written the rest of it, and Lior had already guessed, and guessing had not blunted it at all.

*A binding is not a wall. It is a bargain. The Devourer agreed to sleep, but it did not agree to starve, and Alessa was not strong enough to make it starve. So the binding feeds it. Slowly. A trickle, not a feast — enough to keep it sleeping and satisfied, never enough to wake it. And the binding draws that food from the land itself, from the gift-veins, from every bearer born into the world our bloodline chained.*

Every bearer. Every gifted child in every village. The strange tiredness that settled on the gifted like a second shadow. The way some of them faded early, dimmed, died quiet. Lior had always blamed the crown's taxes, the hunters, the fear. They had never once thought to blame the mapmakers.

*And here is the crime beneath the crime,* Serafina wrote, and her handwriting changed here, grew cramped and furious, as though she were pressing the words into the page hard enough to leave bruises. *The crown knows. Or the thing wearing the crown's council knows. Somewhere in the last century, our secret became their system. They learned that the binding could be strengthened, the feeding hastened, if bearers were brought close to the anchor. They call it the warding rites. They harvest our people, Lior — whoever you are, whatever name you carry, they harvest our people — and they feed them to the thing my grandmother chained, and they tell the realm it is protection. We built the cage. They built the funnel.*

*We are not the victims of the hunt. We are its original architects. Every Marchetti since Alessa has known, and every Marchetti has kept drawing maps, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping —*

The entry broke off there. When it resumed, pages later, the handwriting was calm again, and worse.

*Stopping means the binding weakens. Stopping means it wakes hungry. I have done the arithmetic a hundred times and it always comes out the same: our silence buys the world its mornings. One child's gift, thinned across a kingdom, is nothing anyone can count. This is how monsters are made, I think. Not out of cruelty. Out of arithmetic.*

*So I draw my false maps and I pay my tithe of complicity and I teach my daughter the sigils, and I hate myself in a voice so quiet no one hears it, and the sun comes up, and the realm endures.*

*But I am old now, and my hands shake, and I have begun to wonder whether the arithmetic was ever true or only convenient. Alessa left notes — fragments — of a second working. An unbinding. She never attempted it. She said the price was too high. I have spent forty years deciding whether she meant the price was too high for the world, or only for a Marchetti.*

*I never decided. Forgive me. Perhaps you will.*

The final entries were short. Road-notes, mostly — the old salt road, the waystations, Cinderhold itself, marked with the converging-lines sigil and a single instruction: *The Underhold lies beneath. Alessa forged the binding there and hid the true rites there. If you have read this far, you are already walking toward it. We always are.*

The last entry was dated eleven days before a date Lior knew: the day the hunters came to the Marchetti house, the day Gran had handed six-year-old Lior a satchel of maps and a lie about visiting an aunt, the day the smoke on the horizon had taught them both what the satchel was really for.

The last line read: *If they come for me, I will burn the house but not the work. Let the work find you. It was always going to.*

* * *

Lior did not remember crossing the room. They were simply there, suddenly, kneeling in the frozen rushes beside Pip's bedroll with the journal crushed against their ribs, and Pip's eyes were open — dark and calm and far too awake for the hour — watching them with the particular patience of children who dream true.

"You found the book," Pip said.

"Yes."

"It's sad."

"It's—" Lior's voice caught. They smoothed the child's hair back from her forehead with a hand that would not quite steady. "Yes. It's sad."

Pip considered them for a moment, her head tipped. Then she reached up and touched Lior's cheek, once, the way you'd touch something to check it was real.

"You're not the bad ones," she said, with the absolute certainty of prophecy or of a five-year-old, and in Pip those had always been the same thing. "The book says bad ones, but you're not. You draw new roads."

Then she closed her eyes and was asleep again within a breath, as if she had delivered exactly the amount of mercy the night required and none more.

Lior stayed kneeling until their knees ached.

Across the room, Dain watched them through half-lidded eyes, saying nothing, offering nothing, simply present — and somehow that was worse than questions. Lior rose, returned to the hearth, and sat with the journal open on their knees, reading the fragments of Alessa's unbinding again and again until the words blurred.

*An unbinding requires a willing vessel of the maker's line,* the fragment said. *The gift that forged the chain must be poured out to break it. All of it. There is no partial version. There is no version where the maker walks away whole.*

All of it.

Lior looked at their hands — cartographer's hands, ink-stained, steady, the hands that had read the land's hidden veins since childhood, that had walked unmapped caverns and led their fragile new family through them when no other path existed. Without the gift they were just a person with a good memory for terrain. Without the gift, the Underhold's wards would stay shut. Without the gift, the dead road beneath the capital — if it existed, if Gran's hints were true — would stay dead.

Without the gift, they could not finish what they had started.

And with it — every day, every mile, every map — they fed the thing in the king. The trickle. The tithe. The arithmetic.

Outside, far off down the valley, a sound drifted on the wind that had no business sounding at this hour in these empty hills: the thin, carrying note of a hunting horn. Once. Twice. Then silence, deliberate as a signature.

Dain was on his feet instantly, sword drawn, standing over Pip. "Patrol?"

"Maybe." Lior closed the journal and slid it inside their coat, over their heart, where it lay like a second heartbeat. "Or maybe just wind through the pass."

They both knew neither of them believed that. Serah Vane had lost their trail in the caverns, but Serah Vane struck Lior as a woman who did not lose things permanently.

Lior banked the fire, shouldered their pack, and stood a moment in the doorway of Cinderhold looking north, toward the deep places the converging lines pointed, toward the Underhold and the true rites and the price waiting there with their family's name on it.

*We built the cage,* Gran's grandmother had written. *Perhaps you will finish what we started.*

"I will," Lior said aloud, to the dark, to the dead, to the child sleeping behind them. "But I'm doing it my way. And I'm not telling them the price."

Behind them, unnoticed, the sigil-mark on the journal's first page had begun — very faintly, in the firelight, like something answering — to glow.
