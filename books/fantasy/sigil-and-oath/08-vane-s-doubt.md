## 8. Vane's Doubt

The summons came at dawn, sealed in black wax.

Serah Vane stood in the courtyard of the Hunter's Bastion and turned the letter over twice before breaking it, because a black-sealed order could mean three things: promotion, execution, or something worse than either. The wax cracked under her thumbnail. She read the words once. Then again. Then she folded the paper with the precise care of a woman who had been taught that trembling hands were a luxury of civilians, and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where it sat like a stone.

*By command of His Majesty's Warding Office: Serah Vane, First Hunter, is ordered to present herself at the Undercroft of the Palace Wardens on the third day of Emberfall, there to oversee the collection of tribute for the realm's warding rites. Failure to comply will be treated as treason.*

*Tribute.* She knew what the word meant in the mouths of clerks. Every hunter did. They simply never said it aloud, the way soldiers never said *dying* when they meant *the wounded who don't come back*. Tribute was the gift-bearers taken in the Hunt — the registered ones, the ones whose names lived in ledgers with neat columns for their gifts and their villages and their value. They were brought to the capital in wagons with slatted sides, and they did not come back either.

Vane had told herself for eleven years that they were taken somewhere kind. That the crown, whatever its cruelties, would not spend what it needed whole. That the warding rites — the great protective workings that kept Thornfell's passes closed to invasion, that kept the plague out of the river-valleys, that kept the realm safe — required fuel, yes, but fuel given freely, or at least painlessly.

She had told herself this the way one tells oneself anything necessary: often, and without looking directly at it.

Hollowmere had burned a hole through all of it.

She thought of the ashes now as she saddled her horse in the grey light. She thought of the smell — not woodsmoke, which has a certain honesty to it, but the sweeter stink of burned thatch and burned wool and other things she refused to name even in her own head. She thought of the ledger she'd carried out of the village square, the registration list, forty-one names, and how she'd checked them against the bodies she could find and come up short by nine, and how Captain Mallet had said, *the young ones are always taken first, Vane, highest tribute-value*, in the flat tone of a man reading a supply manifest.

Highest tribute-value. Children.

She had not slept properly since. Her dreams had a smell now.

* * *

The Undercroft of the Palace Wardens lay beneath the eastern wing of the royal palace, and no map of the city marked it. Vane descended the servants' stair behind her escort — two silent men in undyed wool, faces like closed doors — down past the wine cellars, past the ice vaults, down stairs that grew older and stranger as they went, until the cut stone gave way to masonry so ancient it had no right to be beneath a palace at all. The walls here were carved with sigils worn nearly smooth, and Vane's skin prickled as she passed them, the way it pricked near a strong gift-bearer, except these were only marks in rock.

Only marks in rock, she told herself, and did not believe it.

The air changed at the bottom. It was warm. That was the first wrong thing — deep places should be cold — and it was warm the way a body is warm, with a faint animal humidity, and beneath the smell of tallow candles there was something else, something rich and faintly sweet, like overripe fruit, like blood left standing.

The corridor opened into a chamber that stole the breath from her lungs.

It was vast beyond reason, a hollowed cathedral of living rock, and every surface of it was sigiled — floor, walls, ceiling — in lines of silver that moved. Not glowed. *Moved*, crawling in slow rivers along their channels, converging from a hundred directions toward the center of the chamber, where a dais rose in tiers of black stone.

On the dais stood cages.

Not cages — Vane corrected herself even as she thought it — *cradles*. That was the word the attendants used, she would learn. Cradles of iron and glass, reclined at gentle angles, padded with white linen, each one holding a person. Some were old. Most were not. There was a boy of perhaps ten with his eyes open and utterly empty, staring at nothing. A woman with grey in her hair whose lips moved in endless silent prayer. A girl, younger than the boy, asleep or made to sleep, her chest rising and falling with terrible steadiness, too steady, metronome-steady, like something being kept rather than someone living.

And above them all, fed by the silver rivers of light, hung the crown.

It floated a hand's breadth above an altar of fused bone — actual bone, Vane realized with a lurch, the altar was *made* of bone, femurs and ribs and skulls melted together into a single grey mass — and the crown itself turned slowly, endlessly, though there was no wind. It was gold. It should have been beautiful. It was the most obscene thing she had ever seen, because it was *hungry*. She had no better word. Looking at it felt like looking into a mouth.

A warden-priest in white robes stood beside the dais, consulting a ledger. He looked up as Vane approached, and his face was mild, pleasant, the face of a country physician.

"First Hunter Vane." He smiled. "You've brought the Hollowmere remainder?"

"Nine bearers." Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "In the wagons above."

"Excellent. The rites are due within the fortnight; we were running thin." He said *running thin* the way a cook says *we're low on salt*. He gestured, and attendants in white began moving toward the stair. "You may observe, if you wish. Hunters rarely do, but those who do find it... reassuring, I'm told. To see the good their work protects."

"I would observe," Vane said, because the alternative was to turn and run, and she was still, in that moment, a soldier.

* * *

They brought the nine down in silence. None of them struggled. That was the detail that would haunt her longest, she thought afterward — not screaming, not begging, but the awful docility of people who had registered in good faith, who had believed the crown's promise that registration meant protection, who had walked into the cradles and lain back on the white linen because a priest in a pleasant face told them it was for the realm's warding.

The boy with the empty eyes went into the first cradle. Glass lowered over him. Sigils flared along its rim.

Vane watched the silver light leave him.

There was no gentler way to describe it, and she despised herself for reaching for one. The light rose out of the boy's chest like breath in winter, streaming upward in a thin bright thread, and where it passed, he *dimmed*. Not his skin — something behind his skin. Something that had been him. His fingers, which had twitched at the entry of the light, went still. His breathing slowed to match the girl's metronome rhythm. And the thread of light traveled up, up, into the rivers of silver, into the turning crown, and the crown drank it, and somewhere far above, in a throne room Vane had stood guard in a hundred times, a king she had sworn her life to drew a breath that was not entirely his own.

One by one. Nine threads. The woman praying never stopped moving her lips. The sleeping girl woke at the end, briefly, and looked straight at Vane across the chamber — found her eyes, held them — and Vane saw in that look no hatred at all, which was infinitely worse, only a question, patient and terrible: *why?*

Then the girl dimmed too, and the question stayed in Vane like a splinter driven under a nail.

"You see?" The warden-priest stood beside her, hands folded, voice warm with satisfaction. Above them the crown turned, sated for now, its hunger banked like a fire fed for the night. "The realm endures. Plague stays beyond the valleys. No foreign army has crossed Thornfell in two hundred years. Every gift given here becomes the shield over every hearth in the kingdom. There is no waste in it, First Hunter. No cruelty. Only arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Vane repeated.

"The alternative," he said gently, "is the Crown-Eater waking hungry. And then, Serah Vane, you would learn what cruelty is."

*Crown-Eater.* The name went through her like cold water. She had heard whispers of it — hunters gossiped, hunters always gossiped — a god beneath the palace, an old devouring thing bound and leashed and fed. She had filed it with the other ghost stories soldiers tell in the dark. But the priest said it the way one says *weather*. The way one says *winter is coming, and we must lay in stores*.

The stores were children.

Her faith did not crack all at once. Faiths are not glass; they are rope, and they part strand by strand. She stood in that warm, sweet-smelling dark while the last thread of light sank into the crown, and she felt a strand go, quietly, the one that had whispered *they are taken somewhere kind*. Another parted as she climbed the long stairs back into daylight: the one that said *the king knows, and permits it, and therefore it must be needful*. By the time she reached the courtyard, the rope was half-gone, and what remained was held together by discipline alone, and discipline, she knew from long service, is the weakest fiber of all, because it holds only until the day it decides not to.

* * *

That night she sat in her quarters in the Hunter's Bastion with her orders spread on the desk before her.

New orders. Black wax again. Mallet's seal beneath the Office's — Mallet had been promoted for Hollowmere, she'd heard, *for efficiency* — and the orders named her lead hunter for the next sweep: the Thornfell passes, the smuggler roads, the unregistered bearers fleeing west. Among the names flagged for priority capture, written in a clerk's careful hand, was a description she recognized from a tavern weeks ago and had chosen, in a mercy she still didn't understand, not to act on.

*A cartographer. Gifted. Last seen south of Hollowmere. Possibly traveling with a child.*

Possibly traveling with a child. The words sat on the page like an accusation aimed at her specifically, at the mercy she'd shown, at the hesitation Mallet had noted in his report — *Vane delayed; recommend observation* — and she understood suddenly that this assignment was also a test. Bring in the cartographer and the child, deliver them to the Undercroft, watch them lie down on the white linen, and the rope of her faith would be spliced and knotted and made serviceable again. Or fail, and hang.

She read the orders a third time. She thought of the girl's eyes finding hers across the ritual floor. *Why?*

She thought of the boy's dimming.

She thought of a stranger in a tavern with ink-stained fingers, who had looked at her across a crowded room and known — somehow *known* — what she was, and had not run, and whom she had let walk into the night for reasons she had told herself were tactical.

Outside her window, the capital glittered, ten thousand hearths shielded by the arithmetic of children. Somewhere beneath it all, a crown turned slowly above an altar of bone, digesting.

Serah Vane took the orders to the hearth. She fed them to the flame corner-first, watching the black wax bubble and the clerk's careful hand curl and brown and vanish, the description of the cartographer and the child going up in a breath of smoke that smelled, for one strange moment, like Hollowmere's ashes.

When the last corner whitened and crumbled, she sat back in the dark and understood what she had done. There was no returning from a burned order. There was only forward — west, into Thornfell, after the very people she'd been sent to take, for reasons she could not yet say aloud even to herself.

She packed light: blade, bow, bedroll, the registration ledger with its forty-one names, forty-one small debts she intended to pay.

At the gate, the sentry asked her business. "Royal hunt," she said, and showed him nothing, because there was nothing left to show.

The road west was black under the stars, and she rode it alone, carrying the splinter of a child's question and the memory of dimming light, toward the mountains, toward the fugitives, toward whatever remained of a faith that had burned faster than paper — and behind her, in the warm dark beneath the palace, the Crown-Eater turned in its sleep, and began, very softly, to grow hungry again.
