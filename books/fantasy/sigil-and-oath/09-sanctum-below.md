## 9. Sanctum Below

The journal said *follow the water that forgets to fall*, and for three days Lior had hated their grandmother with a passion that surprised them.

Not the clean hatred of betrayal — that had burned out somewhere around the second night, when they'd read what the Marchettis had done in full and understood it was too large a thing to hate properly. This was something smaller and meaner: the hatred of a cartographer for a mapmaker who wrote her directions like riddles because she could not bear to write them plainly. *Follow the water that forgets to fall.* What did that even mean? Water fell. That was its whole nature. It was the one honest thing in the world.

Then, on the third morning, huddled in the mouth of a cave above a gorge while Vane scouted the ridgeline and Dain slept with his sword across his knees, Lior saw it.

A stream came down the mountainside, white and loud, and at the lip of a granite shelf it simply — stopped. Not pooled. Not diverted. It poured over the edge and vanished into air, into nothing, as though the mountain had opened a throat beneath it and swallowed without sound. No splash. No echo. The water forgot to fall.

"There," Lior said, and their voice cracked on the word.

Pip looked up from where she was braiding grass into a crown for nobody, her small fingers stilling. "There's a hole there."

"There's a door there." Lior stood, joints stiff from cold stone, and felt the old familiar pull behind their eyes — the sigil-gift waking, reading the land's hidden lines the way other people read weather. Even now, even knowing what their bloodline had built with that gift, they couldn't stop it. The lines were all pointing down.

* * *

The descent took the rest of the day.

Dain went first with a torch, broad shoulders filling the crack in the rock, and Lior went second, mapping by feel when the light failed. The passage was natural stone at first — wet, indifferent, ancient in the way caves are ancient — but somewhere past the first hour the walls changed. Lior's fingers found grooves cut deliberate and deep, spiraling sigils worn nearly smooth by centuries of drip-water, and their gift flared so hard they staggered.

"What is it?" Vane asked from behind. She'd been quiet since Grayford was two days behind them, quiet the way soldiers are quiet when they're re-counting everything they believe.

"The walls are writing." Lior pressed their palm flat against the stone. "It's the same hand. The same hand as the journal." They almost said *my grandmother's hand* and couldn't. "These are way-marks. We're walking down a road someone made on purpose."

"A road to where?" Dain asked, though his voice suggested he already knew, and dreaded it.

"To the place where my family chained a god under a king."

No one answered that. Pip slipped her hand into Lior's, small and warm and trusting, and Lior held on tighter than they needed to.

The stair, when they reached it, was the first thing that stopped feeling like geology and started feeling like architecture. Steps cut wide enough for six abreast, descending in a spiral so vast the torchlight couldn't find either wall. And carved into every riser, marching down into the dark like a litany: the binding sigil. The one Pip had drawn in the dirt outside Hollowmere's ashes without knowing what it was. The one Lior had grown up seeing in the margins of family documents and never once asked about, because children don't ask about the things adults go silent over.

"You knew this mark before," Vane said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse than that. It was a fact being set down carefully, like a blade on a table.

"I knew it the way you know your own name. Without ever looking at it." Lior kept walking. "That's how they did it, I think. Hid the truth inside the ordinary. A family sigil. A crest. Who questions a crest?"

"Someone should have," Dain said.

"Yes," Lior said. "Someone should have. For four hundred years, someone should have."

* * *

They smelled the Underhold before they saw it: ink and dust and lamp-oil, the particular smell of a place where paper has been loved longer than people have.

The stair ended in a hall so large the torches became fireflies. Pillars rose out of darkness on either side, each one furred with shelves, and the shelves ran with books — thousands of them, tens of thousands, spines stacked sideways and upside-down, scrolls stuffed into gaps like birds nesting in a cliff. Chains of little copper lamps hung overhead, maybe a third of them lit, swaying faintly in air that should have been dead this far underground.

And in the middle of it all, at a desk drowning in open volumes, sat a woman who appeared to have been assembled from spare parts of several different decades. Grey braid half-unraveled. A man's coat gone green at the seams. Ink to both elbows. She was writing by the light of three candles and talking as she wrote, low and continuous, and it took Lior a moment to realize she was talking to the book.

"—no, that's wrong, you're wrong, I told Aldous in '61 that the third clause degrades if you copy it onto birch, birch drinks the intent right out of the ink, and did he listen—"

She looked up. Her eyes were pale and quick and utterly sane for exactly one second, long enough for Lior to see the intelligence burning behind them, and then she smiled and the sanity scattered like startled birds.

"Visitors," she said. "Four visitors and one small one. Down the stairs, past the weeping water. You're Marchetti."

Lior's blood went cold. Beside them, he heard Dain's hand move toward his sword.

"How—" Vane began.

"You walk like her." The woman stood, joints cracking, and came around the desk peering at Lior with frank, unembarrassed curiosity. "Sable Marchetti. Same walk. Weight on the balls of the feet, always ready to be climbing something. She used to come down every eleven years to correct my copies, and every eleven years she'd stand exactly where you're standing and look exactly that horrified." She stuck out an inky hand. "Wren. Archivist. Last archivist. Possibly last anything."

Lior took the hand. It was like shaking a bundle of sticks. "You knew my grandmother."

"Knew her? I argued with her for sixty years." Wren's grin showed teeth gone slightly crooked. "Best arguments of my life. She was wrong about the ninth clause, incidentally, and I will die knowing it, which is a comfort." Then the grin faded, and for a moment — again just a moment — her face was clear and grave and terribly tired. "You've read the journal."

"All of it."

"Then you know why you're here, even if you haven't let yourself say it yet." Wren turned, coat flapping, and began walking down an aisle of books, talking over her shoulder. "Come along. Mind the third shelf, it bites. Well. It doesn't bite. But it's load-bearing in a way that makes people nervous."

* * *

She led them to a round chamber off the main hall, walled entirely in copper panels engraved edge to edge with script. In the center, sunk into the floor, was a basin of black stone the size of a wagon wheel, dry as bone. Lior's gift screamed the moment they crossed the threshold — not in fear, they realized, but in recognition. Every line of power in the world bent through this room. This was the loom. This was where the thread had been tied.

"This is the Underhold proper," Wren said, lighting more candles with the brisk economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times. "Forged — well, *built*, forging implies heat, though there was heat, there was a great deal of heat — in the year the Crown-Eater came down out of the northern dark wearing a famine like a cloak. Your ancestors weren't villains, whatever the journal made you think. Read it again with kinder eyes. They were desperate people facing a thing that ate desperation."

"It wears the king now," Dain said. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man reporting terrain. "I served him. I carried his shield. There were days he looked at me like a drowning man looks at a boat, and I thought it was melancholy. I thought kings were allowed to be sad."

"He's been drowning for two hundred years," Wren said. "Vhalgorren doesn't possess, knight. It *inhabits*. There's a difference, and the difference is that the king is still in there. Small. Packed away. Watching through his own eyes while it eats his kingdom one gifted child at a time." She said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather, and somehow that was the worst version of it. Pip made a small sound and Lior pulled her close without thinking.

"So we kill it," Vane said. "I've killed things bigger than a man. Tell me how."

"You don't kill it. Four hundred years of your order trying to *manage* it, hunter, and you want to skip straight to killing?" Wren laughed, and it wasn't a kind sound. "You can't kill a hunger. You can only stop feeding it. Which brings us—" she spread her arms at the basin, at the copper walls, at the whole buried architecture of the Marchetti sin "—to the unbinding. The true rite. The one Sable hid in a journal and sent downhill like a message in a bottle, praying someone in her line would eventually grow a spine."

Lior stepped forward. Their heart was pounding, but their mind — their mind was doing what it always did, falling into the map, tracing routes, finding the path through. "Show me."

Wren studied them for a long moment. Whatever she saw seemed to settle something in her.

"The binding has seven anchors," she said. "Seven knots in the leash, laid into the king's coronation oath, renewed at every 'warding ceremony' your crown has ever held. To unbind, you must untie all seven at once, in the room where the oath lives, while the entity is awake and looking at you. Each knot takes a stroke of living sigil-work — not copied, not recited. Drawn fresh, by gift, in the moment." She tapped the copper wall. "The original binding took nine Marchettis working together and it cost three of them their minds. One person alone—"

"One person alone can't do it," Lior said. "So we find another bearer. Vane, the harvest camps, there must be—"

"No." Wren's voice cracked across the room like a whip, and for a heartbeat the madness was gone entirely and something ancient and sorrowful looked out of her face. "Listen to me, child of Sable. Hear the part she saved for last because she couldn't bear to write it plainly. The knots aren't untied *with* the gift." She pointed one inky finger at Lior's chest. "They're untied *out of* it. The rite doesn't use your power. It spends it. All of it. Every knot you loose pours what you are into the unraveling, and when the seventh knot falls, there is nothing left. No lines. No paths. No reading the land like a lover reads a face. Silence, forever, in a world that will finally be singing."

The silence after that was absolute. Somewhere far above, impossibly, they could hear the stream forgetting to fall.

"That's the price," Wren said softly. "Hope with a bill attached. A Marchetti's willing sacrifice — willing, it must be willing, the rite tests it, it will refuse a coerced heart — of the gift itself. Not your life. Something the entity thinks is crueler than your life."

Pip's hand tightened in Lior's. Dain had gone very still. Vane was looking anywhere else in the room, jaw working, a soldier confronted with an arithmetic she couldn't solve.

And Lior — Lior stood in the loom-room where their bloodline had forged the world's longest cruelty, and felt the land's living lines humming through the soles of their boots, through their bones, through the secret place behind their eyes where the sigils lived — and understood, with the terrible clarity of a map suddenly complete, that they had already decided.

They just hadn't told anyone yet.

"Well," said Wren, and relit a candle that hadn't gone out, because her hands were shaking. "Now that's settled in somebody's head, if not anybody's mouth — we should talk about how to get into the throne room alive."

Above them, faint as a memory, the water went on refusing to fall.
