## 10. The Price Named

Dain Corvath had stood in the presence of kings and had never once felt small. He had knelt before Aldric the Third on the day of his anointing, armor bright as morning, and sworn an oath that had cost him everything he'd been taught to want. He had held a shield line at Redwater Ford against three times his number. He had refused to burn children in a village called Hollowmere, and he had walked away from thirty years of honor with nothing but the sword at his hip and the shame in his chest.

None of it — not the oath, not the ford, not the burning refusal — had prepared him for the way Wren said *price*.

The archivist's sanctum was a cathedral of paper grown out of stone. Shelves had been carved directly into the Underhold's living rock, and every one of them sagged under ledgers, folios, scrolls bound in waxed linen, loose sheaves weighted down with river stones. The air smelled of dust and tallow and something older beneath both — cold mineral water, the breath of deep places. A single lamp burned at the center of the chamber, its flame the color of old honey, and around it they had gathered: Lior with their arms crossed tight, Pip curled into Dain's side like a burr, Vane leaning against a shelf with her arms folded in a soldier's rest, and Wren, who moved like a man made of twigs and secrets.

"The unbinding is not a cutting," Wren said. He spoke the way he did everything, in a dry whisper, as though loudness might wake something better left asleep. "You cannot simply sever the binding. The binding is not a rope around the king. It is a root system. It runs through him, through the crown, through the Underhold itself, through—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the distant weight of the capital above them, "—through every gift-bearer whose power has been poured into it since the first Marchetti set stylus to stone."

"Then how?" Vane asked. Her voice was flat, controlled. She had been flat and controlled since Grayford Bridge, since Mallet's blade had opened Dain from collarbone to ribs and her own hand had cut down the partner she'd served beside for eleven years. Dain understood. Grief needed somewhere to go, and hers had gone into stillness.

"There are two ways to end a binding," Wren said. "Break it, or unmake it. Breaking kills the king and feeds the thing wearing him — Vhalgorren would tear free with centuries of harvested strength and no anchor to hold it in one shape. It would walk out of that throne room as a god." He let that sit in the lamplight. Pip's small fingers tightened on Dain's sleeve. "Unmaking is different. Unmaking requires the binding be *fed back*. Every thread must be drawn out through the hand that holds the stylus, and the hand must give something of equal weight to what it draws."

"Equal weight," Lior repeated. Their voice was careful. Too careful. Dain had spent a career reading men's faces across war tables; he knew when someone was measuring their words like coin.

"The rite demands a willing sacrifice," Wren said. "Not blood. Not death — though death may come of it, I will not lie to you. It demands the gift entire. The Marchetti sigil-gift, surrendered freely, poured into the unraveling until there is nothing left to pour. The land's hidden paths will go dark to them forever. And the pouring..." His twig-thin hands turned over, palms up, helpless. "The last time anything like this was attempted, the bearer did not survive the final stroke. The gift is not separate from the bearer, you understand. It grows through them like roots through soil. Pulling it whole is pulling the tree."

Silence took the chamber. The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

Dain looked at Lior. They were staring at the journal open on the reading table — their grandmother's journal, the Marchetti inheritance, the book that had told them their blood built the cage and the leash both. Their face was composed in that particular way Dain had come to know on the road: the cartographer's face, the face they wore when the pass ahead was dangerous and they wanted no one frightened. Calm as inked lines. Nothing showing underneath.

Pip stirred against his side. "Lior won't do it," she said, with the absolute certainty of a child stating weather. "It's too big."

"No one is doing anything tonight," Dain said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. He put his hand on her hair. "Wren. Give us the night. All of us need sleep, and none of us should decide anything with Mallet's hunters somewhere behind us and the capital ahead."

Wren nodded slowly. "Sleep, then. But know this — the rite wants willingness. It will refuse any hand that comes to it unwilling or unworthy of the cost. That is not poetry. That is mechanics. The First Binding was written by a Marchetti who understood that a sacrifice coerced is no sacrifice at all." He gathered his robes about him and shuffled toward the deeper shelves. "I will be among the records if you need me. I am always among the records."

* * *

They argued in the small chamber Wren kept for guests, a hollow in the rock lined with wool blankets gone gray with age and a brazier that gave more smoke than heat. Vane barred the door out of habit. Pip fell asleep almost at once, exhausted past dreaming, her head in Lior's lap while Lior sat rigid against the wall, and Dain watched them both and waited until the child's breathing went slow and even before he spoke.

"It should be me."

Lior's head came up. Vane straightened from the wall.

"Don't," Lior said quietly.

"Hear me out." Dain leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the motion pulled at the bandages across his chest — Vane had stitched him twice since Grayford, and the wound ached like a sermon. "I've been turning it over since Wren finished speaking. The rite wants a willing sacrifice. Wants it, needs it, refuses anything less. Fine. Then ask what each of us can actually offer. Your gift is the key — yes, I know, the binding answers to Marchetti blood, only a Marchetti can hold the stylus. But the *sacrifice*, the thing poured in alongside it — Wren didn't say it had to be your gift alone. He said the rite demands a willing sacrifice of equal weight. My weight is thirty years of oaths. My strength, my sword-arm, whatever's left of my honor. I have spent my whole life being useful to a crown that didn't deserve it. Let me spend the rest of it on something that does."

"You're not listening to the mechanics," Vane said. She'd gone to stand near the brazier, firelight carving the planes of her face. "Wren said the gift grows through the bearer like roots. You don't have roots, Corvath. You have scars. There's a difference."

"I have enough."

"You have a hole in your chest that I sewed shut two days ago," she said. "You couldn't hold a stylus steady through a grocery list, let alone an unbinding."

"Then I hold something else. I hold the line, like Grayford. Someone has to stand between them and whatever comes out of that king when the threads start tearing. That's mine. That's always been mine." He looked back at Lior. "And you — you're the mapmaker. You're the one who finds the road where there isn't one. If we pull this off, this realm is going to need people who can read the land again, people who aren't owned by anyone. Pip might carry something of it. Someone has to teach her. Someone has to *stay*."

"Lior stays," Pip murmured, half-asleep, and Dain's heart turned over.

"They stay," Dain agreed softly. "That's the point."

Lior was silent for a long moment. In the brazier light their face was unreadable, shadowed, the scar through one eyebrow catching orange. When they finally spoke, their voice was level and reasonable and Dain believed not one word of it.

"Wren said the rite refuses the unwilling and the unworthy. It also refuses the *unfitting*, if you read the marginalia closely — I read it twice while you two were arguing over the door bar. The sacrifice has to answer the binding. Like calls to like. The binding was woven from gift-stuff, so gift-stuff is what unravels it. Your honor is very fine, Dain, but it isn't woven into the cage." They smoothed Pip's hair back from her forehead, gentle as anything, and their eyes never left Dain's. "It has to be me. We all know it has to be me. So the question isn't who offers. The question is whether I survive it, and Wren doesn't know, and no one knows, and arguing about it changes nothing."

"So that's it?" Vane's voice cracked its own stillness. "We just — accept? Walk into the throne room knowing the price might be everything?"

"We walk in because the alternative is Vhalgorren walking out," Lior said. "Yes. We accept."

Dain searched their face for the fear that had to be there, the grief, the terror of a person told they might be unmade. He found composure. Only composure, smooth as a charted coastline, and some old knight's instinct in him — the same instinct that had made him refuse the torch at Hollowmere — whispered that coastlines drawn too smooth were coastlines someone had chosen not to survey honestly.

But Pip was asleep in their lap, and Vane was watching with wet eyes she'd deny to her grave, and there was no good moment to press. There never was.

"All right," he said at last. "All right. But hear my oath on it, Lior Marchetti, because I mean to keep it the way I failed to keep the last one. Whatever happens in that throne room — whatever the rite asks, whatever the thing in the crown offers — you do not go into the dark alone. If the price comes due, I pay beside you. That's not negotiable. That's the shield-oath, and you're stuck with it."

Something flickered across Lior's face — quick, quickly buried. Gratitude, or guilt, or both wearing the same mask.

"Stuck with it," they echoed. "Go to sleep, Ser Corvath. You're no use to anyone bleeding through your bandages."

* * *

Dain did not sleep. He dozed in the way soldiers doze, surfacing at every sound, and so he was awake — or near enough — when Lior rose in the deepest hour of the night, easing Pip's head onto the blanket with a tenderness that hurt to watch.

He kept his breathing slow and even. Kept his eyes slitted. Watched Lior take the lamp and cross to the far corner of the chamber, where Wren's records lay stacked, and kneel, and open the journal, and read by honey-colored light with lips moving silently.

He watched them read the same passage four times. He watched their finger trace a line of it, stop, trace it again. He watched their shoulders change — a settling, a decision taking up residence in the body the way a stone takes up residence in a streambed, altering the flow of everything around it forever after.

And he watched them take out the little kit they carried everywhere, the cartographer's kit, pens and inks and scraps of good paper, and begin to write. Not a map. A letter, the pen moving fast, the handwriting cramped with urgency. They wrote for a long time. Twice they stopped and pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. Once they looked back at Pip sleeping and Dain saw their mouth move around words too quiet even for the lamplight to carry — *not you, little dreamer, never you* — and whatever the letter was, Dain understood its shape without reading a word of it.

A letter for afterward. Instructions for the ones left standing. A will.

His chest wound throbbed in time with his heartbeat, and he lay in the dark and hated himself for the cowardice of pretending sleep, and hated more the certainty settling over him like frost: Lior had already decided. Not *might* die. Had already stepped off the edge in their heart and was now simply arranging the world for the fall — folding the family's future into neat squares the way they folded maps, so it would travel well without them.

When the letter was done, Lior sealed it with a drop of wax and a thumbprint — no sigil, Dain noticed, no glow, as if they could not bear to mark it with the thing they meant to lose — and tucked it inside the journal, beneath the grandmother's pages, where no one would look unless looking was already too late.

Then they came back to the blankets. They settled Pip against their side. They closed their eyes.

"Lior," Dain said quietly.

Their eyes opened instantly, fully alert. Cartographer's reflexes. "Mm?"

"You were reading the marginalia again."

For a heartbeat, silence. Then, mild as milk: "Someone should know what we're walking into."

"Mm," Dain said.

He let it go. He watched their breathing lengthen into something like sleep, and he stared at the ceiling of the Underhold and made his own silent arithmetic, the soldier's kind, the kind that ends in plans rather than letters. If the rite demanded a life, he would be between Lior and that demand before the demand finished speaking. If the rite refused him — if it truly answered only to gift-stuff and Marchetti blood — then he would find another way, because there was always another way, because he had once held a bridge at Redwater Ford for six hours on a broken leg and a promise, and promises were the only currency he had ever been rich in.

Above them, miles of stone and earth and the sleeping capital, something ancient turned over in a stolen body and dreamed of feeding.

Below, in the honey-dark of the sanctum, the lamp guttered and steadied, and the sealed letter lay hidden in a dead woman's journal, holding the truth that Lior Marchetti had already paid — in intention, which the rite, perhaps, could smell — and told no one.

Dain closed his eyes at last. Tomorrow they would climb toward the throne room, and the plan would unfold like one of Lior's maps, every route marked, every danger charted.

Every route except the one Lior had already chosen to walk alone.

And Dain Corvath, who had lost one king to his conscience, swore into the dark that he would not lose this one person the same way — even if he had to follow them into the rite itself and tear the price out of its hands.
