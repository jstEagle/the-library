## 11. Ambush at Grayford Bridge

### Dain

The bridge announced itself before they saw it — a long gray spine of stone arcing over the Grayford river gorge, its far end swallowed by pine shadow. Dain had known it in another life. He had crossed it a dozen times in the king's livery, armor bright, banner snapping, escorting magistrates and tax-collectors and once, memorably, a bride to her wedding in the hill country. In those days the bridge had been a road. Now it was a throat, and he could feel the hand that meant to close around it.

He reined his horse to a stop where the treeline broke, and Lior drew up beside him.

"You feel it too," they said. Not a question. The cartographer's eyes had gone distant, unfocused, the way they did when the land spoke to them — when the hidden lines of the world rose up like veins beneath skin. Dain still didn't fully understand the gift. He only knew that when Lior went quiet like that, it was wise to listen.

"Tell me," he said.

"There's a knot on the far side." Lior's voice was low, careful, the voice of someone reading a map drawn in blood. "Men. Six, maybe seven. And something else — something heavy. Iron and old prayer." Their jaw tightened. "A hunter's warding. It's not Vane."

Dain's stomach dropped through the saddle. He had hoped — foolishly, perhaps — that Serah Vane's failure at the caverns would buy them more time. But the crown did not send one hunter after quarry it considered precious. It sent two.

"Mallet," he said.

"You know him."

"I trained him." The words tasted like copper. "Serah Vane thinks with her heart, even when she pretends otherwise. Mallet doesn't have one. He has doctrine. He'll have picked the ground days ago, and this—" Dain gestured at the long, naked span of the bridge, the sheer drop to the white water below, the single road through the gorge walls on either side— "this is perfect ground for him. We can't go around. The gorge runs twenty miles to the ford at Marrow's Rest, and Pip can't ride twenty miles without rest."

Behind them, the wagon creaked. Pip's small face appeared between the slats, dark-circled eyes wide. She hadn't slept properly since the dreams began — the crown made of teeth, the screaming king, the name she'd whispered to Lior two nights past that had made the cartographer go gray as ash. *Vhalgorren.* Dain didn't know what the word meant yet. He only knew it lived in the child now, and that everything they did, they did to keep her ahead of it.

"We could wait for dark," Vane offered. She sat her horse apart from the others, as she always did — close enough to help, far enough to remind them she hadn't yet earned a place at their fire. Her hunter's grays were patched with undyed wool now, the royal sigil cut away, but she still wore her sword like a sentence.

"He'll expect that," Dain said. "Mallet always assumes his enemy is cleverer than he is, so he plans for cleverness. What he never plans for is stupidity." He swung down from the saddle and began unbuckling his shield from the pack-horse. "So we won't be clever."

Lior stared at him. "Dain—"

"I hold the bridge. You take the wagon down the goat track on the north wall — there's one, I remember it, narrow but passable if you walk the horses. By the time Mallet realizes the wagon's gone, I'll have given you an hour." He cinched the shield strap across his forearm, testing the weight. The old familiar weight. His arm remembered it better than his conscience did.

"That's not stupid," Lior said quietly. "That's suicide with extra steps."

"It's arithmetic." He tried to make it light and failed. "One old knight against seven men on ground where only two can fight abreast. That's not a battle, Marchetti. That's a bottleneck with me in it. I can hold a bottleneck."

"For how long?"

"As long as it needs holding."

Pip had climbed down from the wagon. She stood at the edge of the trees, small hands fisted in her skirt, and looked at him with those too-old eyes. "You come back," she said. It wasn't a plea. Children who dreamed true didn't plead; they stated. "You come back or I'll dream you wrong."

Something in Dain's chest cracked along an old fault line. He knelt, armor clanking, until his eyes were level with hers.

"Then dream me loud," he said. "Dream me so hard the whole river hears it."

She threw her arms around his neck. He held her for three breaths — he counted them, the way he counted everything now, hoarding time like a miser — and then he stood, took up his sword, and walked out onto the bridge to meet the morning.

* * *

The mist off the river hung waist-deep across the stones, and Dain walked into it alone, boots ringing on granite, and felt — absurdly, wonderfully — like himself for the first time since Hollowmere burned.

For fifteen years he had been the king's shield. He had sworn the oath at seventeen with his hand on the crown's own altar, and he had believed every word of it: *the realm above my life, the crown above the realm, the king above all.* Then he had ridden to Hollowmere with fire-orders in his pouch and found children behind the doors he was meant to break, and he had learned that oaths are only rope, and rope will hang you as gladly as save you. He had turned his horse from the burning village and become, in a single night, a man without a banner.

But a man without a banner, standing on a bridge with a shield on his arm, was just a man again. Simpler. Cleaner.

They came out of the far mist in a wedge — six soldiers in crown black, and behind them a figure in gray who needed no introduction. Mallet had grown broader since Dain last saw him, thickened through the shoulders like a door built to stop things, and he walked with the unhurried certainty of a man who had already read the ending of the story. His warden's chain hung across his chest, each link stamped with a saint's name, and Dain knew from bitter experience that those links were not decoration. They drank gift-bearers' strength out of the air itself.

"Corvath." Mallet stopped ten paces off. His voice was mild, almost kind, which was the worst thing about him. "I grieved you, you know. A proper service. The chaplain wept."

"I'm told the eulogy was lovely."

"'A good soldier lost to sentiment.'" Mallet tilted his head. "I'd have written it differently. *A good soldier lost to arithmetic.* You've done the sums by now, surely. One of you against six of us. Even you can't make those numbers sing."

"I don't need them to sing," Dain said. "I need them to bleed slowly."

Mallet sighed, genuinely regretful, and lifted two fingers.

The wedge came on. Dain met the first man at the bridge's narrowest point, where the parapet had crumbled and the road shrank to little more than a cart's width, and the world contracted to the oldest grammar he knew: *shield, step, strike.* The first soldier went into the water screaming. The second caught Dain's shield-rim across the teeth and followed him. The third was better — he got inside the shield's arc and put a knife into the gap below Dain's pauldron, and Dain killed him for it with a short, ugly efficiency that no ballad would ever want.

Three down. His breath came ragged. The knife-wound leaked warmth down his ribs, and the mist drank it pink.

"Still stubborn," Mallet observed, from beyond the remaining three. "You know what I don't understand, Corvath? Sentiment is expensive. Every man who dies on this bridge dies because you love people you've known a season more than the realm you served your whole life. That's poor accounting."

"The realm," Dain said, spitting blood, "is a *word*. These people are real."

"Ah." Mallet nodded slowly. "There it is. The disease in a sentence."

And then the fourth soldier screamed — not from Dain's doing, but because a crossbow bolt had sprouted from his thigh, loosed from the northern treeline, and Dain turned in disbelief to see Serah Vane standing on the high bank with her repeater leveled, her face white as the river-foam.

"Vane," Mallet said, and for the first time his mildness cracked. "You're supposed to be *dead*."

"No," Vane called back, and her voice shook, and she fired anyway. "I'm supposed to be *awake*."

* * *

### Vane

Later — much later, when her hands finally stopped shaking — Serah Vane would try to find the exact moment she decided. She never could. Decisions like hers weren't moments; they were rivers, gathering from a hundred tributaries until one day the banks burst.

There was the tavern, and the cartographer's glowing map, and the strange hesitation in her own hand as she'd reached for her irons. There was Hollowmere — God, Hollowmere, the smell of it, the way the registered families had lined up so trustingly because the crown had *told* them registration meant protection. There was the thing she'd seen in the ashes that she had never reported, because there were no words in a hunter's report for a burn-pattern shaped like a mouth.

And there was the ritual. Two weeks ago. The warding rite at Fort Calloway, which she had attended eleven times in her career and never once been permitted to watch closely. This time Captain Mallet had made her watch. Had made her stand at the rail while they brought the bearers down into the warding chamber — a farmer, a weaver, a girl of perhaps nine whose gift was making flowers bloom out of season — and fasten them to the harvesting frames, and open them like letters.

She had watched the light leave the girl. She had watched it travel up the conduits, silver-bright, toward the capital, toward the palace, toward whatever wore the king these days. And she had understood, with the terrible clarity that comes too late to be mercy, that everything she had believed about the Gift Hunt — *containment, safety, order* — was a story told to the hunters so the hunters would tell it to the hunted.

She had burned her orders that night. All of them. In the fort's chapel, page by page, feeding the fire with a soldier's discipline.

Now she stood on the north bank of the Grayford with a crossbow in her hands and her former partner's soldiers dying on the bridge below, and the last tributary joined the river.

"Hold the span!" Mallet roared at his remaining men, and then he did the thing she should have predicted, because he was Mallet and he always chose the crueler sum: he ignored Dain entirely and started forward along the bridge, warden's chain raised, toward the wagon track where Lior and the child were even now leading the horses down the cliff path.

Vane reloaded with fingers that refused to hurry. Bolt, groove, crank. Below her, Dain saw Mallet coming and dragged himself sideways into his path, shield up, bleeding freely now from the knife-wound and a new gash along his scalp, and she heard him say, almost conversationally:

"Not today, Captain."

"Out of my way, Corvath. You're a dead man defending ghosts."

"I'm a live man defending *people*. There's a difference. You wouldn't know it — it's not in the doctrine."

Mallet struck him with the chain. Vane saw the ward-links flare, saw Dain convulse as the blessed iron drank at whatever faint gift lived in every human soul, saw him go to one knee — and she put her next bolt through the shoulder of the soldier raising his blade over Dain's back, and slung the crossbow, and drew her sword, and ran down onto the bridge.

Her boots rang on the stone. Ten years of drills rang in her wrists. The fifth soldier turned to meet her and she killed him with a form she'd learned at fourteen, the one her instructor had called *the honest cut*, and she felt nothing except the rightness of it, cold and clean as well-water.

Which left Mallet.

He straightened over Dain's fallen body and looked at her across ten feet of bloody granite, and his face did something she had never seen it do in all their years together. It softened.

"Oh, Serah." Genuine sorrow. That was the horror of him — the sorrow was genuine. "You were the best of us. You *are* the best of us. Whatever they showed you, whatever lies the bearers fed you—"

"They didn't show me anything," Vane said. "Your own ritual did. Your own *harvest*. I watched a nine-year-old drained to light the king's candles, and I watched you take notes."

"The realm requires—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Don't you dare say *realm* to me on this bridge. I've been saying *realm* for ten years and it turns out the realm is one old man screaming inside his own body and a thing underneath the palace eating children. I know what I serve now, Mallet. Do you?"

He came at her.

Fighting Mallet was fighting a fortress that had studied her for a decade. He knew her openings; she'd shown him half of them herself, sparring in the fort yards, laughing. He drove her back along the span, chain whistling, and every near-miss of the blessed iron made her vision stutter and her knees lie to her. He was stronger. He was fresher. He was better armored.

But he had never once fought angry, and she was drowning in it.

She gave him her left side — the old feint, the one he'd punished a hundred times — and when he committed to punish it again, she dropped under his chain and put her sword through the gap beneath his arm, up and inward, the honest cut, the killing cut, the one they taught last of all because it couldn't be taken back.

Mallet looked down at the blade in his chest with an expression of scholarly disappointment, as though she had made an error in a proof.

"Arithmetic," he said, and fell.

The bridge went quiet. The river roared on below, indifferent, carrying away what it carried.

Vane stood over him, chest heaving, sword dripping, and waited to feel damned. She waited a long time. What came instead was lighter and stranger: the feeling of a pack slipping off a shoulder carried too many miles. She sheathed her sword, knelt beside Dain — alive, groaning, his hand pressed to the ruin of his ribs — and got her shoulder under his arm.

"Up," she said. "You're heavy, Corvath. All that honor. It weighs a ton."

He laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough came up red.

* * *

### Lior

They found them at the bridge's foot — Vane half-carrying Dain, both of them striped red, the mist curling around them like something trying to reclaim its dead. Lior ran the last fifty yards and took Dain's other side without a word, and together they bore him to the wagon, to the blankets, to Pip's small competent hands and the bundle of herbs Wren had pressed on them at parting.

The wound was bad. Lior had seen enough hurt men to know the shape of bad: the knife below the pauldron had gone deep, and the chain's touch had left gray fingerprints on his spirit that no needle would close. He drifted in and out, and when he surfaced he always asked the same question.

"The wagon? The road clear?"

"Clear," Lior lied gently, the second lie their family had ever told, though unlike Dain's lie to Pip, this one was kind. "We're clear. Sleep."

It was Pip who sat vigil, Pip who crushed yarrow and packed the wound with a steadiness that frightened Lior more than any scream would have, Pip who murmured to him in the fever-dark things no child should know how to say. And it was Pip who, on the second night, as the campfire burned low and Dain's breathing went shallow and quick, lifted her head suddenly from his side and said, in a voice stripped of all childhood:

"He stays. I checked."

Lior crossed the camp and knelt beside her. "Checked where, love?"

"In the dreaming." Her eyes reflected the fire like a cat's. "I went looking. The crown-of-teeth knows we're coming now — it turned its head, I *felt* it turn — and it wanted Dain dead, and I argued." She said *argued* the way other children said *played*. "I won. Barely. But Lior—" her small hand closed on their sleeve, hard as a trap— "it knows the name Marchetti now. It said it. It laughed when it said it. Like it's been waiting a long, long time for a Marchetti to come walking toward it."

Across the fire, Vane looked up sharply from sharpening her sword. "Waiting for what? Why?"

Lior's grandmother's journal weighed against their ribs like a second heart, and inside it, in faded brown ink, the binding-sigil that matched the one Pip had drawn without knowing, and beneath it a line Lior had read a hundred times and only now truly heard:

*We bound it with our blood, and so it will know us, always, until the debt is paid.*

"I don't know," Lior said, and held the child as the fire burned down, and did not sleep at all.
