## 12. Tending Wounds

Pip counted the ways a person could burn without fire.

Dain was doing all of them at once. He lay on the pallet in the ruined mill's loft, and even in sleep — *especially* in sleep — his body fought something invisible. Sweat darkened his hair and pooled in the hollow of his throat. His lips moved around words that never quite came out, like a man trying to shout underwater. Every few minutes his whole frame would seize, back arching off the straw, hands clawing at nothing, and then he'd collapse again, breathing in great ragged pulls as if he'd been running from something only he could see.

The wound itself was almost small. That was the worst part, Pip thought. A gash along his ribs where Mallet's blade had found him on the bridge, stitched shut by Serah Vane with the neat, merciless stitches of a woman who had sewn men up before sending them back to die. The cut wasn't deep enough to kill. But the fever had come up out of it like smoke from a banked fire, and now it owned him.

"You should sleep," Vane said from the doorway. She had a bucket in one hand and her sword belt still buckled on, which Pip thought was silly, because they were three days from anywhere and Mallet was either dead or very far away, but she wore it anyway, like she didn't trust quiet places anymore. Maybe she didn't. Pip didn't trust them either, lately.

"I'm keeping watch," Pip said.

"Of what? He's not going anywhere."

"That's what you think when someone's sick," Pip said. "That they can't go anywhere. But they can. They can go somewhere you can't follow." She knew this in the way children know things — completely, without evidence, the knowledge simply *there*, the way her dreams were there. "I'm making sure he stays."

Vane looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind the hunter's eyes — that flicker Pip had learned to read, the one that meant Vane was remembering Hollowmere, remembering the children who hadn't been kept. Then she set down the bucket and crossed the loft and did an unexpected thing: she pulled her cloak off and laid it over Dain's shivering body, tucking it under his chin with two fingers, careful as a woman handling a snare.

"Yell if the fever climbs past his eyes," she said. "If they roll white and stay white, yell louder."

Then she went down the ladder, and Pip was alone with the burning man and the rain.

* * *

The mill creaked. Rain worked at the broken roof in patient handfuls, dripping through onto the floorboards in a rhythm that Pip had already memorized: three drops fast, one slow, a pause, then three more. She'd decided the rhythm meant something. She hadn't decided what yet.

She sat cross-legged beside the pallet with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, and she watched Dain the way she'd once watched a bird with a broken wing her father had tried to save, back before her father was ash on the wind with everyone else in Hollowmere. You couldn't help by touching. You could only witness. Witnessing was its own kind of work. Nobody told children that, but Pip had figured it out.

"Ser Dain," she whispered, because Vane called him that sometimes, half-mocking, and because names were important. Names were *very* important. Pip knew that better than anyone, because she carried names around in her head that nobody had given her — names that arrived in the night wearing dreams like stolen coats. "You have to stay. You're the shield. Shields don't get to leave."

His face twitched. In the fever-light of the single candle, he looked younger than he ever did awake — younger and older at once, the way sleeping people did, all their pretending stripped off. Awake, Dain Corvath was a wall: gray-eyed, granite-jawed, a man built to stand between danger and other people. Asleep, he was just a man with lines carved around his mouth and a scar through one eyebrow and terror moving behind his eyelids.

Pip wondered what he saw. She reached out — slowly, the way you reached toward a strange dog — and laid her small hand flat on his forehead.

Heat poured into her palm like she'd touched a stove.

And the dream took her.

* * *

She was standing in a corridor made of teeth.

Not little teeth. Not the friendly crooked teeth of a village grandmother or the gap-toothed grin of the smugglers who used to buy Lior's maps. These were teeth the size of doors, yellowed and cracked, set in rows that curved away above her and below her and on every side, so that the corridor was less a place than a *mouth*, and Pip understood, with the calm that dreams gave her, that she was standing inside the head of something enormous.

The walls breathed.

Far ahead — or far below; distance worked strangely here — there was a light. It flickered gold and warm and wrong, and Pip walked toward it because in dreams your feet decided things without asking. Her bare feet made no sound on the tooth-floor. The breathing got louder. And as she walked, she began to hear the other sound underneath the breathing, the one she'd heard before, the one that had woken her screaming in the caverns while Lior held her and sang the smuggler's road-song until dawn:

A man screaming inside his own body.

The light grew. It resolved into a crown — a crown of iron and old gold, floating in the dark at the center of all those teeth, turning slowly. And wrapped around the crown, threaded through it like fingers through a cage, was a king. Pip had seen the king's face on the criers' proclamations, on the wanted-bills for gift-bearers, stamped in stern black ink. But the face in the dream was not the proclamation face. The proclamation face was a mask nailed over a scream. This was the scream itself: a man's face stretched thin, eyes rolled back and *aware*, mouth open around a howl that had lasted so long it had worn grooves in him, the way water wears stone.

He was being worn. That was the thing Pip understood that she couldn't have said aloud. The crown was a garment, and the man was inside it, and the thing outside it was —

The teeth closed a little. Just a fraction. Enough.

"*Vhalgorren.*"

The name came from everywhere and nowhere, from the breathing walls and the turning crown and the screaming king, all of them saying it together, or maybe Pip was saying it, or maybe the name was simply true and truth needed no mouth. It landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. *Vhalgorren.* The syllables were cold and old and hungry. She felt them settle somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where her dreams lived, and she knew — the way she'd known the flood was coming before the river rose, the way she'd known which houses in Hollowmere would burn — that this name was a key, and keys were dangerous, and she would carry it anyway because carrying things was what she did now. She carried Lior's secrets and Dain's nightmares and Vane's guilt, all the heavy things the grown-ups thought they hid from her.

The crown turned. The king's rolling eye found hers — one instant of terrible focus, one instant of a drowning man seeing a rope — and his screaming mouth shaped a word around the howl.

It looked like *run.*

But it might have been her name.

* * *

Pip woke with her hand still on Dain's forehead and her own name caught in her throat like a fishbone.

The candle had burned down to a stub. The rain had stopped; the drips through the roof had slowed to one lonely drop, then another, counting time. Dain's fever still radiated against her palm, but his thrashing had gentled, his breathing deeper, and his face had smoothed into something almost like rest. Whether her touch had done that or the fever had simply turned its corner, she couldn't say. Grown-ups always said dreams weren't real. Grown-ups said a lot of things.

She sat back on her heels and hugged her arms around herself and said the name silently, testing it, feeling its weight. *Vhalgorren.* It fit behind her ribs like it had been measured there.

Downstairs, floorboards creaked. Liar — no, *Lior*, she corrected herself, though privately Pip thought Lior Marchetti lied beautifully, with their whole body, every day, pretending to be merely a mapmaker, merely a smuggler, merely a person who hadn't spent their life reading the land's hidden veins like a priest reading scripture. Lior climbed the ladder with a bowl of something steaming and their hair stuck up on one side where they'd slept sitting against the wall, and their eyes found Pip first, the way they always did, a quick flick of checking — *is she safe, is she whole* — before going to Dain.

"Fever?" Lior asked, setting the bowl down. Barley and wild onion, by the smell. Someone had raided the miller's abandoned stores.

"Still hot. But he stopped fighting." Pip hesitated. Her heart had started doing something strange, beating quick and hard, because she knew what came next and part of her wanted to keep the name hidden, tucked behind her ribs where it was warm and hers. But hiding things from Lior felt like hiding a lit candle in a hay barn. "Lior. I dreamed."

Lior went still. Pip had learned to read that stillness too. It wasn't calm. It was a deer hearing a twig snap.

"Tell me," Lior said quietly.

So Pip told. She told about the corridor of teeth and the breathing walls and the crown turning in the dark. She told about the king inside it, screaming, worn like a coat, aware. She told about the eye finding hers, and the word his mouth had shaped — *run, or maybe my name, I couldn't tell* — and Lior listened without moving except that their hands slowly curled closed, knuckles whitening, as if they were holding something down.

"And it said a name," Pip finished. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "The whole dream said it. The teeth and the crown and the king, all together." She made herself say it out loud, into the candlelight, into the room where Dain burned and the rain dripped and the world felt very thin. "*Vhalgorren.*"

Lior made a sound Pip had never heard from them before. Not a gasp. Not a word. Something between a breath and a wound.

They stood up so fast the stool tipped over, and they crossed to the corner where their pack lay, and they dug through it with shaking hands — actually shaking, Pip saw, actually trembling — until they came out with the book. Grandmother Marchetti's journal, salvaged from the ruined waystation, its leather cover soft as old skin, its pages crowded with cramped handwriting and diagrams that hurt Pip's eyes to look at, lines that seemed to squirm when she wasn't focusing directly on them.

Lior turned pages. Fast, then slower, then fast again, hunting. The candle guttered. Downstairs, Vane's boots paced, paused, paced.

"Here," Lior whispered.

They brought the journal to the candle and tilted it, and Pip leaned in and looked, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the drafty mill.

There, drawn in faded brown ink in the margin of a page dense with binding-rites, was a sigil. A circle of interlocking strokes, sharp as teeth, arranged in rows that curved inward — and beneath it, in her grandmother's spidery hand, a single word.

*Vhalgorren.*

"It's a name-sigil," Lior said. Their voice was strange, flattened, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant. "Names have shapes, in the old craft. This —" their finger hovered over the drawing without touching it, the way you'd hover over a snake — "this is what we've been calling the entity. The thing in the crown. My family wrote its name down. They must have written it down when they bound it, because you can't bind what you won't name, and —" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Pip. You dreamed its true name. The name my grandmother only wrote in secret, in a book she hid in a ruin, a hundred years ago."

"Is that bad?"

Lior laughed — one short, cracked note with no humor in it at all. They crouched down to Pip's level, which they always did, which was one of the thousand small reasons Pip had decided on the first night that these people were hers now, hers to keep, hers to guard with whatever her strange head could do. Lior's eyes were wet at the edges and fierce underneath.

"It means," Lior said, "that the dream wasn't just a dream. It means you're not just *seeing* it, sweetheart. You're *touching* it. And it might be able to touch back." Their hand rose and hovered near her cheek, not quite landing, asking permission the way they always asked. Pip nodded, and the hand settled, warm and steady, steadier than the voice had been. "Which means I need you to tell me everything. Every dream, every scrap, even the ones that feel silly. Especially those. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Pip said. Then, because lying to Lior felt like lying to a mirror: "Even the scary ones."

"Especially the scary ones." Lior's thumb brushed her cheekbone. "Thank you. I mean it. You may have just handed us the only weapon that matters."

Behind them, from the pallet, a dry rasp: "Weapon against *what*?"

They both spun. Dain's eyes were open — fever-bright, unfocused, but open, fixed on the ceiling beams as if reading something written there. His hand had found the edge of Vane's cloak and gripped it.

"The bridge," he rasped. "Did we — is Mallet—"

"Mallet's gone. You held the span. We're safe." Lior crossed to him and pressed the bowl of barley into his hands, guiding it. "Eat. Slowly."

Dain ate three spoonfuls with the grim discipline of a man following orders, then sagged back. His eyes slid to Pip, and something in them softened even through the fever-glass. "Little dreamer," he murmured. "You kept watch?"

"All night," Pip said. "You tried to leave twice. I made you stay."

Something that might have been a smile ghosted across his cracked lips. "Good soldier," he breathed, and was under again, breath evening out, the fever still banked in him but burning lower now, a fire settling into coals.

* * *

Later — much later, when the candle was a puddle and grey light was seeping through the broken roof and Vane had come up twice with fresh water and left again without a word — Pip sat by the window gap with her knees drawn up, watching the mist move through the valley.

Lior sat beside her. They'd been quiet a long time, the journal open on their lap, and Pip could feel them thinking, the way you could feel a storm building behind mountains.

"Lior," she said finally. "In the dream. The king looked at me. And he tried to say something."

Lior's head turned.

"What if it wasn't *run*?" Pip said. "What if it was *help*?"

The mist shifted below them, and far off — so far it was only a smudge, a suggestion, a shadow on the horizon's hem — the towers of the capital stood up out of the morning haze. Somewhere under them, in corridors of bone and old magic, something with a hundred-year hunger was wearing a screaming man like a coat.

Lior looked at the smudge for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was low and certain, and it frightened Pip more than any nightmare, because it sounded exactly like her grandmother's journal read aloud.

"Then we'd better bring the answer to his door."
