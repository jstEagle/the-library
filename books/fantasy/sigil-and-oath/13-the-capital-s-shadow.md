## 13. The Capital's Shadow

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Serah Vane finished the third drawing.

She knelt on the floor of the ruined mill with a piece of charcoal pinched between two fingers, working on a slab of slate she'd pried from a collapsed wall. Around her, the others had gone quiet in that particular way people went quiet when they were watching someone confess. Because that was what this was, she supposed. A confession in lines and angles, every stroke of charcoal an admission of everything she'd spent eleven years serving.

"The outer ring first," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The Wardens' Circuit. It runs the full perimeter of the palace grounds — not walls, exactly. Walls you can climb or tunnel under or bribe your way through. This is older than walls."

She drew it: a broad unbroken circle, and then, branching inward from it at regular intervals, thin strokes like the roots of a tree seen through glass.

"Sigil-work laid into the stone itself when the palace was raised. The masons who built it are dust, but the work holds. Anyone carrying an active gift who crosses the Circuit without a token feels it — like walking into cold water. The warders feel *you*. They can't tell what you are from outside, only that something warm just brushed against their net." She looked up. Lior was watching her with that still, unreadable attention that made her feel like a specimen pinned to a board. "If one of us crosses, we have perhaps forty heartbeats before a patrol reaches the breach point."

"Forty heartbeats," Dain said from the doorway. He stood with his arms crossed, his wounded shoulder bound beneath his cloak, favoring his left side in a way he probably thought no one noticed. Vane had noticed. She'd been noticing things about Dain Corvath for years, back when noticing them had meant filing reports. "And how many warders between the Circuit and the throne room?"

"That depends on which door you're asking about."

"I'm asking about all of them."

Vane turned back to the slate and began filling in the interior. The Great Hall. The Chapel of the Veil, where the 'warding rites' were performed — where she had stood in the smoke and watched children be carried out lighter than they'd gone in. Her hand did not shake. She'd trained it not to shake through worse than this. But there was a tightness in her chest, a pressure like a hand closing slowly around her sternum, and she recognized it as the feeling of a soldier describing the fortress she had sworn to defend to the people sworn to tear its heart out.

"The main approach is the Processional Way. Grand stairs, open court, forty guards on a quiet day. Forget it." Charcoal strokes, crossed out. "The servants' corridors off the east wing are patrolled in pairs, but the pairs change at the ninth bell and again at midnight, and for the length of one bell-toll the corridor between the kitchens and the undercroft is empty. I know because I checked it myself, twice a year, as part of the security review." A dry sound escaped her that might once have been a laugh. "Eleven years of keeping the cage secure. Now I'm teaching you where the bars are thinnest."

"You could stop," Pip said.

Vane glanced over. The child sat cross-legged near the fire with Lior's journal — the grandmother's journal, the Marchetti inheritance — propped open on her knees, though Vane suspected she wasn't reading so much as resting her eyes on it. Pip had a way of looking at people that made them feel seen all the way down, which was a strange thing to say about a nine-year-old, except Pip wasn't quite like other nine-year-olds. Her dreams came true. And sometimes, Vane thought, her waking eyes saw more than they should.

"Stop what?" Vane asked.

"Saying it like that. Like you're still theirs." Pip tilted her head. "You're not. You burned the orders."

*You burned the orders.* Four words that had ended Serah Vane's life as thoroughly as any pyre. She had fed Mallet's harvest writs to the brazier in the safehouse at Grayford while the prisoners she'd been ordered to transport wept and prayed and watched her do it, waiting to learn whether they would live or die. She remembered the smell of the ink burning. She remembered thinking: *this is the first honest thing I have done since I took the oath.*

"I'm not theirs," Vane said. "But I was theirs long enough to know every stone of that place, and knowing is the only thing I have to give."

"It's enough," Lior said quietly.

It was the first thing Lior had said in an hour, and it landed in the room like a coin dropped in a well. Vane met those dark eyes across the firelight and found — not forgiveness. She didn't expect that, not yet, maybe not ever. Lior had lost Hollowmere to hunters wearing the same uniform Vane had worn. But there was something else there, something practical and unsentimental and strangely kind: the acknowledgment that a person was more than the worst uniform they'd ever put on.

"Then we use it," Lior said, and rose, crossing to the slate. "Walk me through it again. Slowly. From the moment we reach the city walls."

* * *

They argued until well past midnight.

The problem, laid bare on the slate, was simple and insoluble: the palace was a sigil-fortress designed by people who had understood gift-bearers better than anyone alive, because the people who had designed it were Marchettis. Vane traced the layers with her fingertip — the Circuit, the inner wards threaded through the foundations, the deep lattice beneath the throne room itself, the one that hummed day and night with harvested power, feeding the thing that wore the king.

"Every route in is watched, warded, or both," Dain said. He'd lowered himself onto a bench, gray-faced, and Vane had pretended not to see Lior press a cup of willow-bark tea into his hands. "I've fought in sieges. There's always a weak point, because fortresses are built by men and men make mistakes. But this—" he gestured at the slate, at the perfect nested rings, "—this wasn't built by men making mistakes. This was built by people who knew exactly what they were locking out. Or locking in."

"The sewers?" Pip offered.

"Flooded and grated," Vane said. "And the grates are warded. I told you — I helped design half these measures. Every clever idea you have, I promise you, I've already had it and closed it."

"Then we don't go in clever," Dain said. "We go in loud. I walk up the Processional Way in my old armor, announce myself, and while every guard in the palace runs to arrest the king's disgraced shield-knight, the rest of you—"

"—are caught inside a warded circuit with no exit and no plan," Vane finished flatly. "Dain. You nearly died at Grayford holding a bridge against six men. Mallet commands the palace garrison now. There are four hundred."

"Four hundred men who watched me carry the king's banner for fifteen years," Dain said, and there was something in his voice Vane hadn't heard before, something old and tired and very dangerous. "Half of them will hesitate. Hesitation kills."

"And the other half?"

"The other half kill me. That's the arithmetic." He said it easily, the way he said everything about himself, and Vane saw Lior's jaw tighten and knew the cartographer was doing sums of a different kind — the kind where the people you loved were the variables.

"No," Lior said. "Nobody walks up the front stairs. Nobody dies buying us forty heartbeats. We need a road they don't know exists."

"There isn't one," Vane said. "Believe me—"

"Lior," Pip said suddenly.

Everyone turned. The child had gone rigid, the journal sliding from her knees, her eyes fixed on nothing — or on something far behind the walls of the world. Vane had seen dreamers before; the crown had taught her to see them, to catalog them, to harvest them. But watching Pip fall into sight while awake was different. Watching it was like watching a window frost over from the inside.

"Pip." Lior was beside her instantly, kneeling, one hand hovering near the child's shoulder without touching. "Pip, stay with us. What do you see?"

"Roads," Pip whispered. "Under the roads. Under the city. Old ones. They're all... tangled, like hair after sleep. But there's one that isn't dead. One that's only sleeping." Her eyes rolled toward Lior, and what was in them made the hairs rise on Vane's neck. "It's waiting for *you*. It knows your name. It's been saying it for a hundred years and nobody came."

* * *

Lior didn't sleep that night. Neither did Vane.

She found the cartographer at the broken eastern wall of the mill an hour before dawn, standing in the gray light with the grandmother's journal open, one hand pressed flat against the old stone as though listening for a pulse. Vane leaned against the doorframe and waited. She was good at waiting. It had been most of her job.

"A dead road," Lior said at last, without turning. "Beneath the capital. My grandmother writes about it — obliquely, the way she wrote about everything that frightened her. The Founders' Way. Built before the palace, before the binding, when the first Marchettis came to the valley. It ran from the river gate to the hilltop where they raised the keep. When the palace went up on top of it, they sealed it. Warded it shut. Not because it was dangerous." Lior's fingers moved along the journal's cramped script. "Because it was *theirs*. A Marchetti road, keyed to Marchetti blood. The king's masons couldn't unmake it, so they buried it and pretended it had never existed."

"And your gift can open it."

"My gift can hear it." Lior finally turned, and in the pre-dawn gray their face was unreadable, but their voice wasn't. Their voice was afraid, and underneath the fear, something bright and terrible that Vane recognized from her own mirror, from the morning she'd decided to burn her orders. "Every road has a voice if you're gifted enough to listen. This one has been silent for a hundred years, sealed behind wards laid by my own family. But Pip says it's sleeping, not dead. And sleeping things wake."

"You'd be opening a door directly beneath the throne room," Vane said. "Into the seat of the thing that eats children. You understand that whatever waits down there, it will feel you coming. The wards, the lattice, the Crown-Eater itself — the moment a Marchetti bloodline touches a Marchetti road, every alarm in that palace that has slept for a century will wake screaming."

"Yes."

"You say that very calmly."

"I've been calm since Hollowmere burned," Lior said. "Calm is just fear that's found a direction."

Vane pushed off the doorframe and came to stand beside them at the wall. Below, the valley lay in blue shadow, and somewhere beyond the eastern hills, days away yet, the capital crouched with its lit windows and its hollow king. She thought about the oath she'd taken at seventeen, hand on the crown's seal, promising to hold the realm's wards against all threats foreign and domestic. She had believed, then, that the wards protected people. She had spent eleven years learning what they actually protected.

"If we do this," she said, "we do it precisely. I'll give you the rotation schedules, the guard posts, the timing of the night bells. I'll draw you the undercroft, the chapel, the service stairs to the gallery above the throne room. Everything I carry in here—" she tapped her temple, "—goes onto your maps. But hear me, Marchetti. If your dead road opens into a trap, if this is some old snare your grandmother's family left for thieves and traitors, then we don't just die. Pip dies. Dain dies. Everyone in this ragged little family of ours dies in a hole under the enemy's house, and no one will even find our bones to bury."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway." Lior closed the journal and held it against their chest, and for a moment the dawn light caught the faint scar of the sigil-mark on the back of their hand — the mark that had started everything, in a tavern, on an ordinary night, with ink that glowed. "Serah. You asked me once why I charted smuggler's roads for a living instead of selling my gift to the guilds. Do you remember?"

"You said the guilds wanted to own the mapmaker along with the map."

"That's right." Lior smiled, thin and crooked. "Well. The crown owns a king. It owns a god. It owns the gifts of everyone it hasn't burned yet. It thinks owning things is the same as ruling them." The smile faded. "But roads aren't owned. Roads are *kept*. Someone keeps them open, keeps them true, keeps them safe for whoever walks them. That's the whole of my family's crime, Serah — we kept a road, and the crown couldn't bear it. So we're going to walk our own road, straight under their feet, and we're going to keep it open all the way to the throne room."

Behind them, a small voice spoke from the shadows of the mill.

"I dreamed the door last night," Pip said. She padded forward, dragging a blanket, her hair a wild halo in the first real light. "The one under the city. It opened for somebody with ink on their hands." She looked up at Lior, solemn as a priestess. "And Lior — when it opened, I could see the throne room floor above it. Through the cracks. And there was light coming down through the cracks, like the floor was bleeding daylight." She shivered. "It wasn't daylight."

Vane felt the cold move through her like a tide. Above them the sky was turning gold and rose, ordinary and beautiful, and beneath the capital, beneath a hundred years of silence, a sealed road waited with its mouth open, and something at the top of it was leaking light that was not light.

"How long to reach the city?" Lior asked quietly.

"At pace, five days," Vane said. "Three if we push and Dain pretends he isn't dying."

"Then we push." Lior was already moving back toward the fire, already unrolling fresh paper, and Vane watched the cartographer begin to draw — not a smuggler's map this time, not a client's chart for coin, but something older and stranger: the shape of a buried road, rendered line by line from a dead woman's journal and a living child's dreams. "Five days to learn every stone you remember, Serah. Every guard, every bell, every bar in the cage. Because when we come up through that floor—"

Lior paused, charcoal poised, and looked at the three of them — the knight, the hunter, the dreamer — gathered in the ruins of a mill at the edge of the world.

"—we come up together, and we don't stop until the crown comes off."

The fire crackled. The dawn strengthened. And far to the east, beneath the palace of the Hollow King, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and turned its attention, slow as a glacier, toward the west — toward a road it had almost forgotten, and the last of the blood that had built it.

Pip stopped halfway to the fire and looked back over her shoulder, east, at nothing.

"He knows we're coming now," she said softly. "He's setting a place for us at the table."
