## 14. Into the Dead Road

The dead road opened where Vane said it would: beneath a collapsed granary in the tanners' quarter, behind a wall of brick so old it had gone soft as bread. Lior pressed their palm to the mortar and felt it the way they felt everything now — a thin silver thread of the land's attention, running down into dark like a root seeking water.

"It's here," they said. "It's been here the whole time. Under their feet."

"Under everyone's feet," Wren had told them, back in the Underhold, in that voice like dry paper turning. "The Marchettis built the capital's foundations over the road because a cage is strongest when no one remembers there was ever a door."

Lior remembered. The journal lay against their ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a second heartbeat.

Dain went first, torch high, armor left behind in favor of leather and silence. He looked wrong without the steel — smaller, somehow, though he filled the passage shoulder to shoulder all the same. Then Vane, blades loose in their sheaths, her hunter's tread gone soft as a cat's. Pip came third, holding Lior's hand, and Lior came last, because someone had to be the door closing behind them.

The stair went down farther than any stair had a right to go.

* * *

They counted two hundred steps and stopped counting. The air changed first — warmer, then cold again, then warm in a way that had nothing to do with fire. It breathed. That was the word Lior's mind kept reaching for and kept rejecting, but there was no other for it. The air *breathed*, slow and vast, drawing in around them and sighing out, and each exhale carried a smell like copper coins left too long in a fist.

The walls changed too. Below the last dressed stone, the corridor became something older — not built so much as grown, or perhaps grown and then built over, the way scar tissue grows over a wound. Ribs of pale material arched overhead, close enough that Dain's torch flame licked them, and they were not stone. Lior touched one and snatched their hand back.

"What is it?" Pip whispered.

"Bone," Lior said. "Fused bone. Thousands of years of it." They made themselves touch it again, because a cartographer who flinched from the map was useless to everyone. It was smooth as river-polished wood and faintly warm, and beneath the warmth, deep down, it *pulsed*. Once every few breaths. Once with the breathing.

"The whole road," Vane said quietly. She had stopped walking. Her face in the torchlight was the color of tallow. "I trained in the palace crypts. I memorized ward-routes through three levels of them. And I never—" She swallowed. "I never knew this was under us. How do you not know this is under you?"

"Because it doesn't want you to know," Lior said. "That's what my grandmother wrote. The binding doesn't just hold it. It hides it. From the king. From the court. From everyone." They looked up at the ribbed dark stretching away ahead of them. "From itself, maybe. I don't think it knows how big it is anymore. I don't think it remembers anything except being hungry."

Pip's grip tightened on their hand. Small fingers, chapped from the mountain cold, gripping hard enough to hurt.

"I'm fine," she said, before anyone asked. She had taken to saying it constantly since Grayford, since Dain had come off the bridge looking like a dropped marionette and Vane had come off it looking like a woman who had cut her own name out of her chest. *I'm fine, I'm fine.* She said it the way other people prayed.

"We know," Lior said, and squeezed back.

They walked on. The bone ribs came closer together, then farther apart, opening into chambers where the road widened into halls — halls with no purpose Lior could name, round as throats, their floors worn glass-smooth by nothing human. In one, the torchlight found marks on the wall: sigils, hundreds of them, carved deep and packed with something black that glittered.

Lior stopped. Their stomach dropped through the floor of them.

They knew these. Not these exact ones — but the *hand*. The flowing vertical strokes, the crossbars that hooked like a shepherd's crook, the little terminal flourishes that no sane scribe would bother with on work this old. They had seen that hand their whole childhood, inked in the margins of every chart their grandmother ever left behind.

"These are Marchetti," they said. Their voice came out strange, flattened. "These are *ours*."

Vane looked at them with something terrible in her face — pity, maybe, or its cousin. "Then your family walked this road," she said. "Carrying those."

"Carrying them *down*," Lior corrected. "Look at the direction of the strokes. These aren't wards to keep something out." They traced the sequence without touching, reading the way they read rivers and ridgelines, the gift rising in them unbidden, silver threads lighting up along the carvings until the whole chamber seemed to breathe light. "They're a leash. A long, long leash, running ahead of us, all the way down. Every generation of my family walked this road and added links."

"And fed it," Dain said. His voice was very low. He had not been the same since the bridge — slower to speak, quicker to look at his own hands — but when he spoke about the thing below, he spoke like a man describing a battle he'd already lost once. "Every bearer harvested. Every rite. All of it walking down this road, link by link, for centuries."

"Yes," Lior said. Because there was no softer word.

Pip tugged their sleeve. "There's more writing. Underneath."

Lior knelt. Beneath the Marchetti sigils, half-erased, gouged over, were older marks — angular, furious, nothing like the family hand. Wren's voice surfaced in memory: *the First Binders did not write. They screamed in shapes.* But some of the shapes were legible still, and Lior read them the way you read a warning scratched on a well cover, and what they said, in the oldest grammar of the land, was:

*IT IS NOT ASLEEP. IT IS WAITING TO BE FORGOTTEN COMPLETELY.*

* * *

An hour deeper — or a day; time went strange down there, stretched like taffy — the pressure began.

Lior felt it first as a headache, a band of iron tightening across the brow. Then as sound: a note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the wet weight of the eyes. Then as thought. That was the worst of it. Thoughts arrived that were not theirs. *Hungry,* said a thought that used Lior's own inner voice. *So long. So long since anything came down the road willingly.*

"Stop," Vane said sharply. "Everyone stop. Put your backs together."

They formed the knot Dain had drilled into them on the smuggler's road — four backs touching, torch held high, faces outward. The corridor here was narrow, the bone ribs pressing close, and the pulse in the walls had quickened. Lior could see it now without touching: a slow red flush traveling along the fused vertebrae of the road, toward them, like blood moving through a vein toward a wound.

"It knows we're here," Dain said.

"It's known since the granary," Lior said. "Maybe since we entered the city. This is just — it turning its attention. Like an eye opening." They pressed the heels of their hands against their temples. The thoughts kept coming, patient, enormous, wearing Lior's voice like a stolen coat. *You are Marchetti. You are mine. Your blood signed my name. Come down. Come down and be the last link, and I will show you every road in the world, every path under every sea—*

"Lior." Pip's voice, small and frightened. "Your nose."

Blood on their lip. Lior wiped it, looked at the red on their fingertips, and understood with cold clarity exactly how much danger they were in — not the flesh-danger, which was real, but the other kind. The kind that didn't kill you. The kind that *kept* you.

"Pip," they said carefully. "Look at me. Not down the corridor. At me."

But Pip wasn't looking at them. Pip was looking down the corridor, into the dark past the torchlight, and her face had gone smooth and dreamy in a way Lior knew from bad nights on the road, from nights when she woke screaming about crowns of teeth. She took a step away from the knot. Then another.

"Pip—"

"It's calling me," she said, in a voice that was hers and yet arranged by something else, each word placed like stones across a stream. "It says it has my mother. It says it keeps everyone. It says nobody is ever really dead down here, they're just *kept*—"

She took another step. Her foot came down on the seam between two bone plates, and the plates flexed — gently, horribly, like a tongue — and the pull came on strong then, Lior felt it even through their own iron band of pain, a current in the air dragging toward the deep places, and Pip leaned into it the way a sleeper leans out of a window.

Dain moved, but he was too far, and Vane was faster but she'd learned the hard way at Hollowmere what grabbing a dreaming child could cost, and it was Lior — Lior, whose blood had built this road, whose blood it wanted — who dropped to one knee in the breathing dark and did the only thing a Marchetti could do.

They drew.

Chalk would have been better. Ink would have been best. They had neither, so they used what they had: the knife from their boot, laid flat against the floor's glass-smooth bone, and their own blood, welling from a shallow line they dragged across their left palm without letting themselves think about it. Pain helped. Pain was a rope back to yourself.

They drew a circle around Pip's next footprint — fast, ragged, but true, every stroke in the family hand, the shepherd's-crook hooks, the flourishes, the grammar their grandmother had beaten into them over winter maps by candlelight. A boundary-sigil. The oldest one. The one mothers drew on cradles in the old stories. *Within this line, the child is the land's, and the land is the child's, and nothing that wears a crown may claim either.*

Their blood sank into the bone and the sigil lit — not the hungry red of the road's pulse but a clean, cool silver, the color of the living lines Lior had spent a lifetime reading, and the current in the air broke against it like a wave against a breakwater.

Pip stopped mid-stride. Blinked. Looked down at the glowing ring around her feet, then up at Lior, kneeling in their own blood, and her face crumpled all at once from dreamy to devastated.

"I heard her," she whispered. "I heard my mama. It *used* her voice."

"I know." Lior got up, crossed the line — the sigil let them pass, recognized them, kin-blood — and gathered her in. She was shaking. So, it turned out, were they. "It lies. That's the whole of what it is, Pip. It's a mouth that only knows how to lie. Everything else got eaten a long time ago."

"It knew things about her. Real things."

"Because it ate people who knew her. That's all its knowledge ever is. Leftovers." Lior held her at arm's length. "Listen to me. When it speaks to you — and it will, again, it likes you, dreamers are rare and it's been starving for rare things — you say my name. Out loud. However small. I'll hear it. That's not magic. That's just family."

Pip scrubbed her face with both sleeves and nodded, jaw set in a way that was far too old for her, far too familiar. Lior had seen that exact jaw on their own reflection, the morning after Hollowmere burned.

Behind them, Vane crouched at the edge of the sigil, studying it with a hunter's eye. "How long will it hold?"

"As long as my blood stays in it. Days, maybe. But it's not a wall, it's a — a claim. A counter-claim." Lior flexed their cut hand, watching the blood bead. "It says this child belongs to the land, not the crown. And down here, apparently, the land still honors the old signatures."

"Your family's signatures," Dain said.

"Our family's signatures." Lior looked down the corridor, into the dark where the red pulse traveled away toward the deep places, carrying news of them home to the thing that waited. "Which means it knows we're coming. Which means it's had centuries to decide what to do about the last Marchetti walking its road."

"Then we don't give it centuries," Vane said. She rose, drew one blade an inch to check it, sheathed it. "We give it tonight."

* * *

They slept in shifts inside a ring of Lior's blood — four circles overlapping, a chain of small silver claims in the vast dark, and Lior lay awake through most of their own watch, listening to the road breathe.

Near dawn-that-wasn't-dawn, Pip stirred against their side and murmured in her sleep. Not screaming. Murmuring, clear and calm, the way she spoke true dreams, and Lior bent close to catch it.

"He's almost empty," she said. "The king. There's hardly any him left. Just enough to be sorry." Her brow furrowed. "And it's *scared*. It doesn't know I can see it being scared. It thinks fear is a thing it ate last."

Lior lay back and stared at the ribbed dark overhead, at the red pulse slowing, slowing, like a great heart settling after a fright.

Scared. The Crown-Eater, the devourer, the thing that had worn kings like garments for three hundred years — scared of four ragged fugitives and a child's dreams.

Or scared of something else entirely. Something it could see coming down its own road, link by broken link, in the handwriting of the family that had chained it.

Lior closed their bleeding hand and, for the first time since the granary wall, smiled.

"Hold on," they whispered to the dark, to the hollow king, to whatever small sorry remnant still sat behind the hollow eyes. "We're coming. And we're bringing the map."

Far below, the road exhaled — long, slow, and afraid.
