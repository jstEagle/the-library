## 15. The Court of the Hollow King

The dead road ended the way a held breath ends — all at once, and with no warning that it was about to.

Dain felt the ceiling of stone above them thin, felt the pressure change in his ears the way it did before a storm broke over the ramparts of some castle he'd once been sworn to defend. Lior's hand came up, palm flat, fingers splayed against the dark as though pressing against glass. The sigils inked along their forearm glowed a faint, sickly amber, and beneath them Dain could see the veins standing out like roots under bark.

"Here," Lior whispered. "It opens here. But listen to me — all of you." Their voice was low and rough, scraped raw by three days beneath the capital. "What we come up into is not a cellar. It is the heart of everything. Whatever you see, whatever you hear — do not run. Running is how it finds your edges."

Pip pressed close against Dain's leg. He put his hand on her head without thinking about it, the way he had once rested his gauntlet on the shoulders of squires before battles, back when his touch had meant *you are safe* instead of *I will do what I can.* The child was trembling, but her eyes were dry. She had dreamed this room already. He could see it in her face — the terrible recognition of someone walking into a memory they never asked for.

"And if I see him?" Vane said quietly. She stood at the rear, sword drawn, her former partner's blood still ghosting the scabbard's mouth where she had wiped it a hundred times and never quite clean. "If I see Aldric on that throne?"

"Then you remember he was your king," Lior said, "and that what wears him is not."

Dain said nothing. He had made his oath to Aldric Vaile twenty-two years ago, kneeling on cold marble while an old priest wrapped white silk around his forearm. *Shield of the realm,* the words had gone. *Body and soul, until death unbinds me.* He had kept that oath through two wars and one massacre he still could not say aloud without tasting ash. He had broken it only once — the night he refused to light the torch at Hollowmere — and every day since, he had wondered whether breaking it had saved his soul or merely traded it for a different kind of servitude.

Now he was going to stand in front of the man he had sworn to protect and learn which it had been.

Lior drew a slow breath and pushed upward. The stone did not open so much as *thin* — the way fog thins, the way a lie thins when it has been told too long. Light spilled down through it, gold and censer-sweet, and then they were rising through it, one after another, into warmth and incense and the sound of singing.

* * *

The throne room of Vaile Keep was exactly as Dain remembered it, and that was the first horror.

He had expected ruin. He had expected, in some secret chamber of himself, a place rotted through, blackened, wrong — some physical corruption to match the thing he now knew sat upon the throne. Instead there was the same vaulted ceiling painted with the ascension of Saint Corvel, the same pillars of white mountain-marble veined with silver, the same great windows throwing colored light across a floor polished by four hundred years of ceremony. The banners of the nine provinces hung from the rafters. The choir loft was full. Everything was beautiful.

Everything was beautiful, and nothing in it was alive.

He understood that within three heartbeats. The courtiers lining the aisle in their silks — they moved, they swayed, they turned their heads when the procession of acolytes passed, but their eyes were fixed on the middle distance, and their chests rose and fell with the mechanical regularity of bellows. The singers in the loft produced notes of perfect purity, and not one of their faces carried anything at all. Even the incense smoke rose too straight, too slow, as if the air itself had been taught its choreography and dared not improvise.

*Warding ceremony.* He had heard the phrase a thousand times in the king's service, spoken with reverence: the monthly rite by which the crown's wards were renewed, the realm protected, the Gift kept safely bound away from dangerous hands. He had stood honor guard at six of them. He had thought them tedious.

He looked at the dais now and understood what he had been guarding.

Aldric Vaile sat upon the Ironrose Throne, and Dain's knees nearly buckled, because the last time he had seen his king — five years ago, when Dain had still been Ser Dain Corvath, Shield of the Realm, and not a nameless fugitive in a stolen coat — Aldric had been fifty-four years old and straight-backed, gray coming into his beard like frost coming into a field, his laugh carrying the length of the great hall.

The figure on the throne was ninety years old if he was a day. He was shrunken inside robes of state that fit him the way a shroud fits, pools of cloth gathered around a body that had been poured out of itself. His hands lay on the armrests like two pale spiders, knuckles swollen, skin translucent enough to show the blue wiring of veins. His hair had gone entirely white and hung lank past his shoulders. And his eyes—

His eyes were open. That was the worst of it. They were open, and they were empty, fixed on the middle distance just like the courtiers' eyes, and yet behind that emptiness something flickered, faint and desperate, the way a candle gutters behind horn panes in a high wind.

The acolytes finished their chant. A high priestess in bone-white vestments ascended the dais bearing a basin, and Dain saw what floated in it and had to lock his jaw against the sound that tried to climb out of his throat.

Sigils. Dozens of them, cut from living skin — small squares of it, each marked in ink that still glistened, each one taken from a gift-bearer. Harvested. The word Vane had used in the Underhold, her voice shaking apart around it. *They call it harvesting.*

The priestess began laying them along the arms of the throne, and with each one she placed, the king's body jerked — small, rhythmic spasms, like a man being shocked awake again and again against his will.

And then the king's mouth opened, and Vhalgorren spoke.

* * *

Dain had heard the entity's voice once before, though he hadn't known it. Three years ago, at a winter court, Aldric had paused mid-sentence during an address and continued in a register no human throat should own — deeper than depth, resonant in the bones of everyone listening, so that the whole hall had shivered as one animal. Courtiers had called it the king's *gravitas*. There had been laughter afterward, wine, music.

There was no laughter now. The voice rolled down from the dais and filled the throne room the way water fills a drowned bell, and every syllable landed in Dain's chest like a fist.

*"Bring the tithe forward."*

The acolytes parted. Two wardens dragged a figure up the aisle — a girl, sixteen perhaps, wrists bound in white silk, her feet barely touching the floor. Her gift-marked arms had been shaved and oiled for the rite; Dain could see the fresh ink of registration numbers on her inner wrist. Registered. Compliant. She had done everything the proclamation demanded, given her name to the criers, signed the rolls, trusted the crown — and the crown had sent her here, to be laid out on the steps of the throne like an offering on an altar.

Beside him, he heard Vane's breath go ragged. He did not look at her. He knew what she was seeing: the prisoners she had burned her orders rather than surrender, lined up in that same posture, waiting for the same knife. He knew because he was seeing Hollowmere — the registered families of Hollowmere, who had believed the same promise.

The girl was weeping silently. That was somehow worse than screaming. She had learned already that screaming changed nothing here.

*"Kneel the vessel,"* Vhalgorren said through the king's mouth, and the priestess bent the girl down at the foot of the dais, and the old horror in Dain's chest — the one he had carried since Hollowmere, the one shaped like a torch and a locked granary door — rose up in him with such force that he took a step forward before he knew he was moving.

Lior's hand closed on his arm. Not restraining. *Anchoring.* Their eyes met, and Lior shook their head once, minutely, and their expression was not caution. It was agony. It was *not yet, not yet, or we lose everything including her.*

Dain stood frozen in the shadow of a pillar, thirty feet from his king, wearing a dead man's coat, holding a sword he had sworn never to draw against the crown, and did nothing.

That was when the king looked at him.

* * *

It should not have been possible. The courtiers' eyes slid past the pillar shadows where the four of them crouched as though the shadows were walls; Vhalgorren's attention was fixed on the tithe-girl, on the rite, on the slow machinery of its feeding. But between one spasm and the next, between one syllable of the entity's liturgy and the next, Aldric Vaile's eyes moved.

They found Dain across thirty feet of marble and colored light, and they were no longer empty.

Dain would carry those eyes to his grave. He had seen men dying, men mad, men betrayed. He had never seen a man *imploring* — not with his face, which barely moved anymore, but with his eyes alone, two living things drowning in a dead sea, fixing on his shield-knight of twenty-two years ago with a focus so absolute it was almost a touch.

The king's lips did not move. His throat made no sound. But his eyes said it, clear as any words ever spoken in that hall:

*Ser Dain. My shield. End it.*

And then, because even now, even hollowed to a husk, the man had been a king and a father and a friend, the eyes added the rest:

*I beg you. Before it takes another.*

Dain's vision blurred. He realized distantly that his whole body was shaking, that his hand on his sword hilt had gone white-knuckled, that every oath he had ever sworn was tearing loose inside him like banners in a gale. *End it.* One step. Two. He had killed men at greater distance with less cause. He could cross the floor before the wardens finished turning. He could give Aldric Vaile the only mercy left in this gilded abattoir, and damn the mission, and damn the realm, and damn the careful arithmetic Lior had spent weeks building.

The king's eyes held his, begging, begging —

— and then Vhalgorren noticed.

Dain felt it happen. The temperature of the room dropped as though a door had opened onto winter. The incense smoke stopped rising. Every courtier's head turned toward the pillar at once, in perfect unison, a hundred faces moving as one mechanism, and the king's eyes went dark — snuffed like lamps — and the mouth that had been a suffering man's mouth opened wide, far wider than a mouth should open, and the voice that came out was no longer borrowing Aldric Vaile's timbre at all.

*"A KNIGHT."*

The word struck the pillars and came back doubled, tripled. The choir faltered and resumed, obedient, indifferent.

*"I REMEMBER YOU, SHIELD. YOU STOOD AT MY RIGHT HAND AND NEVER ONCE LOOKED UPON MY FACE. YOU KEPT YOUR EYES ON THE ROADS AND THE CROWDS, AS SHIELDS DO. SO YOU NEVER SAW WHAT SAT BENEATH THE CROWN."* The ruined body on the throne shifted, robes sliding off shoulders that were beginning, horribly, to move of their own accord, joints articulating in sequences no anatomy permitted. *"AND NOW YOU COME INTO MY COURT WEARING EXILE LIKE A COAT, WITH A CARTOGRAPHER'S BLOOD IN YOUR VEINS AND A DREAMER AT YOUR KNEE."*

Pip made a small sound. Dain's hand found her shoulder and pulled her behind him, and he heard Vane's blade whisper free of its scabbard, and he heard Lior breathing fast and controlled beside him — the breathing of someone doing rapid, desperate arithmetic.

*"YOU HAVE WALKED MY DEAD ROADS. YOU HAVE READ MY FIRST BINDING. AND YET YOU STAND IN MY HOUSE AND CALL IT A CRIME."* The entity's amusement, when it came, vibrated the stained glass. *"THE MARCHETTIS BUILT MY CAGE, LITTLE SCRIBBLER. YOUR GRANDMOTHER MIXED THE MORTAR. EVERY GIFT I HAVE TASTED FOR TWO HUNDRED YEARS WAS POURED INTO MY CUP BY HANDS THAT SIGNED THEMSELVES *SERVANTS OF THE REALM.* ASK YOUR COMPANION THERE WHAT THE OATH OF A SHIELD IS WORTH. ASK HER WHAT THE ORDERS OF A HUNTER ARE WORTH. EVERYTHING IN THIS ROOM WAS BUILT ON KNEELING."*

The tithe-girl was still kneeling at the foot of the dais. The priestess stood over her with a curved blade of black glass, waiting — Dain understood with a lurch — waiting for the entity's permission, for the ceremony to resume, for the feeding to continue. The girl had lifted her head. She was looking at the four shadows by the pillar. Looking at them the way the drowning look at a shore.

And Aldric Vaile surfaced one more time.

It lasted three seconds. Later, Dain would count them, torture himself with them, wonder forever whether he could have used them better. Three seconds in which the hollow eyes flooded with the man again, and the man's cracked lips moved, shaping silent words with the last strength in a body devoured from the inside:

*Not me. Save her. Please —*

Then the tide of the entity rolled back in, and the king's face went slack, and Vhalgorren turned its stolen gaze upon the priestess and said, in a voice like a tomb settling:

*"Proceed."*

* * *

The blade came up.

Everything Dain had ever been — shield-knight, oath-breaker, fugitive, the man who had chosen conscience over duty and hated himself for both — narrowed to a single point, and that point was a girl of sixteen kneeling on white marble with black glass rising above her.

He did not hear Lior shout. He did not hear Vane swear. He was moving before thought caught up, bursting from the pillar's shadow into the open light of the court, sword clearing its scabbard, bellowing the war-cry of a house whose banners had been struck from the rafters five years ago:

"CORVATH! CORVATH AND THE FREE ROAD!"

Behind him he heard Vane's boots hit marble, heard Lior's voice crack open into words that were not words — sigils, spoken aloud, the air itself beginning to burn amber — and ahead of him the priestess turned, unhurried, and a hundred dead courtiers turned with her, and the thing on the throne began, in its voice like a drowned bell, to laugh.

The blade started to fall.

Dain was still ten feet away.
