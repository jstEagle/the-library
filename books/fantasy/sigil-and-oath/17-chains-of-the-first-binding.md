## 17. Chains of the First Binding

They took Lior's hands first.

Two guards in black-lacquered half-plate, faces hidden behind visors chased with the crown's sigil — a circle of interlocking teeth, though most folk never looked closely enough to see what the teeth were made of. The guards bound Lior's wrists with cord woven through with cold iron thread, and the moment it touched skin, the world went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. Like a room where someone had just stopped speaking mid-word.

Lior had felt the land's lines every day of their life: the slow silver pulse of water under stone, the green hum of roots in springtime, the deep patient patience of mountains that thought in centuries. Even beneath the capital, even in the dead road's bone-fused corridors, the lines had been there — twisted, starved, bent toward the palace like rivers dammed by a cruel hand. But they had been *there*.

Now, through the iron, nothing. Lior was a cartographer standing in an unmapped country with no eyes.

*Don't show them that it frightens you,* they told themselves, and lifted their chin as the guards marched them up out of the Underhold's dark, through service passages that smelled of tallow and old incense, into light.

The throne room of Vhalgorren's king was not what the ballads promised. No vaulting gold. No banners. The windows were shuttered with iron, and hundreds of candles burned in tiers along the walls, their flames unnaturally still, as if the air itself were afraid to move. The floor was pale marble worn into a shallow groove down its center — a channel, Lior realized with a lurch, a channel running from the great doors all the way to the dais, and the groove was stained brown in places, old stains, many years of them.

The throne sat empty.

Before it, on the lowest step, stood a table of black stone, and on the table lay a book bound in something that was not leather, its pages thick and soft-looking, its cover worked with the Marchetti mark: the four-pointed compass rose, the star that meant *the way through*.

Their family's mark. Their family's book. Chained open by clasps of iron at the four corners.

Mallet stood beside the table. He had cleaned the bridge's blood off himself, but there was a fresh bandage at his throat where Dain's blade had kissed him, and his eyes when they found Lior held no heat at all — which was worse than hatred. Hatred was a kind of respect. This was the look of a man examining a tool.

"Kneel," he said.

Lior knelt. Not because they were told to. Because kneeling put their face close to the marble, and the marble still carried a ghost of the dead road below, and a cartographer learned everything they could about the ground before the ground learned anything about them.

Through the tall doors at the far end came the sound of small feet, dragging, and a voice Lior knew better than their own heartbeat saying, fiercely, "I can walk. I said I can *walk*, you great lump—"

Pip.

They had traded her for this. That was the arithmetic Mallet had offered at Grayford's ruin, and Lior had agreed before he finished speaking, and Vane had grabbed their arm hard enough to bruise and said *no, no, we find another way*, and Dain, gray-faced and bandaged, leaning on his sword like a second spine, had said only, "Then we all go," and so here they all were — because Pip had been in Mallet's hands, and Lior would have traded anything, and everyone in this room knew it.

The guards brought her forward. She was unharmed. Someone had even washed her face and braided her hair, which frightened Lior more than bruises would have. You did not groom a lamb you intended to keep.

Her eyes found Lior across the candlelit distance, and she did not cry. She mouthed one word: *ready?*

As if she were the one coming to rescue them.

"Ah." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls and the candles and the stained channel in the floor, and then, horribly, from the man who walked out from behind the throne's shadow.

He had been handsome once; you could see the architecture of it still, the strong jaw, the broad shoulders. But the flesh hung on him like clothing worn too long, and his eyes were the eyes Lior had seen in Pip's drawings — wide, wet, screaming without sound. His mouth smiled. His eyes begged.

The king. What was left of him.

"You are the last Marchetti," the thing wearing him said, and its voice was layered, a man's voice underneath and something vast speaking over it, the way thunder speaks over the falling tree. "Blood of the binders. Do you know what your grandmother wrote in her little journal? *We did not cage a god. We harnessed a harvest.* She was proud of it. Are you proud, cartographer?"

"I'm a mapmaker," Lior said. "I draw roads. Roads go where people choose."

The hollow king laughed, and the candles guttered though no wind moved. "Choose. Yes. You will choose now, in fact. Kneel at the table."

Guards hauled Lior up and marched them the length of the stained channel to the black stone table. Up close, the chained book breathed. There was no other word for it. The pages swelled and settled, swelled and settled, slow as a sleeping animal's flank.

"The binding weakens," Vhalgorren said, and now it spoke only through the king's mouth, and the king's ruined eyes rolled toward Lior with something that might have been hope, or might have been horror at hoping. "Every century, a bearer must renew it. Every century, a Marchetti has come to this table and redrawn the chains. Your gift is the ink. The child—" the smile widened, showing teeth that were slightly, subtly wrong, too many or too few, "—the child is the page."

"No," Lior said.

"Yes. A renewal requires a vessel. Gift-bearers are vessels; that is all any of you have ever been. The girl dreams true — rare stock, excellent paper. Her gift will soak into the binding and hold it fast another hundred years, and you will have bought your realm another century of peace, and yourself a comfortable life, and her a long sleep without pain. Your grandmother would be proud after all."

Lior looked at Pip. Pip looked back, chin up, fists balled, terrified and refusing to show it, and Lior thought: *she dreamed a crown made of teeth. She dreamed the name. She drew the sigil in the dirt with a stick because it lived in her head and wouldn't leave her alone.* 

And Lior thought: *Grandmother, what did you do? What did we all do?*

"Begin," said Vhalgorren.

The iron clasps on the book opened themselves.

* * *

The pages showed the binding entire.

Lior had expected riddles, or at least difficulty. Instead the old script resolved itself the moment they looked — because it was written in the Marchetti hand, the hand Lior had learned copying survey notes at their grandmother's knee, the same slanted letters, the same shorthand strokes. The original binding was a map. Of course it was. It was a map of a god, drawn in chains: every line a leash, every sigil a stake driven through the vast body of the thing beneath the mountain, gathering its hunger and routing it, channel by channel, into the crown.

And at the center, the master stroke, the keystone line — the one that gathered all the lesser chains and knotted them — was drawn to be renewed by a living hand, fed by a living gift, anchored in a living vessel.

The ritual was simple. That was the obscenity of it. Any apprentice could perform it. That was how it had survived: not hidden, just *easy*.

Lior flexed their bound hands. The iron cord bit. Without their gift, the sigils they drew would be mere ink — pretty shapes, dead letters. The rite required a bearer's blood to wake the lines.

"Your hands," Mallet said, and cut the cord.

Blood returned to Lior's fingers in a flood of pins and needles, and with it — faint, far away, like hearing a river through a hillside — the land's voice came back. Thin. Muffled. But there. The iron thread had dampened it, not severed it; the crown needed its cartographer functional.

A knife lay on the table beside the book, its blade dark with generations of use. Lior picked it up. Behind them, they heard Pip's breath quicken, and Dain's low voice, steady as a wall: "Look at me, little bird. Not at them. At me."

Vane stood near the doors, arms bound, two guards flanking her. She caught Lior's eye and gave the smallest shake of her head — *don't* — and then, almost invisible, a flick of her gaze downward, toward the floor, toward the channel, toward—

Toward the groove in the marble. The channel ran from the doors to the dais, and the dais was built over the wellshaft down which the harvested gifts had been poured for centuries, and the whole room, Lior saw suddenly, *saw* it with the thin thread of gift they had left, the whole room was itself a sigil. The throne room was the final character in the binding. The candles were nodes. The channel was the connecting stroke. Everything in this room had been placed by hands that understood maps.

Which meant everything in this room could be *redrawn*.

Lior dipped the knife and opened their palm, letting blood fall into a dish of ink that a silent attendant slid forward. The blood bloomed in the black ink like smoke in water.

"Renewal," Vhalgorren prompted, savoring it. "Begin with the anchor stroke."

Lior began.

They drew the anchor stroke exactly as the book prescribed — or nearly exactly. A cartographer's first law: a map is a lie agreed upon. Change one symbol, shift one north, and the reader walks confidently off a cliff. Lior had spent a lifetime making maps that told careful lies to smugglers' clients, hiding passes in blank spaces, turning rivers into ridgelines. They had never once made a map with more love than the one they made now.

The anchor stroke went down true. It had to; Vhalgorren watched every line like a hawk watching a snare, and the entity knew these sigils better than any living being. So Lior gave it truth — and then, in the flourish at the stroke's end, in the tiny terminal hook that no one but a Marchetti would ever look at twice, they turned the line back on itself. A hairpin. A switchback. On a mountain map, a switchback was how you climbed an impossible slope: you did not fight the grade, you doubled it against itself until the impossible became a road.

The sigil drank Lior's blood and woke, glowing faint gold, and Vhalgorren sighed with pleasure through the king's mouth.

"Again."

The second stroke: the gathering line, the one that would draw Pip's dreaming gift down into the binding's heart. Lior drew it true, and curved its tail into a spiral — and a spiral, drawn inward, was a chain, but read outward, from center to rim, it was a key. Same line. Same ink. Entirely different sentence. It depended on which way the current flowed, and the current, Lior knew — had always known, had felt it in every pass and riverbed they'd ever charted — the current followed the deepest groove. And the deepest groove in this room was the channel in the floor, running from the doors *to* the dais. Inward. Downward. Toward the well.

But channels could be dammed. Channels could be turned.

Stroke by stroke, Lior redrew the binding. To Vhalgorren, watching hungrily, it looked like renewal: the ancient characters taking shape again, gold-bright, drinking the bearer's blood. Only the flourishes differed. Only the endings. A hook here, a reversal there, a spiral wound the wrong way, a terminal dot placed a finger's width left of true — and each tiny treason was invisible unless you knew what to look for, and the only ones alive who knew what to look for were kneeling at the table with their own blood in the ink.

Sweat ran down Lior's spine. Each stroke cost them; the rite pulled at their gift like a leech, and the thin muffled thread of the land's voice grew thinner with every line, and they let it, they *spent* it gladly, feeding the false renewal while their true work accumulated underneath, stroke upon hidden stroke, a second map laid over the first like a palimpsest.

Unbinding, written inside binding. Freedom, smuggled inside chains.

It was the only smuggling that had ever mattered.

Halfway through, the king's eyes found Lior's. The mouth smiled and spoke Vhalgorren's thunder, but the eyes — the eyes were wet, and fixed, and desperate, and Lior understood suddenly that the man behind the eyes could feel what was being written. Could feel the switchbacks. Could feel, for the first time in sixty years, something in the chains going *slack*.

He did not tell. Whatever was left of him, he did not tell.

"More," Vhalgorren murmured. "Faster. I have waited a long time to be secure."

"So have I," Lior said, and meant it in a way the thing could not imagine, and drew the next stroke.

* * *

The keystone line was last.

It waited on the page like a held breath: the master stroke that would knot every chain together and pour the vessel's gift — Pip's gift, Pip's bright terrible dreams — into the knot forever. When Lior's pen touched it, the whole room leaned in. The candles stilled. Mallet stepped closer. Even the guards shifted, drawn by some animal instinct toward the completion of a thing older than their grandmothers.

Lior looked at Pip one last time.

She stood between two guards, small and straight-backed, and she was not looking at the table or the book or the bleeding god in the king. She was looking at Lior, and her lips moved, shaping words without sound, and Lior read them the way they read any map:

*You promised.*

*I know,* Lior mouthed back. *Watch me keep it.*

They set pen to page and drew the keystone — but reversed. Not a knot: an unraveling. The same line, run backward, from rim to center, from chain to stake, from stake to the buried body of the god itself, and at its end, instead of anchoring into the vessel, it anchored into the one thing in this room that had fed on gifts for six hundred years and never once given anything back.

The crown.

Every stolen gift, every drained child, every harvested bearer poured down that well — it had all pooled somewhere, and the sigil Lior now completed said, in the oldest Marchetti hand, in the language of roads: *all roads lead home. Return to sender.*

The keystone glowed. Then flickered. Then, very softly, it began to turn — the gold light rotating slowly around the character like water finding a new downhill, and Lior felt the room's deep groove reverse its pull, felt the channel in the marble begin to drink upward instead of down, felt the binding's vast attention swing around like a great beast noticing, for the first time in centuries, that the hand on its leash had changed.

Vhalgorren went still.

The candles went stiller.

"What," the entity said, in a voice that was suddenly only one voice, only the king's voice, small and human and horrified, "have you done?"

Lior sat back on their heels, palm bleeding, pen steady, and gave the thing wearing the crown the answer their grandmother should have given it six hundred years ago.

"I redrew the map."

The keystone blazed. Beneath the floor, something immense turned over in its sleep — and opened its eyes.

Far above, in the tower rooms and the ward-chambers, wards began to sing and crack. Somewhere beyond the doors, Serah Vane wrenched her bound arms free of a guard suddenly too busy screaming to hold her. And the hollow king threw back his head, and the scream that came out of him was finally, at long last, entirely his own —

— as the crown began, tooth by tooth, to loosen.
