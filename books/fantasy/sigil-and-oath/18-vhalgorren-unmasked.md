## 18. Vhalgorren Unmasked

Pip had dreamed this room before she ever saw it.

She knew the throne hall the way you know a place from a nightmare — the wrongness of it, the way the banners hung too still, the way the light came from everywhere and warmed nothing. She had drawn it in charcoal on the back of Wren's maps, over and over, until Lior gently took the charcoal away and said, *Maybe don't, little star.* She had dreamed the king on his throne with his mouth full of someone else's voice. She had dreamed the crown made of teeth.

But she had never dreamed what came next. No one could have dreamed it and stayed whole.

They stood at the edge of the dais steps, the five of them — though Pip counted four and a half, because Dain was leaning on Vane's shoulder and leaving red smears on her armor with every breath. The warding ceremony was still going on around them, or trying to. Priests in bone-white robes lay scattered where Vane's blade had found them. The great chains of sigil-light that had held Lior were broken, their ends still writhing like cut snakes, and the air tasted of scorched copper and something older underneath, something like a cellar that had been closed for a very long time.

The king sat on his throne.

He was not moving. That was the first thing Pip noticed, and the first thing that made her stomach fold over on itself. In her dreams he always moved — twitching, weeping, his fingers clawing at the arms of the throne as if trying to climb out of his own skin. Now he was perfectly still, hands flat on his knees, head bowed, and the stillness of him was worse than all the screaming dreams put together. It was the stillness of an empty house.

"Your Majesty," Lior said softly, and took one step forward.

The king's head lifted.

His eyes were wrong. Pip had seen tired eyes, scared eyes, the eyes of people in Hollowmere the night the hunters came. These eyes were not looking *at* them. They were looking *through* them, out of some deep dark place behind the face, and they were grateful. His lips moved without sound, shaping the same word over and over, and Pip read it because she had spent her whole short life learning to read mouths through doors and windows and keyholes.

*Please. Please. Please.*

"He's still in there," she whispered. Her hand found Lior's sleeve. "Lior. He's still *in there*. He's asking—"

"I see him, Pip." Lior's voice was steady, but their hand, when it closed around hers, was shaking. "I see him."

Then the voice came.

It did not come from the king. That was the second thing Pip understood, and understanding it made her want to be small, small as a mouse, small as a dust mote, small enough to hide in the seam of the world. The voice came from the walls, from the floor, from the pillars carved with marching kings, from the space between her own heartbeat and the next. It came from everywhere the way cold comes from everywhere in winter.

*Little Marchetti,* it said. *You brought your blood home after all.*

The temperature dropped. The torches along the walls guttered but did not go out; instead their flames bent sideways, all together, all pointing toward the throne like iron filings to a lodestone. And the king began to move — not rising, not standing, but *unfolding*, his body jerking in ways bodies should not jerk, his spine arching, his jaw dropping open wider than a jaw should go, and out of that open mouth poured shadow.

Not smoke. Not darkness. Shadow with weight to it, shadow with hunger in it, pouring upward and outward and gathering above the throne into a shape that Pip's eyes refused to hold all at once. She caught pieces of it: a shoulder like a cliff face. A hand with too many fingers, each finger wearing a ring, each ring holding a stone that glowed faintly, sickly, *familiarly* — and she realized with a lurch that the stones glowed the same color as the sigils on Lior's maps, the same color as the lines only Lior could see.

Gifts. The rings were full of gifts. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

Centuries of stolen light, worn like jewelry by the thing that had taken them.

"There," Wren breathed somewhere behind her, and the old archivist's voice cracked down the middle. "Vhalgorren. Crown-Eater. Devourer of the Nine Valleys. Oh, gods, it's *wearing him*, it's actually — the binding held, all these years it *held*—"

*Held,* the thing agreed, and its amusement rolled through the hall like a rockslide. *A cage is only impressive until someone opens the door. Come closer, cartographer. Let me look at what my leash has dragged me.*

Pip pressed her face against Lior's side. Through the fabric she could feel Lior's heart going fast, fast, fast, and she matched her breathing to it because that was what Lior had taught her on the smuggler's road: *when you're scared, borrow someone braver's rhythm until yours remembers itself.*

She peeked anyway. She always peeked. That was how she'd gotten every nightmare she'd ever had, and every true dream too, and she didn't know yet which one this would turn out to be.

Vhalgorren looked at Lior.

It had no eyes that Pip could find, but it looked, the way a lantern looks at a moth, the way a well looks at the rain. And when it spoke again its voice had changed — softened, rounded, wrapped in velvet. It sounded almost kind. That was the most frightening thing yet, Pip thought. Monsters that roared were honest. This one purred.

*Do you know what you are?* it asked. *Do you truly? Your grandmother knew. Your mother knew, and died rather than feed me another year. You — you are the last drop of the blood that built my chains, and you have spent your whole life hiding the one thing about yourself that matters. Hiding it from smugglers. From hunters. From children.* A pause, delicate as a knife turning over. *From her.*

Pip felt Lior flinch. Felt it all the way down their arm, through the sleeve, into her own small bones.

*Don't listen,* Dain rasped from Vane's side. He'd dragged himself upright, sword point down against the marble, using it like a cane. Blood ran freely from the wound Mallet's men had given him at the bridge, pooling under his boot heel. "Lior. Whatever it says next, it's bait. It's *always* bait."

*Is it?* Vhalgorren murmured. *Let us test that theory.*

The shadow shifted. And then the hall changed.

Pip gasped, because suddenly they weren't in the throne room anymore — or they were, but the throne room had grown windows that had never been there, tall arched windows opening onto a country Pip had never seen and knew instantly: green valleys stitched with silver rivers, terraced orchards, roads that wandered wherever they pleased instead of going where roads were told. A country humming with light. Every field glowed faintly along its hedgerows. Every river wrote glowing letters on its own surface. The land itself was *awake*, singing, and standing in the middle of it — larger than life, rendered in perfect detail — was Lior.

Lior as Pip had never seen them. Lior with their sleeves pushed back and both hands raised, and the whole valley bending toward them like wheat toward the sun. Roads rearranging themselves at a gesture. Rivers combing their own courses clean. A thousand small lights rising from the earth to circle Lior's shoulders like adoring birds.

*This is what you could unmake my cage to become,* Vhalgorren said, and its purr filled the false sky. *Not the binder. Never again the jailer, the feeder, the ashamed one hiding in tavern corners with a hood pulled low. The land's own voice. Every path yours to draw. Every hidden thing yours to name. No more fear, little cartographer. No more burning villages. No more watching hunters take the gifted in chains while you pretend to be less than you are. I will give you the realm entire, inked and bound in your own hand — and all I ask is what your family asked of me for two hundred years.*

The false windows trembled. The false Lior turned, smiling, and held out one hand, and in that hand was a quill, and beneath the quill, spreading across the green land like a scar, the old binding sigils began to glow — waiting to be renewed. Waiting to be fed.

*A renewal,* Vhalgorren whispered. *One more century of tithes. One more harvest, taken gently, from those who barely feel the loss. The children sleep through it. Did you know that? They dream sweetly while I drink. Ask her.*

Every eye in the real hall turned to Pip.

Her breath went thin. Because it was true — she remembered now, remembered the nights in Hollowmere when she'd woken rested and warm and strangely empty, remembered thinking she'd finally slept without dreaming, and how the neighbors who shared those nights had looked grayer in the morning, quieter, older. She had thought it was winter. She had thought it was grief.

It had been *feeding*. It had been feeding on all of them, and it had made sure the smallest ones felt nothing at all so no one would ever wake up screaming and spoil the meal.

"You lie," Pip said.

Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. But she said it again, louder, stepping out from behind Lior's coat with her fists balled at her sides, because some things you had to say facing forward.

"You lie. Nobody sleeps through losing something. I know what losing feels like. I lost everybody." Her eyes were hot and her chin was wobbling and she hated it, hated crying in front of a monster, but she kept talking anyway. "And you don't give gifts. Hunters give chains and call them protection. Kings give proclamations and call them mercy. You're just — you're just the biggest one of all."

The false country flickered. For half a heartbeat Pip saw past it — saw the throne room, the dead priests, the hollow king slumped on his throne with tears cutting clean tracks through the grime on his face — and then the vision snapped back, greener than ever, sweeter than ever.

*Clever child,* Vhalgorren said, and there was something new in its voice now. Something thinner. Something that might, in a creature that vast, have been desperation. *Clever, gifted, doomed child. Then let me speak plainly to the one whose choice this is.*

The false landscape folded away like a map being closed. The hall returned. The torch flames straightened. And the shadow descended, coiling down and down until it hung directly before Lior, close enough that its edges brushed the floor, close enough that Pip could hear inside it — faint, layered, countless — a sound like a thousand voices breathing in their sleep.

*Renew the binding, and I give you everything,* it said. *Refuse, and I take everything — starting with the ones you love. The knight is already dying; you can see it. The hunter is a traitor twice over and will hang for it. The child—*

It paused. And Pip felt it look at her, really look, the way the dark under a bed looks at you when you dangle your feet too near the edge.

*The child dreams for me whether she wills it or not. Do you imagine I cannot reach into a dreamer? Do you imagine there is any distance far enough, any door locked tight enough, any family loving enough to stand between me and a mind that opens itself every night?*

"No," Pip whispered.

*Yes,* said Vhalgorren, gentle as rot. *So choose, Marchetti. The quill or the grave. The crown or the coffin. Everything, or everyone — and even then, everyone only for a little while.*

Silence. Real silence, this time, the kind that has weight and presses on the ears.

Pip grabbed Lior's hand with both of hers. It was ice-cold. She looked up and saw Lior's face, and what she saw there frightened her more than the monster did — because Lior wasn't looking at Vhalgorren. Lior was looking at the ghost of that green country, at the memory of it, and their expression was the expression of someone standing at a window in winter, looking at summer.

They wanted it. Pip could see that they wanted it, the way she sometimes wanted her mother back even knowing wanting couldn't make it so. Two hundred years of Marchettis had knelt in front of this thing and been offered this exact bargain, and Pip understood suddenly, horribly, why they had all said yes. Not because they were weak. Because the offer was *good*. Because it was tailored, stitch by stitch, to fit the exact shape of your loneliness.

"Lior," she said quietly. "Don't."

Lior looked down at her.

And Pip watched something happen on their face — watched the summer in their eyes get covered, slowly, by something else. Not the old guarded blankness they wore for strangers. Something warmer and sadder and much, much stronger. Their thumb brushed over her knuckles, once, the way it did on bad nights on the road, and they smiled at her, a small crooked smile, and mouthed three silent words she knew better than any sigil.

*I've got you.*

"Here is my answer," Lior said, and their voice carried to every corner of the hall, steady as a surveyor's line. "You want a renewal. Very well. I am the last Marchetti. The rite runs through my blood and no other's. So I'll give you exactly what you asked for, Vhalgorren — a Marchetti, willing, working the binding with their own hands."

Dain made a strangled sound. Vane's head snapped around. But Lior was already walking forward, up the dais steps, toward the shadow and the ruined king and the glowing chains, and Pip saw the quill appear between Lior's fingers — saw it, and saw, just beneath it, half-hidden in their palm, the faint beginning shimmer of sigils being drawn that did not match the binding at all.

Sigils running backward.

Sigils she had seen before, drawn in charcoal by her own sleeping hand, in a ruined waystation, weeks ago.

*Unbinding,* she thought, and her heart slammed against her ribs, joy and terror in the same beat. *They're going to unbind it. Right now. Right in front of it.*

Above the throne, Vhalgorren leaned closer, drinking in the sight of a Marchetti coming willingly to the work, and if a thing made of centuries of stolen souls could smile, Pip thought it was smiling now.

*Begin,* it said.

And Lior touched the quill to the first sigil, and drew.
