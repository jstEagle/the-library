## 21. A New Map

The hammering had become the heartbeat of Hollowmere.

Lior woke to it every morning now — the steady knock of mallets on new timber rising from the village below the waystation where they'd made their camp, a sound that had replaced, in these last weeks, the silence that lived inside their skull. The builders were raising roofs over scorched foundations. Someone had planted beans in a field that still smelled faintly of ash when the wind came wrong. Children — actual children, three of them from the nearest valley farmsteads, sent to help and to gawk at the famous fugitives of Grayford Bridge — ran shrieking between the half-built walls while their mothers pretended not to smile.

Lior stood at the window with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands and watched the sun come up over Thornfell's eastern shoulder, and did what they did every morning: reached for the land's living lines out of habit, found nothing, and let the nothing wash through them like cold water.

It didn't hurt anymore. That was the strange part. In the first days after the unbinding, after Vhalgorren had torn loose and dissolved into a scream that no one who heard it could properly describe afterward, the absence of the gift had been a wound. Lior had lain in a cot in the Underhold with Dain's bandages on their chest and Pip's small hand clamped around their fingers, and wept without sound, because the world had gone mute. Every path they'd ever walked had been a sentence spoken to them by the mountains themselves, and now the mountains said nothing at all. They might as well have been deaf. They might as well have been blind.

But wounds close. And what grew over this one was not scar tissue exactly. It was something quieter. A kind of ordinary.

"You're doing the staring thing," Dain said from the doorway. He moved carefully these days; the wound Mallet's blade had opened at Grayford had healed crooked, and the healers in Hollowmere said he'd carry a hitch in his stride for the rest of his life. He seemed unbothered by it. A knight without a banner, walking with a limp, chopping wood every morning like it was a sacrament. "Pip's been up an hour. She's drawn something on your good map."

Lior closed their eyes briefly. "Which good map?"

"The one you told her never to touch."

"Of course she did."

* * *

The waystation's main room had become, over the weeks, a sort of headquarters for the rebuilding — tables of salvaged wood, stacks of timber plans, Serah Vane's meticulous lists of which families needed what before the first snow. Vane herself was already there, sleeves rolled, arguing amiably with the millwright's widow about load-bearing walls, and she paused when Lior came down to give them one of her long, assessing looks. She still did that. She would probably do it forever — the defector hunter, watching for the moment the people she'd betrayed decided to betray her back. It hadn't come yet. Lior suspected it never would, and suspected also that telling her so wouldn't help.

Pip was at the big table, cross-legged on a chair, tongue poking from the corner of her mouth in fierce concentration. Spread before her was indeed Lior's good map — the master chart of the Thornfell passes, inked over eleven years of smuggler work, the most valuable object Lior owned. On it, in charcoal, in a hand that wobbled but did not lack confidence, someone had drawn a dragon.

It was a very good dragon. It had wings like torn sails and a tail curled around Mount Cindral's peak, and it was breathing fire directly onto the pass Lior had once charged a salt merchant forty crowns to learn.

"Pip."

"I labeled it." She pointed proudly at letters nearly an inch tall. *HERE BE DRAGONS.* "Wren says old maps used to say that when they didn't know what was somewhere. So I put it where you didn't know what was somewhere."

"There are no dragons in Thornfell."

"Wren says there used to be."

"Wren also lives in a hole under a mountain and talks to shelves," Lior said, but they sat down across from her, and after a moment they took up a rag and began, gently, working the charcoal off the vellum. Pip watched them do it with the particular patience of a child who knows she has committed a crime worth committing.

"It's a good map otherwise," Pip offered.

"It is a good map otherwise."

"Did you draw all of it? All by yourself?"

"Most of it. Some of it I copied from other maps. Some of it other people drew for me, and I checked their work." Lior tilted the vellum toward the light, checking for ghost-marks under the charcoal. The dragon, mercifully, had not ground in. "Why do you ask?"

Pip shrugged in the elaborate way of children performing indifference. "I want to learn. How to make them. Real ones, not—" she waved at the cleaned patch, "—not magic ones. Ordinary ones. Like yours used to be before."

*Before.* Before the gift. Before the burning. Before everything. Lior set down the rag.

"You want me to teach you cartography."

"If that's the word. Wren said it was."

"Wren says a lot of words." But Lior was already pulling a fresh sheet from the map case, and a stick of graphite, and smoothing the paper flat with the edge of their palm, and Pip's whole body went still with attention in that way she had — the dreamer's stillness, the stillness of a girl who had seen crowns made of teeth and the name of a god sleeping inside a king, and who had carried those visions at seven years old because there was no one else to carry them. "All right. First lesson. What makes a map true?"

Pip considered this with enormous seriousness. "Drawing it right?"

"No. Anyone can draw a thing right and have the map be a lie. You can draw the road perfectly and leave off the ravine beside it, and travelers will die trusting you." Lior tapped the paper. "A map is true when it tells the truth about what matters. Not everything. What matters. Now — if you were walking from here to Hollowmere village, what would matter?"

"The stream crossing. Because it floods."

"Good. What else?"

"The place where the rocks fall. Where the old man died."

Lior's hand paused. "You remember that?"

"I dreamed it before it happened." Pip said it simply, without drama, the way she said all the terrible things she knew. "That's why I want to make maps. Dreams just happen to you. Maps you choose what goes in them." She looked up, and her eyes were clear and dark and older than seven. "I want to choose things."

For a moment Lior couldn't speak. They looked at this child — this orphan of Hollowmere whose prophetic dreams had led hunters straight to her neighbors' doors, who had then walked into the Underhold and pulled the name *Vhalgorren* out of nightmare like a splinter from flesh, who had guided every stroke of the unbinding with her sight when Lior's own eyes had failed — and thought: *she wants a tool that obeys her instead of the other way around.*

"All right," Lior said quietly. "Then we start with scale."

* * *

They worked through the morning. Lior taught the way their grandmother had taught them, generations of Marchettis compressed into patience: how to pace a distance and count it true, how to read a slope by the way water runs off it, how north is a promise you make to everyone who'll ever hold your map in their hands. Pip turned out to have a surveyor's eye and a forger's steadiness, and by midday she had produced a chart of the waystation yard that was, allowing for a barn drawn slightly larger than the sky, genuinely accurate.

Vane drifted over at some point and stood behind Pip's shoulder, arms crossed, saying nothing, until Pip without looking up said, "You can look, Serah. I'm not hiding anything."

Vane's mouth twitched. "Habit," she said, and stayed, and corrected Pip's hatching of the steep bank behind the well with two economical strokes of her own.

Dain came in smelling of sawdust and split kindling, looked at the table covered in paper, and said, "Should I be worried? Last time this family made a map, we ended up under the capital."

"This one only goes to the well," Pip said.

"A relief," said Dain gravely, and ruffled her hair, and stole a heel of bread, and Lior watched him do it and felt the strange warm ache that had been settling into their chest these past weeks, the ache of a thing knitting. Found family. Wren had called them that once, in the Underhold, in one of the archivist's lucid hours: *you're not a company or a caravan or a conspiracy, you're a family that found itself.* Lior had laughed then. They didn't laugh now. They just let it sit in their chest like a coal that wouldn't go out.

After the noon meal, Pip went down to the village with the millwright's daughter, and the house went quiet, and Lior did what they did every afternoon now: they took the master map and began the slow work of correcting it.

Because that was the grief that remained, and they had learned to live inside it rather than pretend it away. The old charts were full of lines only the gift could have found — smuggler paths that bent where no path should bend, ways through Thornfell that existed because the land had whispered them to a Marchetti. Those lines were dead now. Worse than dead: they were lies. The land had changed since the unbinding. Everyone felt it, even the ungifted — the farmers spoke of springs appearing where none had been, of a stand of birch on the south slope budding green in high summer, of animals unafraid. The realm's magic, running free at last, was rearranging itself like a sleeper turning over. Every line Lior had ever inked was suspect.

So each day they took one route and walked it on foot, honestly, with chain and compass and their own two eyes, and redrew it as an ordinary person would find it. It was slower than the gift. It was slower by years. It was, Lior was discovering, the best work they had ever done.

Today's correction was the shepherd's track above the village, and Lior took it alone, boots crunching on frost-brittle grass, the whole eastern face of Thornfell lit gold and rose in the late light. Halfway up, at the place where the track switchbacked beneath a shelf of gray stone, Lior stopped.

And felt something.

Not the old gift. They knew its absence the way they knew their own missing tooth; they would not mistake an echo for the voice. This was different. Fainter. It was less like hearing the land speak and more like standing near a wall and feeling, through the palm pressed flat against it, the vibration of someone singing in the next room.

Lior stood very still. Below, the village hammered and smoked and lived. Above, the mountain held its gold light. And along the track — along the *ordinary* track, the one any walker could find — there ran a faintness, a warmth, a sense of *rightness*, as though the path itself were pleased to be walked.

They crouched. Pressed their bare hand to the dirt.

Nothing legible. No sigils, no whisper, no hidden way unfolding. Just that hum, that gentle pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a floor. Like the land acknowledging them — not revealing, not gifting, just... recognizing. *You walked me true. I remember.*

Lior sat down in the middle of the path, heedless of the cold coming up through their clothes, and laughed — a short, startled sound that scared a ptarmigan out of the gorse.

Because they understood. Or they almost understood. The gift had been taken — poured out of them into the unraveling, spent to starve a god. But gifts, Wren had written in one of the recovered fragments, are not possessions. They are relationships. The Marchettis had held theirs exclusively, jealously, a private wire into the land's secret heart, and the crown had learned to harvest such wires and drink from them. That was the old inheritance: power hoarded until it became food for monsters.

This was something else. Something the unbinding had left behind, or perhaps something the freed magic of the realm was offering freely, to anyone who walked gently enough to feel it. Not a secret key. A shared hearth. Not *the land tells me what it hides*, but *the land and I are on speaking terms.*

Lior stayed on the path until the light went long and blue, feeling that faint pulse, learning its rhythm the way they'd once learned sigils. Then they walked home in the dark without a lantern, and found they didn't need one, because their feet knew the way — not magically, not anymore, but the plain honest way feet know a path they love.

* * *

Pip was waiting up, sitting cross-legged on her pallet with the blanket around her shoulders like a mantle, and Lior knew before speaking that something had happened, because the child had that stillness again — but a bright stillness, a held-breath stillness, nothing like the gray horror of her old dreams.

"You felt it," Pip said.

Lior stopped in the doorway. "How—"

"I dreamed it. Not a bad dream." She said it fast, urgent, the way she always rushed past the word *dreamed* now, as if to get ahead of their fear. "A good one. I was on a path and the path was singing, really quiet, like when the kettle's almost on, and you were sitting down in the middle of it laughing, and the path was laughing too, kind of. And I woke up and I knew it was today and it was real, because—" she frowned, hunting for it, "—because it didn't feel like the old dreams. The bad ones came *at* me. This one just... was there. Like a window left open."

Lior crossed the room and sat on the edge of her pallet. Outside, Dain and Vane's voices murmured over the evening fire, and the hammering had finally stopped, and somewhere down in Hollowmere someone was singing badly and happily.

"Pip. When you dreamed it — did you feel the singing yourself? Or did you only see me feeling it?"

Pip was quiet a moment, and Lior watched her take the question seriously, watch her turn inward with that terrifying honesty children have. "Both," she said at last. "Like... like when everyone in the house hears the same bell. You don't make the bell. You just all hear it." Her eyes came up, wide and wondering. "Is that what it's like now? For everybody?"

"I don't know." Lior reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind the girl's ear. "Maybe. Maybe that's what free magic is. Not a treasure some people own and a crown harvests. A bell anyone can hear, if they stop shouting long enough to listen." They smiled, and found the smile was unsteady. "Your grandmother's journal called the old gift a leash. Maybe what's growing now is a handshake."

"Do I have it? The handshake thing?"

"I think," Lior said slowly, "that you've always had something like it. Your dreams. I think maybe they were never meant to be a curse that hunters followed. I think they got twisted, the way everything got twisted, while the Crown-Eater fed. And I think—" they hesitated, because the next words were enormous, and they wanted to say them right, "—I think if you want, we find out together. You and me. Ordinary maps first. And then, if the listening comes, we learn it side by side. No leashes. No secrets. Deal?"

Pip stuck out her hand with tremendous formality. "Deal."

They shook on it, solemn as ambassadors, and then Pip ruined it by yawning enormously, and Lior tucked her in, and she was asleep within minutes — truly asleep, no twitch of nightmare, no murmured names of hungry gods. Lior sat a while in the dark beside her, listening to her breathe.

Then they went to the table, took a fresh sheet of vellum, and pinned its corners flat.

At the top, in careful capitals, they wrote: *THE TRUE ROADS OF THORNFELL, AS WALKED AND WITNESSED.* Beneath it, smaller: *Drawn without gift, guile, or fee. Freely given.*

And then Lior dipped the pen, and began the first line of the first map of the rest of their life — and as the nib touched down, that faint warm pulse stirred again under their hand, gentle as a cat leaning into a palm, and somewhere in the room, in the walls, in the wide waking world, the bell rang soft and clear, and this time Lior was almost certain they weren't the only one who heard it.

Down the hall, in her sleep, Pip smiled.
