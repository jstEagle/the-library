## 22. Sigil and Oath

The morning Lior Marchetti finished the atlas, the light came through the workshop window the color of honey and old paper, and for a long while they simply sat with their brush lifted, unwilling to make the final stroke.

One year. One year since the Underhold, since the dead road, since the thing wearing the king had screamed itself apart into starlight and ash. One year since Lior had poured everything they were into an unraveling and woken in a burned village that smelled of new timber instead of smoke.

They had not felt the land's lines since. Not once. The gift that had lived under their skin since childhood — the quiet hum of Thornfell's hidden ways, the sense of stone breathing, of paths unspooling ahead like threads — was gone. Where it had been there was only silence, vast and clean, like a room after the furniture has been carried out.

For the first months, the silence had been unbearable. Lior had walked the rebuilt lanes of Hollowmere pressing their palm to fence posts and doorframes and well stones, waiting for something to answer. Nothing did. They had grieved it the way one grieves a limb: phantom-itching, half-mad-making, present in its absence at every hour.

But grief, they had learned — from Dain, who had lost a kingdom's worth of honor and built a smaller, truer life from the wreckage; from Pip, who had lost everyone and loved them anyway, ferociously, without arithmetic — grief is not the opposite of healing. It is the ground healing grows from.

And lately, the silence had begun to feel less like loss and more like room.

Lior looked down at the atlas spread across the great oak table Dain had hauled up from Grayford's ruined bridge-house, plank by salvaged plank, and sanded smooth with his own hands. *The True Roads of Thornfell*, the title page read, in Lior's best hand. *Charted honestly. Given freely.*

Two hundred and eleven pages. Fourteen months of walking every pass, ford, cave-mouth, and smuggler's cut in the mountains — not with a sigil-gift whispering the way ahead, but with boots, patience, rope, and Pip's sharp eyes and sharper tongue. They had measured distances by counting steps. They had found water by watching where the birds went at dusk. They had mapped the way ordinary people had always mapped things, before magic made shortcuts of them, and it had been slower and harder and more exhausting than anything Lior had ever done.

It had also, they realized now, been the happiest work of their life.

Every road in the book was real. Every distance true. No client's secrets inked in cipher, no smuggler's bribe accepted, no crown's seal pressed into the corner. For twenty years Lior had drawn maps as weapons and currency — knowledge hoarded because knowledge kept you alive when the world wanted gift-bearers registered or burned. This map asked nothing. It simply said: *here is the mountain. Here are its roads. They belong to whoever walks them.*

Lior dipped the brush. On the last leaf — a full-page rendering of the whole range, Thornfell in her entirety, every ridge and river named — they signed the cartouche the way their grandmother had taught them, the old Marchetti flourish, and beneath it wrote:

*For Hollowmere, which burned and did not die. For all who walk.*

Then, after a moment's thought, they added one line more, small, in the lower margin where no censor would ever think to look again because there were no censors anymore:

*No gift shall ever again be owned by a crown.*

Not yet an oath. A promise in ink. Oaths needed witnesses, and witnesses were coming up the lane even now.

* * *

Lior heard them before Lior saw them — heard Dain's low voice rumbling something that made Pip shriek with laughter, heard the particular rhythm of Serah Vane's stride, still too much a soldier's march for a woman who claimed she'd retired.

They came through the gate together, the three of them, dusted with road-grime, and Lior set down the brush and stood in the doorway and let the sight of them do what the land's lines used to do: tell them exactly where they were in the world.

Dain Corvath filled the gate the way he filled most spaces, broad and gray-templed and moving carefully on the leg Mallet's blade had nearly taken at Grayford Bridge. He carried a crate of apples against his hip like it weighed nothing, though Lior could see the careful angle of his shoulder. His left arm still didn't trust him some mornings. He never complained. He'd once told Lior, over cheap wine, that pain was just the body's way of reminding you that you were still the man who chose, and that he intended to go on choosing until it killed him.

"Atlas done?" he asked. He always knew. He'd learned to read Lior's silences the way Lior had once read stone.

"Done."

"Good." He set the apples on the bench. "Because I promised Wren we'd send the first copy south, and if you've been fussing over the margins another month I'm forging your signature."

"You can't forge my signature. You write like a man fighting the paper."

"I *have* fought paper. Paper started it." But he was smiling, that rare slow smile that had cost him so much to learn again, and he put one huge warm hand briefly on the back of Lior's neck — the wordless greeting that had become, somewhere across the year, as natural as breath — and went inside to argue with the stove.

Serah Vane stopped in the yard and looked at the house — at the new roof, the herb beds Pip had tyrannized into order, the sign above the workshop door that read *MARCHETTI & COMPANY, CARTOGRAPHERS* in letters Pip had insisted be painted gold. A year ago she had stood in a tavern doorway with arrest in her throat and mercy in her eyes, and neither of them had known then how much would hang on that hesitation. She wore no crown colors now. She wore a hunter's practical leathers still — habits of the body outlive oaths — but the badge was gone and the sword was a working tool, not an instrument of office, and her face, which had once been all discipline, had weathered into something softer around the eyes.

"You look terrible," Lior said.

"You should see the other surveyors," Vane said. She handed over a wrapped parcel. Inside: southern paper, good stock, the kind Wren couldn't get in the Underhold, plus a jar of plum preserves and a note covered in the archivist's frantic, brilliant scrawl. *"First printing of the atlas must use the OLD INK RECIPE (p. 40 of my notes) or the sigils will smear, I have TESTED this, do NOT let Dain near the ink."*

"She included instructions for you specifically," Lior said.

"She knows me disturbingly well." Vane glanced toward the workshop window, where the finished atlas lay open. Something moved behind her eyes — the same flicker Lior had seen in her the night she'd burned her harvest orders rather than her prisoners. "I passed a crown courier on the north road," she said quietly. "New regent's seal. Asking after you, actually."

Lior's chest tightened, an old reflex. "Asking what?"

"Asking whether you'd take a commission." Vane's mouth twisted. "Official court cartographer. They want the borders redrawn now that the provinces are renegotiating. They offered a title."

"And?"

"And I told the courier you were unavailable, possibly fictional." She shrugged. "Figured you'd want to decide for yourself, but I also figured you'd decide the same way, so I saved us both a week of awkwardness."

Lior laughed — really laughed, the kind that had come back to them slowly over the past year like feeling returning to a numb limb — and Vane allowed herself the ghost of a smile, and went inside to help Dain bully the stove.

That left the third arrival, though Lior had known Pip was there the whole time, because Pip had never in her life successfully snuck up on anyone and didn't believe in trying.

She was sitting on the workshop step, twelve years old now and fiercely proud of it, legs swinging, watching Lior with the patient gravity she reserved for moments that mattered. Her hair was longer. There was a scar on her forearm from a climbing accident in the spring that she showed to visitors with tremendous ceremony. Her dreams had quieted — not gone, never gone entirely, but gentled, the way a river quiets below the rapids. These days she mostly dreamed ordinary things: fish, festivals, Dain losing arguments to the goat. Once a month or so, something stranger surfaced, and when it did, the whole family gathered at the kitchen table and took it seriously, because they remembered what dreams had cost and what they'd saved.

"You finished it," Pip said. Not a question.

"This morning."

"Can I see? Can I touch it? Dain says if I touch it before it dries you'll banish me to the goat shed."

"Dain exaggerates. The goat shed is more of a suggestion."

Pip grinned and scrambled up and slipped past Lior into the workshop with the practiced grace of a child who has learned that adults' treasures are best approached sideways. She stopped at the table. She looked at the atlas for a long time — at the mountains she had crossed starving and terrified, rendered now in clean confident lines; at Hollowmere marked with a small gold star, Lior's private addition, on no surveyor's authority whatsoever.

"It's beautiful," she said softly. Then, with the directness that was Pip's signature and her grace: "Does it make you sad? Doing it without the... you know. Without the hum?"

Lior considered the question honestly, because Pip deserved honest answers; they all had agreed on that early, after too many years of everyone lying kindly to everyone else.

"Some days," Lior said. "Mostly it feels like this." They touched the page — the true road through Wolfjaw Pass, the one the gift would have shown them in a heartbeat, the one they'd found instead by falling into a creek and following the creek uphill. "Like proof. That I was never the map. I was just the one holding the pen."

Pip nodded slowly, filing it away in whatever deep place she filed things, the place the dreams came from. Then her eye caught the lower margin, the small inked line, and she leaned close and read it aloud, sounding the words like a spell she was learning:

"*No gift shall ever again be owned by a crown.*"

"That's the draft," Lior said. "Tonight we make it official."

* * *

They gathered at dusk, as the family had gathered for everything this year — births of lambs, funerals of strangers found on the road, the night Vane finally admitted she was staying, the morning the first wall of the school went up. The kitchen was low-beamed and firelit and smelled of Dain's stew, which was excellent, and Dain's bread, which was a crime, and nobody mentioned the bread because love means discretion about baked goods.

The atlas lay open on the scrubbed table, weighted at the corners with four objects that had become, without anyone planning it, the family's relics: Dain's old shield-boss, polished bright, the royal crest hammered flat and reworked into a simple sunburst. Vane's broken hunter's badge. Pip's drawing of a crown made of teeth, framed in scrap wood, kept not as horror but as history — *this is what we walked out of*. And a single dried sprig of rowan from the ruins of the old Hollowmere, the last thing growing in the ashes when Lior arrived carrying a sleeping child.

Five places at the table. The fifth was Wren's, empty tonight, honored anyway; the archivist was deep in the Underhold cataloguing the freed rites, but they'd sworn by messenger to recite the oath in the dark alone at the same hour, and no one doubted they would, loudly, to the rocks.

Dain poured the wine — real wine, traded fairly, taxed by no one — and stood. He had gotten better at speeches this year, which is to say he now managed complete sentences.

"A year ago I had a king," he said. "He turned out to be a cage. So I got rid of the king and kept the part worth keeping." He looked around the table, at each of them, unhurried. "This. Whatever this is. It doesn't need my shield and it doesn't need my oath of fealty. But it deserves better words than I usually give it, so here's my attempt: I was taught to serve the realm. Turns out the realm is five people and a goat. Best posting I ever had."

Vane snorted. Pip applauded. The goat, outside, screamed agreement or protest; with the goat it was never clear.

Then Lior rose, and dipped the pen one last time, and spoke the words they had drafted that morning, and the others repeated them line by line, voices overlapping in the small warm room, uneven and human and certain:

"*We swear by the roads we walked and the ones we buried our dead beside.*"

"*No gift shall ever again be owned by a crown.*"

"*What any of us carries belongs first to each other.*"

"*Knowledge given freely cannot be harvested. Power held in common cannot be stolen.*"

"*And if ever a throne reaches for what we keep — let every map in this house show it the long way around.*"

When it was done, Lior blotted the oath, and blew it dry, and closed the atlas gently, and that was the whole ceremony. No sigils flared. Nothing ancient stirred. The fire crackled and Pip demanded stew and Dain served the bread with the defiance of a man who knew exactly what it was.

Later — dishes done, Vane asleep in the loft with her boots off for once, Pip dreaming whatever gentle thing she dreamed now — Lior stepped out into the yard one last time.

Thornfell stood black and immense against the stars, the same mountains that had sheltered and hunted and defined them for a lifetime. Lior breathed the cold air and felt, as always, the great clean silence where the gift had been.

But tonight — as on perhaps a dozen nights this year, nights Lior had told no one, not wanting to jinx it, not wanting to name it too soon — the silence was not quite empty. At the edge of perception, faint as heat-shimmer, faint as memory of memory, something moved. Not the old gift. Not the land's secret veins humming their hidden traffic. Something wider. Gentler. Less like a possession and more like a conversation — as if the world, freed of the thing that had fed on it for centuries, was slowly remembering how to speak, and had decided to practice on the person standing in a darkened yard holding a finished atlas against their chest.

Lior stood very still and listened with the whole of themselves.

The lines were stirring. Not theirs alone. Never again theirs alone.

Smiling into the dark, the cartographer went inside to their family, leaving the door unlatched behind them — the oldest oath there is.
