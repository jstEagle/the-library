## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The bells of Verrin rang the hour wrong on purpose.

Everyone knew it. The cathedral's carillon had been melted down during the purges and recast into a single bell that struck six times at five o'clock and never once at six, because the reformers who ordered it had believed—sincerely, with the terrible sincerity of men who burn things—that a city which could not tell time could not tell itself stories either. Ten years later, Verrin told its stories anyway, just earlier in the morning, when nobody official was awake to hear them.

Astrid Lockridge was always awake before the wrong bell.

She worked by feel in the dark of her room above the chandler's shop: three luck-knots tied from tarred twine, each one knotted seven times with her left hand while she thought of nothing at all—which was harder than it sounded, thinking of nothing, especially at that hour, especially with Tam's empty bed across the room still holding the shape of a brother who had not spoken a full sentence in four years. She tucked the knots into her coat pocket beside a twist of paper holding salt scraped from the threshold stones of the old sea-temple. Salt wards. Luck-knots. A jar of honey for bribing herself through the morning.

Nothing illegal. That was the whole art of it. Nothing you could point to and say *there, that is magic*, because there wasn't any magic in them at all—or none that lived. The knots were only knots. The salt was only salt. What they carried was older than law and smaller than proof: intention, mostly, and habit, and the stubborn human conviction that if you paid a woman with kind eyes for a little string tied right, your nets would come up full and your children would sleep through the night.

Astrid had spent ten years making sure that conviction stayed true enough to pay for bread and false enough to stay off the pyre.

* * *

The dawn market assembled itself out of the fog the way it did every morning, piece by piece, as though the city were dreaming it into being and might stop halfway through. Fishwives first, always, their carts rattling over cobbles slick with brine. Then the vegetable men with their lanterns swinging, then the rag-and-bone boys, then the whole slow tide of the Low Quarter pouring down toward the harbor square with its breath steaming and its pockets turned inside out and hopeful.

Astrid set up her tray between Maren's eel stall and the man who sold secondhand boots, which was the best spot in the market and had been hers for six years, ever since she'd cured Maren's daughter of night terrors with nothing but a knot, some salt, and eleven consecutive nights of sitting up with the girl telling her increasingly absurd stories about brave snails. The eels gave her shade in summer and warmth in winter and a running commentary all year round.

"You look like something the tide refused," Maren said, handing her a cup of barley tea without being asked.

"I feel like something the tide argued with and lost."

"Business'll be good today. Fog like this makes people nervous, and nervous people buy string." Maren squinted at the gray wall where the harbor should have been. "My mother used to say fog like this meant the sea was thinking."

"What about?"

"Nobody ever asked it twice and lived to report."

Astrid laughed and arranged her wares on the tray: luck-knots in a woven basket, salt wards in folded paper twists marked with a harmless little spiral that any inquisitor would read as decoration, and—because even charm-sellers had to eat—a small dish of candied ginger that she priced high enough to discourage all but the desperate.

Her first customer came before the light did fully arrive: a young fisherman with rope burns on his palms and his cap wrung in his fists like he was trying to drown it.

"Astrid." He didn't look at her. He looked at the basket. "It's my father. The cough's gone into his chest, and the physicker wants four marks a visit, and I—" He stopped, jaw working. "I don't need a cure. I know what your things do and don't do. I just need him to sleep. He hasn't slept proper in a week and he keeps talking about the water, about going back to the water, and it frightens my mother."

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. This was the part of the work nobody wrote songs about—the arithmetic of honesty. She could sell him a knot and let him believe. Or she could sell him a knot and tell him the truth, which was that knots didn't cure lungs, they only held a person together long enough for the person to do the curing, or the dying, whichever the body had already decided on.

"Two coppers," she said. "And you listen to me. This holds comfort. It doesn't hold breath. You understand?"

He nodded too fast, paid her, and left clutching the twine like a lifeline, which, in every way that mattered and no way that would hold up in a court of law, it was.

Maren watched him go. "You could charge more."

"And then I'd be selling hope at luxury prices, and hope's the one thing in this city that ought to stay cheap."

"You're a bad businesswoman, Lockridge."

"I'm an excellent businesswoman. I've been invisible for ten years. Do you know how hard it is to be invisible and solvent? It's the finest craft in Verrin."

* * *

The patrol came at half past the wrong bell, as patrols did.

You heard them before you saw them: the particular rhythm of boots that belonged to men who wanted you to hear them. Inquisitors' patrols walked like weather coming. The market performed its little pantomime—trays tilted, suspicious goods covered, everyone suddenly fascinated by fish—and Astrid did what she'd done a thousand mornings, which was become furniture.

This was the trick of it, the real craft beneath the knots and salt. Magic in Verrin hadn't been outlawed so much as *attention* had been outlawed. The purges had burned the great sigil-workers, the storm-callers, the women who healed bones with a word—but those fires had gone out years ago. What kept the Low Quarter safe now was smaller and stranger: a collective agreement, unspoken and total, that nothing here was worth noticing. The guards' eyes slid off Maren's stall. They saw a woman selling string and moved along, because a city learns to hide its magicians the way a body learns to hide a splinter—by growing around it.

There were two of them this morning, gray coats, ash-gray sashes, and behind them a third figure Astrid didn't recognize: young, new-sashed, walking with the careful steps of someone recently promoted past his competence. He stopped at her tray.

Astrid became very interested in her tea.

"Spirals," the young inquisitor said. He picked up a salt ward and turned it over. "These spirals. What are they?"

"Decoration," said Astrid, in the flat, bored voice of a woman who has answered this question four hundred times. "Sells better than plain paper. People think a mark means quality."

"It looks like a sigil."

"It looks like a snail shell," said Maren, leaning over. "My granddaughter draws the same ones in the dirt. Are you going to arrest a child's snails, ser?"

The young man's ears went pink. His senior partner appeared at his shoulder with the weary gravity of a man who had confiscated ten thousand snail shells in his career and knew exactly how much paperwork each one generated.

"Charm-seller's licensed?" the senior one asked.

"Guild license, third tier, small goods." Astrid produced the paper from her coat. The Charmwrights' Guild of Verrin existed in a state of legal ambiguity that both the guild and the inquisition preferred not to examine too closely; its charter described its members as *purveyors of traditional folk goods*, a phrase that had survived three decades and four purges through the simple expedient of being too boring to burn.

The senior inquisitor glanced at it, handed it back, and steered his junior away with a hand between the shoulder blades. Over his own shoulder, almost too quiet to catch, he said: "Keep your spirals small, mistress."

Astrid exhaled slowly and did not let her hands shake until they were gone.

That was the hour. That was the whole shape of her life, laid out neat as knots on a tray: small magics, smaller risks, invisibility maintained one boring transaction at a time. She had gotten good at it. She had gotten so good at it that sometimes she forgot there had ever been another version of herself, the girl who'd once drawn a sigil on a doorframe that made the whole street smell of her mother's baking, the girl whose hands used to itch with wanting to make something *big*.

Ten years of small. It was working. It was fine.

She was fine.

* * *

The old woman came near midmorning, when the fog had burned off to a thin gauze and the market had settled into its clatter and haggling.

Astrid noticed her the way you notice anything in the Low Quarter—peripherally, cataloguing without staring. Small, bent, dressed in mourning grays gone soft with age, moving from stall to stall without buying anything. Not a beggar; beggars had a rhythm to them, a practiced pathos. This woman moved like someone killing time, or looking for something she couldn't name.

She stopped at Astrid's tray last.

"How much for a luck-knot?"

"Three coppers. Two, if you tell me what it's for. Luck works better when it knows where it's going."

The old woman smiled, and there was something in the smile that made Astrid's chest ache—a sweetness worn down to the grain, like driftwood. "For my husband. Fifty-one years we've been married, and he's decided to die this week, the stubborn fool, just to spite me. The physickers won't come anymore. So I thought—I don't know what I thought. String, maybe. String is honest."

Astrid's hands went still over the basket.

Here it was again, the arithmetic. But this time the numbers came out differently, and she found herself saying, "Sit down, grandmother. Tell me about him."

So the old woman sat on Maren's spare stool and talked, and Astrid listened the way she'd learned to listen, which was a kind of craft too. His name was Oskar. He'd been a net-mender, then a watchman, then nothing much. He liked pickled herring more than any man should. He sang in the bath. He'd proposed to her in the rain outside the sea-temple, back when the sea-temple was a temple and not a roofless ruin people cut across to save thirty steps.

"He keeps asking for the temple," the old woman said, laughing and crying at once, the way the very old can do without embarrassment. "He says he wants to see the water one more time. As if we haven't seen it every day of our lives. Old men get ideas."

Astrid took out her best twine. And because the old woman had given her a story instead of a coin's worth of grief, she did something she almost never did anymore: she tied the knot with her whole attention. Seven turns, left-handed, and in the silence of the turning she let herself want it—really want it, the way she hadn't let herself want anything big in ten years. *Let him rest easy. Let the fear go out of him like the tide.*

"There," she said, pressing it into the papery hands. "Put it under his pillow. It won't work miracles. It'll work comfort, and comfort is most of what miracles are made of anyway."

The old woman pressed a whole silver mark into her palm—far too much—and shuffled away toward the ruin of the sea-temple, and Astrid watched her go and felt, unexpectedly, like crying, and didn't examine why.

* * *

It happened an hour later, and afterward Astrid could never decide how long it had actually taken. A heartbeat. An afternoon. Both.

The sound came first—not a sound, exactly, but a pressure, like the moment before thunder when the air remembers what it's for. The market noise thinned. Gulls hung motionless over the harbor. And down by the ruined sea-temple, where the old woman had gone, something opened.

Astrid felt it in her teeth. In her scars. In the faded birthmark on her inner wrist, shaped like a coiled line, which her mother had called a beauty mark and which Astrid had privately, superstitiously, spent her whole life covering with her sleeve.

Every head in the market turned toward the temple ruins at once, the way iron filings turn to a lodestone, and no one seemed to find this strange except Astrid.

Then the flare.

It started in her pocket—no. Not her pocket. Under her skin. One of her sigils, one of the inert marks inked into her forearm years ago and long since dead, dead as law, dead as everything else from before—it *lit*. Not visibly. Worse than visibly. It lit the way a lamp lights inside a closed box: she felt the glow against the inside of her own bones, a warmth spreading up her arm like sunrise under the skin, and through it poured something vast and patient and terribly, terribly hungry.

And threaded through that vastness, faint as a voice through a wall, she heard a prayer being answered.

*Let him see the water,* the old woman was praying, somewhere down among the broken columns, holding her husband's hand. *Please. Whoever's listening. Let him see the water one more time.*

And something answered: *yes.*

Not in words. In tide. Astrid felt the harbor shift—a swell rolling in against the wind, against the moon, against every law the sea had obeyed since before the city had a name—lifting boats on their moorings, sending fishermen shouting and laughing and crossing themselves with gestures half-remembered from grandmothers. Down in the temple ruin, she knew without seeing, the water had come to the old man. Had climbed the thirty steps of the shortcut and pooled silver among the broken columns, and an old woman was laughing and sobbing while her husband of fifty-one years dipped his fingers in the sea one more time.

The warmth in Astrid's arm faded slowly, reluctantly, like a guest who didn't want to leave. When it was gone, her sigil lay dark and ordinary under her sleeve.

But not, she understood, inert. Not anymore. Something had passed through it. Something had *used* it—as a door, a wire, a straw—and whatever it was, it had been asleep a long time, and it had woken up starving, and it had found her mark the way water finds a crack.

Around her, the market was returning to itself. Gulls remembered how to fly. Maren was staring at the harbor with her mouth open. Somewhere a fishwife whispered a word that hadn't been safe to say aloud in ten years, and no one corrected her.

"Well," said Maren faintly. "The sea's thinking again."

Astrid looked down at her wrist, at the coil-shaped birthmark she had hidden her whole life, and for the first time in ten careful, invisible years, she was afraid—and underneath the fear, worse and warmer, she was *awake*.

Whatever had answered that old woman's prayer had known her sigil. Had known her. And it had left something behind: a pull, faint and directional, deep beneath the city, drawing her attention down toward the mountain like a hook set gently in her chest.

She rolled her sleeve down over the mark, picked up her tray, and began, with great care, to pack up her stall in the middle of the best-selling morning of the year.

Some debts, her guildmaster Halvard liked to say, you collect. Others collect you.

Astrid Lockridge had a terrible feeling she knew which kind had just come calling.
