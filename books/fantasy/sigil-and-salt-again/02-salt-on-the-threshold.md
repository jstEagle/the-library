## 2. Salt on the Threshold

The sigil on Astrid's wrist had been glowing for two days.

Not burning — she'd have minded that less, honestly. Burning she could have treated with comfrey and a curse against whatever fool had hexed her. This was worse. It *glowed*, a faint amber warmth beneath her skin like a coal banked under ash, patient as rent day. She'd covered it with three layers of sleeve and a leather bracer, and it glowed through all of them, which was not how anything in Verrin was supposed to work.

Which was why, at half past midnight, she stood in her kitchen with a bowl of saltwater, a candle stub, and the particular expression of a woman about to poke something that had already bitten her once.

"Right," she told the empty room. "Let's find out what you are."

Her flat above the dye shop was small enough that one step took her from the stove to the window, and the window looked out on nothing but chimney smoke and the grey hump of Mount Ceren rising over the rooftops, the way it always did, indifferent as a mother-in-law. Astrid rolled up her sleeve. The birthmark — because that was all it had ever been, a birthmark, a whorled little spiral of darker skin her mother used to kiss and call *my lucky knot* — sat just below the inside of her elbow.

She'd drawn a copy of it once, years ago, into a charm for a fisherman's wife who wanted safe passage through fog. The charm hadn't worked. None of the ones built off her birthmark worked; she'd learned early that some designs were only good for looking pretty, and she'd sold pretty at a fair markup ever since.

Now the original was lit up like festival glass.

Astrid dipped two fingers in the saltwater and touched them to the mark.

The glow answered. It didn't flare or spark — it *leaned*, the way a cat leans into a hand, and warmth spread up her arm and pooled behind her breastbone, and for one dizzying heartbeat Astrid felt something vast turn its attention toward her the way you'd feel someone reading over your shoulder.

She snatched her hand back and knocked over the salt bowl.

"Fine," she said, to the ceiling, to the mountain, to whoever was eavesdropping. "Fine. Good chat."

* * *

By dawn she had conducted what she generously called an experiment and what Halvard would have called reckless stupidity, if he'd known, which was why he wasn't going to know.

The rules, as far as she could establish them:

One: the sigil woke when prayers were answered nearby. She'd verified this by accident at the market — old Wenna Corr's dying prayer for her grandson, answered by something that had no business answering — and confirmed it twice more since. Yesterday a baker down Salt Lane had prayed, out loud, hands floured and desperate, for his oven to stop scorching the bottoms of everything, and Astrid, passing with a basket of luck-knots she could not sell fast enough lately, had felt her wrist-mark warm like a held hand. The baker's loaves came out perfect. He'd wept into his apron. Astrid had walked very quickly away and thrown up behind a tannery.

Two: the power went *through* her, not *from* her. That was the part that made her hands shake. She hadn't cast anything. She hadn't paid any price. Magic in Verrin cost — everyone knew that, even the people who pretended magic didn't exist. You paid in blood, in years, in favors to things with too many teeth. But this poured through her like water through a channel, and channels don't get to choose what flows in them.

Three — and this was the one she kept turning over like a coin with a suspicious edge — the prayers being answered weren't hers. She hadn't prayed in ten years. Praying was how you got noticed, and getting noticed was how you ended up ash on the Inquisition's ledgers. So whose prayers were lighting her up? And why did they feel, faintly, horribly, *familiar*?

She needed a second opinion. Unfortunately, the only person qualified to give one was Halvard, and telling Halvard meant admitting she'd been poking the thing instead of reporting it, which violated Guild Charter Rule One: *See something, say nothing, unless saying nothing gets us all killed.*

Rule One was very clear on this exact scenario. Rule One had been written by a paranoid man with excellent instincts.

Astrid went anyway. Curiosity, her mother used to say, is just fear wearing nicer shoes.

* * *

Halvard's workshop smelled of beeswax, iron filings, and the ghost of every bad decision made within its walls. It occupied the ground floor of a building that leaned slightly eastward, in the way of buildings in Verrin's low streets, as if even the architecture were tired of standing upright and listening to gossip. Shelves lined every wall, crowded with charms in various states of honesty — the honest ones inert, the dishonest ones labeled "decorative" at triple price.

Halvard himself sat hunched over a bench, squinting at a luck-knot through a jeweler's loupe. He was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and possessed exactly two facial expressions: mild disapproval and profound disapproval. When Astrid came in without knocking, he achieved both simultaneously.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm not on the roster today."

"Then you're early." He set down the loupe. His eyes went to her bracered arm — of course they did; the man missed nothing except social cues and occasionally his own feet. "You're wearing the winter bracer indoors. In summer."

"It's a fashion."

"It's a confession with buckles." He pushed back from the bench, and the chair groaned in relief. "Show me."

"Rule One—"

"Rule One," Halvard said, "applies to guild business. What you've done to your arm is either guild business or it isn't, and if it isn't, I'll go back to my knots and you can go home and be fashionable alone." He held out one broad, scarred hand. "Show me, girl. Please."

It was the *please* that undid her. Halvard rationed pleases like a miser rationing coins, and he spent them only when frightened.

Astrid unbuckled the bracer and pushed up her sleeve.

The silence that followed lasted long enough for her to count the cracks in the ceiling plaster (eleven) and compose two apologies and one denial. Halvard didn't touch the mark. Instead he fetched a candle, held it near, and watched the flame bend toward her arm — toward, not away — like a plant leaning at the sun.

"When," he said quietly.

"The market. Two days ago. Wenna Corr's prayer."

"And since?"

"Three more. Maybe four. I've been—" She exhaled. "I've been testing it. Carefully."

"*Carefully.*" Halvard turned the word over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "You poked a waking god-sigil in your kitchen at midnight, alone, with table salt."

"That's the carefully part. I used salt."

To her enormous relief, something that might have been amusement tugged at the corner of his mouth before discipline reclaimed it. He pulled a stool over, sat, and studied her mark with the expression he usually reserved for counterfeit silver.

"Do you know what your mother did for work? Before the purges?"

"She sold charms. Same as—"

"No." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Before. When we still called it craft instead of contraband. Your mother was a *vessel-craftswoman*. Rarest trade there was. Most of us build vessels — knots, wards, salt-lines, little cups to hold a drop of power. Your mother built herself. Sigils cut into the living skin, so a god could pour through a person like water through a channel."

The word *channel* landed in Astrid's stomach like a swallowed stone. "That sounds—"

"Dangerous. Sacred. Illegal, eventually, but it was illegal last, after it was extinct." Halvard rubbed his jaw. "There were maybe forty vessel-crafters in Verrin when I was young. After the Purges, none. We assumed the order burned them all." His eyes lifted to hers. "We assumed wrong. We assumed *you* were just another charm-brat born with a birthmark."

"It *is* just a birthmark."

"Astrid." He said her name the way you'd set down something fragile. "It's been lit for two days and the flame bends toward it. Birthmarks don't do that. What else?"

So she told him. The lean of it. The warmth. The feeling of attention, vast and unhurried, reading over her shoulder. And then the part she'd been carrying like a full bucket: "The prayers. They're not mine. I haven't prayed since I was twelve. But every time one gets answered near me, it feels—" Her voice betrayed her, dropping to almost nothing. "Familiar. Like a song I knew before I knew words. Halvard, whose prayers am I hearing?"

Halvard was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere a neighbor was arguing with a seagull and losing.

"Sit down," he said.

"I am sitting down."

"Sit down *properly*. This is a properly-sitting story."

* * *

He told it slowly, the way men tell stories they've rehearsed never having to tell.

"When the gods were sealed — and understand, girl, the sealing wasn't punishment, whatever the inquisitors' pamphlets say. It was triage. The old gods were starving by then. A hundred years of the mountain court taking worship-tithe, a century of prayers going down into the dark instead of up. Starving gods get imprecise. A fisherman prays for a good catch, and a starving god gives him a catch by drowning his rival's crew. A widow prays for her husband back, and he comes back *wrong*. We were drowning in answered prayers that should never have been answered. So the court caged them, and the inquisitors burned the crafters, and Verrin got ten quiet years." He folded his hands. "Quiet isn't the same as well. But it was quiet."

"And now?"

"Now something has changed down there. A cage doesn't rust open on its own." He looked at her arm again. "Here's what I know about waking gods, and may I never learn more. When they wake, the first thing they do is collect debts. Old ones. Worship owed. Bargains struck. Names promised. Everything anyone ever whispered to them and forgot — the gods don't forget. They keep accounts the way the sea keeps water."

"Cold dread unspooled in her chest. "What kind of accounts?"

"The kind your mother would have known about." Halvard met her eyes steadily, and there it was again — that gentleness, that terrible gentleness, the tone adults had used around her all through childhood whenever her parents came up. "Your family survived the purges, Astrid. Both of them. Every crafter house on your street burned or scattered, and yours walked through untouched, and nobody ever asked how, because asking questions about surviving families was how you got your own name on a list. But I was guildmaster then too, and I counted. I always counted." He paused. "Someone paid for that survival. Someone always pays."

The kitchen memory surfaced unbidden: her mother at midnight, kneeling at the hearth, feeding the fire something that wasn't wood. Her father standing at the door with his hand on the latch, not watching the street but the floor, as if expecting it to open. Little Tam asleep upstairs, five years old and dreaming, while below him the adults did whatever adults did to buy children tomorrows.

"She never told me anything," Astrid said. "Ten years dead and she never told me *anything*."

"Or she told you everything," Halvard said, "in the only language she had left." He nodded at the mark. "Vessel-craft runs in the blood, girl. If she marked you as a child — and I'd wager my best loupe she did — then that sigil is hers. Her handwriting, on your skin. Which means whatever comes through it comes along roads she paved." He rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and began rummaging through a shelf with the grim focus of a man avoiding eye contact. "When gods wake, they collect. And they collect through their instruments first."

"So what do I do?"

"Two things." He found whatever he'd been hunting — a stub of black chalk — and pressed it into her palm, closing her fingers around it. "First: stop testing it. You're not experimenting, you're ringing a dinner bell and wondering why wolves RSVP."

"Second?"

He finally looked at her, and for once his face managed a third expression, one she'd never seen before and hoped never to see again: pity.

"Second: go home. Go through her things — the real boxes, the ones you've never opened because opening them hurt. Whatever bargain bought your family those quiet years, the terms will be written down somewhere. Mothers like ours write things down." He turned back to his bench, dismissal and mercy in the same motion. "Better you read it than a god recites it."

* * *

Astrid walked home the long way, through the fish market, where the morning catch glittered silver and obscene and everything was exactly as normal as normal had ever been. A child chased gulls. A woman haggled over mackerel with the ferocity of a treaty negotiation. The mountain stood over it all, patient as a creditor.

At the mouth of Salt Lane, a fisherman knelt at the tide-stairs, praying — actually praying, out loud, palms open, the first open prayer she'd seen in Verrin since childhood. Astrid stopped. Against every rule she owned, she listened.

*"Please. Please, let the boats come home. All of them. Even the *Maren Lee*. Even Doss, who owes me money. Let them all come home."*

Beneath her bracer, the sigil warmed — and then *reached*, and Astrid felt the prayer move through her like a current, felt something immense and ravenous on the other end take hold of those words, weigh them, and begin —

She yanked her arm against her ribs and broke the contact so hard she staggered. The fisherman glanced up, saw only a charm-seller pausing to rest, and bent back to his devotions.

All of them, she thought. *Even Doss.* A starving god would love a prayer like that. So generous. So easy to grant literally — bring the boats home, hulls and all, and never mind what state the sailors were in when they arrived.

The sigil pulsed once against her arm, patient as rent day, and went back to its banked glow.

Astrid stood in the sunlit lane with her heart hammering and understood, finally, the shape of the next few hours. There was a locked chest under her bed that had traveled with her through six rented rooms and never once been opened. Inside it: her mother's letters, her mother's tools, and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that her mother had made her swear, at fourteen, feverish and dying, never to unwrap until—

Until what? She'd never said. She'd run out of time to say it.

Astrid started walking. Behind her, out on the grey water, the fishing fleet was coming home early — every boat, every sail, moving against the wind.

Even Doss.
