## 3. What Mother Buried

The chest came down from the rafters the way all of Mother's things did: slowly, resentfully, trailing a decade of dust and at least one spider who had clearly considered it home. Astrid lowered it by rope into the middle of the kitchen floor, where the morning light could get at it, and sat back on her heels to consider.

"You don't have to do this," she told herself out loud, because ten years alone in a house had made her the kind of person who narrated her own hesitation. "You could sell the house as-is. Let some nice family find it. Give them the joy of discovery."

The chest said nothing. Chests were good at that. It was oak, iron-banded, older than Tam, and it had not been opened since the funeral — since Astrid had stood in this same kitchen with her hands folded like a well-behaved grief-stricken daughter while neighbors brought pickled fish and murmured about what a strong woman Maren Lockridge had been, and how strange, wasn't it, how she'd gone in her sleep, peaceful as anything, when everyone knew she hadn't slept properly in twenty years.

Astrid had inherited the house, the debt on the house, the charm-craft tools, and the chest. She'd put the chest in the rafters and left it there through four springs and four salt-fogs, telling herself she'd get to it when she was ready.

She was ready now, or near enough. The sigil on the inside of her wrist — the little luck-knot she'd inked onto a hundred customers' palms without it ever doing more than look pretty — had flared twice since the dawn markets three days ago. Once when old Piet's prayer for his drowned nets was answered by nets that came up heavy and gleaming. Once last night, at two in the morning, for no reason she could see, burning like a coal against her pulse until she got up and drank half a jug of watered wine and stared at the ceiling.

Something was awake out there. Something old. And her mother had known more about old things than any charm-seller in Verrin ought to have.

"Right," Astrid said. "Open it, then."

The lock was rusted shut, which felt appropriate. She fetched a knife and a mallet and persuaded it, apologizing to the wood under her breath the way you did around objects that had belonged to the dead — not because they could hear, but because it kept your own hands steady.

Inside: woolens gone grey with moth-work. A bundle of letters tied in string, addressed to no one. A wooden rattle that had been Tam's before either of them could remember, carved with a spiral that Astrid had always thought was just decoration and now looked at with a slow, cold slide in her stomach, because it matched nothing so much as the sigils waking on her own skin.

And underneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn shut with waxed thread, a flat thing the size of a hymnal.

* * *

She almost didn't open it. She made tea first. She fed the cat, who was not technically hers but had appointed himself to the position with the confidence of a minor official, and who watched her work with the air of someone supervising.

"It's probably recipes," she told him. "Mother's recipes. Salt cod seven ways."

The cat blinked. Cats were also good at that.

She cut the threads. The oilcloth fell away, and inside was a document — real paper, not the cheap rag-stock charm-sellers used, but heavy cream vellum that had no business in a Low Quarter house. Across its face ran lines of writing in an ink that wasn't ink. It caught the light wrong. When she tilted the page, the letters seemed to sit slightly *above* the surface, like frost on a window, and they moved — not crawling, nothing so dramatic, but settling, the way ash settles, finding new arrangements whenever she wasn't looking directly at them.

Except the header. The header stayed fixed, burned into the vellum in characters that hurt to read for longer than a breath:

**CONTRACT OF PLEDGE AND SURVIVANCE, SEALED IN ASH AND AGREED IN BLOOD. LET THE COURT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN KEEP THE TERMS HEREAFTER WRITTEN.**

Beneath it, in smaller script, the terms unspooled. Astrid read them standing up, because sitting down suddenly felt dangerous, like the floor might be listening.

*In the season of the Purges, when the fires of the Inquisition took the houses of the marked, let it be recorded that Maren Lockridge, charmer, of the parish of Saltrow, did petition the Court for shelter for her children, being two: Astrid, aged nine; and Tamasin, aged six.*

*Let the Court record what it gave: passage unseen past three patrols. A room beneath the mountain, warded and warm, for the space of one winter and such further time as the fires should burn. Bread that does not stale. Sleep that does not dream.*

*Let the Court record what it takes in payment: not gold, which is common; not years, which are cheap. The Court takes the soul of the second-born child, Tamasin, to be held in trust until his majority, at which time he shall descend of his own feet to take up residence below, there to remain for the term of his natural life and thereafter in perpetuity.*

*Sealed in ash. Agreed in blood.*

At the bottom, where a signature would be, was a smear of dried brown that might have been blood pressed flat by a thumb. Beside it, in her mother's hand — the same hand that had written *buy more knotting cord* on a hundred shopping lists, the same hand that had drawn stars on Astrid's slate — was written:

*Maren Lockridge, who is sorry, and would do it again, and is sorry for that too.*

Astrid set the contract down very carefully on the table, walked to the sink, and was sick into it.

* * *

Afterward she sat with her head between her knees and made herself think, because thinking was the one craft her mother had taught her thoroughly. *Panic is a luxury, love. Fear is fuel. Sort them.*

Tam was twenty-six. His majority had passed eleven months ago.

Eleven months ago, Tam had been working the herring boats out of Gullmark, sending money home, laughing too loud in taverns, alive in every ordinary way. Eleven months ago — she counted backward, and the counting went cold — eleven months ago, Tam had come down with a fever no physician could name. He'd been found at the tide line below the old smugglers' stair, soaked to the skin, with no memory of how he'd gotten there. Everyone said he must have been drunk. Tam, who drank less than anyone she knew. The fever had lasted nine days, and when it broke he was different. Not sick-different. *Thin*-different. Like a coat worn in rain and dried badly. He laughed still, but sometimes a beat late, like a man catching up with a joke.

And then, six weeks ago, he'd stopped writing. And then the letter had come in his handwriting but not quite his voice: *Gone to seek work inland. Don't worry. I'm well. I'm better than well.* And no address.

He had descended of his own feet. The contract had seen to the walking, probably. The court beneath the mountain had simply reached up and collected, gently, the way you'd lift a sleeping child from a cart.

"Oh, Mam," Astrid said to the empty kitchen. Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Oh, you clever, cowardly, *loving* fool."

Because here was the thing — the thing that made betrayal and grief sit down at the same table and refuse to leave: it had worked. All of it. The purges had taken forty families off Saltrow alone. The Lockridges had walked through three patrols untouched, slept warm under the mountain while the city burned its marked, come out in spring with their skins and their secrets both intact. Astrid owed her life — her whole careful, hidden, charm-selling, invisible decade of a life — to that piece of paper.

Her mother had bought their survival with her son's soul, sealed it in ash, sewn it in oilcloth, hoisted it into the rafters, and never once in nineteen years said a word.

Or — Astrid turned the thought over, examining it the way she examined a suspect coin — maybe she had said words. Maybe every odd lesson had been a word. *Never make a bargain you can't read aloud, love. Always count what's asked against what's given. If a stranger offers help in a bad winter, ask what they want twice, and if they say 'nothing,' run.* Maybe every night her mother had stood in the kitchen doorway watching Tam sleep had been a sentence in a confession that never found its language.

"She meant to tell me," Astrid said. The cat, unmoved, washed his paw. "She meant to tell me and she died first. That's — that has to be it. That's the kind thing to believe."

But her mother hadn't believed in kind things. Her mother had believed in prepared things.

* * *

The afternoon went to inventory, because grief with nothing in its hands turns on itself. Astrid laid the contract's contents beside everything else in the chest and began, methodically, to re-read her childhood.

The letters tied in string were all in cipher — her mother's charm-cipher, the one Astrid had been taught at nine as a "game." She decoded the first three by rote while her hands shook, and they were mundane: cord prices, a recipe, a complaint about the neighbor's goat. The fourth was different.

*If you're reading these, then I've failed to say it plain, which means I've likely failed at the other thing too. Check the hearthstone. Count the marks. Nine means safe. Ten means the Court remembers us. Never eleven.*

Astrid knelt at the hearth with a candle and counted the small notches scratched into the underside of the lintel stone, hidden behind sixty years of soot.

Ten.

She sat back down hard on the cold flagstones. Ten meant the Court remembered them. Which meant — her mind supplied, helpfully, in her mother's dry voice — *the debt has come due, love, and somebody's already been round to say so.*

There was more. Of course there was more. Under the hearthstone itself, in a cavity lined with lead foil — lead, against scrying; her mother had feared eyes, always eyes — lay the rest: a stub of black chalk that wasn't chalk, a ring of keys stamped with sigils she didn't recognize, and a scrap of hide, cured thin as onion skin, on which was drawn a single mark. A spiral, coiled tight as a snail shell, with nine teeth along its outer edge.

It matched the rattle. It matched the sigils waking on her wrists. And it matched, with a certainty that dropped through her like a stone through deep water, something else entirely — the seal pressed into the wax of the one letter that had come from Tam after he'd gone inland. The letter she'd assumed he'd borrowed someone's stamp for.

He hadn't borrowed anyone's stamp. Whatever had taken him had sent her a receipt.

Astrid laughed. It came out wrong, high and cracked, and scared even the cat, who decamped to the windowsill with dignity. Because that was the joke, wasn't it — the great cosmic jest of it: she had spent ten years learning to be invisible, small, unremarkable, a seller of charms too tiny to notice, and the entire time the biggest magic in her family had been sleeping overhead in a chest, signed in her brother's blood-price, waiting for her to grow tall enough to carry it.

"I need to know the law," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the walls from closing. "Their law. The Court's law. Contracts have loopholes; everything has loopholes; Mam taught me that. And I need someone who's read the bone ledgers and lived."

Which narrowed the list in Verrin to exactly one name, and the name was a disgrace, and the disgrace drank in the Low Quarter starting at midday and ending when someone carried her out.

Sera Duvane. The inquisitor who had quit — who hadn't merely resigned, but had stood in the Hall of Records and burned her own order's archive of marked families, and been stripped of rank and branded for it, and had somehow survived the branding, which people whispered about and never explained. Astrid had seen her twice across a market crowd, a broad grey woman drinking alone like a fortress with the gates welded shut.

Asking her for help meant becoming visible. Truly visible, for the first time in ten years, to exactly the sort of person visibility was supposed to kill you around.

Astrid looked at the contract on her table, at the ash-script rearranging itself patiently, endlessly, like something that had all the time in the world because it had already won. She looked at Tam's forged-happy letter. She thought about him below the mountain, in a room warded and warm, eating bread that did not stale, sleeping sleep that did not dream — and she understood with a lurch that *sleep that does not dream* was not a gift. It was storage. It was how you kept a soul fresh.

She folded the contract into the oilcloth. She put the spiral-hide, the keys, and the not-chalk into her satchel next to her honest chalks and her dishonest cords. She filled the kettle for the cat's dish, because he liked it fresh, and he had after all witnessed a significant moment in family history.

Then she pulled on her coat, pinned her hair up under her scarf the way you did when you wanted the watch to look through you, and opened the door onto the salt-grey street.

Halfway down the lane she stopped, went back, and took the wooden rattle too.

Some things you carried whether or not they helped. Some things you carried because they were Tam's, and because somewhere under the mountain, in the quiet dark, a man was forgetting why a baby's rattle mattered, and someone had to remember for him.

The Low Quarter was a twenty-minute walk. Astrid made it in twelve, rehearsing the whole way what she'd say to a disgraced inquisitor, and getting steadily, gloriously nowhere.
