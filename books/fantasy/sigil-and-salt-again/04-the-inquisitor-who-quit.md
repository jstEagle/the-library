## 4. The Inquisitor Who Quit

The Gull's Rest did not deserve its name. There were no gulls in the Low Quarter—too far from the water, too deep under the shadow of the mountain—and there was certainly no rest. What it had was cheap gin, a floor that had learned to forgive spills, and a landlady who asked no questions because questions cost extra.

Sera Duvane sat at her usual table in the corner, the one with the wobbly leg she'd fixed herself with a folded prayer-book she'd never gotten around to burning. She'd been an inquisitor for nineteen years. She'd been not-an-inquisitor for three. The prayer-book was the last of it, and she kept it under the table leg as a kind of joke only she found funny, which was, she'd decided, the best kind of joke anyway.

She was on her second gin and her first regret of the evening when the door opened and trouble walked in wearing a fishmonger's apron.

Duvane noticed things. It was the one skill they hadn't been able to burn out of her. So she noticed that the woman moved like someone who had spent ten years practicing how not to be noticed, which was itself noticeable if you knew what to look for. She noticed the way the woman's eyes swept the room once—quick, professional, cataloguing exits—and then deliberately went vague, the way you'd dim a lamp when the patrol passed. And she noticed the small burn-scar on the back of the woman's left hand, shaped like nothing at all unless you happened to know that sigil-work scarred in reverse: the mark appeared where the charm had *failed* to take, a little negative-space signature of the craft.

Charm-seller. Unlicensed, by the apron disguise. Good ones, or she wouldn't have lasted ten years in Verrin after the purges.

And now she was walking straight toward Duvane's table, which meant either she didn't know who Duvane was—and that would be almost sweet—or she did, and she was desperate enough to be stupid about it.

"Is this seat taken?" the woman asked.

"It's taken by my solitude," Duvane said, "but solitude doesn't drink fast enough to defend its claim. Sit."

The woman sat. Up close she was younger than Duvane had guessed—thirty, maybe, with salt-bleached brown hair escaping a kerchief and eyes the gray of a harbor before weather. "You're Sera Duvane."

"That depends entirely on what you want."

"I want information."

"Everyone does. Most people want it free, which is why I stopped giving it." Duvane raised two fingers toward the landlady. "Gin for my guest. If you're going to waste my evening, do it lubricated."

"My name is Astrid Lockridge."

"I know." Duvane let that sit. "Your mother was Maren Lockridge. Salt-warder, third circle, worked the harbor wards before the Reckoning. Died eight years ago of a cough the physicians swore was nothing, which is what they always say when they've been paid to say it. You have a brother, Tam, who hasn't been seen in public since the purges ended, and a stall at the dawn market where you sell luck-knots so weak the guild examiner would laugh himself sick if he ever bothered to look." She paused. "I know a lot of things. It was my job. Then it wasn't, and now it's just a habit, like biting your nails."

Astrid had gone very still—the stillness of prey doing arithmetic. "If you know all that," she said carefully, "why haven't you reported me?"

"Reported you to whom?" Duvane laughed, and it came out harsher than she intended. "Girl, I burned eleven years of the Order's records in a coal furnace behind the Chapterhouse of Saint Verity. Do you know what's in the Order's files? Names. Thousands of them. Charm-sellers, hedge-witches, women who tied knots over cradles, men who whispered to their nets. People whose entire crime was being *useful* while magic was inconvenient." She turned her glass slowly on the sticky wood. "I burned them because one day I read a file and recognized my own sister in it. She'd been flagged twice and released twice, and the third flag was already written, just waiting for a slow week and a quota. So I fed the archive to the fire, and then I fed my oath to the same fire, and now I drink gin in a room that smells like bilgewater and pretend that was enough."

The landlady brought the gin. Astrid wrapped both hands around it without drinking, the way people do when they need something to hold.

"You quit," Astrid said. Not a question. An accusation, almost, though Duvane couldn't decide against whom.

"I *left*. Quitting implies you stop wanting something. I still want justice every morning when I wake up. I've just made peace with the fact that wanting it and having it are different trades." Duvane studied her. "Now. You didn't come across town to hear my memoirs. Charm-sellers don't seek out ex-inquisitors for nostalgia. So either your sigils woke up—which I'd bet my last coin on, because half the Low Quarter's prayers have started getting answered lately and answered *wrong*—or something worse."

Something crossed Astrid's face then, quick as a fish under ice. Grief, but old grief, freshly disturbed. "My brother," she said. "His soul. My mother pledged it. To the court beneath the mountain. Before he was born, maybe. Or when he was small—I don't know yet. I found the contract."

Duvane set down her glass very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and she had spent three years teaching them not to.

"A contract," she repeated. "In ash-seal?"

"Yes. How did you—"

"Because I *filed* those contracts, once upon a time. Or rather, I filed the records of them, which the court keeps, and the Order keeps copies of, and everyone pretends the court beneath the mountain is a story we tell children to keep them off the smugglers' stair." Duvane leaned forward, voice dropping. "Listen to me, Lockridge, because I'm only going to be generous this once. The court is real. It's older than Verrin, older than the Order, older than the gods the Order caged. It doesn't deal in magic—it deals in *obligation*, which is worse, because magic can be outlawed but a debt is a debt everywhere, in every jurisdiction, including the ones that don't exist yet. Your mother traded your brother's soul for something. Survival, most likely, during the worst years. And the court will hold that paper until the mountain wears down to a hill."

"Then I'll get it back."

"With what? Good intentions?" Duvane snorted. "The court has laws, girl. Terrible, ancient, meticulous laws, but laws. They are the only thing about it that resembles mercy. A pledge can be redeemed—but only by an equivalent pledge. That's the first rule. Soul for soul, name for name, memory for memory. Equivalent exchange, weighed on scales older than language."

"Then I'll pay whatever it costs."

"No," Duvane said flatly, "you won't, because you don't know what 'whatever it costs' means, and the court does. It knows exactly. It has had centuries to learn precisely what desperate people will sign away, and it has developed a taste for the things people don't understand they're signing. You'll walk down there full of love and fury and you'll come back hollowed out and technically compliant, and your brother will still belong to them, because the fine print will have eaten you." She drained her gin. "I've seen the ledgers. I've seen what comes back up the stair."

Astrid's jaw tightened. "Then tell me the loopholes. You said you know them."

For a long moment Duvane said nothing. The Gull's Rest murmured around them—a dice game going wrong near the bar, rain starting against the shutters, the landlady humming something tuneless and defiant. Duvane looked at this young woman with her dead mother's stubbornness written all over her, and felt the old familiar ache, the one that lived where her oath used to be.

"The loopholes," she said finally, "are three. First: debts recorded in error may be voided, but proving error requires the original contract, and the originals live in the bone archive, which is guarded, warded, and *bored*—which is worse than guarded. Second: a debt transfers with consent. If someone else willingly assumes the obligation, the original debtor walks free. This is the loophole everyone thinks they'll use and no one survives using, because the court weighs willingness, and it can smell a lie of the heart the way sharks smell blood." She held up a third finger, then hesitated.

"And third?"

"Third is the erased ones." Duvane's voice went quiet. "Before the purges, the court kept accounts on everything—gods included. When the Order caged the old gods, the court struck them from its ledgers. Wiped the entries. But bone remembers, Lockridge. Bone archive, bone ledgers, bone law. Something erased leaves a hole, and holes in the record are the one place the court's own laws go blind. What happens in a hole in the record... isn't recorded." She smiled without any humor in it. "That's not a loophole. That's an absence shaped like one. And I'd sooner teach you to juggle knives blindfolded than point you at it."

Astrid was quiet for a moment. Then, softly: "Why are you telling me any of this? You said you'd only be generous once. Why be generous at all?"

Duvane turned her glass again. Around and around. "Because twenty-two years ago I was a recruit with good handwriting and a talent for noticing things, and a senior inquisitor told me the same speech I just gave you—that the system was terrible but the loopholes were worse, that clever girls stayed inside the lines. And I stayed inside the lines for nineteen years, right up until the lines were drawn around my sister's name." She looked up. "I'm not going to watch you stay inside the lines until they draw one around your brother. I've paid my dues to cowardice. I own the whole subscription."

"So you'll help me?"

"I'll help you *think*, which is more dangerous and less flattering." Duvane pulled a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper from her coat—the coat still had the deep pockets of her old office, though she'd dyed it a respectable drab. She began sketching: a mountain, a spiral stair, a series of chambers marked in a shorthand only she could still read. "The smugglers' stair enters through the salt caves below the Tidegate. Three days' walk if you're careful, five if you're smart. The outer halls are where the Clerk works—you'll meet the Clerk eventually, everyone does, and when you do, remember one thing: it cannot lie, but it will never tell you anything you needed to know. Everything true and nothing useful. That's not deception, legally speaking. Legally speaking it's hospitality."

She drew the archive: a long chamber, rows of shelves, and at the far end a door she marked with a small x. "Don't ask about the x."

"What's behind the x?"

"Didn't I just say?"

Astrid almost smiled. It transformed her face, briefly, into someone much younger and much more tired. "You sound like my mother."

"Your mother sounds like me. We were both trained by institutions that ate people politely." Duvane finished the sketch and slid it across. "Memorize it and burn it. And Lockridge—" she caught the woman's wrist, gently, feeling the old scar-tissue of failed charms under her thumb, "—whatever woke your sigils, whatever is answering prayers out there in the dark, it's not on your side. It's not on anyone's side. Starving things aren't loyal; they're hungry. Whatever bargain you're dreaming of making down there, make sure you're not just trading one cage for another with better lighting."

Astrid looked down at her own hand, at the faint negative-sigil scar, and Duvane saw her flex her fingers as if something underneath the skin had shifted.

"I know," Astrid said. "It's already started. Yesterday a man prayed for his dying daughter and got his wish—and my ward flared like a struck match. I didn't cast anything. I didn't pray anything. It just... borrowed me."

Borrowed. Duvane's stomach dropped, slow and cold, the way it used to drop in the old days when a file opened onto something with too many names in it.

"Borrowed," she repeated. "Through your sigils."

"They've been waking up. One by one. Like lamps."

"Like *doors*," Duvane said quietly. "Girl, a sigil isn't a lamp. It's a lock. And locks don't flare open from the inside." She stood, dropping coins on the table, suddenly needing air and hating that she needed it. Nineteen years of noticing things, and every one of those things was now screaming at once. "Come on. We're leaving through the back. And on the way, you're going to tell me exactly which sigils have woken, in order, because if I'm right about the sequence, then whatever's climbing up out of the dark isn't borrowing you at all."

Astrid frowned. "Then what is it doing?"

Duvane pushed open the back door onto the rain-wet alley, where the mountain loomed black against the clouds, and answered before she could stop herself:

"It's *answering* you."

* * *

They walked the long way through the Low Quarter, because Duvane knew which streets the patrols forgot and which gutters ran clean, and because she wanted time to think and thinking required motion, like a millstone. Rain came down in fine salt-smelling threads. Lamps guttered in windows. Somewhere a child was crying and somewhere else a mother was praying, and Duvane caught herself listening for whether the prayer got answered, and hated that she listened.

Astrid talked as they walked. Low, quick, a confession delivered to the cobblestones: the market, the dying prayer, the flare of heat along her forearm where a ward-sigil had slept for ten years. The second sigil waking two nights later. The dreams—she mentioned the dreams reluctantly, as if admitting to a fever—dreams of standing in a vast dry gallery, reading names off shelves, and each name she read aloud, she forgot something small when she woke. The color of her mother's kitchen. The words to a lullaby.

Duvane stopped walking.

"In order," she said. "Which sigils, in order. Tell me exactly."

Astrid told her. Harbor-ward first. Then the knot-charm. Then—

"—the birthmark one. Except it isn't a charm, it's just... always been there. Under my shoulder blade. Mother said it was a stain."

Duvane laughed. It was not a pleasant sound, even by her standards. It was the sound of a woman watching a very old, very bad theory put on its coat and walk into the room.

"A stain," she said. "Maren Lockridge, may she rest however she managed to, you absolute *liar*." She resumed walking, faster now. "That's not a stain. That's a seal. And it's not yours, Lockridge—it's a *custody mark*. The Order used them, before my time, before the cages. When something too large to kill was bound instead, the binding was anchored in a living line—a family, a bloodline, a body. The anchor carries the mark. The mark holds the door." She glanced sideways at Astrid's pale face. "You're not a charm-seller who got lucky. You're a *hinge*, girl. And hinges don't get to choose which side of the door the wind comes from."

Ahead of them, the alley opened onto the Low Quarter's crooked square, where the shrine of some forgotten saint leaned drunkenly against a chandler's shop. A small crowd had gathered there, silent in the rain, and Duvane—because she noticed things—noticed that they were all looking up.

At the mountain.

At the mountain, where a light had begun to move behind the stone. Slow. Rhythmic. Patient as a heartbeat, if a heart could beat once a minute and mean it.

"Oh," Duvane said softly. "Oh, that's not good."

Beside her, Astrid's sleeve began, very gently, to glow.
