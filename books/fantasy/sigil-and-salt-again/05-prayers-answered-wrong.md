## 5. Prayers Answered Wrong

The gull came down out of a sky the color of old pewter and landed on the fishmonger's stall like it owned the deed.

"Shoo," said Old Maretta, waving a mackerel at it. "Shoo, you feathered tax collector."

The gull did not shoo. The gull looked at her with an expression of profound, ancient patience—the look of something that had been listening to prayers since before Verrin had walls—and then it spoke.

*"Your wish is granted,"* it said, in a voice like a bell dropped down a well. *"You prayed for your rivals' boats to be empty. They are empty. Rejoice."*

Maretta stood very still, holding the mackerel like a scepter she no longer wanted.

Down the quay, three fishing crews were standing on the dock staring at boats that had come back heavy with catch and light with crew. The *Kindly Tide* had gone out with six men. It had come back with five barrels of herring and four men, one of whom was not breathing, and none of whom could explain why the sea had simply opened its mouth and swallowed Jorin Hale between one wave and the next, neat as a coin into a purse.

The gull ruffled its feathers, satisfied, and flew off to find someone else's wish to ruin.

* * *

Three streets up and half a world away, Astrid Lockridge was having a bad morning that was about to get considerably worse.

She'd set up her corner by the tanner's wall as always—blanket, tray, the little arrangement of luck-knots and salt wards that looked, to any passing inquisitor, like a woman selling knickknacks to fools. Business had been strange all week. Strange-strange, not just slow-strange. People weren't buying charms so much as clutching them, the way you'd clutch a rope over deep water.

And her sigils were doing something they hadn't done in ten years of careful, disciplined nothing.

They were humming.

Not flaring—not yet—but there was a warmth in the inked lines along her forearms, under her sleeves, like a cat curled against her skin. She'd checked twice for fever. She'd checked a third time because the first two checks had been interrupted by the distinct sensation of being *listened to*.

"You look pale, Lockridge," said Bess Undertree, who sold candles two stalls down and considered Astrid's spiritual state her personal business. "Eat something. You're thin as a confession."

"I'm always thin as a confession."

"Yes, but today you're *pale* as one too." Bess leaned across the gap between stalls and lowered her voice to the register she used for gossip, prices, and treason. "You heard about the fisherman? Drowned his own crewmate just by wishing hard enough. And the widow on Candle Street—prayed for her landlord to leave her be, and now he can't remember how to find his own front door. Wanders the street crying. His wife's beside herself."

Astrid's hands went still over her tray.

"That's not—" she started, and stopped, because finishing the sentence aloud was dangerous. *That's not how magic works* was not a sentence a charm-seller wanted overheard, because the obvious follow-up was *how would you know?*

"It's the gods," Bess said, with the relish of a woman who had waited her whole life for interesting times and only recently realized what she'd asked for. "The old ones. My gran said they never left, they just got hungry and quiet. Like cats."

"Like cats," Astrid repeated faintly.

"Mm. And you know what hungry cats do when you finally open the pantry door."

"They eat everything in it," said Astrid, "and then they're sick on the rug."

Bess nodded solemnly, as if this were scripture, which in a sense it was.

All morning, Astrid's sigils hummed. And all morning, she noticed the pattern she'd been avoiding noticing: every time a prayer got answered somewhere in the city—a wish granted, cruel or kind or merely literal—the hum swelled. When the widow's landlord wept, the line along her wrist warmed like a kettle. When Jorin Hale went into the sea, every sigil she carried had flared so bright she'd had to shove her arms under her blanket and pretend to have cramps.

Her sigils weren't making wishes come true. But they were *on the circuit*. Somewhere between the praying mouth and the answering ear, her little stolen threads of power were acting as wire.

She was infrastructure.

"Oh," she said quietly, to nobody, "that's not good."

* * *

By afternoon, the stories had multiplied the way stories do, and also the way plagues do.

In the Weavers' Row, a girl who prayed to be beautiful woke with a face so symmetrical and perfect that her own mother screamed, and strangers followed her through the market weeping, and she hadn't been able to stop smiling since, which was somehow the worst part.

At the Saltgate, a watchman who'd muttered a wish for quiet on his shift got a silence so total that sound itself seemed afraid to enter the street, and now people walked through it whispering, and a dog had gone in after a ball and come out the other side without its bark.

Outside the Cathedral of the Reformed Faith—which taught, officially, that the old gods were dead, defeated, and definitely not listening—a priest prayed sincerely for a sign from heaven, and received a rain of small silver fish that lasted eleven minutes and has since become the subject of three separate theological inquiries and one extremely popular pie.

Each story arrived at Astrid's corner carried by a different customer, and each arrival made her sigils sing a little louder, until by evening she felt less like a woman and more like a tuning fork that someone had struck and refused to let settle.

She packed up early. She walked home the long way, past the harbor, where the *Kindly Tide*'s crew had nailed a strip of black cloth to the mast and where Old Maretta sat alone at her gutted stall, not crying, just looking at the water with an expression Astrid recognized from mirrors: the look of someone recalculating everything they believed about safety.

"You," Maretta said, without turning. "Charm-girl. You sell wards against drowning?"

Astrid's mouth opened. Closed. Ten years of discipline said *no such thing, I sell knots, madam, decorative knots.* But the sigils along her arm pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat asking permission.

"I sell luck-knots," she said. "I can't promise—"

"No. Course you can't." Maretta finally turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry. "Nobody can promise anything anymore. That's the trouble, isn't it? For ten years nobody promised anything and everybody kept their souls. Now things are promising again." She looked back at the gray water. "Jorin owed me money. Little joke between us, he'd say, *Mare, when I die you'll finally be rich.*" Her jaw worked. "I didn't pray for it. Whatever's out there—I want that understood. I didn't pray for it."

"I know," Astrid said, and meant it, and hated that she knew it the way she did—from the inside, from the warm hum of ink, from the taste of borrowed power on the air like copper and incense.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told anyone, the thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone:

Some of those answered prayers had passed through *her*. She'd felt them go. Faint, flickering, half-formed wishes from strangers whose names she'd never learn—and her sigils had caught each one and passed it along, eager little couriers, and somewhere below or beyond, something starving had opened its mouth.

The gods weren't choosing which prayers to answer. Not exactly. They were answering *everything*, indiscriminately, the way a drowning man grabs everything within reach. And her sigils—her careful, tiny, invisible lifework—were part of the net hauling wishes down to them.

She had spent ten years learning to be too small to notice. She had built invisibility stitch by stitch, ward by ward, and it turned out she'd built it *into* the machine.

* * *

That night she climbed to her room above the chandler's shop and laid out every sigil she owned on the floorboards: the painted ones, the knotted ones, the ones inked into leather strips and bone buttons and one truly regrettable attempt at a ceramic tile. Dozens of them. A decade of work, arranged in a spiral, glowing faintly in the dark like embers deciding whether to become a fire.

"Right," she told them sternly. "We're going to have a conversation about boundaries."

The sigils glowed. If anything, brighter.

She tried smothering them under salt. They glowed through the salt, which should have been impossible, and was probably now a new category of impossible she'd have to live with. She tried wrapping them in wool, in lead foil bought from a very confused metalworker, in a hymnal she'd purchased specifically for its aggressively dull theology. Nothing dimmed them. They weren't generating their own light; they were *conducting*, picking up the city's desperate static and passing it along, and you cannot wrap a wire in enough blankets to make it stop being a wire.

Somewhere below the floorboards, below the street, below the bedrock—she was increasingly sure of the direction, though she couldn't have said why—a vast attention stirred and turned toward the little bright web strung across Verrin's underside, and among all its threads it noticed one knot that glowed warmer than the rest, one node where the current pooled and eddied, one small mortal woman lying on her floor surrounded by her life's work, talking to it.

The attention settled on her like a hand.

Astrid lay very still. The sigils blazed. And in the space behind her eyes, not in words—words would come later, words were for negotiations, and this was not a negotiation—she felt something take inventory of her. Counting her threads. Measuring her capacity. A merchant assessing stock, or a lord assessing levies.

Then it moved on, drawn by some larger grief elsewhere in the city, and the pressure lifted, and Astrid discovered she'd been holding her breath long enough to see stars.

"Okay," she whispered to the ceiling. "Okay. New plan."

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, the sum that kept coming out wrong no matter how she arranged it: the gods were awake, and starving, and answering everything. Every prayer granted fed them a little and cost the city a lot. Wishes were being paid for in drowned fishermen and erased landlords and girls who couldn't stop smiling. Left alone, the hunger would grow, and the answers would get bigger, and Verrin would be picked clean by kindness as thoroughly as by cruelty—gutted by grace, one literal-minded miracle at a time.

And she was not a bystander. That was the bitter joke of it. She'd spent ten years selling charms too small to notice, and it turned out she'd been building antennae. The circuit ran through her. Which meant the guilt ran through her too, and so—uncomfortably, inevitably—did the responsibility.

If anyone in Verrin understood how the old bargains worked, how power flowed and what it cost, it was the people who'd survived the purges by knowing things institutions didn't. And if anyone knew what happened when gods and governments collided—

Astrid sat up in the dark, sigils pulsing around her like a ring of patient orange eyes, and thought about the one person in Verrin who had stood inside the Inquisition and walked out again. Who knew the mountain court's laws, and worse, its loopholes. Who drank in the Low Quarter at hours that suggested either despair or research.

Sera Duvane. The inquisitor who quit. The inquisitor who burned her own order's records and vanished into the bottom of a bottle, if the rumors were right, and rumors usually were, but they were the best map Astrid had.

She looked down at her glowing arms—at the beautiful, treacherous, humming proof that her decade of careful smallness was over.

"Well," she said to the sigils, and to the listening dark beneath the world, and to whatever hungry thing had just taken her measure. "You shouldn't have counted me."

She reached for her coat, and her boots, and the last of her courage, and went to find a drunk ex-inquisitor before the city drowned in answered prayers.

The sigils, she noticed, lit her way the whole there—eager, loyal, and utterly indifferent to what she actually wanted, which was, she was beginning to understand, the defining feature of gods.

And, she suspected with a sinking feeling, of whatever she was becoming.
