## 6. Down the Stair of Teeth

The maps Duvane gave her were drawn on fish skin, which Astrid decided was either very practical or very insulting, depending on how much you trusted the sea.

"They came off a smuggler I arrested in my second year," Duvane had said, pressing the roll into her hands with the particular care of a woman handing over something she'd sworn she'd destroyed. "He was so proud of them he wept when I confiscated them. I wept too, a little. They're good maps." She'd tapped the topmost one with a scarred finger. "The stair has three hundred and eleven steps. Count them. If you lose count, stop and go back, because losing count down there means something took it from you."

"And what do I do if something takes it from me?"

"Nothing," Duvane had said pleasantly. "You've already lost. That's why you count."

So Astrid counted.

She started at the mouth of the stair at low tide, in the hour before dawn when even the gulls hadn't opened shop yet. The entrance was behind the old whale-oil warehouse on Gull Street, behind a door that looked like it led to nothing but damp and disappointment, which was exactly the sort of door smugglers loved. The salt wards she'd laid across it three years ago—small, deniable, the kind of magic that could pass for superstition if an inquisitor squinted—were still intact. That surprised her. It also meant that whatever lived below had never bothered coming up.

That was either comforting or ominous, and Astrid had learned long ago that the two feelings shared a wardrobe.

*One,* she thought, stepping down into the dark. *Two.*

The sigils on her forearms woke as soon as her foot touched the first step. Not flaring—not like the market, not like the night the dying woman's prayer had been answered by something old and hungry and glad—but warming, the way a cat warms a windowsill. Present. Attentive. She'd wrapped them in linen bandages against the cold, and now the linen glowed faintly through the weave, like embers under ash.

"You could be less obvious," she muttered to them.

They did not become less obvious. Her sigils had developed opinions over the past week, and none of those opinions involved discretion.

*Eleven. Twelve.*

The stair was carved, not built—each step cut from the mountain's own bone-grey stone, worn smooth in the middle by centuries of feet that had no business existing, because the smugglers' trade below Verrin was barely fifty years old. Someone had been walking this stair for much, much longer. Astrid ran her thumb along the wall as she descended and felt grooves there, deliberate ones. Letters? No. Lines. Sigils, maybe, or the ancestors of sigils, the way a fishing boat is the ancestor of a warship.

Her birthmark—the one that matched a god who had forgotten its own name—itched where it sat below her left ear.

"Don't start," she told it.

It started anyway.

*Forty. Forty-one.*

Duvane's notes, inked in the margins in her cramped, angry hand, said: *Outer halls begin around step 200. Do not speak your name aloud after step 150. Do not accept gifts. Do not eat anything, even if it looks like bread, especially if it looks like bread.* And then, in smaller letters, almost sheepish: *I only ever went as far as 240. Everything past that is rumor and the mapmaker's optimism.*

Astrid had asked her, once, why she'd stopped at 240.

Duvane had looked at her for a long moment with those grey eyes that had seen too many confessions to be impressed by any of them. "Because at step 239 I heard something breathing," she'd said, "and at step 240 I realized the breathing wasn't coming from below me. It was coming from the walls. And I decided I liked being an inquisitor more than I liked knowing things." A pause. "Then I burned the records and stopped liking being an inquisitor, so it all worked out."

*Ninety. Ninety-one.*

The air changed around step eighty. Warmer, and tasting of iron and old candle smoke, though there were no candles. Somewhere far beneath her, water dripped with the patience of something that had been dripping since before Verrin had a name. And threaded through it all, so faint she might have imagined it, was a sound like pages turning.

Bone ledgers, Duvane had called them. Bargains recorded in bone. Astrid tried not to think about what kind of bone, or whose, or whether the ledger minded being read.

*One hundred twenty. One hundred twenty-one.*

At step 149 she stopped, because Duvane's note said *do not speak your name aloud after step 150*, and Astrid wanted to say it out loud one last time while it was still safe. It seemed important. Names were like that—you didn't notice their weight until someone told you to put yours down.

"Astrid Lockridge," she whispered to the dark.

The dark did not answer, but somewhere below, very far below, a page turned.

*One hundred fifty.*

* * *

The outer halls began at step 204, and they were beautiful, and Astrid hated herself a little for finding them beautiful.

The stair opened without warning into a gallery vast enough that her lantern-light simply gave up, swallowed by distances the flame couldn't negotiate. Pillars rose out of the dark on either side of her—no, not pillars. Teeth. The whole hall was built inside something's jaw, or built to look like it, each column a fang the size of a ship's mast, and between them stretched shelves. Rows upon rows upon rows of shelves, ascending into blackness, and on every shelf, books.

Not paper books. Books of pale, dense material, bound in sinew that gleamed like wet pearl, their spines etched with characters that hurt slightly to look at, the way a bright sky hurts after a dark room.

Astrid stood at the threshold and counted her own heartbeats instead of steps, because Duvane's counting rules had run out, and because her hands were shaking.

"This," she said quietly, "is a library."

"It is a *ledger*," said a voice from everywhere, mild as a clerk announcing closing time. "Libraries are for people who might change their minds."

She nearly dropped the lantern. The sigils on her arms blazed through the bandages, and for one absurd moment she considered blowing out the light and pretending to be furniture.

"I'm looking for a record," she managed. "A contract. From about ten years ago. The Lockridge family."

Silence, of the thoughtful kind. Then, from the darkness between two fangs, a shape resolved: tall, narrow, robed in something the color of unlit lamps, and where its face should have been there was a smooth expanse of bone, featureless as an egg. It held a stylus made of what looked suspiciously like a finger bone, and it regarded her with the patient menace of every bureaucrat who has ever enjoyed saying no.

"The Lockridge family," it repeated. Its voice was dry and pleasant, like paper sliding. "There are four entries bearing that name. Which debtor are you?"

"I'm not—" Astrid stopped. Swallowed. "I'm here about my brother. Tam."

"Ah." The Clerk tilted its blank face, and somehow that blankness conveyed interest, the way a closed door conveys mystery. "The installment account. Yes. He's a favorite of the archivists. Such regular payments." It turned, robes whispering, and began to walk between the shelves. "Follow me. Touch nothing. Read everything I permit. Sign nothing, although—" the bone face turned back toward her, and Astrid would have sworn the thing was smiling, "—you will want to. They always want to."

* * *

The shelf the Clerk led her to was lower than the others, close to the floor, as if the book it held had been placed within reach of a child. That detail landed in Astrid's chest like a fishhook.

"Lockridge, Tammas," the Clerk said, drawing out a slim volume bound in bone the color of candle wax. "Contract executed the ninth of Frostmonth, ten years past. Creditor: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Debtor: Maren Lockridge, acting for her household." It opened the cover with a tenderness that made Astrid's skin crawl. "Collateral: one soul, juvenile, held in trust against continued family solvency. Payments rendered to date: memory, in monthly installments. Balance outstanding:"

The Clerk paused.

Astrid realized it was waiting for her to ask.

"Balance outstanding," she said. Her voice came out flat and hard, and she was grateful to it for cooperating.

"All of him," said the Clerk. "Eventually. The contract is structured generously—Maren Lockridge negotiated well, for a frightened woman with no leverage. Her children would live. Her daughter would remain unnoticed—a remarkable clause, that; we rarely grant invisibility covenants, but the mother was persuasive in her desperation. In exchange, the son's soul serves as surety, paid down gradually, memory by memory, year by year, until—" the stylus traced a line in the air, a gentle downward slope, "—nothing remains but the debt itself. He is currently at sixty percent. Another six years, perhaps seven, and the account closes clean."

"Clean," Astrid repeated.

"Paid in full. The soul reverts to the court. It is a very tidy arrangement." The Clerk offered the open book toward her, angling the page into her lantern-light. "Would you like to see his name?"

And there it was.

*Tammas Lockridge*, written in characters that glowed faintly, warm as banked coals, and beneath the name, entry after entry after entry, dated in her mother's careful hand—or transcribed in it, which was worse, which meant her mother had come down here, had stood where Astrid was standing, had signed:

*Frostmonth: he forgets the smell of the harbor.*
*Thawmonth: he forgets his sixth birthday.*
*Seedmonth: he forgets the song she used to sing.*

Entry after entry. Ten years of them. Each one small. Each one reasonable. Each one a piece of her brother, sold a spoonful at a time to keep the rest of them fed and breathing and invisible.

Astrid had known, since she found the ash-sealed contract in her mother's things, what had been done. Knowing it and seeing it written in a ledger of bone, itemized, *bookkept*—those were different animals entirely. Her eyes blurred. The sigils on her arms burned hot enough that the linen bandages began to smoke, very slightly, and she didn't care.

"He doesn't remember her singing," Astrid said. "He asks me sometimes. Why he can't remember any songs."

"The installments are extracted in order of emotional density," the Clerk said, in the tone of a man explaining rainfall. "Songs are dense. First loves are denser. We work downward toward the trivial. By the end, he will not miss anything, because there will be nothing left to do the missing." It closed the book gently. "It is, I am told by the court, a mercy. Slow debts are kinder than fast ones."

"Do you believe that?"

The bone face regarded her. For a moment—half a heartbeat—the endless patience in it flickered, and underneath was something old and tired and possibly, horribly, sad.

"What I believe is not recorded here," the Clerk said. "What is recorded here is that you have read the entry. That is now part of the account. Witnessed knowledge accrues interest, Astrid Lockridge."

Her name, spoken aloud, in this place, after step 150. Cold walked up her spine on small feet.

"What does that mean?" she asked. "What does it *cost*?"

"That depends," said the Clerk, "on what you do next. Debtors' kin have three options, historically. Option one: you walk away, and the account proceeds as written, and in six years your brother comes home to you complete in body and empty in every other respect, and the court considers the matter closed." The stylus tapped the shelf, once. "Option two: you attempt to redeem the collateral. You may. The court honors redemption. But a soul may be bought back only by an equivalent pledge, and equivalent means *equivalent*—a soul for a soul, a name for a name, a lifetime for a lifetime. We are very precise. Precision is the whole of our law."

"And option three?"

"There is no option three," said the Clerk. "There has never been an option three." It replaced the book on its low shelf, within reach of a child, and smoothed the spine with something almost like affection. "But in four hundred years of keeping these accounts, I have observed that humans do not accept menus. You will invent an option three. You always do. It is why the archive keeps growing." The blank face turned fully toward her, and the lantern-light slid across the bone like water over ice. "I find it exhausting. Go home, Astrid Lockridge. Count your steps. Live your small life. Your brother's account is in excellent standing."

"My brother is *collateral*."

"Yes," the Clerk agreed pleasantly. "Excellent standing."

* * *

She climbed.

She climbed because her legs decided to before her mind caught up, and because some animal part of her understood that standing in a bone archive arguing with a faceless clerk was not survival behavior. She counted going up—*three hundred, two ninety-nine*—and the numbers came easily, and that frightened her more than losing them would have, because it meant nothing down there had taken anything from her yet.

Yet.

*Two hundred forty,* she thought, passing the step where Duvane had heard breathing in the walls. The walls breathed now too. She was almost certain of it. Or perhaps it was her own pulse, amplified by stone and fear and the slow burn of sigils that wouldn't dim.

*One hundred fifty.* She spoke her name aloud again, just to feel it in her mouth. "Astrid Lockridge." It tasted the same. That was probably fine. That was probably fine.

*Seventy. Sixty-nine.*

Somewhere around step forty, the awe curdled.

It happened between one step and the next, the way milk turns—gradually and then all at once. She had gone down expecting a monster, and she had found a *library*. She had expected claws, and she had found a filing system. And the horror of it settled into her now, cold and clear: the court beneath the mountain didn't need claws. Claws were for prey that struggled. The court had something better. It had *terms*. It had a frightened woman, ten years ago, signing away pieces of her son in exchange for her daughter's safety, and it had recorded every spoonful in glowing script, and it had called the arrangement *generous*, and the worst part—the part that made Astrid stop on step thirty-eight and press her forehead against the cold stone wall—

The worst part was that her mother would have agreed. Would have signed it twice as long, given half the chance. Because Tam got to live. Because Astrid got to stay invisible. Because love, cornered, will take any deal on the table and call it mercy.

"She did the best she could," Astrid whispered to the wall.

The wall said nothing. Below her, faint as a turning page, something waited.

She straightened. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, the way her mother used to wipe hers, and she kept climbing.

By the time she reached the last step, the awe was gone entirely, boiled off like spray on hot iron, and what remained was simpler and much more dangerous. The Clerk had offered her a menu with two options and predicted she'd invent a third. Fine. She'd spent ten years learning every loophole in Verrin's law of silence—every charm small enough to slip under notice, every ward that could pass for superstition, every crack in every rule where a clever woman could fit her whole life.

The court beneath the mountain had laws. Duvane had said so, drunk and bitter: *Everything down there runs on law, girl. Law is the only thing gods fear, because law can be broken by anyone who reads it carefully enough.*

Astrid stepped out of the whale-oil warehouse into the grey dawn of Gull Street, smelling of salt and smoke and the deep places, her bandages scorched, her eyes red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

Three hundred and eleven steps down. Three hundred and eleven steps back. And somewhere in between, a page with her brother's name on it, waiting to be stolen.

She went to find Duvane.

"We're going to need better maps," she said, when the ex-inquisitor opened the door.
