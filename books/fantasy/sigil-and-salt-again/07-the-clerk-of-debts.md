## 7. The Clerk of Debts

The stair of teeth spat her out into a hallway that had opinions about her boots.

Astrid felt it before she understood it—a faint tugging at her soles, as if the floor were reading them. Cataloguing them. Deciding what they were worth and filing the answer somewhere she'd never be allowed to look.

"Right," she muttered, to no one, because there was no one. There hadn't been anyone for three hours. "Perfectly normal floor."

The smugglers' stair behind her was a bad memory now: a spiral of stone carved—she'd checked twice, and then a third time because she'd hoped she was wrong—with actual teeth, thousands of them, grown into the walls like barnacles. Duvane's stolen maps had labeled it *Stair of Teeth, avoid if possible, use if desperate*. Astrid had been desperate. She was beginning to suspect that down here, *desperate* was simply the local currency.

The hallway ahead was long and pale and lit by nothing she could name. The light didn't come from anywhere; it simply agreed to exist, the way certain clerks agreed to see you only after making you feel that seeing you was a favor. The walls were lined with niches, and in each niche sat a ledger. Bone spines. Bone pages. She could hear them, faintly, when she held her breath—a sound like dry leaves turning themselves.

She walked. Her sigils hummed against her skin, low and uneasy, like cats before weather. Three of them were awake now—the knot at her wrist, the ward over her heart, the small crooked mark behind her ear that her mother had inked there when Astrid was six and told her it was a beauty spot. All three pulsed in time with her footsteps, which meant they were afraid too, or pretending to be her so the dark wouldn't notice the difference.

*When gods wake, they collect old debts.* Halvard's voice, gravel and warning. Well. She wasn't here about gods. She was here about a boy who whittled boats out of soap and cried at weddings and had been sleeping too deeply for two years, so deeply that once she'd held a candle flame near his fingers just to watch him flinch.

He hadn't flinched.

She found the door by accident, which she would later understand was the point. It stood where a wall should have been, plain and gray, with a brass plate screwed on at eye level. The plate read:

**DEBTS — INQUIRIES — FORMS**

And beneath that, smaller, in letters that seemed embarrassed to be seen:

**Please take a number.**

There was no dispenser. There was no number. There was only the door, and the door opened before she could decide whether to knock, which struck her as profoundly rude, and she stepped through anyway, because the alternative was standing in a tooth-lined hallway forever, and she'd done enough of that sort of thing in her life already.

* * *

The room inside was an office. That was the horror of it. After the stair of teeth and the bone ledgers and the light that existed by consensus, she had braced herself for a cathedral of horrors, a throne of ribs, something with too many mouths. Instead: a desk. A high counter. A queue marked out in velvet rope, though the rope was made of hair and the stanchions were finger bones polished to a courteous shine. Shelves rose into darkness, stacked with ledgers beyond counting, and somewhere above, unseen hands were filing things with a patience that felt older than sin.

Behind the counter sat the Clerk.

It wore gray robes of a cut so unremarkable that looking away from them was effortless. Its face was smooth as an egg—not blank, exactly, but *unwritten*, features present in the way a contract's fine print is present: technically there, never read. When it moved, its sleeves did not rustle. When it breathed—if it breathed—the room did not bother to notice.

It looked up. Or oriented itself toward her. Same effect.

"Good morning," said the Clerk, in a voice like a drawer opening. "You are not scheduled."

"I—" Astrid's mouth, traitorously, went for charm first, the market reflex, ten years of smiling at people who might report her. "I don't have an appointment, no. I was hoping—"

"You are not scheduled," the Clerk repeated, with the gentle finality of a tide. "Walk-ins are processed on the fourth day of the week. Today is not the fourth day of the week. Today is a day that has been set aside for reconciliation of arrears."

"It's Thursday," said Astrid.

The Clerk paused. Somewhere overhead, a ledger turned a page by itself.

"That depends," said the Clerk, "on whose calendar you are using." And then, before she could marshal a response to that, it extended one gray sleeve toward the velvet rope of hair. "However. You are already inside the rope. This creates a precedent. Precedents are regrettable but binding. Please state your business."

Astrid's heart was doing something acrobatic and undignified. She gripped the counter's edge—cool, smooth, slightly yielding, like touching the flank of something pretending to be furniture—and made herself say it.

"My brother. Tam Lockridge. I'm told his soul is held here. I want it back."

The Clerk did not gasp. The Clerk did not blink. The Clerk reached beneath the counter with unhurried grace and produced a ledger the size of a paving stone, opened it to a ribboned page, and ran one finger down columns of script that squirmed slightly under attention, like worms aware of birds.

"Lockridge," it said. "Tam. Soul, one. Collateral. Pledged by—" the finger stopped, and something in the unwritten face managed, somehow, to become careful, "—the pledgor. Mother, deceased. Debt satisfied in full; collateral retained per terms, section nine, subsection four, paragraph eleven, footnote six."

"There's a footnote?"

"There is always a footnote," said the Clerk, and Astrid heard—she would swear later she heard—the ghost of pride in it. "Footnote six states that satisfaction of the primary debt does not extinguish the custodial interest. The court keeps what it keeps. It is very good at keeping."

"I want to redeem him."

"Then you are in luck," said the Clerk, "as redemption is available." It said this the way a fishmonger says *fresh today*. "Redemption requires an equivalent pledge. One soul, freely offered, of comparable value. Do you have a soul?"

"Yes."

"Is it comparable?"

"I don't know. How do I find out?"

"Appraisal takes six weeks," said the Clerk. "There is a form."

"There's always a form," Astrid said, before she could stop herself, and the Clerk tilted its egg-smooth head a degree, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense of having pleased it, the way you might please a landslide by falling downhill.

"Correct," it said warmly. "Forms are how we love you."

* * *

Here is what Astrid learned in the next hour, and she learned it the way drowning people learn water:

The court beneath the mountain did not run on cruelty. Cruelty was inefficient. It ran on *procedure*, and procedure was patient, and patience, given enough shelves, becomes a kind of hunger.

The Clerk produced forms the way other beings produced breath. Form 12-C: Petition for Appraisal of Mortal Soul, Comparable Class. Form 7-Null: Waiver of Objection to Retention. Form 3-Rose: Acknowledgment of Visitation Rights (Soul May Be Viewed Through Glass Twice Per Year, Holidays Excepted). Each form arrived with a quill that wrote only in ink mixed from the signer's own certainty, and each required, in addition to a signature, a small honest sentence about why the signer deserved what they were asking for.

"Why do I need to write that?" Astrid asked, staring at question fourteen.

"To establish merit," said the Clerk.

"And if my reason isn't good enough?"

"Then your application is denied, and denial is recorded permanently, and future applications are weighted against prior denials at a rate of one point five."

"So if I apply wrong once, I've made it harder forever."

"Yes," said the Clerk, with unmistakable contentment. "That is the system working."

Astrid looked down at Form 12-C. Question one: *Name of petitioner.* Her quill hovered. Ten years of practice said: give less than your whole name, give a street name, give *Ash* or *Lark* or nothing. But the quill was warm in her hand, and the warmth had a texture, and the texture was *listening*, and she understood with cold clarity that if she lied on this form, the lie would go into a ledger, and the ledger would keep it, and keeping was what this place was *good at*.

She wrote: *Astrid Lockridge.* Her whole name, in her own handwriting, and watched it sink into the paper like a coin into deep water.

Question two: *Relationship to collateral.* She wrote: *Sister.*

Question three: *Nature of pledge offered in exchange.*

And there it was. The trap with the bow on it. Because the form did not ask *whether* she would pledge. It asked *what*, in the tone of a hostess asking whether she preferred the duck or the hen, and every line below it was ruled and waiting, and the quill was warm, and the room was quiet, and somewhere above her Tam was sleeping in a cell with his memories sold off in installments he couldn't remember agreeing to, and wouldn't it be simple? Wouldn't it be *so simple*? One soul for another. Hers was probably comparable. She'd kept it mostly intact. A few scars, some guilt, a decade of selling luck-knots to people who needed them more than bread—but intact. Comparable. *Appraisable.*

"Astrid Lockridge," said the Clerk, softly, and her name in its drawer-of-a voice sounded already filed, already shelved, already *kept*. "May I observe something?"

"Can I stop you?"

"No." A pause. "But courtesy improves outcomes. Statistically." The unwritten face inclined toward her. "You are hesitating. Hesitation is healthy. Most petitioners do not hesitate. They arrive grieving, and grief makes excellent ink, and they sign, and forty years later their grandchildren visit through glass twice per year, holidays excepted, and wave at a great-great-aunt who does not remember waving back." The Clerk folded its gray hands. "I am not permitted to advise you. But I am permitted to describe patterns. Would you like me to describe patterns?"

"No," said Astrid.

"They are instructive—"

"*No.*"

The word came out of her with edges on it. The nearest ledger flinched—an actual flinch, a shiver down its bone spine—and the light in the room dimmed by a polite fraction, as if the room itself had leaned away.

The Clerk regarded her. If it had eyes, they would have blinked. Instead it simply went still in a new way, the way a scale goes still when both pans are empty.

"Noted," it said. "Refusal to receive pattern description is itself recordable. Shall I record it?"

"Record whatever you like," Astrid said. Her voice shook, and she let it, because hiding the shake felt like one more form. "Actually—no. Don't. Record *this*: I'm not signing. Not today. Not for an equivalent. You can keep your forms, and your footnotes, and your glass twice a year. I'm not trading a soul for a soul in a building that thinks grief is ink. I came here to get my brother back, not to replace him. Find another way, or I will."

Silence. The real kind, the kind with weight.

Then the Clerk did something she had not been warned about, not by Duvane, not by Halvard, not by any of the guild's whispered lore about the mountain court. It smiled. It had no mouth to smile with, and it smiled anyway, a slow rearrangement of the unwritten, and it said:

"Defiance is also a form."

"What?"

"Form 0," said the Clerk, and produced—from nowhere, from the air, from the marrow of the world—a single sheet, blank except for a line at the bottom. "The oldest one. Every petitioner signs it eventually, whether they know it or not. Refusal, acceptance, flight, surrender—all of it is Form 0, completed retroactively. You cannot not sign. You can only choose *when*, and the interest accrues either way." It slid the blank sheet across the counter with terrible gentleness. "Take your time. Time is, after all, our most reliable asset."

Astrid looked at the blank page. The blank page looked, in some manner she could not defend to a rational person, back.

And she thought of her mother—of ash-sealed contracts and desperate arithmetic done by candlelight, of a woman who had signed *something* in a room very much like this one, believing she was saving her children, and had saved one and spent the other without ever being told which was which. Love, poured into their forms. Grief, ground into their ink. Her mother had played this game on their board, by their rules, and the rules had eaten the difference between winning and losing until there was no difference left.

Astrid put the quill down.

Not threw. *Put.* Carefully. Parallel to the counter's edge. Because she had a sudden, blazing suspicion that even the angle at which she abandoned it would be entered somewhere, in some column, and she would be damned—possibly literally, possibly in triplicate—if she gave them a messy entry.

"I'll take my time elsewhere," she said.

"The exit," said the Clerk, "is behind you."

"The exit," said Astrid, backing away, "had better be."

It was. Of course it was. Doors here did what they were filed under. She walked backward out of the office of debts, past the velvet rope of hair, past the finger-bone stanchions, and the last thing she saw was the Clerk lifting Form 0 and setting it, blank and patient, in a tray marked **PENDING**, where it settled among hundreds of others like a leaf joining a compost heap of souls.

* * *

She ran.

Not immediately—first she walked, fast, with the studied calm of a woman leaving a shop without buying anything, because some animal part of her believed that if she looked like a customer, the building would treat her like one. But the hallway's opinion of her boots had curdled. The tugging at her soles had become a pulling, gentle and insistent, like a child at a sleeve: *stay, sit, sign, stay.* The niches of bone ledgers turned their pages as she passed, all together, all at once, a long dry whisper running down the corridor after her like a tide going out—or coming in.

So she ran.

Her sigils woke fully as she fled, all three burning cold and bright against her skin, and the light they cast was wrong for this place—it was *salt-light*, dawn-colored, market-colored, the color of luck-knots and cheap charms and her mother's kitchen—and the ledgers recoiled from it as she passed, slamming their own covers like startled birds.

Left. Right. Down. The maps in her head dissolved into pure motion. Behind her, somewhere, a drawer opened. Then another. Then a sound she refused to name: the sound of a vast filing system consulting itself about her, cross-referencing, deciding—and arriving, with a click like a stamp coming down, at a conclusion.

**PENDING** became, she was quite sure, **IN PROGRESS**.

She burst through a doorway into a gallery she didn't recognize—deeper than the office, older, the stone gone rough and living—and skidded to a stop because the corridor ahead ended in a wall of chains.

Not a few chains. *Chains*, hundreds of them, thick as her arm, running from ceiling to floor to walls, and threaded through them, woven into them like thread through a loom, was something that had once been enormous and was now folded very small, and it was breathing.

It lifted what might have been a head.

"Oh," it said, in a voice like a bell dropped down a well. "Oh—you're *new*."

Behind her, far off but getting closer, came the soft, tireless sound of paperwork in pursuit.

"Please," said the chained thing, and its voice cracked on the word like ice on a pond. "Before they file you—I've been down here so long—I've lost something, and I think—I think it was *me*—"

Astrid pressed her back against the door she'd come through and found, with sinking certainty, that it had already locked itself behind her, politely, in accordance with procedure.

Somewhere in the dark, a quill scratched.
