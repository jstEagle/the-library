## 8. A God With No Name

Astrid had been lost for the better part of an hour when she decided to stop pretending otherwise.

The deep galleries beneath the mountain court did not obey any logic she recognized. Corridors branched and rejoined like veins in a wrist. The walls were not stone, exactly—they were something denser, older, shot through with pale threads that glimmered when she passed, the way a sleeping dog's ear twitches at a familiar footstep. Her stolen lamp threw more shadow than light. Somewhere behind her, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be accidental, as though the mountain itself were counting.

"Left at the third rib," she muttered, quoting Duvane's map from memory. "Then down past the weeping arch."

There had been no weeping arch. There had been an arch, certainly, carved with faces whose expressions she'd declined to study closely, but it had been silent as everything else down here. She was beginning to suspect that Duvane's maps were less a guide than a dare.

She pressed her palm against the wall to steady herself—and hissed.

The sigils on her forearm had woken again. All of them, this time, glowing through her sleeve like coals raked over with ash, humming a note she felt in her teeth rather than heard. Since the Clerk's hall, since she'd fled up one corridor and then—foolishly, desperately—down another to escape the thing's patient footsteps, they hadn't stopped. They pulled. Not toward the exit. Toward *down*.

"No," she told them, the way you'd tell a dog not to chase gulls. "We are going *up*. Up is where the sky is. Up is where people are, and doors, and the general concept of daylight."

The sigils pulsed, unimpressed.

Astrid leaned her forehead against the cool wall and let herself have three seconds of honest fear. Then she straightened, because ten years of selling luck-knots to nervous brides had taught her that fear was a luxury you paid for by the minute, and she was running low on coin.

"Fine," she said. "Down. But if we die, I'm haunting you specifically."

She turned left, following the pull, and the corridor obliged by becoming a stair.

* * *

The stair went further than any stair had a right to. She counted two hundred steps and then stopped counting because the number had begun to feel like an accusation. The air changed as she descended—thinner, then thicker, then strange, tasting faintly of iron and old rain. The pale threads in the walls grew brighter here, converging, all of them flowing downward like streams finding a river.

At the bottom was a door.

It was not locked. That was the first wrong thing. Everything else in this place had been sealed, warded, recorded—the Clerk's halls full of bone ledgers and rules stacked on rules. But this door stood ajar, its frame carved with sigils she didn't recognize, and every one of those sigils had been *scratched out*. Deliberately. Methodically. Gouged deep enough that the grooves still held shadows even in lamplight.

Someone had wanted this door forgotten so thoroughly that they'd erased the very idea of locking it.

Astrid stood before it for a long moment, lamp raised, listening. From beyond came a sound she took a moment to place, because it was so entirely out of keeping with everything else down here.

Humming.

Someone—or something—was humming a tuneless little melody, wandering and restarting, the way you hum when you can't remember how the song goes but are certain there *was* a song.

Every sensible bone in her body said *leave*. Every sensible bone had been saying leave since the fisherman drowned, since Halvard's warning, since the ash-sealed contract with Tam's name in it. And every single one of them had been overruled, one after another, by the stubborn remainder of her that could not walk away from a debt.

Astrid pushed the door open.

* * *

The chamber beyond was vast and low-ceilinged, a cavern rather than a room, and it was full of chains.

Not hanging from the ceiling—growing out of the floor, or perhaps the floor had grown around them, pale metal fused into the same veined substance as the walls. The chains ran inward from every direction, converging at the center of the chamber, where—

Where there was a shape.

It might once have been tall. It had clearly once been *something*, because the chains were not casual things; they had been forged for it, fitted to it, each link wide enough to circle a tree trunk. What remained sat slumped in the cradle of them like laundry left out in the rain: a form made of dimness, edges soft, features worn smooth as sea glass. Where a face should be there was a suggestion of a face, the way clouds suggest ships. It had no eyes, but it turned toward her anyway, the whole of it tilting like a plant finding sun.

And it said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole:

"Oh! Oh, hello. Hello. Is someone there? Someone is there."

Astrid's lamp trembled. "I'm—" Her voice cracked; she cleared her throat. "I'm no one. Wrong turn."

"No, no, you're *someone*." The shape strained forward; the chains drew taut with a sound like a ship groaning. "You're lit. You're marked. You're—" It stopped. The suggestion of a face seemed to sharpen, focusing. "You're carrying my work."

"Your work?"

"The marks." It gestured—or tried to; the chain caught it short—with an arm that ended in a hand only half-committed to being a hand. "On your arm. Under your sleeve. I know the hand that drew them. I know it the way you know your own signature. Except—" The voice faltered, and something in the faltering was so nakedly lost that Astrid's chest hurt before she understood why. "Except I don't know whose signature it is. I don't know whose anything it is. Do you know? Do you know who I am?"

The question hung in the cavern air, enormous and small at once.

"I don't," Astrid said carefully. "I've never seen you before."

"No," the shape agreed, sagging back into its chains. "Neither have I. Isn't that funny. Neither have I." And it resumed its humming, the tuneless wandering melody, picking up at a different point this time, as though hoping the song would remember itself from a new beginning.

Astrid should have left. She knew that with the flat certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant. Whatever this was—a prisoner, a trap, a test set by the Clerk—it was none of her business, and her business was already a catastrophe with her brother's name on it.

But her sigils were burning now, fully alight, and they weren't pulling anymore. They were *reaching*. And under their glow, on the inside of her left forearm, in the place she had never shown anyone, in the place her mother had kissed once and called *lucky*—

Her birthmark had begun to shine.

* * *

She rolled up her sleeve before she could talk herself out of it. The mark stood out against her skin: a spiral crossed by three lines, small as a thumbnail, the color of old ink. She'd had it since birth. Her mother had traced it with a fingertip while nursing her, according to family legend, and hummed.

The shape went utterly still.

"That," it whispered. "That's mine."

"It's mine. I was born with it."

"Yes," it said, wonderingly. "Yes, you were. And it's mine. Both things. Aren't both things wonderful?" It laughed—a broken, delighted sound, like bells sold off a drowned church. "I put that mark on the world. I don't remember doing it. I don't remember *me*. But I know that mark the way a river knows its bed. I signed it. Everywhere. On the hulls of boats and the brows of children and the lintels of houses where people wanted—" Its voice snagged. "Wanted something. Wanted safe passage. Wanted fair winds. Wanted *me*. They used to want me. Why can't I remember what for?"

Astrid stood very still, the way you stand when a wild animal has come close and you do not want to be the reason it runs—or the reason it doesn't.

"You're a god," she said quietly.

The word landed strangely. The shape flinched as though she'd thrown it.

"Don't say that."

"Why not?"

"Because gods have names." The chains creaked as it curled inward, smaller. "Gods have names, and temples, and days of the week, and little brass offerings, and enemies with better publicity. Gods get *remembered*. I looked. I've had a long time to look. There's nothing. Not erased—erased leaves a hole, and holes have edges, and you can learn the shape of yourself from the edges of your hole. This is worse. This is smooth. It's as if I was never—" A long pause. The humming started again, then stopped mid-note. "Do you know what it's like to be certain you were loved and unable to find a single person who remembers loving you?"

Astrid thought of her mother. Of the contract in ash. Of Tam, somewhere above or below or within this mountain, his memories sold off in installments he couldn't recall agreeing to.

"Yes," she said. "Actually. I think I might."

* * *

She sat down. It seemed important, suddenly, not to loom. She settled cross-legged on the cold floor just outside the reach of the nearest chain, lamp between them like a campfire, and the god watched her with its face-shaped absence, and after a while it relaxed by degrees, the way a stray does.

"You fled from the Clerk," it observed.

"How could you possibly know that?"

"The whole mountain knows. The mountain is mostly Clerk. It thinks in ledgers." The god wrinkled its non-nose. "Terrible thoughts. All columns and conditions. When someone runs, the mountain *itches*." It peered at her. "You refused to sign. Everyone signs. That's the whole architecture of the place. Everyone signs eventually, because the alternative is being unrecorded, and being unrecorded is—" It gestured at itself, a small, terrible flourish. "Well. You see the exhibit."

"What happens to things that go unrecorded?"

"They stop having happened." The god said it lightly, the way you'd describe weather. "That's the court's genius, if you want to call it that. It doesn't kill you. Killing leaves a body, a grief, a story. It just closes your ledger. And a closed ledger is a very quiet kind of never-having-been." It tilted its head. "Your marks are awake, by the way. Has anyone told you that? They're singing. Badly. You should really have them tuned."

"They woke up recently," Astrid admitted. "When the prayers started getting answered."

"Ah." Something moved across the smooth face—too quick to read. "Yes. The waking. My siblings, probably. Or cousins. Or whatever we are to each other; kinship gets vague when everyone's starving. They're answering prayers because prayer is food, and they've been fed on fumes for a generation, and a starving thing isn't picky about *how* it eats." The god's voice dropped. "Is it bad? Out there. The answers."

"A fisherman prayed for his rivals' boats to fail. They failed. With the rivals aboard."

The god was quiet for a moment. "That sounds like Mereth," it said, and then went rigid. "*Mereth.*" It said the name twice more, tasting it, turning it over. "Mereth. Tide-turner. Gray-cloak. That's—that's a name, that's a real name, I *have* one of those, I have—" The delight collapsed as fast as it came. "No. No, that's borrowed. That's from a hymn I overheard through the rock, forty years ago, maybe sixty. It's not mine. It's just the closest thing to mine I own." It folded its half-hands in its lap. "I'm sorry. You come all this way and I make you watch me try on other people's names. It's rude. My mother would be appalled." A pause. "Did I have a mother? I feel like I had a mother. Everyone has a mother. Even mountains have mothers, and I'm fairly sure I'm not a mountain."

Astrid found, to her surprise, that her eyes were stinging.

"You're not a mountain," she managed.

"Small mercies." The god studied her. "You're crying. Did I do that? I don't think I did that. I'd remember doing that. Probably. I'd like to be the sort of thing that remembers."

"It's not you. It's—" She laughed, wet and helpless. "You're the saddest creature I've ever met, and I once comforted a tax collector."

That startled a laugh out of the god too, rusty and surprised, and for a moment the cavern felt almost warm.

* * *

"Why me?" she asked, later. Minutes or hours; time down here was a rumor. "Why does my birthmark match your sigil? Why do my charms wake when I carry them near you? What am I to you?"

The god was quiet for a long time. The chains shifted as it leaned closer, as far as they allowed, and the pale threads in the walls brightened around them both, attentive.

"I don't know," it said. "But I know this much, the way I know the mark: I didn't give that sign to many. It was expensive, giving it. It cost me something real each time. So I gave it to—" It counted on fingers that kept changing number. "Few. A handful. People who owed me nothing and asked for nothing, which is rarer than you'd think among praying folk. And I bound the sign to a promise. I don't remember the promise. But promises outlast memory; that's practically their job." Its voice softened. "Whatever I promised, I think I promised it to your line. Which means either your grandmother cheated me beautifully, or I loved your family very much once."

"My mother made bargains," Astrid said. The words came out bitter. "With courts. With powers. She liked to keep our survival collateralized."

"Mm. Mothers do that. Mine may have eaten a continent; I genuinely can't check." The god's absent face tilted. "You hate her a little."

"I hate her a lot, currently. It's recent. I'm pacing myself."

"Good. Hate is a fine ration. It keeps." The god smiled without any features to smile with, and somehow it worked. "But save some room. Hate goes stale faster than love does. Love keeps fermenting. In my experience—which is, admittedly, a blank page with one sentence written on it in someone else's handwriting."

Astrid wiped her eyes with her heel of her hand. "What sentence?"

"*Ask it its name,*" the god recited. "That's the whole of what I know about myself. Someone wrote it somewhere I can't reach, and it's been rattling around my emptiness ever since like a pea in a drum." It leaned forward, chains whispering, and its voice dropped to something raw and unguarded, stripped of all its jokes. "So I'm asking. Not knowing who to ask, or what answer I'm owed, or whether asking is even allowed. I'm asking you, marked one, daughter-of-my-sign, stranger-who-stayed: *do you know my name?*"

And Astrid opened her mouth to say *no*—because she didn't, because no one did, because the records were smooth as sea glass—

—and her birthmark burned white-hot, and every sigil on her body flared at once, and from somewhere deeper than memory, deeper than the archive, deeper than the mountain, a word rose in her throat like a bubble rising through dark water.

She clapped her hand over her mouth.

The god stared at her. The whole cavern seemed to lean in.

"You know it," it breathed. "It's *in* you. It's been in you all along. That's why the marks wake near me—you're not carrying my work, child. You're carrying my *name*."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that did not belong in the deep galleries at all: footsteps. Measured. Patient. Accompanied by the soft rustle of pages turning.

The Clerk had found the door it had never needed to lock.
