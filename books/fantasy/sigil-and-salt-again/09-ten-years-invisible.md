## 9. Ten Years Invisible

### Mother

Maren Lockridge taught her daughter knots the way other mothers taught prayers: early, gently, and with the understanding that someday the words might save her life.

"Again," she said, and six-year-old Astrid stuck out her tongue in concentration and fumbled the overhand loop for the fourth time.

"It's *hard*," Astrid announced, with the gravity of a judge sentencing a criminal.

"So is breathing, if you think about it too hard." Maren reached over and loosened the girl's white-knuckled grip on the cord. "Charm-craft isn't about forcing. It's about asking politely. A knot is just a question your hands ask the world."

"What's it asking?"

"That things hold together." Maren smiled despite herself. Her daughter had inherited Tam's talent for questions that went straight through the floorboards of an answer. "Here. Watch my fingers, not mine—watch the *rope*. The rope knows what it wants to do."

They sat at the kitchen table of the little house on Candlemaker's Row, salt drying in bowls by the window, the smell of beeswax and brine thick enough to lean against. Outside, Verrin went about its morning: fish carts rattling, gulls arguing over the harbor's leavings, and—new this season—the sound of boots. More boots than before. Inquisitors' patrols had doubled since spring.

Astrid got the loop right on the fifth try. A tiny warmth bloomed under her fingers, faint as a candle seen through fog—a luck-thread taking hold, barely enough magic to keep a fisherman's line from snagging.

"I did it!"

"You did it." Maren kissed the top of her head. "Now we never speak of it where anyone can hear."

"Why not?"

"Because," Maren said, tying off the charm with hands that had done this ten thousand times, "the world is currently run by people who believe that small kindnesses are a disease. We're going to let them go on believing it."

Astrid considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, including whether crabs had feelings. "That seems silly."

"It is entirely silly," Maren agreed. "And it is also the law. Welcome to being grown up. It gets worse."

### Astrid

Ten years later, Astrid could tie a warding knot blindfolded, underwater, mid-sneeze, while reciting the Inquisitorial Code of Public Safety as cover. She'd learned that last trick from necessity, when a patrol once stopped her at the fish gate and she'd kept her hands busy under her shawl, finishing a salt-ward while loudly complaining about the price of mackerel.

She was sixteen the autumn the purges closed in like a hand around the city's throat, and she remembered that year not as dates but as smells: the char-smell after the shrine burnings, the ink-smell of the new registry offices, the vinegar smell of the solution they used to scrub sigils off doorposts.

"They took the Alchemist's Guild yesterday," Tam whispered across the dinner table. He was fourteen, all elbows and worry. "Whole building. Mr. Corvin from the chandlery saw them load people into wagons. Old Mr. Corvin. He doesn't see well. If *he* noticed—"

"Eat your soup," Maren said.

"Mum—"

"Eat your soup and lower your voice, because Mrs. Pellow next door reports everything to the registry office in exchange for lamp oil, and I would rather not be arrested between the soup course and the bread."

It was a measure of how strange their lives had become that Tam actually ate his soup, obediently, while discussing the end of the world.

Astrid looked at her mother across the table. Maren was doing her calm face, the one she wore like armor, but her spoon trembled—one hair's width, back and forth—and Astrid, who had been raised to notice the rope rather than the hands, noticed.

Afterward, in the kitchen, while Tam slept, Maren sat with the shutters drawn and a single stub of candle, writing something on a scrap of parchment. Not charm-work. Charm-work was knots and sigils and spoken intent. This was *writing*. Words in a formal hand, arranged in columns, with a space at the bottom for a signature.

"Mum? What's that?"

Maren covered it with her palm, quick as a snare closing. Then, slowly, she turned it face up anyway, because there were things she had decided her children would not learn about by surprise.

"A letter," she said. "To someone who should not exist."

### Mother

The court beneath the mountain was real. Every charm-seller in Verrin knew the old stories: that before the city had laws, it had bargains; that beneath the granite roots of Mount Verrin there was a hall where debts were written in bone and honored past death. The inquisitors called such stories superstition. The guild called them dangerous nonsense. Both were wrong for the same reason—they had never been desperate enough to test it.

Maren had tested it exactly once, years ago, when Tam was born blue-lunged and failing, and she had traded three drops of her own blood and a favor unnamed for his first breath. The Clerk—it had a voice like dry paper turning pages—had said: *"A favor unnamed shall be named when the debt comes due."*

She had spent eleven years hoping the debt was lost. Debts were not lost. That was rather the point of the court.

So now, with the purges eating the guilds one by one and the registry lists including, as of Tuesday, the name *Lockridge*, she wrote her request in the old formal script:

*I have two children. The law will take me within the month. I ask that they survive me. Name your price.*

She sealed it in ash—burned paper pressed into a sigil of submission—and buried it beneath the hearthstone at midnight, whispering the address her own grandmother had taught her, back when grandmothers could still teach such things.

Three days later, the answer came up through the floorboards like cold air. It arrived not as paper but as *certainty*: a set of terms unfolding in her mind, patient, precise, and terrible in its reasonableness.

*One soul, pledged, held in trust until majority. The debtor may choose which.*

She sat down on the kitchen floor and laughed until it turned into something else, because of course. Of course. The court didn't want coin or blood or worship—not anymore, not starved and caged as it was. It wanted what it had always wanted: collateral. Continuity. A promise with teeth.

She thought of Tam, fourteen, who asked questions that went through floors, who would fight, who would break himself trying to escape any cage.

She thought of Astrid, sixteen, who had learned invisibility so thoroughly it had become her nature, who could survive anything if she believed she was protecting someone.

Forgive me, she thought, to whichever of you reads this someday. Forgive me for choosing, and forgive me for choosing you.

Then she went upstairs and watched her daughter sleep, and memorized her face, because she understood with complete clarity that she was spending the rest of her life buying this moment.

* * *

### Astrid

The night the soldiers came for the charm-sellers' row, it was raining, and rain made everything easier and harder—easier to move unheard, harder to pretend you weren't crying.

Astrid stood in the doorway of the burning house two streets over, watching the shrine-tokens melt in the window, and felt her mother's hand close on her shoulder.

"Not here," Maren murmured. "Not watching. Inside."

Inside, Tam was packing with the frantic efficiency of the terrified—wrapping salt-bowls in his spare shirt as though salt were what mattered, and maybe to him it was. Astrid grabbed his wrist.

"Tamsin. Look at me. Wherever Mum takes us tonight, whatever happens—we don't ask questions. Not there. Promise me."

He blinked. "What do you mean, 'there'? Where's there?"

"I don't know," Astrid admitted. "But I know Mum, and I know that face she's making, and I know we're not going anywhere ordinary."

Their mother came in behind them, shaking rain from her shawl, and for a moment she simply stood there looking at her children—her impossible, questioning boy and her careful, watchful girl—and something in her expression was so raw that Astrid's stomach dropped.

"Sit down," Maren said. "Both of you. There are things you need to hear, and I need to say them before my courage goes soft, which it is threatening to do."

She told them about the mountain court. She told them about the bargain she'd struck when Tam lay dying, and the second bargain she'd struck three days ago. She told them the terms without softening them, because she was Maren Lockridge, and she had never once lied to her children about anything worse than the existence of the sweetshop fairy.

"The court holds Tam's soul in trust," she finished. "In exchange, you both live. You both get to grow old. When he reaches his majority—" her voice caught, steadied, "—they'll come for him quietly. No wagon, no fire. He'll simply... belong to them. And Astrid, listen to me, because this is the part that matters: the debt is *mine*. My signature, my sin. But contracts pass down. Blood calls to blood. One day you'll be offered a way to change these terms, and I need you to remember that whatever you pay—I already paid more."

There was a long silence. Rain ticked against the shutters.

Tam spoke first, because Tam always spoke first. "You sold *my soul*?" His voice cracked down the middle. "Without asking?"

"Yes," their mother said simply. "Would you have said yes?"

"No! Obviously no!"

"No," Maren agreed. "You'd have said no and died of blue-lung at two years old, and I'd have followed you inside a year, and your sister would have been alone in a city that burns people like us. So I didn't ask. That is what love looks like when it's desperate. It looks unforgivable." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, as though fixing a smudge. "You may hate me. That's allowed. Hate me *alive*."

Astrid hadn't spoken. She was staring at her mother's hands—those clever, tireless hands that had taught her every knot she knew—and thinking about ropes.

A knot, her mother had always said, is just a question your hands ask the world. *That things hold together.*

Every knot slips eventually. Every charm fades. But a contract sealed in bone—that was a knot tied on the far side of the world, in a hall beneath a mountain, and it would hold for generations unless someone untied it from the inside.

"Mum," Astrid said quietly. "Show me the seal. The ash-seal. Show me exactly how you made it."

Maren looked at her daughter—at sixteen years old and already untying knots in her head—and something moved behind her eyes, grief and pride fighting to a draw.

"No," she said. "Absolutely not. You will forget this entire conversation, you will survive, and you will live a small, safe, invisible life."

"Mum—"

"*No.*"

But Astrid had her mother's memory, and her mother's stubbornness, and ten years later—in a rented room above a chandlery, with waking sigils burning on her skin and a contract's shape haunting her sleep—she would remember every word of that rainy night, every term of that terrible bargain, and the exact motion of her mother's hands folding the parchment into ash.

She would remember, and she would act. But first, she would need help—the kind that came from people who knew where the bones were buried.

Which meant, tomorrow, she was going to have to walk into the Low Quarter and find the most dangerous drunk in Verrin: Sera Duvane, the inquisitor who quit.

### Mother

Later—much later, in the last year, when the sickness was already hollowing her—Maren Lockridge sat by her daughter's bed and watched her sleep, and permitted herself one honest hour.

Astrid had turned out better than she deserved. Careful, kind, funny in her dry way. A good charm-seller, a better sister. And carrying, folded small behind her ribs, the question Maren had tried so hard never to plant: *how do I undo this?*

"I'm sorry," she whispered to the sleeping room. "I'm sorry, and thank you, and look after each other. That was always the whole spell. Everything else was knots."

On the table beside her lay a sealed letter marked *For Astrid, when the sigils wake.* She had written it a year ago, in the formal script, and hidden it in the bottom of the button box, because her daughter would absolutely go through the button box eventually, and because some truths could only be survived if you found them yourself.

Outside, dawn came up gray over Verrin, and somewhere beneath the mountain, in a hall lined with bone ledgers, a page bearing the name *Tamsin Lockridge* waited in the dark—patient as debt, quiet as ash—for the daughter who was coming to read it.
