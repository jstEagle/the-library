## 10. The Guild's Last Vote

The guildhall smelled of tallow and old rain, the way it had smelled for as long as Astrid could remember—which was, she reflected, exactly the problem with guildhalls. They kept everything. The water stains on the ceiling from the flood of her ninth year. The scorch mark where Master Ovelin had set his own beard alight demonstrating a fire-ward in the days when demonstrations were still legal. The bench by the window that creaked in a particular minor key whenever anyone sat on it, so that every meeting of the Charmwrights' Benevolent Association of Verrin began with a small, involuntary chorus of complaint.

She had walked past this door ten thousand times. Tonight was the first time she had ever been afraid of it.

Inside, eleven people sat around the long table beneath the guild's motto, *Small Magics, Honest Work*, carved into wood so old the letters had gone soft as scars. Eleven. There had been thirty when Astrid joined. There had been sixty when her mother did.

Halvard sat at the head, hands folded, silver-bearded, patient as a stone that had decided to be patient on purpose. To his left, Mistress Corva, who made wedding charms and had buried three husbands and outlived the law that said her craft was crime. To his right, old Fenwick, half-asleep as always, his hearing charm—a thing of such exquisite delicacy that even the inquisitors had once admired it before confiscating it—long since replaced by a brass horn he refused to admit he needed.

And at the far end, squeezed together like chicks on a ledge: the young ones. Petra, nineteen, all elbows and indignation. Joss, twenty-two, who dyed his hair with walnut ink to look older and fooled no one. Little Maren, seventeen, who still flinched at raised voices and had the steadiest hands in the guild.

They had come because Astrid had sent word through the usual channels—*urgent, all hands, no excuses*—and in ten years she had never once used those words.

"Astrid," Halvard said, and his voice was gentle in the way that made her stomach drop, because gentle Halvard meant serious Halvard. "You look like a woman who has been somewhere she shouldn't."

"I've been under the mountain."

The room went very quiet. Even Fenwick woke up.

* * *

She told them all of it.

She stood at the head of the table—she couldn't sit; her legs wouldn't agree to it—and she told them about the sigils waking, one by one, like lamps being lit in a house everyone thought empty. She told them about the contract sealed in ash, her mother's handwriting steady and desperate at once: *the soul of Tam Lockridge, pledged to the court beneath the mountain, in payment for the lives of his kin.* She told them about the Clerk with its face like an unwritten page, and the bone ledgers, and the stair of teeth, and the wretched chained thing in the deep galleries that had begged her for its name while wearing, faint and faded on its ruined skin, the same mark she'd been born with above her left hip.

"The gods are awake," she said. "Starving, and awake, and answering prayers wrong. And my mother helped build the cage they're in. And my brother is a line item in their accounting." Her voice cracked on *brother*, and she let it. "I'm going back down. I'm going to steal Tam's page out of their ledger. I'm asking you to help me."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and texture, that presses on the ears.

It was broken, as it often was, by Fenwick, who cupped his hand behind his brass horn and said, "Did she say ledger or *porridge*?"

"Ledger, you old goat," said Corva.

"Ah." Fenwick nodded gravely. "Worse, then."

A few people laughed—the thin, startled laughter of people who have been holding their breath—and Astrid felt something in her chest loosen a single notch. That was the guild's oldest magic, really. Not the knots and wards. This. Making room for fear by making room for each other.

Then Halvard spoke, and the loosening stopped.

"No," he said.

Not angry. Just final, the way a door is final.

"Halvard—" Astrid began.

"Sit down, child, and let an old man count corpses for you." He rose, slowly, one hand on the table. "Thirty years ago this guild numbered sixty souls. Do you know what we are now? Eleven. Eleven, hiding in a cellar under a fishmonger's shop, selling luck-knots to grandmothers and calling ourselves a *Benevolent Association* because the word *guild* gets people burned." He tapped the table on each name. "Ovelin. Taken in the second purge. Sera and Bram Wexley, taken together. Young Dannel, who was sixteen. I could go on. I have gone on, every night, for thirty years, and I will tell you what I have learned: the mountain does not lose. The mountain *waits*. It waited out your mother. It will wait out you. And if you go down those stairs with our help—if you go down there carrying our names—it will not take only you. It will take the page, and then it will take the thief, and then it will follow the thread back up to this table and take the rest."

"So we just stay hidden?" Petra was on her feet, which for Petra was her natural state. "Forever? Until there's two of us? One? We're already dead, Halvard, we're just doing it slowly!"

"We are *alive* slowly," Halvard said, "which is the only speed there is."

"They answered a prayer last week," Petra pressed on, turning to the room. "Down at the Tannery Row shrine. A woman prayed for her husband's cough to stop. You know what happened? The cough stopped. Because the god reached into his chest and took his lungs' whole argument with the world, and now he breathes like a bellows with a hole in it, and the healers say he'll be dead by spring. That's what starving gods do. That's what's coming for all of us. And you want to talk about *waiting*?"

"I want to talk about arithmetic," Halvard said quietly. "One brother against eleven lives. I am sorry, Astrid. I am sorry with everything in me. But grief makes terrible bookkeepers, and I have been keeping these books a long time."

The words landed like stones in still water. Astrid watched ripples move across faces she loved. Corva looking down at her knotted hands. Fenwick studying the ceiling stains. Maren, white as salt, staring at nothing.

Her own face, she knew, gave away nothing. Ten years of practice. But inside, some small cruel voice agreed with him. *One brother against eleven lives.* The arithmetic was sound. The arithmetic had always been sound. Her mother had done the same sum, all those years ago, and written her answer in ash.

"That's not the sum," Astrid heard herself say.

Halvard looked at her.

"You think the choice is Tam or us. It isn't. The choice is whether we do this while we still have hands to do it with, or whether we wait until the gods finish gutting the city and the court comes up the stairs to collect *everyone* whose names are in those ledgers." She pulled the folded scrap of paper from her coat—Duvane's copy, the transcription she'd risked her neck for—and laid it flat on the table. "Because we're in here too, Halvard. All of us. Every charm this guild ever sold during the purges was bought with borrowed time, and the mountain keeps accounts the way winter keeps debts. My mother didn't sell Tam's soul to save *four* people. She sold it to save forty. The whole Low Quarter network. Everyone who ever carried a knot of ours through a checkpoint. The contract runs nine pages. I read four before I ran."

She hadn't planned to say that last part. It came out anyway, and she saw it strike home—saw Corva's hands go still, saw Maren's eyes go wide, saw even Fenwick sit up straight.

"Nine pages," Corva whispered. "Who else signed?"

"Widow Anselm. The Ferryman twins. Old Mother Quill, may she rest, though apparently her signature doesn't." Astrid's throat was tight. "My mother spent this whole city to buy us time, and the bill is coming due, and I am the only person alive who knows where the receipt is."

* * *

They argued for three hours.

Petra argued first and loudest: they owed the dead, they owed the city, and besides, she'd rather burn doing something than smolder doing nothing. Joss backed her, in his careful way—not with passion but with lists, actual lists, of prayers answered wrong across Verrin in the past month, gathered from his customers, each one a small catastrophe wearing the shape of mercy. A fisherman's rivals drowned. A baker's bread that never ran out but also never fed anyone. A child's wish for her father to *never leave again*, granted by a fever that kept him abed and would keep him there.

Maren didn't argue at all. She sat with her hands flat on the table and listened the way she did everything—completely—and when Astrid finally asked her, gently, what she thought, Maren said, "I think my grandmother's name is on page six," and that ended more arguments than any speech could have.

Corva argued both sides, which was her nature. She argued for caution with the weariness of a woman who had survived three purges by never being interesting. Then she argued for action with the fury of a woman who had survived three purges by watching everyone else be taken instead. In the end she said only, "I'm too old to run again. Whatever we decide, decide it tonight, because I won't spend another decade looking over my shoulder waiting for this to find us."

Fenwick, when finally cornered, removed his brass horn entirely, which was his version of standing at attention.

"I can't hear half of what's been said," he announced, "and I understand it perfectly. When I was young, the gods were everywhere and mostly harmless, like cats. Then the law came and said no cats, and we all pretended the mice weren't a problem. Well." He gestured vaguely at the world. "Mice."

Only Halvard held out.

He held out beautifully. He cited precedent, and history, and the specific case of the Saltmarsh cell, wiped out to a soul in the third purge for a raid half as bold as the one Astrid proposed. He cited the odds, which were bad, and the consequences, which were worse. He cited, at last, his own heart, which surprised everyone, including perhaps himself.

"I trained half of you," he said, and his voice, for the first time all night, was not stone. "I taught your mothers' generation to tie a ward-knot. I have buried more of this family than I have taught, and I swore—I *swore*, over Ovelin's empty grave—that I would never again be the man who sent guild children down into the dark." He looked at Astrid, and his eyes were wet and furious. "You are asking me to break that oath."

"No," Astrid said softly. "I'm asking you to keep a different one. The one carved over your head."

Everyone looked up. *Small Magics, Honest Work.*

"Nobody's asking you to raise an army, Halvard. I'm asking for a distraction, a forged prayer, and a door held open for ninety seconds. That's small magic. That's the smallest magic there is." She swallowed. "Honest work is telling you the truth: I can't do this alone. I tried alone. Alone is how you end up negotiating with a faceless clerk who offers you a form to sign and means it kindly, which is somehow worse. I need my guild."

The silence came back, but it was a different silence now. Warmer. Frightened still—but frightened *together*, which changed its whole chemistry.

Halvard looked around the table. At Petra, vibrating. At Joss, calm behind his lists. At Maren, whose grandmother's name was on page six. At Corva, who met his eye and gave the smallest nod, the nod of a woman who had already decided and was merely informing him. At Fenwick, who put his horn back on, which was his version of a battle cry.

"Vote," Halvard said at last, heavily, like a man setting down something he'd carried a long way. "All in favor of aiding Astrid Lockridge in the theft of the debt-ledger page bearing the contract of Tam Lockridge—all in favor, say aye."

"Aye," said Petra, instantly, loudly.

"Aye," said Joss.

"Aye," said Maren, barely a whisper, but it carried.

"Aye," said Corva. "God forgive us and mind the knots."

"Aye," said Fenwick. "Whatever it was."

Halvard closed his eyes. Astrid watched him stand at the edge of his own oath for a long moment, toes over the drop.

"Aye," he said.

It cracked something open in the room. Not joy—no one was fool enough for joy—but something adjacent to it, something with hope's shape and fear's weight. Hands found hands across the table. Petra whooped and immediately apologized. Corva was already pulling thread from her sleeve, because Corva processed all emotion through knotwork, and by the time the candles guttered low she had the beginnings of a ward-pattern sketched in red silk that made Astrid's waking sigils hum against her skin like struck bells.

"It'll be close," Halvard said quietly, beside her, while the others bent over the maps. His voice was rough. "The vote. Four to two, with abstentions. If Maren's grandmother hadn't been on that page..." He didn't finish.

"But she is," Astrid said.

"She is." He sighed, long and slow. "For what it's worth, child—I hope you're right about the arithmetic. And if you're wrong, I'll haunt you personally. I have contacts among the dead."

Despite everything, Astrid laughed. It came out wet, but it came out.

* * *

Later—much later, when the maps were marked and the watches assigned and the young ones sent home to sleep, or pretend to—Astrid stood alone in the doorway of the guildhall, looking back at the motto on the wall.

Behind her, in the dark of the stairwell, Halvard paused. "Go home, Astrid. Sleep. Tomorrow we make history or we make martyrs, and either way it goes better rested."

She nodded. But she stayed a moment longer, one hand flat against the doorframe, feeling the faint pulse of her waking sigils answer the guild's old ward-marks in the wood—two heartbeats finding each other after years apart.

That was when she saw it: a smear of soot on the threshold stone, fresh, deliberate, drawn in a line she recognized from Duvane's stolen maps.

The mark of the court's enforcers.

Not a warning. A receipt.

They knew she'd been down. They knew where she slept. And by the position of the mark—still sharp-edged, still smelling of the burning—they had left it within the hour.

Somewhere above the Low Quarter, faint through the ceiling beams, a church bell began to ring out of turn. Then a second bell. Then shouting, and the smell of smoke that was not tallow and not rain.

Halvard appeared at her shoulder, followed the line of her gaze down to the soot, and went very still.

"Astrid," he said. "How fast can you wake the others?"
