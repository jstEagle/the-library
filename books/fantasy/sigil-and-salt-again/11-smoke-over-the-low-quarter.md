## 11. Smoke Over the Low Quarter

Sera Duvane had learned to measure mornings by the quality of the watered wine at the Gull's Rest, and by that measure this one was a catastrophe before she'd even finished her first cup.

The wine was fine. It was everything else.

She heard the bells first—the deep ones from the Citadel, not the harbor bells that meant weather or fish prices. The deep bells meant the Order of the Quiet Flame was moving in strength. She hadn't heard them since the night she'd burned eleven years of records and walked out of the Citadel with ash still under her fingernails.

Then came the light. Orange, wrong for dawn, climbing the sky over the Low Quarter like a fever breaking.

"Fire," said Oskar the barkeep, without looking up from his polishing. He was a large, deliberate man who treated apocalypse and spilled ale with the same unhurried competence. "Or something wearing fire's coat."

"Something wearing fire's coat," Duvane agreed, and reached for her boots.

"You're going out there." It wasn't a question. Oskar had stopped asking questions about her years ago; he simply stated facts now, the way a man reads off a ledger. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look you get when you pretend you don't have a look."

Duvane pulled on her coat—her old coat, the one with the lining she'd never gotten around to replacing after she'd cut out the Order's insignia—and went to see what the mountain had sent.

* * *

The Low Quarter burned in pieces, which was somehow worse than if it had burned whole. The enforcers were precise. That was the horror of it. They weren't razing the district; they were *editing* it. A chandler's shop gone to cinders while the bakery beside it stood untouched, its windows fogged with morning bread-smell. A tenement hollowed out around a single door on the third floor, left standing like a tooth in a rotted jaw.

Duvane knew what they were doing because she had once been trained to do it. Punishment as cartography. The court beneath the mountain didn't rage—it *annotated*. Every burn marked a debt unpaid, an escape unaccounted for, a lesson delivered in the only language the bone ledgers respected.

And every burn circled inward, toward the charm-seller's garret above the dyer's shop on Tanner's Row.

Astrid Lockridge wasn't home. Duvane had checked twice already, weaving through the crowds of the displaced with her collar up, and the little room above the dye vats stood empty, its window open, its sill dusted with salt in patterns Duvane pretended not to recognize. Wards. The girl had warded her own doorway against the court's servants, and the wards had held—one scorch mark on the threshold where something had tried the latch and thought better of it.

Clever girl. Dead clever, if she didn't stop being clever long enough to be smart.

"You."

The voice came from behind, and it was a voice Duvane knew the way you know a scar. She turned.

Inquisitor-Captain Maren Voss stood in the smoke like she'd been carved out of it: gray uniform, gray eyes, the brand of rank gleaming at her throat. Ten years ago they had shared a table in the archives, two young women who believed the work mattered. Then Duvane had read too deeply into the old records and Voss had read exactly as deep as she was told to.

"Duvane." Voss said the name like a diagnosis. "You look terrible."

"I drink professionally. It shows initiative."

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "The Order has reviewed your file. In light of recent events, the Grand Inquisitor is prepared to offer full reinstatement. Rank restored. Records sealed. Your—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—indiscretion of eleven years ago reclassified as an authorized deep-cover operation."

Duvane laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her like smoke from a chimney, gray and acrid. "Authorized. Eleven years of drinking cheap wine and losing arguments with fishermen, all sanctioned from on high. The Order truly does think of everything."

"The Order thinks of its own." Voss stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and here it was—the real offer, the hook under the bait. "Sera. You know what's coming. The mountain is waking up. Whatever your sympathies, whatever guilt you're carrying, you cannot stand between the court and what it wants. But you can stand inside the wall when it comes down. Bring in the Lockridge woman. Hand over whatever she's found. And come home."

*Home.* The word did its work. Duvane felt it do its work, and hated herself a little for the warmth of it—the imagined weight of the archive key again, the smell of lamp oil and old paper, colleagues who said her name without the flat caution people used in the Low Quarter. Belonging was a drug, and she'd been clean for eleven years, and here was Voss holding out the pipe.

"You've seen the burns," Duvane said quietly. "The chandler. The tenement. How many dead?"

"Fourteen confirmed. Collateral."

"They weren't collateral. They were punctuation." She looked past Voss, at the smoke. "I used to write those sentences, Mare. I'm done writing them."

Voss studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw made her sigh—not angry, almost sad. "You have until the moon turns. After that, harboring a debtor of the court makes you one yourself." She pressed a folded paper into Duvane's hand—a writ, official seal, her own name written in the reinstatement clause like a promise. "It stays open. Doors usually do, for people who used to hold keys."

Then she walked back into the smoke, gray into gray, and Duvane stood alone in the street with the paper in her hand and fourteen deaths hanging in the air between them.

She should have thrown the writ away. She told herself she would throw it away.

She put it in her pocket instead, and hated how carefully she folded it first.

* * *

She found Astrid at the Gull's Rest, of all places, sitting in the back booth with her hood up and a bowl of Oskar's stew untouched in front of her. She looked like someone who had been running since before dawn and had run out of city.

"They hit Tanner's Row," Astrid said, before Duvane even sat down. Her voice was scraped raw. "Not my building. Around it. The dyer's family got out, but the Ferrows next door—" She stopped. Started again. "Mrs. Ferrow bought a luck-knot from me three weeks ago. Three coppers. She wanted rain for her garden."

"And?"

"And she got it." Astrid's hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the world might lift off otherwise. "It hasn't stopped raining on that garden since. Not on the house. Not on the street. Just the garden, Duvane. A column of rain, day and night, until the roots drowned and the walls went soft and the whole thing came down this morning in the fire-panic, everyone pushing, nobody watching where—" She closed her eyes. "Three coppers. I took three coppers and it killed her."

"That wasn't you."

"It was my knot. My sigil. My circuit." She opened her eyes, and Duvane saw the thing she'd been afraid of seeing: not guilt, exactly. Guilt could be carried. This was the beginning of understanding, and understanding was heavier. "The gods are awake and they're answering through anything I've touched. I'm not selling charms anymore. I'm leaving doors open."

Duvane sat down across from her and, after a moment's consideration, moved the stew closer to her. "Eat. Guilt digests worse on an empty stomach; I have extensive professional experience."

Astrid almost smiled. Almost. "Voss found you."

"She found me." Duvane turned her cup, watched the dregs swirl. Here was the moment, then. The honest thing or the easy thing, and both of them sitting right there in her pocket. "Reinstatement. Full pardon. All I have to do is deliver you."

Silence. Oskar clinked bottles somewhere far away. A gull screamed outside with the particular outrage of gulls everywhere.

"And?" Astrid said.

"And I told her I was done writing their sentences." Duvane met her eyes. "But I won't lie to you, Lockridge, because lying to you would make me the kind of person who lies to friends, and I've only recently acquired a friend and I'd like to keep her in good condition. Part of me wanted to say yes. Not most of me. But some of me. A piece about yea big." She held up thumb and finger, a small measured gap. "Belonging does that. You spend enough years outside the wall, you forget the wall is also a cage. You just remember it keeps the rain off."

Astrid looked at her for a long time. Then she pushed the stew aside, leaned forward, and said, "Then we'd better make sure there's no wall left to belong to. Either wall. The Citadel's or the mountain's." Her voice dropped. "I found Tam's contract. I know the terms now. And I know what I have to trade to break them, and I need—" She faltered, the first crack in her resolve. "I need people who know the way in. You know the court's law. You know its loopholes. You told me once you'd burned them rather than serve them. Burn them with me instead."

Duvane took out the writ. Unfolded it. Looked at her own name in the reinstatement clause—at the life being offered, warm and lit and safe behind thick stone.

Then she held it over the candle flame.

The paper caught quickly, the way official documents always do, as if they're eager for it. She let it burn down to her fingertips and dropped the last corner into the stew, where it hissed and died among the barley.

"Well," said Oskar, materializing with a cloth and infinite weariness. "That's coming out of your tab."

"Put it on the Order's account," Duvane said. "They owe me eleven years of back pay."

Astrid laughed—a real laugh, cracked and startled out of her, the first sound in hours that wasn't smoke or grief. And Duvane felt the strangest thing settle in her chest, lighter than belonging and harder to name: the sense that she had just chosen the losing side of a war, and that for the first time in eleven years, she was on the right one anyway.

Outside, the deep bells rang again. Closer now. And beneath their bronze voices, faint but unmistakable, came a new sound—stone grinding against stone, somewhere beneath the city, like a great door being forced open from the other side.

Astrid went pale. "That's not the enforcers."

"No," Duvane said, standing, hand going to the knife she'd sworn she'd retired. "That's the mountain. And I think, Lockridge, it's tired of sending letters."
