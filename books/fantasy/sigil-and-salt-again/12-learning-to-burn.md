## 12. Learning to Burn

The cellar under Halvard's shop had once stored pickled eels, and it still smelled like it, which Astrid decided was as good a place as any to learn how to unmake herself.

"Again," said the god.

It sat on an overturned barrel in the corner, wrapped in one of Halvard's old coats, looking like nothing so much as a vagrant who had wandered in from a very long walk. That was the trick of it, she was learning. The nameless god could look like almost anything, because it had no fixed shape of its own—only the shapes other people expected. Right now it wore the face of a tired fishwife, because that was the last face it had borrowed before they'd come down out of the mountain, and it hadn't yet thought to change.

"You say 'again' like that means something," Astrid said. Her hands were shaking. She flexed her fingers and watched the sigils along her forearms glow faintly through the skin, like lanterns behind cloth. "I've done it six times."

"You've done it four times. Twice you did something else entirely and screamed."

"That's still practice."

The fishwife-face smiled, which was deeply unsettling. "In my day—and I want you to appreciate that I have no idea how long my day was—we called this *kindling*. You do not light a fire by arguing with the wood."

"No," Astrid said, "you light it by burning the wood. Which is rather my point."

She looked down at the little workbench Halvard had cleared for her: a stub of tallow candle, a bowl of salt water, three luck-knots in various stages of unraveling. Ordinary things. Charm-seller's stock. Ten years ago she'd learned to make these small protections so well that no inquisitor would ever notice them—a knot here, a ward there, magic shrunk down to the size of a kindness. Now she was trying to do the opposite. She was trying to make the small things enormous.

Because somewhere beneath the mountain, in a cell carved out of the dark, her brother was being spent installment by installment, and the only power she had found that might reach him belonged to a god with no name and no memory and, apparently, no sense of pacing.

"Breathe," the god said.

"I am breathing."

"You are breathing the way a drowning woman breathes." It rose from the barrel, and for a moment its outline flickered—the fishwife, then something vast and winged, then a pillar of eyes, then the fishwife again, embarrassed. "Astrid. The power does not come *from* your sigils. Your sigils are doors. The power comes from me. And every time you open a door, some part of what you are walks through it and doesn't come back. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"Yes."

"Do you *believe* it?"

She picked up the candle stub. "My mother used to say that believing is just wanting with better posture. Light the candle, Astrid. Stop fussing."

The god went very still. "That's not what I sound like."

"It's what you sound like when you're lecturing me." She struck the flint, touched flame to wick, and set the candle in the center of the chalked circle. "Now. Watch."

* * *

Here is what Astrid knew about her mother, Maerin Lockridge, after ten years of grief:

She had smelled of salt and tallow. She had hummed off-key when she worked, always the same tuneless thing, a song with no words that Astrid had never been able to find again in any tavern or temple. She had a scar on her left thumb from a carving knife, earned the winter Tam was born. She had laughed with her whole chest, so that strangers in the market would turn around to see what was so wonderful. She had been afraid, all those last years, of something she never named, and she had gone gray early, and she had died in her sleep with her hands folded like a woman who had finally finished a very long piece of mending.

These were the facts. Astrid kept them the way she kept her charms: sorted, protected, taken out only when needed.

The first spell cost her the smell.

She didn't notice at first. The working itself was simple—or the god insisted it was simple, which turned out to be a different thing. Open the door. Let the nameless thing pour through. Shape it with intention, the way you'd shape poured wax. The candle flame stood up straight and turned white, white as bone, white as the ash-seal on her mother's contract, and the cellar filled with a warmth like standing in a doorway in summer, and for three full seconds Astrid felt *enormous*, felt like a window instead of a woman—

And then it closed, and she was on her knees on the cold flagstones, and the candle was burned to a puddle, and everything seemed fine until she reached instinctively for the memory of her mother's smell—that salt-and-tallow comfort she'd carried through every bad night of the last decade—and found a hole where it should have been. Not a painful hole. A tidy one. The way a tooth feels after it's gone: the tongue keeps going back to check.

"What did I lose?" she asked. Her voice came out flat.

The god crouched beside her. It had stopped wearing the fishwife's face; now it wore no face at all, which was somehow kinder. "Something small. A scent, I think. I can't choose for you. The fire takes what's nearest to the door."

"The nearest to the door," Astrid repeated. "Meaning what I was thinking about while I cast."

"Meaning what you were holding onto." The god's voice gentled. "You were thinking of her. So the fire ate her. It will always eat what you love best, because that is what stands closest when you open yourself. This is why gods go mad, little charm-seller. We burn ourselves out of ourselves, century by century, until there is nothing left but the burning." It paused. "I would know. I think. There's a shape in me where knowing used to be."

Astrid sat back against the eel-smelling wall and laughed, a short ugly laugh with no humor in it. "So the plan is: I burn my mother away piece by piece to save my brother. And when I get to him, I won't remember why he mattered."

"Yes," said the god, without flinching. "Unless we're cleverer than that."

"Are we cleverer than that?"

"I genuinely don't know. I've lost the part of me that kept track."

* * *

They got cleverer. Slowly, badly, with a great deal of arguing.

The trick, Astrid discovered, was misdirection—not of the god, but of herself. If she filled her mind, in the moment of casting, with something disposable, the fire took the disposable thing. She practiced with lists. Market prices. The names of streets in the Tanners' Quarter. The rules of a dice game Duvane had taught her, which she recited aloud while the white flame roared up her arms and the sigils blazed like festival lanterns.

"*Two ones is snake-eyes, pays triple, unless the caster's left hand is on the table, in which case—*" The candle became a torch. The torch became a column. "*—in which case the house keeps the pot and everyone pretends that's fair.*"

The flame guttered out. Astrid swayed, caught herself on the bench, and took inventory. Still had her mother's laugh. Still had the scar on the thumb. Lost... she frowned... the dice game. Entirely. Duvane would be insufferable.

"You're getting good at this," the god said, and there was something in its voice that might have been pride and might have been grief. With no memories of its own, Astrid suspected, it had started borrowing hers—which meant every lesson cost it too, in a way. It remembered her mother now better than she did. Sometimes it hummed the tuneless song while she worked, and she never told it to stop, because it was the only place the song still existed.

"There's a problem," she said, on the ninth day.

"There are several problems. Pick one."

"When I burn a memory, it doesn't come back. But the *power*—" She held up her hand. The sigils had stopped fading between workings now. They lived under her skin like veins of dawn, faintly glowing even at rest, and when she caught her reflection in the salt water bowl, her eyes had begun to do a thing she didn't like: they reflected light that wasn't there. "The power stays. It accumulates. I'm a jar filling up with something, and I don't know what happens when the jar is full."

The god was quiet for a long time. In the quiet, Astrid heard the mountain above them settle, a deep stone groan, like a giant turning over in bed. Nine days since the raid on the Low Quarter. Nine days since Duvane had burned her oath and taken the brand across both palms, and the court's enforcers had withdrawn to lick their wounds and count their losses. Everyone in Verrin knew the quiet wouldn't hold. Quiet, in Astrid's experience, was just the sound a storm makes while it decides.

"When the jar is full," the god said at last, "you stop being a jar."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest, which is the poor man's reassurance." It moved closer, and its non-face softened into something almost human. "Listen to me, Astrid Lockridge. I have watched a thousand mortals touch what you're touching, and here is what I know: the ones who try to hold it all become storms. The ones who refuse it become food. But the ones who *spend* it—who pour it out as fast as it comes in, on purpose, toward something—they stay people. Bent people. Scarred people. But people."

"So I keep casting."

"So you keep casting. And you keep choosing what it costs. That's the whole art. Not avoiding the fire. Aiming it."

* * *

On the eleventh day, Duvane came down the cellar stairs with a bottle in one hand and a ledger page wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, and stopped dead when she saw Astrid.

"What?" Astrid said.

"You're glowing."

"I'm practicing."

"You're glowing, Lockridge. You look like a saint in a chapel window, if saints drank tea and had flour on their sleeves." Duvane set the bottle on the bench, unwrapped the oilcloth, and spread the page flat: a rubbing she'd taken of the bone archive's outer index, smuggled out past two checkpoints and a very bribable clerk. Names, dates, debt-codes. Somewhere in those columns of scratched characters was the true location of Tam's contract—the Clerk had let them steal a decoy, and Duvane had spent eleven furious days finding out where the real page slept. "Also, you're doing it wrong. Whatever it is. Your third sigil is inverted."

Astrid looked down. The third sigil—the spiral at her wrist—was indeed burning backwards, coiling the wrong way, drinking light instead of shedding it. "Since when do ex-inquisitors read god-marks?"

"Since I burned forty years of my order's records and read all of them first." Duvane pulled up a stool, wincing as her branded palms scraped the wood. "Sit. Show me the whole sequence. And then you're going to tell me exactly what each working has cost you, because I've been counting the gaps in your conversation, and Astrid—you've stopped mentioning your mother entirely."

The cellar went very still. Even the god, humming its borrowed song in the corner, fell silent.

"I mention her," Astrid said.

"You mention the contract. The bargain. The debt. You haven't once mentioned her *laughing*. Not since the candle working." Duvane's voice was gentle in the way that surgeons' voices are gentle. "I catalogued confessions for twenty years. I know what forgetting looks like from the outside. It looks like efficiency."

Astrid opened her mouth to argue and found, reaching for her mother's laugh, that she could remember that it had existed—that she had a fact about it, *she laughed with her whole chest*—but the sound itself was gone. Not dimmed. Gone, the way the smell was gone. A tooth-shaped hole. Two of them now.

"Oh," she said quietly.

"Yeah," said Duvane.

"Oh, *no.*"

"We'll fix it," said the god, rising, and for the first time since she'd met it, the nameless thing sounded afraid. "There's a way. When we take the contract back—when Tam's soul returns to him—the debts unwind. What the court holds, the court releases. And your memories—"

"—were never the court's," Astrid finished. The cold arrived slowly, the way cold arrives in deep water. "They were mine. I spent them myself. Nobody owes them back to me." She looked at her glowing hands, at the beautiful, accumulating light, and understood at last what she was doing: buying her brother's life with her mother, coin by coin, and the shop did not give refunds.

Duvane put her bandaged hand over Astrid's wrist, careful of the sigils. "Then we make the remaining coins count. One job. One clean theft. We go in, we take the true page, we get out before you have to burn anything else." She tapped the rubbing. "And I know exactly which shelf it sleeps on."

Above them, the mountain groaned again—longer this time, less like settling and more like something shifting its weight toward the stairs.

The god began, softly, to hum the tuneless song, keeping it safe the only way it could.

Astrid wiped the flour from her sleeves, took up the chalk, and drew the circle for one more practice—one more coin, aimed true.
