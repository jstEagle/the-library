## 13. The Ledger Heist

The bone archive smelled the way old churches smell, if old churches were built inside a mouth.

That was Halvard's observation, delivered in a whisper as the six of them crouched in the smugglers' stair's final landing, and Astrid had to admit it was accurate. The walls of the stair had been teeth once—carved fangs worn smooth by centuries of climbing feet—but here at the bottom they gave way to ribs. Great pale arches curved overhead, and between them stretched shelves, and on the shelves lay bones. Not skeletons. *Records.* Femurs inscribed with names. Ribs coiled with terms of payment. Skulls with contracts curled inside them like letters in envelopes, waiting for someone to break the seal.

"Everyone remember their parts?" Duvane murmured. She was dressed in her old order's grays, laundered and pressed until they looked almost like authority again. Her credentials hung at her throat—a lead seal stamped with the eye-and-quill of the Inquisition, lifted from an evidence locker eleven years ago along with everything else she'd burned that night.

"We remember," said Wren, the youngest guild member, who was visibly vibrating. "Distraction, forgery, extraction. I get to be distracting."

"You are naturally distracting," Halvard said. "Tonight you simply get paid for it."

Astrid checked her sigils one last time. They woke under her fingertips now, all of them, a faint warmth like banked coals. She'd spent three days learning what the nameless god could push through them, and three nights paying for it in memories: her mother's laugh, gone. The smell of the charm-shop in winter, gone. The words to a lullaby she'd sung to Tam when he was small, gone, and that one still hurt so much she'd had to sit down after.

What remained was enough. It had to be.

"Duvane," she said quietly. "Once we're in. No heroics."

"I make no promises I intend to keep," Duvane said, "but yes. Fine."

* * *

Wren went first, and Wren was magnificent.

She came up out of the stair into the archive's antechamber wearing the ragged robes of a penitent, hair wild, eyes wide, and threw herself at the feet of the two bone-clerks standing guard. Her prayer rang off the rib-vaults, pitched perfectly between desperation and madness:

"Oh powers beneath, oh patient ones, I have sinned against my sister and she is dying and I have nothing to offer but my grief, take my grief, take anything, just tell me if she'll live—"

It was a forged prayer. Wren had spent two days learning to shape it from Duvane's stolen notes—the court answered prayers, that was its whole function, its whole hunger—and the clerks straightened like hounds catching scent. One produced a stylus of black iron. The other opened a ledger-rib and began, eagerly, to record the petition.

Which meant their backs were turned. Which meant the rest of them could move.

Astrid slipped through the archway with Halvard at her shoulder and Duvane ahead, walking not creeping—inquisitors did not creep, Duvane had insisted; inquisitors walked like the building belonged to them—and the archive opened around them, aisle after aisle of shelved bone receding into dark. Somewhere a clerk's quill scratched. Somewhere else, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"The debt shelves," Duvane breathed. "Third gallery. Family contracts, purge-era. Follow me and touch *nothing*."

They touched several things anyway, because the aisles were narrow and the bones crowded close, and every brush of Astrid's sleeve against a femur made her skin crawl. The records weren't inert. She could feel them noticing her—the way she'd once felt her own sigils notice, back before everything woke. A thousand small attentions turning in the dark like flowers toward an unexpected sun.

"They know you're here," Halvard whispered.

"Everything down here knows I'm here."

"Then let us be quick about being known."

* * *

In the antechamber, Wren was running out of sins.

The problem with a forged prayer, she was discovering, was that the court took attendance. The clerk with the iron stylus had recorded her petition and now stood waiting for something to happen—for the prayer to be *answered*, which required power, which required the thing upstairs that Wren's job was to prevent anyone from noticing was missing.

So Wren prayed harder. She invented a sister. She gave the sister consumption, a fiancé, a gambling debt, and a parrot named Captain. She wept with tremendous sincerity over the parrot. And the clerks leaned in, fascinated, because in eleven years of the purges' quiet aftermath no one had come down here and prayed *this much*, and hunger, even bureaucratic hunger, has a weakness for novelty.

"Please," Wren gasped, clutching the nearer clerk's robe. "Is there a form? There must be a form. My sister would want there to be a form."

"There is a form," the clerk said, in a voice like a drawer opening.

"Could I see the form? For comfort?"

And that was how Wren got the second clerk to open the administrative alcove, and that was where she found the bell-pull, and that was when things began going wrong on schedule, though nobody had put it on the schedule.

* * *

The third gallery held the purge-era debts, and Astrid found the shelf by feel before she found it by sight—her sigils flared warm as she passed, warmer, *warmest*, and there it was: a skull the size of two fists, sealed with ash-grey wax, humming faintly with a signature she knew better than her own handwriting.

Her mother's.

"She sealed it herself," Astrid said. Her voice came out strange. "Not through a clerk. She wrote the contract. That's why the wax is grey—that's ash-sealing, the old way. The way they taught before the purges."

Duvane went very still. "If your mother wrote it herself, the original isn't in the ledger system. The ledgers hold copies. Transcriptions." She looked at the skull with new respect and new horror. "This is the true contract. This is the only contract."

"Then we take it and go," Halvard said.

Astrid reached out. Her fingers trembled—not with fear, she realized, but with the memory of her mother's hands doing this same work, sealing this same bargain, in some cellar while soldiers walked the streets above. Love and desperation. She understood it now. She hated that she understood it.

She lifted the skull from the shelf.

Every bone in the third gallery sighed.

It wasn't sound, exactly. It was attention—the whole archive's attention, thousands of records shifting their weight, and Astrid felt the change in the air the way you feel weather coming. Down the aisles, clerk-lanterns flickered up one by one, greenish and patient.

"Well," said Halvard. "I believe our distraction may be concluded."

* * *

Wren heard the sigh too, and did the bravest stupidest thing anyone had ever done in the Low Quarter, which was saying something, given the competition.

She pulled the bell-pull.

The alarm that answered was not a bell. It was a voice—a vast, dry, unhurried voice that filled the entire archive from the foundations up, a voice that had been recording debts since before Verrin had a name:

**"ANOMALY. GALLERY THREE. THE DEBT STIRS UNRECORDED."**

"That's us," Wren told the clerks apologetically, and hit the first one with the inkwell.

* * *

They ran.

The aisles blurred past. Behind them, green lanterns bloomed in sequence like a lit fuse, and the dry voice kept speaking, calm as arithmetic, announcing their position in language that made Astrid's teeth ache: *intrusion logged, intrusion logged, redemption attempt logged, penalty schedule engaged.*

Duvane ran like a woman remembering why she'd been feared once, gray robes streaming, shouting orders in official cadence—"Seal gallery five! Containment protocol! *Move!*"—and half the clerks obeyed her, because eleven years hadn't scrubbed the habit of obedience out of the institution's bones. It bought them thirty seconds. Thirty seconds is a long time when you're running for a stair full of teeth.

Halvard carried the skull. He'd taken it from Astrid without asking, tucking it into his coat like contraband brandy, and he ran with the peculiar rolling gait of a man who had spent forty years dodging inquisitors and saw no reason to stop now.

At the stair's mouth, Wren caught up to them, ink-stained and breathless and grinning like a festival.

"We did it," she panted. "We actually—we *did* it—"

Astrid felt it before any of them did: the temperature dropping, the air going formal. At the bottom of the stair, where the last tooth-arch framed the way out, a figure stood waiting.

It was tall. It wore gray. Where its face should have been there was a smooth expanse of something like vellum, and on the vellum, lines of writing moved slowly, rearranging themselves, considering.

The Clerk.

"No," Astrid said. "No—you were in the negotiation hall. We *saw* you—"

"You saw a functionary," the Clerk said. Its voice was the dry voice, the alarm voice, scaled down to conversation. "I am the office. The office is everywhere the records are." It tilted its blank head. "You have removed an item from the collection. Item 4471-C. Contract of pledge, soul of Tammas Lockridge, authored by Maren Lockridge, deceased. Please return it, and the penalty schedule will be reduced to merely ruinous."

Behind Astrid, Halvard's coat rustled. The skull was humming louder now, and the hum had a shape to it, and the shape was her brother's name.

"And if we don't?" Duvane asked. Her hand had drifted to the lead seal at her throat—an old reflex, reaching for authority that had never once protected her.

"Then I will note that the redemption attempt failed," the Clerk said, "and I will collect the penalties. The city above has grown fond of its quiet. You would be surprised," and here the moving script on its face seemed almost to arrange itself into something like regret, "how much noise a mountain can make."

Astrid stepped forward. Her sigils were burning now, all of them, the nameless god's borrowed power rising in her like floodwater, and she let it show. Let the light bleed through her sleeves. Let the Clerk see what she'd become in three days of practice and payment.

"You anticipated us," she said. "Fine. Anticipate *this*. Every record in this archive is a chain, and I have been learning locks."

Something flickered across the Clerk's face-script—she would have sworn later it was interest.

"Ah," it said softly. "The unbound variable. Yes. We wondered when you would arrive." The vellum face smoothed. "Take your copy home, thief. Read it well. You will find the terms... instructive." It stepped aside, and the stair stood open, and that frightened Astrid more than any fight could have. "But understand: your mother was cleverer than you know. What you carry is true, and complete, and *not sufficient*. The pledge was written twice. Once in ash, for the family's survival." The script rippled. "And once more, elsewhere, in something that does not burn."

"What else?" Astrid demanded. "Where?"

The Clerk considered her with its faceless face, and somewhere in the deep galleries below, chained in the dark, the nameless god stirred in its sleep and moaned a word that might once have been a warning.

"Ask the god you've been spending," the Clerk said. "It signed too."

* * *

They climbed for a long time before anyone spoke.

Above them, Verrin's dawn was coming up grey and salt-smelling, ordinary, oblivious. Halvard carried the skull wrapped in his coat like a sleeping child. Wren had stopped grinning somewhere around the fourth gallery and hadn't started again. Duvane walked with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, over the lead seal, as if holding a wound closed.

It was Astrid who finally broke the silence, and her voice was very quiet.

"It signed. My mother's contract needed a countersignature. Something old enough to make it binding under the old law." She looked at her own hands, at the sigils glowing faintly beneath the skin like veins of light. "And I've been burning through its power for three days. Spending it. Drawing on the exact thing that holds my brother's chains shut."

Nobody had an answer for that. The stair rose around them, tooth after worn tooth, and far below, growing fainter but never quite fading, they could hear the sound of the archive re-shelving itself—and beneath it, almost gentle, the Clerk's dry voice making a new entry in a ledger no one had seen:

*Redemption attempt: anticipated. The bait is taken. The debtor walks free carrying exactly what we wished her to carry.*

*Proceed to phase two.*
