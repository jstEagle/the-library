## 15. Tam, Awake

The first thing Tam knew each morning was the sound of the ledger turning.

It came through the wall of his cell like a slow tide — the whisper of bone pages shifting somewhere far below, the Clerk's pen scratching out entries in a script that hurt to listen to. He had learned, over years he could not count, to measure time by it. One page-turn per hour. Twelve hundred pages in a season. Somewhere in that vast dry rustling was his name, written and rewritten, sold and resold, until even the ink had probably forgotten what it originally meant.

He sat up on the stone shelf that served as bed and chair and everything else, and did the morning inventory.

Name: Tam Lockridge. That one still held.

Face: he pressed his palms to his cheeks. Present. Rough-cheeked. A scar over the left eyebrow from the time Astrid had dared him to jump the canal gap behind the fishmongers' row and he had, characteristically, missed.

Sister: Astrid. Brown hair she cut herself and always cut too short at the left side. Laughed through her nose when something was truly funny, which was rare, which made it worth waiting for. Sold charms at dawn. Hummed off-key when she thought no one could hear. He held onto that last detail fiercely, because three days ago — or three months; the cell kept its own calendar and it was a liar — he had reached for her face and found only a shape where it should have been, and the terror of that had lasted until he found the humming.

Memories were going. He knew this the way you know rain is coming: not by seeing it fall but by the smell of the air. The court took them in installments. There was no contract for him to sign, no pen, no moment of choice he could point to afterward and say *there, that's where I agreed*. There was only the Clerk's voice, patient as dripping water, explaining that interest had accrued, that the principal required servicing, that a portion of his recollection of the harbor would satisfy the quarter's payment. And then the harbor would be gone. Not dimmed. Gone, the way a tooth is gone — a strange smooth absence where something used to fit.

He had tried, once, to keep a record. He'd scratched notes into the wall with a chip of bone: *Astrid's laugh is through the nose. Mother smelled of tallow and rosemary. The harbor smells of tar and cinnamon.* But the walls here forgot things too. He would look at his own handwriting some mornings and feel nothing at all, as if a stranger had been carving recipes in his cell.

So instead he memorized. Every day, the same list, spoken aloud to the dark in a voice gone rusty from disuse:

"Astrid. Nose-laugh. Cuts her own hair badly. Mother, tallow and rosemary. Harbor, tar and cinnamon. I am Tam. I am Tam. I am—"

He stopped.

He was almost sure there had been a fourth item. Something about a door, or a dog, or a song. It slid away from him like a fish under a dock, and he sat with the ache of it for a while, and then he laughed — a small, cracked sound, because what else could you do — and said, "Right. Three things, then. Quality over quantity."

The ledger turned a page below him. Somewhere in Verrin above, it was morning. He liked to imagine the markets: the charm-sellers' row with their knotted cords and little jars of salt, the fishwives arguing about prices with the fervor other people reserved for religion, the gulls conducting their eternal racket overhead. He had worked the docks once. Or he had meant to work the docks once. Or someone had told him about the docks. The memory had a suspicious secondhand quality to it lately, like a coat that didn't quite fit across the shoulders.

*You're fading,* he told himself, in the tone Astrid used for delivering bad news gently, which was to say the tone of someone announcing the weather had turned murderous. *Best get used to it.*

*No,* answered another voice, younger and more stubborn, the one that had jumped the canal gap. *Best get out.*

*With what?* asked the first voice reasonably. *They've got your name under glass.*

The argument was interrupted by footsteps.

Tam went still. Footsteps in the deep galleries were rare enough to be an event, and these were wrong in a way his body understood before his mind did — too quick, too human, striking sparks off the stone instead of gliding over it the way the court's servants moved. Servants here didn't walk so much as arrive. These steps belonged to someone who had climbed a very long stair and was doing it again, badly, breathing hard.

And beneath the breathing, faintly, hopelessly off-key: humming.

Tam stood up so fast the cell tilted.

"Astrid," he said, and then louder, pressing both hands flat against the door, "Astrid! In here! The one that locks from outside, third alcove past the bone arch, you can't miss it, it's the ugly one!"

The humming stopped. Silence. Then, muffled through six feet of mountain: "...Tam?"

Her voice. Even filtered through stone it was her voice, and something in his chest cracked open like ice going out in spring. He laughed and pressed his forehead to the door and felt ridiculous and didn't care.

"Yes! Yes, it's me, unless they've started making copies, in which case I'm the one who owes money!"

"You don't owe money," her voice said, closer now, furious and shaking all at once. "I owe money. Apparently. For both of us. Since before either of us knew. Hold on. There's a lock. There's a lock made of *teeth*, Tam, whose idea—"

"Mother's, probably. Everything awful down here is." He stepped back as the lock began to grind, and used the moment to do the inventory one more time, fast, because whatever happened next he wanted to be holding all of himself when it did. *Astrid. Nose-laugh. Bad haircut. Tallow and rosemary. Tar and cinnamon. I am Tam Lockridge and my sister has come down a mountain to commit crimes for me.*

The door swung open.

She looked terrible. That was his first thought, and he loved her for it. Her hair was singed at one temple, her coat was scorched along one sleeve, there was ash on her cheekbone and what appeared to be dried soup on her collar, and her eyes were lit from inside with a faint gold light that was absolutely new and absolutely terrifying. Sigils crawled up her forearms like ivy deciding to bloom, glowing softly, and when she saw him they flared brighter, as if recognizing family.

"Hi," Tam said.

"Hi yourself." She looked at him the way you look at a house you grew up in after a long war — checking every window, every beam, cataloguing damage. "You're thin."

"You're glowing."

"It's temporary." She said it too quickly. "Mostly. We're working on it."

"We?"

Behind her, filling the corridor awkwardly, stood a woman Tam recognized without knowing how — tall, gray-streaked, one hand wrapped in fresh bandages branded with a mark he somehow knew meant *inquisitor, formerly*. And behind her, folded into the shadows like a debt nobody wanted to collect, crouched a figure that was not a person. It was roughly person-shaped the way a storm cloud is roughly sheep-shaped. Its edges kept forgetting themselves. When Tam looked directly at it, his vision slid sideways, and he caught a glimpse of something vast and starving wearing rags.

"That's a god," he said.

"He doesn't remember his name," Astrid said. "Long story. Several long stories. Do you want the short version or the version with the fisherman?"

"The fisherman version sounds like it comes with context I'm not emotionally prepared for."

"Smart. Short version: Mother sold your soul to pay for our survival during the purges. The gods are waking up because everyone starved them for a generation. My sigils woke up with them. Duvane burned her oath. The guild voted — barely — to help me steal your contract. We stole a contract. It was the wrong contract. The Clerk is very good at paperwork." She delivered all of this in one breath, the way she'd once confessed to breaking his model ship, and he watched her watch him for the reaction, braced for blame.

Tam considered it all. Then he said, "Did you eat? You have soup on your collar, but I feel like that might be old soup."

Astrid made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and crossed the cell in two strides and hugged him hard enough to crack a rib, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, because there are moments language is simply not equipped for, and this was one of them, and the god in the doorway politely studied the ceiling it couldn't see.

"I found you," she whispered into his shoulder. "Ten years of being careful and invisible and quiet, and none of it mattered, and I found you anyway."

"You found me," Tam agreed. His eyes were burning. He decided to allow it. "Now we just have the small matter of escaping an immortal bureaucracy with a god in tow."

"The guild is creating a distraction upstairs."

"What kind of distraction?"

"A forged prayer, a fire, and Halvard pretending to be a tax inspector."

"Halvard will be a *terrible* tax inspector."

"He's already been mistaken for one twice. It's going beautifully."

* *

They should have run immediately. Any sensible fugitive would have run immediately. Instead they sat on the floor of the cell — because Astrid's legs had finally given out, and because Tam had ten years of questions and a sister in front of him, and some hungers outrank survival — and talked.

Duvane kept watch in the corridor, arms crossed, radiating the specific patience of someone who had once hunted people exactly like the people she was now helping escape. The nameless god settled against the wall outside and became, briefly, almost restful, the way a sleeping bear is almost furniture.

"So the memories," Tam said. He had told her about the installments, the harbor, the missing fourth item on his list. Astrid had gone very still, the way she went still before doing something reckless. "Before you ask — yes, I've tried to keep track. No, it doesn't work. The walls forget. I forget the walls forgetting. It's very tidy. Whoever designed it should be proud."

"There's a way back," Astrid said quietly. "From what I can tell. Memories that are traded aren't destroyed, they're *held*. Filed. If we unbind the archive—"

"If." He smiled at her. "There's a lot of 'if' in your plan, Astrid."

"There's a lot of 'if' in every plan. 'If' is just 'when' with anxiety."

That was so perfectly her that he had to look away for a second, at the wall, at his own faded carvings, anywhere.

"Astrid." He made himself say it plainly. "What does it cost? Getting me back. Don't lie, you're bad at it, you blink sideways."

"I do not blink sideways."

"You're blinking sideways right now."

She was silent for a moment. Outside, the god shifted, and its silence had a listening quality. "Every spell I cast eats something," she said finally. "Not blood. Not years. *Memories.* Mostly of Mother. The power comes through me and it takes its fee on the way. I've been rationing. I've been careful. I still have most of—" She stopped. Started again. "I have enough."

"Enough for what?"

"For whatever it takes."

And there it was. Tam looked at his sister — singed, glowing, ash-cheeked, sitting in the dust of a cell built to hold souls, talking about *whatever it takes* in the voice of someone who had already spent more than she'd admitted — and felt the heartbreak arrive not as a blow but as a slow flood, cold water rising around his ankles.

Because he knew her. Better than anyone alive. And he knew that *whatever it takes* meant exactly that, and that she would pay it smiling, and that by the end there might not be enough of her left to remember why she'd paid it.

"No," he said.

"It's not a negotiation—"

"Astrid. Listen to me." He took her hands. The sigils on her wrists were warm as sun-baked stone. "I've had a lot of time down here to think, which is the one thing this place gives you in unlimited quantities. And I've done the arithmetic. You want to unbind an archive full of debts. Debts that hold this city together like mortar holds bricks. Every bargain, every pledge, every soul-collateral arrangement for a generation — dissolved. The gods come up out of the dark with no rules and no practice and appetites like open graves. You've seen what they do to prayers *now*, when they're half-starved and cautious. What happens when they're loose and grateful and completely unsupervised?"

"They'll learn," she said. "I'll teach them. I'm becoming something that can stand between—"

"*Becoming.*" He gripped her hands harder. "That's the word, isn't it. You're becoming. Look at your arms, Astrid. Look at your eyes. How much of you is going to be left at the end of this? You came down here to save one soul and you're spending yours in installments to do it, and that's — that's the same bargain, don't you see? That's Mother's bargain. *One life weighed against the rest, paid in secret, by someone who loved too much to do the math honestly.*"

The words landed. He watched them land, watched her flinch, watched the gold light in her eyes gutter like a candle in a draft.

"I'd rather be lost," Tam said, and his voice broke on it, and he let it break, because hiding it would have been worse. "Do you hear me? I would rather fade. I would rather forget the harbor and the cinnamon and eventually your nose-laugh, God help me, than have you trade the whole city for one brother. I am not worth Verrin. Nobody is worth Verrin. That's rather the point of Verrin."

"You're worth it to me."

"I know." Gently now. "That's what makes it dangerous. Love's a wonderful compass and a terrible map. It points true and it doesn't show you the swamps between here and north."

Astrid pulled her hands free and stood up and walked three paces to the wall and stood with her back to him, and the sigils along her spine pulsed slowly, like a second heartbeat having an argument with the first.

"I didn't climb down a mountain," she said at last, "to hear my little brother lecture me about self-preservation."

"I know. Terrible timing. It's the only kind of lecture I get to give."

A wet, furious laugh escaped her. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, smearing the ash into new patterns, and turned around, and for a moment she was eight years old again, caught red-handed with his broken ship, defiant and sorry all at once.

"Compromise," she said.

"I'm listening."

"We do it my way, but I let the guild help. Really help. I stop trying to carry it alone." She held up a finger before he could speak. "And I promise — I *promise* — that if it comes down to the city or you, I choose the city. Out loud. Witnessed." She glanced at the doorway. "Duvane. Witness this."

From the corridor, dry as old paper: "Witnessed. Reluctantly. Both parts."

"And the god witnesses too," Astrid added. "Gods keep promises better than people. It's the one nice thing about them."

The nameless god made a sound like wind through a canyon, which Tam chose to interpret as agreement.

"Fine," Tam said. "But I'm adding a clause. If I start fading faster — if they take more while we work — you don't escalate. You don't spend bigger. You come get me and we leave with whatever's left of me, and we live with it, and I will spend whatever remains of my memory learning to love you as a stranger, and I will manage it, because you're apparently very lovable even to people who just met you."

"That's the saddest romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I've had time to workshop it."

She came back across the cell and hugged him again, briefer this time, fiercer, and when she pulled away her face had set into the expression he knew from market days — the one that meant the haggling was over and the deal was struck and heaven help anyone who tried to reopen terms.

"Up, then," she said. "We have a long climb, a stolen page that's probably worthless, a guild running a con involving Halvard in a tax inspector's hat, and a court full of things that are about to notice us. Stay close, stay quiet, and if anything offers you a contract—"

"Sign nothing. Lick nothing. Make eye contact with nothing."

"You *have* been paying attention."

"It's the one thing this place gives you in unlimited quantities."

* *

They made it four corridors.

Four corridors of Duvane's silent hand-signals, of the god folding itself impossibly small to slip through gaps, of Astrid's sigils dimming to embers as she learned — visibly, painfully — to hold her light in. Four corridors of Tam walking out of his prison on legs that shook, counting his inventory with every step like a miser touching coins. *Astrid. Nose-laugh. Tallow and rosemary. Tar and cinnamon. I am Tam Lockridge and I am leaving.*

Then the corridor ahead filled with light that was not light, and the air went thick as tallow, and every sigil on Astrid's skin blazed awake at once, unbidden, screaming attention.

At the far end of the gallery, where the stair to the surface should have been, stood the Clerk.

It had brought colleagues.

"Oh," said Tam, into the ringing silence. "That's not good."

Beside him, Astrid exhaled slowly, and the gold in her eyes steadied into something older and harder, and the nameless god rose up behind them both like weather coming over the mountains.

"No," Astrid agreed. "But it's about to be interesting."
