## 16. The Court Beneath the Mountain

They did not drag her, in the end. That was the first surprise.

Astrid had expected chains, or the bone-white hands of enforcers, or at minimum some undignified shuffling through corridors with her arms pinned behind her back. Instead, after the heist collapsed around them—after Duvane's shout and Halvard's blood on the archive floor and the forged prayers curling up like burnt paper—the floor itself had simply opened beneath her feet, politely, like a host pulling out a chair.

She'd fallen for a long time. Long enough to notice that the walls of the shaft were lined with ledger pages, thousands of them, fluttering like moths, each one whispering a name.

Now she stood in a hall so vast that the ceiling was a guess rather than a fact. The mountain's heart was hollowed into something like a courtroom and something like a cathedral and something like, she thought with a hysterical flicker of humor, the world's most oppressive counting-house. Rows of desks stretched into the dark, each one unmanned, each one stacked with bone ledgers whose spines clicked softly, like beetles dreaming.

And above it all, on a dais of black salt, sat the court.

There were nine of them. She counted twice because the first count felt impossible. They were not gods—that much her waking sigils told her at once, the marks along her forearms lying flat and cold, unimpressed. They were something older and drier. Figures robed in what looked like settled sediment, their faces smooth as river stones, worn featureless by ages of decisions. Only their hands were detailed: long, precise, ink-stained fingers folded atop ledgers of their own.

The center figure spoke without moving anything Astrid could identify as a mouth. The voice arrived inside her skull, patient as dripping water.

"Astrid Lockridge. Daughter of Maren Lockridge. Sister of Tammas. Charm-seller, unlicensed. Debtor's kin. Thief." A pause, the length of a page turning. "Welcome."

"Lovely place," Astrid said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was one of the few family talents she'd inherited. "Though I'll be honest, the décor is very *bone*. You might want a plant."

The stone faces did not react. Somewhere far off, a ledger clicked.

"You attempted theft from the archive," said the figure to the left.

"We succeeded," Astrid corrected. "The page is in my boot. Well—it was. It turned out to be a forgery your Clerk planted, which, frankly, rude. But the attempt was successful. The failure was separate."

"The distinction is noted," said the center figure, and there was—she would have sworn it—a dryness in the voice that might, in a being with a face, have been amusement. "You are not what we expected."

"What did you expect?"

"Maren's daughter to be careful. Careful things stay home."

Astrid's jaw tightened. "You knew my mother."

"We keep accounts," said the court, all nine voices braiding together now, "of everything. Sit."

She hadn't noticed the chair behind her until it was mentioned. It was plain wood, unremarkable, and somehow more menacing than all the bone in the room. She sat, because her legs had opinions of their own.

* * *

They showed her the truth the way accountants show figures: without drama, which made it worse.

Light gathered above the dais—not warm light, but the pale glow of memory rendered in ledger-ink—and images formed in the air. Verrin, a generation ago. The purges. She saw streets she'd grown up in, but younger, louder, hung with charms and shrine-lamps and the bright ragged banners of the small gods. She saw the old days when every fisherman's boat had its own minor deity of knots, when bakers prayed to something that lived in ovens and got answers.

Then the inquisitors came, and the images burned.

But here was the part no street ballad ever sang: the burning didn't kill the gods. Nothing so clean. The purges drove them mad with hunger—prayers unanswered curdling into something feral—and the city began to tear itself apart. Astrid watched vignettes she recognized from Chapter Five of her own nightmares: wishes granted with cruel literalism, blessings that drowned, luck that killed.

"So the court caged them," Astrid said slowly. "After the purges. To stop the madness."

"To stop the *hunger*," the court corrected. "A god unanswered does not die. It sharpens. We built the cage. We wrote the law that magic must sleep, so that prayer would go unheard, so that the gods would starve slowly instead of feeding wildly. It has held for thirty years. Your city lives. Unhappily, dimly, but it lives."

"And my mother helped build it."

The image shifted. Maren Lockridge, young, exhausted, holding a baby—Tam, she realized, his fists tiny against her collarbone—standing before this same dais. Astrid's chest went tight. Her mother had never spoken of the purges except to say *we survived*, in the flat voice she used for things too heavy to lift twice.

"She came with nothing," the court said. "Her husband dead in the second purge. Two children. No guild protection—your charm-craft was already outlawed, and she practiced it anyway, badly, bravely. She offered us a bargain. Her craft, her service, her silence—in exchange for her children's survival during the settling years."

"And the price," Astrid said. The words tasted like ash and salt. "Tam's soul. As collateral."

"She did not read the full terms." For the first time, something moved in the stone faces—a ripple, almost like discomfort. "Few do. She believed she pledged her *service*. The Clerk of that era interpreted generously. In our favor. This is the nature of courts."

Astrid laughed, and it came out cracked. "So it wasn't even malice. It was fine print. My brother has been down here fading for ten years because of *fine print*."

"He has been *held*. There is a difference. Held souls do not suffer. They simply... wait, and thin." The center figure tilted its head, a gesture so human it hurt. "You want him back. Obviously. You have crossed a city, robbed an archive, allied with a disgraced inquisitor and—" a pause, and the sigils on Astrid's arm prickled, "—a certain nameless party we do not discuss in open session. So let us skip the negotiation theater you are preparing. We offer you a trade."

"I don't sign things," Astrid said. "I've learned that lesson secondhand."

"This one you will want to hear." The court leaned forward, nine bodies as one. "Our Clerk is retiring. Thirty years of maintaining the cage, and the seat grows... heavy. We require a successor. Someone mortal enough to remember what mortals lose. Someone clever enough to find loopholes—which you have demonstrated, repeatedly, expensively. Someone who understands both sides of a bargain."

The hall went very quiet. Even the ledgers stopped clicking.

"Serve as our Clerk," the court said, "and Tammas walks free. Whole. His memories restored, his debt dissolved, his name struck from every ledger in this mountain. He will wake tomorrow in Verrin with no debt at all. And you will hold the pen, Astrid Lockridge. Every contract that comes through this court will pass under *your* hand. No more generous interpretations. No more fine print devouring widows' sons. You could rewrite the terms of every bargain in Verrin. You could make this court *fair*."

* * *

That was the trap, of course. Astrid knew it was a trap the way she knew a rigged scale—by the way it balanced too perfectly.

And still. Still.

She sat in the plain wooden chair and let herself imagine it, because she was only human, and imagining was free. Herself at the great desk, the bone ledgers obedient under her hand. A fisherman's widow coming to plead, and Astrid reading the terms aloud, plainly, striking the clause that would take the boat. A child's prayer routed properly, answered gently, billed fairly. The cage maintained—but tended, softened, its bars wrapped in mercy. Tam safe. The city safe. Everything handled. Everything *ordered*.

It would work, too. That was the horrible part. She was good at small print; she'd spent ten years writing charms just small enough to escape notice. She understood desperate people and the bargains they'd sign. She would be a *good* Clerk.

"You're showing me how good it would feel," she said quietly.

"We are showing you what it is," the court replied. "We hide nothing. That is also policy."

"No, you're hiding one thing." She stood up. Her legs held this time. "You're hiding what happens to the Clerk. Where did the last one go? Because I met someone in your deep galleries who doesn't remember its own name, and I'm starting to think this whole mountain is a machine for eating identities and calling it employment."

Silence. Then, softly, the sound of a single ledger opening somewhere in the dark.

"The Clerk serves permanently," the court admitted. "The role requires... continuity. Memories of prior service persist. New attachments are discouraged."

"Discouraged." Astrid thought of her mother's face in the memory-light, tired and fierce. Thought of Tam asking her, just hours ago in his cell, *don't save me at the city's expense*—and here was the court offering her exactly that trade dressed in honest clothes. Thought of Duvane, burning her oath, taking the brand across her hands because some prices should be paid visibly.

"You know what my mother's real mistake was?" Astrid said. "Not the fine print. It was believing that safety could be bought once and for all. That she could pay a permanent price for a temporary problem and call it love." She spread her hands; the sigils along her forearms caught the ledger-light and glowed faintly, stubbornly alive. "I sell charms too small to notice. Do you know why they work? Because small magics are *kind*. They bend. They fit the person. Your cage is the opposite of everything I've ever made."

"The alternative," the court said, and its patience finally frayed at the edges, "is that you refuse, and we return you to the surface empty-handed, and the cage continues as it has, and your brother thins another decade, and the starving gods eventually crack it anyway, and everyone loses slowly instead of quickly. Is that your preference? Sentiment over arithmetic?"

"My preference," Astrid said, "is a third option. There's always a third option. It's usually worse and better at the same time."

"There is no third option. The arithmetic is closed."

"Yeah," Astrid said, and smiled, and felt the smile do something strange to her face—felt the sigils stir beneath her skin like waking birds. "See, that's what the last Clerk probably said. Right before somebody unbound every record in your archive."

The nine stone faces turned toward each other. For the first time, the court conferred among itself in a language that predated speech, a grinding like glaciers doing sums.

And in that moment of divided attention, Astrid felt it: the nameless god, deep in the galleries below, pressing against the edge of her awareness like a cat against a door. Not begging anymore. *Waiting.* Its sigil matched the birthmark over her heart, and the birthmark was warm, and the warmth was spreading, and somewhere far above her, muffled by a hundred fathoms of salt and stone, she heard the mountain make a sound it had not made in thirty years.

A cracking sound.

"You should know," Astrid told the court pleasantly, backing toward the hall's great doors, which had begun, on their own initiative, to swing open, "that I already gave away the key. And the person I gave it to? Doesn't believe in arithmetic."

The center figure rose from its seat. All nine rose. Bone ledgers across the vast dark hall began clicking all at once, faster, frantic, pages turning themselves as if searching for a record that no longer existed.

"*What have you done?*" the court asked.

Astrid Lockridge, charm-seller, unlicensed, daughter of Maren, sister of Tam, walked backward through the opening doors into the shaking dark and said the truest thing she'd said in ten years:

"Something too big to notice."
