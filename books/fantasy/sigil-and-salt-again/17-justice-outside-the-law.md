## 17. Justice Outside the Law

The guildhall smelled of tallow and burnt rosemary, which was what Halvard's kitchen always produced when he was frightened enough to cook.

Astrid stood in the middle of the long table with her boots still crusted in bone-dust from the archive, holding the stolen ledger page like a dead bird. Around her sat the eleven surviving members of the Charmwrights' Guild of Verrin — a number that had been fourteen a week ago, before the enforcers came down on the Low Quarter like a dropped ceiling.

"The page is false," she said again, because no one had answered the first time. "The Clerk let us take it. The real contract is somewhere deeper, and Tam is still down there, and I'm going back."

"You're going back," Halvard said, not looking up from the pot he was stirring as though it had personally wronged him, "into the court that just burned half the Low Quarter to send you a message. And your plan is what, exactly? Ask nicely?"

"My plan," Astrid said, "is to stop asking anything of them at all."

She set the forged page down. She put her hand flat over it, over the ash-seal and the bone-ink, and felt the sigils along her forearm wake and hum like bees under the skin. Ten years she had kept them small. Ten years of luck-knots and salt wards, of being too minor to notice. And now every mark on her body was a lit window, and something vast was looking through them.

"There's a god chained beneath their archive," she said. "Erased. No name in any record, which means no leash in any record either — except one. Me." She turned her arm over so they could see the birthmark, the old spiral that had never been ink at all. "It matches. It's been matching my whole life. My mother didn't just sell Tam's soul to buy our survival. She sold *me* first, or part of me, or the right to me — and the court has been sitting on that deed for twenty years, waiting for me to be useful."

Old Maren, who had taught three generations of charm-sellers how to knot a ward without waking an inquisitor, spoke from the end of the table. Her voice was dry as her hands. "And if you free it?"

"Then every record in that archive comes unbound. Every debt. Every pledge. Every soul held as collateral." Astrid swallowed. "Including ones people might want kept. Including bargains that are holding other things together. I don't know what breaks when they do. That's the honest answer."

The silence went around the table like a passed cup, each of them drinking from it.

Halvard finally set down his spoon. He looked older than he had at dawn, and he'd looked old at dawn. "When gods wake, they collect old debts," he said. "I told you that in the spring, girl, over eggs, and you laughed."

"I did," Astrid said. "I'm not laughing now."

"No." He rubbed his face. "You're about to do something worse than laughing. You're about to do something *right*, and rights like this don't come clean." He pointed the spoon at her, dripping. "The guild votes against this. Formally. For the record, so that when the mountain falls on us, we can say we told you not to."

"That's very brave of you, Halvard."

"I'm seventy-one. Bravery is for people with cartilage left." But his eyes were wet, and when she kissed the top of his head on her way out, he gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise and said, into the fabric of her sleeve, "Come back ugly and old and complaining. That's all I ask. Come back ordinary."

"I've never once managed it," Astrid said, "but I'll try."

* * *

Duvane was waiting for her at the mouth of the smugglers' stair, sitting on a broken milestone with her brand-new scars wrapped in linen, sharpening a knife she clearly had no intention of using.

"They gave you the vote?" she asked.

"They voted no. Eleven to one."

"And here you are anyway." Duvane stood, wincing. Her hands were ruined — the inquisitor's brand she'd taken across both palms rather than swear the oath of reinstatement had healed into something that would never hold a pen comfortably again. She flexed them anyway, out of habit or defiance. "Good. I hate committees."

"You're supposed to talk me out of it. You're the sensible one."

"I burned my own order's records, Astrid. Sensible left me years ago and didn't leave a forwarding address." She shouldered her pack. "Besides, I've read the court's law front to back, twice, once while drunk. There's exactly one thing in it stronger than the Clerk's authority, and it's this: a debt cannot bind a party who was never named. Your mother pledged *her* name and *Tam's* soul. If there's a third party in that contract — if there's a god whose name was struck from everything — then the whole architecture rests on a hole. And holes," she said, starting down the stair, "are where I've lived my entire adult life."

The Stair of Teeth went down through the mountain's throat the way a swallow goes down a gullet: narrow, wet, and lined with things that had once been proud. The smugglers had carved it generations ago, and the court had let it stand because even predators need mice. Astrid counted the turns to keep her fear arithmetic instead of animal. Forty-one turns. At forty-two, the air changed — went cold and mineral and faintly sweet, like a chapel that had learned to lie.

At the bottom, the outer halls opened up, and the bone ledgers stretched away in ranks taller than houses.

They had seen them before, on the descent for the heist, but seeing them again was worse. Shelf after shelf of femurs and ribs and skulls, each inscribed with names and sums, each one a person reduced to an entry. Somewhere in those ranks was a page with Tam's true name on it, and the terms of his slow installment-sale, and the interest accruing.

The deep galleries lay beyond, past the Clerk's counting-floor, where the erased thing waited.

* * *

It heard them coming. Of course it did. It had been listening to footsteps for longer than Verrin had had a name.

"You came back," it said. Its voice was like wind through a keyhole — thin, but with an enormous dark space behind it. It hung in its chains in the deepest gallery, and it was less a body than a suggestion of one, a shape the eye kept sliding off, wrapped in bindings that were not rope and not iron but *writing*. Lines of script wound around it from crown to foot, dense as a moth's cocoon, and every line ended in the same blank space where a name should have been.

"I came back," Astrid said.

"You brought the scarred one. Good. Scarred ones argue honestly." The god's attention settled on Duvane, then returned to Astrid, and Astrid felt it the way you feel sun through glass — warmth with a barrier in between. "You've been practicing. I can taste it. Every spell you cast costs you a memory of her, doesn't it? Your mother. You smell of her absence. Grief is very nutritious."

"Don't," Astrid said. "Don't be kind to me yet. I haven't decided anything."

"You've decided. You walked forty-two turns with your jaw set like that. You only get that jaw when you've decided and you're angry about it." The chains creaked as the god leaned closer, and the blank spaces in its bindings seemed to lean too. "Ask me."

"What happens if I break these?"

"Everything," the god said, with terrible cheerfulness. "Every record in the archive is written in the same law that binds me. Unbind the unnamed and you unbind the named. Debts dissolve. Pledges lapse. Souls walk free — including ones that were caged for reasons, little sigil-bearer. The court didn't chain the gods after the purges out of spite alone. Some of us were starving, and starvation makes gods imprecise, and imprecise gods drown fishermen." Its voice dropped. "You will open every cage. Not just mine. And some of what flies out will be hungry, and none of it will thank you, and the city above will learn what prayer meant before the law taught it manners."

Astrid looked at Duvane. Duvane looked back, and shrugged with the particular eloquence of a woman who had already burned one career and saw no reason to hoard the ashes.

"And if I don't?" Astrid said. "If I go home, and the guild hides, and the court keeps its ledger?"

"Then your brother is paid out in installments until nothing's left, the Low Quarter burns by degrees instead of all at once, and in a hundred years someone else stands where you're standing, angrier, with fewer options." The god smiled, and its smile was the worst thing in the gallery, because it was gentle. "I won't pretend you have a good choice. I've been down here since before your city had walls. I have watched everyone who found me make the same arithmetic. Nobody has ever chosen the chains. But they all hoped, walking away, that they'd chosen something cleaner than they had."

"I know," Astrid said quietly. "That's why I stopped hoping for clean."

She rolled up her sleeve. The birthmark caught the gallery's lightless light and threw it back gold.

"Tell me how."

* * *

It was insultingly simple, in the way that the truest things usually are.

"A debt binds a name," the god said. "Your mother's name holds your brother. The court's names hold the cages. But a name freely given can unlock what a name taken built. Speak yourself into the foundation of their law. Be the key. Not the payment — the payment is how they eat you. The key is how you open the door and stay holding the handle."

"And what's left of me afterward?"

"Something new," the god said. "Something neither of us can spell."

Duvane cleared her throat. "For the record, I object. Strongly. In the manner of a friend."

"Noted for the record," Astrid said, and her voice cracked on *friend*, and she pretended it hadn't, and Duvane kindly pretended not to notice, and that was the last easy moment any of them got for a very long time.

She walked to the base of the god's chains, where the first line of binding-script rooted itself into the living rock of the mountain — into, she understood now, the same stone that the court's whole legal architecture grew from. Everything above, every ledger, every cell, every sealed contract, was a branch of this root.

She put her palm against the stone. Her sigils woke all at once, every mark she'd ever inked and the one she'd been born with, blazing in a wheel of light up her arm and across her collarbone and — she felt it happen — beginning to climb her throat.

"Hey," she said, to the god, conversationally, because if she didn't keep her voice level she was going to scream. "What was your name? Before they took it?"

The silence went on so long she thought it wouldn't answer.

"I don't remember," it said at last, and there was nothing cheerful in it now. "That's the whole of the punishment, little sigil. Not the chains. The forgetting. Now — say it. Say you."

Astrid closed her eyes. Behind them, unbidden, came her mother's kitchen, the smell of rosemary, the sound of a woman humming off-key while teaching a seven-year-old to tie a luck-knot. One of the memories she could still afford. She spent it gladly, like a coin flipped to a beggar, and felt it go, and spoke into the root of the law:

"My name is Astrid Lockridge. Daughter of Ysolde. Sister of Tam. Charm-seller of the Low Quarter, guild of the small magics. I was never yours to bargain with — and I am choosing, freely, to be the key."

She turned herself in the lock.

* * *

Later — though *later* was a slippery word for some time afterward — Astrid would struggle to describe what unbinding felt like from the inside. The nearest she ever got was this: it was like being the last knot in a net, and feeling every other knot come loose at once, and discovering that the net had been holding up a sky.

The sound started below her feet. A crack, then a chord, then a chord of chords, as if the mountain had been an instrument strung far too tight for centuries and someone had finally, mercifully, cut the strings. The binding-script unwound from the god in rivers of dying letters, each character guttering out like a moth in a candle, and where the letters fell on the stone, the stone *forgot what it was doing*.

Above them, through forty-two turns of stair, the bone archive began to sing.

Not a nice song. A great groaning chorus of ledgers failing, contracts unraveling mid-clause, souls realizing all at once that they were free and not knowing what to do with their hands. Astrid felt each dissolution pass through her like weather. Somewhere a fisherman's drowned rivals woke on the sea floor, gasping and furious and alive. Somewhere a marriage sworn under duress came apart like wet paper. Somewhere a caged thing older than the purges stretched limbs it had forgotten having, and began, slowly, hungrily, to climb.

Debts dissolved. All of them. The fair ones and the foul ones alike, because law does not sort itself when the lawgiver is busy screaming.

And the mountain began to crack.

Dust came down the galleries in gray curtains. Duvane grabbed Astrid's arm — the branded hand, and Astrid would carry the memory of that grip, searing through cloth, for whatever remained of her life. "The ceiling—"

"Let it crack," Astrid said, and was horrified to hear that her voice had changed, had picked up harmonics, had gone from one note to many. "Let the whole rotten vault come down. They built a courthouse on top of a prison on top of a graveyard and called it justice. Let it—"

The god stepped out of its chains.

It did not so much stand as *arrive*, unfolding into the space its bindings had denied it, and even unfinished, even nameless, even starved down to a rumor of itself, it filled the gallery the way dawn fills a valley. It looked down at Astrid with something that was not quite gratitude and not quite pity and was perhaps the only honest thing she'd ever been given.

"You did it," it said. "No clean hands. Look at them."

She looked. Her palms were glowing. Not symbolically — actually, luminously, lines of light running through the skin like veins of ore, and where the light pooled, her fingerprints were resolving into characters. Sigils. She was becoming legible.

"Oh," she said, very small, inside all that vastness. "Oh, this is the price, isn't it. This is what 'key' means."

"It's what it *starts* to mean," the god said. "The rest, you'll learn upstairs. Because you're going upstairs, little sigil-bearer. We both are. Everything we freed is going upstairs, and the city above is about to remember what prayer was, all at once, with no one to teach it manners." It extended something that was approximately a hand. "Unless you'd prefer to stay here under the rubble?"

From above, muffled by stone, came a sound that made Duvane go white: voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Rising through the cracks in the world like water through a breached hull — prayers, every desperate unsanctioned prayer in Verrin being *answered simultaneously*, and the answering sounding, from down here, like a storm learning to speak.

Astrid took the god's hand.

"Took the words out of my mouth," she said. "All of them, apparently. Let's go teach the weather some manners."

They ran for the stairs as the mountain came apart behind them, and above, through the widening seams in the world, the gods of Verrin poured into the streets like light through a shattered window — and every one of them was hungry, and every one of them was listening, and the first prayer to reach Astrid's ears as she surfaced into the burning night was a child's, small and terrified and terribly specific:

*Please. Please, somebody. Make it stop.*

And every freed god in the city turned to answer at once.
