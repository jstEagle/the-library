## 18. When the Gods Remember

The first god to remember itself was small and wet and had been a tide pool for three hundred years.

It had no name anyone living recalled—*Brackish-That-Waits*, perhaps, or *The Kindly Drown*, though it had never drowned anyone kindly in its existence. It remembered only this: it had been worshipped once, by fishwives who left mackerel on the rocks, and it had answered their prayers with good hauls and gentle weather, and then one day the prayers stopped, and the chains came, and it had slept.

Now the mountain was cracked open like an egg, and Brackish-That-Waits poured into Verrin through the storm drains, and the first prayer it heard was a boy on the harbor steps wishing his little sister would *just stop talking for five minutes.*

The god granted it. Thoroughly. The girl's voice went out like a snuffed candle, and she stood on the dock with her mouth moving and no sound coming, and her brother screamed, which was, at least, a prayer of a kind.

Astrid felt it happen from six streets away.

She felt everything now. That was the trouble with being half-dissolved into pure sigil: her boundaries had gone generous. She was a lattice of burning lines wearing the approximate shape of a woman, and every answered prayer in Verrin rang through her like a struck bell. She could not stop hearing them. She did not especially want to. That frightened her more than the burning did.

"Five minutes," she said, arriving at the harbor in a rustle of light, "he said five minutes. Not forever. You have to listen to the *whole* prayer."

The tide-pool god regarded her from a puddle. It had no face, but she felt its attention settle on her the way cold water settles into boots.

*You are marked,* it said, in a voice like kelp dragging over stone. *Marked things belong to someone.*

"I'm marked by myself," Astrid said. "New arrangement. Now put the girl's voice back."

*Why?*

"Because when you were worshipped properly, your fishwives trusted you not to be cruel. This is cruelty. You've forgotten how to be anything else because you're starving, and I—" she gestured at herself, at the sigils crawling up her arms like ivy made of dawn, "—apparently I'm what's for dinner. So eat politely."

There was a long pause, oceanic in quality. Then the girl gasped, mid-word, and finished her sentence about herring, and her brother burst into tears and hugged her, and somewhere under the stones a small wet god learned—or relearned—the concept of *terms*.

Astrid walked on. There were so many more.

* * *

The second god to wake fully was enormous, and it woke angry, and its name had been *Vesh-Ammon, Who Weighs the Harvest*, before the purges scraped it off every granary door in the province.

It surfaced in the grain district at noon, rising up between the warehouses like a man standing up in a bathtub, all shoulders and shadow and the smell of threshed wheat. The first prayer it heard was a miller's wife praying that her husband's competitor's mill would fail.

Vesh-Ammon, who had been starved for a generation and had forgotten that prayers had shapes, reached down and made the competing mill fail. All of it. Structurally. With the competitor's family inside.

Astrid arrived running, which was a strange thing to watch, because her feet barely touched the ground anymore—she moved the way ink moves across wet paper, flowing toward the wound.

"No," she said. "No, no, *no*. Get them out. Get them out *now*."

*They prayed against him,* the harvest god said, and its voice was the sound of a full barn creaking. *Prayer is direction. I go where directed.*

"You go where *asked*, and asking isn't commanding! Your fishwives knew that! Your farmers knew that! They asked for rain, they didn't ask you to drown the neighbors' fields—they'd have been horrified!" She planted herself between the collapsing mill and the god, arms spread, sigils flaring so bright that shadows fled from her in all directions. "You are not a weapon. You were never a weapon. You were a *neighbor* who happened to be enormous. Act like it."

*I am hungry.*

"I know." And here her voice gentled, because she did know; she could feel the god's hunger as if it were her own stomach, a hollow ache three hundred years deep. "I know. But hunger isn't a law. Come here—I'll show you how to eat without breaking your teeth."

She taught him, there in the grain dust, the way her mother had taught her charm-craft: slowly, with examples, with a certain wry patience. A prayer for a good harvest could be answered by better soil, by rain at the right time, by blight turning aside—not by ruining rivals. A prayer for safety could be answered by strong doors and steady hands, not by removing everyone who might ever cause harm, which was, Astrid pointed out, "everyone, eventually, including the person praying."

Vesh-Ammon listened. It took him most of an hour, and he got it wrong twice more—once spectacularly, involving a wedding and a hailstorm—but by dusk he was pulling a family from rubble he had made, setting bones with fingers like plow-blades, and muttering, in a voice like a granary door, *"asked, not commanded. Asked, not commanded."*

Astrid leaned against a wall that wasn't really there and let herself shake for exactly ten seconds. Then she moved on.

* * *

By evening, the city was a choir screaming in a hundred voices, and Astrid was its exhausted choirmaster.

She went street to street. She talked a river-god out of flooding the tannery district because a dyer had wished his rival's colors would "run"—the god had taken it literally, and the canal was briefly the color of a bruise. She found a hearth-spirit, small and round and weeping embers, who had answered a widow's prayer for warmth by igniting the Low Quarter's eastern row, and she stood in the fire itself—it didn't burn her; nothing burned her anymore, which was its own quiet grief—and coaxed the spirit into drawing the flames back along their own paths like thread pulled through cloth. Half the row survived. The people who ran out into the street saw a woman made of glowing lines walking unharmed through the smoke, and some of them knelt, and she hated that, and understood it, and kept walking.

*Please,* said a voice from a gutter, small and cracked. *Please, my daughter is sick, please, anyone.*

Astrid turned. A new god was waking in the alley—a thin one, a ragged one, something that had perhaps once been prayed to by beggars, its name worn away entirely. It rose out of the dark like smoke given ambition, and it heard the prayer, and it began to reach—

"Wait," Astrid said.

The god paused. They all paused, now. Word was spreading beneath the streets, god to god, passed along the old ley-lines like gossip in a market: *there is a marked one walking the city, and she will teach you the terms.*

"Sick how?" Astrid asked the father, kneeling. Her knees still worked, mostly. She was keeping them. She was keeping her hands, and her laugh, and her habit of counting coins twice, and every other scrap of herself she could hold onto, the way a woman bailing a boat keeps bailing.

"Fever. Three days. The physiker wants four silver."

Astrid looked at the ragged god. "Here's your terms. You don't take the fever out—that's surgery, and you're too starved for fine work, you'll take something else with it. You cool her. You ease the pain. You give her body room to fight. The rest is hers and the physiker's. Can you do that much, gently?"

The beggar-god trembled. *Gently,* it repeated, tasting the word like a man tasting bread after famine. *I... think I remember gently.*

"Then go on."

It flowed past her into the tenement, and Astrid sat down on the step to wait, because she wanted to know, needed to know, whether it would work. Above her, the sky was the wrong color—amber and violet, lit from below by burning streets and above by gods remembering themselves, auroras of returning divinity. Somewhere a bell tower rang wildly, though no one was ringing it; a bell-god, probably, celebrating. Somewhere else, someone was singing. She chose to believe it was celebration and not another crisis. She was too tired to check.

The god emerged after an hour. In the window behind it, a girl slept, sweat-soaked but breathing easy, her fever broken to a warm simmer.

*Gently,* the beggar-god said again, wonderingly. And then, shyly, to Astrid: *What is my name? Do you know it?*

"No," Astrid said softly. "But you just earned a new one. Something like *The-Cool-Hand*. People will pray to that, if you keep answering like this. Names grow back. Everything grows back. That's rather the whole point of tonight."

The god drifted away down the alley, practicing gentleness on the rats, who were confused but appreciative.

* * *

Near midnight, the biggest of them found her.

She was sitting on the steps of the Salt Cathedral—desecrated in the purges, its altars chiseled blank, now glowing faintly as old wards woke along its walls like a body remembering sensation. She was eating an apple someone had left as an offering, because apparently offerings resumed immediately and she was, technically, a recipient, and being made of light did not cure hunger. It was a good apple. Small mercies.

The god that settled across the cathedral square was mountainous in the literal sense—a piece of the deep court's architecture given legs, granite and iron and the memory of chains, and when it spoke, windows rattled politely.

*MOTHER-OF-LINES,* it said. *THE OTHERS SPEAK OF YOU.*

"Do they." She didn't stand. Her legs had filed a formal complaint hours ago.

*THEY SAY YOU TEACH TERMS. THEY SAY YOU STAND BETWEEN US AND OUR FOOD AND MAKE US CHEW.*

"That's about the size of it, yes."

*WE WERE CAGED FOR A GENERATION. THE COURT BUILT THE CAGE. MORTALS HELPED BUILD IT.* The god's voice ground like millstones. *WE COULD UNMAKE THIS CITY IN AN HOUR. EVERY PRAYER ANSWERED AT FULL STRENGTH. EVERY WISH GRANTED TO ITS ROOTS. IT WOULD BE OVER BY DAWN.*

"It would," Astrid agreed. "And then what? You'd be gods of ash. You'd have eaten your own worshippers, and in ten years you'd be starving in the ruins, and someone would build another cage, and honestly? You'd deserve it." She stood then, slowly, and let her sigils blaze—not as threat, but as proof, the living covenant walking around in a woman-shaped frame. "Or. You learn. You bargain. You answer fairly, take fair payment, leave mortals their sovereignty. It's slower. It's harder. It means hearing 'no' sometimes, and meaning it. But it means that in a hundred years, there are still fishwives leaving mackerel on the rocks. Still children praying at shrines. Still *food*."

Silence, seismic in scale. The whole square held its breath—even the wind, even the fires two districts over seemed to lean in.

*AND WHO ENFORCES THESE TERMS?* the mountain-god asked. *WHO HOLDS THE LEDGER NOW?*

And Astrid opened her mouth to say *we'll figure that out together*, because that had been the plan, the loose and hopeful plan—

—and then she felt it.

Down. Far down. Beneath the cracked mountain, in the deep archive where bone ledgers recorded every debt ever sworn, something had begun to move. The Clerk. The Clerk, whose contracts had built the cage, whose bureaucracy had survived the mountain cracking open the way cockroaches survive earthquakes, was already writing. She could feel the scratch of its pen through the stone, through her sigils, through whatever she was now. New pages. New terms. New bargains being drafted in the dark while the gods danced above—contracts that would fold the freed gods back into service, fold the city into order, fold *her* into a line item.

The mountain-god watched her face change.

*YOU FELT SOMETHING,* it said.

"Yeah," Astrid said, and dropped the apple core, and felt the strangest thing rise in her chest—not fear, exactly, and not exhaustion either, though both were there. It was something older than either. The feeling of a charm taking hold. Of a knot tightening true. Of work, finally, being almost done. "I felt the Clerk picking up its pen."

*THEN WHAT?*

"Now," she said, and the light along her arms arranged itself into shapes she almost recognized—her mother's knots, the guild's marks, Tam's name written somewhere safe inside her where she could carry it, "now I go down and teach *that* one the terms. And it's going to be the hardest lesson yet, because the Clerk doesn't want food or worship or freedom. It wants everything signed, sealed, and filed. And I—" she laughed, tired and bright and a little unhinged, and the sound rang off the cathedral stones like a blessing, "—I have developed a *real* allergy to fine print."

She stepped off the cathedral steps and the air took her, and the mountain-god watched the mother-of-lines pour downward through stone and root and ancient stair, a ribbon of light descending into the dark, and for the first time in three hundred years, it bowed—to no altar, to no idol, but to the direction she had gone.

Below, in the deep archive, the pen scratched on.

Above, in the burning, healing city, the gods held their breath, and waited to learn what their fate would cost—and who, in the end, would pay it.
