## 19. The Price Named Twice

The heart of the court was not what Astrid had expected.

She had imagined something grander, perhaps—obsidian and dread, a throne of teeth to match the stair. Instead the chamber at the mountain's root was small and round and terribly ordinary, like a counting-house that had been buried alive. Shelves lined the walls from floor to ceiling, and every shelf was buckling now, ledgers sliding loose as the whole archive above them shuddered and shed its bindings. Freed names drifted through the air like ash in reverse, rising instead of falling, and where they touched the shelves the bone covers cracked open and went blank.

In the center of the room stood a desk.

Behind the desk stood the Clerk.

It had no face, but Astrid had learned, over these long terrible days, to read it anyway—the tilt of its featureless head, the way its hands smoothed the front of its gray robe when it was about to say something unpleasant. Right now both hands were smoothing very hard indeed.

"Astrid Lockridge," the Clerk said. "You are not scheduled."

"I know." Her voice came out strange. Half of it was hers; half of it was line and curve and glow, sigils moving beneath her skin like fish under ice. She could feel herself thinning at the edges, words she'd known yesterday gone soft as wet paper. She'd forgotten the name of Halvard's dog this morning. She'd forgotten whether Halvard *had* a dog. "I'm here ahead of my appointment. Consider it an early payment."

The Clerk's hands went still. "There is no early payment for what you intend."

"There is if I write the terms myself." She set her palm flat on the desk. The wood was warm. Everything down here was warm, like the inside of a mouth. "New covenant. Magic lawful. Souls sovereign. Every debt in this archive dissolved or renegotiated fairly—and I mean *fairly*, not the way you people do fair, which is a word you use right before you take someone's brother."

The chamber groaned. Somewhere far above, stone split with a sound like a ship breaking up.

"The mountain is failing," the Clerk said. "When it fails, everything recorded here fails with it. You are offering to rebuild the prison while standing inside it."

"I'm offering to build something better than a prison. A covenant needs an anchor. A name spoken into the foundation stone, held there forever, so the terms can't be quietly amended by anyone with a nice pen." She smiled, and felt her own sigils smile with her, which was an unsettling sensation she decided not to examine. "You've been anchoring your law in stolen souls for a generation. I'm offering you mine. Freely given. That's stronger than anything you've ever had."

For a long moment the Clerk did nothing at all. Then it reached beneath the desk and produced a form.

Astrid stared at it. "You're joking."

"The court does not joke," said the Clerk, holding out a pen. "But it appreciates good paperwork."

* * *

Tam found the stairs by falling down them.

That was, he reflected later, more or less how he'd done everything important in his life—by accident, at speed, with someone shouting his name behind him. This time the someone was Duvane, who had shouted *wait, you idiot* with the crisp authority of a woman who had once commanded inquisitors and now mostly commanded barkeeps.

He hadn't waited. His sister was somewhere below him, in a mountain that was coming apart, and he had spent eleven years as collateral. He was done waiting for things.

The Stair of Teeth had changed since she'd last described it to him—or since he'd overheard her describing it to Duvane while pretending he was asleep, which amounted to the same thing. The teeth were gone. In their place, the walls were soft with drifting light, and prayers moved through the passages like minnows: *let her live, let me find work, let the fever break, please, please, I'll be good.* Freed gods chased after them, vast and clumsy and delighted, like dogs finally let off their chains, answering everything at once and badly.

One of them noticed Tam on the stairs. It had no shape he could hold onto—just an impression of tide, and salt, and enormous attention.

"You," it rumbled, in a voice like a hull scraping sand. "Little mortal. You smell of the Lockridge woman. Ask. We answer now. We answer *everything*."

"Er," said Tam, who had survived the purges, the debt, and eleven years of memory sold off in installments largely by knowing when to keep his mouth shut. "No, thank you. I'm in rather a hurry."

The god made a sound that might have been offense or laughter. "Nobody says no anymore. It's wonderful. Say no again."

"No, thank you," Tam repeated politely, and kept walking, and behind him heard the god telling another god, in tones of wonder, *"It said no. They can still say no."*

The heart-chamber door stood open. Of course it did. Astrid had never in her life closed a door she meant to walk through.

He stepped inside and saw her at the desk, glowing like a page written in fire, a pen in her hand and a form before her, and he understood everything at once—the way he always understood Astrid, immediately and too late to stop her.

"No," Tam said.

* * *

Astrid's hand froze above the signature line.

She knew that voice. She knew the particular exasperated fondness in it, the way it always arrived half a heartbeat after she'd already committed to whatever foolishness it was objecting to. She turned, slowly, and there he was—whole, solid, *remembering*, his eyes clear for the first time since she'd pulled him out of his cell three floors and one catastrophe ago.

"Tam." Her voice cracked down the middle. "You're supposed to be upstairs with Duvane."

"Duvane said wait. So naturally I'm here." He crossed the chamber, and for a moment they just looked at each other, and all the arguments fell away, because he was alive and present and hers, and she had crossed a mountain and unmade an archive and half-unmade herself to make it so.

Then he looked at the form.

"Absolutely not," Tam said.

"Tam—"

"No. No, no. I know that look. I've known that look since we were children and you traded my winter boots for a 'genuine dragon scale' that was actually a painted oyster shell." He picked up the form and read it, fast, the way their mother had taught them both to read contracts—which, Astrid thought with a fresh twist of grief, was a lesson their mother had apparently learned the hard way. "'The undersigned surrenders all claim to their name, past, present, and future, to be held in perpetuity as the anchor-stone of the covenant.' Perpetuity, Astrid."

"It's a strong word. I like strong words."

"It means *forever*. It means you forget Mother. It means you forget—" his voice caught— "you forget me. You've already lost pieces. I watched you try to remember my birthday this morning and get as far as 'sometime in autumn.'"

"Late autumn," Astrid said. "October-ish. I'm keeping October-ish."

"That's not a date, that's a rumor of a date."

And despite everything—despite the cracking mountain and the dissolving archive and the faceless bureaucrat watching them with the patient air of a creature that had seen every family argument ever conducted over a contract—Astrid laughed. It came out of her like a bird startled out of a burning hedge, sudden and bright and helpless.

"Oh, don't laugh," Tam said, laughing himself, furiously wiping his eyes. "This isn't funny. This is the least funny thing that has ever happened, and I once watched Halvard fall into his own luck-knot display."

* * *

The Clerk cleared a throat it did not have.

"If the siblings have concluded," it said, "the offer stands. One name, freely surrendered, anchors the covenant. The debtor Tam Lockridge will be released regardless—the new terms supersede all prior instruments. But the anchor must be named before the mountain finishes settling, or the covenant fails, the archive burns, and every freed god descends upon Verrin with no law between it and their hunger."

Silence. Dust sifted down through the rising light.

Then Tam stepped forward and put his hand on the form.

"Don't," Astrid said sharply.

"Somebody has to sign it."

"Not you."

"Why not me?" He rounded on her, and eleven years came up in him at once, all the years he'd spent as a ledger entry, a line item, a thing owed. "It should have been me from the start! Mother pledged *my* soul, Astrid. I've already been currency once. I know how it works. You don't sign away your name to a bureaucracy—I *lived* in the bureaucracy. I know what perpetuity feels like from the inside. It feels like watching your sister through glass and forgetting, year by year, why the glass matters." His hand pressed flatter against the page. "Let me be the anchor. Let me be useful. Let me pay our family's debts for once instead of being the debt."

"Tam." Astrid took his face in her hands. Her fingers left faint trails of light on his skin, sigil-glow bleeding where she touched him, and he didn't pull away. "Listen to me. What Mother did—pledging you—it wasn't noble. It was desperate and it was wrong and I have spent this entire nightmare learning the difference. Love that pays with other people isn't love. It's just arithmetic with better manners." She smiled, wobbly. "If you sign, I spend forever as a glowing wall ornament knowing my brother erased himself for me. That's not saving me. That's just a nicer cage with me outside it."

"And if you sign?" His voice broke. "I spend forever carrying your name in my head, remembering you, and you don't remember me at all. How is that different?"

"It's different," Astrid said, "because I choose it. That's the whole point of the covenant, Tam. Souls sovereign. Nobody gets spent without their own yes." She kissed his forehead, and the light of her spread gently across his hair. "Besides. Someone has to remember October-ish."

"I hate you," Tam said, crying freely now. "I hate you so much."

"I know," said Astrid. "It's mutual. It's the strongest thing we've got."

* * *

The Clerk slid the form back across the desk and offered the pen.

Astrid took it. It was bone, naturally. Everything down here was bone or ash or salt; the court had never met a material it couldn't file.

"One amendment first," she said.

The Clerk's head tilted. "The terms were negotiated."

"The terms were negotiated by *me*, which means I get amendments. Clause nine. The anchor retains one memory. Not a name—not identity—but one moment, chosen by the anchor, held intact in the foundation stone for as long as the covenant stands." She looked at Tam. "Otherwise this is just your old trick with better manners, and I didn't come all this way to become you with a nicer font."

The Clerk considered. Above them, the mountain groaned, settling into its new shape, or refusing to.

"The court finds the amendment... acceptable," it said at last, and Astrid had the distinct impression that somewhere in its endless gray interior, something almost like respect had been filed. "Name your moment."

Astrid didn't even have to think. Of everything the power had eaten—her mother's face, her mother's voice, the recipe for fever-tea, the color of the sky on the morning of the purges—one moment had survived every withdrawal, guarded at the bottom of her like a coin sewn into a hem.

"The night before Mother signed the contract," she said. "We didn't know yet. All four of us—Mother, me, Tam—we sat on the roof of the house in the Low Quarter and ate burnt honey-bread and watched the harbor lights, and Tam was scared of the dark and pretended not to be, and Mother sang with her mouth full, and nobody knew yet what it would cost. For one evening we were just poor and happy." Her voice steadied. "That one. Hold that one forever."

Tam made a sound beside her that was not entirely a sob and not entirely a laugh. "You remembered the honey-bread."

"I told you. October-ish and honey-bread. I'm keeping the essentials."

* * *

Tam watched his sister pick up the pen.

He wanted, one last time, to grab her wrist, to tear the form, to shout that there had to be another way—that there was *always* another way, that was practically her motto, she'd built a life on finding the crack in every wall. But he had watched her refuse the Clerk's cage, refuse the guild's caution, refuse every easy exit down a long descending road, and he understood at last that this wasn't surrender. This was the same thing she'd always done, taken to its final term: finding the one bargain nobody else would dare to strike, and striking it first.

"You'll be magnificent," he said. "Terrible and magnificent. Like Halvard's accounting."

"High praise." She poised the pen. "Tam. When you leave offerings at whatever shrine they build—and you will, you're sentimental, you get it from Mother—make them small ones. I was always a charm-seller. I deal in small magics."

"Small offerings," he promised. "Burnt honey-bread. Every anniversary."

"October-ish," she said.

"October-ish," he agreed, and his heart broke and healed in the same breath, the way hearts do when they are used properly.

Astrid Lockridge signed her name.

The letters left her as she wrote them—he felt it happen, felt the word *Astrid* go out of the world and into the stone, felt the space where his sister had been fill up with line and curve and covenant-light. The foundation stone drank the ink. The mountain stopped groaning. All around them, the collapsing archive settled into stillness, shelves straightening, blank ledgers closing softly like eyes going to sleep.

And where Astrid had stood there was—something else. A figure of glowing marks, taller than she'd been, simpler than she'd been, a person reduced to her truest handwriting. It turned toward Tam. It had no face, but it tilted its head in a way he knew, a way that said *stop crying, you idiot, people will talk.*

"Hi," Tam whispered.

The sigil-figure raised one luminous hand and, with great deliberation, flicked his ear.

He laughed through the tears. "Yeah," he said. "Love you too."

* * *

They walked out together—Tam and the thing that had been his sister, and behind them the Clerk, already at work re-shelving a world, paused in its filing to watch them go.

"Anchors," the Clerk murmured to the empty chamber, in the tone of a functionary discovering an emotion it would later deny under oath, "are not supposed to wave goodbye."

But the figure at the doorway raised one hand of light and line, and waved.

Above them, dawn was coming down through the cracks in the mountain, and Verrin was waking to a world where magic was lawful and souls were sovereign and every prayer would need, for the first time in a generation, to be answered *fairly*—and the gods, freed and hungry and utterly without practice, were already gathering at the shrine where the new anchor stood, waiting to learn how.

The first of them arrived before Tam had even reached the stairs.
