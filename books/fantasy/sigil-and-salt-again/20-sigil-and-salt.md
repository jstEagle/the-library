## 20. Sigil and Salt

Tam had always assumed that the end of the world would be louder.

The court beneath the mountain was coming apart around them—bone ledgers unbinding into drifts of pale ash, pillars of salt cracking like ice on a spring pond, the Clerk's many hands fluttering in what might have been panic or might have been paperwork—but there was a stillness at the center of it, a held breath, and his sister was standing in the middle of that breath as if she'd been waiting for it all her life.

She was already half light. He could see the stone floor through her left arm. Where her veins had been, lines now glowed—a script he almost recognized, because he *did* recognize it, because it was hers, because she had spent ten years writing small charms in this same looping hand on bits of twine and fishbone and once, memorably, on the inside of his boot soles for luck, which had worked, incidentally, better than anything else she'd ever sold.

"Astrid," he said. His voice came out cracked. "Astrid, wait."

"There's no waiting left in this place, I think." She turned to him and smiled, and even her smile was becoming lines, luminous and precise. "Look at you, though. You're standing. You're *talking*. Do you know how long it's been since someone talked to me down here? The Clerk hums. That's not conversation."

"You're doing the thing," Tam said. "The joke thing. When you're terrified."

"I'm not terrified."

"You used to do it when Mother asked about the rent."

Her smile flickered—grief moving through light like a shadow through a window. "Fine. Yes. Terrified. Also certain. It's an uncomfortable combination. I don't recommend it."

Above them, through the fissures in the mountain's heart, Verrin was burning and blooming at once. Freed gods poured through the streets like weather. Every prayer ever whispered into salt water or cheap wine or the crook of an elbow was being answered at once, generously, catastrophically, the way a starving man eats. And somewhere in that flood, Astrid's sigils were holding—she was the circuit, the living mark, the one thread of order in a city unraveling into miracle.

But threads fray. Even glowing ones. Especially glowing ones.

"The covenant needs a name," she said. "That's the whole trick of it. The old cage ran on silence—no names spoken, no bargains honored, everything starved and locked. To open it properly, to make magic lawful instead of just loose, something has to stand in the foundation stone and *be* the law. A name, given freely, permanently." She looked down at her dissolving hands. "Mine's the only one that fits anymore. I've been becoming it for months. Might as well finish the sentence."

"Take mine."

"No."

"Take mine, Astrid—"

"*No.*" The word rang off the salt walls, and every unbound ledger in the archive shivered. She closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were mostly light, and gentler for it. "Tam. You gave enough. Ten years of installments. Your memories, sold off in little pieces you don't remember selling—that was Mother's debt, and then it was mine, and you never got a single day of choosing any of it. This is my choice. Let me have it. It's the first big one I've ever made without anyone starving in the background."

"That's a terrible reason to die."

"I'm not dying. I'm being *repurposed*." A laugh moved through her, and where it passed, the light steadied. "There's a difference. Ask any guildmaster."

He wanted to argue. He had arguments prepared—he'd been composing them since the cell, actually, since the moment he'd told her not to save him at the city's expense and she had looked at him with that particular Lockridge stubbornness and said, *noted, ignored, love you*—but standing here, watching her become something vast and kind, he found all his arguments were made of wanting, and wanting had never once changed her mind about anything.

So instead he said: "Then take the memory with you. Of me. So some piece of you knows why."

Something in her light went soft. "I was going to ask."

* * *

They did it the way their mother had taught them to seal a charm—except scaled up past all sense, the way Astrid did everything.

The foundation stone sat at the court's root: a slab of black granite older than the mountain above it, carved over with ten thousand generations of names, each one worn smooth by the weight of having been spoken into it. The Clerk stood beside it, its faceless head bowed, its hundred hands folded. For the first time since Tam had met it, it looked less like a functionary and more like a mourner.

"You understand the terms," the Clerk said. Its voice was dry paper. "This is not reversible. There is no ledger entry for reversal. I have checked. I check often."

"I know," Astrid said.

"For what it is worth—and I am told by the newly freed powers that worth is a thing one may now assign freely—it was an honor to process your family's accounts. Your mother bargained hard. You bargain harder. It is rare, in my long tenure, to be out-negotiated by someone who cries during the negotiation."

"I wasn't crying."

"You were crying throughout."

"*Clerk.*"

"It is a fact of record." One of its hands produced, from nowhere, a small slip of bone, and offered it to Tam. "For the brother. A receipt. The court is dissolving most of its functions, but receipts are eternal. It is the one law even we could not unmake."

Tam took it. On it, in characters that shifted like minnows, was written: *One soul, redeemed in full. Paid: one name, freely given. Balance owed: nothing. Balance carried forward: everything.*

His eyes stung. "Thanks," he managed.

"Do not thank me. I am a bureaucracy. We are constitutionally incapable of accepting gratitude; it gums up the works." The Clerk stepped back from the stone. "Whenever you are ready, Sigil-Locked Astrid Lockridge, daughter of Maren, sister of Tam, maker of luck-knots, hider of charms in boot soles."

"How do you know about the boot soles?"

"It is in the ledgers. Everything is in the ledgers." A pause. "Was. Was in the ledgers."

Astrid laughed, and the laugh was bells, and the laugh was also, unmistakably, her—the same snorting, undignified sound she'd made when Halvard once tripped over his own ward-rope at the guild meeting, the sound that had gotten them both thrown out of three different dawn markets. Tam memorized it. He decided, right then, that he would keep it somewhere no god could repossess.

She knelt at the stone. Her knees didn't quite touch it; she was more line than flesh now, a figure drawn in gold script wearing the memory of a person. She pressed both palms flat against the granite.

"Mother taught us the sealing words," she said quietly. "'By salt and by sigil, by debt and by gift.' She used them for luck-knots. Two coppers apiece." Her voice caught. "I used to think they were just words. Everything she said was just words until it wasn't."

"Astrid," Tam said.

"Yeah?"

"I'll bring offerings. Good ones. Not the cheap incense. The expensive stuff."

"Don't you dare waste money on me."

"I'm going to waste *so much* money on you."

She laughed again—that bell-snort—and then she leaned forward and spoke her name into the stone.

Not "Astrid." Not "Lockridge." All of it. The name their mother had chosen, and the secret middle name she'd hated, and the nickname the dock kids had given her when she was seven and gap-toothed, and the name the guild knew her by, and the name Duvane had growled across a burning rooftop two nights ago, and the name the nameless god had whispered back to her in the deep galleries like a returned key. Every name she had ever been, spoken whole, in one breath, into the black granite of the world's oldest receipt.

The mountain drank it.

* * *

Afterward, Tam could never fully describe what happened next, though he tried, in later years, in taverns, for free drinks, with varying success.

The light didn't explode. It *settled*—the way snow settles, the way a house settles at night, creaking into its true shape. Astrid's outline brightened until he had to look away, and when he could look again she was everywhere: in the cracks of the salt pillars, in the ash of the ledgers, in the seams between the world and the world. Lines of gold script ran up through the mountain's broken heart and out into the dark, and along those lines, Tam could feel—not hear, exactly, but feel—the terms of a new bargain taking hold.

Magic lawful. Souls sovereign. Prayers answered fairly, or not at all, but never cruelly. Debts honored, debts forgiven, debts *chosen*. The cage became a door. The door became a hearth.

And the starving gods, pouring through Verrin's burning streets, stopped mid-swoop, mid-miracle, mid-mistake—and read the terms written in light along their own skins—and settled.

He felt the moment the fisherman's drowned rivals came back coughing and furious. He felt a plague of boils lift off a cheating merchant who had, in fairness, earned them, but who would now simply be *embarrassed* instead. He felt a thousand small mercies redistribute themselves across the city like coins finding pockets.

The mountain quieted. The salt stopped falling. Somewhere far above, dawn was starting, and for the first time in a generation, dawn over Verrin was just dawn—no patrols, no pyres, no prayers going unanswered into the gray air.

"TAM."

Duvane's voice, echoing down the stair of teeth. He turned to see her climbing toward him, hands still branded, face streaked with soot and something that was absolutely not crying, inquisitors didn't cry, she'd have burned him for suggesting it. Behind her came Halvard, ancient and grim, carrying the youngest guild members on his back like a stubborn pack mule, and behind *them*, impossibly, the nameless god—whole now, tall now, wearing its recovered name like a crown it kept touching to make sure.

"She did it?" Duvane demanded.

"She did it."

"And she's—"

"Yeah."

Duvane looked at the foundation stone for a long moment. Then she knelt, awkwardly, brand-scarred hands on her knees, and said gruffly, to the light, "You were the worst criminal I ever failed to arrest. It was the honor of my career." She stood up fast. "Right. Mountain's collapsing. Everyone upstairs. Now."

* * *

They walked out of the mountain together, through halls that were gently, politely dismantling themselves—no violence in it, just architecture remembering it was allowed to be ordinary rock. The Clerk walked with them as far as the threshold, carrying a single ledger, the last one, which it said it was keeping for sentimental reasons, and which Tam suspected contained only one page: theirs.

At the mouth of the cave, morning hit them like a blessing. Verrin lay below, scorched in places, shining in others, alive. Gulls wheeled over the harbor screaming prayers, and the sea answered them with fish, fairly portioned.

Tam stood at the edge of the light and felt it happen—the transfer, the last clause of the covenant, the part Astrid hadn't warned him about because she'd known he'd refuse.

Her memories of herself poured into him like warmth into cold hands.

He remembered being her. He remembered the smell of the dawn market, brine and charcoal. He remembered the first charm she ever sold—a luck-knot to a nervous bride, two coppers, and how Astrid had run home and danced on the table. He remembered the purges, the fear, the night their mother sat them down with a candle and a contract and lied to them with love in her voice. He remembered ten years of careful invisibility, and the terrible bright joy of the first sigil waking, and every step of the long road down. He remembered her name—all of her names—and the exact sound of her laugh.

He fell to his knees on the mountainside and wept, openly, thoroughly, while Duvane stood beside him and pretended to inspect the horizon.

"She's still up there," he said finally. "I can feel her. In the stone. In the—" He gestured helplessly at everything. "In the *rules*. She's the rules now. She'd hate that. She broke every rule she ever met."

"Maybe that's why they picked her," Duvane said. "Who better to write laws than someone who knows exactly where they chafe?"

Below them, in the recovering city, people were already gathering at shrines, testing the new covenant with small, careful prayers. A child's voice rose from the Low Quarter—thin, clear, asking for something small and honest.

And high on the mountainside, in the seam of the world, a line of gold script paused.

Almost as if it were listening.

Almost as if it were deciding.

Tam wiped his face, tucked the Clerk's receipt into his shirt, over his heart, and started down toward the city—toward the shrine, toward the offerings, toward the long work of loving someone the world had made into light.

He'd bring the good incense. She'd hate it. It was the least he could do.
