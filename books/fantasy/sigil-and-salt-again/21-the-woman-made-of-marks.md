## 21. The Woman Made of Marks

The shrine had been a fishmonger's stall once, back when the Low Quarter smelled of brine and desperation instead of brine and incense. Tam Lockridge knew this because he'd bought herring from it, in another life, from a woman named Odile who'd cheated him on the weight every single time and slipped him an extra fish anyway when his mother was dying. Now the stall was gone. In its place stood three walls of pale stone, a roof of copper gone green, and a doorway with no door—just a threshold lined with salt that nobody ever swept away and nobody ever stepped on.

Inside, the woman made of marks knelt among the offerings.

Tam watched her from across the lane, the way he did most mornings now, a loaf of yesterday's bread warm under his arm and a small clay cup of honeyed milk balanced in his other hand. He told himself it wasn't watching, exactly. It was tending. There was a difference. Watching was what you did to something strange. Tending was what you did to someone you loved.

She was kneeling the way Astrid used to kneel at their mother's bedside—weight on the left knee, right foot tucked, hands loose in her lap as if she might need them for anything at any moment. But where Astrid had been all freckles and chapped knuckles and ink stains she never quite scrubbed out, this figure was lines. Glowing lines, fine as thread, laid over a shape that was only roughly a woman: sigils in slow motion across her shoulders, wards turning over themselves along her arms like water finding its level, and at the center of her chest, where a heart would be, a mark that pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. The way a lighthouse pulses—not because it wants to, but because that is its work.

The city had gotten used to her faster than Tam had expected. Verrin was good at getting used to things; it was practically the civic religion. Within a season there were licensed charm-wrights on three corners of the Saltmarket, guild-stamped luck-knots sold openly beside the eels, and a new office above the old assize court called the Bureau of Fair Bargains, whose clerks wore grey and were, according to everyone, insufferable in exactly the way clerks have always been insufferable. Magic was lawful again. Small magic, honest magic, magic with a receipt.

And in the shrine where the fishmonger's stall had been, the nameless god kept its recovered name, and the woman made of marks kept the god company.

Tam crossed the lane.

"Morning," he said, because you said morning to people. His voice came out rougher than he wanted. It always did, these days. A year of talking to a figure of light will roughen anyone's voice.

She turned. She had no face, not properly—there was a suggestion of one, the way there's a suggestion of a face in the knot of a tree—but she turned toward him with her whole self, the way Astrid used to turn, all attention, nothing held back.

"I brought bread," he said. "Stale. On purpose. You always liked it stale, you said fresh bread was showing off."

He set the loaf on the offering shelf, between a fisherman's model boat and a child's doll with one eye, and poured the honeyed milk into the little stone basin that had been carved there by a mason who charged half price and wept the whole time, or so the story went.

The sigil-woman rose. Lines shifted along her body like a sentence rearranging itself, and she drifted—she didn't walk, exactly, though sometimes she pretended to, planting glowing feet on the flagstones out of courtesy—to the basin. She looked at the milk. She looked at the bread. And then she did the thing she did some mornings and not others, the thing that undid Tam a little more each time:

She paused.

Her head tilted. The marks along her throat brightened, dimmed, brightened—the rhythm of someone trying to place a tune heard through a wall. Her hand came up, hovering over the loaf without touching it.

"You did like it stale," Tam said quietly. "You said—" His voice caught. He cleared his throat. "You said fresh bread was just wheat bragging."

The pause stretched. Somewhere behind the shrine, a gull screamed its opinion about everything. The pulse in her chest flickered, quickened—

—and then settled. The marks dimmed to their ordinary glow. She withdrew her hand and inclined her head toward him, courteous, grateful, a hostess acknowledging a guest whose name she couldn't quite recall but whose visits she clearly expected.

Tam smiled at her anyway. He'd gotten good at smiling anyway. It was, he'd decided, the family trade. Their mother had done it through the worst years of the purges, smiling at neighbors while signing away her son's soul in ash and salt. Astrid had done it through ten years of selling charms too small to notice. And now he did it, standing in front of the thing his sister had become, offering her stale bread and pretending that was enough.

It wasn't enough. But it was what there was.

* * *

He went to the Bureau after, because grief is no excuse for missing appointments, a lesson the Clerk had taught him in ways he still found in himself like splinters.

The Bureau of Fair Bargains occupied a building that had once housed the inquisitors' assay office. Someone had decided the symmetry was important. Inside, the walls were lined with ledgers—not bone anymore, thank every merciful power, but good honest paper—and the clerks worked at long desks beneath windows that let in actual sunlight, which Tam suspected was less architectural mercy than deliberate policy. Hard to feel devoured in daylight.

"Citizens' grievance?" asked the clerk at the intake desk. She was young, grey-robed, and had the air of someone who had personally reorganized the filing system and would fight you about it.

"Grievance," Tam agreed. "Ongoing. Case number—" he recited it from memory, because of course he had it memorized, "—four-eleven-oh-nine."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "The Lockridge restoration case." She said it the way people said famous shipwrecks. "You're the brother."

"I'm the brother."

"Status review is quarterly. Your next is—" she checked, unnecessarily, because she clearly also had it memorized, "—in six weeks."

"I know. I'm not here about the status." He leaned on the desk. "I'm here about the covenant clause. Section nine. The one about offerings."

The clerk's professional mask flickered. "Section nine is... unusual."

"Section nine says that offerings made to a bound power must be answered fairly within a season, or the bond dissolves." Tam kept his voice even. "The woman at the shrine has been receiving my offerings for a year. Bread, milk, salt, songs—I sang to her in midwinter, clerk, I sang *badly*, which should count double. And she answers. Every time. She tends the shrine, she keeps the god's name safe, she settles disputes between petitioners. That's fair answer. So why does the Bureau keep sending assessors to watch her like she's a debt waiting to come due?"

The clerk looked at him for a long moment. Then she lowered her voice, which in the Bureau was the equivalent of handing over state secrets.

"Because she isn't in the ledger, Master Lockridge. That's the problem. When your sister spoke her name into the foundation stone, the covenant recorded the sacrifice. Name surrendered, soul sovereign, magic lawful—all of it written fair and square. But the *woman*—" she tapped the desk, "—the woman who tends the shrine, who pauses at your bread, who chose last month to help the cooper's daughter when she could have refused—that woman is doing things the covenant didn't require. Things it didn't *anticipate*. Free acts, from a surrendered name. The assessors can't classify her. And the Bureau hates what it can't classify almost as much as the old court hated what it couldn't collect."

Tam absorbed this. "So she's a clerical error."

"She's a *miracle* with insufficient documentation," the clerk said, with the weariness of her profession. "There's a difference. Barely."

* * *

That evening he climbed the shrine steps with his sister's old charm-kit under his arm.

He'd found it in spring, packed in waxcloth in the crawlspace of the house on Tanner's Row, alongside their mother's contract-sealed-in-ash, which the Bureau had taken for the archive, and a bundle of letters he hadn't been able to read yet. The kit itself was scarred leather, smelling of salt and tallow, with loops for awls he'd sharpened a hundred times and a lining pocket where Astrid used to hide the charms too risky to sell—too alive, she'd say, laughing, too *chatty*.

He set the kit on the offering shelf next to the bread.

"For the god," he said aloud, because the god was there too—you could feel it, a vast attention folded up small in the shrine's back corner like a cat in a sunbeam. Since Astrid had unbound it, the nameless god had remembered itself slowly, in pieces, the way a drowning man remembers the shore. It had a name now. Nobody in Verrin would say it casually—it sounded like ice settling on a lake, like a door in an empty house—but it had one, and it kept it wrapped in the shrine's salt, and it was, by all reports, learning gentleness at roughly the same pace as a glacier learns dancing.

"But mostly for her," Tam added. "In case she remembers what the loops are for."

The sigil-woman drifted over. She examined the kit. And then—this was new—she reached out and touched it.

Not hovered. *Touched.* Glowing fingers resting on scarred leather, and where they rested, the marks on her skin rearranged, spiraling into patterns Tam knew down to his bones: the first stitch of a luck-knot. The one their mother taught them both on a kitchen stool, before the purges, before everything, when magic was just a game with string.

His breath went out of him.

"Astrid?"

The figure stilled. All over her body, the sigils slowed—not dimmed, *slowed*, like a river remembering it was once rain deciding which way to fall. The pulse in her chest stuttered. Brightened. Stuttered again.

And from somewhere inside the lattice of light, faint as a voice through years and stone, came something that was almost words. Not her voice. Not quite. The shape of her voice, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot.

"*...wheat... bragging...*"

Tam sat down on the shrine steps very suddenly, because his legs had resigned without notice.

The sigil-woman looked at him. The pause held—one heartbeat, two, five, ten—an eternity by the standards of the year he'd endured. The gulls went quiet. Even the god's vast attention seemed to lean closer.

Then, gently, the moment passed. The marks resumed their patient turning. She inclined her head to him—grateful, courteous, *almost*—and drifted back to her place among the offerings, kneeling left-knee-down, hands loose, ready for anything.

Tam stayed until full dark. Before he left, he did what he'd started doing every night, the ritual nobody had asked of him and nobody could stop him from performing: he took the chalk from his pocket and wrote, on the shrine's outer wall, low where the salt line began, one small mark. A luck-knot's first stitch. The same one, every night. Three hundred and forty-one nights now.

A record, in the only language he trusted. *I was here. You were loved. Come back whenever you're ready.*

As he walked home through the licensed lamplight of the thriving, magical, insufferably well-documented city, he passed the Bureau's evening notice-board, where new postings went up at dusk. Most were routine: charm-license renewals, a recall on unstable love-knots, the usual.

One notice was different. Fresh ink. Official seal.

**BY ORDER OF THE BUREAU OF FAIR BARGAINS:** *Anomalous entity, designation S-1 ("the Sigil-Woman"), having performed an unclassified free act (see incident report 4110-A: voluntary aid, cooper's daughter) and a second unclassified act (see incident report 4110-B: partial memory response, subject T. Lockridge), is hereby summoned to classification hearing. Failure to appear will result in reclassification of status from ANOMALY to DEBT.*

*Hearing scheduled: dawn, day after tomorrow, Bureau chambers.*

Tam read it twice. Around him, the lamplighters moved down the lane, kindling small lawful magics one post at a time, and somewhere behind him the shrine glowed softly against the dark, and the woman made of marks knelt among the offerings, pausing, almost, nearly, not yet.

A debt.

They were going to call his sister a *debt*.

Tam stood very still in the lamplight, and felt something in him settle into place with an awful, familiar clarity—the same clarity that had carried him down the Stair of Teeth, that had faced the Clerk, that had argued with gods. His sister had given her name so that no one in Verrin would ever be collateral again.

Apparently the paperwork disagreed.

He turned toward the Low Quarter, toward Duvane's rooms, toward Halvard's guildhall, walking faster with every step, already composing the argument that would save her—or burn the Bureau's precious ledgers trying.
