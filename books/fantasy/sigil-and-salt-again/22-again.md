## 22. Again

A year is a long time to be made of light.

Not that Astrid had anything to compare it against. She had been a woman once, and women measured time in aches and appointments, in the sour smell of dawn markets and the particular weight of a coin purse going from full to empty. Now she was lines and glow, sigil-work given legs and something like patience, and time passed through her the way weather passes through an open window—felt, but not held.

She tended the shrine at the foot of the mountain because tending was what she was for now. The shrine had been a customs house before the covenant, then a smugglers' den, then rubble; the masons of Verrin had rebuilt it in pale stone with no roof, so that the sky came all the way down into it, and the old god who lived there could look up whenever it wanted and remember being bigger.

The old god had its name back. It had turned out to be called *Ossuin*, Keeper of Small Mercies, which everyone agreed was a slightly disappointing name for something that had once cracked a mountain, though Ossuin itself was delighted. It spent most of its days now in the shape of a gray heron, standing in the shrine's shallow reflecting pool on one leg, judging people silently.

"You're brooding," Ossuin said, without turning its head. Herons can do that. Gods can do it better.

"I don't brood," said Astrid. "I *diffuse*. There's a difference."

"You've been diffusing in the same corner for three hours."

"It's a good corner. The morning light comes through the east arch and makes my edges look intentional."

Ossuin clacked its beak, which was heron for laughter. "The anniversary crowd will be here soon. Try to have your edges together."

* * *

They came every day, of course. That was the point of everything—the covenant carved into the foundation stone, the ledger unbound, the court beneath the mountain converted from a debtors' prison into something more like a very stern notary office. Magic was lawful in Verrin now. Not free—nothing worth having was free—but fair. You prayed, and something answered, and the terms were written where you could read them.

But on the anniversary they came in numbers, because on the anniversary a year ago, a woman made of marks had stood in the burning heart of the court and spoken her own name into the stone, and the world had rearranged itself around the syllables.

Tam came first, as he always did. He carried offerings the way their mother used to carry market baskets—everything useful, nothing precious, arranged with a practicality that was its own kind of love. Today it was salt in a blue jar, a spool of good thread, and a honey cake from the baker on Coriander Street who still didn't charge him, because Tam had once untangled a luck-knot for the man's daughter and the baker considered the debt eternal.

"You know I can't eat the cake," Astrid said, coalescing a little closer to solid so she could properly appreciate his face. She had learned to do that—gather herself denser when someone she loved needed looking at. It cost her nothing anymore. Most things cost her nothing anymore. That was the strange arithmetic of what she'd become: she had given up her name and been paid in a kind of freedom she hadn't known existed.

"The cake isn't for you," Tam said. "It's for the shrine. Shrines eat cakes. Everyone knows this." He set it on the offering ledge, next to the jar and the thread, and then he did what he always did, which was stand there a moment too long, watching her glow, wearing the expression of a man carrying something heavy and fragile that he refused to set down.

"Say it," she said gently.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not saying since you walked in. You have a face like a locked drawer."

He laughed, and it cracked halfway through into something else. "I dreamed about you last night," he said. "The real you. Before. You were arguing with a fishmonger about the price of eels, and you were *furious*, and it was wonderful. I woke up and I couldn't remember—" His hands moved, helpless. "I couldn't remember your voice doing that. The angry voice. I remember that you argued about eels, because I carry that memory for you, but remembering *that* you argued isn't the same as hearing it."

Astrid drifted closer. Where she passed, the air smelled faintly of salt and hot metal, the way a struck flint smells.

"Tam. Look at me."

He looked.

"I am the least gone person you will ever meet. I'm everywhere the bargains are. Every fair prayer answered in this city goes through me, and every one of them has a little shape, a little *her* in it. Somewhere in the last year I have haggled over eels eleven thousand times. The fishmonger never wins. It's tradition."

"That's not the same."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. But it isn't nothing either. And—" she hesitated, because the next part was new, had happened only last week, and she hadn't told anyone yet, not even Ossuin, especially not Ossuin, who would make a *face*— "and lately it feels less like echoes. Lately it feels like gathering."

"Gathering?"

"When I answer a prayer well—when I get it right, when the bargain lands true and the person walks away whole—it's like... sediment. Like a river dropping silt. Something settles in me and stays." She spread her hands, all line and light. "I don't know what I'm becoming, little brother. But I don't think I'm eroding anymore. I think I'm accreting."

Tam stared at her. Then, because he was their mother's son and grief had taught him to grab joy with both hands before it changed the subject, he grinned. "Accreting. Listen to you. Ten years selling two-copper charms and now you talk like a ledger."

"I *was* a ledger for a while. It leaves a mark."

* * *

The anniversary crowds thickened through the morning. Verrin had gotten good at anniversaries; the city had a lot of grief to mark and had decided, as a civic project, to mark it with pastries. Stalls lined the processional way. Children ran everywhere underfoot, which Astrid approved of deeply—children saw her clearly, without the flinching adults sometimes couldn't help. To a child, a woman made of glowing sigils was simply the best thing they'd ever seen, and they said so, loudly, at volume, and asked if she could fly (no), whether she was a ghost (no), and whether she was the same lady who had fixed their grandmother's knee (yes, and tell your grandmother the payment plan stands).

Duvane came at midday, gray at the temples now, her branded hands wrapped in plain linen out of habit rather than need. She had taken over the reconciling of the court's records—someone had to, and she was the only person alive who understood both the old law and the new—and she complained about it constantly, comprehensively, and with evident satisfaction.

"The Clerk's successor filed forty-one petitions in mirror-script again," she announced, dropping onto the shrine steps beside Tam. "Mirror-script. Because the old court wrote in mirror-script and the new court thinks tradition is a personality. I spent six hours reading backwards."

"You could ask Astrid to look at them," Tam said. "She reads every script there is now."

"I am not asking the living covenant to do my filing."

"She'd love it."

"I know she'd love it. That's why I won't ask." Duvane glanced up at Astrid, and for a moment the ex-inquisitor's habitual armor slipped, and underneath was the woman who had burned her own oath and taken the brand across both hands rather than deliver a friend. "You look well," she said gruffly. "For a document."

"For a document," Astrid agreed warmly. "You look terrible. For a person."

"Thank you. I've been working on it."

Halvard arrived later, older, slower on the stairs, leaning on a cane he pretended was ornamental. He had voted against her, that night in the guildhall—a year ago now—and he had never apologized, and she had never wanted him to. Someone had to argue for silence. Someone had to be wrong in the direction of caution, or courage stopped meaning anything. He brought her a charm, which was the funniest thing anyone did all day: a small, clumsy luck-knot, tied by his own arthritic fingers, the kind of thing she used to sell in the dawn markets for two coppers.

"It won't work," he said. "Obviously. It's just string."

"It's the best thing anyone's brought me," she said, and hung it, glowing, around the neck of the shrine's statue of Ossuin, where the heron-god regarded it with one yellow eye and pronounced it, after long consideration, *adequate*.

* * *

It was late afternoon when the child came.

Astrid noticed her the way she noticed everything now—all at once, from every side, the whole shrine a single attentive ear. A girl, maybe seven, thin in the elbows, with the careful quiet of a child who has learned not to take up space. She slipped away from the crowds, past the stalls, past the statue, and knelt at the edge of the reflecting pool where Ossuin stood sentinel, and she pressed her small palms together, and she prayed.

Most prayers came to Astrid like letters—written, addressed, legible. This one came like a fist knocking on a door and then running away. The girl prayed fast and half-ashamed, eyes squeezed shut:

*Please. Please. Da says the boat's cursed since the storm took the nets, and he says we'll lose the boat, and if we lose the boat we lose the house, and Mam cries at night when she thinks I'm asleep. Please fix it. I don't have anything to trade. I checked. I'm sorry.*

And there it was—the old pull, the current that ran through Astrid's every line. Answering was what she was. The covenant required fairness, not generosity: a prayer needed terms, a price, an exchange. That was the whole architecture of the peace she had bought with her name. The gods of Verrin answered fairly now, and *fairly* meant *with payment*, and a seven-year-old with empty pockets stood at the edge of a pool holding nothing but her mother's crying.

Astrid felt the machinery of the covenant engage, smooth and impartial, preparing the standard response: *terms unavailable; prayer deferred.* Fair. Correct. The kind of justice that lets a child walk home with her hands still folded around nothing.

A year ago, in another life, in a body with a name, Astrid Lockridge would have broken the rules without even noticing she was breaking them. She'd have sold the charm for a copper and thrown in the second one free. She'd have lied to the ledger and called it mercy.

But that Astrid was dispersed. That Astrid was sediment. Unless—

Unless the act itself could hold her.

She gathered. Not toward solidity, exactly—toward *attention*. She let the covenant's machinery run its course and then, deliberately, in front of the foundation stone and the heron-god and the whole listening sky, she reached outside it.

"Little one," she said.

The girl's eyes flew open. She looked at the pool, at the heron, at the glowing figure that had stepped—there was no other word for it—*out of the air* beside her.

"You're her," the girl breathed. "You're the lady off the coins." (Verrin had put her on the commemorative coins. She had opinions. Nobody had asked.)

"I'm her. What's your name?"

"Maret. Maret Sallow."

"Well, Maret Sallow. Here's the trouble. The covenant runs on exchange—you knew that, everyone knows that—and you came with empty pockets. So we're going to have to be clever." Astrid crouched, lines of light settling like a woman folding a skirt. "Your father's boat. Is it a good boat? Under the bad luck, underneath—is it sound?"

Maret thought about it with the total seriousness of a seven-year-old being consulted as an expert. "Da says she's the best hull on the coast. He says the sea's just got it in for her."

"Boats are like that." Astrid smiled, and the smile was the first thing in a year that felt like it came from somewhere deep and specific and *hers*. "Here are your terms, then. No coin. Instead: your father takes his boat out at dawn tomorrow, and he finds the storm-torn nets—he doesn't buy new ones, he *mends*. And when he mends them, he teaches you every knot he knows. All of them. Even the ugly ones. Can you promise that?"

"He already teaches me knots."

"Then you'll promise to learn them *well*, and one day teach them to somebody who needs them. That's the trade. A knot passed down is worth more than a knot tied. That's the oldest rule there is, and I should know—I used to sell them for two coppers."

Maret pressed her palms together again, solemn as a magistrate. "I promise."

"Witnessed," said Ossuin, from the pool, in the vast voice it saved for contracts, which made Maret jump a foot in the air.

And Astrid answered the prayer.

She felt it go out of her—not power, exactly, but *arrangement*: a nudge here, a loosening there, a rope finding its fray a day early instead of a mile out at sea, a fisherman waking with an itch in his hands to mend rather than mourn. Small mercies. She was, after all, the living sigil of a god whose recovered name meant Keeper of Small Mercies; some partnerships were inevitable.

And the moment the bargain sealed—true, and fair, and *kind*—the sediment settled.

It didn't feel like the other times. The other times had been grains. This was a *shingle*, a whole flat stone of herself dropping into place, and she gasped, or did whatever a being of light does instead, because suddenly she could remember—

—the fishmonger's face, red and outraged, the eels slapping the counter—

—her own voice, furious, glorious: *"Two coppers means TWO COPPERS, Jorun, I don't care what the harbor rate is—"*

—Tam, age nine, laughing so hard he fell off the dock.

"Oh," Astrid said softly. "Oh, hello."

"What?" said Maret. "What happened? Did I do it wrong?"

"You did it perfectly." Astrid's glow had shifted; she could feel it, warmer, denser, a shade more *person-shaped* than she'd been at breakfast. Somewhere inside the pattern of her, a fragment had re-coalesced around the act itself—around the choosing, the reaching outside the law, the mercy that paid its own way. Hope, earned. Not given. Never given; that was the whole point of her.

Ossuin waded to the pool's edge and looked up at her with one ancient yellow eye, and the heron's expression was, for once, entirely unjudging. "*Accreting,*" it said.

"Don't make a face."

"This is my face not making a face."

* * *

That evening, when the crowds had gone and the offerings sat in the blue dusk—salt, thread, one honey cake, one string luck-knot rated adequate—Tam climbed the shrine steps and found his sister standing very still at the pool's edge, brighter than he had ever seen her, and laughing quietly at nothing.

"What?" he said.

"Jorun," she said. "The fishmonger. I remembered his *face*, Tam. I remembered the exact sound the eels made." She turned, and her light caught the water, and for a heartbeat—just a heartbeat—the lines of her resolved into something almost like a young woman with salt-hazed hair and her mother's stubborn chin, standing in the last of the sun.

Tam made a sound he would deny to his grave.

"It's starting," Astrid said, and her voice had edges now, and warmth, and a trace of the angry eel-arguing music underneath. "I chose today, Tam. Outside the covenant, for a girl with empty pockets. And the choosing *held* me." She lifted her hands, all line and glow, and watched them shimmer. "Every fair bargain gathers a grain of me. But every *mercy*—every mercy gathers a stone. So I'm going to keep choosing. As often as I can. For as long as it takes."

"How long?" Tam whispered.

She smiled, and the smile was entirely, unmistakably Astrid Lockridge's, crooked on the left.

"How long does it take to build a person out of kindness?" she said. "Ask me again next anniversary. Bring the honey cake. And Tam—" her light flickered, brightened, steadied, like a lamp deciding to stay lit— "next year, bring the eels. I want to argue about the price."
