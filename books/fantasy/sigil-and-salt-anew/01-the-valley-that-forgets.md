## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Salt Valley has one rule, and it isn't written anywhere: forget.

Forget the shape your gift took. Forget the sound it made in your hands. Forget the night you were carried across the flats, salt crusted in your eyelashes, while someone who loved you whispered *don't look back, don't look back, it can't follow you if you don't look back.*

I was six when they carried me. I looked back anyway. Old habit. You'll see.

My name is Yara Villalobos, and for twenty years I have been the best net-mender in the valley, which is a little like being the tallest person in a ditch. The nets come to me torn—gull-tears, rock-tears, the occasional ambitious fish—and I sit on my porch with the brine drying white on my knuckles and I knot them whole again. It's good work. Honest work. Work that asks nothing of me except patience and fingers that remember what they're told.

The fingers are the problem, you understand. They remember other things too.

Look at them now, if you like. Long hands, salt-bleached, a mender's hands—except for the marks. They start at the wrists and climb to the elbows like ivy made of lightning: pale branching scars, silver-white against brown skin, Lichtenberg figures the physickers called them, back when there were physickers who'd seen such things and lived to gossip about it. Storm-scarred. The night the sky came down through me, it left its signature, the way anything does when it passes through you hard enough.

I wear sleeves. Even here. Even in the one place in Vessia where magic cannot cross the flats, where every soul past the salt line traded their gifts for safety—I wear sleeves. Because the scars aren't just scars. Anyone who knows what they're looking at knows what they mean. And what they mean is: *this one didn't lose their gift. This one buried it alive, and it's still breathing under the floorboards.*

So: sleeves. And silence. And twenty years of being nobody, which is a kind of art, and I confess I got good at it.

* * *

Mornings in the valley start with salt. You taste it before you open your eyes—it sits on your tongue all night, seeps up through the ground, hangs in the air off the flats like a held breath. The elders say the salt is a mercy. The salt is the reason the hunters never came. The salt is why we live.

What the elders don't say, because saying it would require admitting it, is that the salt is also a wall. And walls keep things out, but they keep things in, too. Keep things *down*, sometimes. Things buried alive.

I was on the porch with Halvard's trawl net spread across my knees when I saw the figure on the flats.

You have to understand what that means. Nobody walks the flats. The flats are three days of white nothing in every direction, cracked salt crust over poisoned earth, the kind of landscape that eats travelers and doesn't bother with a burial. The valley sits in its bowl of gray stone like an eye that closed itself on purpose. People arrive the way water arrives—rarely, and by accident, and usually half-dead.

This figure wasn't half-dead. This figure walked like she had somewhere to be and resented the distance.

She was tall, wrapped in a road-dusted traveling cloak the color of rust, and she moved across the salt with her chin up, which is either courage or stupidity, and in my experience the two travel together like old married couples. As she drew closer I saw she was young—younger than me, maybe twenty-five—with dark skin gone ashy with salt dust, close-cropped hair, and eyes that scanned the valley rim the way you'd scan a room for exits.

Then she stopped. Ten yards from the salt line—the invisible border where the flats end and our gray stone begins—and she stood there, swaying slightly, looking at the boundary like it was a door she'd been told was locked.

And then she looked at me.

I want to be honest with you, since we've come this far: I felt it before I understood it. A warmth behind my sternum, faint and wrong, like a coal someone had breathed on. My scarred forearms prickled under the sleeves. The air above the salt line shimmered—not heat-shimmer; heat-shimmer rises, this *bent*—and the woman smiled, small and tired, like she'd confirmed something.

"Fire-reader," I said, before I could stop myself. The words came out flat. "That's what you are."

Her eyebrows went up. "And you're a storm-caller wearing long sleeves in high summer." She said it without malice, almost gently, the way you'd note the weather. "Interesting valley you've got here. Everyone pretending so hard I could hear it from the flats."

"The salt blocks magic," I said. "It doesn't block sense. Some of us still have eyes."

"Mm." She stepped over the salt line—casually, one boot, then the other, like crossing a threshold into a tavern—and the shimmer died, and the coal behind my sternum went cold, and I hated how much I noticed both. "Odessa," she said. "Formerly of the Ember Court archives, currently of nowhere in particular. And you're the one they call the net-mender who never talks."

"They call me that?"

"They call everyone something reductive. It's how exiles comfort themselves." She pulled back her hood. Up close her face was sharp and handsome and exhausted, with a fresh burn scar along her jaw that hadn't finished healing. "Is there food? I've eaten nothing but salted lizard for nine days, and I want you to know that I say that as a woman who once ate a candle."

Despite myself—despite twenty years of practice at not engaging—I snorted. "The candle was better?"

"The candle had structure." She looked past me at the valley: the low stone houses, the nets strung between poles like the skeletons of enormous fish, the terraced gardens going green-gray up the slopes. Her face did something complicated. "It's smaller than I imagined."

"Everything is," I said, and went inside to get bread, because apparently twenty years of invisibility could be undone by a woman who'd eaten a candle.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about the valley elders, and I say this with love, or whatever the emotion is that lives next door to love and does its chores: they survived. That's the whole of their philosophy. They survived the purges of Aldric's grandfather, the binding riots of his father, the quiet disappearances of Aldric's early reign, and they did it by perfecting the art of being forgotten. Don't shine. Don't sing. Don't practice. Bury the gift deep and grieve it properly, like a death, and then plant beans over the grave and get on with your life.

It worked. I'm living proof it worked. Twenty years, no wardens, no hunters, no crown's men riding the flats. The salt holds. The forgetting holds.

Elder Maren is the eldest of them, a small dry woman like a stick of driftwood that learned to talk, and she was the one who carried me across the flats when I was six, and she is the one whose face I saw in my dreams for years afterward—set and sorry and certain. She found Odessa and me at my table, Odessa working through her third bowl of barley soup with the focus of a soldier, and Maren's face did the thing faces do here when a stranger arrives: hope and dread fighting to a draw.

"You crossed the flats alone," Maren said.

"I crossed the flats running," Odessa corrected. She set down her spoon. And then she said the thing that cracked the valley like a hairline crack in a bell—you don't see it, but from that moment, everything rings wrong.

"The crown announced a purge. Three weeks ago, from the capital. Every gifted person in Vessia is to be registered with the warden corps within the year. After registration—" she paused, and I watched her choose honesty over kindness, and I've thought since that the pause was the most generous thing she could have done, "—after registration comes binding. All of it. Every gift, everywhere, bound permanently. They're calling it the Peace of Aldric."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside a gull screamed, indifferent.

"That's not possible," Maren said finally. "Binding on that scale requires—" She stopped herself. We all heard what she didn't say: *requires dragon-fire, and the dragons are gone.* "The crown doesn't have the means."

"The crown has means you can't imagine," Odessa said. "I worked in the Ember Court archives. I read fire, elder—that's my gift, I read what flame remembers—and three months ago I was summoned to court to authenticate a document by candlelight. And I saw the king." Her voice dropped, and the burn scar on her jaw caught the light. "Aldric himself, in the flesh, holding audience. Except his shadow moved against the light. Against it. When the candles flickered left, his shadow leaned right. And when he passed the great hearth, the fire *flinched*."

"That's a story," Maren said. "A frightening story."

"It's a true one. I ran because of it. I've been running since, and the wardens are behind me—not chasing me specifically, though they'd like my head for deserting my registration summons, but sweeping. Valley by valley. And theirs—" she nodded around my kitchen, at the nets, the salt-stained walls, the whole careful architecture of forgetting, "—is on their list. Your salt won't matter. They have something now that lets iron cross the flats. Binding-iron that doesn't touch the ground."

I should tell you what happened inside me when she said that. Nothing visible. I sat very still and kept my face like a closed door, because that's the discipline, that's the whole practice. But underneath: the floorboards rattled. The thing I'd buried for twenty years turned over in its sleep and pressed its palms against the underside of my ribs, and I thought, absurdly, of the storm-scarred skin under my sleeves itching, itching, like a limb waking up.

"And what do you propose?" Maren asked, dry as ever. "That we run? Where? There is nowhere the crown's writ doesn't reach. That's rather the point of crowns."

"No," Odessa said. "I propose you stop pretending the wall will hold forever, and decide what you'll do the day it doesn't. Because it's coming. I've seen the muster rolls. Warden companies moving west along the Ember Road. Toward the salt flats." She looked at me then—directly, deliberately, the way nobody in this valley had looked at me in years, straight at the place where my secrets lived. "Toward here."

* * *

They convened the elders that evening, of course. The valley has one answer to every crisis, and it's a meeting.

I wasn't invited. Net-menders who never talk don't get invited. But my house sits below the meeting hall, and sound carries down the hill in the evening wind, and I sat on my porch with a net in my lap I wasn't mending, and I listened to them debate whether to shelter a fire-reader.

I'll spare you the full hour. It rhymed. *We survived by being small. She is loud. She is marked. The wardens follow marked people. Pity her, yes, from a distance, the way you pity a lighthouse—from far away, because it exists to draw storms.* Maren's voice rose last, tired and final: "The valley's safety is bought with a price we agreed to pay. We cannot unpay it for one stranger. The vote stands. She rests tonight and moves on at dawn."

And here is the thing I did, the thing I'm telling you about because everything after this grows from it:

I stood up.

I walked up the hill, in the dark, with my sleeves down and my heart going like a drum in a procession, and I pushed open the door of the meeting hall, and forty faces turned toward me—forty faces that had watched me grow up silent and safe and small—and I heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized because it had been twenty years since I'd used it for anything louder than *your net's ready*:

"She stays."

Silence. Elder Maren looked at me with those set-and-sorry-and-certain eyes, and said, gently, the way you speak to someone sleepwalking: "Yara. Child. You know what we agreed. We survive by—"

"I know what we agreed." My hands had curled into fists at my sides, and I could feel the scars pulling tight, and beneath them, deep down, something turning over again, restless, listening. "I've paid the price. Twenty years, I paid it. I wore the sleeves. I kept the silence. I mended every net in this valley and never once asked any of you what you gave up, because asking felt like breaking the rule." I looked around the hall, at the exiles, the buried gifts, the beans planted over graves. "But she's right. Something's coming that doesn't care about our rule. And when it gets here, I want to know that when I had one chance to be a person instead of a hiding place, I took it."

Nobody spoke. The lamp flames stood perfectly still, the way flames do when the air itself is paying attention.

Maren sighed, long and slow, like wind leaving a cave. "One night," she said. "One night, and she goes at dawn. And Yara—" her voice softened into something almost like grief, "—you should think hard about what you just dug up. Some things, once uncovered, don't go back under."

"Oh, I know," I said. Under my sleeves, my forearms were burning, and far away, out past the valley rim, past the flats, thunder rolled—a soft, distant sound, easy to dismiss, easy to forget.

Except I felt it answer me.

"One night," I agreed, and turned for the door, and behind me Odessa caught my eye across the hall, and she wasn't smiling anymore. She was looking at me the way you look at a fuse you've just realized is lit.

Outside, the stars over the salt flats were bright and cold and indifferent, and the wind coming off the white waste smelled—wrong. Faintly, impossibly, beneath the salt: smoke. And iron.

Something was already crossing the flats.
