## 2. Salt on the Tongue

The Salt Valley has a way of deciding things before anyone speaks. The elders call it consensus. I've lived here twenty-three years, and I can tell you it's mostly weather — a pressure change in the room, a shift in how people look at their boots — and by the time someone calls a meeting, the decision has already fallen like rain.

So when Maren Halev rapped her knuckles on the meetinghouse door at dusk and said, "Council tonight. All of you," I knew what the weather was before I stepped inside. Everyone did. Odessa stood at the far end of the room with her travel cloak still on, like a woman who'd already been told she'd be walking back out into the dark.

I want to be honest about myself here, because honesty is the one luxury the valley never taxed. For most of my life, my strategy for survival had been a simple one: be furniture. Be the third bench from the window, the person who mends nets and says "good morning" and never, ever makes eye contact with anything that looks like history. It worked. Twenty years of working. And then a woman with fire in her eyes sat down across from me on a salt flat and ruined everything by needing something from me that wasn't silence.

I took my usual place against the wall. Old habits.

* * *

The meetinghouse smelled of tallow smoke and dried fish and the particular staleness of a building where important things are decided badly on a regular schedule. Eleven elders sat in the crescent of chairs at the front. Maren presided because nobody had ever bothered to vote otherwise; she'd been holding the valley together since before I was born, and holding a thing together is its own kind of authority, even when your hands shake.

"Sit," she said to Odessa, not unkindly. Not kindly either.

Odessa sat. She had the good sense to keep her hood down — hiding would have looked like guilt — but she kept her gloved hands folded in her lap, which I understood was its own kind of statement. In the valley, gifted folk wore gloves the way penitents wear ash. You didn't have to. Nobody made you. But everyone knew what bare hands meant, and bare hands meant *look at me*, and looking was the first sin.

"The council has heard the newcomer's account," Maren began. "We thank her for it."

A murmur went around. *The newcomer.* Two days in the valley and she was already being filed under a word that meant *temporary*. We had a whole taxonomy of impermanence here: newcomers, passers-through, the ones who stayed a winter and left, the ones who stayed a winter and were asked to leave. Odessa was being sorted into the second-to-last drawer before anyone had opened it.

"She tells us the crown has proclaimed a hunt," Maren continued. Her voice was flat, the voice of a woman reading a bill of lading. "Registration of all gifted persons. Then... binding." She said the word the way you'd say *drowning*. "She tells us wardens are moving west along the Ember Road. She tells us they have iron that crosses salt."

That got everyone talking at once. Salt was theology here. Salt was the reason our grandparents had walked three hundred miles over dead grass to build houses out of driftwood and stubbornness. Magic couldn't cross the flats. That wasn't faith; it was physics, or whatever Vessia called physics when it wanted to feel holy about it. If iron could cross salt, then the floor of the world had a hole in it, and we were all standing over it.

"Quiet," said Tam Ossler, who ran the drying sheds and considered loudness his personal property.

Maren waited for quiet the way a rock waits for water. Then she turned to Odessa. "You understand our difficulty."

"I understand it better than you do," Odessa said.

Someone hissed. Odessa didn't flinch. I noticed that about her early: she took hits like a woman who'd been practicing.

"My gift is fire-reading," she went on, addressing the crescent of elders as if they were an audience rather than a jury. "I can see what flame has seen. Every hearth remembers. Every campfire is a ledger. If I sit by your fire tonight, I can tell you what happened beside it last week. Last year." She paused. "And if a warden's tracker-fire passes within ten miles of this valley, it will remember *me*, because fire always notices the one thing in the room that talks back. Yes. I'm a beacon. I'm the loudest candle in Vessia right now. I know exactly what I cost you."

"Then you understand," said Elder Prue, who taught the children their letters and had never once forgiven anyone anything, "that sheltering you is not charity. It is suicide with extra steps."

"That's one framing," Odessa said dryly.

"It's arithmetic," Prue snapped.

"And what does your arithmetic say happens when the wardens finish with the rest of us?" Odessa's voice didn't rise, which somehow made it worse, the way deep water is worse than waves. "Because I watched them work, in Corrow. A hedge-witch named Anselm. He grew tomatoes through frost. That was his whole crime — he put his palm on a seedling and it decided spring was worth trying. They bound him in the town square and the binding doesn't stop at the gift. I don't know how to explain it except — imagine someone reached into your chest and took out the part of you that decides mornings are worth getting up for. What's left walks around. Eats. Obeys. It isn't death. Death would be kinder and cheaper and faster."

Silence. The good kind, briefly. The kind where people are actually listening instead of just waiting.

Then Maren said, "We are sorry for him," in a tone that closed doors, and I realized the listening was over and the verdict was arriving on schedule, like the tide.

"We are a valley of two hundred souls," Maren went on. "Most of us gave up our gifts at the border. Some of us never had any. What we have is salt, and fish, and each other, and a very long tradition of surviving by being beneath notice. This woman brings a proclamation, a war, and a beacon lit on her head. The council votes: the newcomer departs at first light. Provisioned, escorted to the flats' edge, treated with every courtesy. But she departs."

Hands rose around the crescent. Ten of eleven, I counted. Prue raised hers so fast she nearly dislocated something. Even old Sef, who once argued for an hour about whether a drowned man should be buried facing the sea or the sky, voted yes without looking up.

Odessa nodded slowly, like a woman confirming a diagnosis she'd already read in her own chart. She started gathering her cloak.

And I heard myself say, "No."

* * *

Here is what you need to know about the sound of your own voice after years of not using it: it comes out wrong. Rusty. Too loud in the wrong places. My "no" landed in that room like a dropped plate, and forty faces turned toward the wall where the furniture had apparently developed opinions.

Maren squinted at me. "Yara?"

Yara Villalobos. Net-mender. Bench, third from window. The one whose parents walked in from the east with storm-scars on their hands and never spoke about why. I had spent my whole life making sure that name weighed nothing.

"No," I said again, and this time it came out steadier, which surprised both of us. "Vote's not done. One hand hasn't gone up yet." I lifted mine.

Prue actually laughed, short and ugly. "The net-mender wants a vote? On what qualification? Have you ever risked anything in your life, child, besides thread count?"

There it was. The question underneath every question I'd dodged for twenty years. And the honest answer was *no* — no, I hadn't, I'd built a life out of smallness the way other people build boats out of wreckage, and I'd called it wisdom, and I'd slept fine.

Except I hadn't slept fine. Not since Odessa had taken off her glove on the flats and shown me the burn-scarred lines across her palm, and told me fire remembered, and asked me — casually, terribly — whether storms remembered too. Because she'd seen my hands. Bare-handed, stupidly, I'd handed her a coil of rope, and she'd looked at the pale branching scars across my knuckles and wrists, the Lichtenberg flowers lightning leaves behind when it passes through a body and spares it, and she'd known. Of course she'd known. You don't get marked by a storm as a child and stay unremarkable forever. You just stay quiet long enough, in a valley built for quiet.

"You're right," I said to Prue, and her laugh died in her throat, because she'd expected a fight and gotten surrender, which is much more unsettling. "I haven't risked anything. I've been the best coward this valley ever produced. I hid my hands for twenty years. When the wardens came through eight years ago asking questions, I stood in a fish shed and let them search my neighbor Kellin's house while I knew — *knew* — there was a charm under his floorboard, and I said nothing, and they found it, and he walked to the flats alone in winter with nothing." My voice cracked on that last part, and I let it. Let them all hear it. "He waved to me from the salt. He thought I hadn't known. He *thanked* me."

Nobody moved. Somewhere a lamp guttered.

"So maybe my vote counts for nothing," I said. "But I'm casting it anyway, and here's what it says: sending her out is not survival. It's just choosing whose turn comes next. She's a beacon today. Tomorrow it's Bram's granddaughter who warms milk without a stove. Next week it's me, and every one of you knows it, because half of you helped my parents bury whatever was left of what they were when they crossed that line. Salt keeps magic out. It doesn't keep the crown out. Not anymore. Not if iron crosses the flats. The only question the crown has left for us is the order of our names."

Maren's face had gone very still — the stillness of a woman doing sums she didn't want finished. "You heard iron crossed the salt. You believe a stranger's tale that easily?"

"I believe her because she showed me what fire remembers," I said. "And fire remembers a king. Ask her. Go on — ask her what she saw at court before she ran."

Every head turned to Odessa. She hesitated — I saw her weigh it, the way you weigh whether to show someone the wound or just the bandage — and then she pulled off her other glove and held her bare palm toward the nearest lamp.

The flame leaned toward her. Actually leaned, like a plant toward sun, and in the heart of it images moved: a throne room, gold and shadow, and a figure on the throne wearing Aldric's crown and Aldric's face, and — I felt the whole room go cold at once — the king's shadow falling the *wrong way*, crawling up the wall behind him against the light, moving when he didn't, reaching for the chained figures kneeling below the dais the way a mouth reaches for food.

The lamp snapped back to normal. Odessa closed her fist and the memory was gone.

"That," she said quietly, "is what I ran from. And that is what is coming here, whether I walk out your gate tomorrow or not. Me leaving buys you a season. Maybe two. Then the hunters run out of easier valleys."

Silence again, but different now. The weather had changed. I could feel the pressure dropping.

Maren looked at me for a long moment. There was something complicated in her face — anger, yes, at being challenged in front of the whole valley by the quietest person in it, but underneath that, older and sadder, something that might have been relief. As if she'd been waiting twenty years for the furniture to speak and had begun to fear it never would.

"The vote stands open, then," she said finally. "All those for the newcomer departing at first light."

Nine hands. Prue's first, always Prue's.

"All those for sheltering her."

My hand. And then — a heartbeat later, a terrible, graceless, wonderful heartbeat — Tam Ossler's, the loud man from the drying sheds, rising halfway out of his chair like the arm itself embarrassed him. And then old Sef. And then a fourth hand near the door that belonged to a woman I only knew as Wren's mother, whose name I realized with a jolt of shame I had never learned despite twenty years of sharing a valley with her.

Four to nine. The motion failed. Odessa would be walking out at dawn, provisioned and escorted and treated with every courtesy, and I had burned twenty years of invisibility to buy her nothing.

But Maren wasn't looking at the count. She was looking at the four hands, and then at Odessa, and then — longest of all — at me.

"Provisioned," she repeated, softly, as though tasting the word. "Escorted to the flats' edge." She rubbed her eyes. "Who among us knows the flats' edge best? Who has walked the eastern salt since they were a child?"

I knew what she was doing. Everyone knew what she was doing. It was the oldest trick in the valley's book: give the troublemaker a job, and the job becomes the sentence.

"I do," I said.

"Mm." Maren settled back. "Then it's decided. Odessa departs at first light, provisioned. Yara will escort her to the line. Council adjourned."

Chairs scraped. People filed out past me without meeting my eyes, which in the valley is either contempt or gratitude, and I honestly couldn't tell which. Prue paused beside me long enough to say, "Twenty years of silence, and *this* is what you spend it on," which I chose to take as a compliment.

Odessa waited by the door until the room emptied. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road.

"You know what you just did," she said.

"Bought you a longer escort than you asked for."

"You know what I mean." She glanced at my hands, at the pale scars I'd stopped bothering to hide from her. "They'll ask you to come back alone. And you'll stand on that salt line, and the valley will be behind you, and the whole rest of the world will be in front of you, and you'll have to decide which direction your life points. Storms remember, Yara. So do I. Whatever you choose—" she shrugged into her cloak, "—don't take all night deciding. Wardens move fast when they smell rain coming."

Outside, the wind had come up from the east, off the flats, carrying salt and something else underneath it — a faint, wrong, metallic tang, like the air before lightning, though the sky was clear and full of stars.

I stood in the doorway a long time, watching the horizon, and tried to convince myself that the taste on the wind was only salt.

It wasn't.
