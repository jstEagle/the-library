## 3. What the Crown Announced

I have read fire my whole life, so let me tell you what fire says about a lie: it stutters.

Not much. A candle flame telling a falsehood flickers maybe half a heartbeat out of rhythm, the way a man's voice catches when his wife asks where he was last night. Most people never notice. But I spent eleven years in the capital reading the court's fires—chandeliers, hearths, the little oil lamps the clerks used to burn the midnight tallow—and I can tell you that the palace of King Aldric the Steadfast lied constantly, cheerfully, and in perfect unison, like a choir that had rehearsed.

That should have been my first clue. It wasn't. Palaces always lie. That's practically their function, like chimneys and smoke.

No, my first clue was the king's shadow.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and a woman who reads fire ought to know better than to skip to the ending. Endings are where the burning happens. You want to start with the kindling.

* * *

I came to Vessia's court the way most dangerous things come to courts: recommended by someone who wanted me where he could see me. Lord Casserly had found me in the provinces reading a noblewoman's sickroom fire—telling her daughter that the fever would break by morning, which it did—and instead of reporting me for the gift, which would have meant a cell and then worse, he hired me. *Better a flame-reader in your pocket than in your enemy's*, was his reasoning, delivered with the smugness of a man who believes he has invented an idea as old as extortion.

So for eleven years I stood at the edges of great rooms and watched the fires behave, and I told Lord Casserly which petitioners were lying, which ambassadors were afraid, which ministers had been drinking more than their offices could survive. I was very good at it. I want that on the record, because everything I'm about to tell you happened in a palace full of people who were also very good at their jobs, and the horror of it is not that they were fools. The horror is that they weren't.

The proclamation came down on a Tuesday. I remember because Tuesdays were the days the chancellery burned its ledgers—the ones with errors, the ones with debts forgiven, the ones with names that had become inconvenient—and the courtyard fire pit always smelled like scorched vellum and old money. The criers went out at dawn with the royal seal stamped in red wax the size of a dinner plate, and by midmorning every square in the capital had heard it read aloud.

I'll give you the gist, since the official version ran to four pages of legalese designed to make atrocity sound like accounting:

*By order of His Majesty King Aldric, second of his name, Protector of the Realms, all persons possessing unregistered gifts of the art shall present themselves at their nearest warden post within forty days to be counted and recorded. Registration is an act of loyalty. Those registered will receive the crown's protection.*

And then, in smaller words, the way you slip arsenic into wine:

*Those who do not register will be presumed enemies of the realm. Upon apprehension, such persons will be bound, that their gifts may no longer endanger the peace of Vessia.*

Bound. People in the squares heard that word and nodded along, because *bound* sounded tidy, administrative, like having your name entered in a ledger. What it actually means is this: there is iron—cold-forged, worked with rituals I still can't fully explain—that closes a gift the way a hand closes around a bird's throat. Not gently. Not reversibly, if the smiths did their work right, and the crown's smiths always did their work right. The gift doesn't die. It stays inside you, awake, pressed down under all that cold, forever. Every hour of every day for the rest of your life, you feel it pushing against the iron like a woman locked in a chest, screaming through the lid.

I know what that feels like now. We'll get to that too.

On the day of the proclamation, though, I didn't know yet. On the day of the proclamation I was standing in the gallery above the throne room, doing my job, watching the great hearthfires while the chancellor read the decree to the assembled court, and I noticed something so strange that at first I thought my gift had finally cracked from overuse, like an eye that starts seeing double.

Every fire in the room stuttered at once.

All of them. The twin hearths, the cressets by the doors, the little lamp beside the chancellor's lectern—all of them skipped a beat, together, precisely, the way soldiers turn their heads at a command. And then they resumed, and the chancellor kept reading about loyalty and protection, and three hundred nobles murmured approval, and I stood there with my skin crawling and thought: *what in the nine hells just answered them?*

Fires don't listen. That's not how it works. Fire doesn't understand language; it understands feeling, intention, the emotional weather of a room. A whole room full of people being frightened makes flames lean and gutter. A room full of joy makes them stand up bright. But a command—a specific spoken sentence landing on fire like a hand pressing it flat? No reader had ever reported such a thing. Fire isn't a dog. Fire doesn't heel.

Except apparently, in Aldric's throne room, it did.

* * *

Let me back up and tell you about the weeks before, because the proclamation didn't come from nowhere, and neither did the thing I saw in the king. Dread has a smell. It arrives ahead of the hunters the way weather arrives ahead of the storm—you feel the pressure change long before the first thunder. I felt it for a month and told myself I was imagining it, which is the national pastime of everyone who lives near power.

It started small. Wardens in the capital began carrying new irons—not the old registration brands, which were basically heated stamps and an insult, but something heavier, blacker, that I'd never seen before. I read one once, from across a courtyard. Fire won't tell you what metal is, exactly, but it tells you how metal *feels* to it, the way you can tell silk from rope by how it moves. And this iron felt wrong the way a dead thing feels wrong. The flame near it bent away. Bent *away*. Fire leans toward almost everything—it's the neediest element, hungry for fuel, for air, for attention—but it leaned away from those irons like a child avoiding a certain uncle.

Then the gifted servants started disappearing. Not arrested—arrests are public, arrests are theater; the crown loves theater. Just... gone. The kitchen boy who could keep cream from curdling with a touch. The laundress whose whites came out impossibly bright. A chanter in the cathedral choir whose voice could quiet grief, whom half the grieving widows of the court relied upon and nobody officially knew about. One morning they were there. The next morning their quarters were empty and their names had been struck from the household rolls, and when I asked a friendly clerk about it she said, "Reassigned," in the flat voice of someone who has learned not to wonder.

Lord Casserly told me not to ask questions. "There's a program," he said. "His Majesty's concern for public safety. Uncontrolled talents, accidents, the Kellam bridge disaster last spring." The Kellam bridge disaster had been real—a hedge-witch with more weather-sense than sense had called up a gust to save a falling mason and dropped a cart team into the river instead. Four dead. The broadsheets had milked it for a month. I understood the logic. Fear needs only one bad example to grow on, the way mold needs one damp corner.

"Are they being bound?" I asked him. "The reassigned ones?"

He looked at me for a moment too long, and his fireplace—this beautiful marble confection he lit even in summer, purely for show, purely so I'd have something to read—guttered sideways, guilty as a schoolboy.

"You're paid to watch fires, Mistress Odessa," he said, "not to have opinions."

Which is true, and I want to be fair to myself here: I was comfortable. Eleven years of comfort. A warm room, a wage, a position slightly above suspicion because I was useful. When you live inside a machine that grinds people, and the machine lets you ride on top, you find marvelous reasons why the grinding is necessary, or regrettable but unavoidable, or possibly not even happening. I had a whole drawer of reasons. I wore them out one at a time until the day of the proclamation, and then I ran out of drawers entirely.

* * *

Now. The king.

I saw Aldric face-to-face exactly twice in eleven years, which is normal—even dukes went years between audiences. Both times were in the great hall during formal presentations, both times from sixty feet away, both times with a hundred candelabra between us to read. And both times, the same wrongness.

Here's what fire told me about King Aldric the Steadfast: nothing.

You have to understand how strange that is. Fire tells you *everything* about everyone. It cannot help itself. A person walks into a room and every flame in it lights up with their interior weather—their fear, their hunger, their boredom, the argument they had at breakfast sitting in them like swallowed stones. Kings are the worst, or the best, depending on your taste: kings burn with themselves, all that self-regard feeding every candle in the room. In eleven years I read ten thousand souls, and every single one of them arrived in the fire loud as a struck bell.

Except him. The king arrived in the fire silent. The flames near him didn't lean toward or away, didn't brighten or dim, didn't do anything at all. They behaved toward Aldric the way they behaved toward furniture. Toward *stone*.

The first time, I told myself the distance was the problem, or the crowd, or my own distraction. The second time, a year later, I positioned myself deliberately by the western cressets, the closest fire to the dais, and I watched, and I got the same silence—and something else. Something I've turned over ten thousand times since, trying to make it mean something kinder than what it means.

His shadow moved against the light.

It was late afternoon, and the sun through the great eastern windows threw the court's shadows long across the floor, all of them leaning west like wheat in wind. Everyone's shadow leaned west. The king's leaned east. Not dramatically—if you hadn't been looking, really *looking*, you'd have called it a trick of the pillars. But I had been looking, and I watched his shadow lean east, against the light, slowly, the way a man leans to look around a corner. And then it stopped, and returned to obedience, and King Aldric the Steadfast smiled at the ambassador from Sorrel and said something gracious, and the hall applauded, and every fire in the room stayed silent as a held breath.

I have read fire my whole life. Fire told me the truth about that room: whatever sat on the throne wearing Aldric's face, it cast a shadow that moved on its own, and it made no impression on any flame, and every fire in the palace obeyed its voice without being asked.

A living king burns. Whatever that was, it did not burn. It was a cold spot in the shape of a man, and the cold spot was hungry—I know that sounds mad, since how does a fire-reader know a thing is hungry when the fire says nothing about it? But absence has texture. Silence after music has a shape. And that silence in the throne room had the exact shape of an open mouth.

* * *

After the proclamation, things moved fast, the way rot moves fast once it shows at the surface.

Forty days to register. Within a week, warden posts had queues around the block—mostly the desperate and the dim, folk whose gifts were small enough that they'd convinced themselves registration meant safety. The tailor who could find anyone's measurements with a glance. The farrier whose horses never went lame. They queued up, gave their names, got their marks in the ledger, and were told, thank you kindly, you'll be summoned for processing.

Nobody explained processing. When the first registered families began to vanish from their homes—quietly, at night, carts with canvas covers—the broadsheets printed nothing, and the town criers announced grain prices, and the city performed the miracle of collective not-seeing that cities do better than any gifted trick ever invented.

I did my own arithmetic. My gift was known to exactly one powerful man, and that man had looked guilty when I asked about the bindings. Guilt in a courtier is not conscience; guilt is a man deciding whether you're worth more sold or protected. Lord Casserly's fire had been guttering for weeks—short, nervous, ashamed. There was only one direction that arithmetic ran.

So I did what our family had always done, the thing the Villalobos line had done for generations, the thing every clever coward in Vessia did when the walls closed in: I ran for the Salt Valley.

I want to defend that choice, even now. Even knowing everything. The Salt Valley is the one place in the kingdom magic cannot follow—the salt flats kill it at the border, ground every gift to nothing, make the gifted as plain as anyone else. For two hundred years it's been where our kind went to disappear. Trade your gift for your life. Live plain. Mend nets. Forget. The elders call it mercy. Some nights I've called it that too.

Other nights I've called it what it is: a tomb with good lighting, where we bury ourselves politely and call the burial peace.

But I'll say this for the valley: it takes you in without asking what you were. So I took what gold I could carry, left Lord Casserly a note thanking him for eleven years of employment—he'd read it twice and then burn it, and his fire would tell me nothing ever again—and I walked out of the capital on a grey morning with the proclamation nailed to every gate behind me, red wax seals gleaming like wounds.

Forty days. That's what the crown had given every gifted soul in Vessia: forty days to walk into a warden post and put their necks on the ledger, or run, or hide, or die. And somewhere in that machinery of counting, I knew—fire had told me, in its wordless way, in the bent flames and the silent throne room and the shadows leaning wrong—knew that the count wasn't the point. Crowns don't count what they intend to keep. They count what they intend to *take*.

Something on that throne was eating, and the proclamation was a menu.

* * *

The road north took nineteen days, and I'll spare you most of it except for the part that matters, the part I carry like a stone in my boot.

Outside the market town of Harrow's Ford, on the twelfth day, I passed a warden convoy going south toward the capital. Cages on wheels. Not prisoner carts—the open kind, the shameful kind—but covered wagons with ventilation slits, the way you transport things you don't want seen and don't want dead. At least not yet.

As the wagons passed, a gap in the canvas slid sideways—an inch, no more—and a hand came through. Small. A child's hand, fingers spread against the daylight.

And the lantern hanging from the driver's bench *leaped*. Leaped toward that hand like a dog toward its master, flame stretching, straining, desperate—and I felt it, plain as speech, what the fire felt through the little one's touch: terror, and underneath the terror something so bright and stubborn and alive that the flame couldn't help reaching for it, the way flowers can't help turning toward the only window.

Then the canvas fell shut, the flame sank back down like a scolded animal, and the wagons rolled on south toward the throne, toward the cold spot wearing a dead king's smile, toward whatever mouth was waiting to be fed.

I stood in the road until the dust settled. Then I turned north again and walked, faster than before, toward the salt flats, toward the one place in Vessia where nothing could follow me—not wardens, not iron, not the thing on the throne.

Toward safety. Toward forgetting. Toward the tomb with good lighting.

I told myself I'd be safe there. I told myself the flats would swallow my gift the way they swallowed everyone's, and I'd mend nets, and I'd keep my head down, and the storm passing over the world beyond the salt would pass me by, the way storms always had.

I believed it, too, right up until the night I limped into the valley, half-dead and hollowed out, and the first face that met mine across a cookfire belonged to a kid with storm-scarred hands and eyes like someone who'd been hiding so long they'd forgotten they were lost.

Yara Villalobos. Remember the name. Everyone else soon would.

Because here's what the fire never managed to tell me, all those years in all those palaces, reading all those lies: sometimes the smallest flame in the coldest room is the one that ends up setting the sky on fire. And sometimes dread doesn't arrive ahead of the hunters at all.

Sometimes it arrives ahead of the *answer*.
