## 4. Riders on the Flats

The morning the wardens came, I was mending a net that had never once caught anything worth keeping.

That's not an insult to the net. That's just Salt Valley. The flats give up brine shrimp and bitter greens and not much else, and every one of us who fled here learned to call that enough. I'd gotten good at it—good at calling things enough. Twenty years of practice. You could say it was my only gift.

I sat on the dock at Brinehole with the net in my lap and my hands doing their work without me, which is how I liked my hands best. Busy hands don't shake. And these days they'd been shaking more than usual, ever since Odessa arrived with her talk of proclamations and registration and *binding*, whatever that meant, and her story about a king whose shadow moved against the light. The elders had voted to bar her from the common hall but not from the valley itself—a compromise nobody loved and everybody pretended was wisdom—and so she'd been sleeping in old Maret's drying shed, and I'd been bringing her bread, and we'd been talking at night about things I had spent two decades not talking about.

Like what binding meant. Like why my palms itched when storms gathered over the mountains, far off where weather couldn't follow us down into the flats.

I was thinking about that itching when I heard the hoofbeats.

Here is something you should understand about salt: nothing crosses it quietly. The flats crunch underfoot like a crust of old bread. Every exile learns to read the sound of footsteps on the pan—the soft shuffle of a neighbor, the heavier tread of a cart, the careful picking way of a heron hunting shrimp. Hoofbeats on salt are wrong the way a scream is wrong. Horses hate the pan. Their hooves slide, they balk, they throw riders who try to force them across, because horses know something we all pretend we've forgotten: the salt line is not a border. It's a wound in the world, and everything with sense avoids it.

These hoofbeats didn't slow at the line.

I looked up. The sky over the eastern flats was the flat white of late autumn, no color in it anywhere, and against that white came six riders in a wedge formation, moving at a canter across ground that should have turned them back. Their armor drank the light instead of throwing it. Even at that distance I could see the iron at their throats and wrists—bands of it, dull grey, and around each band a shimmer like heat-haze, except the haze bent the wrong direction.

My hands stopped working before my head did. The needle dropped from my fingers and rolled between the planks.

*Binding-iron.* Odessa had described it two nights ago, turning her cup and cup in circles without drinking. Iron worked by binder-craft, she said, iron that carried its purpose inside itself the way a seed carries a tree. And the salt kept out magic—kept out anything that lived by magic—but iron like that wasn't magic anymore. It was a tool. A dead thing. And salt has never once stopped a dead thing from being carried.

"They found a way," I said out loud, stupidly, to nobody. To the net. To twenty years of believing the flats would hold.

The riders reached the shore road and did not stop. They fanned out along the waterline with the ease of people following a map drawn in advance, and the lead rider raised one gauntleted hand, and the air around the whole company went strange—bent slightly inward, like the world wincing. Then the shimmer spread. I watched it crawl outward from their bands along the wet sand, a lattice of grey light stitching itself across the shore, and where it passed, the salt hissed.

Not steamed. *Hissed.* Like something being told to be quiet.

I ran. I'm not proud of how fast or how gracelessly; I ran up from Brinehole toward the commons, shouting before I knew I was shouting, and the word I shouted was Odessa's name, because she was the one who'd seen this coming and none of us had listened, and there is a particular flavor of shame in running to tell someone *you were right* while your lungs burn.

She met me halfway, barefoot in the cold, hair unbound, eyes already fixed on the east. Fire-readers see things early, she says—or said, once, before she learned to keep her seeing quiet—and whatever she saw in the horizon made her go very still.

"How many?" she asked.

"Six. Maybe support behind them."

"Then it's a marking run, not a culling." She started walking, fast, toward the elders' hall. "They'll want names first. Lists. They always start with lists." She glanced back at me. "Yara. Whatever happens—you are nobody. Say it until you believe it."

But here's the problem with spending twenty years being nobody: you get very good at disappearing, and very bad at standing still while other people are afraid.

* * *

By midmorning the whole valley had gathered in the commons, which was exactly what we should not have done and exactly what frightened people always do. Two hundred exiles standing in a silent clump while six riders walked their horses slowly through us—not through the crowd, through our lives. They looked at the drying racks. At the shrine with its salt-crusted offering stones. At Maret's shed, where Odessa stood at the edge of the crowd with her hood up and her hands hidden.

The lead warden dismounted. Up close, the iron bands were worse: black metal shot through with veins of something pale that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. He wore the crown's sigil stamped into a tabard gone grey with road dust, and he had the manner of a man reading a shopping list.

"By decree of the Crown of Vessia," he read, from an actual paper scroll, as though the valley might have somehow missed the news, "all persons possessed of unregistered gift shall present themselves for binding within the hour. Refusal will be construed as sedition." He rolled the scroll up. "You have been counted as you stand. There are two hundred and eleven of you."

A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through wheat.

"Two hundred and twelve," said the warden, and smiled thinly, and looked directly at me. "We counted twice."

I want to say I felt fear. Fear would have been clean. What I felt was worse—it was recognition, the horrible click of a lock I hadn't known was mine. Because the itch in my palms had been getting louder all morning, since the moment the shimmer crossed the salt, and now, standing thirty feet from binding-iron, it wasn't an itch anymore. It was a hum. Deep in the bones of my hands, in the old scars I'd never explained to anyone, something was waking up and stretching after twenty years of sleep, and it was *glad.*

"You," the warden said, still looking at me. "Come forward."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. And I understood, with terrible clarity, that he didn't know my name—he was fishing, watching faces, listening for the crackle of suppressed power the way you'd listen for a fire behind a wall. But I also understood that if I stepped forward, everyone in this valley would learn in one afternoon what I'd spent half my life hiding, and if I didn't—

If I didn't, he'd start pulling people out of the crowd until someone cracked.

"I said—" he began.

"She's a net-mender," said old Maret, stepping out in front of me with the courage of the very old, who have less left to lose and know exactly how much. "Half-blind and arthritic. If you want her, you can carry her."

The warden looked at Maret the way you'd look at a stain. His hand drifted to the band at his wrist, and the pale veins in the iron brightened, and the air between him and Maret began to bend.

And that—that—is the moment I lost control.

Later, Odessa would tell me it happens like that for everyone eventually. Not at the moment of greatest danger to yourself, but the moment you watch danger reach for someone else. Suppression isn't strength, she said. It's a dam, and dams don't care whose village floods.

The hum in my hands became a roar. I felt it climb my arms like cold water running backward, up through my shoulders, into my chest, and the sky—which had been flat white all morning, dead, salted, empty—*answered.* I felt it answer. That's the part I can't explain properly, the part that still wakes me at night: it wasn't lightning coming down. It was the sky reaching *up*, called by something in me that had been starving for twenty years and recognized its own food.

The clouds tore open above the commons.

The bolt that came down didn't strike the warden. Thank every saltless god for small mercies—it struck the ground between him and Maret, close enough that the shockwave knocked a dozen people off their feet and split the packed earth in a white-hot seam. Thunder detonated overhead with a crack like the sky snapping shut, and every bird on the flats went up at once, a black sheet of them, and the horses screamed and broke, three of the six bolting east across the pan with their riders clinging on.

In the ringing silence after, I stood with my arms still raised, though I didn't remember raising them, and ozone stank in my nostrils, and my hands—

My hands were glowing. Faintly, along the old scars, a blue-white tracery like veins of lightning trapped under the skin, pulsing in time with my heartbeat. Visible from across the commons. Visible from the saddle.

Every eye in the valley was on me. Every eye among the wardens too.

The lead warden picked himself up off the ground with the unhurried care of a man who has been knocked down before and expects to be again. He studied me for a long moment—my face, my hands, the smoking seam in the earth—and I watched him do arithmetic. Six riders, one storm-caller, two hundred witnesses. Whatever sum he arrived at, he didn't like it.

"Storm-touched," he said softly. Almost reverently. "Twenty years we thought your line was ash."

He remounted. The remaining riders closed around him, and he looked down at me from the saddle with something that wasn't anger. It was interest. Interest is so much worse.

"The crown will want to know your name," he said.

"It doesn't," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "There's nothing left up there to want anything."

Something flickered across his face at that—something that might have been doubt, quickly ironed away. He turned his horse. "Restitch your nets, valley folk. We'll be back with more than six."

They rode east, and the grey lattice faded from the sand behind them, and the salt stopped hissing, and the sky closed up again like a healing wound, leaving nothing behind but a smell of rain that would never fall here.

* * *

Afterward, I couldn't stop shaking.

Not from fear—I kept waiting for the fear and it wouldn't come. My body shook the way a struck bell shakes, still ringing with the thing that had passed through it. Odessa found me sitting on the dock at Brinehole, back where I'd started, the mended net abandoned beside me. She sat down without asking and handed me a flask of plum brandy that tasted like it had been distilled from regret.

"Well," she said. "That's one way to stay anonymous."

"Don't."

"Sorry." She wasn't, particularly. She watched the water for a while. "How does it feel?"

I thought about lying to her. I thought about saying *terrible*, *frightening*, *I want it gone*—the correct answers, the answers that would let me stay in the valley, maybe, if the elders were feeling generous, if the wardens' list didn't already have my description on it.

Instead I heard myself say: "*Loud.* Twenty years I've been holding it down and I thought the silence was peace. It wasn't. It was just quiet the way a held breath is quiet." I flexed my fingers. The faint glow was gone now, but underneath the skin, the hum remained, patient as tide. "And then it woke up, and it was like—like a limb waking up. Pins and needles. Hurting because it's alive again."

Odessa nodded slowly. "That's the truest thing anyone's said in this valley in years. Probably why nobody says it."

Down the shoreline, I could see the elders gathering outside the hall, and even at this distance I could read the shape of their meeting in the set of their shoulders. I'd grown up reading that shape. Anger, fear, and the oldest instinct of exile communities everywhere: cut away the part that draws the wolves.

"They're going to ask you to leave," Odessa said.

"I know."

"And?"

I looked at my hands. Ordinary hands, work-worn, scarred, mended ten thousand nets with them. Except now I knew what lived in them, and knowing is the one thing you can't unknow.

"I spent my whole life being quiet," I said, "so that other people could feel safe. And today a man with iron on his wrists nearly dragged Maret into the street anyway. So no—I'm done paying rent in silence for a protection that doesn't work." I stood up. My legs held. "If they ask me to leave, I'll go. But I'll go because I choose to. There's a difference, and I intend to make everyone here choke on it."

Odessa grinned, quick and sharp, and for the first time since she'd limped into our valley half-starved with news of the hunt, she looked like someone with a destination rather than a past. "Then pack light," she said. "Because I know where the king keeps his lies, Yara Villalobos, and I think it's time somebody paid him a visit."

Behind us, the elders' door opened.
