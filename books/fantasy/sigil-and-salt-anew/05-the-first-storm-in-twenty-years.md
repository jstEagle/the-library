## 5. The First Storm in Twenty Years

I woke on my back in the shallows of the salt pan with rain hitting my face like a personal grudge.

That was the first impossible thing. The second was the sky above me, which had been a flat white nothing for twenty years and was now a churning bruise of cloud, split open down the middle by a bolt of lightning that I felt in my teeth before I heard it. The third impossible thing was that the wardens were running.

Not walking briskly. Not withdrawing in good order. Running — three of them, four, their binding-iron chains clanking against their armor as they scrambled back toward the salt line like men who had remembered an urgent appointment elsewhere. The artifact they'd carried across the flats lay abandoned in the mud, smoking faintly, its iron rings blackened.

I sat up. My hands were shaking. Not from cold.

They were glowing. Faintly, the way coals glow after the fire's gone to bed — pale blue light threading up between my knuckles, tracing the old storm-scars along my wrists like ink following a woodgrain. And every hair on my arms was standing straight up, and the air tasted of copper and ozone, and somewhere overhead the thunder was still rolling around the valley walls like a dog looking for someone to bite.

"Yara." Odessa's voice, close. She crouched beside me in the flooded salt grass, her dark hair plastered to her face, her eyes very wide. "Yara, look at me."

"I'm looking at you."

"Do you know what you just did?"

I looked at the sky. At the fleeing wardens. At old Tam Wicker, standing hip-deep in what used to be his net-mending pond, holding his hat to his chest with an expression I'd never seen him wear before — not fear exactly, but something adjacent to it, something that had fear for a cousin.

"No," I said honestly. "But I think I'd like to learn."

* * *

Here is what happened, as best anyone could piece it together afterward, because I missed most of it myself.

The wardens came over the salt line at dusk, six of them on horses that wore iron shoes, carrying a device Perrin-the-binder would later describe to me in loving technical detail: a lattice of binding-iron wound through with silver thread, which let them carry the null-metal across ground where it should have been poison to them. The valley's one protection, cracked open by a clever man's invention. They rode into the settlement with a list. Names, gathered from informants, from intercepted letters, from the careful records the crown keeps of everyone who ever fled into exile. Odessa's name was at the top. Mine, apparently, was not far behind — because the crown does not forget who was born gifted, even if the gift has never once shown itself, even if the child in question has spent two decades being aggressively, exhaustingly ordinary.

They found Odessa mending a kettle in the common house. They put the binding-iron collar on her before she could finish standing up. Bramble — no, not Bramble yet, that comes later — anyway. They collared her, and they started reading names off their list, and the elders did nothing, because the elders have spent forty years perfecting the art of doing nothing, and they are very, very good at it.

And I — I was standing in the doorway of the net shed with a coil of rope in my hands, watching them put iron on a woman who had argued for sheltering me, and something in my chest simply said *no*.

Not in words. It didn't feel like a decision. It felt like a dam failing. Twenty years of holding it down — every swallowed thundercloud, every summer storm I'd watched from under the eaves and refused to feel, every time I'd pressed my palms flat against my thighs when the wind picked up and told myself *not here, not here, magic can't cross the salt* — all of it went out of me at once, upward, and the sky answered.

The first bolt took the warden captain's horse out from under him. I'm told he lived. The second hit the binding-iron device itself, which is apparently either extremely brave or extremely stupid of lightning, depending on which binder you ask, and shattered it into scrap. The third went straight up, a column of white fire punching through the clouds, and after that the storm simply *arrived* — as if it had been waiting outside the valley walls for twenty years, patient as a debt, and now the door was open.

Then I woke up in the shallows.

* * *

By full dark, the wardens were gone. All six, retreating east across the flats with their dead horse and their broken toy, and the rain was still falling, soft now, almost apologetic, and the whole valley stood in the mud and stared at a sky none of them had seen do anything more dramatic than haze since before I was born.

Odessa sat beside me on the step of the net shed with a blanket around her shoulders and the red welt of the binding-iron still printed on her throat. She hadn't said much since pulling me out of the water. Neither had anyone else. But I could feel the valley looking at me. Forty pairs of eyes, and every single one of them had changed its mind about something in the last hour.

"You knew," I said finally. "When you arrived. You said the hunters would come eventually. You didn't say I was on any list."

"I didn't know your name was on it." She turned the blanket's edge in her fingers. "I knew what you were, though. From the moment I saw your hands. Fire reads people, Yara, and yours read like a struck bell. I thought maybe you already knew and were choosing silence. Some exiles do."

"I wasn't choosing anything. I was hiding so well I hid from myself."

"That," she said, "is the most honest sentence anyone's said to me since I left the capital."

Old Maren Voss came up the path then, lantern held high, her face set in the particular arrangement she reserved for funerals and tax disputes. Behind her came the rest of the elder council, seven of them, arranged in a half-moon like a jury that had already deliberated.

Maren stopped three paces away. She looked at me for a long time. When I was nine years old she'd taught me to gut fish; when I was fourteen she'd signed the paper that kept me registered as ungifted; when I was nineteen she'd told me, gently, that some doors are better left unopened, and I had believed her for eleven years.

"Yara," she said. "You saved us tonight. Understand that we know it. Every soul in this valley owes you their safety, and we will not forget it."

"But," I said.

"But the wardens saw your face. They took your name. In three days' time there will be thirty of them, and they will not come with one broken trinket — they will come with enough binding-iron to collar this entire valley, and they will burn whatever stands between them and you." Her voice didn't waver. That was the worst part. Maren Voss had never once in her life raised her voice, because she had never needed to. "The valley survives because nothing in it shines. You are shining, child. You cannot stay."

The rain dripped off the eaves. Somewhere a shutter banged. I noticed, distantly, that my hands had stopped glowing, and that I missed it, and that missing it frightened me more than the glowing had.

"I've lived here my whole life," I said. "This is the place that doesn't want me. And now it doesn't want me either."

"It isn't about wanting—"

"No," I said, and stood up, and discovered I was steadier on my feet than I had any right to be. "It's about survival. I understand survival. I've been doing it since I was born. Go on, then. Say the rest of it."

Nobody said the rest of it. They didn't need to. The half-moon of elders opened slightly, the way a door opens, and that was the verdict delivered without a word: the path out of the valley was open, and I was expected to take it, and everyone would feel terrible about it, and everyone would sleep fine anyway.

I walked past them. My legs worked better than expected. At the edge of the lantern light I stopped, because Odessa had risen too, and she was standing there with her blanket and her welted throat and an expression I couldn't read by firelight.

"Well," I said. "You got what you wanted. I'm leaving the valley."

"I didn't want this."

"No. But you wanted me to stop hiding, and congratulations, mission accomplished. I'm going to go hide somewhere else, only louder."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Where will you go?"

"How should I know? East, probably. Away. There's a lot of away."

"There is," she agreed. "Though most of it leads to the capital eventually, if you walk long enough. Roads are funny that way. Everything drains downhill toward the crown." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, though there was no one near enough to hear. "You asked me once why I really came here. Not for shelter. I'll tell you on the road, if you want. It concerns the king. What I saw at court. Why the proclamation frightens me more than the hunters do."

"The king is a story," I said. "A face on coins. Nobody's seen him in years."

"Exactly," said Odessa, and the way she said it made the word *exactly* sound like a warning bell.

* * *

I packed that night, or rather I failed to pack, because everything I owned fit in one canvas bag and had always fit in one canvas bag, and there is a particular kind of grief in discovering that twenty years of a life weighs less than eight pounds.

I sat on my cot until the candle burned low, listening to the rain — real rain, actual rain, the first I'd heard since childhood — and tried to feel angry at the elders, and couldn't quite manage it. That was the humiliating truth of it. They weren't wrong. If I stayed, they'd die for me, and I'd watched enough of this valley's history to know how its sacrifices went: quietly, gratefully, and completely. Maren Voss had gutted a thousand fish beside me and lied to crown clerks for me and done everything short of bleeding for me, and her asking me to leave was the kindest arithmetic she knew how to do.

So instead of anger I felt something worse. I felt *seen*. Twenty years of being nobody, of being the quiet one who mended nets and kept her head down and never once gave the sky a reason to notice her — and in one evening I had become the loudest thing this valley had produced in a generation. The wardens had my name. Whatever came next would come looking for *Yara Villalobos*, specifically, personally, and no amount of salt would hide me again, because I had proven that the hiding was always a choice, and I had chosen otherwise, spectacularly, in front of witnesses.

My hands ached. Not the scars — deeper than the scars, down in the bones, like the memory of a limb waking up. All night the ache pulsed in time with the distant thunder, and I lay there understanding, finally, that the thunder was answering me. That it had always been answering me. That the sky over the Salt Valley had spent twenty years pressing its ear against a door.

Near dawn I gave up on sleep, shouldered my eight pounds of a life, and walked out into the wet grey morning. Odessa was waiting at the eastern edge of the settlement, pack on her back, no blanket now, the collar-welt faded to a yellow smudge on her throat. She'd clearly been waiting a while. She had the look of someone who had learned to wait professionally.

"You don't have to come," I said. "You're free here. Well. As free as anywhere."

"The wardens have my name too, remember? Top of the list." She fell into step beside me, and we started east together, boots squelching in the saturated salt grass. "Besides. You're going to need help. You have a gift the size of a cathedral and the training of a housecat. Left alone, you'll level a province by midsummer."

"That's insulting."

"It's meant to be. Insult keeps people humble, and humility keeps people alive." We walked a while in silence. The flats spread out ahead of us, silver-grey under the clearing sky, and beyond them, faint on the horizon, the green line of the world I had never once entered. "There's a road," she said. "Runs from the coast pass all the way to Vessa City. Three weeks' walk, if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

She glanced sideways at me, and this time she did smile, small and sharp. "Then I suspect we'll manage. Weather and I have recently become acquainted with someone influential."

I laughed. It surprised me — a rough, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. And beneath the laugh, beneath the grief sitting in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed, something else was stirring. Not comfort. Purpose, maybe. Or its rough draft.

Behind us the valley shrank into the mist, my whole life dissolving into grey. Ahead of us the world waited, wide and dangerous and full of hunters who knew my name.

"So tell me," I said, as the salt flats took our weight and the last of the valley's lanterns blinked out behind us. "About the king. Tell me what you saw."

Odessa was quiet for three steps. Four. Five.

"What I saw," she said, "was a shadow that moved against the light. And Yara — I don't think Aldric has been alive for years."
