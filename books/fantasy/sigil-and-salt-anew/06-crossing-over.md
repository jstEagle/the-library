## 6. Crossing Over

The salt line looked exactly the same from the wrong side of it.

I'd spent my whole life on the eastern edge of the flats, where the white crust gave way to grey scrub and the valley's crooked fence posts, and I had never once thought about what lay beyond except as an abstraction—*out there*, a direction rather than a place. Now I stood at the boundary with a pack on my back, a fire-reader at my shoulder, and the entire population of Salt Hollow watching me from a respectful distance, the way you watch someone wade into water that has drowned people before.

"You don't have to do this today," Odessa said. "We could walk the perimeter for an hour first. Let your body remember it has edges."

"My body remembers fine." My hands did not. They hung at my sides like two strangers who had followed me out of habit.

Behind us, old Marta called out—not unkindly—"The storm-caller leaves! Mind the rain doesn't follow you home!" A few nervous laughs. Twenty years ago that would have been a blessing. Yesterday it had been the words they used to send me off, because lightning had split the sky over their heads and written my name in fire across the wardens' retreating backs, and the elders had decided that a marked person was a lit candle in a dry barn.

I raised one hand without turning around. It felt like the right amount of farewell for people who had fed me and feared me in equal measure since childhood.

"Walk," Odessa said gently. "Don't run. Running is a kind of flinching."

"I wasn't going to run."

"You were absolutely going to run."

She was right, which was irritating. I took a step instead, then another, my boots crunching through the salt crust, and the elders' faces shrank behind me into smudges of brown and grey against the white. The flats stretched ahead, blinding under the midmorning sun, featureless except for the occasional bleached bone of some animal that had misjudged the crossing generations ago. Nothing grew here. Nothing *could* grow here—that was the point. Salt was the valley's wall and its promise: *magic cannot cross, magic cannot follow, magic cannot find you.*

It had never occurred to me, in twenty-odd years of living inside that promise, to ask what else it kept out.

Because here was the thing nobody in Salt Hollow said aloud, though every exile knew it in their bones: the salt didn't just block magic from entering. It blocked it from *reaching*. And what lived inside a gifted person didn't stop existing just because it couldn't get through. It waited. It pooled. It pressed against the inside of your skin for years, decades, a lifetime, patient as water finding the crack in a dam, and you learned not to notice the pressure the way you learn not to notice your own heartbeat.

I noticed it now.

Three steps past where the elders could still see us clearly, something shifted in my chest—a sensation like a held breath finally beginning to release, except the breath had been held for so long that releasing it hurt. I stopped walking.

"Odessa."

"I know." She hadn't stopped; she'd turned to face me, walking backward now, watching my face with the particular attention she gave to fires. "Keep moving. Slowly. Tell me what you feel."

"Like—" I flexed my fingers. They tingled. All of them, down to the nail beds, a prickling warmth spreading from my palms up my forearms like sunburn arriving all at once. "Like my hands fell asleep when I was six and someone forgot to tell them we were awake again."

"That's accurate," she said. "Terrifyingly accurate. Keep talking. Describe it. Naming things gives you handles to hold them by."

"It's—" The prickling climbed my shoulders, my throat, spread across my scalp until my whole body hummed like a struck bell. "It's everywhere. Under my skin. Like there's a second skin underneath and it's *waking up*."

"Good. That's good. Is it painful?"

"Yes."

"Bad pain or growing pain?"

"How would I know? I've never had either!"

That startled a laugh out of her—short, surprised, quickly bitten off. "Fair point. Growing pain, probably. If it turns bad—the kind of bad that whites out your vision—you sit down immediately and breathe. Agreed?"

"Agreed." My voice came out thinner than I wanted.

We walked on. The flats opened around us, vast and silent and utterly empty of anything but light, and with every step the humming grew. Not louder exactly. Denser. As if the air itself were thickening around me, filling with something invisible that only I could feel pressing inward—and then, worse, pressing *outward*, meeting the incoming flood halfway, the two pressures grinding against each other somewhere behind my sternum.

By the time the valley was a pale smear behind us and the far ridge of the flats shimmered ahead, I was sweating. My vision had gone strange—colors too bright, shadows edged in faint silver, the heat-haze over the salt resolving into shapes that almost meant something. When I looked at my hands, I could *see* the power in them now, or see its absence filled in: a faint coruscating flicker beneath the skin of my knuckles, blue-white, restless, like summer lightning trapped in glass.

"Odessa," I said again, and this time my voice cracked in the middle of her name.

"I've got you." She took my arm. Her grip was surprisingly strong, her palm cool against my burning skin. "You're about half a mile from the salt line's true end—it thins gradually, doesn't just stop. Which means you're feeling it build in layers, and your body keeps expecting each layer to be the last. It isn't. There are more. Maybe another dozen before it lets go entirely."

"A dozen more of *this*?"

"Of this, and then the actual moment, which is different."

"Wonderful. I can't wait."

"You can and you will." She steered me forward. "Breathe, Yara. In through the nose, out slow through the mouth. Like blowing on embers you don't want to flare."

"I don't know how to blow on anything. I mended nets for fifteen years."

"Then mend this. You're holding yourself together with tension—every muscle clenched, jaw locked, shoulders up around your ears. That's how you survived the valley, and it worked, I'm not knocking it. But tension is a container, and containers *break*. What you want is a membrane. Something that moves with the pressure instead of resisting it."

"And how do I make a membrane?"

"Breathe like you mean it. Unclench everything you're not using. And stop thinking of it as an invasion." She tapped my chest, once, over the sternum. "This isn't something attacking you. This is *you*. Every bit of it. It's been you since before you could talk. You're not being flooded—you're being *reminded*."

I tried. I breathed. I unclenched my jaw, which promptly made the humming surge up through my teeth, and I clenched it again, and Odessa sighed with the patience of someone who had done this before with other frightened exiles crossing other boundaries.

"Like breath, not like a wall," she repeated. "Again."

*

The last hundred yards took an hour.

Not because we walked slowly—because I kept stopping. Each layer of thinning salt peeled something open in me that I hadn't known was closed, and each peeling came with its own small crisis: a wave of dizziness, a burst of involuntary tears that embarrassed us both, once a full-body shudder that dropped me to one knee on the crust while phantom thunder rolled somewhere behind my eyes. Odessa stayed with me through all of it, crouched at my shoulder, narrating in her low, even voice.

"This one's fear. Fear tastes like copper, did you know that? Gifted folk who suppress long enough start tasting their own emotions. You'll taste mine soon too, probably. Sorry in advance—I'm mostly nerves and stubbornness."

"This one's grief. Don't fight it. Grief is just love with nowhere to go, and yours has been going nowhere for twenty years. Let it go somewhere."

"This one might actually be joy. Yes, really. Check your hands—are they reaching for something? That's joy. Your body remembers what it wants before your mind agrees to want it."

And through it all, the pressure built. The flicker beneath my skin brightened. The silver-edged world sharpened until I could see the wind itself moving over the flats in long translucent ribbons, could see the static charge gathering in a cloudless sky like a held note, could see—God help me—*everything*, the whole breathing circuitry of a world I had been blind to since childhood, waking up all at once in eyes that weren't ready for it.

Then we crossed the true line, and there was no more gradual.

It hit like falling upward.

One step, the world was dense and singing. The next step, the salt's dampening field ended as if a door had been cut out of the air, and twenty years of dammed-up storm came through the gap with the force of everything it had ever been denied.

I heard myself scream. I don't remember deciding to. The sound tore out of me along with the rest of it—light bursting up my spine, out through my arms, crackling off my fingers in wild blue arcs that scorched the salt crust black around my feet. My knees buckled. The sky above us, clear a moment before, *boiled*—clouds spinning up out of nothing, spiraling inward overhead with a low groan of gathering thunder, answering me the way iron filings answer a magnet, and I knelt in the center of it with lightning dancing over my arms and no more control over any of it than a drowning man has over the sea.

"Yara!" Odessa's voice came from very far away. "Look at me! Yara!"

I couldn't. Everything was too much. Every nerve ending was a live wire, every heartbeat a thunderclap, the power pouring through me faster than I could contain it and burning channels through me as it went—painful, ecstatic, *alive* in a way that made the previous twenty years look like a painting of life rather than the thing itself. This was what I had been. This was what I had given up without ever being asked. This was—

A hand seized my wrist. Small, cool, insistent.

"*Hold it.*"

Odessa crouched in front of me, and her eyes had gone molten—flickering with ember-light, the way they did when she read fire, except there was no fire here, only me, and she was reading *me* instead, and whatever she saw made her grip tighten.

"Listen to me. You are not the storm. You are the *shore*."

"What?" The word came out ragged, half lightning.

"The shore. Waves come, waves go. The shore doesn't chase them and it doesn't flee them. It *holds its shape* and lets the water move. Right now you think you have to grab every wave or drown. You don't. Breathe. One wave comes—one breath in—and you let it *pass through you* and go. Out. Into the sky, where it belongs. You're not a dam. You're a door."

"A door," I gasped.

"A door. Open on both sides. Nothing gets stuck. Breathe in—there's the wave—and *let it go*."

I breathed in. Lightning poured in with it. I breathed out—and this time, instead of clamping down, instead of fighting, I *opened*, and the charge rushed out of me in a long rolling exhalation that leapt from my fingertips into the ground and the air and the boiling clouds overhead, which rumbled, gratifyingly, and began very slowly to unknot.

Another breath. Another wave. In, and through, and out. The screaming arcs on my arms dimmed to a faint shimmer. The pressure behind my sternum eased from *unbearable* to merely enormous. My vision cleared by degrees, the silver edges softening, the world settling from unbearable brilliance back toward merely vivid.

"There you are," Odessa murmured. "There you are. Again."

We stayed like that for a long time—me kneeling in a circle of scorched salt, her hand on my wrist, both of us breathing together, in and out, waves passing through and passing on. Slowly, slowly, the storm overhead unraveled into ordinary clouds, ordinary wind. The humming under my skin didn't stop. But it changed. It settled from a shriek into a current, from a current into... presence. Constant, warm, alive, thrumming just below the surface like a river under ice.

Like a limb waking up. Painful, pins-and-needles, flooding with blood and sensation after years of numbness—and *mine*, unmistakably mine, asking nothing except to be let through.

I laughed. It came out wet and shaky and slightly hysterical. "I can hear the weather."

"You can hear the weather," Odessa agreed. Her ember-glow had faded; her eyes were just eyes again, tired and kind. "Congratulations. It sounds like gossip, doesn't it? Wind always exaggerates."

"It does. It's terrible. The wind east of here says something about smoke."

The words left my mouth before I understood them. Then I felt them—the thread of attention I hadn't known I was holding, extended eastward on the wind like a net cast wide, and the information coming back along it: distant, faint, but distinct. Burning. Woodsmoke and something else underneath, something chemical and wrong, like pitch and hot iron.

Odessa went still. "How far?"

"Far. Half a day's walk? Maybe less, if the wind's honest." I pulled the thread back in, carefully, marveling at the feel of it even now, even with dread rising in my throat. "There's a waystation on this road, isn't there? The Ember Road, you called it."

"There is." Her face had gone grim. "And if the wind's telling the truth—"

"Then somebody's burning," I finished. "Or something's been burned already, and we're smelling what's left."

I got up off my knees. My legs shook. My hands still shimmered faintly at the knuckles, and the whole world still sang with currents I was only beginning to learn the names of, and I was exhausted down to the marrow, wrung out, half-blind with fatigue—

—and more awake than I had ever been in my life.

"Teach me the rest on the way," I said. "All of it. Whatever you know about holding this thing. Because if there are hunters burning their way along that road, then the next time I call lightning, it's going to be on purpose."

Odessa looked at me for a long moment—at my scorched boots, my shaking hands, my face, which I suspect wore the expression of someone who has just discovered they've been asleep for twenty years and intends to stay angry about it. Then she shouldered her pack and turned east, toward the smoke.

"Stay close," she said. "And Yara—if the wind starts saying anything about dragons, wake me immediately."
