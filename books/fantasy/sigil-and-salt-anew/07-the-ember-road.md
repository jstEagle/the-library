## 7. The Ember Road

I have read fire my whole life, and I will tell you what nobody wants to hear: fire is honest. It doesn't lie about what it burned or why. People lie. Crowns lie. But a fire tells you everything, if you're willing to look at the shape of its ash.

Three days out of the Salt Valley, the road told us something we weren't ready to know.

* * *

We'd fallen into a rhythm by then, Yara and I, though rhythm is a generous word for two people who barely spoke and walked like they were each trying to outrun their own shadow. Yara walked ahead. Always ahead. I think they believed that if they kept moving fast enough, the valley's elders wouldn't be able to catch up with their disappointment.

They were wrong, of course. Elders travel at the speed of guilt.

The land had changed since the flats. Green crept back in—scrub pine, gorse, long grass gone gold at the tips—and every mile of it made Yara flinch a little, like the world was too loud after years of salt silence. I watched them flexing their hands as we walked. The storm-scars. Pale lines across the knuckles like old lightning had decided to leave its signature there.

"You keep doing that," I said on the third morning.

"Doing what."

"Flexing. Like you're testing whether your fingers still work."

"They do." A pause. "It's not the fingers I'm testing."

I let it go. There are conversations you can't have on an open road with the sky listening, and that was one of them. Besides, I had my own problems. My gift of reading fire meant that every hearth, every campfire, every cook-smoke curling from a chimney within ten miles announced itself to me like a drunk cousin shouting my name across a market square. And somewhere behind us, I knew, there were hunters who could read *me* the same way—a gifted person lights up in the world's awareness like a struck match in a dark room.

So we walked small. We banked our fires to coals before dawn. We kept off the main road where we could.

Which is why we smelled the waystation before we saw it.

* * *

Smoke should smell like breakfast, or warmth, or home. This smoke smelled wrong. It smelled like wet ash and something sweeter underneath—the reek of binding-iron worked hot, which has a tang like blood left too long in a copper pot. My stomach knew what my mind didn't yet. I stopped walking.

Yara took three more steps before they noticed. "What?"

"Fire," I said. "Old one. Two days, maybe three." I closed my eyes and reached for it, the way you'd reach into a pocket for a stone you already know the shape of. Every fire leaves a residue in the world, a memory written in heat, and if I'm quiet enough I can read it backward: how it started, how it moved, what it ate.

This one had started at the door.

"It was set," I said. "Deliberate. Front entrance, low and fast, meant to drive everyone inside toward the back." I opened my eyes. "And there was iron involved. A lot of it."

Yara had gone very still in the way I was learning meant their gift was waking up under their skin, a dog lifting its head before it growls. "A waystation," they said. "Who burns a waystation?"

"Nobody burns a waystation." I started forward. "Somebody burns the people in it."

The Ember Road waystation had been a fixture on this stretch for longer than either of us had been alive. Travelers' rest, stable, a well with sweet water. The kind of place where you paid a copper for soup and got gossip free. It sat at the crossroads ahead of us now like a pulled tooth—a black gap in the green, walls charred to their bones, roof collapsed inward, and the smell rolling off it thick enough to chew.

Yara broke into a run. I followed, and I want it noted that I followed even though every instinct I had was screaming at me to walk the other direction, because that's the thing about traveling with someone whose spine is made of other people's emergencies: eventually their spine becomes yours.

The stable had burned first, or maybe last—I couldn't tell through the tangle—but the main house was the worst. Doorway blown outward. Inside, the furniture reduced to black sculptures of themselves. A child's wooden horse by the hearth, half-eaten by flame, one glass eye staring up at the rafters that weren't anymore.

And iron. Binding-iron collars, scattered across the floor like dropped jewelry. Six of them. Some scorched, some intact, all of them empty.

"Six," Yara said. Their voice had gone flat, which I'd learned was worse than shaking. "Six collars."

"Five adults, one small." I crouched by the smallest collar. It was sized for a neck no thicker than my wrist. "They bound them here. In their own home. Then—" I gestured at the room, at the answer written in ash. Bound gifts make people docile, empty, easy to transport. But these people hadn't been transported. They'd been—

"Why burn them?" Yara asked. "If they were bound, why not just take them?"

I looked at the collars again. At the scorch marks. At the pattern of the burn, which I was reading now with my gift fully open, and wishing I wasn't.

"Because some of them fought," I said quietly. "Or because the wardens wanted a message. Or because—" I stopped myself. "There's a survivor's chance, is what I'm saying. Root cellars don't always burn through. Look."

I pointed to the corner of the kitchen, where a hatch door hung half-open over a dark square cut into the earth. The flames had licked around it but the stone lip had held, and beneath it, darkness.

Yara was moving before I finished speaking. They hauled the hatch wide and dropped to their knees at the edge of the hole, peering down into blackness that smelled of raw potato and cold dirt and, faintly, underneath it all, living breath.

"Hello?" Yara's voice echoed down. "Is anyone down there?"

Silence. Then, so faint I almost missed it, a sound I'll carry to my grave: a small voice, cracked with thirst, saying, "Are you the warden come back?"

* * *

She came up into the light like something being born backwards—clawing at the ladder rungs, blinking, filthy, tiny. Eight years old, maybe nine. Brown hair matted with cellar dust. She wore a man's shirt belted with rope and boots two sizes too big, and she held a kitchen knife in a grip that would have been fierce if her hand hadn't been shaking so hard.

Yara froze. I watched them take in the knife, the size of her, the state of her, and I watched something happen in their face that I couldn't name yet but would understand later. It was the look of a person seeing their own history standing in front of them wearing a smaller body.

"We're not wardens," Yara said, very gently, hands open and empty. "My name is Yara. This is Odessa. We're... we're like you."

"Liar." The word came out rusty. "Nobody's like me. Mam said never tell. Never tell anybody, Bramble, not ever, not even when they ask nice." Her chin came up. "Are you asking nice?"

"The nicest," I said, and I crouched down too, slow, keeping my hands visible. "But here's how you'll know we're telling the truth. I can read fire. That's my gift. And your house—" I had to stop, because saying it plainly felt like a cruelty, but she deserved plain. "Your house burned two days ago. If I were a warden, I'd have known that already. Wardens don't need to be told what they did."

Bramble's eyes went to the ruined room. To the little wooden horse. Something in her face crumpled and reassembled itself into something harder, and I thought: *there it is, that's what survival looks like on a child, it looks like armor bolted on crooked.*

"Mam pushed me down the ladder," she said. "She put the knife in my hand and said stay quiet till the quiet goes all the way quiet. And then the door came in and Papa shouted and there was light, orange light, everywhere, and I heard—" Her voice snagged. "I heard Mam say a word. Just one word. Over and over. Like a prayer but angry."

"What word?" Yara asked.

"*Run.*"

The word sat between us. Yara closed their eyes.

"She gave you the knife," I said. "That was love, that was. Sharpest thing she could give you."

"I cut a rat with it," Bramble said, with terrible pride. "In the dark. It screamed."

"I believe it."

Then her legs simply quit holding her, and Yara caught her before she hit the ground, and that was the moment I understood what the elders in the Salt Valley had never grasped about Yara Villalobos: they had spent twenty years practicing stillness, and all that stored-up motion had to go somewhere, and it was going to go into protecting this child with the single-minded ferocity of a storm that has finally found somewhere to land.

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of the stable, because Bramble refused to leave—"Mam said wait for her"—and none of us had the heart to argue with grief's arithmetic. Yara built a fire small enough to hide in a teacup, and I fed it clean wood, and Bramble sat between us wrapped in Yara's cloak like a comma between two hard sentences.

She talked, in the way of children who have been alone too long with their own thoughts—as if the words had been queuing inside her for two days and were now coming out in a mob. Her mam grew healing herbs and could coax them through frost. Her papa could find water with a forked stick. Her brother Wren, who was twelve and insufferable, could make bread rise just by humming at it. Small magics. Kitchen magics. The kind the crown called *unregistered anomalies* in its proclamation, as if a boy humming loaves into being were a threat to the realm.

"They hummed at him," Bramble said suddenly, apropos of nothing, staring into the fire. "The wardens. When they put the collars on. One of them hummed Wren's tune back at him. Wrong, though. All wrong notes." Her fists clenched in Yara's cloak. "I hate him. I hate him so much I swallowed it and it's still burning."

I looked at Yara across the fire. They were watching Bramble with an expression I can only describe as recognition wearing grief's clothing.

"Bramble," Yara said. "When you're ready—not tonight, maybe not for a while—we're going to the capital. To fix this. To unmake the thing that sent those men. And until you're ready, you don't have to decide anything. You can just be with us. Eat our food. Hate loudly. Whatever you need."

Bramble considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Do you have any food?"

"A bit of dried apple."

"Then yes. Provisionally."

Despite everything—despite the ash and the collars and the word *run* still hanging in the air—I laughed. It startled all three of us.

Later, when Bramble slept against Yara's shoulder, Yara spoke without looking at me, voice pitched under the wind.

"The elders were right about one thing. Out here, we're beacons. Every mile closer to the capital, we shine brighter."

"And yet."

"And yet." Yara looked down at the sleeping child. "I spent twenty years learning to be invisible. Three days out and I've already—" Their jaw tightened. "I can't walk past a root cellar, Odessa. I can't be the person who walks past."

"No," I said. "You really can't. I've watched you try. It's like watching a river try to be a rock."

"That's supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be true. Comfort's extra."

Yara almost smiled. Then their gaze drifted to the crossroads, to the signpost standing scorched but upright, pointing east toward the capital, west toward nowhere, south toward the valley that had voted them out.

And that's when I saw it—the fresh paper nailed to the post, white as an accusation, ink still sharp. A proclamation. The crown's seal at the bottom, stamped in wax the color of dried blood.

I stood, crossed to it, and read it aloud so Yara wouldn't have to move and wake the child:

*"By order of the Regent's Voice, in the king's name: all persons of unsanctioned gift shall present themselves for binding within thirty days of this posting. Shelterers of the unbound shall share their sentence. Reward offered for information leading to the seizure of fugitives."*

*Shelterers.* The word landed on all three of us, awake, asleep, and in-between.

"They've named us criminals for existing," Yara said softly. "And now anyone who helps us is criminal too."

"Yes."

"So every kindness costs somebody."

"Every kindness has always cost somebody," I said. "Now it's just priced honestly."

Yara was quiet a long moment. Then they tucked the cloak more carefully around Bramble, and when they spoke again, the flatness was gone from their voice, replaced by something with edges.

"Thirty days," they said. "Then we'd better get to the capital before the proclamation does. Because I've seen what happens to waystations." They looked at me, and the storm-scars on their hands caught the firelight like veins of silver. "And I'll be damned if I watch it happen to an inn full of people like us."

I didn't ask how they knew about the Gilded Wren. I hadn't mentioned it aloud since the valley. But fire reads true, and so, apparently, does Yara.

We broke camp before dawn, three shadows walking east on the ember road, toward the city, toward the crown, toward whatever waited for us at the end of the hunt's long arithmetic.

Behind us, the waystation smoldered its last. Ahead, the horizon was already turning the color of binding-iron.

And somewhere on the road behind—or perhaps ahead, the world being what it was—wardens were reading our names off lists we hadn't signed.
