## 8. A Tavern of Small Magics

Three days on the Ember Road taught me things the Salt Valley never could. That the world outside smells like woodsmoke and wet earth and, occasionally, fear. That Odessa hums when she walks and denies it when asked. That Bramble can sleep anywhere, including standing up, including mid-sentence, which she proved by falling asleep while telling me about her grandmother's cat.

And that I had been living my whole life with a hand pressed over my own mouth without ever noticing.

The magic came back at the salt line like blood into a sleeping limb—that was Odessa's word for it, *limb*, and she was right. It prickled. It ached. It wanted to move and didn't trust me to let it. By the second day I'd learned to keep it down the way you keep down a sneeze in a quiet room: constantly, exhausting, with the constant knowledge that one good startle would undo everything.

Which is why, when we crested the hill at dusk on the third day and saw the Gilded Wren sitting in its hollow with its windows lit gold against the coming dark, my first instinct was not relief.

My first instinct was: *that many candles is a confession.*

"An inn," I said flatly. "In the middle of nowhere. Lit up like a festival."

"It's not an inn," Odessa said. "Well. It's an inn. But it's also—" She waved a hand, searching for the word.

"A trap?" Bramble offered helpfully, around a mouthful of road-bread.

"A door," Odessa said. "The kind you can only find if somebody on the other side wants you to."

I looked at her. She looked back with the particular patience of someone who has already decided what you're going to do and is simply waiting for your dignity to catch up.

"And how does it know who to want?" I asked.

"You'll see." She started down the hill. "Come on. The stew's real, the beds are real, and the people are mostly real too, which is more than you'll get anywhere else within a hundred miles."

* * *

The Gilded Wren did not look gilded up close. It looked tired—the way a person looks after a long day of pretending to be fine. The plaster was patched in three different shades. The sign above the door showed a bird whose gilt had flaked away until only one wing still shone, so that it appeared to be either ascending or falling, depending on your mood.

Bramble tugged my sleeve. "What if they don't like us?"

"What's not to like? You're charming. Odessa's useful. I'm..." I considered. "Present."

"That's not nothing," Bramble said seriously. "Grandmother always said half of surviving is just being present when the bread comes out."

Odessa stopped at the door and turned to face us both, and for once the humor drained out of her face entirely, leaving something older and wearier underneath. "Rules," she said. "One: don't show anything unless they ask. Two: if they ask, show true—these people have smelled lies before, and they bite. Three:" she glanced at Bramble, and her voice gentled, "nobody here will hurt you. But some of them will look at you like you're a ghost, because you're what they couldn't save. Let them look."

Bramble nodded solemnly, the way children nod at rules they intend to follow exactly as long as convenient.

Then Odessa knocked on the inn door—a specific rhythm, three quick, two slow, one more quick—and I understood. The rhythm was the wanting. The door only opened for people who knew the knock, or for people brought by someone who did.

It opened on a woman built like a barrel, flour to her elbows, with eyes that went over each of our faces the way a moneylender counts coins.

"Odessa Fire-Reader." Not a question. "You're late."

"The road was on fire," Odessa said. "Literally. It slowed us down."

"Mm." The woman stepped aside. "Kitchen's warm. Don't track ash on my floors."

We went in, and the warmth hit me like a physical thing—not just heat from the ovens but something else, something under it, a hum I felt in my teeth. Every hair on my arms stood up. My suppressed gift stirred against its leash like a dog smelling another dog through a fence.

*They're all gifted,* I realized. *Every single person in this room.*

There were maybe fifteen people in the common hall. A man mending a boot whose needle moved without his hands touching it. Two women playing cards where the cards occasionally rearranged themselves out of sheer boredom. A girl scrubbing pots while the water boiled itself and scolded her in small steam-whistles. Nobody hid it. Nobody even seemed aware they were doing it, the way you're not aware of breathing until someone mentions it.

Twenty years in the valley, where every gift was buried so deep we'd forgotten the graves' locations, and here was a room full of people wearing theirs like coats.

I must have made a sound, because Odessa glanced at me with something almost like pity. "First time?"

"I don't know what to do with my face," I admitted.

"Put it away. You'll scare the stew."

* * *

The stew was lamb and barley and something green I couldn't name, and it was the best thing I had ever eaten, which I told the barrel-woman—her name was Marta—and she received the compliment the way a fortress receives a diplomatic envoy: politely, and with suspicion.

We ate at a corner table. Bramble fell asleep between her third bowl and her fourth, mid-chew, her head tipping onto my shoulder with the boneless trust of the very young. I sat very still so as not to wake her, and found I didn't mind the weight at all.

That was when he came out of the back room.

He was ordinary-looking, which I later learned was a professional habit: medium height, medium build, brown hair going grey at the temples, hands that were careful with everything they touched. He carried a lantern he didn't need—the hall was bright—and set it on our table anyway, and I watched the flame inside it bend toward Odessa like a plant toward sun.

"Fire-reader," he said quietly. "Marta says you came off the Ember Road. Which stretch?"

"The burned waystation. Mile forty."

Something crossed his face—grief with the edges worn smooth from handling. "Then you found the cellar."

"We found Bramble," Odessa said.

His eyes went to the child asleep against my shoulder, and whatever he was feeling, he folded it away with the neatness of long practice. "Her family?"

"Gone," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended.

He nodded slowly. Then he pulled out a chair, uninvited, and sat, and looked at me with an attention that made my skin prickle. "And you. You've been holding something down since you walked in. All night. Your jaw's been clenched so hard I can hear your teeth from across the room."

"I grind them," I said. "Habit."

"No," he said, not unkindly. "You don't. I spent eleven years teaching people to hold power down. I know the posture. You're carrying a storm behind your ribs and you've been carrying it so long you've forgotten it isn't part of your skeleton."

The hall's noise seemed to recede. The fire-hum in my teeth got louder.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Perrin." A pause. "Formerly binder-third-class, Royal Office of Containment, Vessia. Currently the man who pours the drinks."

"A royal binder," I said. "Running a tavern full of the people you used to bind."

"Yes." He met my stare without flinching. "It's exactly as strange as it sounds. Would you like to hear why, or would you rather spend the evening deciding I'm a warden in disguise and ruining everyone's dinner?"

"Honestly, both options appeal."

"He's all right," Odessa said. "I checked him months ago. Fire doesn't lie about people, Yara. His burns clean."

Perrin raised an eyebrow. "You read me?"

"Everyone reads differently. Yours is like a ledger. Very organized guilt." She took a drink. "Page one is very long."

Despite everything—despite the road, the waystation, the child drooling on my shoulder—I laughed. It startled me. I hadn't laughed properly since before the wardens came to the flats, and possibly longer than that.

"All right," Perrin said. "You want truth. Here's the first piece, free, because you'll need it before you sleep tonight." He leaned forward, lowered his voice, and the hall's hum seemed to lean in with him. "You're headed to the capital. Odessa told Marta as much, and Marta tells me everything, because Marta is the reason this place has survived six years. So hear me now: whatever you think is waiting for you there, it's worse. And it's stranger."

"The king," I said. "The purge. The binding orders."

"The king is dead."

He said it plainly, the way you'd say *the well is dry*, and I felt Bramble shift in her sleep and forced myself to stay still.

"Dead how?" Odessa asked. Though I noticed her hand had gone still on the table. She'd seen something at court. She'd told me about the shadow moving wrong. She wasn't asking because she doubted. She was asking because she needed someone else to say it out loud.

"Dead four years ago," Perrin said. "Fever, some said. Poison, others whispered. The official record says a fall from horseback during the autumn hunt. I helped write the official record." His jaw tightened. "I was there. I watched Aldric die. And then I watched the thing that came after pick up his crown and put it on, and every binder in the throne room knelt, because kneeling was easier than understanding what we were seeing."

"What is it?" I asked. "The thing."

"I don't fully know. None of us do. What I know is what it needs." He held up fingers, counting. "It needs fire—dragon-fire specifically, though any great burning will do in a pinch, and there haven't been pinch-worthy burnings in years. It needs gifts—bound ones, harvested. When a magician is 'bound,' their gift doesn't die. It goes somewhere. We were told it went into the iron. It doesn't. It goes into the crown." His voice dropped further. "Hundreds of bound magicians, Yara. Their gifts are in that coronet, feeding it, keeping it looking like a king instead of like whatever it actually is."

The stew in my stomach had gone to lead. Against my shoulder, Bramble breathed on, small and even, and I thought about root cellars and burned waystations and the mile-forty ash that Odessa wouldn't talk about.

"And the purge," I said. "The registration. The hunt."

"Harvest season," Perrin said simply. "It's running low. Whatever it is, it consumes. Slowly, but it consumes. Four years of feasting on the court's stockpile, and now it's hungry enough to send wardens into the Salt Valley itself." He looked at me. "Yes, we heard about the flats. Word travels fast when the impossible happens. Lightning on the salt line, out of a clear sky, scattering a warden patrol. There are people in this room who felt it from here. Weather-gifts carry far."

Heat crawled up my neck. "It wasn't deliberate."

"No," he agreed. "That's what worries me. Deliberate storms can be reasoned with. Yours is a keg with a lit fuse walking toward the capital." He spread his hands. "So. Here's my offer, and it's not charity, because charity makes people stupid. Stay the night. Eat. Sleep behind wards I laid myself. In the morning, if you want to walk on alone, I'll give you provisions and directions that avoid the checkpoints. If you want to understand what you're walking toward—if you want to be more than a fuse—I'll teach you. I owe this kingdom a debt measured in hundreds of caged gifts. Teaching you to hold yours might pay down an ounce of it."

"Why would you help us?" I asked. "Really. Strip it down. What's in it for you?"

He was quiet a moment. Around us the hall went on—cards shuffled themselves, the pot-scrubbing girl argued with her kettle—but our table had become its own small country.

"Because I knelt," he said finally. "Eleven years, I knelt, and told myself it was mercy, that binding was kinder than killing, that I was saving magicians from the pyres the old councils wanted. And then I bound a girl of nineteen who begged me not to take it, and I watched her go empty, and I went home and couldn't remember her name by morning, because the iron takes the memory too, and I realized I had been the polite face of a harvest." His hands, those careful hands, flattened on the table. "Deserting wasn't courage. It was the minimum. This—" he gestured at the hall, the humming, gifted, unhidden room, "—is also the minimum. You asking me why I'd help is like asking a drowning man why he'd grab a rope. I'm not helping you. I'm climbing."

Nobody spoke. Bramble sighed in her sleep and burrowed closer.

Odessa reached across the table and touched the lantern's flame with one fingertip; it leaned into her touch like a cat. "Told you," she said to me. "Clean burns."

* * *

They gave us a room under the eaves, two narrow beds and a window that looked out over dark hills. Bramble went down like a stone and stayed there. Odessa took the other bed and was asleep within minutes, humming even in her dreams, a low fire-crackle tune.

I lay awake and listened to the building.

That was the thing I couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't grown up in the valley. In the Salt Valley, silence was the whole architecture. Silence under the floorboards, silence in the walls, twenty years of everyone agreeing not to be what they were. You could hear the silence. It had a texture, like wool pulled over the ears.

The Gilded Wren hummed. Not loudly. The needle-man's gift murmured in its sleep upstairs. The kettle-girl dreamed in steam. Somewhere below, Marta banked the ovens with a word that glowed faintly through the floor. A whole building of small magics, none of them grand, all of them awake, all of them *allowed*.

I pressed my palm flat against my sternum, against the storm coiled there, and for the first time in my life I didn't press to suppress. I pressed the way you press a bruise—to check whether it's real.

It was real. It answered. A flicker ran up my arm and lit the ceiling for half a heartbeat, a pale web of light like veins of silver in stone, and downstairs I heard Marta pause in her work, and feel her notice, and—this was the part that undid me—hear her simply continue banking the coals. No alarm. No elders summoned. No vote in the meetinghouse about whether I was too dangerous to shelter.

Just a woman with flour on her elbows, letting a stranger's lightning pass overhead like weather.

I didn't cry. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because I have been accused of crying at inconvenient moments my entire life, and I did not cry in that attic room in the Gilded Wren. My eyes were merely wet, which is a completely different phenomenon, medically speaking.

Sleep took me eventually, and for once the dream wasn't salt.

* * *

Morning came with the smell of bread and the sound of argument.

"—telling you, the checkpoint at Harrow Ford is doubled. Doubled. They're not checking for smugglers anymore, they're checking faces." That was Marta, low and sharp, in the kitchen below.

"And I'm telling you Binding Day is in nine days and if we don't move before it, we never will." A new voice, a man's, tight with urgency. "They're bringing three hundred of them, Marta. Three hundred gifted folk to the ceremony square to be bound in front of the whole city, as a *lesson*. If that happens—"

"If what happens?" Odessa, from the doorway. I hadn't heard her come down. I followed, Bramble's hand in mine, her hair a disaster she refused to acknowledge.

The kitchen held Marta, the urgent man—a weather-reader, I'd learn, with rain-grey eyes that never quite focused on the present—and Perrin, standing very still by the oven with a loaf in his bare hands, which should have burned him and didn't, and which told me something about the size of his own buried gift before anyone said a word about it.

Perrin set the loaf down. He looked at Odessa, then at me, and I watched him make a decision the way you watch a man decide to open a door he's kept locked for years.

"Binding Day," he said. "Nine days from now. The largest harvest in the kingdom's history, staged as a public celebration. And we have nine days to decide whether this tavern's worth of small magics is enough to stop it." His eyes settled on me, and there was no kindness in them now, only arithmetic. "Or whether we need a storm."

The bread smell filled the kitchen. Bramble squeezed my hand.

Outside, far off, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky, and every gifted person in the room turned to look at me.
