## 9. Perrin's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a room when a man decides to tell the truth. I've heard it twice in my life. The first time was in the king's own council chamber, when Aldric asked me—asked *me*, his binder, his clever little instrument-maker—whether the binding-iron could be made to work on something larger than a person.

I said yes.

The second time was last night, in the cellar of the Gilded Wren, with a storm-caller who flinched at raised voices, a fire-reader who watched me like I was a ledger she intended to audit, and a child asleep against a flour sack who had already lost more than any of us and would lose more still before this was done.

I told them yes again. This time there was no king to please. Only the truth, sitting on the table between us like something I'd dragged in from the rain.

* * *

Let me start where all my sins start: with arithmetic.

I was twenty-three years old and the finest theoretical binder in the Royal Collegium, which is a sentence that should embarrass me more than it does, because "finest theoretical binder" turns out to mean "the man most skilled at making cages." I came up from the river provinces with chalk-stained cuffs and a head full of elegant proofs, and I genuinely believed—I want you to understand this, I *genuinely believed*—that binding was mercy.

You have to picture Vessia as it was then. Two great houses of magicians, the Emberwrights and the Stormwardens, each convinced the other was one bad harvest away from open war. Guilds arming themselves with gifts instead of steel. A boy in Marrowfield who froze a well solid in summer and drowned three people when it thawed wrong. Every week another story like that, and every story got louder as it traveled, the way stories do.

And I sat in my tower room with my beautiful mathematics and thought: what if power could be *held*? Not destroyed—we tried destruction, it doesn't take; magic is like water, it finds its level—but held. Gently. Temporarily. Until cooler heads prevailed.

I invented the iron for a war that never quite arrived. That's the joke of it. The civil war I was saving everyone from never happened, because the thing I built to prevent it made war unnecessary. Why fight your rival guilds when you can simply *bind* them? Why argue with a magician when you can make him ordinary?

The crown didn't have to twist my arm. That's the part I can't forgive, and believe me, I've tried. They just showed me the problem and let my own cleverness do the rest. Warden-captain Hesk gave me a workshop, six apprentices, and a budget, and called me *the kingdom's quiet shield*. I was thirty-one before I understood what I'd actually built: not a shield. A leash. And leashes, whatever their original purpose, are always eventually picked up by someone who wants to hold them.

* * *

"Say the part about the ritual," Odessa said. She sat with her arms crossed and her back to the wall, the way people sit when they've spent months being hunted and have stopped pretending otherwise. Her eyes were banked coals. Fire-readers always look at you like they're checking you for kindling. Given what she'd seen of my work, I suppose I qualified.

"The ritual," I said. My mouth tasted like the cellar we were sitting in, which was dust and old tallow. "Yes."

Yara hadn't moved since I started talking. They sat very still in the manner of someone holding a storm behind their teeth—which, I've since learned, is not entirely a metaphor—and they listened the way masons listen to a building groan. Waiting to find out if tonight was the night it came down.

Bramble slept through the worst of it, thank every small god. Children need their sleep even when the adults are performing autopsies on their own souls.

"Aldric was dying," I said. "This was—eleven years ago now. The wasting sickness. He hid it well, but I was his binder; I read the signature of his life every morning the way a physician reads a pulse. It was going out. Slowly, but it was going out, and everyone who loved him knew it, and everyone who feared him knew it, and both groups began doing sums."

"And you were in the second group?" Yara asked. Not cruelly. Just precisely.

"I was in neither group. I was in the third group—the one that thinks it's above groups." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way my laughs do these days. "The chancellor came to me. Corvane. You won't find his name in any history worth reading, because histories are written by survivors and he did not survive being wrong about me. He said the succession would tear the kingdom apart. Two claimants, each with gifted backers, each with wardens. He said children would burn in their beds. He said—he was very good, you must understand, he said everything I had been saying to myself for years, back to me, in a voice that sounded like duty."

Odessa's jaw tightened. "He wanted the throne for himself."

"He wanted the *shape* of the throne. I don't think he fully understood what he was calling up. I'm not sure I did either, and I wrote half the equations." I rubbed my hands together. Old habit. In the workshop we used to warm them over the forge while talking through problems, and I have never stopped reaching for a forge that isn't there. "Here is what I contributed, so no one can later say I was an innocent tool. The binding-iron holds a gift. Fine. But a gift isn't a lamp you snuff—it's a river, and a dammed river has to go somewhere. I designed the siphon. The mechanism that takes what's bound and routes it elsewhere. I told myself it would power wards. Lighthouses. The great barrier works along the northern coast. Beneficial applications. I drew up the plans myself, on good paper, with a steady hand."

"And the ritual itself?"

"The ritual needed a vessel that the whole kingdom already believed in. You can't put a stolen river into just any jug; the jug has to be shaped like authority or the magic won't settle. A crown is—" I gestured vaguely at my own head, at the ghost of a circlet I haven't worn in fifteen years and hope never to wear again. "A crown is the oldest container we have. Anointed, sworn-over, soaked in centuries of oaths. Every coronation is a binding rite, if you look at it plainly. We just never looked."

"So they put the hunger in the crown," Yara said slowly, "and put the crown on a dying king."

"On a dead king. By the end. Though 'dead' turned out to be a generous word for what Aldric became, or what remained of him. The siphon needed a living flame to draw from—a true fire, old fire—and there was exactly one source left in Vessia." I watched Odessa's face as I said it, and saw her arrive at the answer a heartbeat before I spoke it. "They took the dragon."

* * *

I want to be honest about the night I finally understood, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I am badly overdrawn everywhere else.

It was three years after the coronation. I was still court binder, still telling myself the same bedtime story—that the bindings were temporary, that order was a bridge and not a destination. A warden brought in a hedge-witch from the eastern fens, some grandmother who'd cured a lord's horse without a license. Standard processing. Iron collar, registration, transfer.

Except the transfer went wrong. The siphon stuttered—the engineers still argue about why—and for perhaps four seconds the flow reversed. Everything she'd given up came back into her at once, and everything the crown had taken from hundreds before her poured through the room like a flood through a broken lock.

I felt it. All of it. Grief and green-growing things and someone's mother-tongue and a weather-sense and a hundred small domestic magics, candle-lighting and milk-charming and the finding of lost keys, all of it screaming past me looking for its owners. And underneath it, at the bottom of the flood, something else. Something that was not anyone's gift at all. Something patient and enormous and *hungry*, turning over in its sleep at the bottom of the crown, the way a pike turns over in a pond when it feels the shadow of prey.

Four seconds. Then the engineers fixed the stutter and the grandmother went blank-eyed to her cell and I went back to my rooms and was sick until dawn.

That was the night I packed a single bag, walked out of the palace under my own name—no one stops the quiet shield—and did not stop walking until I reached a tavern two kingdoms' gossip away that smelled of bread and concealment charms, and asked the woman behind the bar whether she needed a bookkeeper who was very good with numbers and very sorry.

Marta hired me. Marta also made me sleep in the cellar for the first month, which I considered fair.

* * *

"So you ran," Odessa said, when I'd finished. The coals in her eyes had gone from banked to openly burning. "After eleven years of feeding it. After designing the teeth that bit Bramble's family. You *ran*, and kept accounts."

"Yes," I said. There was no version of the answer that sounded better, so I gave the plain one. "I ran. I kept accounts. I memorized patrol rotations and siphon schedules and the names of every binder still loyal, and I passed them to Marta's network, and none of it—not one page of it—unbites a single tooth. You're right. Running is not penance. It's just running with better posture."

Silence. Yara looked at me across the lamplight, and I braced for the verdict. I've learned to read the moment before condemnation arrives; it has a texture, like weather before hail.

But Yara said, "The valley exiled me last week for saving it." Their voice was quiet. "So I'm maybe not the best judge of unforgivable."

"That's not—" I started.

"It's exactly what it is," they said. "You built the cage. Then you spent years learning how the cage opens. Those aren't the same hands, but they're attached to the same man, and I'd rather have the second pair pointed at the crown than the first pair pointed at me." They paused, and something moved behind their eyes that might have been the storm thinking. "Tell me the one thing you know that no one else alive knows."

I thought about it. Honestly, carefully, the way I used to think about proofs, because this was a proof too, and getting it wrong would cost more than a failed theorem.

"The Binding Day ceremony," I said. "Every year, midsummer. The crown processes through the capital and renews the great bindings publicly—it's theater, mostly, a reminder of who holds the leashes. But the renewal requires the siphon to run at full draw for the length of the ceremony. Every bound gift in Vessia flows through the crown at once. Including—" and here my voice did something humiliating, cracked slightly, so I steadied it—"including the dragon-fire. For one hour, the entire mechanism stands exposed. One hour, once a year, when the crown is not a fortress but a funnel."

Odessa leaned forward. "When is it?"

"Nine days," I said. "Nine days from tomorrow."

Bramble stirred against her flour sack and murmured something in her sleep—her mother's name, I think, though I pretended not to hear, because some things you're not meant to overhear even in a confession.

Yara stood. Storm-light wasn't in the room, but it was in them; I could feel the pressure change the way sailors feel it before the horizon goes dark. "Then nine days is enough," they said. "Tell us everything. Start with the palace doors."

And that—God help me—is when I understood that I hadn't confessed at all. Confession ends with absolution or it ends with a rope. What I'd done was hand three fugitives and a sleeping child the blueprint to the cage I built, and ask them to break it, and watch them say yes.

Penance, it turns out, isn't shame. Shame is just the receipt. Penance is standing in front of the thing you broke with your sleeves rolled up, saying: I know how it works. Let me show you where it's weakest.

I rolled up my sleeves.

"Now," I said, pulling the chalk from my pocket—an old habit, the only honest inheritance the Collegium ever gave me—and drawing the first line of the palace schematics on the cellar floor, "let's talk about the west gate."
