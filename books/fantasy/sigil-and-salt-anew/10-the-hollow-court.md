## 10. The Hollow Court

The warden uniform itched like a personal insult.

"You're pulling at the collar," Odessa murmured without looking at me. She walked half a step ahead, the way she always did when she wanted to be mistaken for someone in charge, and the gray wool of her conscript coat hung off her shoulders with the weary authority of a woman who had been ordered to do something stupid and intended to survive it anyway.

"It's trying to strangle me."

"Good. Let it. A conscript who looks comfortable gets noticed." She adjusted the iron collar-pin at her throat—the little badge that said *bound and loyal* in a language of hinges and locks—and I felt my stomach drop through the cobblestones for the hundredth time since we'd passed the city gate.

That was the thing about Vessia's capital, I was learning. It didn't hide what it did to people like us. It wore it. Every third person on the Processional Way wore one of those pins, or a wrist-band, or a dull gray ribbon that meant *registered, pending*. They smiled at each other in bread lines. They thanked the wardens who checked their papers. The city had learned to say thank you to the knife, because the alternative was noticing the knife, and nobody here could afford to notice anything anymore.

Bramble walked between us with her hood up and her small hands buried in her sleeves, which is where she kept them whenever she was frightened, which is where they had been since we entered the walls. Perrin had wanted to leave her at the Wren with Marisol and the others. Bramble had looked at him with an expression I was beginning to recognize as the child's version of a drawn blade, and that had been the end of the discussion.

"Remember," Perrin had told us at dawn, pressing three sets of forged conscription papers into our hands, "you are not sneaking in. Sneaking is suspicious. You are trudging. Trudge. You've been marched from the coast for six weeks and you hate everything, especially your boots."

I hated my boots. That part required no acting.

* * *

The palace did not look like a place where a king lived. It looked like a place where a king had been embalmed and then, through some clerical error, propped back up.

It rose out of the city's heart in pale stone and black spires, beautiful the way a skull is beautiful—everything essential removed, everything left arranged just so. Scaffolding clung to its flanks where workers swarmed like patient ants, but the work wasn't repair. I watched a crew lower something long and jointed into a shaft on the eastern face, wrapped in canvas, and I watched the canvas steam faintly even in the cold morning air.

"That's not construction," I said quietly.

"No," Odessa said. "That's plumbing."

"For what?"

She didn't answer, and her silence answered for her. Fire-plumbing. Siphon-works. We were walking toward the thing she'd described on the road, night after night, in the low voice she used for truths too large for normal volume: the king who was not a king, the flame that fed him, the gifts that poured into him like wine into a bottomless cup.

At the servants' gate, a warden with a ledger checked our papers against a list, glanced at our faces, and waved us through with the enthusiasm of a man checking boxes until he died. Conscription details came in weekly now. The crown was building something, and it needed hands, and it did not care whose.

We were assigned to the underhalls. Of course we were. The universe has a sense of humor; it's just never been a kind one.

* * *

The underhalls of the palace were older than the palace above them. You could feel it in the stones—the ones down there weren't cut, they were *quarried*, vast blocks hauled up from somewhere deep and fitted together by masons who had believed, correctly, that whatever they built would need to last longer than any kingdom.

We carried crates. That was the work: crates of black iron rods, each one humming faintly against my palms like a trapped wasp. Binding-iron. I'd held one piece before, briefly, on the salt flats, and I'd hoped never to hold another. Now I carried them by the dozen, and every trip deeper into the dark made the hum louder, or maybe made me more able to hear it, and I began to understand that the hum wasn't the metal at all.

It was what was in the metal.

"They're full," Bramble whispered on our fourth descent. Her voice was very small. "The rods. There's—it's like hearing a lot of people talking all at once but nobody's using words."

"Don't listen," Odessa said, but gently, the way you say it when you know listening is the only decent thing a person can do.

"I'm not *trying* to—"

"I know. Just—don't reach toward them. Whatever you hear, don't answer it." Odessa's jaw was tight. "Perrin made these. He can tell you what answering costs."

We descended again. And again. And on the sixth descent, the corridor opened.

* * *

I have stood in a storm's eye. I have felt the sky gather itself above the valley like a fist deciding whether to close. I thought I understood what power looked like.

I knew nothing.

The chamber beneath the palace was a hollowed mountain, and it was *full of fire*—but not free fire. Not the wild gold-and-orange thing that lives in hearths and hearts and dragons. This fire moved in channels, carved into the floor in great spiraling grooves, flowing like water through an aqueduct, and every channel ran inward, toward the center, toward the throne.

Not a throne. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only weapon I had down there. It was a *chair*—a high-backed chair of black iron set atop a pillar of fused glass—and on the chair sat the king of Vessia.

Except.

The light in that chamber came from the fire-channels themselves, and it should have thrown his shadow huge against the far wall. It threw nothing. Where his shadow should have fallen there was only more floor, more stone, as though the light simply declined to acknowledge him, or as though he declined to acknowledge the light, and between those two refusals there was a man-shaped absence wearing Aldric's crown.

He was feeding.

I don't know how else to say it. Along each fire-channel, at intervals, knelt figures in warden gray—but not wardens, I realized, not really, because their wrists were locked in iron cuffs that ran on chains into the channels themselves, and from each cuff, thin as spider silk and bright as a struck match, threads of light rose up out of the bound magicians' skin and streamed away across the chamber into the channels, into the flow, into the center, into *him*.

They weren't screaming. That was the worst part. They knelt with their heads bowed and their faces slack, and the light poured out of them steadily, patiently, the way sap leaves a tapped tree, and some of them—I could see it in the ones nearest us—some of them were smiling.

"He makes them happy," Bramble breathed. Her hand had found mine and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "He gives them back a little bit of what he takes so they won't fight. That's—that's crueler than—"

"Yes," Odessa said.

A line of new prisoners waited along the near wall, chained wrist to wrist, still in their traveling clothes. A farmer. An old woman with river-gray hair. A boy no older than Bramble, staring at the kneeling feeders with the particular blankness of someone doing arithmetic they don't want to finish. Wardens moved among them with ledgers, calling names, and at each name a clerk made a mark, and I understood suddenly, completely, what the marks were for. Inventory. The gifts of Vessia, catalogued and queued, waiting to be poured into the cup.

And the cup sat on its glass pillar, and the cup was empty, and the cup was starving, and the cup was *king*.

* * *

Our overseer—a broad woman with a scarred chin and the dead eyes of someone promoted past the point of feeling—set us to stacking crates along the chamber's rim, close enough to see everything, close enough to be seen seeing nothing.

So I stacked crates. And I watched.

The channels converged on the pillar in nine great rivers of light, and where they met the base of the glass, the fire climbed. It went up the pillar like ivy, like veins, like roots running backward into soil, and it vanished into the seated figure at the top, and the figure *brightened*—just slightly, just at the edges, the way coals breathe when stirred. His face was Aldric's face. I'd seen it on coins and proclamations my whole life, the strong jaw, the patient eyes. But coins don't move, and this face barely did. It was a portrait that occasionally remembered to blink.

Once, while we worked, he turned his head.

Not toward the prisoners. Not toward the wardens. Toward the *channels*—toward the flow itself—with an expression I will carry to my grave: the expression of a man drinking after a long thirst, except that the thirst never ended, and somewhere behind his patient coin-face eyes there was something *else*, something old and enormous and pressed flat into the shape of a king, and for one terrible moment I felt it notice the room the way a fish notices the water.

Then it looked away, and I discovered I had been holding my breath for a very long time.

"The dragon," Odessa whispered beside me. She'd gone the color of tallow. "Yara. The fire. Can't you feel it? It's not ordinary flame. It's *her*. Some of it's her."

I could feel it. Under the stolen light, under the drained gifts, underneath everything, there was a bass note in the fire-channels, deep and slow and grieving, a heartbeat so large it took minutes between beats. The fire that fed the king wasn't fuel. It was a creature. Somewhere below us—or within us, within the palace's bones—something ancient was burning alive to keep a dead man sitting upright.

"How many dragons used to be in Vessia?" I asked, though I knew.

"Nine," Odessa said. "When I was born."

* * *

We finished the crates. We turned to leave with the other conscripts, heads down, trudging, invisible. And then the clerk at the prisoner line called a name that stopped my blood.

"Bramble Thorn—" No. Not that name. But a name like it, a hedge-name, a root-cellar name, and a small figure in the prisoner line lifted her head, and it wasn't our Bramble, it was a boy, it was fine, it was fine—

But our Bramble had gone rigid beside me, and her grip on my sleeve was shaking, and the light in the chamber seemed to lean toward her the way grass leans toward sun, and I understood with cold clarity that the fire in this room *knew* her. Knew the ember-witch blood in her. Was already tasting it.

One of the bound feeders near the wall raised her head. Her eyes found Bramble across forty feet of carved stone, and her smile widened, and she mouthed something—*hello, little sister*—with lips drained nearly white, and Bramble made a sound small enough to fit inside a closed fist.

"Walk," Odessa said, barely moving her lips. "Trudge. Eyes down. We come back. I swear it on the fire itself, we come back for all of them."

We trudged. It was the hardest walking of my life.

* * *

In the barracks corridor afterward, in the gray wash of lamplight, I finally let myself shake.

Odessa crouched in front of me the way you steady a horse. "Breathe."

"I saw him." My voice came out wrong, flattened. "There's nothing *in* him, Dess. There's no king. There's no person. It's a hunger wearing a shape because the shape opens doors. And we're going to walk out of here and sleep tonight and eat tomorrow while that room keeps pouring people into it forever."

"Yes."

"We have to go back."

"Yes."

"Not eventually. Not when we're ready. Perrin needs to see this. Everyone at the Wren needs to see this. There's a ceremony coming—Binding Day, the proclamation said, hundreds of them, a whole harvest in one room—" I heard my own voice rise and made myself stop, made myself press my palms flat against my knees the way Perrin had taught me, storm held like breath, breath held like a secret. "The channels all run to one place. Did you see? Everything he steals flows through one point. One pillar. One crown."

Odessa studied me for a long moment, and something in her face shifted—not comfort, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone watching weather change.

"You're not afraid anymore," she said.

"I'm terrified."

"No. You were afraid before. This is different." She stood and offered me her hand. "This is knowing."

I took her hand and stood, and above us, through fathoms of quarried stone, the false king drank his endless drink, and the last dragon's heart beat its slow grief through the fire, and I thought: *one point. One crown. One storm.*

Somewhere in the palace above, a bell began to ring the hour, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through a wall, I heard the channels surge—hungry, expectant—as if the whole buried machine had heard my thinking and agreed.

Binding Day was eleven days away.

We would need all of them.
