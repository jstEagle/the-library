## 11. The Last Dragon

I have read fire my whole life, and I thought I knew what it could say.

Fire is a gossip. Fire is a braggart. Fire tells you what burned and how fast and whether the person who lit it was angry or afraid or merely careless with a candle. I've read hearth-fires and funeral pyres, forges and forest blazes, and every one of them chattered like a market square. Fire has opinions. Fire has moods. Fire, in my experience, has never once had *grief*.

Until the underpalace.

We came down through a maintenance shaft Perrin remembered from his binder days—a vertical throat of old stone behind a false wall in the wine cellars, slick with condensation, smelling of sulfur and something older than sulfur. Yara went first because Yara always goes first now, storm-scarred hands raised against the dark as if lightning might be coaxed out of bare rock. Bramble clung to my back like a burr, her small weight trembling. Perrin brought up the rear with a shuttered lantern and the expression of a man walking into his own confession made physical.

The shaft ended at a door that should not have existed: black iron, unmarked, warm to the touch. Not forge-warm. *Body*-warm.

"Binding-iron," Perrin said quietly. "A lot of it. This isn't a lock, it's a—"

"Cage," Bramble finished, in a voice too small for the word.

Yara pressed their palm flat against the metal and flinched. "It's humming."

It wasn't humming. It was *breathing*. And when I laid my own hand beside Yara's and opened that sense I keep folded away—the one that reads flame like other people read faces—I stopped being able to breathe at all.

Because beneath us, through forty feet of stone and iron and the accumulated cruelty of a dynasty, something enormous was dying. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a mountain dies, which is to say so gradually that no single moment can be called the death, and yet all of it is nothing but dying.

"Odessa?" Yara's voice came from very far away. "What do you see?"

I didn't see. That was the thing. For the first time in my life I wasn't reading fire from outside it, the way you'd read a letter someone else wrote. I was standing close enough to a flame this vast that its words were pouring directly into me, unfiltered, untranslated, and they were not words at all but *weight*—centuries of them, pressing down like snowfall on a roof built for rain.

"She's thinking," I said, before I understood I was going to say it aloud. "Gods help me, she's been thinking the whole time."

* * *

Perrin picked the cage-door's mechanism with hands that shook only a little, which for Perrin constitutes heroic composure. The iron groaned open onto a gallery—a narrow stone walkway ringing a chamber so large the lantern light simply gave up partway across it.

And there she was.

Vaskarath.

I knew the name the way every gifted child knows it, from lullabies and warnings: the last dragon of Vessia, the fire that crowned the first king, the flame that was promised sanctuary in exchange for service. Sanctuary. I want you to hold that word in your mouth while I describe what we saw, and see how long it survives.

She lay coiled in the center of the chamber like a fallen constellation, and she was chained—not with rope, not with iron alone, but with *ritual*. Dozens of binding-anchors studded the floor around her, each one connected to her body by lines of pale light that pulsed in slow rhythm. Siphon-lines. Every pulse drew something out of her and sent it up through channels in the rock, up toward the palace above, up toward the throne room where a crowned absence wore a dead man's face and called itself a king.

Her scales were the color of embers at the end of a long night—deep red shading into ash-grey along her belly, along the edges of her wings, everywhere the hunger had fed longest. She was vast beyond sense; her skull alone was bigger than the Gilded Wren's common room. Her eye, when it found us on the gallery, was the size of a cart wheel and the color of cooling gold.

And she was *tired*. That was the first thing her fire told me, before anything else, before the words even formed. Tired the way nothing mortal has ever been tired. She had been burning for two thousand years and giving most of herself away for three hundred of them, and she had reached the place where the fire no longer asked whether she wanted to burn. It simply did, because stopping would mean everything she'd ever warmed going cold.

"Oh," said Bramble, from my back, in a whisper. "Oh, she's *sad*."

"Yes," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "Yes, little one. She is."

* * *

Here is what I heard, standing on that gallery with my gift wide open and no way to close it:

I heard her remember hatching. Actual memory, carried in flame, given to me whole: darkness and warmth and the drumbeat of her mother's heart through the shell, and then the world arriving all at once—cold air, starlight, the smell of pine and snow. She was the last of eleven. She never learned what happened to the other ten, only that each year there were fewer fires in the world to read, until hers was the only voice left speaking in a language of heat.

I heard her remember the first king, Aldric's ancestor, kneeling before her in this same chamber when it was open sky and not a prison. *Lend me your flame,* he'd said, *and Vessia will shelter your line forever.* She had laughed—dragons laugh, it sounds like a landslide deciding to be gentle—and she had lent it. A trickle. A kindness. A candle held out to a cold traveler.

And I heard her remember, over centuries, the trickle becoming a stream becoming a river becoming a theft. Each generation of kings taking more, promising more, building chains disguised as honors. Until the year the true Aldric came down here—alone, at night, an old man by then—and knelt again, and wept, and said: *Something is coming for my crown. Something that eats. When it takes me, it will take you too. I am sorry. I was not strong enough to stop what my family became.*

He had tried to warn her. That was the worst of it, or maybe the best. The dead king hadn't been a collaborator; he'd been a hostage who spent his last years trying to leave a message in a bottle, and the message was a dragon, and nobody had come.

Then the hunger arrived, wearing his face, and it had drunk deep, and it had been drinking ever since.

"I can't—" I put my hand over my mouth. The fire-words were still coming, faster now, tumbling. "She's talking to me. She's been alone with her thoughts for three hundred years and now she won't *stop*—"

"Can you answer her?" Yara asked. They had gone very still, the way they go still when a storm is deciding something about them.

"I don't know. I've never—fire doesn't usually talk *back*—"

But I tried. I took the ember-sense of her, the great slow furnace of her attention, and I did the only thing I could think of: I thought *we see you* as hard as I have ever thought anything, and pushed it into the light pulsing off her scales.

The chamber changed.

Every siphon-line flickered. The vast gold eye widened. And the temperature of the room rose—not dangerously, not threateningly, just the way a room warms when someone in it stops pretending to be asleep.

*SMALL ONE*, her fire said, and it was less a sound than a sunrise happening inside my skull. *YOU READ. THE KINGS NEVER READ. THEY ONLY TOOK.*

"Yes," I managed. "We're not—they're not with us. We came to free you."

*I KNOW WHY YOU CAME.* A pause, and in the pause, grief so old it had become geology. *I HAVE WATCHED YOU SINCE YOU ENTERED MY MOUNTAIN. THE STORM-CHILD. THE DESERTER. THE SMALL WITCH WHO SMELLS OF ROOTS AND TEARS.* Her gaze moved across us, deliberate, weighing. *AND I KNOW WHAT YOUR PLAN IS, BECAUSE IT IS A GOOD PLAN AND GOOD PLANS ARE RARE. SEVER ME DURING THE CEREMONY, WHEN THE HUNGER DRINKS DEEPEST AND ITS ATTENTION IS ABOVE.*

"Then you'll help us," Yara said.

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Bramble to slide off my back and stand between us all, fists clenched, glaring up at a creature that could have ended her with an exhale.

*STORM-CHILD,* Vaskarath said at last, and her fire had gone quiet in a way that frightened me more than rage would have. *DO YOU KNOW WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE SIPHON SEVERS? THREE HUNDRED YEARS OF STOLEN FLAME COMES HOME TO ME ALL AT ONCE. EVERY GIFT THE HUNGER BOUND AND FED INTO ME—THE WEATHER-READERS, THE EMBER-SPEAKERS, THE HEALERS, THE CHILDREN—ALL OF IT RETURNS TO ITS SOURCE THROUGH MY BODY.* The great eye closed. Opened. *I DO NOT KNOW IF I CAN HOLD THAT MUCH FIRE AND REMAIN MYSELF. I MAY BECOME SOMETHING ELSE. I MAY BURN THIS CITY ACCIDENTALLY, THE WAY A DYING MAN KNOCKS OVER CANDLES. OR I MAY SIMPLY DIE, AND TAKE MAGIC'S LAST LIVING SOURCE WITH ME.*

"So we shouldn't free you," Yara said slowly. "Is that what you're saying?"

*I AM SAYING FREE ME ANYWAY.* The words came out like a bell struck once. *BECAUSE THE ALTERNATIVE IS THAT THE HUNGER DRINKS ME DRY WITHIN THE YEAR, AND THEN THERE IS NO MAGIC IN VESSIA AT ALL, EVER, AND YOUR GRANDCHILDREN WILL TELL FAIRY TALES ABOUT PEOPLE LIKE YOU. I WOULD RATHER END AS A CHOICE THAN CONTINUE AS A MEAL.*

Bramble started crying. Quietly, furiously, wiping her face with her sleeve like the tears were a personal betrayal. "That's not fair," she said. "That's not *fair*, you've been down here forever and you're *good*, I can tell you're good—"

*SMALL WITCH.* The dragon's voice gentled, if fire can be said to gentle, and I felt it happen, felt the vast furnace bank itself low for the sake of a child. *GOODNESS IS NOT THE SAME AS SAFETY. LEARN THAT NOW AND IT WILL SAVE YOU GRIEF LATER.* A pause. *THOUGH FOR WHAT IT IS WORTH: I THINK I WOULD LIKE TO SEE THE SKY AGAIN BEFORE I DECIDE WHAT I AM.*

* * *

We stayed as long as we dared. An hour, perhaps two, sitting on the gallery like pilgrims while she talked—or rather, while she *poured*, three centuries of solitude finding its first listeners. She told us about the shape of clouds before the kings built spires to pierce them. She told us the names of rivers that no longer exist. She recited, once, the full genealogy of the royal house of Vessia with the tone of someone describing a long illness, and then she taught me something I will carry until I die: how to listen to a flame without opening yourself to it entirely. How to leave a door in the wall of yourself. *A READER WHO CANNOT LOOK AWAY,* she said, *IS ONLY A MIRROR. BE A WINDOW.*

When Perrin finally stood and said, with visible anguish, "The changing of the siphon-guard is in an hour. If we're caught down here—"

"We go," Yara said. But they didn't move. They were looking at the chains—at the pale lines of light running from the anchors into the dragon's body—and their hands had curled into fists, and the air around them smelled the way it does before summer rain.

"Yara." I touched their shoulder. "Not today. Today we learn the locks. Binding Day, we cut them."

"I know." Their voice was rough. "I know. It's just—" They looked up at her, at the ash-grey edges of her wings, at the slow pulse of the siphons drinking her life upward. "She's been down here since before my grandmother's grandmother. And everyone above us is having dinner."

*YES,* said Vaskarath, and her fire curled around the word almost tenderly. *THAT IS EXACTLY WHAT IT IS LIKE. GO NOW, STORM-CHILD. AND LISTEN WELL, BECAUSE I WILL ONLY SAY THIS ONCE:* The great head lowered until her eye was level with us, close enough that I could feel the ancient heat of her breath. *WHEN YOU SEVER THE SIPHON, DO NOT CUT THE LINES CLEANLY. SHATTER THEM. LET THE STOLEN GIFTS SCATTER BACK TO THEIR OWNERS BEFORE THEY PASS THROUGH ME. I AM STRONG ENOUGH TO OPEN THE WAY. I AM NOT STRONG ENOUGH TO FILTER THREE HUNDRED YEARS OF THEFT.*

"And if we shatter them," Perrin said, paling, "the binding-iron network reverses. On every bound magician in the kingdom. All at once."

*YES,* said the dragon, and something moved through her fire that I can only call hope, though it was hope with teeth. *EVERY CAGE IN VESSIA OPENS ON THE SAME NIGHT. CHOOSE YOUR DAY ACCORDINGLY.*

* * *

We climbed back up the maintenance shaft in silence. At the false wall, Perrin paused with his hand on the stone and said, to no one in particular, "I built the anchor lattice. Third ring, south quadrant. My design." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "She knew my name. She's known my name for twenty years."

"Perrin—"

"Don't." He pushed through the wall. "Don't absolve me. Just let me build the thing that undoes it."

Bramble fell asleep against my side on the walk back to the Wren, exhausted past tears, and I carried her through the dark streets of the capital thinking about windows and mirrors, about doors left in walls, about a child who hid in a root cellar while her family was bound and who had just told a dragon she was *good*—and meant it, and been believed.

Above us, the palace spires caught the moonlight, beautiful as knives. Somewhere up there, the crowned hunger sat on its stolen throne, drinking its nightly ration of a dying woman's fire, counting down to Binding Day with the patience of a thing that has never once lost.

It didn't know we'd been below. It didn't know its furnace had learned our names.

And it certainly didn't know that as we slipped back into the city's ordinary midnight, Odessa Quill—fire-reader, exile, coward by her own long accounting—was already composing the message that would go out with dawn through every safehouse and hedge-hearth in the resistance network, five words that would change everything:

*The dragon is awake. Hurry.*
