## 12. Bramble Stolen

There is a particular sound a city makes when it decides you don't belong to it. I'd heard it my whole life in reverse—the Salt Valley's silence, which was a kind of belonging—and now, three days into wearing a warden's grey coat in the capital of Vessia, I finally heard its opposite: the clatter and cough and commerce of ten thousand people who would hand me to the crown if they knew what slept under my skin.

I was getting good at not hearing it.

That should have been my first warning. Comfort is a trap with soft walls.

* * *

The plan, such as Odessa had built it, was simple in the way that bridges are simple: cross the gap, don't look down. We were conscripts now, third-shift wardens assigned to the Sable Precinct, faces under hoods, names on a roster we'd stolen from a man whose snoring could have qualified as weather. Perrin had forged our papers himself, with the same hands that had forged the binding-iron, and he'd said something while he worked that I kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it.

"I know exactly how they'll look for you," he'd said. "Because I taught them."

Bramble was supposed to stay at the safehouse. That was the whole point of the argument we'd had, the one Odessa won and then lost, because Bramble had looked up at us with those grave amber eyes and said, "If I stay where it's safe, then safe is just another word for alone." She was nine. Nine-year-olds should not be able to dismantle an adult's logic before breakfast. But she'd been hiding in a root cellar while her family was bound, and she had decided, with the terrible clarity of children who have already survived the worst thing, that she would rather stand next to us than behind us.

So we compromised, which is a word adults use when children win. Bramble came with us as far as the Ember Market, where she'd sit in plain sight selling little carved charms—ungifted charms, just wood and string—while we walked our warden rounds two streets over. A child selling trinkets draws no eye. A child hiding draws every eye. It was, I will note for the record, Odessa's idea, and she defended it with the flat certainty of someone reading fire, which is what she'd done: she'd read the market's flames and seen nothing coming.

Fire, as it turns out, does not read everything.

* * *

The morning of it started ordinary, which I have since learned to distrust more than any omen. Ordinary mornings are the ones that break you.

Our rounds took us past the granary, past the tithe-house with its new iron cage bolted above the door—a display cage, empty, meant to be understood without explanation. The city had gotten good at understanding without explanation. People stepped around the cage the way you step around a beggar: quickly, eyes down, grateful it wasn't you.

"Third shift reports," said the sergeant, a woman named Hesk with a jaw like a drawer slamming shut. She handed us the day's list. "Two registrations lapsed. One unregistered gift reported in the Ember Market. Child, possibly. Hedge-stock."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once, the way it does before lightning.

"Child?" Odessa asked, and her voice was perfect, bored, professional. Mine was still somewhere back at the safehouse, screaming.

"Ember-speaker, they think. Little charms that warm themselves. Someone saw one move on its own." Hesk shrugged. "Crown wants it brought in gentle. Bound stock feeds better unbruised."

*Bound stock feeds better unbruised.* I made myself hold the sentence in my head, turn it, examine it, because if I let myself feel it I would do something unforgivable in front of a sergeant with a drawer-slam jaw. Somewhere under the palace, chained in the dark, the last dragon burned so that a dead king could keep pretending to be alive, and the fuel wasn't only dragon-fire anymore. It was this. It was children's small warm magics, harvested like apples.

"We'll see to it," Odessa said.

We walked toward the Ember Market at a pace we carefully did not increase.

* * *

I saw Bramble before I saw anything wrong, which is how I know, in hindsight, that some part of me had already understood. She was at her corner with her tray of charms, and she was smiling at a customer, and her smile was the practiced kind, the kind you wear when your body has decided to be brave before your heart has been consulted.

Three wardens stood near her. Not our precinct. Real ones, with real rosters and real iron, and one of them was holding one of her charms up to the light, and the charm was glowing, faintly, gold as a coal, warming his palm without his permission.

"No," I said. Out loud. In the street. In my warden's voice, which was worse, because it carried authority.

Odessa's hand closed on my arm. *Not here. Not yet.*

But the warden holding the charm had heard something—or felt it, the way animals feel weather—and he turned, and his eyes went past me, past Odessa, and landed on the small girl with the tray, and I watched him do the arithmetic. Child. Charms. Ember-glow. Report.

"Bramble!" I called, and dropped all pretense of being anyone but what I was. "Run!"

She ran. Of course she ran; she'd had practice running since before she could write her own name. She got eleven steps. I counted them because I have never stopped counting them. Eleven steps toward the mouth of Copperlane, where the crowd was thick and a small body could vanish, and then a warden's hand closed on her hood and yanked, and she went down hard on the cobbles, and the sound she made—

The sound she made was not a scream. That's the thing I need you to understand. She didn't scream. She made a small, furious, animal noise, the noise of someone who has been taken before, who knows exactly what being taken means, and who refuses on principle to give them the satisfaction of fear. She bit the warden's wrist. She clawed. She was nine years old and fighting like the last ember of a house fire, and the second warden pulled out a coil of binding-iron, thin as wire, black as a drowned thing, and reached for her wrists.

Something in me broke loose.

I want to describe it honestly, because honesty is the only penance I can pay. It didn't feel like power. It felt like a dam failing—not water bursting through, but the whole structure ceasing to matter, the river remembering that it had never needed permission. My storm-scarred hands, the hands I'd hidden under salt and wool for twenty years, lit up blue-white from wrist to fingertip, and the sky above the Ember Market answered.

It had been a grey morning. It stopped being anything. The clouds came down like a lid, spinning, green-black at their hearts, and the first bolt hit the cobblestones thirty feet from anyone—I swear that, whatever else is true, the first bolt missed everyone, because even uncontrolled, some animal part of me aimed away from Bramble—and the street split like bread.

Then the aiming stopped.

* * *

Here is what rage without control looks like from the inside: it doesn't look like anything. That's the horror of it. There is no vantage point, no self watching itself. There is only the storm, and the storm is not angry, the storm has no opinions, the storm simply *is*, total and indifferent, and I was inside it the way a spark is inside a wildfire—present, contributing, utterly unable to steer.

Lightning fell in sheets. The granary's roof peeled back like a lid off a pot. The tithe-house—with its empty display cage, its mute threat—took a bolt through the eaves and folded inward, stone shrieking, and I remember thinking, in the last flicker of myself, *good,* and being frightened by how much I meant it.

People ran. Most of them lived. I tell myself most of them lived, because I have to, because the count I keep at night includes a baker's boy with a burned shoulder and a woman struck deaf by the thunderclap and a man whose leg was broken by falling masonry, and *most* is the best I can honestly claim. Half a city block came down in smoke and rain. The wardens holding Bramble were thrown—thrown, not killed, thrown like dolls, and the binding-iron coil spun off into the wreckage and lay there hissing in a puddle like something dying.

And through all of it, one thread of me stayed knotted to a single point: the small figure on the cobbles, scrambling up, looking around at the apocalypse wearing her friend's face, and finding me in the center of it.

Her eyes weren't wide with fear of the wardens anymore.

They were wide with fear of me.

* * *

I came back to myself kneeling in a puddle, rain hammering my shoulders, steam rising off my hands. The storm was withdrawing the way a tide withdraws—reluctantly, leaving debris. My ears rang. Somewhere close, a wall settled with a groan. The air tasted of ozone and burnt flour from the granary, and under that, faintly, blood.

"Yara." Odessa, crouched beside me, her hood gone, her face streaked with soot. Her hands were on my shoulders, shaking me. "Yara. Look at me. She's fine. She's fine, she's right there—"

Bramble was right there. Standing six feet away in the rain, holding her empty tray against her chest like a shield, staring at me.

"Bramble," I said. My voice came out ruined, scraped raw by thunder. "I—"

"You did that," she said. Not a question. Not even an accusation. Just a fact, laid down between us like the binding-iron lying in the puddle, and a nine-year-old looking at it.

"The whole block," I said. "I did the whole block."

"Yes," she said. And then, because she was Bramble, because she was the child who hid in a root cellar and came out deciding to stand next to people instead of behind them, she crossed the six feet of rubble-strewn mud and put her small hand on my smoking wrist. "They were going to take me," she said. "You stopped them."

"That's not—" I swallowed. "There are easier ways to stop someone than ending the weather."

"Maybe." Her thumb moved over the old storm-scars, careful, curious, the way she touched everything she was learning. "But none of them would've worked."

Odessa hauled us both up. Around us, the market was a wound—smoke, rain, the groan of damaged buildings, people emerging from doorways to stare at the three figures standing in the ruin. And among the staring faces, moving fast, more grey coats. Wardens from three precincts converging, drawn by the loudest unregistered gift in Vessian history.

"They'll call it an attack," Odessa said, low and fast, steering us into Copperlane's mouth. "A magical assault on the capital. Every warden in the city just got a description of you, Yara. Not a rumor. A *description*. Storm-caller, storm-scarred, last seen in a warden's coat." Her jaw tightened. "Perrin's coat. They'll tear the Gilded Wren apart looking for whoever dressed you."

"Perrin—"

"Is either gone or captured by nightfall unless we warn him first. Move."

We moved. Behind us, the rain kept falling on the half-block I had killed, and ahead of us the city narrowed into alleys, and I carried two things out of the Ember Market that I had not carried in: Bramble's hand in mine, gripping hard, choosing me anyway—and the memory of her eyes going wide, the exact moment the storm showed her what I was capable of becoming.

The usurper fed on bound gifts. I had always known that. What I learned in the rain, kneeling in the wreck of a city block, was that the crown didn't need to bind me.

It only needed to wait until I did it to myself.

* * *

We reached the sewer grate off Tanner's Row as the bells began—alarm bells, rolling across the rooftops, calling every grey coat in Vessia toward the smoking quarter. Odessa pried the grate with a knife. Bramble went down first, sure-footed in the dark, and I paused at the lip of it, one last look back at the city I'd just introduced myself to.

On the horizon, past the spires, the palace stood untouched and gleaming, and I could have sworn—though Odessa says the distance makes it impossible—that I felt something up there turn its attention toward the storm's fading edge. Something patient. Something that had just heard my name spoken in lightning, and found it interesting.

I climbed down into the dark, and the grate clanged shut above us, and somewhere below the city, in the undercity tunnels where hunted things learn to live, Perrin was waiting to teach me the difference between a storm and a weapon.

I only hoped I could learn it before the waiting thing in the palace decided I was worth collecting.
