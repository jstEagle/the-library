## 13. Learning to Hold Lightning

The undercity of Vessarion smells like old rain and older secrets, and I have spent enough nights down here to know both intimately. The sewers beneath the capital were built by the same dynasty that built the palace above—grand vaulted channels meant to carry away the filth of ten thousand hearths—and like everything that dynasty built, they were overengineered for a problem that never came and underbuilt for the one that did.

Which is to say: there is room down here for six fugitives, one captured binder's guilt, and exactly nowhere to practice storm-calling without bringing half the district down on our heads.

"You're going to have to stop doing that," I said, on our third night in the flooded cistern we'd claimed as home, when Yara's hair began to lift off their neck in fine dark threads and the water around their boots started crawling uphill.

"Doing what?" Yara asked, which was either genuine ignorance or the most impressive deadpan I'd encountered since leaving court, and I genuinely could not tell which.

"The air is ionizing. Your shadow has gone slightly wrong. And Bramble's spoon just stood up in her bowl."

Bramble looked at her spoon. The spoon, showing more sense than most of us, lay back down.

Yara closed their eyes and breathed out through their nose, slow and deliberate, the way Odessa had taught them on the road—the breath-holding exercise, magic kept in the lungs like smoke you weren't ready to exhale. It worked, mostly. It had gotten them this far. But I'd watched them do it a dozen times now, and every time I saw the same thing underneath: not control. Containment. A fist clenched around something alive, held shut by will alone, and will is a muscle, and muscles tire.

I knew, because I had built the thing that exploited exactly that weakness.

* * *

Let me be precise about my crimes, because Yara was going to learn them anyway, and I preferred they learned them from me rather than from the way my hands shook around binding-iron.

Ten years ago I was the youngest royal binder in Vessia's history, appointed at twenty-six because I had solved a problem no one else could solve: how to make iron hold a gift. Before me, binding was crude—a collar, a brand, a suppression that broke the gifted as often as it broke their power. I made it elegant. I made it *humane*, or so I told myself. My bindings didn't shatter the gift; they shaped it into a loop that fed back on itself, quiet and self-sustaining. No pain. No screaming. Just a person walking around with their own magic folded up inside them like a letter never sent.

The court loved me. The gifted feared me quietly, because even humane cages are cages, and I told myself their fear was the price of order. There had been a war coming—I believed this then, I believe it less now—gifted houses against ungifted lords, fire against grain, and someone had to stand between. I stood between. I built the instruments of standing-between. And then, in the last year of King Aldric's life, I built the ritual that let something else wear his crown.

I did not know that was what I was building. That is the sentence I have repeated to myself for years, and it is true, and it does not matter, because the difference between the man who builds a lock and the man whose lock is used on his own kingdom is mostly a matter of timing.

So when I say I taught Yara Villalobos binding techniques in a sewer under the city they were born to save, understand what I was actually doing: I was taking apart my own machine with my hands, bolt by bolt, and handing the pieces to a storm.

* * *

"Show me again," Yara said, "what happens when it goes wrong."

We stood in the dry gallery above the cistern—an old inspection corridor, brick-vaulted, its far end choked with collapsed masonry that would, I hoped, absorb whatever Yara failed to contain. Odessa sat on an overturned cart with Bramble asleep against her shoulder, having insisted on watching despite my objections. Perrin, she'd said, if you're going to confess your sins, we should all get to see the penance. She had a way of saying devastating things in the tone of someone discussing bread prices.

"What happens," I said, "is that your gift looks for the shape you give it. If you give it no shape, it takes the nearest one—which is usually fear, which is usually *outward*. Half a city block, last time."

"I didn't mean—"

"I know you didn't. That's rather the point." I picked up the length of copper wire I'd salvaged from a broken conduit and wound it loosely around my left hand. "Binding-iron works on three principles. Everyone thinks it's about force. It isn't. It's about *shape*. First principle: a gift flows toward the path of least resistance. Second: a gift cannot be destroyed, only redirected. Third—" I paused, because this was the principle I hated most, the one I'd written into the royal manuals in my own neat hand. "Third: a bound gift turns inward eventually. Against its holder. Every binding I ever placed was a debt, and the interest came due."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you've been binding yourself for twenty years, Yara. Badly, unconsciously, but the valley salt did the same work my iron did—it gave your storm nowhere to go, so it went inward. You are carrying twenty years of unpaid interest." I watched them take that in. Their hands, those scarred hands they'd hidden their whole life, curled slightly at their sides. "When you finally let go, all of it comes out at once. That's why it's half a city block instead of a thunderclap."

"So what do I do? Unbind faster?"

"No. You pay as you go." I tossed them the wire. "Catch."

They caught it. The wire hummed faintly against their palm—not from any enchantment in it, copper holds almost nothing, but from what Yara's skin did to it. Tiny blue sparks crawled the length of the coil like insects waking.

"That feeling," I said. "That's your gift finding a path. Right now it's a bad path—random, leaking. We're going to teach it better ones. Not walls. Channels."

Odessa spoke from the cart, drowsy but sharp-eyed. "You invented the cage, Perrin. Now you're teaching locksport."

"I'm teaching architecture," I said. "There's a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my life demonstrating it."

* * *

The training, if I'm honest, nearly killed us both in the first week.

Not because Yara lacked talent—they had talent the way rivers have water—but because their talent had grown wild in the dark for two decades, and wild things do not distinguish between the hand that feeds them and the hand that reaches too fast. On the fourth day, working on the grounding exercise—feet planted, gift drawn down through the body and into wet stone like lightning into a lightning rod—Yara lost the thread of it for perhaps half a second. Half a second was enough. The discharge took me off my feet, threw me eight feet down the corridor, and left a Y-shaped scorch on the brick wall that I later decided to keep, as a sort of monument.

I woke with Bramble's small face hovering over mine, ferociously solemn. "You're not dead," she reported. "Your eyebrows are different though."

"A fair trade," I rasped, "for the lesson."

And it was a lesson, though not the one I'd planned. Because when I got my breath back and looked at Yara, they were standing very still with their hands pressed flat against their thighs, and their face was the face of someone watching a door close.

"I hurt you," they said.

"You startled me."

"I *hurt* you. This is—" Their voice cracked and reassembled itself lower. "This is why the valley sent me away. This is why I kept it down. Every time it gets loose, someone pays. Maybe it should just stay buried."

And there it was. Twenty years of the elders' careful silence, the scarred hands kept gloved, the whole architecture of shame that a valley built to keep itself safe and had, in the process, taught a child that their own nature was a debt they could never repay. I had heard that logic before. I had *written* that logic before, in gentler language, in the preface to the Royal Manual of Binding: *the gifted suffer most when ungoverned.* I had put chains on people and called the chafing kindness.

I got up off the floor. My back informed me, at length, of its opinions.

"Yara. Sit down. No—stand, actually, stand there and look at that wall." I pointed at the scorch mark. "What do you see?"

"...A mistake."

"I see a half-second lapse during a first-week exercise, producing a scorch mark and a bruised binder. Do you know what a half-second lapse produces in a trained weather-caller? A redirected gust. In a trained ember-speaker? A candle snuffed. What you produced was enormous because you are enormous, and untrained, and terrified, and those are all fixable except possibly the enormous, which frankly I'd keep." I stepped closer. "You want to bury it. I understand. I spent ten years teaching other people to bury theirs, and I will tell you what I learned in the worst possible classroom: buried gifts don't die. They wait. And everything that waits underground eventually comes up under someone's foundations."

Bramble, from her perch on the cart, said with the terrible clarity of seven-year-olds: "Like roots cracking the paving stones. Mama said that about the oak on Ember Road."

"Exactly like that," I said, and did not look at the burned waystation in my memory, or the small cellar door, or the family that hadn't found a cellar. "Discipline is not suppression, Yara. Suppression says *no*. Discipline says *not yet, not here, not that way—this way*. A river dammed floods. A river channeled turns a millwheel, carries boats, drinks a city. Same water. The difference is whether anyone bothered to love it enough to give it somewhere to go."

Yara was quiet for a long time. The cistern below dripped its slow count. Then they held up their hand, palm out, and let the sparks come—not flung, not clamped, just *invited*, crawling across their fingers in slow blue constellations, and their expression as they watched them was the expression of someone greeting a part of themselves they'd been taught to apologize for.

"Not yet," they said to the sparks. "But this way."

It was, I am not ashamed to say, the closest I have come to prayer in fifteen years.

* * *

We built the curriculum out of wreckage, which felt appropriate.

I taught them the Three Loops—the feedback structures at the heart of every binding I'd ever laid—but inverted, run outward instead of inward, so the gift circled through the body and back into the world instead of folding against itself. Where my iron said *turn here, and here, and never leave the circuit*, my voice said *turn here, and here, and choose your own exit*. The geometry was nearly identical. That was the bitter joke of it, the joke I laughed at alone at night while the others slept: I had always known how gifts wanted to move. I had simply spent my career moving them where the crown wanted.

Yara learned the grounding first, then the channeling, then—hardest, slowest—the *naming*. That was Odessa's addition, actually. Fire-reader that she was, she understood instinctively that power answers to what you call it, and she sat Yara down one evening and said, "Stop calling it 'it.' What is your storm's name?"

"It doesn't have a name."

"Everything that survives twenty years in the dark has a name. Ask it."

Yara asked. I won't repeat what answer they got—that belongs to them—but I will say that after that night the exercises changed. The sparks stopped leaking and started *listening*. When Yara raised wind in the cistern, it moved in spirals instead of gusts. When they grounded a charge through the wet stone, the stone warmed gently instead of fracturing. Small mercies. Enormous mercies. The difference between a weapon and a self, measured in millimeters of intention.

I taught them one more thing, near the end of that first month, and it cost me more than all the rest together.

"Hold out your hand," I said, and produced from my coat the thing I had carried since the raid on the Wren: a single link of binding-iron, torn from Perrin-the-binder's own kit, the metal dull and hungry-looking even now. Yara flinched from it. Everyone did. It was designed to be flinched from.

"This is mine," I said. "I forged the first of these when I was twenty-four. I want you to break it."

"I can't—"

"You can. Not with force—force is what it eats. With shape. It works by giving a gift a closed loop, yes? So open the loop. Find its circle and draw a line out of it. Treat it like a knot that wants untying, because that's all it ever was. All it ever *was*." My voice did something embarrassing on the last word, and I let it, because hiding it would have been one more binding and I was, that month, trying to practice what I preached.

Yara took the link. Held it. Closed their eyes. And I felt it happen—I felt it in my teeth, in the old scars along my forearms where my own binding-iron had kissed me—a sensation like a held breath releasing after years, like a letter finally opened. The link split down its seam with a sound like a sigh and fell into two harmless curls of metal on the floor.

Yara stared at them. Then at me. "That's what it feels like," they said softly. "Being unbound. That's what everyone feels."

"Yes," I said. "Now you know what I took from people. And what I'd like to give back."

Bramble picked up one of the curling halves, turned it over, and handed it back to me with great ceremony. "For your collection of mistakes," she said. "So you remember."

Out of the mouths of hedge-witches. I kept it. Of course I kept it.

* * *

On the last night of that first month, Odessa came down from the grate where she kept watch on the street above, moving fast and quiet in the way that meant fast news.

"Wardens doubled the checkpoints on Binding Day routes," she said. "Ceremony's in nine days. They're building the binding-stage in Coronation Square—big enough for four hundred."

"Four hundred," Yara repeated. Their hands had gone still at their sides, but it was a different stillness than the old kind—not a fist, not a cage. A drawn bow.

"Perrin." Odessa's eyes found mine in the lamplight. "Those loops you taught Yara. The inverted ones. Could you run them at scale? Through a crowd? Through a *crown*?"

I thought about it. I thought about the geometry of the coronet, which I had helped design, which I knew the way a locksmith knows a lock—including the flaw I'd built into it and never told anyone, because at the time I'd told myself it was a safeguard and not a confession waiting for a purpose.

"Yes," I said. "If someone can get me close enough. And if the storm can hold."

Yara looked at their hands. Blue light moved under the skin there, patient now, channeled, *named*—a river that knew its banks and remembered the sea.

"It can hold," they said.

Above us, muffled through forty feet of brick and earth, the city rang its bells for the changing of the watch, and nine days suddenly sounded like no time at all.
