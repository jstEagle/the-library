## 14. The Resistance of Small Gifts

The Gilded Wren smelled different in the mornings, before the lamps were lit and the first pot of barley tea went on. In the dark it was all woodsmoke and spilled ale and the ghost of a hundred conversations that had never quite been safe to have anywhere else. I'd been there three days since we'd come down out of the sewers with Perrin's absence sitting on my chest like a wet wool coat, and I still woke every morning expecting to hear him thumping around the kitchen, complaining about how Bramble had charred his best skillet again.

He wasn't there. He was in a warden cell somewhere under the Spire, or worse, and the only thing worse than that was a list I didn't want to finish.

So I got up instead. That was the whole trick of grief, I was learning: you got up, and you did the next thing, and if the next thing was small enough you could do it without thinking about the size of everything else.

The next thing this morning was small indeed. It was a hedge-witch named Maren who came up from the cellar before dawn with dirt still under her fingernails and a basket of beans that grew too fast.

"Watch," she said, and she wasn't asking me, exactly. She was asking the room, which happened to contain me.

She set three dried beans on the table, murmured something that sounded like an argument rather than an incantation, and the beans split, sprouted, climbed, flowered, and hung heavy with new pods in the space it takes to pour tea. Then she swept them into her hand, scowled at them like they'd disappointed her, and said, "That's nothing. That's garden magic. My gran did it better."

"It's not nothing," Odessa said from the doorway. She had that look she got when she was doing arithmetic nobody else could see. "How many kitchens feed the garrison?"

Maren blinked. "I—hundreds. Thousands."

"And how many of them would notice one sack of beans that spoiled overnight? Or bread that rose wrong? Or a crop that came in thin?" Odessa set her cup down. "You're not nothing, Maren. You're a supply line."

I watched the woman's face do something complicated — the same thing mine had done when Odessa first told me my storm wasn't a curse but a *name*, and names could be spoken on purpose. It was the face of someone being told their smallness was actually a door.

That was the week the Wren stopped being an inn and started being a war.

* * *

Perrin had left instructions, because of course he had. The man treated his own capture like a scheduling inconvenience. Tucked inside a false bottom of the bar, beneath a bottle of plum brandy he'd labeled *DO NOT DRINK, YARA, I MEAN IT*, we found his ledger: years of notes on the binding-iron, the siphon lines that ran beneath the capital like roots under a tree, and — most importantly — the ceremony.

Binding Day. Once a year, the crown paraded its captured magicians through the streets and bound them publicly, feeding their gifts into the great reservoir beneath the palace. A festival, they called it. Bunting and bells and a hundred people led in chains to have themselves hollowed out for the king's dinner.

This year, the proclamation said, it would be the largest Binding Day in history. The purge had been *fruitful*. The words made my teeth ache.

"They'll want a spectacle," Odessa said, pacing the length of the common room, which she'd claimed as hers by right of never sitting down. "A crowned procession. The usurper will appear in person — it always does, on Binding Day. It has to. The crowd's awe is part of the meal."

"So we don't hit the garrison," I said. "We hit the plumbing."

Bramble looked up from where she was braiding straw into little charms that weren't charms, just fidgeting, just a child keeping her hands busy so her head stayed quiet. "Plumbing," she repeated, delighted.

"The siphon," I said. "Perrin's notes say the gifts don't go straight into the crown. They go into a reservoir first — a holding tank of stolen fire under the processional square. The crown feeds from it during the ceremony. If we sever the connection while the reservoir is full—" I looked at Odessa. "What happens when a thing that eats is handed a meal it can't swallow?"

Odessa smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "It chokes."

* * *

They came to us over the following days, quiet as snowfall. That was the thing about the network Perrin had built and never told us the size of: it wasn't an army. It was a rumor with hands.

A weather-reader named Sef arrived first, a narrow man with river-gray eyes who could feel a storm coming three days off and had spent forty years pretending to be merely unlucky about picnics. He spread a hand-drawn map of the city's rooftops on the common room table and traced the wind patterns with one finger.

"Binding Day, the wind comes off the eastern hills by midmorning," he said. "If someone were to ask it to come harder — and I'm not saying anyone should, I'm saying it *could* — you'd get a crosswind strong enough to snap banners, scatter pigeons, blind archers on the rooftops. Ten minutes of bad weather. Maybe fifteen."

"Fifteen minutes," Odessa repeated, writing it down.

"I can't make rain," Sef said quickly, apologetically, as though he owed us an apology for the shape of his gift. "Just wind. Just knowing."

"Just knowing wins wars," I said, and he looked at me like I'd handed him something breakable.

Then came the ember-speakers — two sisters, Tolliver twins by marriage, who could coax heat from cold iron and, more usefully, persuade it to leave. One touch from either of them and a lock's tempering went soft as wax; a hinge forgot how to hold; a chain remembered it was only links. They demonstrated on the kitchen stove, which had been sulking since Bramble's last attempt at supper, and had it purring in seconds.

"Chains," Odessa said, her voice going careful. "The prisoners on Binding Day are held in binding-iron. Can you—"

"We can't unbind a gift," the elder sister said. "Nobody can unbind what the iron's done. But the iron itself, the manacles, the collars—" she flexed her fingers, "—we can make them jewelry."

And then there was Maren and her beans, and a tanner who could make leather remember any shape it had ever been (useful for documents, devastating for seals), and a boy of maybe sixteen who could talk to rats, and a woman whose gift was so small she'd gone unregistered her entire life: she could make one person, touching her, utterly forgettable for as long as she held their hand.

"One person," she said. "One at a time. And they have to be touching me."

"That's enough," Odessa said. "That's more than enough."

I stood at the back of the room most nights, listening to these people lay their small, strange cards on the table one by one, and I felt something in my chest that I didn't have a word for yet. In the Salt Valley we'd called ourselves exiles, but really we'd been *amputees* — everyone quietly grieving a limb, everyone agreeing not to mention the empty sleeve. These people hadn't amputated anything. They'd spent their whole lives folding their gifts up small enough to fit through the world's keyholes, and now here they were, unfolding.

It was the least magical thing in the room and the most powerful: they knew each other. Not their gifts. *Them.* Sef knew that Maren hummed when she worked. The rat-boy knew that the younger Tolliver twin was scared of deep water. They'd been afraid together for years, and fear, it turns out, is a kind of intimacy that doesn't wash out.

I'd known these people less than a season, and Bramble had already decided I was hers — she'd taken to sleeping across the threshold of whatever room I was in, a small straw-braided barricade against nightmares — and Odessa had seen me at my worst, storm-lit and screaming, and hadn't flinched. When the rat-boy cracked a joke about wardens, Bramble laughed, and Odessa laughed, and I laughed, and the sound of it startled me. I couldn't remember the last time I'd heard myself laugh in the valley. I'd been so busy being invisible that I'd forgotten what my own voice sounded like when it wasn't guarding something.

Found family, Perrin would have called it, and then made a self-deprecating joke to cover how much he meant it.

We were going to get him back. I wrote it in my head every night like a line I was memorizing. Whatever else Binding Day was, it was also that.

* * *

The plan, when it finally assembled itself, looked less like a battle plan than like a recipe — a dozen ingredients, each useless alone.

Sef would call the wind for fifteen minutes of chaos as the procession reached the square. The forgettable woman — her name was Ansel, and she apologized for it twice — would walk beside me with her hand in mine, and the two of us would be furniture, background, nothing, all the way to the reservoir access beneath the processional dais. The Tollivers would go in ahead of time, hired onto the ceremony crews as laborers — the crown, in its arrogance, still used gifted labor for manual work, never imagining the tools might bite — and soften every chain and collar in the prisoner pens. Maren's fast-spoiling beans and the tanner's remembering-leather would see to it that the garrison ate badly and marched on boots that split. And the rat-boy, whose name was Pip and who wore his gift like a joke he was in on, would run messages through the walls of the city faster than any courier the crown employed.

"And the siphon itself?" I asked.

The room went quiet. We'd saved that question for last, the way you save the deepest hole for the end of digging.

"Perrin's notes say the junction chamber has a wheel-valve, warded in binding-iron," Odessa said. "Iron that Perrin himself forged." She looked at me. "Which means it needs someone who can stand in front of binding-iron and not be caged. Someone whose gift doesn't live in their blood where the iron bites, but—" she hesitated, "—above it. In the sky."

Everyone looked at me.

"I've never opened a warded valve in my life," I said.

"You've never called a true storm until two weeks ago, either," Odessa said. "And then you brought down a garrison wall."

"That was rage. Rage isn't a plan."

"No," she agreed. "But it's proof of range." She crossed the room and put both hands on my shoulders, and her eyes were fire-reading steady. "Yara. Every person in this room has spent their life making their gift small. You're the only one who spent your life making yours *silent*. Binding Day, you get to decide which one you are. Not the valley. Not the crown. You."

I thought about the salt flats, white and silent, and how I'd loved them and how they'd been a wound dressed as a home. I thought about lightning leaving my hands and the terrible rightness of it. I thought about Perrin holding a door.

"Fine," I said. "But if I electrocute myself opening a valve, somebody tell the story properly. Make me funny."

Bramble raised her hand. "Can I help?"

Every adult in the room started to speak at once, and every adult in the room stopped at once, because we'd all seen her face. She'd lost her family to the binders. She'd hidden in a root cellar while the world above burned. She was nine years old and she was asking to fight, and no single answer we gave her was going to be the right one.

"You're our reserve," Odessa said gently. "The most important job in any battle is the person who keeps the ember alive so the fire can be rebuilt after. Can you keep the ember?"

Bramble considered this with the gravity of a queen reviewing troops. "I can keep the ember," she said.

"Then you're the most important person here," I told her, and the terrifying thing was that I meant it.

* * *

Two nights before Binding Day, I couldn't sleep, and found Odessa on the roof of the Wren, watching the distant glow of the capital like a hearth-fire you're not sure is friendly.

"Can't you sleep either?" I asked.

"I'm reading the city," she said. "It burns differently when it's frightened. Right now it's burning like a held breath." She glanced at me. "Sit. You'll catch cold standing there looking heroic."

I sat. The tiles were warm from the day. Below us, through the floorboards, came the murmur of the Wren's late guests — ordinary sounds, tankards and laughter, the inn wearing its disguise.

"When this is done," I said, "whatever 'done' turns out to mean — what happens to all of them? Sef and Maren and the sisters. People whose gifts are small enough to hide, if they want to go back to hiding."

Odessa was quiet a moment. "Some will," she said. "Hiding kept them alive. You don't stop loving a wall just because you finally climb over it." She turned to look at me fully. "But some won't. Some of them are going to discover, in about two days, that they've been an army all along. You can't unknow that."

"Is that good or bad?"

"Yes," she said, and I laughed despite everything.

Below us, a lamp went out. Above us, the stars did their ancient indifferent work. Somewhere between those two lights was a city full of chained people and a crowned hunger, and in two days we were going to walk into its mouth carrying beans and wind and softened chains and one very stubborn storm.

"Odessa," I said. "If Perrin's alive in there—"

"He is."

"If he's alive, and we win, what do we even do with him? He built the iron. Half these people's lives were ruined by something he made."

"He'll carry it," she said simply. "That's what penance is. Not dying for it. Carrying it, in front of everyone, and building the next thing anyway." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "You'd know. You carried the valley for twenty years."

"That was different. The valley was just cowardice with scenery."

"Mm," said Odessa, who had never once let me insult myself without filing an objection. "And what do you call a person who walks into the heart of the enemy's ceremony to break open the cage that holds everyone's stolen fire?"

I didn't have an answer ready. She didn't wait for one.

"Get some sleep, storm-caller," she said. "Day after tomorrow, we teach a crown to choke."

* * *

Binding Day eve, the Wren filled up.

Not with guests — with *us*. The whole improbable network, gathered under one roof for the final briefing, and I stood at the bar where Perrin used to stand and looked out at faces I'd known for days or weeks and would apparently trust with my life forever, and I understood at last what the elders of the Salt Valley had never grasped: safety was never a place. Safety was a room full of people who know exactly what you are and stay anyway.

Sef traced the winds one last time. The Tollivers practiced their softening touch on a practice manacle until it drooped like taffy. Maren checked her seed-pouches. Ansel flexed her fingers and asked, once more, whether one forgettable person was really enough, and Odessa said, "You're enough," in a voice that closed the subject.

Pip's rats had brought news at dusk: the prisoner wagons were already moving toward the capital. Hundreds of them. Among the names scrawled on a scrap the rats had stolen from a warden's desk — because even paper could be a spy, if you asked nicely — was one entry that made my blood go hot and then cold:

*Prisoner 114: P. Ashgrave. Status: intact. Reserved for ceremony.*

Reserved. For ceremony. They were going to bind him on the dais, in front of everyone, as the centerpiece. The deserter binder, displayed. The usurper wanted him broken publicly — or wanted *him*, specifically, fed to the crown.

I read the scrap three times. Then I folded it, and put it in my pocket, over my heart, where I keep the things that decide things.

"Change of plans," I said, and the room went still, every small gift turning toward me like flowers toward a sun they'd chosen themselves. "We're not just severing the siphon anymore."

I laid the scrap on the bar.

"We're getting Perrin back."
