## 15. Betrayal at the Wren

I have always believed that people break in predictable ways. Give me a man three days hungry and I can tell you what he'll sell. Give me a woman who's watched her children bound and I can tell you what she'll burn. It's not cynicism; it's craft. I spent eleven years designing the instruments of the crown's cruelty, and the first thing you learn in that trade is that iron is never the real mechanism. Fear is. Iron is just what fear looks like when you pour it into a mold.

So when Tam Vetch came to the Gilded Wren on a Tuesday, soaked to the ribs and shaking, I knew within a minute that we were going to have a problem.

He was a weather-reader, or had been—a small gift, the kind that lets you feel rain coming in your teeth and tell a farmer when to bring in his hay. Useless to the crown except as proof that he existed, that he was gifted, that he belonged in a ledger and then in irons. He'd walked two hundred miles from the eastern marches after his village turned him in for a loaf of bread and a pardon that never came.

Odessa fed him stew. Bramble—God help me, Bramble—sat on the bench across from him and showed him how she could make a candle flame dance like a puppet, because that's what children do: they offer their best thing to strangers.

And Tam Vetch sat there with his spoon halfway to his mouth, and I watched him do arithmetic.

I've seen that look before. In the binder's hall at the capital, on the faces of men deciding which prisoners to process first. It's not evil. That's the terrible part. It's just hunger meeting opportunity, and opportunity winning. His eyes went from Bramble's dancing flame to Odessa's fire-marked hands to me—I didn't know what he saw when he looked at me, but whatever it was, it was worth something—and then down to his stew, and there it was. The calculation finishing itself behind his face like a debt being tallied.

*Three days hungry,* I thought. *But not for food.*

* * *

"You're sure," Yara said.

It was past midnight and the five of us were crowded into my room above the kitchen—the one with the false wall and the tunnel map pinned behind it. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, Perrin's old life slept in crates: binding-iron blanks, ritual chalk, the molds for collars I had designed with my own clever hands back when I still told myself I was saving lives.

"I'm sure he's desperate," I said. "I'm sure he's been asking questions in the wrong tone. And I'm sure that Marta's cousin saw him talking to a tinker on the south road yesterday, and that tinker sells lamp oil to the warden garrison." I rubbed my eyes. "I'm not sure he's already sold us. But I've bet worse odds on better information."

"Then we move tonight," Yara said. No hesitation. That was the thing about Yara that kept catching me off guard—they'd spent twenty years practicing silence, and now that they'd stopped, they'd stopped all the way. When they decided something, the decision landed like weather. "Bramble first. Then the wounded. We can be in the sewers before—"

"No."

Everyone looked at Odessa. She stood by the window with her arms crossed, firelight from the street lamp below catching the burn-scarred lattice of her hands.

"No?" Yara said.

"If Tam's already talked, the wardens won't wait for morning either. We run now, in the dark, in the rain, with Hesta still feverish and old Wren barely able to walk, and we do it through streets where every gate has a warden on it by dawn." She shook her head. "Or we hold here. This house has stood eight years. It has doors worth holding and walls that don't talk. If they come, they come to a fight, not a chase."

"And if they come with forty men?" I asked quietly.

"Then we lose a building," Odessa said. "We've lost more than buildings before."

It wasn't a bad argument. It was even mostly right. But I knew something she didn't, something I hadn't said out loud because saying it made it true: if the wardens came to the Gilded Wren, they wouldn't come for a building. They'd come for names. For the network. For the list of safe houses and couriers and sympathetic bakers that lived in my head and in the ledger hidden under my floorboards, and no door in Vessia was worth holding against what they did to people to get lists.

I said none of this. Instead I said, "We split the difference. Everyone sleeps ready to move. I take first watch."

Yara studied me for a long moment. Storm-gray eyes, the kind that make you feel like a landscape under survey. "You'll wake us?"

"On my life," I said, and hated myself for the phrasing, because I meant it as reassurance and heard it, immediately, as a promise I might have to keep in the worst possible way.

* * *

They came at the hour when even cats give up on the night. Third bell, maybe half past. I know because I was watching the bell tower through the gap in the shutters, the way I'd watched it every night for eight years, and because the wardens arrived exactly when a competent commander would arrive—at the bottom of the watch cycle, when the watcher's eyes are sandbags and the inn's fires are banked low.

Except I wasn't tired. I'd taken a double dose of bitterleaf tea and I was sitting in the dark common room with a whetstone and a knife I had no intention of using well, running the evening over again. Tam Vetch's face. The arithmetic. The tinker on the south road.

Here is what I should have done: woken them anyway. Dragged everyone into the tunnels at midnight on a maybe. Here is what I actually did: I trusted my own read of the odds, the same read that had gotten eleven years of prisoners processed efficiently and humanely, *humanely*, God, the words we use—and I let five people sleep so that they'd be rested for a journey I'd already half-decided we'd take at dawn.

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three raps. The signal—one long, two short—came after, muffled and wrong, an octave too high. Tam Vetch had a thin voice. I'd noticed it at dinner. I notice everything; it's my curse and my profession.

I put down the knife. I went to the cellar hatch instead of the front door, and I lifted the floorboard, and I took out the ledger, and I held it over the banked coals of the kitchen hearth.

Eight years of names. The widow in Corrow Lane who hid ember-speakers in her flour barrels. The ferryman who charged resistance runners half fare and never asked why. Two hundred entries in my cramped binder's hand, each one a person who'd decided to trust a former royal binder because he seemed sorry, and sorry, it turns out, is a currency that spends beautifully right up until it doesn't.

The paper caught reluctantly. Old vellum resists. I stood there feeding it page by page into the coals while the polite knocking became impolite pounding, and I want to tell you I felt noble. I felt like a man burning his own fingers off, page by page, and doing the arithmetic I'd accused Tam Vetch of doing, except mine came out the other way. Names against a building. Names against my skin. There was no version of the sum where the names lost.

By the time the front door came down—it took them four minutes and a battering ram, which says something for Marta's joinery—the ledger was ash and I was standing in the common room with my hands raised and soot to my elbows, wearing the calmest expression of my entire career.

"Wardens of the Crown," announced the officer in the doorway, a broad woman with a voice like a slammed drawer. "In the name of King Aldric, by decree of Binding Law—"

"He's dead," I said. "Your king. Has been for years. You're collecting taxes for a hat."

She hit me with a gauntleted fist and, honestly, fair enough.

* * *

They bound my hands with my own iron.

I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in some future where anyone bothers with records: they cuffed me with binding-iron stamped with the sigil of the Royal Office of Harmonious Gifts, and the stamp was mine. I drew that sigil at twenty-six years old, hunched over a drafting table, proud of the filigree. The filigree serves a purpose, I'd explained to my juniors. A frightened mind accepts ornament. People will submit to anything beautiful enough.

My own cuffs bit into my wrists and began, gently, lovingly, to drink.

Binding-iron doesn't hurt, at first. That's the genius of it, if you want to call theft genius. It sips. It takes the edges off your gift until you can't find them, the way fog takes the edges off a hill, and then one day you reach for yourself and there's just—flat country. I designed the dosage curve. I knew exactly what was happening to me as it happened, could have recited the stages, and knowing made it worse, the way knowing the name of a disease never once cured anybody.

They marched us into the yard. Us being me, Marta, old Wren, and Hesta, who was still feverish and stumbled on the wet cobbles and was hauled up by the arm hard enough that I heard something in her shoulder go. Four of us. Because the others were gone.

I'd gotten them out. That was the part I kept turning over like a stone as they loaded us into the wagon. While I burned the ledger, Yara had moved—quietly, quickly, the way water finds the low path—and by the time the battering ram sang its first note against Marta's door, the cellar hatch was closed and the tunnel mouth in the coal shed was reset and five people I had promised, *on my life*, to protect were underground and moving toward the river culvert.

Tam Vetch stood by the gate in the rain, wringing his hat in his hands, not looking at anyone. The officer spoke to him briefly, handed him something—a chit, a pass, thirty pieces of whatever the crown pays in now—and he walked away down the lane without once turning around, which was, I suppose, the smartest thing he ever did, and I hope it keeps him warm.

"Funny thing," the officer said, falling into step beside the wagon. She had my file open. My actual file, with the sketch of my face from fifteen years ago, back when my hairline and my conscience were both intact. "Perrin Ashgrave. Royal Binder, First Class. Designer of the Harmonic Restraint system. Decorated twice." She looked up. "You could have been sitting in a nice office right now, old man. What happened to you?"

What happened to me. I looked at her—young, competent, certain, the exact person I'd been training junior officers to become—and I felt the iron sip at my wrists and thought: *a question arrived that your manuals don't answer.*

"A man died in my chair," I said. "A hedge-witch, ninety years old, gift of making bread rise. She fought the binding. They always fight, at first, and usually it's nothing, but she was strong in her way, in her small way, and the iron—" I stopped. The wagon jolted over the cobblestones toward the garrison, toward the cells, toward whatever the crown does with binders who defect, and I found I couldn't perform the story anymore. Not the clean version. "She looked at me," I said finally, "and she said, *you poor boy, who did this to you?* And nobody had ever asked me that. So I left."

The officer wrote something down. Probably *unstable*. Probably *broken*. Both accurate.

* * *

The cell they gave me was in the garrison basement, which meant I could hear the pipes. All of them. The whole municipal water system of the capital runs beneath the streets like a second city, and I had spent one very instructive week of my defection learning its map from a sewer-warden who owed me a favor, and I had drawn that map, and I had given that map to Yara, and I had made Yara memorize the junction under Tanner's Row where the resistance runners could surface inside the old tannery.

Which meant that as I sat in the dark listening to water move through the veins of the city, I could track them. Roughly. By ear and by faith. South along the main culvert. West at the junction. Toward the river, toward the tannery, toward Odessa's contact whose name I'd burned rather than surrender.

They were out. Bramble was out. Whatever else the night cost—and it had cost Marta's shoulder, Hesta's freedom, my iron-drained hands, eight years of work reduced to a smell of smoke—that part had held. On my life. I'd meant the phrase as a figure of speech and it had turned literal, the way promises do when you make them to people who are worth keeping them for.

The cell door had a viewing slot. Through it, eventually, came lamplight and boots and the voice of the broad officer, speaking to someone in the corridor.

"—the storm-caller's confirmed, then? Villalobos. Salt Valley. The description matches."

"The refugee's account matches," said a second voice, drier, higher. "Description, history, the lightning over the flats last month. The crown wants them alive. Priority capture."

"And the fire-reader?"

"Alive if convenient."

I put my forehead against the cold stone and laughed, quietly, because the alternative was screaming, and screaming in a warden garrison gets you visited by people with questions. *Alive if convenient.* Odessa would be furious at the phrasing. She'd say convenience is a word invented by cowards.

They had our names. All the names that mattered. The purge had reached into the last corner of Vessia where magic used to sleep safe, and it had followed the salt, and it had found the storm.

Somewhere under the city, water moved through pipes toward a river, carrying, in its way, five people I loved. Somewhere above, the crown was sharpening itself. And somewhere between those two facts, in the space I'd spent fifteen years pretending didn't exist, a choice was waiting for me—the same choice, always, the only one I'd ever really had: what I would do next with the things I'd built.

I pressed my scarred palms flat against the floor and listened to the city breathe through its iron veins, and I began, silently, to plan.
