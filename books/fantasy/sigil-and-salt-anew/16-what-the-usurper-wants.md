## 16. What the Usurper Wants

They took my boots.

I want that on the record, in whatever history gets written about this—assuming anyone survives to write it. Not my staff, not the storm-glass pendant Odessa pressed into my hand three days ago, not even the knife I'd forgotten I was carrying until a warden with an unfortunate sense of humor pulled it from my boot and said, "Well, look at that." They took the boots too. Everything sharp, everything useful, everything mine. Then they put me in a cell so clean it looked like it had been scrubbed by someone terrified of being blamed for dust.

The cell was in the palace itself. I knew because of the windows—or rather, the absence of them. The Gilded Wren's people had spent weeks teaching us the capital's bones: which streets ran over old aqueducts, which buildings had been noble houses before Aldric's grandfather paved the city in ambition. Nothing about those lessons had prepared me for the inside of the Spire, where the walls were white as bone and the silence had a texture to it, like wool packed in your ears.

They'd taken me at the river gate. That was the plan going wrong, if you're wondering—the plan had been *don't get taken at the river gate*. But plans are what you make when you haven't met the future yet, and the future had turned out to contain six wardens, a net woven through with binding-iron thread, and my own hesitation, one half-second too long, when I saw Bramble's face across the square and thought, *not her, not again.*

So they'd taken me instead of her. Small mercies. I was trying very hard to count them.

*You're alive,* I told myself, sitting on the cot with my bare feet on cold stone. *You're alive and unbound and your boots are probably in an evidence room somewhere with your name on a tag.* It wasn't much of a litany. It was what I had.

The door opened without a sound. Hinges oiled by someone who understood that fear is quieter when you don't hear it coming.

Two wardens. Not the ones from the river gate. These wore the pale gray of the inner guard, faces like men who had been told what they'd see today and had decided, in advance, not to have opinions about it.

"The crown will see you," the taller one said.

"Tell it I'm not dressed," I said.

Neither of them smiled. Wardens never do. I've started to suspect it's in the recruitment criteria, right after *able to lift fifty pounds* and *comfortable lying to children.*

* * *

They walked me up. And up. The Spire's staircases were designed, I think, by someone who hated knees and loved symbolism—every landing wider than the last, every corridor longer, so that by the time you arrived anywhere you already felt small and far from home. The binding-iron in the walls hummed against my skin like a struck tuning fork. After twenty years behind the salt line, magic still felt new to me, raw and loud—but iron felt louder. Iron said *you are measured, you are catalogued, you are known.* I hated how well it worked.

Then a door, tall as a chapel's, and a room that made no sense until it made terrible sense.

The throne room of Vessia should have been full of light. Every account agreed: the Sun Hall, they called it, windows floor to ceiling, gold leaf catching dawn. What I stood in instead was dim. The windows were there—I could see their shapes, tall and arched along both walls—but no sun came through them. Something dark clung to the glass from the outside, or perhaps from the inside; I couldn't tell. The light that remained came from braziers burning a flame the color of a dying coal, orange shading into something bruised and violet at the heart.

And on the throne, the king.

Aldric the Third. I'd seen his likeness on coins all my life, back when coins reached the valley—strong jaw, kind eyes, the sculptor's flattery every monarch buys. The thing on the throne had the jaw. It had the eyes. It had the crown, a circlet of black metal set with stones that drank the brazier-light rather than returning it.

It did not have a shadow.

I looked twice. Odessa had told us—*his shadow moved against the light*—and I'd filed it away as the poetry of a frightened woman describing something she couldn't name. But no. The braziers threw light everywhere in that room, and the king cast nothing. Where his shoulders should have thrown darkness onto the wall behind him, the wall was simply... wall. He sat in the world like a hole sits in cloth.

"You are the storm-caller."

His voice was worse than the missing shadow. It was a good voice—warm, measured, the voice of a man who had given a thousand speeches and been beloved for most of them—and it came out of him the way sound comes out of an empty jar. Right shape. Wrong everything else.

"Yara Villalobos," I said. "And you're not."

The wardens flinched. The king-thing did not. It tilted its head, and the movement was almost right, almost human, the way a puppet's movement is almost walking.

"No," it agreed. "I am not."

* * *

I had imagined this meeting, in the way you imagine falling—you know it will happen someday, you rehearse the terror, and then it arrives wearing details you never planned for. In my imaginings, the usurper roared. It threatened. It was a villain from Bramble's stories, all appetite and no patience.

What it did was study me. Patiently. The way I used to study nets with a bad knot, working out where the weakness lived.

"Do you know what I am?" it asked.

"Hungry," I said. "Old. Bad at choosing jewelry."

Something moved behind its face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would be kept, the way a hook keeps the shape of a fish. "Older than the kingdom. Older than the salt. When your valley was seabed, I was already hungry." It rose from the throne, and I learned something new about fear: it has layers. There is the fear of dying, which is simple. And there is the fear of standing very still while something ancient crosses a room toward you, which is not simple at all.

It stopped ten feet away. Courteous. As if we were neighbors discussing a fence.

"I have worn many shapes," it said. "A god, once, in a country whose name the sea took back. A plague. A law. Shapes are difficult, do you understand? A plague can only rule through fear, and fear burns out. A law can only rule through habit, and habit dies with the generation that made it." It lifted one hand, and the fingers were Aldric's fingers, tanned and capable, a swordsman's hand. "But a crown. A crown rules through love and fear and habit and law and God, all at once, all funneling down to a single point. Do you know how easy it became, once I found the crown? Centuries of work, and then one evening a young king on a battlefield prayed for victory, and I simply... answered."

"Aldric died for you."

"Aldric *became* me." For a moment—just a moment—the voice frayed, and underneath it I heard something vast and dry, like wind through a dead forest. "He is still here. I keep him. He sees what I see. Some nights he weeps, and I taste it, and it is—" The fraying stopped. The warmth returned, seamless as a mask settling. "He was tired, Yara Villalobos. His kingdom was tearing itself apart. The gifted against the ungifted, fire against rope, brother against brother. He wanted peace. I offered peace. All it cost was everything he was, poured slowly into me, a cup that never empties." It gestured, gently, at the violet-hearted flames. "Do you know what sustains a thing like me? Not worship. Not blood. *Gifts.* Every bound magician feeds the crown. Every gift they surrender becomes my strength. Your kind built my kingdom for me, one binding at a time, and called it safety."

My hands had gone cold. Twenty years of salt, I thought. Twenty years of exiles trading their gifts for a quiet life, and every surrendered gift had gone somewhere. Had gone *here*. The valley hadn't been a refuge. It had been a farm.

"You're telling me this," I said, "because you want something."

"I always want something. Wanting is what I am." It smiled then, and the smile fit Aldric's face perfectly, and that was the worst thing yet—that it could wear joy so well when it had none. "I want you beside me."

* * *

The room went very quiet. Even the braziers seemed to lean in.

"Consider," it said, and began to walk again, slow circles, a teacher's pacing. "You spent twenty years in the salt. You know what it is to be cut off from yourself. You have just gotten it back—what, two seasons ago?—and already it terrifies you. Half a city block, was it? When they took the child." News travels fast when the news is catastrophe. "You felt it, didn't you. The moment the lightning left you. Not triumph. *Relief*, that it was over, and horror, that you were glad."

"Don't," I said.

"It will happen again. It will always happen again, because you are a storm pretending to be a person, and storms cannot be people no matter how kindly you ask them." Its voice softened, and the softness was surgical. "But I can teach you what I taught myself across ten centuries. Shape. Purpose. A hunger with direction is no longer hunger—it is *appetite*, and appetite can be satisfied. Stand beside me. Keep your gift whole—no binding, ever, I promise you that. Rule the weather of a nation. No more hiding. No more salt. No more elders voting on whether you deserve shelter." It stopped, directly before me, close enough that I could smell the throne room on it: cold stone and something sweetish, like fruit rotting slowly in a closed room. "No one else will ever offer you this. Every other path ends in a cage or a grave. I am offering you the only door out of yourself."

And here is the part I will not pretend away, because Odessa says a lie of omission is still a lie, and Bramble is learning to read me too well these days:

For three heartbeats, I wanted it.

Not the power. Power has never tempted me; power is just weight, and I've carried enough. What tempted me was the *ending*. The end of flinching. The end of feeling the storm rise in my chest and thinking, *please, not now, not here, not in front of people I love.* The end of being a danger with a heartbeat. It offered me peace the way a drowning man is offered the bottom of the river—restfully, permanently, and at the price of everything above the surface.

Three heartbeats. Then I thought of Bramble in a root cellar, listening to her family scream, and how she had come out of that cellar still able to laugh at Perrin's terrible jokes. I thought of Odessa reading fire like scripture and refusing, every day, to let it make her cruel. I thought of Perrin, who built the cage and then spent years learning to pick locks, because shame that doesn't become work is just decoration.

They were all afraid too. All of them. Every single one of them afraid, and none of them hollow.

"No," I said.

The word came out small. I made it come out anyway.

"No," I said again, louder, and this time I heard what was underneath it, what had been underneath it since the salt flats, since the first lightning split the sky over my head: not fear. Not exactly. *Recognition.* "You're wrong about what I am. I'm not a storm pretending to be a person. I'm a person who happens to storm. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and you wouldn't know that because you've never been anything except hunger wearing a costume." I stepped forward—one step, into the space between us, barefoot on cold stone, boots-less and unimpressed. "Aldric wanted peace. Fine. Here's what peace actually looks like: it looks like people being allowed to be whole. You wouldn't recognize it. You eat it."

The thing in the king's face went still.

Then, softly, with something that in any other creature I might have called respect: "They always refuse. Did you know that? In ten centuries, at the last moment, they always refuse. It is the single most inefficient feature of your species." It turned back toward the throne. "Take her to the Binding Day preparations. She'll be bound with the others. Her gift will feed me, and her refusal will be a footnote."

"That's the second time you've gotten my pronouns wrong," I said, as the wardens took my arms. "First the boots, now this. Is it a hunger thing, or—"

The doors opened. The binding-iron hummed. And as they marched me out, I felt it—a flicker, deep beneath the floor, beneath the palace, beneath the bedrock itself. A heat. A pulse. Slow as a sleeping heart, and failing, failing, failing.

Odessa had told us what slept under the Spire. I hadn't believed I'd feel it through forty feet of stone.

I felt it now. The last dragon, dying in the dark—and somewhere above her, on Binding Day, hundreds of gifts waiting to be poured into a cup that never empties.

We had four days. Maybe less.

I counted my mercies again, all the way down the stairs. Still not many. But the storm in my chest was awake, and calm, and *mine*—and for the first time since the river gate, that felt like enough to build on.
