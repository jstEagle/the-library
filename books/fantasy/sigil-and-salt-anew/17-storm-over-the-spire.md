## 17. Storm Over the Spire

The cell they'd put me in had one virtue: it was honest about being a cage.

No cushions, no false comfort, just binding-iron mesh over the window like a scab of gray metal, and a drain in the floor that suggested whoever designed it expected the occupants to be here a while—or not to care much about their dignity while they were. I sat on the bench with my wrists still humming from the manacles and thought about the crown-thing's offer. *A place beside me.* It had said it the way you'd offer someone a chair by the fire. That was the worst part. Not the hunger in its voice, but the courtesy.

Somewhere above me, Vaskarath burned. I could feel it now—had been able to feel it since they strapped the iron on me, which was a joke so bitter I almost laughed. The binding didn't stop my gift. It just pressed it down flat, like a hand on a struck bell, and everything underneath kept ringing.

Then the lights went out.

Not dimmed. *Went out*, all at once, the way a held breath goes out, and in the dark I heard Bramble's voice in my head—not literally, she wasn't gifted that way, but memory has good timing—*Yara, we're coming, don't do anything stupid.*

Too late, sprout. I'd already done the stupid thing. I'd let them take me.

* * *

What actually happened, as far as I could piece it together afterward: Odessa had spent two days doing what Odessa does, which is walk into places that should kill her and read the fire until the fire tells her secrets. She'd found the garrison's ember-ward—a warded lantern that kept the whole spire's defenses lit—and she'd stood in front of it in her kitchen-girl disguise and told it, quietly, in the old fire-tongue, to go out. And because fire is a gossip and Odessa speaks its language fluently, it did.

Bramble came through the drains. Of course she did. She's small enough to fold herself into spaces that would embarrass a cat, and she carries hedge-magic in her pockets like a child carries river stones—useless-looking things that turn out to be exactly what you need. She unlocked my cell with a charm her grandmother taught her, which was meant for opening jam jars and worked fine on royal iron, because iron doesn't care how grand you think it is.

"Hi," she said, when the door swung open. Her face was smudged with soot and there was a bruise blooming along her jaw that made something in my chest go very quiet and very cold. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy," I said. "There wasn't one. That's the problem."

She hugged me then, quick and fierce, and I let myself have three seconds of it before Odessa's voice hissed from the corridor: "Less reunion, more running."

We ran.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about escaping a fortress built into a spire: there are only so many directions, and all of them eventually become stairs, and stairs are where garrisons live.

We made it four levels down before the wardens found us. They poured into the stairwell from below—six of them, then ten, iron-tipped pikes and those blank gray masks they wear so you can't see their faces and start thinking of them as people. Behind them, the alarm bells began, and beneath the bells I felt the garrison waking up around us like a body rolling over in bed.

Odessa put her back to mine. "Tell me you have something," she said.

"I have a lot of things," I said. "None of them useful."

"Yara."

The truth was I had one thing, and it was the thing I was most afraid of. Since the undercity, since Perrin's lessons—the breathing, the shaping, *the storm is not weather happening to you, it's a language you're speaking*—I'd felt it coiled in me like a rope under tension. But every time I reached for it, I remembered the city block. The half-collapsed tenement. The woman who'd lost her bakery and nearly lost more. Rage without reins. I had promised myself I wouldn't be that again.

The wardens started up the stairs.

"Odessa," I said, "when I do this, get behind me. Actually behind me. Like, regret-it-later distance."

"What are you going to—"

I stopped holding the rope.

It wasn't like the first time. That first time in the valley, twenty years of suppression tearing loose—that had been a dam breaking, all flood and no direction. This was different. Perrin's voice was in my head, calm and guilt-ridden and precise: *Feel where it wants to go. Then tell it where you want it to go. Those are allowed to be different things.*

So I felt it. The storm in me turned toward the wardens like a dog hearing its name, hungry and eager and not particular about method, and I said—out loud, because Perrin said saying it helps, and I'll try anything once—*"No. Not them. Around them."*

Lightning took the stairwell.

I won't pretend I can describe it accurately. It went through the stone above their heads and the iron railings beside them and the pikes in their hands, and it found every scrap of metal and sang along it, and the wardens dropped—not dead, I checked, I *made sure*, that was part of telling it where to go—dropped and twitched and lay smoking gently in the dark like a row of extinguished candles. The smell of ozone filled the stairwell. My hair was standing straight up. Bramble made a sound that was half gasp and half applause.

"Oh," said Odessa, staring at me. "*Oh.*"

"Don't," I said. My hands were shaking. "Don't look at me like that yet. I'm not done being terrified."

* * *

Because here's the thing I learned in the next hour, running down through the spire with a storm on a leash: terror doesn't leave when you take control. It just changes shape.

Every time I called the lightning—through a barred gate, across a courtyard, into the mechanism of a portcullis so it seized shut behind us—I felt both things at once. The triumph, bright and clean, like the first breath after drowning. And underneath it, a cold little voice taking inventory: *you could do more than that. You could do so much more than that.* The storm wanted scope. It wanted the horizon. It looked at the garrison and saw not an obstacle but an *audience*.

That was the fear. Not that I'd lose control. That some part of me didn't want it.

We burst out onto the lower parade ground and the sky was waiting for me.

I hadn't noticed, underground, but the weather had been listening. All that pressure I'd been pushing down into iron and stone had to go somewhere, and it had gone up—into clouds stacked black and violet over the capital, into air so charged the hair on my arms rose before I even reached for it. Overhead, the Spire's beacon tower flickered, its flame-fed wards guttering where Odessa's work had spread. The garrison bells were clanging. Wardens were pouring out of barracks doors, and above them all, at the top of everything, the crown-thing's seat glowed faintly with stolen fire.

"Yara." Odessa caught my arm. "The garrison. If we run now, they regroup, they hunt us, they hunt everyone at the Wren. Perrin's still in their cells. If we're going to do Binding Day, we need them broken *now*."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you look like someone deciding whether to jump off a cliff."

"I'm deciding whether to *be* the cliff," I said, and she had the grace not to laugh, mostly.

Bramble tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the sky, then at me, with the uncomplicated faith of a nine-year-old who had watched me level a city block and decided, apparently, that this was simply what family did. "Big one," she said. "Like in the stories."

"No," I said. Then, honestly: "Yes. But stay behind the well house. Both of you. If I get this wrong, I want witnesses who survive to describe my failure accurately."

* * *

I walked to the center of the parade ground, because it seemed right, because if I was going to do this I was going to do it standing up in the open where the storm could see me and I could see it, no walls between us, no lies.

Perrin's lessons. Breath first. The storm is not other; it never was. Every time I'd called lightning out of panic or grief or rage, I'd treated it like a wild animal lunging on a rope, and it had behaved like one. But it wasn't an animal. It was *me*. Twenty years of salt-flat silence, and underneath it a child who had watched thunder roll over the valley rim and ached like a missing limb. The storm wasn't something that happened to me. It was something I had been refusing to say.

So I said it.

I raised my hands—not dramatic, though I'll admit the drama was available—and I breathed the way Perrin taught me, and I spoke to the sky in the only language I've ever been fluent in.

"*Now.*"

The world went white.

Thunder didn't follow the lightning; it arrived with it, simultaneous, a single concussion that I felt in my teeth and my bones and the soles of my feet. The first bolt hit the beacon tower and the second hit the armory and the third, fourth, fifth—I stopped counting—walked across the garrison yard in deliberate steps, striking the weapon racks, the signal mast, the iron-reinforced gates, everywhere the wardens' power lived and nowhere their bodies did. I could feel each strike like a word in a sentence, and I knew—I *knew*, with a clarity that frightened me more than any of it—exactly where every bolt would land before it fell. Not chaos. Composition.

The garrison broke. There's no other word for it. Doors banged open and men poured out and then poured back in, masks cracked, pikes fused, the alarm bells themselves struck dumb by a bolt that melted the bell-yoke into a modern sculpture. Someone shouted something about a demon. Someone else shouted something more accurate about weather. By the time the last bolt grounded itself in the parade ground and left a branching scar of fulgurite like frozen lightning in the stone, the Warden Spire's garrison—three hundred strong, the fist that had hammered the Salt Valley flat—was cowering in its own barracks, and the sky over the capital was raining.

I stood in the downpour and shook.

Not from cold. From the inventory again, the cold little voice, except now it wasn't cold, it was *awed*, and awe scared me worse. Because I had done exactly what I meant to do. Every strike. Every target. Nothing accidental, nothing spared, nothing wasted. And some deep animal part of me had *loved* it—the scope, the sentence, the sky answering me like it had been waiting twenty years for me to call.

"Yara." Odessa's hand on my shoulder. Rain streaming off her chin. "Look at me."

I looked.

"You're afraid you liked it," she said. Fire-reader. Of course she'd seen it; I was probably glowing like a hearth.

"Yeah."

"Good," she said. "People who aren't afraid of what they can do are the ones the crown hires. You're afraid, and you did it anyway, carefully, and nobody died. That's not the monster, Yara. That's the opposite."

Bramble came pelting out from behind the well house, soaked to the bone and grinning like the rain was a festival. "THAT WAS—" she yelled, over the drumming water, "—THE BEST THING I HAVE EVER SEEN, AND I ONCE SAW A GOAT EAT A HAT."

"Thank you, Bramble."

"The goat ate the WHOLE hat."

"Thank you, Bramble."

But I was laughing. Wet and shaky and slightly unhinged, but laughing, standing in the ruins of the garrison's power with rain washing the ozone out of the air, and for the first time since the salt line, the storm inside me wasn't coiled or straining or screaming. It was just... there. Part of the weather of me. Mine.

* * *

We didn't linger. Somewhere above, the crown-thing had felt all of it—I had no doubt, I could practically feel its attention pressing down the Spire like a thumb on a bruise—and whatever it made of a storm-caller dismantling its garrison, it would not make nothing.

At the edge of the yard, Odessa stopped. She was looking back at the Spire, at the high dark windows, and her face had gone strange.

"Yara," she said slowly. "When your lightning hit the beacon tower. The fire there—it spoke to me. Just a thread, just for a second." She swallowed. "It was Vaskarath. And she said—" Odessa's voice cracked, steadied. "She said *tell the storm-child: three days. On Binding Day, the chain can be cut, but only from inside, and only while the crown is feeding.*"

Rain ran off my hands. Above us, the last clouds were tearing open, showing stars.

Three days. The resistance scattered in the sewers, Perrin in a cell, and the largest garrison in the capital limping—but the crown-thing awake now, and watching, and hungry.

"Well," I said. "Then we'd better go find our people. We've got a ceremony to crash."
