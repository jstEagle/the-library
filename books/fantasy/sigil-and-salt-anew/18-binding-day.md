## 18. Binding Day

**Yara**

The scaffolds had been built overnight.

That was the thing about the capital—Vaskarath's Rest, they called it now, though the dragon beneath it hadn't rested in years—that it could dress atrocity in bunting faster than any city I'd ever seen. By dawn the great plaza before the palace steps was strung with pennants of royal blue and gold, and carpenters who probably went home to their children and kissed them goodnight had hammered together forty wooden platforms, each one fitted with a post, each post fitted with a collar of black iron.

Binding-iron. Perrin's iron. I tried not to look at him when I thought that, because he always caught it, and his face did something complicated and terrible every time.

"Four hundred names on today's list," Odessa murmured beside me. We stood in the press of the crowd, two among ten thousand come to watch. She wore a fishmonger's apron over her clothes and smelled convincingly of herring, which she claimed was a gift in itself. "I counted the wagons last night. They'll process the children first."

"Why first?"

"Because it breaks everyone else faster." She said it flatly, the way you say things you've had to say too many times. "A parent who watches their child bound will kneel for the collar themselves just to stop the screaming. It's efficient. The crown-thing thinks in efficiencies."

I looked down at my hands. The storm-scars across my knuckles had gone silver since the Spire, since the night I'd torn a garrison apart with weather and learned that lightning would answer me like a hound answers its name. Under my sleeves, my skin prickled with static. The whole plaza felt charged—not from me, from the crowd. Ten thousand people packed shoulder to shoulder, most of them ungifted, all of them afraid, and fear is just another kind of electricity looking for ground.

*Today,* I told myself. *You walk into the middle of it and you do not flinch.*

The plan was simple, which is to say it was insane, which is to say Perrin had designed it, and Perrin designed everything the way a man designs his own penance: load-bearing guilt in every joint.

The ceremony would begin at midbell. The wardens would march four hundred bound-and-collared prisoners onto the platforms. The crown-thing—the king, the hollow king, whatever we were calling the hunger wearing Aldric's face this week—would appear on the balcony to preside. And while every eye in Vessia turned upward toward that crowned absence, Odessa would go down into the dark under the palace where the last dragon burned in her chains, and Bramble would do what only a nine-year-old hedge-witch could do, and I—

I would walk into the heart of the crowd and wait for the sky.

"You're doing the breathing," Odessa said.

"I'm doing the breathing."

"The breathing where you look like you're about to be sick."

"That's the breathing I know best."

Her mouth twitched. Then her hand found mine and squeezed once, hard, and let go, and she melted into the crowd toward the palace's eastern service stair, and I stood alone in a sea of strangers with a storm under my skin and a name sewn into my collar that wasn't mine.

The bells began.

* * *

**Odessa**

I have read fire my whole life, and fire has never once lied to me. People lie. Crowns lie. Fire just burns, and what it burns tells you everything.

The tunnels beneath the palace were old—older than Aldric, older than his grandfather, older than the kingdom's habit of writing itself down. The masons who cut them had been paid in salt and silence, and the silence held even now. I moved through the dark with a shuttered lantern and Bramble's small hand in mine, and the air grew warmer with every step, until the warmth became heat and the heat became something that pressed against my gift like a voice pressing against a door.

*She knows we're coming,* I signed to Bramble, because sound carried strangely down here and the child had taught me half the hand-talk her family used in the root cellar. *She's known for days.*

Bramble signed back: *Is she scared?*

I hesitated. Children deserve truth; that was the one rule I'd kept since the waystation, since I'd pulled Bramble out of the earth while her family's ashes were still settling on the wheat field above. But some truths are a weight, and she was so small, and she already carried more than—

*Yes,* I signed. *But not of us.*

We came around the last bend and there she was.

I had heard about Vaskarath my whole life the way you hear about the sea—secondhand, diminished, a story made safe by distance. Nothing prepares you. She filled the cavern the way grief fills a house. Her scales were the color of cooling embers, grey shot through with veins of dying orange, and the siphon-crown bolted to her skull drank from her in long visible pulses, light running up the chain-links into the palace above like blood running the wrong way through a heart.

Her eye opened. Old. Amber. Unbelievably tired.

*Fire-reader,* she said, and her voice arrived inside my bones rather than my ears. *Little thief of flame. You've come at last. I have watched your candle approaching for three nights.*

"We've come to free you," I said aloud, because signing felt absurd in front of something that had watched kingdoms rise and fall from underneath them.

*Free.* The word moved through her like a shudder. *Do you know how long I have worn this? Do you know what they feed me, little reader? Not coal. Not wood. They feed me the bound. Every gift they cage, they pour into me, and pour me into the crown, and the crown wears the dead man's face and calls itself a kingdom.* Her nostril flared, and the heat of it singed my eyebrows. *And you bring a child into my dark.*

Bramble stepped forward before I could stop her. She looked up at an eye the size of a cartwheel and she wasn't afraid—I will remember that until I die, I will tell it at every fire I ever sit beside—and she said, "Yara sent us. Yara says nobody gets left in the dark. Not even dragons. *Especially* not dragons."

Vaskarath was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, something in the vast tired voice had cracked open, very slightly, like ice on a river in thaw.

*Then we should hurry,* she said. *The chain is anchored in seven places. Six are iron. One is older than iron, and it will need your fire, reader, and the child's growing things, and a storm I can feel sitting on the city like a hand on a drum.*

Above us, faint through fifty feet of stone, the bells finished striking midbell.

The Binding Day ceremony had begun.

* * *

**Perrin**

They put me in a holding cell beneath the warden barracks, which was, I will admit, a professional humiliation. I built these cells. I specified the lock mechanisms. I wrote the manual.

The irony was not lost on me. Very little had been lost on me lately except, apparently, my entire moral compass, for approximately fifteen years.

The cell was iron-lined, of course—it was meant for the gifted, and I had helped design it to drink magic the way sand drinks rain—but here was the flaw I had never fixed because I had never imagined needing to: the iron drank *magic*. It did nothing whatsoever about a former Royal Binder's memory of every mechanism he'd ever built, or the bent nail he'd spent six hours palming from the floorboards, or the fact that despair, given enough time, refines itself into something remarkably like engineering.

The lock surrendered at the second bell. The corridor guard surrendered at the second bell and eleven minutes, though not willingly—he was a boy, maybe nineteen, and I knocked him out as gently as a man can hit someone and took his keys and his cloak and hated myself with a clean, familiar efficiency.

The barracks emptied toward the plaza for the ceremony. That was the design flaw of tyrannies, if you wanted one: they needed everyone watching. A kingdom built on spectacle must, on its greatest day, empty its own pockets.

I walked out through the warden gate wearing a warden's cloak, past the armory, past the kennels, and stopped at the supply shed where the reserve collars were stored. Forty crates of binding-iron, each link stamped with the royal sigil, each stamped—beneath the sigil, small enough that only its maker would ever look—with my maker's mark.

I had left myself a door. Fifteen years ago, a younger, more frightened, more complicit man had built a flaw into every collar he forged: a resonance key, a frequency at which the iron's grip harmonized instead of clenched. I had told myself it was for maintenance. I had known, even then, in the place where I kept the things I couldn't afford to look at, that it was for exactly this day. That someday I would stand in front of my own work and reverse it.

I set the resonator—a tuning fork, essentially, a tuning fork that could unmake cages—and struck it against the doorframe.

The sound it made was almost nothing. A hum below hearing. But down the length of the plaza, on forty scaffold platforms, four hundred collars of black iron shivered like struck glass.

Somewhere in the crowd, I knew, Yara Villalobos felt it too—the pressure lifting off the prisoners' gifts, the caged magic stretching like a limb waking. And somewhere above the city, I hoped, the clouds were taking notice.

I pulled the hood up and walked toward the plaza, carrying my sin back to the scene of it.

* * *

**Yara**

The prisoners came in wagons, and the crowd cheered.

I want you to understand that. I need you to, because I still need to understand it myself. They cheered. Bakers and clerks and grandmothers, people who bought bread and complained about taxes and loved their neighbors—they cheered as the wagons rolled past, because the proclamation had spent a year teaching them that the gifted were a sickness and the collars were medicine, and fear is a patient teacher and hatred is its star pupil.

A woman next to me lifted her child onto her shoulders to see better. "There, love," she said, pointing at a girl in the third wagon, a girl no older than Bramble with a bruise on her cheekbone. "See the bad ones going to be made safe?"

The girl in the wagon looked out at the crowd with the flat gray eyes of someone who has stopped expecting anything, and my hands closed into fists, and the hair on my arms stood up, and somewhere overhead thunder muttered like a dog waking.

*Not yet.* Odessa's voice in my memory. *Not until the dragon's free. If you break early, they'll seal the underpalace and she burns with everyone else. Hold it, Yara. Hold it like breath.*

I held it. Gods, I held it. I held it the way you hold a scream at a funeral, the way you hold a door against a flood. The static crawled along my scars and pooled in my palms and I breathed it down, breath by breath, while the wardens marched the prisoners up onto the platforms and locked them to the posts, four hundred souls in rows, and the crowd's cheering swelled, and high on the palace balcony a door opened.

The king came out.

Or the shape of him. From this distance he looked fine—regal, even, silver-haired and straight-backed in the blue-and-gold. But I had seen him close, in the throne room, in the worst hour of my life, and I knew what the distance hid: the way his shadow fell wrong, toward the light instead of away from it. The way his eyes were windows in a house where no one lived. The way the crown sat on him not like a possession but like a mouth.

He raised his hands, and ten thousand people went quiet, and his voice—his stolen, resonant, hollow voice—rolled out over the plaza:

"My beloved subjects. Today we make our kingdom whole."

Below me, deep under the stone, I felt it—a tremor, then a groan, then a sound like a mountain changing its mind. The crowd stirred. Wardens turned toward the palace.

And in the eastern tower, a chain older than iron snapped like a harp string breaking.

*Now,* said the sky, in my own voice.

I didn't call the storm. I *was* the storm, for one white-hot instant—every cloud over Vaskarath's Rest answered at once, the whole gray ceiling of the world lighting from within, and I walked forward through the parting crowd with lightning walking with me, step for step, and I raised my scarred hands over four hundred bound prisoners and forty scaffolds and one crowned lie, and I said, loudly enough for the whole plaza, in the valley accent I had spent twenty years burying:

"No. Today you don't."

* * *

**Odessa**

The last anchor broke, and Vaskarath rose.

I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it. A dragon unfolding after years in chains is not movement; it is geology deciding otherwise. The cavern roof came down around her and she came up through it, through the palace foundations, through the great hall where courtiers scattered like startled birds, and she emerged into daylight for the first time in living memory with the siphon-crown shattered on her brow and her wings opening wider than the plaza itself.

She did not roar. That was the terrifying part. She simply *breathed*, one long exhalation of ember-light that washed over the palace walls, and every binding-collar within her sight—the reserves in the shed, the irons on the prisoners' posts—went dark and dead, unmade, reversed, as if Perrin's little tuning fork had been a whisper and this was the shout.

On the platforms, four hundred people stood blinking in the sudden absence of iron, their gifts flooding home into them like rivers remembering the sea.

And the crowd—the crowd that had cheered—made a new sound. Not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. The sound people make when the story they were told turns out to be a mask, and something true looks out from behind it.

Bramble's hand found mine in the dust and chaos. Above us, wreathed in smoke and morning light, the last dragon turned her ancient head toward the balcony where the hollow king stood, and opened her jaws, and said, in a voice that flattened banners and buckled flagstones:

*"LITTLE THIEF OF CROWNS. YOUR FURNACE HAS QUIT."*

The thing wearing Aldric's face turned. Its shadow fell toward the sun.

It looked directly—at me, at Bramble, at the dragon, and then, with terrible deliberateness, out across the plaza to where a storm-caller stood in the heart of the crowd with lightning crowning her like a second coronet.

It smiled.

And every warden in the plaza drew steel and turned—not toward the dragon, not toward the prisoners, but toward Yara.

* * *

**Yara**

Ten thousand people between me and the palace steps. Four hundred newly freed magicians on forty scaffolds, dazed, unarmed, some of them barely more than children. Two hundred wardens with drawn blades converging on one person. A dragon overhead. A hollow king smiling.

And Odessa's voice in my head, steady as a lighthouse: *Whatever happens next—we're right behind you. All of us. Walk.*

So I walked.

Toward the steps. Toward the crown. Toward the thing that had eaten a kingdom from the inside, with the storm gathered in my fists and four hundred freed voices rising behind me—not cheering, not yet, but rising, a sound like a tide deciding to come in—and I thought of the Salt Valley, and the nets I'd mended, and every year I'd spent being small on purpose, and I let all of it go, and the sky came down to meet me.

The first warden reached me. The lightning did not ask my permission.

The crown-thing began, unhurried, to descend the steps.
