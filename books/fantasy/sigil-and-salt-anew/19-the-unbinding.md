## 19. The Unbinding

The thing about walking into a crowd that wants you dead is that eventually your feet learn the rhythm of it, and then it's almost like dancing.

Almost.

I moved through the Binding Day throng the way Odessa had taught me to move through fire—slowly, respectfully, as if it might let me pass if I didn't remind it I existed. Around me, Vessia had turned out in its finest. Banners of white and gold hung from every balcony in Coronation Square. Children sat on their fathers' shoulders. A choir was singing something about the king's eternal mercy, which would have been funnier if three hundred people weren't lined up in binding-chains below the spire, waiting for their gifts to be scooped out and fed to a dead man's hat.

Bramble's small hand was tucked in mine. That was the plan's one non-negotiable, established somewhere around hour nine of sewer-crawling: *I go where Yara goes.* She'd said it with her chin up and her chin is at approximately my elbow, so it hadn't been a very intimidating stance, but she'd meant it, and Bramble meaning things has always been more dangerous than most people's armies.

"You're doing the face," she murmured.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to do something stupid."

"I'm about to do something necessary."

"That's what stupid calls itself when it dresses up." She squeezed my fingers. "Perrin says the crown will know you the moment you touch the siphon. So don't touch it until Odessa's done."

"Odessa's done," came a voice in my ear—not magic, just Perrin's relay system, hedge-witch whispers passed hand to hand through the crowd like contraband. *The chain is cut. The dragon is free. Gods help us all.*

Above us, the sky over the palace shuddered.

I felt it before anyone else did—the way you feel a door opening in a house you thought had no doors. Twenty years of salt-flat silence, and now, suddenly, the whole world was *loud*. Vaskarath's fire, unchained after centuries, roared up through the palace foundations like a river remembering it was a river. Every gifted person in that square felt it too. I watched a woman near me lift her head like a deer scenting rain. I watched an old man begin, very quietly, to cry.

The choir faltered.

On the ceremonial dais, beneath the spire with its cage of black iron, the king raised his hands for silence—and the square gave it to him, because three hundred years of habit dies hard even when the king is a hunger wearing a coronet.

"My children," it said, and its voice was Aldric's voice, warm and reasonable and wrong, "today we complete what we began. Today every wild gift returns to the crown, where it belongs. Today, Vessia becomes whole."

*Today,* I thought, *you become a hat again.*

The first bound prisoner was led forward—a boy, maybe sixteen, ember-gifted, shaking so hard his chains sang. The wardens raised the binding-iron collar. And that was when the dragon-fire hit the siphon.

I'll be honest: we'd planned for the siphon to break. We had not planned for it to break *sideways*.

Vaskarath's flame, freed and furious, came up through the spire like a geyser through a cracked well-shaft, and instead of feeding the crown it scattered—ribbons of living fire arcing out over the square, touching the binding-irons on the prisoners' throats, and *unmaking* them. Collars fell open like startled birds. Gifts that had been caged for years came home to their owners all at once, and three hundred people in binding-chains lit up like festival lanterns.

The square went mad. Not with fear—with *recognition*. Somewhere a woman laughed and the laugh turned into a storm of sparks off her fingertips. Somewhere a man's shadow stood up and bowed to him. The choir stopped singing the king's mercy and started singing nothing at all, because half of them were busy discovering they could sing in colors.

And above it all, the crown-thing turned its head and looked directly at me.

It knew. Of course it knew. It had known since the moment the dragon woke, maybe since the moment I crossed the salt line with lightning still tangled in my hair. Its shadow moved against the light—Odessa had been right, gods, she'd been so horribly right—and the shadow reached for me across forty feet of flagstones like a hand made of night.

"Bramble," I said. "Run."

"No."

"Bramble—"

"You'll need someone to tell this story properly later," she said, and let go of my hand, and ran *toward* the dais steps, because that child has never once done the sensible thing, and I love her with the whole stupid storm of me.

The shadow caught me first.

It closed over me like cold water, and inside it I heard everything the crown had ever eaten—every bound gift screaming down the long throat of that hunger, decades of stolen thunder and borrowed light, all of it churning in the dark. It pressed against my skin looking for the door it had smelled in me since the valley. Looking for the storm.

*You could stop fighting,* it said, in the king's kind voice, in a hundred kings' kind voices. *You could rest. I have room.*

"Yeah," I said. "I've heard that pitch before. The valley elders had a version of it. So did the salt flats. Turns out everywhere I've ever hidden told me the same story: *be smaller, be quieter, be less.*" I let the lightning rise. All of it. Every volt I'd swallowed for twenty years, plus the new stuff, plus the dragon-fire threading through the air like golden thread. "I'm done being less."

I called the storm by name.

Mine. Not the valley's, not the sky's, not the usurper's. *Yara Villalobos's storm*, the one that had been waiting under my skin since I was six years old and the lightning found me, the one that had scarred my hands and gotten me exiled from the place of exile, the one I'd spent a lifetime pretending was somebody else's weather.

It came up through the flagstones. It came down through the clouds. It met in the middle, where I was standing, and for one terrible, perfect moment I was the middle.

The shadow burned away like fog at noon.

What was left was the crown-thing itself, stripped of Aldric's shape, and I finally saw it plain: a hunger old as the first throne, coiled inside the gold, wearing dead men because dead men can't say no. It shrieked—a sound like a kingdom being unwritten—and lashed out with everything it had stolen.

So I gave it more.

That's the part nobody warns you about, the part Perrin couldn't teach because he'd never managed it himself: you don't fight a hunger by starving it. You fight it by feeding it past its edges. I poured the storm into the coronet—my storm, the dragon's fire, and underneath it all, threaded through the lightning like a signature, the one thing the crown had never been able to digest:

Salt.

Twenty years of Salt Valley lived in my hands. The taste of it, the weight of it, the flat white silence where magic goes to wait. I wrapped the lightning in salt-memory and drove it home, and the hunger took it, gulped it down, and discovered that some things don't nourish. Some things only *end*.

The crown went white.

Not burning—*unbinding*. Every gift it had ever swallowed tore free at once, a fountain of small magics erupting into the sky: weather-sense and ember-whisper and shadow-walking and a hundred gifts whose names I'll never know, streaming upward like reversed rain, seeking their owners or, their owners gone, simply choosing new ones. The square glowed. People held up their hands and caught pieces of themselves out of the air.

And in the heart of the whiteness, something else came loose.

He was faint, the way old ink is faint. A tall man in a king's coat, worn thin by years of being worn. Aldric—or what was left of him, the true shade the hunger had kept as a handle, the man-shaped key it had used to open the kingdom. He looked at his own hands. He looked at the crown lying dead and hollow on the dais. He looked at me.

I braced for rage. For blame. For the terrible gratitude of the dead.

Instead, the shade of King Aldric bowed. Low. The bow a servant gives, not a king—one hand over his heart, head bent, holding it a breath longer than courtesy requires.

*Storm-caller,* he said, and his voice was just a voice now, tired and human. *I tried to hold it. In the end I couldn't even hold myself. You did what crowns are too heavy to do.* He straightened, and there was peace in his face, the specific peace of a man putting down something he'd carried too far. *Magic answers no throne. Say it loudly. Say it often. Make them sick of hearing it.*

"I will," I said. "Probably annoyingly often."

Something that might have been a smile crossed his fading face. Then the wind took him—not sadly, not violently, just the way wind takes anything that's finally ready to let go—and the last true king of Vessia dissolved into the morning light, and the morning light didn't seem to mind.

* * *

Afterward, the square was very quiet, in the way of places where everyone is crying and doesn't want to admit it.

The binding-chains lay in heaps, already rusting. Wardens stood in confused clumps, some reaching for irons that no longer answered them, some simply taking off their helmets and sitting down on the flagstones like men waking from a long fever. One of them, an older woman with grey in her braid, looked at her own bare hands, flexed them, and began to laugh—helpless, disbelieving, the laugh of someone who has just remembered something essential about herself.

Bramble found me before anyone else did. She always does.

"You did the thing," she said.

"I did the thing."

"The big thing. The whole—" she gestured at the sky, at the drifting motes of returned magic, at the smoking absence where a spire used to be. "All of it."

"All of it."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge sentencing. Then she punched me in the thigh, which is as high as she reaches, and said, "Don't ever make me watch that again," and buried her face in my side, and I held onto her while the square slowly filled with people who had spent years learning to be small and were, all of them, at once, finished with it.

Odessa arrived at a run, soot-faced, singed, grinning like a woman who'd just heard the best gossip of the century—which, technically, she had. Behind her, filling the sky over the ruined palace, Vaskarath circled once, twice, her vast wings blotting out the sun, and every person in Vessia got to see the last dragon fly free over the capital on Binding Day, which is the sort of image that rewrites a country whether you want it rewritten or not.

"She says," Odessa translated, hand pressed to her ear where the fire spoke, "that you burn clean, storm-child. She says she hasn't tasted a storm like yours since the world was young." A pause. Her grin went crooked. "And she says the council of regents is already arguing about who inherits the empty throne, and she finds them *hilarious*."

I looked at the dead crown on the dais. At the regents' banners already being lowered by sensible clerks. At the hundreds of people in the square turning, slowly, one by one, toward the girl covered in salt-dust and lightning-scars who had just unmade a monarchy's oldest theft.

They were starting to walk toward me.

"Oh no," I said.

"Oh yes," said Odessa, delighted.

"Odessa. They're going to want to give me something. They're going to want to put something on my head. I can see it happening in real time."

"Mm-hm."

"I refused a crown before it was cool to refuse a crown."

"Then you'd better think fast," she said, nodding past my shoulder, "because here comes the first one."

I turned. The grey-braided warden was approaching through the crowd, and behind her came dozens more—gifted and ungifted, wardens and prisoners, the whole improbable tangle of Vessia—and the warden dropped to one knee in front of me, and the crowd began to hush, and somewhere in the back a herald cleared his throat with the unmistakable intention of announcing something.

Bramble squeezed my hand. "Whatever you're going to say," she whispered, "say it loud enough for the dragon to hear. She'll spread it around."

I breathed in. Salt and rain and smoke and the bright clean smell of a world with no crown over its magic.

"Well," I said. "Let's start with this: nobody kneels."
