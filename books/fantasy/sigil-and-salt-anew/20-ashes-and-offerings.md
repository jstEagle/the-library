## 20. Ashes and Offerings

I have read fire my whole life, and I will tell you what no one believes until they see it: fire is honest. It does not perform. It does not flatter. When a thing burns, it burns all the way through, and what it leaves behind is the truth of what it was.

The morning after Binding Day, the capital smelled like the truth of itself.

Smoke still hung over the spire in grey ribbons, and the plaza where hundreds had come to be bound was littered with broken binding-iron—Perrin's iron, reversed, its cages sprung open like dead birds. People moved through it slowly, touching their own wrists, their own throats, as if checking that they were still theirs. Some of them were weeping. Some were laughing, which frightened them more. A woman stood at the center of it all with her hands raised to the sky, palms open, calling down a small, perfect rain that fell only on her, and she kept saying *it's mine, it's mine, it's mine* like a prayer she'd been forbidden to say.

I understood her. I had spent years reading other people's fires because I could not bear to tend my own.

Yara slept for two days.

Not in a bed—the palace had beds, but Yara took one look at the royal linens and said, with the flat finality of someone who has been exiled from one sanctuary already, "I'm not sleeping in a king's bed," and so we made them a pallet in the guard barracks among the conscripts who had thrown down their irons, and there they lay like something felled, storm-grey and silent, hands scarred and empty at last.

Bramble guarded them. She appointed herself without discussion, sat cross-legged at the foot of the pallet with her hedge-witch charms strung around her neck, and informed anyone who approached—including two regents' messengers and one very confused captain of the city watch—that Yara was *resting* and would receive visitors when the lightning said so. No one argued. I think half the city believed the lightning might actually weigh in.

I did not sleep. I went below.

* * *

Vaskarath was waiting for me.

The great doors beneath the palace had been blown open during the unbinding—I still don't know whether by Perrin's reversed iron or by the dragon herself—and the chamber beyond was scorched black in a fan-shape forty feet high, the mark of every frustrated breath she'd ever taken against her chains. The chains themselves lay in coils on the floor, and they were not metal, not really. They were binding-iron worked into links the size of my torso, and where they had touched her scales for—gods, how long? centuries?—the scales had grown pale and cold, like frost-bitten skin.

She watched me come down the stairs with one eye the size of a cartwheel, amber shot through with veins of gold that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart.

"You are the one who listens," she said. Her voice came not through air but through fire—I felt it in the ember-sense behind my eyes, in the place where I read flame. "The little fire-reader. You heard me dying."

"I heard you," I said. My voice cracked on the second word. I hadn't let myself cry yet—not during the battle, not while pulling Bramble out from under a collapsed colonnade, not while holding pressure on Perrin's wounds—but standing in front of an ancient being who had spent lifetimes as someone's furnace, my body apparently decided it was done waiting. "I'm sorry. We should have come sooner. The world should have—"

"The world does not owe me rescue." Smoke curled from her nostrils, lazy, almost amused. "You came. That is more than the last five hundred years offered."

I wiped my face with both hands, which accomplished nothing except redistributing the ash. "What will you do now? Fly free? The mountains north of Vessia—"

"Child." The word rumbled through the floor. "I am older than Vessia. I remember when this valley was a lake and your salt flats were its bed. Do you know what I am?"

"A dragon."

"A furnace," she said, "is what they made me. But what I *am* is a keeper of fire. Fire wants to be shared. That is its nature—it eats, it gives light, it spreads. They chained me because they thought my fire was a treasure to be hoarded by whoever held the chain." Her tail moved, slow, stirring the ash. "They were wrong about many things."

"What do you mean?"

"Come here, little listener. Stand in my light."

I should have been afraid. Every story I'd ever heard said run. But I had listened to this creature dying for weeks through Odessa-the-beacon, through the fire-sense that had made hunters chase me across half of Vessia, and her fire had never once lied to me. I walked into the heat.

It did not burn. It *filled*. Like standing in sunlight after a lifetime of candlelight, like the first breath after drowning. And in that light I saw—or the fire showed me—what she meant.

"There are others like you," I whispered. "Other keepers."

"Were. Scattered. Slain. I am the last, which is why they wanted me, and why they nearly had everything." Her eye turned, tracking me as I stepped back, shaking. "But hear the offer, fire-reader, because I make it once: I will remain. Not chained—*choosing*. I will nest in the deep places under this city, and I will give my fire freely to any gift that asks. Weather-callers and hedge-witches, ember-speakers and shadow-readers. Magic need never again be a thing one person owns and another person loses. Let it be a hearth instead of a hoard. Let everyone warm their hands and no one hold the poker over another's head."

I stared at her. "Why? After what they did—"

"Because the alternative is that they win," she said simply. "They taught your kingdom that magic is power, and power is possession, and possession justifies chains. If I fly away and hoard myself, I agree with them. If I stay and give—" smoke curled, thoughtful, "—then every child who ever feared their own gift learns that it was never the gift that was the monster."

I thought of Yara, asleep upstairs, hands empty and scarred. I thought of Bramble's family, bound and hollowed. I thought of Perrin, who built the chains and then spent ten years learning to hate himself for it.

"Yes," I said. "Yes. On behalf of—well, I don't actually represent anyone, but yes."

Something happened to her mouth that I chose to interpret as a smile. "Good. Now go tell your storm-child to stop sleeping like the dead. The city above is about to do something foolish, and it will want a witness."

* * *

The foolish thing, it turned out, was us. Or rather, the absence of anyone knowing what came next.

The council of regents convened in the great hall on the third day—a room I had only seen from the shadows, back when we wore warden grey and my heart lived in my throat. Now I walked in through the front doors beside Yara and Bramble, and the difference was so absurd I almost laughed. The same pillars. The same banners of Aldric's line, already being quietly taken down by servants who moved with the brisk efficiency of people who had always known the king was a lie.

There were seven regents left. Two had fled in the night. One had been found, it was said, trying to eat the contents of the treasury alone, which told you everything about the caliber of person the old system promoted.

Lord Cassel—oldest, driest, a man whose voice sounded like parchment being folded—rose when we entered. "The kingdom requires a sovereign," he announced, as though reporting weather. "The usurper is destroyed. The true king is dead and cannot be revived. By law and custom, the crown must pass—"

"To whom?" Yara asked. They didn't raise their voice. They never needed to anymore; since the unbinding, when Yara spoke, the air paid attention. Somewhere overhead, thunder muttered agreement, and Cassel visibly decided not to comment on it.

"The people have already chosen," Cassel said. His eyes went to Yara, and I watched him do the arithmetic: the storm that shattered the garrison, the storm that broke the crown, the figure who walked into a crowd of thousands and unbound them all. "The gifted one. The storm-caller. The realm knows the name Villalobos now. Let the crown—"

"No."

It was quiet, the way a drawn blade is quiet.

Cassel blinked. "My lord—my *lady*—the succession—"

"I'm neither," Yara said. "And no. You're doing the thing again. The exact thing. Something terrible wears the crown, so we find someone good to wear the crown, and then in eighty years their grandchild's regent is feeding dragons to it." They looked around the hall, at the banners coming down, at the regents shuffling their papers. "The problem was never *who* holds the crown. The problem is that magic answers to a throne at all. That's what I burned out of it. I'm not putting it back so I can be comfortable."

"Then what do you propose?" snapped Lady Vereen, sharp-faced, the only one of the seven who looked more angry than afraid. "Chaos? Every hedge-witch in the realm a law unto themselves?"

"An accord," Yara said. "Magic answers no throne. Gifts answer to their keepers, and keepers answer to each other. A circle—stewards, gifted and ungifted both—to hold what needs holding. Not rulers. Stewards." They paused, and something in their face softened, just slightly. "And the dragon has already agreed to be part of it. Freely given fire, for anyone who asks. No chains. No registries. No Binding Day."

Silence. Then Vereen, slowly: "The dragon. You speak of the dragon as a member of a council."

"She speaks for herself quite well, actually," Bramble piped up from Yara's elbow. "I've met her. She likes riddles and she says humans taste bad, which I found reassuring."

I pressed my lips together to keep from laughing, because this was, unbelievably, serious statecraft.

Cassel looked at Yara for a long moment. Then, with the weariness of a man watching the world he understood dissolve, he sat down. "The realm will want a symbol," he said. "People follow symbols. If not a crown—"

"Then they'll have to learn to follow each other," Yara said. "It's harder. It works better."

* * *

That evening, the city gathered in the ruined plaza—not summoned, just arrived, the way water finds low ground. Someone had built a bonfire from the wreckage of the binding-iron, and Vaskarath's presence rose through the streets like warmth from a hearth; you couldn't see her, but you could feel her underneath everything, patient and vast, and every fire in every window burned a little taller and a little kinder than it should have.

Perrin was carried out on a stretcher to see it. He'd insisted. The healers had fought him for two days and lost, partly because Perrin is stubborn and mostly because Bramble snuck him a strengthening charm that the healers never identified.

He looked terrible. Grey, hollowed, his left arm bound and splinted where the wardens' irons had burned him during his escape, his breathing wet and labored. But his eyes were clear, and when they laid him down near the fire, he looked at the flames for a long time.

"I built those," he said quietly, nodding at the melting iron. "Every link. I told myself it was mercy. Registration instead of slaughter. Binding instead of death." His jaw worked. "Ten years of telling myself that."

"Perrin." Yara crouched beside him. "You reversed it. Your own work, turned inside out, to free everyone. Whatever you built, you unbuilt. That counts."

"It counts," Perrin agreed, "because I'll spend the rest of my life making it count. Those aren't the same thing." He turned his head, and his gaze found mine across the fire. "Odessa. The dragon's offer. Will it hold? Can she truly give fire without it becoming... another crown? Another thing possessed?"

I reached out with the ember-sense, the way I had a thousand times these past months, and read the fire burning in the plaza bonfire, and beyond it the deeper fire banked beneath the city, ancient and enormous and *choosing*.

"It holds," I said. "It's the truest thing I've ever read."

Perrin closed his eyes. For a moment he looked ten years younger, or maybe just finally his own age. "Then perhaps," he murmured, "I broke exactly enough things to deserve to help build this one."

Bramble sat down next to him and put her small hand on his splinted arm, and began, very seriously, to tell him about the root cellar, and the six days in the dark, and the charm her mother had woven into her braid before the wardens came—the one that had kept her hidden, the one she was going to teach to every hiding child in Vessia if it took the rest of her life. Perrin listened the way penitents listen. Around them, the crowd thickened: the rain-woman from the first morning, laughing now as she called water for a farmer's dry field; weather-readers comparing notes with ember-speakers; a former warden standing awkwardly at the edge until a hedge-witch waved him in.

Nobody led it. That was the miracle. Nobody led it, and it happened anyway.

Near midnight, someone started singing—an old valley song, one I knew from the exile roads, about salt and rain meeting at the edge of things. Voices caught it raggedly, imperfectly, and carried it. Yara stood at the edge of the firelight, arms crossed, watching their work hold, and I came to stand beside them.

"They'll ask you again," I said. "The regents. The crowds. Someone will put a crown on a cushion and carry it toward you, and the crowd will cheer, because crowds love a coronation more than they love freedom."

"I know." Yara's scarred hands flexed once, open, closed. "Let them ask. I've got a better answer every time."

"And when they don't accept the answer?"

Yara smiled—tired, real, the first smile I'd seen on them that wasn't edged with lightning. "Then I'll introduce them to my steward friends. All of them. Especially the one with the teeth."

Above us, far above, thunder rolled across a sky that belonged to no king, and beneath our feet, ancient fire settled into its new shape: not a furnace, not a hoard—a hearth, waiting with the door open.

We had won. I kept turning the word over, testing it, the way you test a bone you're not sure is set. It held. But even as the song swelled and the ashes drifted up like grey snow, I caught Yara glancing east, toward the salt flats, toward a valley of elders who had voted to bar a stranger and banish a child—and I saw in their face that some fires, once lit, do not rest until they have burned all the way home.
