## 22. Where the Salt Meets the Rain

The salt flats hadn't moved. That was the first thing I noticed, and the most absurd. I had crossed half a kingdom, unmade a crown, survived a Binding Day that would be sung about in taverns for the next hundred years—badly, and with the wrong details—and here was the valley, exactly where I'd left it, white and flat and indifferent as an unwashed plate.

"You're scowling at geography," Odessa said beside me.

"I'm greeting it properly."

Bramble, riding pillion behind me on the mule we'd named Turnip for reasons that had seemed hilarious in Vessia and now merely seemed like a fact of our lives, leaned around my shoulder to squint at the horizon. "It's very... shiny," she said.

"It's salt," I said. "It does one thing and does it forever."

"Like Perrin's cooking."

From somewhere behind us came the long-suffering sound of a former royal binder who had heard every joke about his stew and intended to survive hearing them all. "The stew was *edible*," he called. "That is more than can be said for your attempt at bread, small witch."

"That bread was *experimental*."

We crested the last rise before the flats, and there it all was: the valley cradled in its bowl of white, the low grey houses huddled together like people at a funeral, the nets strung between poles, drying. Smoke from cookfires standing perfectly straight in the windless air. Somewhere down there were the elders who had voted to send me away, the neighbors who had never once asked about my hands, the whole soft machinery of forgetting I had been born into and had mistaken, for twenty years, for peace.

My storm-scarred hands flexed on the reins. They still ached when weather was coming. They ached now, though the sky above the valley was a clean, pale blue, innocent as a lie told well.

"Well," I said. "Nobody's thrown anything yet."

"They can't see us yet," Bramble pointed out helpfully.

"Give it time."

* * *

We walked the mule across the flats because riding felt wrong—the way you don't run in a sickroom. The salt crunched under our boots with that particular sound I had grown up on and then not heard for over a year, and hearing it again did something strange to my chest, like a key turning in a lock I'd assumed was rusted shut.

Odessa walked close enough that her sleeve brushed mine. She'd been quiet since the ridge, which for Odessa meant she was reading something. Not fire this time. Me.

"Say it," I said finally.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You've been composing it since the ridge. You have a face for composing."

"My face is *serene*."

"Your face is a war council."

She laughed, short and unwilling, and then went quiet again, and then said it anyway, because Odessa had never once in her life let a true thing go unsaid just to spare someone's feelings. It was one of the ten thousand reasons I loved her, and roughly nine thousand of those reasons were also deeply inconvenient.

"They'll ask you to stay," she said. "And you'll want to say yes out of guilt, and you shouldn't, and also—" she kicked a crust of salt, watched it shatter, "—you might say yes anyway, and I'd understand that too, and I'd hate it, and I'd stay if you asked me to."

I stopped walking.

Turnip stopped walking, because Turnip had opinions about momentum and held them loosely.

"Odessa."

"Don't make it a moment. I'll combust."

"You just offered to move to a salt desert where magic doesn't work, for me."

"I said *if you asked*. There's a difference. There's dignity in the conditional tense." But her ears had gone red, and her hand found mine without either of us deciding anything, scarred palm against fire-reader's callus, and we stood there on the white flats like two idiots in a ballad, while behind us Bramble made a noise of profound theatrical suffering and Perrin pretended very hard to be interested in the horizon.

"We're not staying," I said. "Not permanently. But I'm not running through this time, either. I'm walking in the front way, and I'm asking them to come out."

"And if they won't?"

"Then they won't. And the valley will keep doing its one thing, forever, and I'll visit." I squeezed her hand. "But I think they will. People surprise you, once you stop giving them scripts."

* * *

They saw us coming, of course. A valley has nothing to do but watch its edges.

By the time we reached the first houses, a line of elders stood in the lane outside Maren's weaving shed, arms folded, faces arranged into expressions I recognized from every vote they'd ever taken: gravity worn like a garment, decision already made underneath. Old Sefa at the center, as always, her spine straight as a net-pole. Behind them, filling doorways and leaning off fences, the rest of the valley—every face I'd grown up among, every person who had watched my hands scar and never once said the word *storm* aloud, because saying it might invite it.

Sefa looked at me for a long moment. Then past me, at the others. At Odessa, whose gift she had voted to bar. At Perrin, whose binding-iron sigils were inked up both forearms where he made no effort anymore to hide them. At Bramble, who was eleven and had survived things no eleven-year-old should and stared back at the elders with the flat, unimpressed gaze of a cat being introduced to a dog.

"Yara Villalobos," Sefa said.

"Sefa Harrow. Still upright, I see."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the shape a smile leaves in ash. "You were told not to come back."

"I was told a lot of things." I spread my arms. The scars caught the light. "Here I am. All of me. No hiding the hands, no hunching, no pretending I'm only good with knots. This is what you exiled. I thought you should see it."

Silence. The wind had picked up slightly—I hadn't done that, I checked, I *checked*—and stirred the dust in the lane.

"You brought wardens' marks into our valley," someone muttered. An old man, Tovan, who had taught me to mend trawl nets and had never once met my eyes after my fourteenth summer.

"He brought himself," Perrin said mildly. "The marks are just ink. Though I'll note the binding-iron those sigils describe is currently being melted down into plowshares by order of the Concord, so if anyone here wants to lodge a complaint, the queue forms behind the dragon."

"The *dragon*," Tovan said faintly.

"She sends regards," Bramble said. "Well. She sends a feeling. It's mostly regards-shaped."

I held up a hand before this could become a whole thing. "I didn't come to gloat. I came to ask something. The purge is over. The crown that ordered it is slag. Magic answers no throne now—that's law, or near enough to law that the regents are too frightened to argue. Which means the thing this valley was built to hide from is gone." I looked at each elder in turn. "You built a beautiful place here. A place where people could set their gifts down and breathe. I know what that cost. I lived inside that cost for twenty years. But hiding was a survival strategy, not a purpose. And survival strategies are supposed to end when the danger does."

"And if we prefer our silence?" Sefa asked.

"Then keep it. Truly. But some of the people behind you—" I nodded at the doorways, the fences, the children peering around their mothers' skirts, "—have been holding gifts under their tongues for years, swallowing them like stones. I could name names. I *was* one of the names. And the world outside isn't the world you fled anymore. There's a school forming in Vessia. There's a Concord circle that includes hedge-witches and fisherwomen and one extremely smug former binder. There's a dragon who will talk to anyone who brings her questions and doesn't waste her time." I paused. "There's rain now, Sefa. Real rain, past the flats. The storms reach the coast again. I checked on the way in."

Another silence, longer. In it I heard the valley breathing—that particular quality of quiet I'd forgotten, the hush of a place where everyone is listening for something they hope never comes.

It was not Sefa who broke it. It was a girl. Fifteen, maybe. Jena Coalson's daughter, the one who'd been born the winter I left. She stepped out from behind her mother's skirt with her chin up and her fists clenched, and when she opened her mouth, frost bloomed across the fence rail beside her, delicate as lace, entirely involuntary.

"I want to go," she said, to the elders, to the lane, to me, to nobody. "I've wanted to go since I was eight. My hands are cold *all the time* and nobody would ever say why."

Her mother made a small wounded sound and did not pull her back.

Sefa closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked older and lighter at once, like a woman setting down a load she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy. "One girl," she said. "As a start. The valley will provision her. And Yara—" her gaze settled on me, and for the first time in my life I saw her look at my hands without flinching, "—you'll escort her. And when you bring word of how she fares, you'll bring it to *me*, and we'll speak as equals, and I will apologize properly, because I owe you an apology I have been rehearsing for twenty years and it deserves an audience."

I blinked. Of all the outcomes I'd rehearsed on the road—and I had rehearsed many, including several involving thrown objects—this was not one.

"That's..." I cleared my throat. "That's acceptable."

"It's more than acceptable, it's *generous*, and don't you dare cry about it in my lane, Villalobos, the salt's wet enough."

Behind me, Bramble snorted. Odessa's hand found mine again. And somewhere overhead, quite without my permission—or perhaps with it; I honestly couldn't tell anymore—a single cloud gathered itself out of the blue and let fall exactly three drops of rain onto the white flats, where they hissed and vanished, leaving three small dark stars on the salt.

Everyone stared at me.

"That wasn't me," I said.

"It was a little you," Odessa murmured.

"It was *atmospheric conditions*."

* * *

We stayed three days. Long enough for the valley's curiosity to overcome its caution, which in Salt Valley terms was practically a riot.

Perrin spent an afternoon surrounded by elders demanding to know exactly how binding-iron worked, so they could recognize it if it ever came again, and another afternoon being fed until he begged mercy. Bramble taught a ring of children a cantrip for making dandelions sneeze, which caused a minor diplomatic incident with the goats. Odessa sat with the valley's fire-tenders and read their hearth-flames for them, telling each family small truths—their fires were content, mostly, and lonely, sometimes, in ways that made several women cry and hug her, to her visible alarm.

And me? I mended nets.

Old habit. Old love, actually, though it had taken me a year and a kingdom to admit it. I sat on the dock with Tovan—who eventually, gruffly, sat beside me—and we worked in the rhythm I'd learned before I could write, and on the second day he said, without looking up, "You always were better at the double-lacing than anyone," and that was the whole apology, and it was somehow enough.

On the third evening, Sefa found me at the edge of the flats, where the salt gave way to the first stubborn grasses of the outer shore, and where the evening clouds were stacking up over the sea in great silver towers—the kind of sky I used to ache at, and now simply admired, the way you admire a relative who's calmed down with age.

"You're leaving tomorrow," she said. Not a question.

"At dawn. Jena's girl will ride with us as far as the Ember Road. There's a wagon from the Wren network meeting us there."

"And after?"

"After, we go home." I said it and then heard it, really heard it, and had to stand with the strangeness of it. Home. Not a place I was hiding in. A place with a crooked chimney in Vessia that Bramble had charmed to smoke in the shape of insults when she was annoyed, and a garden Perrin was attempting, and Odessa's boots by the door, and a room kept ready for whoever needed it next, because there was always someone next.

Sefa followed my gaze to the storm-clouds. "It's strange," she said. "All these years I thought we were safe here. Now I look at that sky and I think—we were just *small* here. There's a difference, and I was too proud to see it."

"Small isn't shameful," I said. "It just shouldn't be the whole story."

"No." She was quiet a moment. Then she took something from her pocket and pressed it into my hand: a small lump of valley salt, worn smooth, drilled through with a hole and threaded on a leather cord. "Every exile got one, once. We stopped making them when I was young. It was meant to remind you where safety lay." She closed my fingers around it. "Wear it backwards. Let it remind you instead that safety was never the same thing as living."

I put it on. It sat warm against my sternum, right over the place where the crown's hunger had once tried to root itself, and where my own name lived now, unhidden, unashamed.

"Thank you, Sefa."

"Go home, storm-caller. And send rain when you think of us." A pause. The ghost-smile again. "Not too much. We're still a valley."

* * *

Dawn came up gold and grey, and we stood at the salt line with Jena's daughter—Mira, her name was Mira, and she flinched at first when I told her, then decided to be delighted by it—and the flats stretched white behind us all the way to the mountains, and the road ran green ahead toward everything else.

Bramble was asleep on Turnip before we'd gone a mile. Perrin was sketching plowshare designs. Odessa rode knee-to-knee with me, and when the first clouds of the day rolled in off the sea, fat and ordinary and full of honest water, she tilted her face up and let the rain hit her and grinned like the fire-reader she was, unafraid of a little weather.

"Any of this yours?" she asked.

"Not a drop."

"Liar."

"Fine. Maybe the big one. Over the ridge. As a farewell."

"As a *farewell*."

"Some people wave."

The rain came down proper then, warm and steady, drumming on the salt behind us and the grass ahead of us, and Mira stuck her hand out from under the wagon canvas and laughed when it soaked her, and I realized I was laughing too, and that my hands—scarred, aching, alive—were open to the sky, holding nothing, needing nothing, belonging everywhere the rain fell and a few places it didn't.

Somewhere far to the east, deep under a mountain, an old dragon lifted her head and tasted the storm on the wind, and I felt her amusement arrive like a knock at the door, familiar as breakfast:

*Show-off.*

"Absolutely," I said out loud, to no one, to everyone, and turned the mule toward home.
