## 4. The Salt-Witch of Greyhollow

Greyhollow did not appear on any map August had ever drawn, and she had drawn every map worth owning in the Thornfell marches.

That was the first thing she liked about it.

The village—if it could be called that—clung to the underside of a granite shelf three days' hard walk north of the Weeping Stair, tucked into a fold of mountain so deep that the sun reached it for perhaps an hour at midsummer and never at all in winter. The houses were built of stacked slate and roofed with salt-cured hides gone stiff as armor. Everything smelled of brine and cold stone and something older underneath, something like the inside of a shell.

August came down the goat track in the last grey light of evening with the bloodstained map wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, where it had lived for six days now like a second heartbeat. She had tried to leave it behind twice. Both times she'd made it half a mile before the ache in her ribs turned her around—not fear of losing it, though that was there too, but a physical wrongness, like walking out on a limb that hadn't finished growing into her.

She did not like what that meant. Maps did not grow into people. Maps were tools, and tools could be set down.

*The crown is not the king.* The courier's whisper had followed her across four passes and two rivers, through a checkpoint ambush she'd barely crawled out of, through nights spent in hollow trees listening for hounds. She had turned the words over until they lost their shape and then found it again, sharper each time. A riddle with teeth.

And there was exactly one person in the Thornfells who might know why a dying man had bled a map into a stranger's hands instead of carrying it home to his masters.

Maren Voss. The salt-witch of Greyhollow. Exiled from the Salt Cathedral at Whiteharbor eleven years ago for heresy—though no one agreed on which heresy, and the stories contradicted one another so thoroughly that August had long ago concluded they were all true. That she had spoken the unspeakable name of the sea into consecrated salt. That she had refused to bless the king's fleet. That she had told a bishop, to his face, that grief was not a resource to be harvested but a debt to be honored—and that the crown's whole prosperity rested on harvesting it.

August believed that last story most. It was the only one that sounded like something a woman would be exiled for.

The village watch—two old men with billhooks who saw her coming long before she saw them, because of course they did; nothing moved on that track without Greyhollow knowing—let her pass without a word. Word traveled ahead of her anyway. It always did, in places like this. *A stranger, alone, armed lightly, walking like someone who knows the ground.* They would have decided within the hour whether she was trouble, and by the time she reached the witch's door they would have already told Maren Voss everything about her except the things that mattered.

The house stood apart from the others, at the shelf's dripping edge, where water seeped through the rock face in a hundred thin threads and froze into curtains of ice in winter. Salt crystals grew along the sill in pale ferns. There was no sign, no mark, no symbol of trade or craft. Just a door of driftwood, silvered with age, and above it a single line cut into the lintel in a script August didn't recognize—a spiral broken open, uncoiling, unfinished.

She raised her hand to knock.

"Knock," said a voice from behind her, "and I'll know you're a fool. Fools get turned away."

August turned slowly, hand drifting to the knife at her hip more from habit than intent. A woman stood at the edge of the salt garden below the house, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms crusted white to the wrist as if she'd been wading in the sea rather than tending rows of crystallizing pans. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, grey-streaked hair bound back with cord, and her eyes were the flat pale grey of weathered slate—the eyes of someone who had looked at the horizon so long that looking back had become her whole expression.

"You're Maren Voss," August said.

"And you're lost," the woman said. "Or you're not, which is worse. Lost people I can point downhill. People who come to my door on purpose usually want something the world shouldn't give them." She wiped her hands on her apron, leaving fresh white streaks. "So. Which are you?"

"My name is August Coldharbour."

Something flickered across the slate-grey eyes. Not recognition—August was careful enough that her name meant nothing—but assessment. Recalculation. "Coldharbour," Maren repeated, tasting it. "Border name. Old border name."

"I need to understand a sigil."

"Everyone needs something. Sigils, curses, love charms, revenge. Half of them want the same thing under different words: they want the world to owe them." Maren climbed the path toward the house, unhurried, forcing August either to follow or stand like a post. "What kind of sigil?"

"The kind that binds a king."

Maren stopped climbing. For a long moment the only sound was the drip of water through the rock and the wind worrying the salt-cured hides on the neighboring roofs. Then she laughed—one short, harsh note with no humor in it at all.

"Eleven years," she said, "I've been in this hole, and the world finally sends me a visitor worth the walk down. Come inside, Coldharbour. And touch nothing white."

* * *

The interior of the house was dim and dense with smell: brine, tallow, dried kelp, ink, and beneath it all the mineral coldness of deep stone. Shelves lined every wall, crowded not with books but with jars—hundreds of them, thousands, each holding salt of a slightly different color and grain, labeled in that same broken-spiral script. August's cartographer's eye catalogued them helplessly: rose-grey, bone-white, faint blue, black as iron filings, one jar near the ceiling that seemed to hold light the way water holds a drowned star.

"Sit," Maren said, indicating a stool by the hearth. She herself remained standing, arms folded, studying August with the frank rudeness of someone who had stopped performing courtesy decades ago. "Now. You're going to tell me how you came by this problem, and you're going to tell me true, because I'll know if you don't. Salt doesn't lie and neither do I, and we're the same thing these days."

August weighed her options and found, as always, that they were fewer than she pretended. She told it straight: the courier at the ford, the royal seals, the bloodstained map, the words *the crown is not the king*, the inquisitors' hounds behind her. She left out the weeping—left out the way the vault appeared on the parchment only when her tears touched it, left out the six nights she had sat awake telling herself it was a trick of lamplight and damp. Some things you didn't hand to a stranger. Even a stranger whose jars glowed.

But Maren Voss watched her tell it, and August had the unpleasant certainty that the woman heard the omissions anyway, the way you can hear the gap where a tooth used to be.

When August finished, Maren said nothing for a while. She went to a shelf, took down a jar of coarse grey salt, and set it on the table between them like a chess piece.

"A binding on a king," she said. "You understand what you're asking. The Salt King isn't a story we tell children to keep them from the tide pools. He's real, he's old, and he's the reason the realm eats while the borders starve—or thinks it is. Every harvest blessing, every coronation oath, every coin minted in Whiteharbor carries his mark. The crown wears him like a ring wears a finger." Her mouth twisted. "I said as much, once, in a room full of bishops. They gave me a mule, a bag of salt, and a choice between exile and the quiet rooms beneath the cathedral. I chose the mule."

"Why?" August asked. "Why refuse? One blessing costs you nothing."

"One blessing," Maren said, "is how it starts. You draw a little circle, you speak a little grief into it, you ask the Salt King to carry what's too heavy for the grieving. And he does. That's the horror of him, girl—he *does*. He takes the sorrow and gives back order, and everyone breathes easier, and nobody asks where the sorrow goes." She tapped the jar. "It goes into him. And he grows. And the man wearing the crown gets a little less man every year, until one day there's a thing on the throne wearing a father's face, and the daughter comes to court and he looks through her like she's glass."

August went very still. "How do you know about the daughter?"

Maren's slate eyes didn't blink. "Because I know what the binding does, and I can do sums. Now." She uncorked the jar. "Before I teach you anything—before I even confirm your map is what you think it is—you're going to do one thing for me."

"Name it."

"Grief," Maren said simply. "Freely given. Not performed. Not traded. Given." She poured a thin line of salt onto the scarred tabletop, then drew one fingertip through it, tracing a shape August almost recognized—an echo of the spiral over her door, closed this time, complete. "This is a truth-pan. Old craft, pre-cathedral, the kind they burned the last practitioners for. Whatever you carry, you'll lay it down here, and the salt will show me its weight. If you lie, the line stays clean. If you grieve honestly—" she shrugged, "—the pan will weep. Salt draws water. Water tells truth. That's the whole of my heresy, if you want it in one sentence."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you drink my tea, admire my jars, and walk back down the mountain none the wiser, and in a year the Salt King finishes eating your king and everything he rules, including, eventually, this village and its stubborn old witch." Maren met her eyes. "I'd survive it. I've survived worse. But I'd rather not."

August looked at the line of salt. Her hands, she noticed, had begun to shake—not with fear. With something worse. With the specific dread of a woman who had built her entire life on the principle that she owed no one anything, least of all her own history, being asked to open the ledger.

"I haven't grieved properly in ten years," she said quietly. "I made a rule against it. Never be seen, never be owed. Grief is a debt you take on. So I just... didn't."

"I know the rule," Maren said, and for the first time her voice softened by a degree, like frost giving way at noon. "I invented a version of it myself. Exile is just invisibility with worse weather." She nodded at the salt. "Lay it down, Coldharbour. Whatever it is. The pan won't judge. Neither will I. But I need to know what the map has to work with."

August understood then, with a lurch, what Maren meant. *What the map has to work with.* As if the map were alive. As if it fed.

She thought about refusing. She thought about the ford, the courier's fingers slick with blood pressing the oilcloth into her hands, the terrible intimacy of being chosen by a dying stranger. She thought about ten years of unmapped roads and unnamed camps, competence worn like armor over a loneliness so old it had fossilized into personality.

She put her hand flat on the table, beside the salt line, and began to speak.

* * *

She spoke of Thornfell village, though she had not said its name aloud in a decade. She spoke of her mother teaching her letters by drawing coastlines in wood ash. Of her father's survey chains hanging by the door, clicking in the wind like patient insects. Of the year the king's assessors came to count the valley's souls and found forty-one too many unregistered, and the year after that when the soldiers came to correct the arithmetic.

She did not describe the fire. Her voice simply stopped working in the middle of a sentence about her mother's hands, and what came out instead was a sound she hadn't made since she was nineteen years old—raw, graceless, the crying of someone badly out of practice, because she was.

And the salt line wept.

Not metaphorically. Water rose through the table's grain, beading along the traced spiral, and the salt drank it and darkened and began to move—slowly, granule by granule, reforming itself into shapes. August watched through blurred eyes as the pan drew a coastline she knew better than her own pulse. Drew a valley. Drew a cluster of small marks like houses, and then drew the marks again, crossed out, and crossed out again, over and over, the way grief actually works—not once but perpetually, every night, forever.

"There it is," Maren said softly. She wasn't gloating. She looked tired, and old, and sad, and somehow that was worse than triumph. "Ten years of it, packed down dry. No wonder the map chose you. Grief like that isn't a wound, Coldharbour. It's a well. And there are things in this world that have been thirsty a very long time."

"What things?" August's voice came out scraped.

Maren didn't answer directly. She swept the salt into her palm, and as she did, August saw the last shape the pan had made before collapsing: not the valley, not the houses, but a single figure standing at the edge of the drawing, small and distinct, rendered in black grains that hadn't been in the jar.

A woman. Alone. Holding a map.

"That's enough for tonight," Maren said, and corked the jar with a finality that closed the subject like a door.

* * *

They ate bread and smoked fish and drank tea brewed from something bitter and alpine, and Maren asked questions in the sideways manner of someone assembling a picture from fragments: Where had August learned her roads? Who else knew she carried the map? Had she seen the inquisitor himself, or only his hounds?

"Hale," August said. "Corwin Hale. He was at the ford an hour after I ran. He interrogated the ferryman—I heard it from the reeds. He's thorough. He'll have my description by now, and my direction."

"Hale." Maren chewed the name like gristle. "Whiteharbor's favorite son. They say he's never lost a case. They also say he pays the families of the convicted out of his own purse when the sentence leaves orphans." She snorted. "The dangerous ones are never the cruel ones. Cruelty you can predict. Conviction will follow you into hell and apologize for the inconvenience."

"Then we should move."

"We should sleep," Maren corrected. "You look like something a cartographer erased by accident. Tomorrow we open your map properly, and I show you what the sigil-work underneath it means, and you're going to wish very hard that you'd stayed lost." She stood, joints cracking, and paused at the doorway to the back room. "One more thing, since the pan already said it. Your map—weeps when you weep. Yes?"

August's spine went cold. "I never told you that."

"You didn't have to. It's keyed to grief—that's the whole architecture of the Salt King's craft. Sorrow is his currency, his lock, and his key." Maren's slate eyes held hers across the dim room, and in them August saw something she recognized with a jolt: the exact wariness of a woman deciding, against all her own rules, to trust. "Which means whatever's waiting at the end of that map already knows precisely how to reach you, August Coldharbour. It knows your mother's hands. It knows the fire. Sleep with your knife close."

After Maren withdrew, August sat a long time by the banked hearth, the oilcloth bundle heavy against her ribs, listening to the water thread through the rock outside—drip, drip, drip, patient as a countdown.

Through the wall, faintly, she heard Maren speaking low and rhythmic, the cadence of someone reciting old craft over old salt, and one phrase surfaced clearly enough to catch:

"—a Coldharbour's grief, freely given, as it was given before—"

August's blood turned to meltwater.

*Given before.*

Her family's name. In a ritual. In a witch's mouth, in a heretic's hut at the edge of the map, spoken like a line from a contract already signed.

She did not sleep. She sat sharpening her knife and watching the door, and somewhere past midnight, far down the southern passes, she imagined she could hear hounds—though that, she told herself, was impossible.

It was not impossible. It was merely early.
