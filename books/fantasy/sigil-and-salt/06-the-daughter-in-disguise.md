## 6. The Daughter in Disguise

The town of Marrowgate sat on the border like a coin half-swallowed by a slot—half of it owing fealty to the crown, the other half to whoever held the toll bridge that week. August had passed through it eleven times over ten years, and each time she had learned something new about how a place rots when it belongs to no one and everyone: which wells ran sweet, which innkeepers sold names to the crown's riders, which alleys swallowed footsteps whole.

She came down from the high road at dusk, when the fog off the Saltmere flats rolled in thick enough to make even her maps useless, and let herself be ordinary. A drover's oilskin. A limp she didn't have. Her hair bound under a wool cap, her hands cracked with walnut stain so they looked like any laborer's hands. The map rode against her ribs, wrapped in waxed linen, warm as a second heartbeat.

Three days since the ford. Three days since the courier's blood had soaked through his glove into her palm, since he'd whispered *the crown is not the king* with the last air in his lungs. She had not slept more than an hour at a stretch since. Not because of the inquisitors—she could outlast inquisitors; she'd been doing it since she was fourteen—but because the map was *there*, against her chest, and every time she closed her eyes she felt it listening.

*It wants something from me.* She kept the thought on a short leash. Fear fed things like that. She'd learned that much already, watching salt bloom where grief pooled too long.

Marrowgate's market square still held its evening crowd: fishwives haggling by lamplight, a fiddler playing for coppers outside the Gilded Eel, soldiers from the garrison drinking their wages at the door of the taproom. August moved through it all like water through reeds, buying a heel of bread, a twist of salt pork, nothing that required her to speak above a mumble. She was halfway across the square, calculating the hours until the north gate changed watch, when she felt it.

A touch at her hip. Light as a moth landing.

Her knife was out before her mind finished the thought. She spun, caught a wrist—a small wrist, fine-boned, far too clean for a pickpocket working this square—and twisted. The boy—no, not a boy, she saw now, a girl in a boy's coat, cap pulled low, a smudge of soot artfully applied to one cheekbone—stumbled into her, and August pinned the arm behind her back and walked her two swift steps into the shadow between a fishmonger's stall and the wall, all in the time it took the fiddler to change tunes.

"Give it back," August said quietly.

"Give what back?" The voice was low, pitched down, but it cracked at the edges with something that wasn't fear. Indignation. Genuine, aristocratic indignation, the kind that had never once been told *no* without consequence.

August turned out the girl's pocket with her free hand. Her own purse, yes—and beneath it, lifted from some other mark, a silver locket worked with the royal crest: a stag crowned in salt-flowers. And beneath *that*, folded into a scrap of oilcloth, a signet ring. Old gold. The king's own seal, worn smooth as a river stone.

The square went very quiet inside August's head. The fiddler played on. The fishwives argued. None of them knew that the world had just tilted.

"That's not yours," August said.

"Neither is yours." The girl twisted—not clumsily, but with training, court-trained, the kind of fencing-master footwork that translated badly to alleywork—and nearly slipped the hold. August tightened her grip and felt the girl wince, felt something else too: the tremor in her that wasn't cold. It was hunger. Days of it. "You're hurting me."

"You were robbing me." August leaned close, keeping both their faces in shadow. "So here's what happens now. You tell me who you are, or I walk you to the garrison and let them ask. They're less gentle than I am."

Something shifted in the girl's breathing. When she spoke again, the street-pitch was gone, and what remained underneath made August's skin prickle—the vowels of the palace, rounded and precise, worn thin by weeks of hard travel.

"If you take me to the garrison," the girl said, "you'll be dead before midnight. So will I. So think carefully, smuggler, about whose company you're threatening to put me in."

"I'm not a smuggler."

"No?" A breath that might have been a laugh. "You hold a knife like you were born holding it. Your coat has three hidden pockets—I found two. And you checked the rooftops twice since you entered the square, once for each gate. Only two kinds of people do that. Smugglers and spies." A pause. "And I've been watching spies all my life. They stand differently."

August's blood ran slow and cold. She had been seen. For the first time in ten years, someone had *seen* her, read her like a broadsheet, and the violation of it was almost worse than the danger.

"Who are you?" she asked again, softer.

The girl was silent a moment. Then, in a voice stripped of everything but exhaustion: "You already know. You saw the ring."

August looked down at the signet in her palm. The king's seal. Worn smooth—worn by a hand that had worried it for years, turning it, pressing it into wax, pressing it against a forehead in prayer or despair. She thought of the dying courier. *The crown is not the king.*

"Isolde," she said. Just the name. It fell between them like a dropped blade.

The girl went rigid in her grip. Then, incredibly, she laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it at all.

"Well," said Princess Isolde Aurelian, heir-presumptive to a throne that no longer knew her face, "at least I don't have to invent a surname."

* * *

August did not take her to a safehouse. Safehouses were for people whose faces weren't stamped on every wanted bill from here to the capital. Instead she took her to the one place in Marrowgate no soldier would look: the flooded cellar of a derelict tannery, where the smell of old lime and rotting hides drove off anyone with functioning nostrils, and where August had cached blankets, lamp oil, and dried food years ago against exactly this kind of night.

Isolde sat on an upturned barrel, hands wrapped around a tin cup of barley tea, and drank like someone who had forgotten what warmth was. In the lamplight August could see what the disguise had hidden: the sharp, handsome bones of the Aurelian line, the shadows under her eyes deep enough to drown in, the calluses on her fingers that no princess should have—rope-burn, blisters healed badly, the marks of someone who had climbed fences and slept in ditches and learned, recently and brutally, what her body could endure.

"You've been out here six weeks," August said. It wasn't a guess. The state of her boots said it.

"Seven." Isolde set down the cup. "Give or take. Time gets strange when you're running." She looked around the cellar with distaste she didn't bother hiding. "You live like this?"

"I live nowhere. That's rather the point."

"Yes." Something flickered across Isolde's face—envy, maybe, or its cousin. "I used to think invisibility was a curse reserved for servants and the dead. Now I'd trade my title for it."

"Why did you run?"

Isolde's jaw tightened. August watched her decide whether to lie, watched the lie assemble itself behind her eyes—the practiced deflections of a court-raised mind—and then watched her discard it, piece by piece, because there was no one left to perform for.

"Because my father doesn't know me anymore." The words came out flat, sanded smooth by repetition, the way a story gets when it's been told only to oneself. "Not metaphorically. Not 'the king is distant, the king is burdened.' He looks at me and there is *nothing*. Like looking into a room where the fire's gone out. Six months ago he started forgetting things. Names. Dates. Then he started forgetting *people*. Ministers he'd known thirty years. My mother's grave—he stood at it and asked the chaplain whose it was." Her voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse. "And then I found him talking to his crown."

August went still. "Talking to it."

"At night. When he thinks he's alone. He takes it off its stand—the coronation crown, the old one, the heavy thing with the salt-iron band—and he holds it in his lap and he *talks*. Conversations. Long ones. Sometimes he weeps. Sometimes he laughs, which is infinitely worse." Isolde stared into her cup. "I listened at the door once. I shouldn't have. His side of it sounded like a man begging. The other side—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There wasn't another voice. But the candles all bent toward him. Every flame in the room, leaning like grass in wind. Toward the crown."

The map pressed warm against August's ribs. She thought of impossible coastlines, of a vault that surfaced only through tears, of a courier dying in the shallows with royal seals on his case. *The crown is not the king.* Pieces she hadn't wanted began fitting together, and she hated each click of them.

"When did you leave?"

"The night he called me by my mother's name and asked why I'd come to him wearing our daughter's face." Isolde's composure finally cracked—not into tears, but into fury, white-hot and controlled, the anger of someone raised never to show it. "He looked straight at me. He loved me in that moment, I could see it, and it was love for a woman ten years dead, and there was nothing of *me* anywhere in his eyes. So I stole a horse, a coat, and forty crowns, and I left. And then the forty crowns ran out, and it turns out princesses are spectacularly unqualified for poverty."

Despite everything—despite the dread sitting in her chest like swallowed river-stone—August felt the corner of her mouth twitch. "You were robbing a fishwife when I caught you."

"A *guard captain's wife*, actually. I'd been casing her for an hour. She had a purse like a swollen tick." Isolde's chin lifted. "I was good at it. I got better fast. Hunger is an excellent tutor."

"It is." August crouched by the cache, unwrapped a brick of dried figs, and tossed it over. Isolde caught it one-handed and ate with the unselfconscious ferocity of the truly starving, and August turned away to give her that privacy, feeding the lamp instead, watching the flame stand perfectly upright in the dead air.

She should send her away. That was the rule. Never be seen, never be owed, never tie yourself to anything that bleeds. A fugitive princess was a bonfire in a dry field; every inquisitor in the borderlands would smell the smoke within the week. The smart move—the survival move, the move ten years of discipline demanded—was to point Isolde at the mountain roads, give her a day's head start, and vanish.

But the courier had broken the rule first. He had bled on her and made her owed.

"Your father," August said, without turning around. "When he talks to the crown. What does he say? Do you remember the words?"

Isolde was quiet long enough that August glanced back. The princess had gone pale in the lamplight, the figs forgotten.

"He says *not yet*. Over and over. 'Not yet, not yet, I am not finished.'" Isolde hugged her arms around herself. "And once—only once—I heard him say something else. Clear as church bells. He said, '*It is not a cage if you built the bars yourself.*'" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "What does that mean? What is happening to him? Because I went to every physician, every priest, every hedge-witch who'd see me, and they all said grief, madness, age—and none of them, not one, would meet my eyes while they said it. As if they were reciting something they'd been *told* to say."

The lamp flame leaned. Just slightly. Toward Isolde, or toward August—or toward the waxed bundle against August's ribs, which had gone from warm to hot, a coal of attention through linen and cloth.

August's mouth had gone dry. *It is not a cage if you built the bars yourself.* She thought of a drowned vault beneath Thornfell, of ink older than kingdoms, of a binding signed in blood by hands that might have been her own great-grandmother's. She thought of a realm that had not known a true famine, a plague, a war of succession, in a hundred years—and of the price of such peace, written somewhere in salt.

"Sit down," August said.

"I am sitting."

"Then sit *still*." She lowered herself onto the opposite barrel, knees almost touching Isolde's, and made herself meet those furious, frightened, royal eyes. "I'm going to tell you something, and you're going to think I'm mad, and then you're going to realize I'm the first person in seven months who isn't lying to you. Can you bear that?"

Isolde laughed again, that broken sound. "I've borne worse. Recently I ate a boot heel."

"Chewier than figs?"

"Slightly." But she braced, and waited.

So August told her. Not everything—never everything, the rule still breathed in her even now—but the ford, the courier, the seals, the words *the crown is not the king*. And then, because Isolde's father was the man being eaten and silence would have been a cruelty, she told her about the map: the geography that couldn't exist, the drowned vault, the way it answered sorrow. She did not touch the bundle as she spoke. She didn't dare.

Isolde listened the way she must have listened to ambassadors—with total, unnerving attention, missing nothing—and when August finished, the princess was silent for a long moment.

"My father is bound," she said at last. "To something. And that something eats—" her voice caught, steadied— "it eats what people feel when they lose things. And it gives back... order. Peace. A hundred years of nothing going wrong." She looked at her own hands, the rope-burned, blistered hands of a runaway. "Everyone always said his reign was blessed. *The Still King*, they call him in the provinces. Nothing burns, nothing floods, nothing changes." Her laugh now was worse than any of the others. "Nothing changes. God. We thought it was virtue."

"It's appetite," August said quietly. "Dressed as virtue."

"And the courier gave the proof to *you*. A stranger. Why?"

"Because I was there." August heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't soften it. "Because I was invisible and he was dying and I was the only person in the world who could carry a secret safely. Congratulations, Highness. You're now the second."

Isolde studied her for a long moment. The lamplight moved; the flame had gone strange again, leaning, straining, and August realized distantly that it was leaning toward the map, toward the heat of it, like the candles in the king's chamber bending toward the crown.

"You hate this," Isolde said. "Carrying it."

"With my whole life."

"But you'll do it anyway." It wasn't a question. "Because someone has to, and you've apparently decided that someone is you, and you resent every second of it." Isolde picked up the last fig, considered it, and set it down with deliberate care—an odd, formal gesture, a courtier's gesture, repurposed. "Good. I don't trust volunteers. Volunteers want things. Resentful duty is the only honest kind."

"That's a terrible philosophy."

"It's the philosophy that kept my father's kingdom standing for a century, so it can hardly be worthless." Isolde rose, wincing, and stood there in her stolen boy's coat with her stolen locket and her unbearable spine-straight posture, and August saw it clearly for the first time: the princess wasn't running *from* the court. She'd been running toward something, blindly, the way a compass needle runs, and she'd simply had no name for north until tonight. "You're going to Thornfell. To the vault. Don't insult us both by denying it—you checked the mountain passes before you ever entered that square, I watched you do the arithmetic on your fingers."

August said nothing. Denying it would, as Isolde said, insult them both.

"Take me with you."

"Absolutely not."

"He is my *father*." The fury flared again, and this time it lit the whole cellar, and August understood that she was looking at the most dangerous thing in Marrowgate: love with nowhere to go. "Whatever wears him, whatever eats him—I am the one person in this realm who has a reason to reach him that isn't politics. You want to unbind your sigil, break your cage, whatever it is. Fine. But someone has to reach the man inside it, and I am telling you, August Coldharbour—" and August went cold, because she had never given her name, not once, not in the square, not in the cellar— "that no one else alive can do it."

"How do you know my name?"

Isolde smiled, and it was the first smile August had seen from her that wasn't broken, and it was terrifying. "You're not the only one who watches rooftops. There are people in the borderlands who remember the village of Coldharbour. Who remember *why* it burned. They talk about you the way sailors talk about a lucky wind. I've been trying to find you for three weeks, August. The pickpocketing was not an accident."

The lamp guttered. Against August's ribs, the map pulsed once, slow and satisfied, like a heart that had finally gotten what it wanted.

Outside, far off across the flats, a bell began to ring—the garrison alarm, one stroke, two, three, the rhythm that meant *riders on the road, search the town*. And beneath the bell, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the north gate, came a sound August had hoped never to hear again so soon: the long, thin cry of inquisitor horns, sweeping Marrowgate street by street, closing like a hand.
