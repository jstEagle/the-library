## 7. Roads That Remember

The road out of Greyhollow was called the Widow's Spine, and August had walked it eleven times in ten years without once seeing it do anything stranger than crumble.

She was already regretting that confidence by the second hour.

The Spine ran along a ridge of black stone above the treeline, narrow as a coffin lid, and it had one virtue: it could not be seen from the valley below. Crown patrols took the river road. Smugglers took the Spine. For a decade the arrangement had held like a law of nature—until this morning, when August crested the first shoulder of the ridge and found the road bending left where it had always bent right.

She stopped so abruptly that Isolde walked into her back.

"Problem?" the princess asked, in the flat drawl she'd adopted along with her stolen coat. Three days out of Greyhollow and she still hadn't learned to walk quietly; her boots struck the stone like a drummer announcing them to every ear in the mountains.

"Maybe." August crouched, laid her palm flat on the rock. It was warm. Ridge-stone in autumn should have been cold enough to bite. "Or maybe I'm tired."

"You're never tired," Isolde said. "You told us. Twice."

Behind them, unhurried, came the tap-tap-tap of Maren Voss's salt-wood staff. The witch climbed the way water poured downhill—inevitably, without visible effort—and when she reached them she did not ask what the problem was. She simply looked at the bend in the road for a long moment, then spat over the edge.

"That's not where it goes," she said.

"No," August agreed. "It isn't."

"Roads don't move."

"This one has."

Maren's eyes were pale as skimmed milk, and they fixed on August with an expression that might have been interest or might have been hunger. "Then either you've been lying about knowing these hills, cartographer's girl, or something down there is *editing* them."

August looked at the valley. Far below, half-hidden in morning haze, the river road wound silver between the pines—the road the crown used, the road that led, eventually, toward Bellhaven and the lowland towns whose names had begun appearing on the bloodstained map with small annotations in no hand she recognized. *Weeping here. Weeping here. Weeping most of all.*

The map rode against her ribs in its oilcloth wrapping, and she felt it now the way she'd felt it every day since Greyhollow: a faint warmth, a patient weight, like a sleeping animal pressed to her chest.

"We keep moving," she said. "Left fork. Carefully."

* * *

By midday the road had betrayed her three more times.

It wasn't dramatic. That was what made it terrible. A switchback that shouldn't exist appeared exactly where a switchback would be convenient. A stretch of scree they'd have had to climb on hands and knees resolved instead into smooth flagstones, old ones, worn in the middle by centuries of feet that had never walked there. Once, rounding a shoulder of granite, August looked back the way they'd come and saw their own footprints ending in bare stone twenty paces behind them, erased as neatly as if a hand had dragged a wet cloth across a slate.

Isolde noticed at the same moment. She went very still, and for once said nothing at all, which from her was louder than screaming.

"It's following grief," Maren said. She had stopped walking entirely and stood with her staff planted, head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear. "Geography remembers what happened on it. Every road is a scar. Most scars stay quiet." Her milky eyes slid to August. "But if something starts *feeding* on sorrow—if it grows fat on it—it stops being content to live in people. It starts living in the land. And the land begins to rearrange itself around its appetite."

"The Salt King," Isolde said.

"The Salt King," Maren agreed. "And we are walking straight through his pantry."

August unslung her pack and sat down on the impossible flagstones, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her. "Then tell me how to fight it. You promised in Greyhollow you'd teach me sigil-craft. Teach me now."

"Now? On the moving road?"

"There's no better time. If the ground can change under my feet, I want to know what else can."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years—and lowered herself onto the stone across from August with the groan of a woman twice her age. "Fine. First lesson. Sigils aren't magic."

Isolde snorted. "Wonderful. The witch says the witchcraft isn't real."

"They're *grammar*, girl." Maren rapped her staff on the flagstone. "Magic is the language. Sigils are how you write it down so it stays written. Every binding, every ward, every curse your grandmother ever muttered over a fevered child—that's grammar. Subject. Verb. Object. You want a ward? *This stone shall not pass fire.* Subject, verb, object, sealed with intent. You want a curse? Same thing, meaner verb." She pulled a stub of chalk-white mineral from her coat—salt compressed and dried into something hard enough to mark stone—and drew a single line on the flagstone. "That's a stroke. The simplest unit. One stroke means *boundary*. Put two together—" she added a crossing line, "—and you get *division*. Three, arranged thus—" a third stroke, angled, "—and you have *severance*. See how meaning compounds?"

August leaned forward despite herself. Ten years of reading maps had taught her that symbols were never innocent; a contour line was a promise about the shape of the world, and a border was a threat dressed in ink. But this was different. This was writing that *did* things.

"And the Salt King's sigil?" she asked. "The one on the map. What's its grammar?"

Maren's rusted laugh died. She looked out at the valley, at the silver thread of the river road, and her voice dropped low. "*All grief flows to me.* That's the whole sentence. Subject, verb, object. Elegant. Horrible. Whoever wrote it made themselves the object of an entire kingdom's mourning, and the kingdom has been paying the toll ever since." She turned back. "Which brings us to lesson two. Intent seals the sigil. Grammar without intent is just graffiti. So before you ever draw a stroke, you must know—not hope, not wish, *know*—what you're willing to pay for it."

"Pay with what?"

"What do you think, smuggler? Nothing in this world moves for free. Not roads, not rivers, not grief." Maren tapped her own chest, once, over the heart. "Intent is paid in the self."

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang that August did not remember existing, though she conceded privately that her memory had lost all authority on this road. Maren chalked a boundary-sigil in a ring around their fire—two strokes, division, *this circle shall not be crossed by what serves the crown or the salt*—and the flames burned steadier inside it than any fire August had ever built.

They ate hard bread and harder cheese. Isolde produced, from some hidden pocket, a flask of plum brandy that she claimed to have bought and everyone knew she'd stolen, and passed it around with the solemnity of a coronation.

"To the moving road," she said. "May it move us somewhere with beds."

"To grammar," Maren said, and drank.

August took the flask last. The brandy burned pleasantly, and the fire crackled, and for a moment—just a moment—she let herself feel the shape of the thing she was part of. Three women on a ridge that couldn't decide where it was, sharing stolen liquor under a boundary drawn in salt. It was absurd. It was the closest thing to family she'd had since the crown put her village to the torch, and the thought frightened her more than the shifting road did, because things you belonged to could be taken away, and things you loved were hostages waiting to be claimed.

"So," Isolde said, into the silence, with the delicacy of someone walking into a room full of glassware. "August. The rule. Never be seen, never be owed. Where does that leave us? Because I owe you. You pulled me out of that gutter in Harrowgate when you could have handed me to the constable and collected whatever bounty follows a runaway princess."

"Bounty's probably better than what I'm getting now," August said.

"I'm serious."

"I know you're serious. You're always serious. It's exhausting." August turned the flask in her ink-stained fingers. "The rule exists because debts are leashes. Someone owes you, they resent you. You owe them, they own you. Invisible and unindebted, nobody can pull your strings." She looked up. "But you're right. It doesn't leave room for this." She gestured at the fire, the circle, the three of them. "So maybe the rule needs amending."

"Amending how?"

"Never be seen by people who'd use it. Never be owed by anyone who'd collect." She passed the flask back. "You two don't qualify."

Isolde blinked. Maren made the rusted sound again, deeper this time, almost warm. "Ten years alone and she cracks in three days," the witch said. "Grief makes strange mortar."

"Grief is the only mortar," Maren added, before anyone could laugh. "Didn't I say? The map opens to tears. The land reshapes around mourning. Everything in this business runs on sorrow—it's the realm's true currency, and the Salt King holds the mint." Her milky gaze moved from face to face. "Which is why he'll come for us. Not with soldiers. With our own dead. He'll show each of us exactly what we've lost, and he'll make it look so kind, and he'll wait for us to walk toward it."

The fire popped. Somewhere far down the valley, thin and carried on the wind, came a sound that might have been singing.

Nobody reached for the brandy again after that.

* * *

The terror arrived properly an hour past midnight.

August woke to Isolde shaking her shoulder, and for one disoriented heartbeat she didn't understand what she was seeing: the hollow, the banked fire, the boundary-ring—and beyond it, filling the entire mouth of the hollow, standing water. Black, mirror-flat, stretching back into the dark where there should have been nothing but ridge and sky. They were on a mountaintop. There was no water. There had never been water.

In its surface, faces.

Not reflections. Faces looking *up* through the water as though it were the underside of a lake and they were drowned beneath it, mouths open, eyes wide, and among them—August's chest seized—she knew some of them. The miller's daughter from Coldharbour. Old Tam with his crooked smile. People who had died screaming when the king's soldiers came, now floating in silent procession across a mountain lake that could not exist.

"Don't look at them long," Maren whispered. She was already on her feet, staff raised, chalk in her other hand. "It's not them. It's him wearing them. He's showing us the menu."

The water began to rise. Silently. Without sloshing. It climbed the walls of the hollow like something pouring upward, and the faces rose with it, and the nearest one—Tam, Tam who had taught her to fish, Tam whose skull had been broken on his own doorstep—opened its mouth, and the voice that came out was gentle as a lullaby.

"*August.* You carry my map so carefully. Come closer. Let me see what you've lost. Let me hold it for you. I hold everything. No one need grieve alone, ever again—"

"*No.*" Maren drove her staff into the ground and began to chalk, fast, strokes flying—three, five, seven, severance upon severance, her lips moving through the grammar at speed. "Circle holds, circle holds, *circle holds*—"

The water touched the boundary and recoiled as if scalded. The faces pressed closer. Tam's gentle voice cracked, curdled, became something vast and briny and furious underneath: "*You will all grieve. You will all flow to me. The sentence is already written—*"

"Then I'm editing it," August snarled, and the words tore out of her with a force that surprised her, with ten years of ash behind them. "You hear me? Whatever you are—whatever he signed—you don't get my grief. You don't get *anyone's*. It's ours. It's the only thing they couldn't burn."

The water shuddered. For a moment every face turned to look at her at once, dozens of drowned eyes, and August felt the Salt King's attention settle on her like a hand on the back of her neck—vast, cold, and horribly, genuinely curious.

Then Maren finished the last stroke, spoke a word that tasted like iron, and slammed her palm against the seal. The boundary flared white. The water collapsed—not drained, *collapsed*, folding inward and downward and gone, leaving only wet stone and the smell of low tide on a mountaintop.

Silence. The fire had gone out. The stars were wrong overhead—or perhaps the road had moved them again while they slept—but they were bright, and ordinary, and August clung to their ordinariness like a rope.

"Well," Isolde said at last, her voice shaking only slightly. "I take back everything I said about your lessons being boring, Maren."

"Lesson three," Maren said hoarsely, sinking down beside the dead fire. "Draw your boundaries *before* you need them."

August didn't sit. She stood at the edge of the hollow, looking north, where the road descended toward the lowlands—and where, in the grey pre-dawn, she could now see what the shifting geography had been hiding from her. The ridge bent, impossibly, gently, like a current drawing a boat toward a whirlpool. Every switchback, every convenient flagstone, every impossible bend had been steering them the same direction all along.

Toward Bellhaven. Toward the place on the map marked, in that hand no one owned, *weeping most of all*.

"He wants us to go there," she said quietly. Behind her, Maren and Isolde came to stand at her shoulders, and she felt them there, solid and warm and hers. "He's been paving the road for us."

"Then we go," Isolde said. "Carefully."

"We go," August agreed. "Off-road. My way." She touched the oilcloth bundle over her heart, and it pulsed once against her palm, eager, like a thing that had been waiting a very long time to come home.
