## 8. The Burning of Bellhaven

Isolde had learned to read a road by its silences, and the silence around Bellhaven was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

For three days they had traveled the lowland track out of the Thornfell foothills, August leading them along routes that existed on no map Isolde had ever seen in her father's library—and she had seen all of them, once, before she stopped being allowed into the map room. The smuggler roads bent and swayed like river reeds. Twice Maren had pressed salt to the earth and muttered until the ground remembered which way it was supposed to lie. Isolde was learning to sleep with one ear open, to eat standing up, to trust a woman who trusted no one. She was learning, in short, to be someone else entirely. The name she wore now was *Wren*, a tanner's daughter from the east marches, and she practiced it the way she had once practiced courtly verse: until it sat naturally on the tongue.

But nothing in her education—not the tutors, not the etiquette masters, not the years of watching her father's court smile at itself—had prepared her for what the road gave them on the fourth morning.

They smelled it first. Not smoke, though there was smoke, thin and white as breath on glass. It was something sweeter underneath, mineral and wrong, like the air inside a crypt that has been sealed too long. August stopped so abruptly that Isolde walked into her back.

"What is it?" Isolde whispered.

August didn't answer. She was looking down the long green shoulder of the valley where Bellhaven sat beside its river, and her face—the face Isolde had spent weeks learning to read, all its guardedness and dry humor—had gone carefully, terribly blank.

"Gods below," Maren said softly. "It's already here."

* * *

From the ridge above the village, Bellhaven looked as though winter had fallen on a single place and nowhere else.

Every rooftop, every fence post, every field stone wore a crust of white. But it was not snow. Snow lay soft; this lay *hard*, crystalline, catching the morning light and throwing it back in facets so bright they hurt to look at. It climbed the walls of the cottages like ivy made of frost and glass. It sheathed the well. It furred the branches of the orchard trees, and where it grew thickest—around the village square, around the small stone church with its crown-topped spire—it had built structures, spires and buttresses of translucent crystal, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful.

And through it all ran the people.

Isolde counted eleven before she stopped counting. They moved slowly between the crystal growths, barefoot in the frost, their clothes stiff with salt bloom. Their eyes were open but nothing behind them was home. And they were singing.

The sound drifted up the ridge, thin and sweet and perfectly in tune, dozens of voices braided into one. Isolde knew the melody. Of course she knew it. It was the Coronation Hymn, the one sung at her father's crowning, the one every child in the realm learned before they learned their letters. *Blessed be the crown, blessed be the hand that holds it.* She had sung it herself at seven years old, standing on a dais in white silk while ten thousand subjects cheered.

Here, sung by hollow-eyed farmers in a dead village, it sounded like drowning.

"We should go around," Maren said. There was no judgment in it, only practicality, and Isolde loved her a little for offering the escape. "Whatever did this is still active. We can't help these people."

"We're not helping anyone by leaving," Isolde heard herself say.

Her voice came out strange—flat, royal, the voice she hadn't used since she'd fled the palace with stolen coins in her boots. August turned to look at her, and Isolde saw the question in her face: *who are you right now?*

Isolde didn't know. She only knew that the singing would not stop, and that somewhere beneath the hymn she could hear words being worn smooth, like stones in a current: *blessed be, blessed be, blessed be.*

"My father's reign feeds on grief," she said. "That's what you told me. That's what the map says." Her hands had closed into fists without her permission. "I've heard those words for weeks now. I've nodded along. I've *believed* them, I think. But I have never once looked at them."

She started down the ridge.

* * *

Up close, the crystals sang too.

That was the horror Isolde could not escape, walking the frozen lanes of Bellhaven: the growths themselves were part of the choir. Each spire hummed faintly when the wind moved across it, harmonizing with the villagers, and the combined sound settled into Isolde's chest like water filling a boat. She kept touching her own sternum to make sure her heart was still beating on its own schedule.

A woman stood at a cottage door, her hand resting flat against a crystal sheet that had grown over the window. Inside, visible through the cloudy translucence, a bed. A shape in the bed. Small. Child-sized.

"Goodwife," Isolde said gently. "Can you hear me?"

The woman turned. Her face was weathered and kind and utterly vacant, and when she smiled Isolde felt tears startle hot behind her eyes, because the smile was genuine. Whatever lived in this woman now, it was happy.

"He's coming," the woman said. Her voice was rusted from disuse, or from singing. "He's been listening so long, and now he's nearly ready to come down and hold us. He hears everything, you know. Every sorrow. He keeps them all safe for us." She patted the crystal over the window, tenderly, the way one pats a sleeping child's door. "Our griefs. He keeps them safe. We don't have to carry them anymore."

"Who keeps them?" Isolde asked, though she knew. She had known since the ford, since the courier's blood on August's hands, since the night she had stood outside her father's study and heard him speaking to his crown in a voice that was and was not his own. *The crown is not the king.* Who had said that? The dying man. Who had believed it? No one. Not yet.

"The king," the woman said simply, and smiled again, and turned back to her window, and resumed singing.

Isolde stood in the lane with salt dust settling on her shoulders like a benediction, and understood, finally and completely, what her father's power was made of.

Not taxes. Not armies. Not law, though Hale had once told her, in his earnest, infuriating way, that law was the skeleton of mercy. It was made of *this*: a woman who did not have to grieve her dying child because something else carried it for her. A village that did not have to feel the famine year or the fever season or the sons lost to conscription, because the Salt King gathered every sorrow like a tithe and left only sweetness, only obedience, only song.

And the price—

The price was that the woman at the window was no longer a woman. The price was that her child was dying alone behind crystal, unheld, because holding required a self, and hers had been paid away. The price was eleven singers in a square and none of them home.

*I have eaten from his table,* Isolde thought. *All my life. Silk and sweetmeats and safety, and every plate was paid for with someone's grief.*

* * *

She found August at the well.

The smuggler had gone still in the way she went still before violence or revelation, one hand resting on the crystal rim, her head bowed. Up close Isolde could see that the white crust had grown in patterns—fine lines, branching, deliberate. Sigils. Hundreds of them, tiny as stitching, covering every surface of the village like lacework.

"It's a harvest site," August said quietly, without looking up. "I've seen the edges of this before. Smugglers talk about white villages the way sailors talk about whirlpools. You give them a wide berth and you don't ask questions." She lifted her hand from the well. Where her palm had touched, the crystal had brightened, just slightly. Feeding. "This isn't an edge anymore. This is deep in the middle of something."

"Maren said we couldn't help them."

"Maren's right." August finally looked at her, and there was something raw under her usual iron. "You can't cut grief out of a person, Isolde. If we break the crystals, if we drag these people out of the song—they'll feel everything they've been spared. All of it, at once, with no practice carrying it. Some of them will die of it. The ones whose children are behind those windows—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I've seen men survive a blade and not survive a memory."

"Then what do we do?" Isolde's voice cracked on it. "We just walk away? We let my father's—" She couldn't say *god*. It wasn't a god. It was worse than a god; gods at least asked for worship freely given. "We let *it* keep them?"

"For now." August said it like pulling teeth. "For now, yes. Because the thing that saves them isn't a sword, it's the vault. It's the unbinding. And we don't get there if we die in this square trying to sing eleven people awake."

Isolde turned away. She was crying now, properly, ugly, the way she hadn't cried since the night she left the palace, and she didn't care who saw. Let Wren the tanner's daughter weep. Someone in this wretched, salted, singing village ought to.

"I used to think my father was a good king," she said. "I mean it. Even after he stopped knowing me—even then, I thought, the realm is fed, the roads are safe, whatever costs him, it costs *him*. He carries it, not us. That was the story. The king bears the burden." She laughed, wet and awful. "Do you know what the burden actually is? Us. Our grief. He doesn't carry it *for* us, he carries it *away from* us, and what's left behind is—" she gestured at the lane, at the singers, at the crystal lacework creeping up the church spire toward the crown carved at its top, "—this. This is what's left behind."

August said nothing for a long moment. Then, awkwardly, with the gracelessness of a woman who had not comforted another living soul in a decade, she put a hand on Isolde's shoulder.

"When we unbind it," August said, "these people wake up. All of them. And it will be the worst day of their lives, and they'll survive it, because that's what people do when someone stands with them instead of above them." Her grip tightened. "Your father took the burden so no one would stand with anyone. That's the whole trick. That's the whole rotten architecture. What we're building is the opposite."

Isolde wiped her face with her sleeve, leaving a streak of salt damp across her cheek, and almost laughed at the symmetry.

"You sound like you believe that."

"I'm a smuggler," August said. "Believing things is how I stay alive."

* * *

They found the child last.

Maren had gone ahead toward the church, checking the sigil-work, cataloguing the growth patterns with a scholar's grim efficiency—and it was Maren's sharp intake of breath that drew them, and it was the silence that stopped them at the door. One voice missing from the choir. The smallest one.

Inside, the church was a garden of glass. Crystal pillars rose between the pews, and light fell through them in rainbow shards, and it would have been holy if holiness had ever looked like this. At the altar, kneeling before the crown-topped spire, was a boy of perhaps eight years, his small body half-sheathed in white bloom, singing the Coronation Hymn in a clear, unwavering voice.

His mother was not among the villagers outside. Isolde understood, with a lurch, that the shape in the bed behind the crystal window had not been the whole story. Somewhere in this village was a grave with no marker, and a grief so large it had called the Salt King's attention like blood calls sharks.

"No," Isolde whispered.

The boy turned his head. His eyes were the worst thing yet—not vacant like the others, but *full*, brimming, wet, aware, and smiling through it. A child's face wearing an adult serenity that had no business existing.

"Don't be sad," he said. His voice was his own and also not, layered, resonant, a cistern-deep echo under a boyish treble. "He showed me her. Mama. He keeps her safe too. Everyone's sad is kept safe. When the king comes down, no one will ever have to be sad again." He beamed at them, radiant, terrible. "You can be sad. I can see it on you. You're carrying so much. Give it to him. He wants it. He *loves* it."

"Isolde." Maren's hand closed hard around her arm. "Don't answer it. It's not the boy talking."

But Isolde was already stepping forward, shrugging off the witch's grip, dropping to her knees on the crystal-dusted floor so that her eyes were level with the child's. Close enough to see the salt bloom creeping up his neck like a collar. Close enough to see that beneath the serenity, in the very back of his eyes, something small and human was watching her. Screaming, maybe. Or maybe she only needed it to be.

"I know who you are," she said to the thing behind his eyes. "I grew up in your house."

The singing in the square faltered—one beat, two—then resumed.

"You wear my father's crown," Isolde continued, and her voice did not shake, and she marveled at that even as terror poured cold down her spine. "You sit in his chair. You eat his griefs and call it mercy. So look at me. Look at what you make of children. Look at what your kingdom costs, and tell me again that it's kindness."

The boy's smile widened. Crystals chimed softly throughout the church, a sound like distant bells, like approval.

"He told me about you," the boy said, and the echo in his voice deepened, and Isolde's blood went to ice. "The daughter who ran away. He's sad about you. He keeps your sadness safe too, princess. He keeps it in a special place. He says—" the boy tilted his head, listening to something vast and patient and hungry, "—he says you'll come home soon. He says everyone comes home."

Then the boy closed his mouth, and turned back to the altar, and resumed his hymn, and whatever awareness had flickered in the depths of his eyes was gone, drowned, kept safe.

* * *

They did not speak until the ridge had swallowed Bellhaven behind them.

Even then, Maren spoke first, and her voice was stripped of all its usual dryness. "It knows where we are. It knew *her*." She glanced at Isolde, apologetic and afraid in equal measure. "That wasn't a village anymore, girl. That was a mouth. And we walked into it and told it our names."

"Then we move faster," August said. Her stride had lengthened; the smuggler was fully awake now, every sense aimed forward. "No more lowland tracks. We go high, we go cold, we go where nothing sings. Vault first. Everything else second."

Isolde walked between them and said nothing at all. The boy's voice followed her, lodged under her ribs like a splinter. *He keeps your sadness safe too, princess.*

Because that was the unbearable truth she could not say aloud: some traitorous chamber of her heart had wanted to say yes. Standing in that crystal church, hearing grief described as a weight that could simply be set down—she had *wanted* it. She missed her father. She was tired of running, tired of salt in her boots and lies in her mouth, tired of being a princess with no throne and a daughter with no father. The Salt King had offered rest, and some starving part of her had leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

If it could seduce her in five minutes, what had ten years done to the man who wore the crown?

That night, camped in the high passes where the wind scoured away all sound, Isolde sat her watch and watched the dark valley below, waiting for any glimmer of white. And it was there, in the coldest hour, that she saw it: far to the south, down in the lowlands, a line of torches moving with military precision along the road they had abandoned. Too many torches. Too orderly.

Soldiers.

And riding at the head of the column, a figure whose pale cloak caught the moonlight like a banner—inquisitor's white, hunting white, the color of the law coming up the mountain after them.

Beside her, August rose silently from her bedroll, followed her gaze, and said, very quietly:

"They found the village."
