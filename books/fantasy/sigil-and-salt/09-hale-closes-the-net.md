## 9. Hale Closes the Net

The interrogation room in Fort Marrow smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and Inquisitor Corwin Hale had learned, over eleven years of service to the Crown's justice, that the smell was the most honest thing about it.

He sat across a scarred oak table from the smuggler they'd pulled out of the Bellhaven road two days past—a wiry man with frostbitten ears and the flat, patient eyes of someone who had spent his whole life being underestimated. The man's name, according to the garrison ledger, was Tam Wicker. According to Tam Wicker himself, it was nothing at all, because he hadn't spoken since they'd brought him in.

Hale did not raise his voice. He never raised his voice. He had learned early—first as a garrison clerk in the lowlands, later under old Inquisitor Dree at the southern assizes—that volume was what men used when they had run out of patience and wanted everyone else to pay for it. Patience, properly kept, was a weapon with a longer reach than any blade.

"You're cold," Hale said now, setting a tin cup of broth on the table between them. Not pushing it toward him. Just setting it down, equidistant, so that taking it would be the prisoner's own decision. "The cells on the east side have a draft off the mountain. The ones on the west face the kitchens and stay warm through the night. I can move you. It costs you nothing but a conversation."

Wicker looked at the broth. Looked at him. Said nothing.

"Your companions talked," Hale said, which was true. Two of the four had talked within an hour of capture, the way most men did—not from weakness, but from the simple human terror of being alone inside a story no one else could hear. "They told me about the caravan routes. They told me about the tolls. What they couldn't tell me—and this is the interesting part, Master Wicker—is who guides you through the passes. Because your own people wouldn't say her name. Do you understand how unusual that is? Men who break in an hour over warm bread will not give up one name. That name is worth more to them than their comfort. I want to know why."

Something moved behind Wicker's eyes. Not fear. Hale catalogued it carefully: recognition, maybe, or the particular stubborn pride of people who had decided long ago that the Crown was a weather system rather than a government—something that happened *to* you, that you survived by keeping your head down and your business quiet.

"I'll tell you what I know," Hale continued, unfolding a sheet of paper though he needed no notes; he never needed notes. "Four days ago, a royal courier was found dead at the Greywater ford. Throat cut by inquisitorial steel—I've confirmed the blade pattern myself, which means one of my own colleagues killed him, or wants us to believe so. Before he died, he was seen speaking to a woman. A traveler. Alone. The ford keeper's daughter saw her from the reed bank: tall, hooded, moved like someone who knew the ground before she stepped on it. Within the hour, three of my riders were dead in the shallows and the woman was gone."

He let the silence do its work. Silence, in Hale's experience, was the only interrogator more patient than he was.

"The courier carried something," he said. "A map, we believe. And whoever holds that map is moving north through Thornfell right now, and every road she takes she knows better than the men I send down them. I have lost nine soldiers in these mountains in six days. Nine. Good men, most of them conscripted from farms that needed them, dying in fog for a piece of vellum." He leaned forward slightly. "I don't enjoy that arithmetic, Master Wicker. Help me end it."

Wicker finally reached for the broth. He drank half of it in slow, deliberate swallows, set the cup down, and said, in a voice like gravel settling:

"She saved my brother's leg on the Kestrel switchbacks. Ice took him, four winters back. Carried him six miles down a goat track in the dark, splinted it with a cart axle, never told us her name. Just said, *don't be seen, don't be owed.*" He shrugged. "You want me to sell that? Sell it to *you*?"

"No," Hale said honestly. "I want you to sell me the roads. Names are loyalty. Roads are just geography." He slid a fresh map of Thornfell across the table—the official survey, twenty years out of date, its passes marked in confident red ink that he had long since stopped trusting. "Show me where she'd go. If you were her. If you had the king's own seal in your pocket and my riders at your back."

Wicker studied the map for a long moment. Then, with the air of a man doing something mildly treasonous and finding it less painful than expected, he tapped one cracked fingernail against the paper.

"Not the Widow's Pass. Too obvious, too watched. Not the low road—she'd never take a road with a name." His finger drifted north, into the blank white space where the official survey simply gave up, marked only with the cartographer's faint apology: *elevation uncertain.* "Here. The old salt-roads. They're not on any chart because the charts were drawn by men who asked permission to enter. She doesn't ask permission." He glanced up, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "You can't catch her, Inquisitor. You can only be somewhere before she decides to be."

Hale copied the route onto his own notes in small, precise script. "And if I put a hundred men along the salt-roads?"

"She'd smell them a week out. She reads ground the way priests read scripture." Wicker sat back. "But—" and here he hesitated, and Hale saw the crack open, the small betrayal that every prisoner eventually commits against the people who trusted him, not from malice but from the unbearable weight of being the one who knows. "There's only one way through the high country she can't avoid. The Weeping Stair. It's a chokepoint—single file, cliff on one side, drop on the other, and the rock weeps water year-round so there's no dry footing to wait on. If she's going where I think she's going, she has to pass it. Everyone going deep into Thornfell has to pass it."

"Why would she be going deep?"

Wicker's mouth twisted. "Because of what's happening to Bellhaven," he said. "You've seen it. We all have. And folk are starting to say things they didn't used to say. Things about the crown." He met Hale's eyes, and for a moment the smuggler's caution dropped away entirely, leaving something raw underneath. "Things I'm not paid enough to repeat to a man wearing the king's silver."

* * *

That evening, Hale stood in the fort's map room with the official survey spread before him and Wicker's corrections inked in his own hand, and he felt the familiar architecture of certainty shift beneath him by a fraction of a degree—so slight a movement that anyone watching would have seen nothing at all.

He had been an inquisitor for eleven years because he believed in the law the way some men believed in God: not because it was kind, but because it was the only thing standing between the realm and the appetite of the strong. He had grown up in a river town where the miller's sons took what they liked and the magistrate looked elsewhere, until a circuit judge came through and hanged two of them, and Corwin Hale, aged nine, had understood in one burning moment that order was mercy. That the alternative to law was not freedom. It was the miller's sons.

Everything he had done since—the long assizes, the confessions drawn out with patience instead of pain, the reputation for fairness that even condemned men acknowledged—had flowed from that single conviction. He did not torture. He did not frame. He caught the guilty and proved it, and the proof mattered more than the punishment, because a realm that punished without proof was just a larger version of the miller's yard.

So when Captain Vess of the garrison brought him the sealed orders that evening—wax bearing the privy signet, instructions in a clerk's hand so formal it might have been written by no one at all—he read them twice, expecting to find the loophole, the condition, the clause that made sense of them.

There was none.

*Upon locating the bearer of the stolen instrument, no expenditure of lives is to be spared in her destruction. Witnesses among the border settlements are likewise compromised. Purify as required.*

*Purify as required.*

He read it a third time. Vess stood at attention by the door, a broad, decent man with a farmer's shoulders and a soldier's unease poorly hidden behind regulation posture.

"Burn orders," Vess said quietly. "For witnesses. That's what it comes to, sir. There's a village full of grieving folk up at Bellhaven singing songs to a crown, and someone in the capital has decided that's contagion."

"People say foolish things when they grieve," Hale said. "It isn't treason. Grief isn't treason."

"No," Vess agreed. "But the order doesn't distinguish, does it?" He shifted his weight. "I've got two hundred men requisitioned for your sweep, per the warrant. Half of them are border-born. Some of them have cousins in Bellhaven." He paused, choosing his next words with visible care. "I've served thirty years, Inquisitor. I've burned granaries in wartime and called it strategy. But this isn't war, and those aren't granaries. So I'll ask you plain, because you've got a name for asking plain: when we find this woman—if she's everything the reports say, a guide who's never harmed a soul beyond evading us—are we destroying her because she's guilty? Or because she's *inconvenient*?"

Hale did not answer immediately. He looked down at the map, at the red thread he had drawn through the salt-roads toward the Weeping Stair, the net closing around a woman whose only crime, so far as the evidence showed, was holding a map that powerful people were willing to kill for.

Which raised the question he had been circling for six days and refusing to land on: what was *on* the map? A courier carrying royal seals, murdered—apparently—by royal agents. A chase conducted with a secrecy so total that Hale's own superiors communicated in euphemism. *Purify as required.* In eleven years he had read a thousand warrants, and honest power wrote plainly. Only frightened power wrote like this.

"The law says she's guilty of theft and obstruction," Hale said at last. "The law doesn't care why the crown wants her dead."

"No," Vess said. "But you do. That's why they sent you, I expect. A man who'll find her." He straightened. "Orders are orders, sir. When do we march?"

"At first light," Hale said. "Two hundred men. We hold the Stair and the low approaches. And Captain—" he caught Vess at the door, and heard his own voice come out quieter than intended, "—if it comes to the village. Bellhaven. We do it by the book. Individual assessment, written record, no collective punishment. If the crown wants ash, the crown can write its name to every house."

Vess studied him for a moment. "And if the crown won't wait for the book?"

Hale turned back to the map. Below the Weeping Stair, in the blank white country where the survey gave up, his pen had left a small ink blot—an accident, a tremor, nothing.

"Then the crown and I will have a disagreement," he said, "and I have never yet lost one of those with the law on my side."

But that night, sleepless in the fort's cold guest quarters, he found himself thinking not of the fugitive woman but of the courier at the ford, and of the blade that killed him—inquisitorial steel, the pattern unmistakable. Which meant either that one of his colleagues had gone rogue, or that the rogue wore a chain of office and signed orders in euphemisms, and either possibility sat in Hale's chest like a swallowed stone.

Justice through law. It had held his whole life together the way mortar holds a wall. He had never once tested whether the wall was load-bearing, or merely decorative—whether the law served justice, or whether he had spent eleven years serving the law while justice waited outside in the cold, uninvited.

At dawn the column marched: two hundred men in the king's grey, banners furled, boots striking frost from the mountain road. Hale rode at the head with Wicker's corrections folded against his heart and the burn order sealed in his dispatch case like a thing he was ashamed to carry.

Behind them, far to the south, Bellhaven's white glow was visible even by day—a strange crystalline shimmer above the tree line, beautiful the way frost is beautiful, the way fire is beautiful, all the things that are lovely precisely because they consume.

Hale did not look at it. He looked north, toward the Stair, toward the net he had spent six days weaving, and told himself—as the column wound upward into the fog—that whatever waited for them in the high country, the law would be enough.

Somewhere below the cloud line, a woman who knew every stone in Thornfell was already reading their dust on the wind, and deciding, with the patience of her kind, exactly where she would let them find her—or where she would make certain they never did.
