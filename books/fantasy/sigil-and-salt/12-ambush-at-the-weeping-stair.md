## 12. Ambush at the Weeping Stair

The stairwell had been weeping for a thousand years, and August had learned to read its tears the way other people read weather.

Water ran down the walls in slow, mineral-bright threads, feeding the pale lichen that glowed faintly where Maren's salt-lamps passed. Each step was worn into a shallow bowl by feet that had climbed here before the kingdom had a name. August counted them out of habit—forty-one steps from the archive door to the first landing—and told herself the counting was calm. It was not. It was prayer, or something close to it.

Behind her, Isolde's breathing was too quick. Ahead, Maren carried the copied sigil-pages wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a sleeping child.

"Forty-two," August murmured. "Then the landing narrows. Single file until the cistern."

"You said that already," Isolde whispered.

"I say things twice when I want them believed twice."

The dark above them shifted.

August felt it before she heard it—a change in the air pressure, the way the mountain held its breath—and then came the sound she had spent ten years training herself to fear: boot-heels on stone, many of them, descending in disciplined rhythm. Not smugglers. Smugglers never marched in step.

"Lamp," she breathed.

Maren shuttered it to a coal. The lichen's glow remained, thin and greenish, enough to see each other's faces and nothing else. August pressed back against the wall, pulling Isolde with her, and put one hand on the old woman's shoulder: *down, stay down*.

They came around the landing in a column of six, then eight, then more—crown soldiers in grey-and-white, halberds angled inward, and behind them a lantern that threw light upward so their faces stayed shadowed. At the center walked a tall man in a long coat, unhurried, his boots finding the wet steps without slipping. He did not need to search the walls. He was reading them.

Corwin Hale.

August had seen him once before, across the ford, through reeds, at a distance that had let her pretend he was a story. Up close he was worse: not cruel, not theatrical, just *certain*, moving through the drowned dark of Thornfell as though order were something you could carry into any room.

He stopped three steps below them. The soldiers fanned out, and the stairwell became a throat closing.

"The archive is sealed by crown writ," Hale said. His voice echoed gently off the wet stone, almost kind. "Everything taken from it will be surrendered. No one needs to be hurt."

Maren laughed—low, dry, terrible. "No one *needs* to be hurt," she repeated. "That's what they sang in Greyhollow too, boy. Right up until the pyres."

"That was not my doing."

"It never is. That's the trick of it."

Hale's eyes found August in the lichen-light. She watched him take her in—the road-worn coat, the ink-stained fingers, the way she stood between him and the others without appearing to stand anywhere at all. Recognition moved through his face like water under ice.

"The cartographer," he said. "The one who knows every pass." He shook his head slowly, and there was something almost like grief in it. "Do you know how many men I've lost in these mountains looking for roads only you can walk? You could have saved them. You chose not to."

"I chose not to be *found*," August said. "There's a difference."

"There isn't. Not to the people who die because of what you keep hidden."

Isolde made a small sound beside her. August's hand found the princess's wrist and pressed: *still*. But Isolde had gone rigid in a way that had nothing to do with fear, and August understood, with a cold drop through her stomach, that the girl was looking at Hale the way one looks at a familiar face in an unfamiliar place.

She knew him. Of course she knew him. He was the king's inquisitor; he had probably dined in her father's hall.

"Hale," Isolde said, and her voice cracked the stairwell's tension like a struck bell. "Corwin Hale."

Every halberd turned toward her. Hale went very still.

"My lady?" he said, and the word cost him something visible. "You shouldn't be here."

"No," Isolde agreed. Her chin came up, and even soaked, exhausted, half-starved, she managed to look like the court portraits August had glimpsed on wanted-notices and shrine-walls alike. "I shouldn't. And yet my father's men are pointing weapons at me in a hole beneath his own mountain, so perhaps we should all reconsider what *should* means."

A soldier glanced at Hale. Another lowered his halberd an inch, uncertain. August watched the arithmetic happen behind the inquisitor's eyes—duty, doubt, the terrible weight of a face he could not fail to recognize—and she understood that Isolde had just spent the only coin she had left.

It nearly worked. It nearly bought them the moment they needed.

Then Hale said, quietly, "Show me your hands, my lady. Both of them."

"Why?"

"Because the Salt King's servants wear stolen faces, and grief makes us stupid. Because I have buried two men this month who swore oaths to women who looked exactly like the people they loved." His voice did not rise at all. "Show me your hands."

Isolde hesitated—and August saw why before Hale did. Ink. Days of copying sigils in the archive had left the princess's fingers stained black to the second knuckle, the mark of someone who had been handling forbidden texts, not fleeing a father.

Hale saw it. Something in his jaw tightened.

"You've been reading," he said. "You know what's down here. Then you know better than anyone what happens if that knowledge walks out of this mountain in the wrong hands." He stepped forward, onto the step below theirs, close enough now that August could smell lamp-oil and cold iron. "Give me the pages, Isolde. I'll take them to your father myself. Whatever's wrong with him—whatever the crown has become—I'll put it in front of him in words even a possessed king can't ignore. That's how this works. That's how it has *always* worked. Through law, through record, through proof laid on a table."

"And if the table is the thing that's broken?" August asked.

Hale looked at her. For a heartbeat—one long, dripping heartbeat—something flickered behind his certainty, some memory of a village he'd burned or a name he'd written in a ledger that should have stayed a person. Then it sealed over.

"Take them," he said. "Gently. All three."

The soldiers moved.

What followed happened in the space of four breaths, and August would remember it afterward as a series of fragments stitched together by terror.

Maren's hand came out of her coat holding a pinch of grey salt, and she spoke a word that was less a word than a *shape*, and the nearest soldier's lantern flame turned white and screamed. The man dropped it; burning oil spread across the wet steps in a sheet of blue fire that threw all their shadows huge against the weeping walls. A halberd swung at August; she caught the haft and used the soldier's own momentum to send him sprawling down five steps into another. Isolde—small, fast, court-trained in ways August had never asked about—drove her elbow into a throat and wrenched free.

But there were too many, and the stairwell was narrow, and Maren's fire was dying as fast as it spread. August felt hands close on her arms, her shoulders, wrenching the map-satchel's strap—

And then the mountain itself intervened.

The weeping walls began, all at once, to weep harder.

Water burst from the ancient channels—not a trickle but a flood, cold and sudden, as though the stairwell had been holding its breath for a millennium and had chosen this moment to exhale. The soldiers staggered. Torches hissed out. And from somewhere far above, down the throat of the stairwell, came a sound like a choir singing underwater: low, vast, patient.

The Salt King knew they were here. And whatever else the entity wanted, it did not want the pages going back up the mountain in crown hands.

Soldiers screamed and slipped. Hale shouted orders—*fall back, hold the line, LIGHT, someone give me light*—and in the churning dark August grabbed Isolde's collar, Maren seized August's sleeve, and the three of them went *down*, past the soldiers, past the landing, into the flooded lower galleries where no crown writ had ever reached.

* * *

They ran through water to their knees, then their thighs, following the map's impossible geography—the corridors that existed only when grieved over, the doors that opened onto rooms the mountain had swallowed centuries ago. Behind them the sounds of pursuit dissolved into splashing, shouting, and finally silence, because no soldier trained on parade grounds could follow a Coldharbour into the drowned places.

Only when they reached a dry shelf of rock above a black underground lake did August allow them to stop. Maren collapsed, coughing. Isolde slid down the wall, shaking. August stood at the water's edge, chest heaving, and realized her hands would not stop trembling.

"He let us go."

Isolde looked up. "What?"

"At the last. When the water came." August turned. "I looked back. Everyone else was fighting the flood. He wasn't. He was standing on the landing, watching us go down, and he had his hand raised—" she demonstrated, palm out, the universal gesture for *hold* — "and no one followed."

Maren spat water and laughed her dry laugh. "Inquisitors don't let people go, girl. They let people run, so they can learn which direction they run in."

"Maybe." August sat down heavily beside Isolde. "Or maybe a man who's spent his whole life believing the law protects people just watched the law try to drown three women in the dark, and something in him cracked."

Isolde was quiet for a long moment. Water dripped from her hair. When she spoke, her voice was strange—hollowed out and refilled with something new.

"When I was nine," she said, "Corwin Hale found me in the palace kitchens after midnight, eating stolen honey. I was supposed to be asleep. Any other officer would have dragged me to my governess. He sat down, took a spoon, and ate honey with me until neither of us could stomach any more, and then he walked me back and told no one." She stared at the black lake. "He believes in the law the way I used to believe in my father. Completely. Which means when it breaks him, it will break him *all the way*."

"Then we'd best be gone before he finds which pieces to pick up," Maren said, hauling herself upright. "Come on. The vault mouth is a day's climb from here, through passages that haven't tasted air since your ancestors signed their names in blood. And unless I'm badly mistaken—"

She stopped. Turned. Looked back the way they had come, into the dark throat of the galleries, where the water had gone very still and very quiet.

"—unless I'm badly mistaken," she finished softly, "something down here has been listening to every word we've said. And I don't think it minds that the inquisitor let us go. I think," and her voice dropped to barely a whisper, "*it wanted him to.*"

Far off in the black water, something vast turned over, slow as a page being read.

And began, patiently, to follow.
