## 13. Audience with the Hollow King

They took her sword at the gate and her name at the door.

The guards who had dragged her from the ambush at the Weeping Stair did not know what they carried. That was the bitter joke of it—Isolde Veyra, Princess of the Realm, daughter of the man whose face was stamped on every coin in the kingdom, marched through the gates of her own childhood home in a smuggler's oilskin coat with salt crusted in her hair, and not one soul recognized her. The captain had searched her twice, found the plain knife she'd let them find, and marked her down as *courier, captured, value unknown*.

Value unknown. She almost laughed as they led her up the processional stair, past the banners of nine generations, past the stained-glass saints her mother had loved and the marble floor she had learned to walk on before she could run. Ten years she had lived in that palace. Now she was luggage.

But the guards were nervous. She could feel it in their grip, in the way they kept their eyes forward, in the too-quick pace. Something was wrong in the palace. The air itself was wrong—dry in a way stone should never be, tasting faintly of the sea though the coast was a hundred leagues east. And everywhere, everywhere, the white crust. It crept along the mortar joints like frost, climbed the corners of doorways in delicate crystalline ferns, bloomed in the cracks of the flagstones underfoot. Servants moved through it without looking at it, the way people stop seeing the mold in their own walls.

She had seen what that whiteness did to Bellhaven. She had walked streets where grieving men and women sang lullabies to a crown on an empty throne, their eyes full of milk and peace. Her stomach had not stopped clenching since.

The doors of the audience chamber opened, and Isolde forgot how to breathe.

Her father sat on the throne beneath the great sun-disc of the Veyra kings, and he was *shining*. Not metaphorically—the light came off him, a pale pearlescent glow that made the courtiers' shadows lean away from him across the floor. He wore the crown, of course. He always wore the crown now; the servants whispered about it even in the years before she fled, how he slept in it, bathed in it, how once when a chamberlain reached to lift it for his coronation-day blessing the king had broken the man's wrist without appearing to notice he'd moved.

He had been a big man once, her father, broad through the shoulders from hawking and war, with a laugh that shook the rafters and hands that could crack walnuts for his children's amusement. The thing on the throne had his outline. His silhouette. But the flesh beneath the skin had gone translucent, faintly luminous, like wax held too near a candle, and where his eyes had been brown—warm, ordinary, her grandfather's eyes—there was now only a soft white gleam, salt-light, the color of moonlight on a frozen tide.

"Your Majesty," the chamberlain announced, "prisoners taken at the Thornfell stair."

*Prisoners.* Plural. August and Maren had been taken separately, she knew—she had heard the scuffle, heard Maren swearing in three languages before the gag went on. They would be brought after her, or held below. Alone, then. Whatever came next, she would face it alone, and some cold practical corner of her mind noted that this was perhaps better. If her father recognized her—if anything of him remained to do the recognizing—she did not want witnesses to what happened next.

The guards shoved her to her knees on the marble. The impact rang up through her bones. She kept her head bowed, watching the white crystals glitter in the seams between stones, and made herself small, made herself nothing, made herself any nameless courier from any border town.

Silence. Long enough that her knees began to ache. Then footsteps—slow, uneven, the gait of a man whose body no longer quite agreed with itself—and the hem of a royal robe swam into the edge of her vision, trailing frost-white dust.

"Look up," said the Hollow King.

His voice was wrong. That was the worst of it, worse than the glow, worse than the crown fused to his brow by ridges of crystal that had grown down into his hairline like roots into soil. His voice had been a bass instrument, deep and easy. This voice was layered, doubled, a man speaking and something vast speaking just behind him, slightly out of time, so that each word arrived twice and the second arrival was always colder.

Isolde looked up.

Up close, the transformation was worse and stranger. There were still lines around his eyes—her father's lines, earned laughing at her mother's terrible jokes. There was still the scar on his jaw from the boar hunt when she was seven, the one she had helped stitch with shaking child's hands while he told her outrageous stories to keep her calm. The Salt King wore her father's face the way a thief wears a stolen coat: correctly, but without warmth.

And yet. In the white gleam of those eyes there was something moving. Something drowning.

"You are weeping," the doubled voice said, with something like curiosity. "You have not been struck. You have not been sentenced. Why do you weep?"

"I don't know, Your Majesty." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Forgive me. The grandeur of the court overwhelms a poor courier."

A pause. Behind him, the assembled courtiers stood motionless in their ranks, and Isolde realized with a creeping horror that they were not merely still—they were *listening*, all of them, heads tilted at the same slight angle, like a field of flowers tracking the sun. The whole court was a single organism now, and its heart sat on the throne wearing her father's face.

"Grandeur," the king repeated. The word seemed to puzzle him. One translucent hand rose and touched the crown, absently, the way a man touches a wound. "Yes. Grandeur. There was—" The hand trembled. "There was a girl who used to hide behind the arras in the west gallery. During council sessions. Red hair. She thought no one knew."

Isolde's heart stopped and restarted somewhere in her throat.

"She bit the Lord Chancellor when he found her once." The doubled voice softened, the second layer thinning, and for one impossible moment only the first voice remained—only *his* voice, tired and human and cracking at the edges. "Seven years old and she bit him hard enough to bruise for a week. I laughed until I cried. I told the Chancellor it was sound statecraft."

"Daddy?" The word escaped her before she could kill it. Small. Childish. Ten years old, that word, older than ten years, and it hung in the vast cold chamber like a bird trapped under glass.

Something happened to the king's face. The glow guttered. His hand fell from the crown and pressed instead against his own chest, fingers curling into the fabric over his heart as though trying to hold shut a door that was bursting open from within. The courtiers swayed in unison. Somewhere in the crystal lattice of the throne room, a note began to hum—a low, resonant tone, felt in the teeth rather than heard, and Isolde understood dimly that the palace itself was responding, that the sigil threaded through every stone of this place had registered an anomaly and was correcting for it.

"No," the king said—but which of him said it? "No, no, you are not here, you cannot be here, I sent you away, I made certain you were far—" His knees buckled. He caught himself on the arm of the throne, and the white light flared and strobed, and through it his face contorted through expressions too fast to read: rage, terror, grief, and underneath all of them, unmistakably, *love*, raw and desperate as a man clawing at ice above a drowning child.

"Listen to me." He dropped to one knee before her. The court gasped as one throat. A king does not kneel; whatever was left of Edvard Veyra knelt anyway, seized her by the shoulders with hands that burned cold, and put his face inches from hers, and the two voices fought openly in his mouth now, the human one gaining ground in ragged surges. "Little star. My Isolde. Listen, because I do not know—I can feel it taking the words back even as I spend them—I do not know how long I have."

"Father—"

"The vault." His grip tightened, painful. "Thornfell. Beneath the mountain, the drowned archive—you have found it, I know you have, the map has found you, it always finds the ones who grieve—" He shuddered, and for a moment his eyes rolled white and empty, and the humming note rose toward a shriek, and then he wrenched himself back, sweat freezing into salt on his brow. "The lock. They will tell you the binding needs blood, needs grief, needs a willing sacrifice, and all of that is true, but it is not the *lock*. The lock is a question. The old cartographers built it that way so that no tyrant could ever turn it alone."

"What question?" she whispered.

He smiled. It was her father's smile, crooked, one side higher than the other, and it broke her heart more thoroughly than everything else combined.

"*Who grieves with you?*" he said. "That is the lock. The original binder answered it alone, and so the binding became a cage, and the cage became a crown, and the crown became this—" he gestured at himself, at the shining court, at the white bloom creeping up the pillars, "—this. Because grief hoarded is power, little star, and grief shared is only grief, and the thing beneath the mountain cannot abide being merely grief. It must be a king. Do you understand? It feeds because it is fed *alone*. It rules because it is answered *alone*."

"And if it's answered by many?"

"Then it starves." Wonder moved through his ruined face. "Then it becomes what it was before we made it a god. Salt. Only salt. But Isolde—" and here his voice failed, and he bowed his head, and the crown's crystals scraped against his forehead, drawing a bead of blood that froze bright red against all that white, the only color in the room, "—the price. The price is real. Someone must stand at the center of the covenant. Someone must hold the new sigil in their own grief, carry it, become the axis all the others turn upon. It will not kill quickly. It will not kill *cleanly*. It will take your solitude forever—all of it, every hidden road, every secret self, every door you might have closed. You will be known, wholly, by everyone, until you die. For a woman who has spent her life unseen—"

"That's not me," Isolde said. "That's not—that's August. Father, that's August Coldharbour, she's the one who—"

"I know." He closed his eyes. "I know who carries my map. I chose her, or it chose her through me, I no longer know which. That is why I am telling *you*. Because she will offer herself. She has already decided—I can taste her intention on the ink, she writes her own name into everything she touches, she simply hasn't admitted it yet—and you must not let her. Do you hear me? The covenant needs a center, yes, but it does not need a martyr. A martyr makes it a religion, and religions grow kings again. It needs someone who *wants* to be seen. Someone for whom visibility is not a wound."

His hands slid from her shoulders. The light was returning to him, flooding back up from wherever it had been held at bay, and his features were smoothing, the human lines dissolving, the doubled voice reasserting itself beneath his words like a tide coming in over footprints.

"They're coming back," he murmured, fast, slurring now, fighting for each syllable. "I can't hold it. Isolde—my girl—forgive me. Forgive me for the crown. Forgive me for the nights I spoke to it instead of you. Forgive me for letting you believe you were sent away when the truth is I *hid* you, I hid you the way you hide a thing too precious to risk, and I was a coward, and hiding was the same as losing—"

"Father, please—"

"—and I love you," he said, in one voice, only one voice, his own, for the last time. "I love you. Tell no one I said it. It is the only thing it cannot take, because I gave it away before it asked."

The white light blazed. The humming note resolved into a chord, vast and serene and utterly inhuman, and when the king straightened and rose back to his throne, the thing that turned to face the court was the Hollow King entire, and the kneeling, the tears, the beggar's love—all of it had been folded away somewhere behind the shine like a letter locked in a drawer.

"The prisoners," the Hollow King said pleasantly, to the chamberlain, in the doubled voice, "will be held in comfort. The woman called Coldharbour especially. She is to be treated with honor. She is to be *expected*."

Guards took Isolde's arms. She did not resist. She could not have said a word if she'd tried; her throat was a ruin, her eyes streaming, and she let them draw her backward across the marble, watching her father's shining face recede, watching the crown blaze above the court of swaying, listening courtiers, watching the white crystals pulse gently in the walls like the heartbeat of something enormous sleeping under the kingdom.

At the doors, the king spoke once more—not loudly, but the acoustics of the chamber carried it to her perfectly, and she knew, she *knew*, that it was meant for her alone:

"Tell the cartographer: the answer must be given freely, by many hands, or not at all. And tell her—" a flicker, a stutter, half a heartbeat of the old warmth, "—tell her my daughter bites."

* * *

They put her in a guest room. Of course they did. Bars on a princess would have been absurd, and besides, the palace had no fear of her; the palace feared nothing anymore. The door locked from outside with a sound like a coin dropping into a well, and Isolde stood in the middle of a bedroom she had slept in for eighteen years—same canopy bed, same view of the eastern towers, same water stain on the ceiling shaped like a leaping fish that her nursemaid had claimed was a lucky omen—and understood that she had been imprisoned in a museum of her own life.

She sat on the floor with her back to the bed, knees drawn up, and let herself shake.

*Forgive me for the nights I spoke to it instead of you.*

She had thought, all these months on the road, that her anger was simple. The king had vanished into his crown; the daughter had been exiled; the crime was abandonment. Clean grief, clean rage, the kind you could carry in a pack. Now her father had knelt in the ruins of his own mind and handed her the truth, and the truth was filthy with complication: he had not abandoned her. He had *hidden* her. He had spent his last free years arranging her escape from a fate he could see closing over him like water, and he had done it so badly, so silently, so much in the manner of kings—who conceal even their tenderness behind ceremony—that she had read his protection as rejection and fled straight into the arms of the very destiny he'd tried to spare her.

She laughed, or sobbed, or both. The water stain leapt overhead, fish-shaped, lucky.

And beneath the grief, turning over and over like a stone in a stream, the practical mind she'd inherited from him worried at the rest: *she will offer herself. She has already decided.*

August. Of course August would. August, who had built a decade of invisibility into a fortress; August, whose rule was *never be seen, never be owed*; August, who would hear the price—solitude surrendered forever, the self laid open to the whole realm—and think, in that flat quiet way of hers, *I was always going to pay with the thing I value least.*

And she would be wrong. Isolde saw it with sudden, total clarity, the way her father must have seen it. A covenant centered on a woman who experienced visibility as annihilation would not be a covenant at all. It would be a wound dressed up as a sacrament. It would grow its own kind of crown.

Footsteps in the corridor. Keys. The lock turned.

Two guards, and between them, wrists bound with cord, gray-faced and furious, Maren Voss was thrust into the room. The salt-witch took one look at Isolde—at the princess, tear-streaked, sitting on the floor of the royal bedchamber—and her eyebrows climbed slowly toward her hairline.

"Well," Maren said. "The pickpocket has expensive taste in hiding places."

"Maren." Isolde got to her feet. Her voice cracked and she didn't care. "Where's August? What did they do with August?"

"Held separate. Standard practice." Maren's sharp eyes narrowed, reading Isolde's face the way she read sigils—line by line, missing nothing. "You've seen him. The king."

"Yes."

"And?"

Isolde crossed to the window. Below, the city sprawled under a sky going the color of dirty pearl, and on every rooftop, every sill, every ledge, the white bloom caught the dying light and glowed softly, patiently, like ten thousand small altars waiting to be prayed to.

"He told me the lock," she said. "And he told me that August has already chosen to die for us—and that we have very little time to prove him wrong."

Down in the holding cells beneath the east tower, she knew, a cartographer with ink-stained hands was sitting alone in the dark, doing sums with her own life. Isolde pressed her palm flat against the cold glass and made a promise to the glowing city, to her father's ghost, to herself.

Not this time. No one was going to pay alone. Not in her father's kingdom, and not in whatever came after it.
