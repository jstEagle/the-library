## 14. The Price Written Down

The safehouse above the tannery smelled of oak bark and old salt, and August had been awake in it for three days.

Not sleepless—she slept, in the way soldiers and smugglers slept, in shallow trenches of rest she could climb out of at a footstep's sound. But she had not truly rested since the Weeping Stair, since Isolde had torn off her hood before a wall of royal soldiers and said *I am Isolde of House Vane, and these are my guests*, since Hale had looked at his princess with his whole law-bound face breaking like river ice. Since they had run down through the dark with the archive's water rising behind them and Maren clutching stolen pages to her chest as though they were infants.

Now the pages lay spread across the trestle table under a shuttered lamp, and August had read them so many times the words had begun to feel less like language than like weather—something that moved through her whether she willed it or not.

*The binding of the Salt King was wrought by Elira Coldharbour, cartographer, in the year the rains failed.*

Her family's name. In ink eight hundred years dead. In the drowned archive beneath Thornfell, in a vault her ancestors had built and sealed and never spoken of again—or if they had spoken, the speaking had not survived into the generations that raised her. Her grandmother had taught her to draw coastlines. Her mother had taught her which stars lied. Neither had ever mentioned that their bloodline had caged a god.

She heard Maren on the stairs—the particular careful tread of the woman climbing them, each step placed like a word chosen late at night—and August did not turn from the table.

"You haven't slept," Maren said.

"I've slept."

"You've *crouched*." Maren came into the lamplight carrying two cups of willow-bark tea, set one within August's reach without ceremony, and stood looking down at the pages with an expression August had learned to read over the long miles from Greyhollow: the salt-witch wore her face like a locked door, but she always glanced, first, at whatever she most wished were elsewhere.

August watched her do it now. Watched her glance flick to the third page, the one bearing the ritual's closing lines, and then away, quick as a pickpocket's hand.

"Maren."

"Drink your tea."

"Maren." August straightened. The chair creaked under her. "You've been strange since the vault. Strangeness I can survive—you're strange the way a storm is strange—but you've been *avoiding* me, and you are very bad at it, and I have spent ten years reading people who were trying not to be read. So." She turned fully around. "Tell me what the last page says."

Maren set down her own cup. She was a big woman, broad through the shoulders, with grey shot through her black hair and hands stained permanently silver at the fingertips from decades of working brine and ash. She had once, August knew, been the crown's own salt-witch, before heresy charges that amounted to *she asked questions*. She had tested August's grief with a ritual that had left August weeping on the floor of a hovel while Maren held her hair back and said nothing kind, because kindness would have ruined it.

That woman did not flinch easily. But she flinched now, small, and August felt the floor tilt under her before a single word was spoken.

"The binding requires grief," Maren said. "That much you know. Grief is what the Salt King eats; grief is what holds him. A cage made of the thing it imprisons—that's sound craft, ugly but sound. What the archive makes clear—" her voice went flat, deliberate, the voice of a woman reciting something she had rehearsed against this exact moment, "—is that the cage must be *keyed*. Not just any mourner. Not just any sorrow. The original binding was sealed with a Coldharbour's grief, freely given, and freely given means—" 

"Freely given," August repeated.

"Willing," Maren said. "It has to be willing, August. It has to be *chosen*. You can't be tricked into it, coerced, drugged, bound and thrown into the vault. The sigil reads intent the way a lock reads a key. If the grief isn't offered—" she spread her stained hands, "—the Salt King stays exactly where he is, wearing our king like a glove, until there's no king left to wear."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the tannery vats breathed their slow sour breath, and a dog barked twice out in the fog-choked street and thought better of it.

"And a Coldharbour's grief," August said quietly. "Meaning mine."

"It means yours."

"How long have you known?"

Maren's jaw worked. "Since the archive. Before. I suspected from the moment the map wept for you—I told you then, didn't I, that maps don't weep for strangers? But I *knew* when I read Elira Coldharbour's name, and I have known for eleven days, and every day I found a reason not to say it, and I am not going to insult you by pretending those reasons were good ones."

"Eleven days." August stood. The chair went over backward and neither of them moved to catch it. "Eleven days you let me walk toward Thornfell believing there was a way through this that didn't end with me in a flooded hole giving my grief to a god. Eleven days you let Isolde plan routes and Hale argue about supply lines and all of us sit around fires talking about *after*, about what we'd do when the king woke up human—as if after were a place we'd all arrive at together."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*"

"Yes." Maren did not step back. August gave her credit for that; most people stepped back. "Because I hoped I was wrong. Because I spent every night since the vault trying to build a countersigil, trying to find a loophole in a contract written by desperate people eight centuries ago, and I wanted—I *needed*—to come to you with a solution instead of a sentence. And because—" her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, August could see her hating it, "—because I love you, you stubborn, invisible woman, and I could not make my mouth form the words that would send you to your death, and I knew that was cowardice, and I chose it anyway, and I would choose it again tonight and hate myself again tomorrow. There. That's my confession. Take it or don't."

The rage came up in August like floodwater.

It came up hot and totalizing, and for a moment she was not in the tannery safehouse at all—she was nine years old in a burning village, watching crown soldiers feed her home to the flames because some clerk in some office had decided Thornfell's border folk were smuggling more than wool. She was seventeen, burying the last of her family alone. She was twenty-five, drawing maps no one would ever see, teaching herself to need no one, because needing people meant owing them and owing them meant being seen and being seen meant being *taken*—

"You should have told me." Her own voice sounded foreign, scraped raw. "You should have told me the first hour you knew. You had no right—"

"I had no right," Maren agreed. "None. I know."

"—to decide what I could bear! You think I'm fragile? You think I needed protecting from the truth? I have carried the truth of my whole life alone, Maren, I have carried it so long I forgot there was another way to carry anything, and the one time—*the one time*—I let people walk beside me, you lie to me too. You lie to me exactly like everyone else."

"I know," Maren said again, and her eyes were wet and furious at themselves. "And here is the worst part, August. Here is the part I'll answer for at whatever altar takes confessions from women like us: I told myself I was lying to protect you. But I was lying to protect *me*. Because as long as I hadn't said it, I wasn't yet the woman who sent you into the vault."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, and the ripples went out and out, and August stood in the middle of them shaking—not with rage anymore, or not only rage—with the terrible recognition of how well she understood. How she had done the same thing her entire life. Every road she'd walked alone, every caravan she'd guided and then vanished from, every kindness she'd refused so she'd never have to repay it—hadn't that been the same arithmetic? Protecting herself from the cost of mattering?

She picked up the fallen chair. Set it upright. Sat down again, because her legs had opinions of their own.

"Get out," she said.

Maren went. At the door she paused, and August felt her hovering there, wanting to say something else, and August said, without turning, "Don't. Not tonight. Just—go."

The door closed softly. The tea steamed untouched.

* * *

She sat with the pages for a long time after that.

The fog thickened outside the shuttered window until the world beyond the glass was a white blindness, and the lamp burned low, and August Coldharbour read the closing lines of an eight-hundred-year-old contract for perhaps the fortieth time, and this time she read them not as a victim reads a verdict but as a cartographer reads terrain.

*Let the grief be given freely, and let it be given wholly, and let the giver be of the line that drew the first line, for a cage drawn in love must be re-drawn in love, and no lesser key shall turn.*

A cage drawn in love must be re-drawn in love.

Elira Coldharbour had done this to save a starving realm. That was in the archive records too—the famine years, the dead fields, the realm tearing itself apart at the seams while the Salt King rose out of the drowned places offering mercy at a price. Elira had bound him. Had poured her grief into the sigil like mortar into a wall. And it had worked, and it had become tyranny, because a mercy held by one pair of hands always does.

August traced the sigil's opening curve with one ink-stained finger. It was beautiful work. Whoever Elira had been, she had drawn like someone who understood that a line is a promise, that every mark commits you to everything that follows. August had spent her life making marks that committed her to nothing—invisible roads, unrecorded passes, maps that existed only in her head and died a little more with every year she refused to write them down.

*Never be seen,* her rule ran. *Never be owed.*

And here was the truth she had been circling for eleven days alongside Maren, the truth she had known in her bones since the dying courier pressed the bloodstained map into her hands and gasped *the crown is not the king*: she had always known it would come to this. Some part of her had known from the ford. Maybe from the beginning. Maybe from the day her village burned and she crawled out of the smoke and swore she would never be visible to power again—because some animal part of her had understood that invisibility was only ever a loan, that the world would eventually present its bill, and she had spent ten years not thinking about what the bill might be.

She had planned for this. God help her, she realized, sitting alone in the fog-bound dark with a dead woman's sigil glowing faintly under her fingertip—she had planned for this the way she planned everything: alone, in advance, without telling anyone, because telling anyone meant owing them and being seen and being *known*, and being known meant that leaving would hurt someone.

She had simply assumed the leaving would be a disappearance. A vanishing between one pass and the next. She had not imagined it would be a vault, and a god, and her grief poured out like ink onto the largest map she would ever draw.

But the shape of it—the shape of *alone*—that she knew like her own handwriting.

August got up. Found paper, the good rag stock she hoarded, and a fresh nib. Sharpened the nib with the small knife her mother had given her, the one with the bone handle worn smooth by three generations of Coldharbour hands. Set the archive pages where she could see them. Began to copy.

She copied the way she did everything: precisely, obsessively, every curve measured against the original, every stroke a vow. The sigil unfolded across the page like a coastline revealing itself—no, not like a coastline. Like a coastline was the wrong image. Like a *river system*, branching and reconverging, every tributary necessary, every channel feeding the whole. And somewhere in the copying, in the rhythm of it, in the scratch of nib on rag paper in a room that smelled of oak bark and old salt, her rage cooled into something harder and quieter and far more dangerous.

Resolve.

They would try to stop her. Of course they would. Isolde would argue law and lineage at her; Hale would argue justice; Maren would argue countersigils until the stars burned out. They would stand in her way with love, which was infinitely harder to fight past than soldiers, and she would have to do it anyway, and she would have to do it *without letting them see her doing it*, because that was the one craft she had mastered above all others.

Vanishing.

She would finish the copy tonight. She would learn the sigil until it lived in her hands the way the smuggler roads lived there, until she could draw it blind, drunk, bleeding, in the dark. And then she would wait for the descent—for the vault, for the moment when the company's attention split between holding off the Salt King's crystallized dead and reaching the broken king—and in that moment she would go forward alone, the way she had gone everywhere alone for ten years, and she would give her grief freely, willingly, wholly, and none of them would be able to stop her because none of them would know until it was done.

It was a good plan. It was, she admitted to the empty room, the best plan she had ever made, because it required no one's permission and broke no one's heart until after it was finished.

The lamp guttered. August dipped her nib and kept drawing.

She was on the fourth branch of the river-system, deep in the sigil's interior channels where the lines began to look almost like writing, almost like names, when she noticed it—a small thing, a cartographer's thing, the kind of detail she would have caught in any map she'd ever studied and nearly missed in her own grief-fogged copying.

In Elira Coldharbour's original, the sigil's central channel—the channel that received the grief, the channel that fed the cage—did not terminate. It branched. Faintly, finely, in strokes so thin they might have been damage or age: dozens of hairline tributaries running outward from the heart of the sigil toward its edges, toward its borders, toward the margins of the page, as if the original binding had been drafted with some possibility in mind that its maker had never dared to complete.

As if the grief had been meant, once, to be shared.

August sat very still. Outside, the fog pressed against the shutters like a hand. Downstairs, faintly, she could hear Maren moving, awake too, awake the same way she was awake, and upstairs in the loft Isolde murmured in a dream, and somewhere across the sleeping town Hale kept his sleepless soldier's vigil, and the whole found, fragile, impossible weight of them sat in August's chest like a stone she could neither swallow nor spit out.

She looked down at her copy—at the clean, closed, solitary channel she had drawn, the cage exactly as Elira had built it, keyed to one grief, one name, one death.

Then she looked at the original's unfinished branches, and her hand, entirely without her permission, began to move toward them.

* * *

She worked until the fog outside thinned toward grey dawn, and when at last she set down the nib, the page before her held two drawings side by side: the binding as it had been, and beside it—rough, tentative, half-erased and redrawn three times—the first sketch of something else. Something with a hundred channels instead of one. Something that looked less like a cage than a *map*.

A map of everyone who grieved.

August stared at it until her eyes burned, and she was still staring when the floorboard on the stairs creaked with Maren's careful tread, and the salt-witch appeared in the doorway with two fresh cups of tea and an apology she'd clearly been composing all night—

—and stopped dead, staring past August's shoulder at the second drawing, at the branching channels, at the impossible revision, her silver-stained fingers going slowly white around the cup.

"August," Maren whispered. "What have you done?"
