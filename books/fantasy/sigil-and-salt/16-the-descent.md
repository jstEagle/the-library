## 16. The Descent

The entrance was not a door. It had never been a door.

August knelt at the base of the Weeping Stair's shattered landing, where the mountain's root met the flooded dark, and pressed her palm flat against stone that sweated cold. Behind her, the company waited with the particular silence of people who had learned that questions cost breath and breath might be needed later for running. Above them—far above, past six switchbacks of stairwell still smoking from Hale's soldiers' torches—the Salt King's dead were coming down.

She could feel them the way she felt weather in her knees. A pressure behind the sternum. A taste like old coins.

"There," she said, and traced the seam with one finger. To anyone else it would have been a crack, a flaw in the rock, the kind of thing a mason curses and works around. But August Coldharbour had spent ten years learning to read the Thornfells the way priests read scripture, and this crack was written in a hand she recognized. Her grandfather's survey marks were carved beside it—a sigil no crown cartographer ever used, a little hooked glyph meaning *water runs here, but not always*.

"The smugglers called this the Drowned Door," she said. "My grandmother called it something less polite."

Maren Voss crouched beside her, salt-white braids swinging, and sniffed the air the way other women sniffed milk. "Brine under the stone. Fresh enough to drink if you're desperate." She glanced sideways at August. "You're certain your family didn't build this whole mountain just to spite me?"

"Pretty sure they built it to spite everyone equally."

Isolde laughed—a short, startled sound, as though laughter had been confiscated from her years ago and she was still surprised when it escaped. She stood with her hood back now; there was no more point in disguise, not after the stairwell, not after she had stepped between Hale's drawn blade and August's throat and said *I am Isolde Aurelian, daughter of the king you serve, and you will lower that sword or explain to him why his heir bled on his own stairs.* The name had worked on Hale better than any weapon. It had cracked him open like frost cracking a windowpane.

Hale himself stood apart, at the edge of the lantern light, arms crossed over the grey coat he'd stopped wearing the insignia of. He had not spoken much since the stairwell. He kept looking at his hands as though they belonged to someone else.

"It will be flooded," he said finally. His voice had the flatness of a man reporting troop movements. "A vault beneath the waterline. You're proposing we walk into a grave."

"I'm proposing," August said, "that we walk into a grave *on purpose*, which is the only kind worth walking into." She pulled the bloodstained map from its oilcloth wrapping and held it up. In the lantern light the impossible geography shimmered—that drowned architecture that appeared only when grief touched the vellum, corridors of sunken stone rendered in ink that moved like tide. "The map shows the way in. It doesn't show the way out. I want everyone to understand that before we go down. Once we're inside, the way out is whatever we can make."

"Cheerful," said Isolde.

"I'm a smuggler. Cheerfulness is extra."

Maren snorted. Hale almost smiled, then remembered he was a man in exile from his own certainty, and put the smile away.

August folded the map away and set her shoulder against the crack. "Everyone take a handhold. When the door opens, don't breathe until I say. The air behind it is older than the kingdom and it will try to fill your lungs with itself."

She found the second mark—her grandmother's hooked glyph again, mirrored—and pressed both thumbs into the stone between them, and thought, as she had been thinking since the archive, about her ancestors. About the Coldharbours who had signed the binding in ink and salt and someone's freely given grief. About the cage they'd forged and the mercy it had curdled into. Grief came easily these days. She let it pool, and pushed it into the stone the way Maren had taught her to push intention into salt.

The mountain sighed.

The crack widened—not breaking, but *opening*, stone folding back on itself along seams a century old, and the smell rolled out: brine and iron and something sweet underneath, like flowers rotting in deep water. Darkness beyond darkness. The sound of water moving somewhere far below, patient as a heartbeat.

"Now," August whispered, and led them into the earth.

* * *

The first gallery was dry, which surprised her, and then frightened her, because the map had promised water and the map did not lie—it only lied about *when*. Ribbed columns held up a ceiling lost in black. Their lanterns threw shadows that moved a half-second too slow, and August chose not to think about that. The walls were carved. Not decorated—*carved*, every surface, floor to unseen height, covered in lines and interlocking figures that resolved, when she looked too long, into faces.

"Don't read the walls," Maren said quietly. She walked with a pouch of salt in each fist, pouring a thin line behind them without looking, an old woman's habit that August now understood was anything but. "It's sigil-work at scale. The whole vault is one sentence, and the sentence is the binding. Read enough of it and you start to *agree* with it."

"What does it say?" Isolde asked.

"'Someone must hold this,'" Maren said. "'Forever.' That's the short version. The long version takes three miles of corridor and a thousand years of rationalization."

They went down. August navigated by the map and by memory—her family's memory, inherited in fragments, glyphs her grandmother had taught her as bedtime stories: *the left-hand fork where the stone drinks light; never trust a passage that echoes twice; count your steps going down so you can lie to yourself about coming up.* Each junction confirmed the last. Ten years of staying invisible, of mapping roads no crown would ever admit existed, and all of it—every cold camp, every checkpoint slipped, every night she'd lain alone under stars telling herself solitude was freedom—had been leading here. She had thought invisibility was her armor. Now she understood it had also been her apprenticeship.

At the third gallery the water began.

It came first as sound—a vast, slow sloshing below, rhythmic, tidal, though they were a hundred leagues from any sea the maps admitted to. Then as cold, rising through their boots. Then as light: a faint phosphorescence in the water itself, pale green-white, illuminating nothing so much as the fact of its own depth.

"The tide's wrong," Hale said. He had unslung his sword and carried it naked now; he'd stopped pretending it was for show somewhere on the fourth switchback. "There's no moon down here to pull it."

"There's something down here pulling it," Maren said grimly.

August checked the map. The drowned corridors glowed faintly where her tears had fallen days ago, and she saw what she'd been dreading: the route to the vault's heart crossed the flooded galleries four times. Four crossings. And the water was rising.

"We move fast," she said. "First crossing is ahead—the causeway. Single file, hands on shoulders if the light goes. Isolde, you're second. You've got the best eyes in the dark—I've seen you pick pockets by feel in a cellar. Call out anything that moves. Maren behind her, then me, Hale at the rear. If anything comes out of the water, it goes through me before it reaches the front."

"That's not how rearguard works," Hale began.

"I know how rearguard works. I'm telling you where things die first." She met his eyes. "You're the one who changed sides two days ago, Inquisitor. Forgive me if I want you where I can see you."

Something flickered across his face—hurt, maybe, or the ghost of the man who had believed order saved lives—but he nodded once and took the rear.

The causeway was a spine of stone barely wider than a coffin, arcing over black water that glowed and breathed. August went first because she always went first; it was the oldest rule she had. Halfway across, the water below them lit from within, and she saw them.

The dead stood in the shallows. Hundreds of them. Crystallized—salt grown through flesh and armor and clothing until each figure shone like a chandelier, translucent, faceted, beautiful in the way that avalanches are beautiful. They were not moving. They were *waiting*, faces tilted up toward the causeway, and their eyes were points of white fire behind crystal, and they were singing.

Not aloud. The song came up through the stone, through boot-soles, through teeth: a low chord of grief so pure it made August's vision blur. She recognized it. It was the same chord the survivors of Bellhaven had hummed, hollow-eyed, saluting the crown. The Salt King's choir. Every soul the binding had fed on, preserved like flies in amber, still grieving, forever.

"Don't listen," Maren breathed. "Don't answer it. It wants you to grieve *in time* with it. Grief in harmony is a door."

August bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted copper and kept walking. Behind her she heard Isolde's breath hitch and steady, hitch and steady—the princess fighting her own tears, fighting for her father who was somewhere ahead of them, drowning in this place's master. She heard Hale murmuring something under his breath, rapid and rhythmic, and realized with a jolt it was a legal recitation—the articles of some code or charter, the law he'd served, being used now as a rosary against despair. Even that, she thought. Even his faith, repurposed. Everyone carrying what they had.

They made the far side. August's legs were shaking. Nobody spoke about the dead. There was nothing to say that the singing hadn't already said better.

* * *

The second crossing nearly killed them.

The causeway there had collapsed sometime in the last century—of course it had; her grandmother's glyph at its head had been defaced, the hooked symbol gouged away by someone who hadn't wanted the road found—and the gap was eight feet of glowing water with no bottom visible. On the far side, the passage continued upward toward the heart of the vault.

"We could swim," Isolde offered.

"No," August and Maren said together.

"You don't swim in water the dead are standing in," Maren elaborated. "That's not superstition, girl, that's arithmetic."

August studied the gap, the walls, the ceiling. And there—her grandfather's glyph again, half-destroyed, but beside it another mark she knew: a spiral with a bar through it. *Counterweight.* Old smuggler engineering. Her people had run cargo through these galleries once, before the crown razed the villages and burned the surveys, and smugglers always built a second way out.

"Hale. Your sword. Give it here, and give me your belt."

He handed them over without argument, which told her more about how far he'd come than any confession could have. She wedged the blade into a seam in the wall, looped the belt through the crossguard, tested it with her full weight, then tied off the leather and swung out over the water.

The dead watched her swing above them. Up close, in the green glow, she could see what they'd been—farmers, soldiers, a woman with a child's small crystallized hand still clasped in hers. One of them raised its face higher as she passed, and its burning eyes followed her, and its mouth moved behind the crystal, shaping words she couldn't hear but somehow understood anyway:

*Take it with you. Take our grief with you. Someone should carry it somewhere.*

"I will," August whispered, and hated herself for promising, and meant it.

She landed on the far ledge, anchored the belt, and hauled the others across one by one—Isolde light and quick as a cat, Maren swearing in three languages, Hale last, heavy and silent, his empty scabbard slapping his thigh. When his boots hit stone, the belt tore through. The sword dropped into the water with hardly a splash, and the dead below turned toward it, and did not touch it. Even in death, apparently, they respected a surrendered weapon.

"My sword," Hale said blankly.

"Consider it a donation to the realm's new government," August said. "Come on. We're close."

"How can you tell?"

"Because my hands won't stop shaking, and they only do that when it matters."

* * *

The third crossing was underwater.

Not deeply—a chest-high gallery where the tide had swallowed the road entire, the ceiling low enough that Maren had to hold her salt-pouches overhead like offerings. They waded in a line, hands linked, the phosphorescent water lighting their faces from below so that they all looked like drowned things themselves. August went first, testing each step with a pole she'd cut from a fallen stanchion. Isolde behind her, gripping her belt. Then Maren. Then Hale, who had taken to counting aloud—steps, breaths, anything—in that flat legal voice, and nobody asked him to stop because it helped, actually, it helped all of them, a human metronome against the singing.

Halfway through, the tide turned.

August felt it before she heard it—a change in pressure against her thighs, the water beginning to *pull*, flowing backward toward the vault's heart as though the whole flooded dark were inhaling. The level rose. Waist. Ribs.

"Faster," she said.

Shoulders. The current grabbed at them like hands. Somewhere behind them, distantly, came a sound she had been dreading since the stairwell: crystal on stone. Footsteps. Many. The dead had left their shallows.

"They're following us," Isolde gasped.

"No," Maren said, and August heard something in the witch's voice she'd never heard before: fear. "They're *escorting* us. There's a difference, and the difference is worse."

Chin-deep. August's pole found a step, a real step, ascending, and she hauled herself onto it and reached back and dragged Isolde up, then Maren, then Hale, all of them streaming, gasping, the water already reclaiming the gallery behind them. And in the green light, wading after them with terrible patience, came the vanguard of the crystallized dead—twenty of them, fifty, their facets catching the glow, their burning eyes fixed forward, their song swelling from a chord into something with *direction* now, a hymn with a destination.

Us, August thought. We're the destination. Or what we're carrying.

"Run," she said. "The heart of the vault is through the next arch. Whatever happens, whatever you see—we get there together. Nobody carries anyone's grief alone. Not anymore. That's the whole point. That's why we're doing this. *Together.*"

Isolde took her left hand. Maren took her right. Hale put his free hand on Maren's shoulder, and for a moment the four of them stood linked in the phosphorescent dark, breathing hard, the dead coming on, the mountain holding its breath.

Then they ran.

Through the arch, up stairs that wept real water now, clean and cold, past carvings that flinched from their lanterns—and the singing rose behind them, and rose, and did not stop, and did not need to stop, because ahead of them the passage opened at last into a vast chamber where the air itself was thick with salt-light, and standing in the center of it, waiting, patient as tide, was something wearing the shape of a king.

It turned. It smiled with a drowned man's kindness.

"You brought them," it said, in a voice like water finding every crack in the world. "How thoughtful. I was hoping you would."

Behind August, Maren whispered, "Gods preserve us. It knows the ritual already started."

And the dead filed in behind them, sealing the archway like a tide closing over a footprint.
