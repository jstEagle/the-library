## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

Ansel Winterbourne had been keeping the Meridian Guild's ledgers for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once lost a column.

This was not a boast. It was simply a fact about her, the way it was a fact about her neighbor Mrs. Halloway that she grew excellent gooseberries and about the cat on the counting-house roof that it had never once caught the pigeon it stalked every morning at dawn. Some creatures were made for their work. Ansel was made for columns.

She sat now in the third-floor tally room of the guildhouse, where the windows were tall and narrow and let in exactly enough light to read by and not one ray more — a design choice, she suspected, intended to discourage lingering. It had never discouraged her. The room smelled of ink, beeswax, and the particular dusty sweetness of old vellum, which Ansel privately considered the smell of order itself. Around her rose the shelves: two hundred years of Meridian accounts, bound in green leather stamped with the guild's sigil, a gate within a circle. Somewhere below, the trading floor murmured like a hive at rest. Somewhere above, the Masters kept their offices behind doors that opened only for the worthy, which by long tradition meant everyone except Ansel.

She did not mind. She had her columns.

The ledger open before her was the autumn reconciliation for the Saltmarket exchange, and it was misbehaving.

"Come along," she murmured to it, tapping her pen against the margin. "You were balanced in spring. What have you been doing all summer?"

The trouble sat on line forty-one. *Received: one year, unblemished, from the estate of Maren Ostler.* Against it, on line forty-two: *Paid out: six months to the widow Corren, three months to the ferryman's boy, three months held in reserve.*

Ansel read it twice. Then a third time, because eleven years of competence had taught her that when an entry looked impossible, the entry was usually wrong — but occasionally, and this was the part that kept her up some nights, the entry was exactly right, and the world around it was what had gone soft.

Years could not be sold. She knew this the way she knew the tide tables: absolutely, and without ever having verified it personally. And yet there it was, in a fair clerk's hand — not hers, not old Master Quillon's, but the round, careful script of a junior named Pell, who had since been promoted upstairs to the Masters' corridor and who no longer answered his door.

And the widow Corren, Ansel happened to know, had taken to her bed last winter aged sixty-one and risen in the spring with color in her cheeks and a new set of teeth, and the whole street had called it a miracle of good broth.

Ansel wrote a small checkmark beside the entry, as she did with everything that balanced, and then she sat looking at the checkmark for a long moment, because it did not balance. Not really. Six months plus three plus three plus three held in reserve came to fifteen months, and the estate had sold twelve. Someone, somewhere, had invented three months out of thin air, or out of something thinner than air.

"Rounding," she said aloud, in the tone of a woman scolding a dog caught on the good chair.

The tally room did not answer. It never did, which was one of its virtues.

* * *

At noon, the under-clerk brought her tea and the day's intake docket, as he had every working day for four years. Tam... no, that wasn't right — the boy's name was Ferren, small and quick, with ink permanently staining the first knuckle of his right hand.

"Master Quillon says the Vellum Street lot sent over their quarterlies early," Ferren reported, setting down the tray, "and he says to tell you—" here Ferren dropped into a startlingly accurate impression of the old man's gravel rasp— "*if she finds another discrepancy I shall hear about it before luncheon and not after.*"

"He means it as a compliment," Ansel said.

"He means it as a threat."

"With Master Quillon those are the same thing." She poured the tea. It was oversteeped, as always; Ferren believed strength of brew was a moral quality. "Thank you. Go eat something that isn't bread."

"Bread has all the food groups," Ferren said with dignity, and fled.

Alone again, Ansel turned to the Vellum Street quarterlies, and the unease she had been carrying all morning shifted its weight, the way a cat resettles in your lap.

Here, on the second page, in a different hand entirely — older, more formal, the kind of script they taught clerks thirty years ago and didn't anymore — was an entry that stopped her pen mid-stroke.

*Traded: the name "Ilsette Vane," complete, to a buyer beyond the gate. Consideration received: favorable winds, duration seven years.*

A name. Traded. Complete.

Ansel set down her pen very carefully, as though it might go off.

She knew the rules of the guild's trade as well as anyone outside the inner offices could. The Meridian Guild dealt in impossibilities — that was its founding charter and its pride. Luck, weighed and portioned. Dreams, bottled at the hour of waking. Weather, in small negotiable quantities. The city of Cindral had grown rich and strange on the guild's commerce, and everyone knew the trade crossed *the Gate*, though nobody outside the guildhouse spoke of the Gate above a whisper, and nobody inside it spoke of it at all. There was a covenant, people said, when they said anything. There was a balance. The Guild kept it.

But names? A name was not weather. A name was not luck. A name was—

Ansel pulled the older ledgers off the shelf, the ones from before her time, and began, with the methodical calm that her colleagues mistook for serenity, to search.

It took her until mid-afternoon. She found nine more. Names traded across decades, always in that same old formal hand or ones like it, always marked *complete*, always with considerations received that made her skin prickle: *favorable tides; a mild winter; the cessation of a fever; one debt forgiven.* And once, chillingly, in the margin beside a traded name, a notation in faded brown ink: *buyer wept. unusual.*

She copied them all onto a loose sheet — not because she intended to do anything with the copies, but because her hands needed occupation while her mind ran ahead of her like a horse that had smelled smoke.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had been circling for eleven years without ever letting herself land.

The trades balanced. That was the genius of the guild's books, the reason the Masters tolerated — no, treasured — a plain woman with good eyes and a suspicious nature: every impossible thing that entered the ledger was met by an equivalent leaving it. Nothing appeared from nothing. The covenant, whatever it truly was, enforced a terrible bookkeeping, and Ansel respected it the way sailors respect the sea.

But these entries did not balance. They couldn't. What did a man give in exchange for a *name*? What price covered a whole human identity, root and branch? The consideration column listed winds and winters and mild fevers' ends — gifts, blessings, things any generous patron might bestow. As if the buyer beyond the gate had not purchased the name at all, but *thanked* someone for taking it.

As if the far side wanted the names gone.

Ansel looked at her neat sheet of transcriptions for a long time. Then she folded it into thirds, slid it beneath the false bottom of her writing box where she kept her private correspondence and her mother's ring, and locked the box.

She was not doing anything, she told herself. She was merely being thorough. A good keeper of accounts noted anomalies. Noting was not accusing. Copying was not conspiring. She was a woman who balanced books, and if the books whispered to her in the evenings, well — clerks who worked with impossible things were permitted a certain imagination, provided it stayed between the margins.

Outside the tall window, the light had gone amber. The bells of the Weighing Hour would ring soon, and the trading floor would empty, and Ansel would go home to her rooms above the chandler's shop and eat supper and sleep, and tomorrow she would come back and balance more columns, and everything would continue exactly as it had for eleven contented years.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. She had checked it for discrepancies herself.

* * *

The Weighing Hour bell rang at six, as it had rung since before the guildhouse had glass in its windows, and Ansel closed the autumn ledger with the small satisfaction of a task completed — mostly completed, she amended, thinking of the three phantom months — and squared her pens.

She was wrapping her scarf when Master Quillon appeared in the doorway.

He was very old and very upright, built like a piece of furniture from a better era, and he had a way of materializing at moments of mild inconvenience that Ansel had long ago stopped finding eerie. Tonight, though, something in his face gave her pause. He looked tired in a way that went past age.

"Winterbourne." His voice was low. "You found the discrepancy in the Saltmarket quarterly."

"Three months unaccounted for," she said. "I've flagged it for the next audit. Probably a transcription error in the original intake — Pell's figures were always—"

"It isn't a transcription error." He said it flatly, without looking at her, and then he did look at her, and what was in his eyes made the back of Ansel's neck prickle. "Leave the flagged page as it is. Don't carry it further. Some entries are recorded for the record's sake alone, and inquiry does them no service. Do you understand me?"

Eleven years of dutiful contentment sat in Ansel's chest like a swallowed stone. "I understand that you're asking me to stop balancing, Master Quillon. That's rather like asking the tide to stop coming in."

Something flickered across the old man's face — she would remember it later, in harder days, and identify it then as grief — and he said, quietly, "Yes. I imagine it is. Good evening, Winterbourne."

And he was gone, his footsteps fading down the stair, and Ansel stood alone in the amber-lit tally room with her scarf half-wrapped and her heart beating oddly fast.

Below her, the trading floor was emptying. Above her, the Masters' corridor lay silent behind its doors. And somewhere in the building — she could not have said how she knew, only that the knowledge arrived whole, the way arithmetic sometimes arrives whole — somewhere in this house of balanced books, something was very badly out of count.

Ansel finished wrapping her scarf. She took her cloak from its peg, blew out the lamps one by one, and was halfway down the stairs when she heard, from the direction of the counting room below, the sound of the great outer doors opening — though the Weighing Hour had passed, and the doors were locked at the Weighing Hour, and she herself held the key.

Then came a heavier sound. A body, she thought distantly, with the part of her mind that never stopped classifying. Someone falling against the counting table. And a voice, ragged and urgent, calling out into the dark of the guildhouse:

"Is anyone — the ledger-keeper — I need the one who keeps the ledgers—"

Ansel stood on the stairs, one hand on the rail, eleven years of quiet competence warring with eleven years of quiet obedience.

The voice called again, weaker now, bleeding through the words.

She went down.
