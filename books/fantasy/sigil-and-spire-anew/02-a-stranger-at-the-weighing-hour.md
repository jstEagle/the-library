## 2. A Stranger at the Weighing Hour

The Meridian Guild closed its books at the weighing hour — six bells past dusk, when the great scale in the atrium was ceremonially balanced by a master who had not once, in Ansel Winterbourne's eleven years of service, found it anything other than balanced.

Ansel did not attend the ceremony. She never attended the ceremony. The ceremony, in her privately held opinion, existed so that masters could stand about in good coats looking solemn while she and the other ledger-keepers did the actual arithmetic that kept the guild from collapsing into a pile of unpaid debts and unfulfilled promises.

Tonight the counting room smelled of tallow, ink, and the particular staleness of a room where forty people had breathed all day without opening a window. Lamps guttered on their stands. Somewhere above, the bell for the weighing hour tolled its six slow notes, and around her the other keepers began the small rituals of ending: capping inkwells, squaring papers, complaining about their feet.

"Ansel." Pellam, the old man who shared her table, leaned over with the air of a man delivering scandal. "Master Corvane's office requisitioned another hundredweight of candlewax this month."

"Did it," said Ansel, not looking up.

"A *hundredweight.* For one office."

"He does his reading late."

"No man reads that much." Pellam lowered his voice to a whisper that would have carried across a market square. "Unless he's burning something."

Ansel made a small note in the margin of her ledger — *C., wax, 100wt, query* — because that was what she did with things that didn't add up. She wrote them down, in tiny careful letters, in margins no one read, and then she went home and slept soundly, because the alternative was thinking about them, and Ansel had built her whole comfortable life on the principle that most problems were simply entries waiting to be reconciled.

She was, everyone agreed, the best keeper the guild had. Not the cleverest — Master Orlin could do figures in his head faster than she could write them down. But cleverness was common in the Meridian Guild. What was rare was Ansel's particular talent: she could look at two columns of numbers that both appeared perfectly correct and know, in the pit of her stomach, before any proof surfaced, that somewhere between them a lie had been folded in like a wrong stitch in a hem.

The guild paid her well for this. The guild gave her a room with a window facing the harbor, three meals, a stipend for books, and a title — Keeper of the Second Ledger — that her mother, were she alive, would have framed and hung in the front room of their little house in Tallow Row.

It was a good life. Ansel reminded herself of this frequently, the way one might remind oneself that a soup is good even while noticing an unfamiliar flavor at the bottom of the bowl.

* * *

The last lamp on the left side of the counting room was hers to extinguish. She always saved it for last because it stood nearest the tall window, and from the window she could see the harbor lights coming up along the breakwater, one by one, like a slow patient constellation. She had told herself once, years ago, that she stayed those extra minutes for the beauty of it. She suspected now it was mostly procrastination — thirty seconds each evening when she belonged to no column, owed nothing, reconciled nothing.

She had just put her hand to the lamp when the door banged open.

Not opened. *Banged.* Hard enough that the draft snuffed two lamps down the row and sent a loose folio spinning off Pellam's abandoned table like a startled bird.

A man came through it sideways, which was how people moved when they were holding themselves together out of habit rather than capacity. He was tall, gray-cloaked, mud to the knees, and there was blood on him — a lot of blood, spread from his left shoulder down across his ribs in a way that suggested the wound beneath had opinions about staying closed.

He got four steps into the counting room. Then his legs resigned their commission all at once, and he went down against a records cabinet hard enough to rattle every drawer, slid to the floor, and sat there breathing in wet, whistling pulls.

Ansel stood very still with her hand still on the lamp.

Her first coherent thought — and she would be ashamed of this later, or perhaps not, because honesty was honesty — was: *that cloak is going to stain the floor, and I am the one who signs off on cleaning requisitions.*

Her second thought arrived a half-beat behind, properly horrified: *he's dying.*

"Hello," she said, which was absurd, but the absurd words came out steady, and steadiness, she had learned, was contagious. She crossed the floor, knelt beside him, and pressed her palms flat over the wound on his shoulder. Warmth immediately welled up between her fingers. "You've been stabbed."

"Observant," the man managed. His face was long and hollowed, a scholar's face gone hungry, and his eyes were the pale gray of weather coming. "They told me... the keeper of the second ledger..." His hand came up — shaking, black under the nails, ink-stained the way her own hands were stained — and seized her wrist with surprising force. "You're her. Winterbourne."

"I'm nobody," Ansel said. "I'm the woman whose floor you're bleeding on."

"Winterbourne." He said it like a password, or a verdict. And then his other hand came up, and it was holding something wrapped in oilcloth, and he pushed it against her chest with the desperate grammar of a man who had maybe ten sentences left in him and intended to spend every one.

"Take it. Don't read it here. Don't let them see you have it. It's a map — Ilex made it — Ilex is *in* it—" A cough took him then, a bad one, folding him forward until his forehead nearly touched her shoulder. When he straightened, there was blood at the corner of his mouth, and his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "The covenant starves. Do you understand me? The covenant *starves*. They're drinking the far side dry and calling it balance. You balance things. That's what they say of you. So *balance this*."

"The covenant—" Ansel began. "Sir, I don't— I file import tariffs on dream-glass. I don't know what you're—"

"You will." Something like a smile crossed his ruined face. "That's the trouble with you keepers. You always find out."

Boots in the corridor. Many boots, moving fast, with the particular synchronized rhythm of men drilled to move as one instrument.

The stranger's eyes flicked toward the door, and Ansel watched — she would replay this moment many times in the weeks to come — watched a man make a calculation with the last of his strength. Not about escape. About *placement.*

"Under the third cabinet," he breathed. "The map. Now."

And because her hands had already obeyed before her mind caught up — because some part of Ansel Winterbourne had apparently decided, without consulting her at all, that this was now her problem — she stuffed the oilcloth bundle down into the gap beneath the third records cabinet, where decades of spilled ink had crusted the floorboards into a shadowed seam no enforcer would voluntarily touch, and sat back on her heels just as the door burst open for the second time.

Enforcers poured in. Six of them, in slate-gray coats with the meridian line stitched in silver at the collar, batons drawn, moving with the smooth menace of people paid by the hour to be frightening. At their center came a woman Ansel knew by reputation if not by acquaintance: Captain Vess, whom the lower clerks called the Cold Compass behind her back, because she always pointed true and felt nothing about it.

Vess took in the scene in one sweep: the dead-or-dying man, the kneeling keeper, the blood.

"Keeper," she said. Her voice was level and unhurried and entirely without warmth, a voice like a well-kept ledger itself. "Step away from the body."

"He's not—" Ansel looked down. The stranger's eyes had gone fixed on the ceiling, and his chest had stopped its terrible laboring, and whatever argument his body had been having with his wounds, the wounds had won. "No," she said quietly. "I suppose he isn't."

She stepped away. It seemed important, suddenly, to follow instructions precisely.

Vess crouched by the body. She did not touch it at first; she studied it, the way Ansel studied a suspicious column. Then, with two gloved fingers, she turned the man's face toward the lamplight.

"Courier," she said, mostly to herself. "One of Ilex's." She looked up, and her gaze settled on Ansel with the mild weight of a paperweight set on a loose page. "You touched him."

"He collapsed. I tried to stop the bleeding."

"What did he give you?"

The question landed so directly, so *certainly*, that Ansel felt the strange vertigo of being asked for something she did not have — except that she did have it, it was wedged under the third cabinet not four feet from the captain's boot, and the only thing standing between her and ruin was a lie.

Ansel had never lied to anyone in authority in her life. It wasn't virtue, particularly. It was that lying required a kind of improvisation she'd never been able to afford; her whole talent was for finding the one true thread in a tangle, and she assumed everyone else's talent was finding hers.

But she looked at the dead man's face, at the blood still pooling with horrible patience beneath him, at the word still ringing in her skull — *the covenant starves* — and heard herself say:

"He gave me his weight. That's all. He grabbed my wrist and fell, and then your people were at the door." She held out both hands, palms up, showing them empty. Ink-stained, honest, ordinary. "Whatever he was carrying, Captain, he didn't give it to me."

For a long moment Vess looked at her hands. Then at her face. Ansel had the distinct impression of being weighed — actually *weighed*, set upon a scale against some standard she couldn't see — and she kept her expression in perfect balance, which was, after all, the one skill she had practiced more than any other.

"Search the room," Vess said, rising. "Thoroughly. And someone fetch the undertakers before he stains the masonry."

They searched. They were good at it. Ansel stood by the tall window with her arms folded and watched them go through cabinets, drawers, the stranger's cloak and pockets, and tried to breathe at the rhythm of a woman with nothing under her fingernails but ink.

They found a brass token in his pocket. A folded letter, sealed, which Vess read without sharing. They found, in his boot heel, a thin sliver of stone that made one young enforcer hiss and drop it as though it had bitten him — a sliver that glimmered faintly, faintly, with light that didn't behave like firelight, before Vess picked it up with a handkerchief and put it away in a lead-lined case.

But they did not look under the third cabinet. Why would they? The third cabinet was famous, in the way of old furniture, for having a permanently sticky drawer and a history of ink spills, and no searcher alive goes willingly to their knees in a crust of forty years' dried ink at the end of a shift.

"Keeper Winterbourne." Vess stopped beside her on her way out. Up close the captain smelled of rain and steel polish, and her eyes were a color Ansel couldn't fix — gray, brown, something between, changing like the ledger-light. "You'll remain available. If anything occurs to you — anything he said, anything he did — you'll bring it to me personally. Not to a master. To me."

"Of course, Captain."

"And Winterbourne." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You're known as the best finder of discrepancies in this building. Whatever he wanted from you — he wanted it because you notice things. So I'd advise you, kindly, to be very careful about what you choose to notice this week."

Then she was gone, and the boots receded, and the counting room was quiet except for the tick of cooling lamps and the slow, awful drip of blood finding the seams in the floorboards.

* * *

Ansel locked the counting room door behind her, which she was technically allowed to do and had technically never done, and stood in the dark for a long moment with her heart going like a debtor fleeing collections.

Then she fetched the lamp, knelt down in forty years of crusted ink — her good skirt, her *only* good skirt — and drew out the oilcloth bundle.

It was lighter than she expected. Flat, rectangular, bound at one edge, and sealed shut with a disc of dark red wax stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize: a circle crossed by a line that bent in the middle, like a road that had changed its mind.

*Ilex is in it,* the courier had said. Which was either madness or poetry or a code, and Ansel had spent eleven years learning that the three were often the same document.

She should turn it in. That was the correct entry in the ledger of proper conduct: *found property of deceased person of interest, surrendered to Captain Vess.* Clean, balanced, defensible. Her mother's voice in her head — practical, Tallow Row to the bone — said exactly that, in exactly that tone.

But her mother had never heard a man spend his last breath saying *the covenant starves* while enforcers who worked for candle-hoarding, wax-burning, secret-keeping masters pounded down the corridor a minute too late. A minute too late, Ansel thought. Not an hour. Not a day. A *minute*. As if they had known precisely when to arrive — after the giving, before the asking.

She thought about the margin note she'd made an hour ago. *C., wax, 100wt, query.* She thought about all the other margin notes, years of them, tiny letters in tiny corners: the shipments of grain that arrived at the granary already counted twice; the pension rolls that included names of keepers retired twenty years; the quiet instruction, passed down from master to master like a family recipe, that certain ledgers were to be copied but never *summed*, because summing them produced totals that embarrassed the upper floors.

She had written them all down and slept soundly. She had called that diligence.

Sitting in the dark on her cold knees, holding a dead courier's map, Ansel admitted for the first time what the margins had been for. Not record-keeping. *Permission.* Every note was a little door left open in a wall she'd built against knowing, and she had told herself she kept them for tidiness, and she had known, in the pit-of-the-stomach way that was her single uncanny gift, that one day she would walk through one.

She unwrapped the oilcloth just enough to confirm it was a map — heavy paper, hand-inked, covered in fine script she'd need better light and better courage to read — and wrapped it again.

Then she did the most dishonest thing of her entire blameless life, and found, to her mild astonishment, that she was good at it after all: she cut the lining from her coat, sewed the map flat against her back with quick ugly stitches, buttoned the coat over it, picked up her satchel, and walked out past the night porter with a nod and a murmured *"late balances, don't wait up"* that sounded so natural she frightened herself.

The harbor lights were all up along the breakwater now. The wind off the water smelled of salt and tar and the far, cold open dark beyond the city.

Ansel pulled her coat tight around the map, around the dead man's last sentence, around the word *starves* beating in her chest like a second pulse, and started home through the gray-lit streets — very aware, with every step, that tomorrow she would have to return to the counting room, sit down at her desk, and open the Second Ledger under the eyes of the guild that had just killed to keep its books closed.

Somewhere beneath the floorboards of the world, she suspected, a new account had been opened tonight with her name in it.

And Ansel Winterbourne, keeper of impossible sums, was terribly afraid she already knew which column she'd been entered in.
