## 3. What the Masters Keep Below

The map weighed nothing and everything.

Ansel had spent the night with it locked in her writing desk — the good desk, the one with a false bottom she'd discovered at nineteen when she'd gone looking for her predecessor's hidden brandy and found instead three decades of unrecorded debts. She'd learned something useful that night: every institution keeps two ledgers, the one it shows and the one it hides. She simply hadn't expected to become the hiding place for a third.

By morning she had talked herself into a plan of magnificent simplicity: return the map. The courier was dead; the guild would want whatever he'd carried; she would place it in Master Corvel's hands and say, *I found this on the floor after the poor man collapsed,* which was even true if you squinted at it sideways. Squinting at truth sideways was, she reflected, essentially what balancing a ledger meant.

"You're up early," said Pell, the junior clerk, as she passed him on the stairs. He was carrying tea for four and wearing the expression of a young man who had recently learned that masters summoned clerks before dawn only for unpleasant reasons.

"Always am," Ansel said. "Ledgers don't balance themselves."

"Mine did once," Pell said solemnly. "I'd entered everything twice by accident. It balanced beautifully. Master Ondrey called it a miracle until he found the error."

"That," said Ansel, "is the most honest theology I've heard in this building."

She smiled as she said it. She did not feel like smiling. The courier's whisper had followed her through the night like a cat determined to be fed: *the covenant starves.* She had no idea what covenant. But she knew what starving looked like in numbers — she'd seen it in the ledgers for years, those entries that shouldn't exist, withdrawals from accounts labeled only with sigils instead of names. She'd assumed they were codes. Codes were normal. Codes were how institutions pretended to be mysterious instead of merely disorganized.

She had not let herself think about where the withdrawals went.

Master Corvel's chambers were on the ninth floor. The stairwell beyond them — the one marked PRIVATE, GUILD COUNCIL ONLY, in letters so old the gilt had worn to a suggestion — was not where Ansel intended to go. Her plan required the ninth floor and nothing more.

But the ninth floor was empty. Corvel's door stood open on a cold hearth, an untouched breakfast, and a note in his precise hand: *Below. All of us. Do not disturb.*

*Do not disturb.* Ansel stood in the doorway for a long moment, holding a dead man's map, in a guildhouse where all five masters had vanished downward together before dawn.

There was a version of her — the version who had worked here eleven years, who had never once taken an unapproved step, whose handwriting was used as the guild's official standard — who would have set the map down neatly on the desk, closed the door, and gone back to her ledgers to wait for instructions.

That version of her had died sometime between midnight and dawn, quietly, without leaving an entry.

Ansel looked at the private stairwell. The door at its top had always been locked. It was not locked now. Whoever had gone down below in such a hurry had left it open behind them, which told her either that they were careless or that they believed no one would dare follow.

She dared. She told herself it was only to return the map — that this was, in fact, her duty, and that duty was the safest word she owned. She tucked the map into the inner pocket of her coat, took a deep breath, and began to descend.

The stairs went further than the building should have allowed. She counted them out of habit — counting was her prayer, her rosary, her way of staying calm in small dark places — and stopped counting somewhere past four hundred, because the number had stopped meaning anything. The walls changed around her. The plaster of the upper floors gave way to older stone, then to stone older than stone, veined with faint lines that might have been mortar or might have been script. The air grew cool and mineral, and beneath the smell of dust there was another smell, one she couldn't name — green, almost, like a meadow remembered rather than smelled.

And there was light ahead. Not lamplight. Something paler, softer, the color of milk held up to the sun.

Ansel stopped on the last step, in the shadow of an archway, and forgot how to breathe.

* * *

The chamber below the guildhouse was vast and round, and it had not been built. It had been *found* — she understood that immediately, the way one understands that a cave is not architecture. Its walls curved inward like the inside of a throat. And at its center, sunk into the floor, was a well.

Not a well for water. A well for *light.*

It rose from the darkness like breath in winter — pale, slow, silvered with threads of every color too faintly to name — and above it, arranged in a crescent, stood the five masters of the Meridian Guild. They had removed their coats of office. That detail struck Ansel strangely, harder than anything else: five old men and women in their shirtsleeves, working, the way farmers work, the way butchers work.

Chains of gold hung from the vaulted ceiling, each ending in a hook, and from the hooks hung — she searched for the word and could not find it, so she made do with the nearest honest one — *lanterns*. Except the lanterns were not glass. They were shapes of woven sigil-light, and into them, from the well, the pale radiance flowed like syrup poured thin.

"It thins every year," Master Corvel was saying. His voice carried in the round chamber with terrible clarity. "The draw comes slower."

"Then we draw deeper." That was Mistress Halloway, the eldest, whose portrait hung in the entry hall and whose smile in the portrait was warmer than any smile Ansel had ever seen her give in person. "The covenant has slack in it. It has always had slack."

"The courier thought otherwise," said Master Ondrey.

"The courier is no longer a concern," Halloway said, and turned back to the well.

Ansel's hand found the wall beside her. The courier — the man who had bled out across her counting floor with his lips shaping words like a prayer — was a line item now. *Resolved.* She felt sick, and beneath the sickness, worse, a kind of vertigo: eleven years of her life rearranging themselves around this chamber like iron filings around a magnet. Every strange entry. Every withdrawal from the sigil-accounts. Every year the guild's fortunes had flourished while harvests failed in the outer provinces and children in the salt-districts aged faster than their parents had.

They weren't buying impossible things through the gate.

They were *draining* something. And calling it trade.

She watched Corvel lift one of the woven lanterns down from its hook with both hands, cradling it like an infant, and carry it to a coffer at the chamber's edge. Inside the coffer — she saw only briefly, before the lid closed — lay rows upon rows of smaller vessels, each glowing faintly, each labeled in the guild's accounting hand. Her own hand. The standard hand. Some clerk, some version of her, had written those labels.

One of the lanterns flickered as Corvel set it down, and for half a heartbeat the light within it shaped itself into something like a face — a mouth, opening. Ansel heard nothing. But the shape of the silence was a scream.

She pressed her back against the cold stone of the archway and made herself very small and very still, and thought, with the detached clarity that had served her through every audit and every crisis: *You have seen the second ledger. You are standing inside it.*

She should leave. She should climb the four hundred steps and put the map on Corvel's desk and go home and be a person who had never come down here. That was still possible. Probably. People survived knowing things all the time — didn't they? Didn't they simply fold the knowledge away like a letter from someone dead, unread, unopened?

But the courier had chosen *her*. Of every clerk in the Meridian Guild, bleeding and dying, he had crawled to her counting room. He had pressed the map into her hands and whispered his last words to *her*, and whatever the covenant was, it was starving, and these five people in their shirtsleeves were eating.

Ansel stayed one moment longer than wisdom allowed. In that moment, Mistress Halloway turned her head — not toward the archway, not quite, but near enough — and said, softly, to the room:

"We are nearly ready. When the last vessel fills, we will need no gate at all."

And the pale light in the well dimmed, just slightly, like a candle guttering, and then steadied again, and Ansel fled upward into the dark.

* * *

She made it as far as the ninth-floor landing before her legs informed her they had opinions.

She sat down on the topmost private stair with the map against her chest and her heart doing arithmetic she couldn't check. Above her, the guildhouse went about its morning — carts rumbling in the yard, Pell's voice drifting up as he argued with a deliveryman about the weight of a shipment of "spices." Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that did not know what filled its cellars.

*We will need no gate at all.*

Which meant the gate would be free. Free to close. Free to starve whatever waited on the other side entirely — or free to open. She didn't know which frightened her more, and she suspected, with a ledger-keeper's instinct for imbalance, that Halloway hadn't distinguished between the two.

The map shifted against her ribs. Only the shift of cloth over paper. Certainly that.

"Found this on the floor after the poor man collapsed," she rehearsed under her breath. "I meant to bring it straight up, but—" But what? But I followed five masters into a hole in the world and watched them bottle light that screams? She rubbed her eyes. "I'm a very poor liar," she told the empty landing. "This is going to go catastrophically."

"Talking to yourself, Winterbourne?"

Ansel came off the stair so fast she nearly fell.

Captain Vess stood at the mouth of the corridor, arms crossed, watching her with the patient attention of a woman who had been watching for some time. Vess was not tall, but she occupied space the way a drawn blade occupies a room. Her enforcer's gray coat was immaculate; her eyes missed nothing, including, apparently, clerks sitting on forbidden staircases at dawn.

"Captain." Ansel smoothed her coat over the map with what she hoped was casual dignity and not the gesture of a smuggler caught mid-crime. "I was — looking for Master Corvel. There's a discrepancy in the third-quarter accounts I wanted to confirm."

"At dawn."

"Discrepancies keep hours," Ansel said. "Like cats. They appear when they choose."

Something moved at the corner of Vess's mouth — not a smile; a reconnaissance of one. "Corvel isn't receiving."

"So I saw. His note said *below.*" Ansel paused, delicately, a jeweler offering a stone for inspection. "Is something happening below? Should I be concerned?"

Vess regarded her for a long, silent moment. Ansel had played cards with enforcers before, at guild festivals, and had learned that they bluffed badly and called well. Vess was calling.

"A courier died in your counting room last night," Vess said.

"He did. Terrible thing. He'd lost a great deal of blood before he ever reached us — I noted it in the incident report."

"I read your report." Vess stepped closer. "It was very thorough. Very neat. Almost suspiciously neat, Winterbourne. Most clerks, finding a dying stranger, forget to record the position of the body."

"I'm a thorough person."

"Mm." Vess's gaze dropped, briefly, to the line of Ansel's coat — to the pocket where the map lay flat and silent and heavy as sin — then rose again. "The courier carried nothing. No satchel. No documents. Nothing in his pockets but a copper farthing and a tooth. Men who run messages for a living don't travel empty-handed, and men who bleed that much don't lose their cargo in a tidy alleyway."

"What are you suggesting, Captain?"

"That someone in this building has something of his," Vess said quietly. "And that whoever it is should think very carefully. The masters take theft seriously. More seriously than murder, even." She stepped back, and her coat settled, and she was once again merely a professional woman with an unpleasant errand. "Enjoy your discrepancies, Winterbourne."

She walked away. Ansel stood on the landing, listening to her boots recede down the corridor, and did not breathe fully until they were gone.

Then, alone in the morning quiet, she allowed herself one shaky, slightly hysterical laugh — because terror and laughter, she was discovering, lived in adjacent rooms and shared a wall.

"She knows," Ansel murmured to the map. "Of course she knows. She's known since last night. And she didn't search my pockets, which means either she's giving me rope, or—" Or she wanted to see what Ansel would do with it. Or she had her own reasons for not reporting what she suspected, and Ansel had spent enough years reading the gaps between entries to know that Captain Vess kept a second ledger of her own.

The map pressed warm against her side. Warmer than body heat could explain.

"Later," Ansel told it sternly, the way one tells a cat. "Absolutely not. We are going upstairs, and we are going to be extremely ordinary, and you are going to stay quiet."

The map did not answer. But as she climbed toward her office and the safety of ordinary ink, Ansel could not shake the image of the well below — that slow pale rising, that face-shaped flicker, the scream with no sound. *We are nearly ready,* Halloway had said.

Ready for what, Ansel wondered. And how much time did a starving covenant have left?

She reached her office, locked the door, and drew the map out into the light. Its wax seal gleamed dully. Beneath her fingers, faint as a pulse under skin, the parchment trembled.

* * *

Vess descended the private stair without a lamp, as she always did. Four hundred and twelve steps. She had counted them years ago and never told anyone why.

At the bottom, the masters were closing the coffer, sealing the morning's draw. Halloway looked up, unsurprised. "Well, Captain?"

"No one followed. The Winterbourne woman was asking questions on the ninth floor."

"And?"

"And she's lying about something," Vess said. "But she's lying badly, which means she hasn't done it before." She hesitated. Hesitation was a luxury she permitted herself perhaps twice a year. "The courier. What was he carrying?"

Halloway smiled the portrait smile. "Nothing that concerns the guard, Captain. Concern yourself with the streets. And Vess—"

"Ma'am."

"If the little clerk grows troublesome, bring her to me alive. Dead clerks raise questions. Frightened ones raise none."

Vess bowed, and turned, and climbed the four hundred and twelve steps with the courier's question lodged in her chest like a splinter: *men who bleed that much don't lose their cargo in a tidy alleyway.*

She had lied to the masters, just now, and they hadn't noticed. That was new. That was worth examining later, carefully, in a room with the door locked.

Because Vess had read the incident report, yes. But she had also read the courier — properly, before the body went cold, with a professional's eye. And stitched into his collar, invisible unless you knew to look, she had found a single thread of gold.

Guild thread. Spun, she was nearly certain, below.

Whatever the dead man had carried, it had never left the building. And Captain Vess intended to find out exactly who was keeping it — and why, for the first time in fifteen years of service, she hoped the thief got clean away.
