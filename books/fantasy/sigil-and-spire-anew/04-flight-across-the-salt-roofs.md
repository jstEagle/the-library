## 4. Flight Across the Salt Roofs

The accusation went up on the guildhouse doors before dawn.

Ansel saw it from the window of a chandler's loft she had no right to be in, three streets and one very undignified climb away from where she'd started the night. Someone had nailed the notice up by lantern light: *ANSEL WINTERBOURNE, KEEPER OF LEDGERS, WANTED FOR THEFT OF GUILD PROPERTY AND THE MURDER OF COURIER EDE.* Below it, in smaller print, a reward figure that made her laugh — a short, hysterical sound she quickly smothered in her sleeve.

"Murder," she whispered. "I didn't even like him, but I certainly didn't kill him."

Well. She hadn't liked him much. He'd bled on her favorite ledger.

The chandler's loft smelled of tallow and honey, and its owner was still asleep below, which meant Ansel had perhaps an hour before someone came up for candles and found a fugitive crouched among the wicks. She took stock of herself with the same methodical attention she gave to end-of-quarter accounts. Boots: stolen from a drying line, two sizes too large, stuffed at the toes with rag. Coat: hers, thank goodness, though now bearing a scorch mark across the left shoulder where an enforcer's binding-spell had missed her by the width of a prayer. Map: against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping bird. Sigil-stone: in her fist, where it had been all night, because whenever she tried to put it down her hand refused to open.

She'd taken it from the vault antechamber without thinking — or rather, her body had thought for her, snatching the pale stone off its velvet cradle while her mind was still screaming *run*. It was the size of a plum and faintly luminous, like moonlight trapped in soap. When she turned it over, something inside it turned too, slow and patient, the way a fish turns under ice.

"You're going to get me killed," she told it.

It did not answer. But it grew, if anything, slightly warmer, which she chose to interpret as encouragement and knew, deep down, was probably just physics.

Below, the city was waking. Meridian's bells rang the hour — six of them, muffled by fog rolling in off the harbor — and somewhere a fishwife began arguing with a gull in terms that would have made a stevedore blush. Normal sounds. Ordinary morning sounds. Except that every door in view now bore a notice, and every notice bore her name, and the name was written in guild ink, which meant it could not be scraped off, painted over, or argued with. Guild ink held its argument forever.

Ansel Winterbourne had spent eleven years believing in that ink. She had balanced its ledgers to the last copper, reconciled its impossible columns, and never once asked what lay behind the sealed well beneath the counting hall — until last night, when she had seen the masters standing around it in their gray robes, drawing up pale light in long silver threads while something on the other side pressed against the seal like a starving face against a window.

Her hands were shaking again. She made them stop. There was work to do, and shaking hands were no good for climbing.

* * *

The salt roofs of Meridian were famous in the way that dangerous things are famous: everyone spoke of them, no one used them. The city's eastern quarter was built in terraces down toward the water, each rooftop crusted white with mineral bloom from the harbor spray, and in winter the whole expanse glittered like a spilled jewel-box — beautiful, treacherous, and utterly unpatrolled, because no enforcer in the guild's employ was fool enough to chase anyone across it.

Which meant, Ansel reasoned, wedging her oversized boots into the gap between a chimney and a rain barrel, that they would absolutely chase her across it. Vess would see the logic of the salt roofs immediately. Vess saw the logic of everything; it was her most irritating quality, back when Ansel had known her only as the captain who signed the requisition forms, and it was considerably more irritating now that she was the captain trying to arrest her.

Ansel climbed out onto the tiles.

The world tilted. That was the first thing. The second thing was that the tiles were slicker than they looked, coated in a rime of salt that turned footing into philosophy — you committed, and then you found out what the roof thought about your commitment. She slid four feet, caught a gutter with both hands, and hung there, boots pedaling air above a drop into a courtyard full of laundry lines and one extremely interested cat.

"All right," she breathed. "All right. We're doing this."

She hauled herself up, redistributed her weight the way she'd seen dockworkers do it — low, centered, respectful of gravity as one respects a temperamental colleague — and took her first real step along the ridgeline.

And something happened that she had not expected, not in any of the frantic scenarios she'd rehearsed through a sleepless night.

It felt *good*.

Not good in a comfortable way. Good in the way that cold water is good, or truth is good — bracing, clarifying, slightly terrible. Eleven years she had spent in the counting room, a woman shaped exactly like her chair, her days ruled by columns and seals and the soft institutional hum of things being done correctly. Now there was wind tearing at her hair, and the whole gray-gold city spread out below her like an opened ledger, and every rooftop ahead was a column waiting to be crossed, and nobody — nobody — knew precisely where she was.

She laughed. Out loud this time. The cat below yowled disapproval.

"Oh, don't judge me," she called down to it, and moved on.

* * *

Vess found the chandler's loft at half past six.

Captain Sera Vess stood in the doorway of the empty room and read it the way she read everything: completely, silently, and without mercy. The candle dust disturbed in a hand-width pattern. A rag, pulled from some poor soul's boot, discarded near the window. The window itself latched from outside — sloppy, desperate, human. She noted it all without writing any of it down, because her memory was a ledger too, and it never needed balancing.

"She went up," said Enforcer Dree, breathing hard beside her. He was young, earnest, and built like a wardrobe, and he clearly hoped the trail would lead somewhere sensible, like a tavern.

"Of course she went up." Vess leaned out the window and studied the salt roofs, white and endless in the morning haze. "A bookkeeper runs along the ground, where she can be cornered. This one saw the well." She said it flatly, but Dree flinched anyway. Word of what the masters did down there had a way of not spreading and yet somehow being everywhere. "She knows what cornered means now. Get the net-teams to the Spire Bridge and the Saltgate stairs. She'll come down eventually. Everyone comes down eventually."

"And if she doesn't?"

Vess stepped onto the windowsill, balanced there a moment — a narrow, precise silhouette against the fog — and stepped out onto the tiles.

"Then we walk the sky," she said. "Move."

* * *

Ansel learned the roofs the way she learned everything: by making mistakes and recording them.

Mistake the first: slate tiles lie about themselves. They look solid; they are liars. Mistake the second: momentum is a currency, and she kept spending it all at once like a drunkard. Mistake the third — and this one nearly ended the account entirely — chimneys lean. This particular chimney, belonging to a rope-maker on Tallow Row, leaned east, and when Ansel grabbed it for balance it leaned further, groaning, and deposited her in a slide down forty feet of salt-crusted slope toward a gap between buildings wide enough to swallow a coach.

She caught the far ledge with her fingertips. Her boots swung over nothing. The sigil-stone, in her pocket, pulsed once — she could have sworn it pulsed — and warmth ran up her arm, and whether it steadied her or merely distracted her from panic she couldn't say, but she got her elbows over the edge and lay there gasping on a stranger's rooftop like a landed fish.

"Never," she informed the sky, "doing that again."

Then she got up and did it again, seventeen more times, because the alternative was the ground, and the ground belonged to the guild.

By midmorning she had developed something like technique. Step where the moss grows — moss means shade means less salt means grip. Trust the ridge-tiles, distrust the pans. When crossing a gap, go fast, not brave. She crossed above the fish market and heard, distantly, her own description being read aloud by a crier, the reward figure causing visible interest among the crowd. She crossed above the guildhouse itself and allowed herself one long look down at its gray spire, at the counting-room windows where she'd spent half her life, and felt — not grief, exactly. More the specific ache of a key that no longer fits any lock it was cut for.

That was when she saw them.

Three figures on the roofs behind her, spaced like compass points, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who had done this before. And ahead, on the ridge of the dye-house, a fourth — slighter, alone, standing perfectly still in the manner of someone who does not need to hurry because the geometry of the situation has already been settled.

Vess.

Even at this distance, Ansel could feel the captain's attention settle on her like a hand on the shoulder. No shout, no signal. Just that patient, professional stillness, and then — deliberately, so Ansel would see it — Vess checked the position of the sun, glanced at the rooftops between them, and began to walk forward. Not run. Walk. As though the outcome were a matter already entered in a ledger awaiting only the date.

Ansel's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for sinking ships.

*Run*, said every nerve she owned. And she did — but her bookkeeper's mind ran alongside her body, cool and quick, doing sums. Three behind, closing. One ahead, patient. The roofs ran east toward the Spire Bridge, where net-teams would wait, because Vess would have posted net-teams, because Vess was Vess. South lay the harbor and water cold enough to stop a heart. North—

North lay the Old Tannery, a warren of collapsed roofs and sagging beams that even the salt-spray hadn't gentled, where the buildings leaned on each other like drunkards and the gaps between them dropped into alleys full of lime pits. No enforcer trained on those roofs. No sane person crossed them at all.

Ansel turned north.

Behind her, faintly, she heard Vess exhale — one short breath, almost approving — and break into a run.

* * *

What followed, Ansel would later remember in fragments, the way one remembers a storm: the tannery roofs groaning under her boots; a beam giving way and her leap across a gap she'd have sworn was impossible; salt wind and lime dust and her own heartbeat loud as a drum-corps; and always, always, the measured footfalls behind her, never gaining, never falling back, keeping pace like a conscience.

Once, on a ridge above the old lime yards, they were close enough to speak.

"Winterbourne." Vess's voice carried flat and clear across the wind. "You're tired. I'm not. Come down and I'll swear you'll see a tribunal instead of the masters' cellar."

"The cellar," Ansel panted, not stopping, "where you draw light out of a starving world?"

Something flickered across Vess's face — Ansel saw it, a hairline crack in the professional mask, gone in an instant. "I follow orders."

"So did the well," Ansel said wildly, "and look how full it isn't," and flung herself across the widest gap yet, a black chasm between the tannery and the granary where the drop was five stories and the landing was a roof that visibly sighed under her.

For one glorious moment she stood alone on the granary roof, chest heaving, wind whipping her hair, the whole city wheeling below her — and the terror was still there, humming in her bones, but threaded through it now was something else, bright and strange and unmistakable. Freedom. Not the tidy freedom of finished work and locked doors, but the wild kind, the kind you can't file or balance. She had spent her whole life inside entries written by other hands, and here, absurdly, dangerously, on a rooftop she had no business surviving, she was writing her own.

Then the granary roof, offended by the day's accumulated abuse, shed its western slope.

Ansel rode the slide down in a clatter of tiles, grabbed the parapet at the bottom, and swung around the corner onto the Saltgate stair-roof just as Vess came over the granary ridge above — close enough now that Ansel could see the frost of exertion on the captain's breath, the net-coil on her shoulder, the eyes doing their tireless arithmetic.

"There's nowhere the roofs don't end," Vess called down.

"Everything ends," Ansel called back. "That's rather my point!"

And then the map moved.

She felt it before she understood it — a shift against her ribs, the oilcloth twitching like something waking. She fumbled it out one-handed, and in the gray daylight the ink was moving, roads rearranging themselves, and a fresh line was drawing itself across the parchment in a thin sure hand: a route, northward, off the roofs entirely, down through the warehouse district to the harbor's drowned quarter — and beneath the route, small and urgent, words surfacing in the ink like bubbles rising through dark water:

*Not the stairs. The culvert. Trust the tide, not the map. Hurry.*

Ansel stared. The map — the dead courier's map, the map she'd hidden from enforcers and slept clutching like a talisman — was giving her directions. In handwriting. With opinions.

"You're supposed to be paper," she whispered.

Above her, Vess had begun to descend the granary wall with terrifying economy, hand over hand, eyes never leaving her prey. Behind, the other enforcers were cutting across the tannery, flanking. The tide-bell out in the harbor began to ring the turn of the water.

There was no time to decide whether maps ought to talk. Ansel stuffed the map back against her ribs, took a breath that tasted of salt and lunacy, and ran for the culvert — feeling, for the first time since the counting room, that she was not running alone.

Somewhere in the ink, faint as a heartbeat, something waited to tell her why.
