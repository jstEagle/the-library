## 6. Three Sigils, One Debt

I had been dead for eleven years before anyone asked me a direct question.

That is not quite fair. Ansel Winterbourne had spoken to me twice already — once to demand what I was, once to tell me to stop glowing at her while she ate. But this was the first time she sat down across from me, in the abandoned counting house on Candlemaker's Row, folded her hands like a woman about to audit the afterlife, and said: "Tell me everything. From the beginning. And don't embellish."

Ilex of the Meridian Guild, cartographer, covenant-keeper, and currently four ounces of ink soaked into calfskin, considered his options.

"Everything," I repeated.

"Everything."

"That will take some time."

"I have nowhere to be," she said, "because you got me branded a thief and exiled from my own life." A pause. Her voice softened by exactly one degree, which from Ansel was roughly equivalent to an embrace. "And because I think you've been waiting eleven years for someone to ask."

She had me there. The dead are patient out of necessity, not virtue.

So I told her. And because ink cannot pace, cannot pour tea, cannot do any of the small physical things that make confession bearable, I did it the only way left to me: I made the map remember, and let her watch.

* * *

We were five. That is where every telling must begin, because five is the number the covenant itself chose, and we were arrogant enough young people to believe the choice was coincidence rather than instruction.

There was Ilex — myself — who drew maps so accurate that the Guild suspected witchcraft and promoted him instead, which tells you most of what you need to know about the Guild. There was Sera Vane, who could hear a lie the way other people hear a wrong note in a song. There was Odo Brask, a harbor warden with forearms like dock rope and a heart that forgave everyone except himself. There was Maren Quill, youngest of us, a bell-ringer's daughter who claimed she could feel the city's moods through the soles of her feet. And there was Corvane.

Yes. *That* Corvane. Hold your judgment until the end; I promise it will only get worse.

We were not heroes. We were friends who met on Thursdays to complain about our employers over bad wine, which in my experience is the raw material from which all heroes are accidentally made.

The year I turned thirty-one, the gate beneath the guildhouse began to sing.

You should understand what the gate is, properly, because the Guild has spent two centuries teaching people to misunderstand it. It is not a door. A door implies rooms on either side, equal and adjacent. The gate is more like — imagine a wound between two bodies that share one blood. Our realm and the far realm were never separate things. They are one thing, folded, and the covenant is the fold line. Five sigils held that fold closed but alive: sealed enough that nothing crossed uninvited, open enough that life could pass between them the way warmth passes through a wall. The far side sent us their light — that pale gold you have seen in old paintings and assumed was artistic license. We sent back... less than they needed, as it turned out. But that failure came later, and it came from us.

When the gate sang, the five of us went down to listen. Not because we were brave. Because we were curious, and because Sera said the song sounded like someone crying, and none of us could sleep after that.

The masters of the Guild met us at the well-mouth, all silver robes and reasonable voices, and told us the singing was a maintenance issue. Told us the sigils were being redistributed for safekeeping. Told us, with great kindness, that the matter was above our station.

Sera heard three lies in that sentence. She grabbed my sleeve under the table hard enough to bruise.

So we stole them first. All five sigils, in one disastrous, magnificent week that Odo still insisted decades later had been *his* idea, though the historical record — meaning my map margins — clearly attributes it to Maren, who picked the vault lock with a bell-hammer and a hairpin and laughed the entire time.

Then came the argument that broke us. Not a shouting argument. The quiet kind, the kind where everyone speaks gently and everyone leaves with wounds.

Keep them together? One place, one guard, one catastrophe away from total loss. Split them apart? Five places, five keepers, five chances for the Guild to hunt them down one by one.

Maren wanted them split. "A chain is only as strong as its weakest link," she said, "but a net holds even when half its threads tear."

Corvane wanted them together. He was the eldest, the most careful, the one who read the old texts. "Five scattered stones," he said, "are five invitations."

I settled it, God forgive me, with a map. I spread out the city and drew five circles and said, look — here, and here, and here — no one place can hold all five safely, but no one hunter can hold all five of *us*. And because I was the clever one, the one whose maps were never wrong, they believed me.

Cleverness, I have learned, is just confidence wearing better clothes. My map was perfect. My plan was not. Maps show where things are. They do not show what things cost.

* * *

Odo took the first sigil into the debtor's prison beneath the harbor, because he was a warden and knew its tunnels, and because he reasoned — correctly, as it happens, though it gave him no comfort — that no thief would ever volunteer to go to debtors' prison.

He did not merely hide there. To bind a sigil to a keeper, you must give it something of yours to hold. Odo gave it his forgiveness. Every grudge, every old injury, every name on the long list of people who had wronged him — he surrendered the whole ledger of it into the stone, and the stone kept it, and he walked out of that binding a man with no grievances left.

You would think that would make him free. Instead it made him hollow in a specific and terrible way, because Odo had built his entire self out of the slow work of forgiving people, and without the list he no longer knew what his hands were for. He stayed in the prison anyway. Voluntarily. Thirty years below the waterline, guarding a stone, surrounded by men whose debts he could no longer bring himself to judge.

"He's still there?" Ansel asked quietly.

"He's still there."

"And the sigil?"

"Under the harbor. Third level down, past the flooded archive, in the cell they used to reserve for wardens who took bribes. Odo thought it was funny." I paused. "It was not funny. But Odo laughing at all, in those years, was worth tolerating."

Ansel wrote something in her notebook. She had started carrying a notebook everywhere since the rooftops, and I pretended not to be flattered that half its entries began with *the map says.*

"The prison floods at high tide," she said. "How does anyone—"

"Through the archive. The drowned archive. It's flooded too, but the shelves stand above the waterline if you know which aisles." I hesitated, because the next part was not mine to soften. "You should know that the warden there trades in truths, Ansel. Corvane's doing, long ago — he seeded the place with his own philosophy before he went wherever he went. Nothing in that prison is given. Everything is bought. And the only currency accepted is true things, said aloud, that cost the speaker something."

She looked up sharply. "What kind of price?"

"The honest kind. Which is the worst kind." I let the ink settle, giving her a moment. "You'll want to prepare something true about yourself before you go down there. Something real. The warden will know the difference between a truth and a performance. They always do."

* * *

But I am ahead of myself, and Ansel chided me once already about embellishment, so let me finish the circle. Five friends. Five stones. Five prices.

Sera Vane took the second sigil north and bound it with her hearing — the gift that caught lies. She gave the stone every lie she had ever told, and it drank them, and afterward she could no longer hear falsehood at all. Imagine that, if you can. Imagine living in a world where every flattery, every polite fiction, every comforting untruth sounds exactly like the truth. She lasted nine years before the noiselessness drove her into a cloister where the sisters had taken vows of silence, and there she sat, they say, listening to nothing, perfectly content, perfectly unreachable. Some say she is still there. Some say the cloister burned. I have never been able to confirm it, and the not-knowing is one of the small hells I have reserved for myself.

Maren took the third sigil into the heart of the city and hung it in the great bell of the Spire, because she was a bell-ringer's daughter and understood that a bell is a promise the city makes to itself every hour. Her binding-price was her memory of her mother's face. She chose it deliberately — she told us that grief fades anyway, that memory is a coin losing its stamp, and that she would rather the stone have something precious than something convenient. She rang that bell for forty years. When she finally died, the whole city swore the noon peal sounded different that day. Lower. Like a voice saying goodbye.

Corvane took the fourth sigil and — 

Here I must pause again, because Ansel was watching me with that auditor's stillness, and I found I could not lie to her. Not because she would catch it. Because she had bled for this map already, hiding it against her ribs while enforcers searched her rooms, and some debts are paid in candor or not at all.

"Corvane is the hermit in the marshes," I said. "The guardian you're walking toward. And I need you to understand what he did before you meet him, because he will not explain it, and you deserve to walk in with your eyes open."

She set down her pen. "Go on."

"We gave him the fourth sigil because he was the most disciplined of us. The most principled. And for twenty years he kept it flawlessly. Then the Guild found him — not with soldiers, with *arguments*. They showed him the far side, Ansel. They opened the gate a crack and made him look at what was actually over there. The gray. The hunger. The ruins full of people who had been waiting generations for a covenant their neighbors' grandchildren had forgotten how to honor. And then they showed him our side — the plenitude, the surplus, the years we were burning like kindling — and they asked him the question that breaks careful men: *which world deserves to live?*"

"What did he say?"

"He said nothing. That was the answer. He handed over the sigil's location and walked into the marshes to become its own punishment. He keeps the stone still — the Guild never bothered to retrieve it, why would they, they'd already taken what mattered — but he keeps it the way a man guards the door of a house he burned down. He will give it to you, if you ask rightly. But he will also give you his confession, and it weighs more than the stone, and you will have to carry both."

Ansel was silent for a long moment. Outside the broken windows of the counting house, Candlemaker's Row went about its evening — lamplighters, cart wheels, someone arguing cheerfully about the price of tallow. The ordinary world, going on atop our extraordinary rot.

"And you," she said finally. "The fifth sigil. What did you pay?"

I had been dreading this question the way the drowning dread the surface — knowing it must come, knowing the air would hurt.

"I didn't pay," I said. "That was the problem."

* * *

I carried the fifth sigil myself, back toward the guildhouse, intending to rehide it somewhere new. The Guild caught me on the causeway at low tide. Four enforcers and Captain Vess's predecessor, a man named Halloway whose name deserves to be remembered only so it can be despised.

They did not torture me. They did something more efficient. They held the sigil against my chest and let it drink directly — no chosen price, no dignified transaction, just a stone pulling whatever it could reach. And what it reached for first, what it pulled out of me root and branch, was my memory of the far realm. Every color of it. The pale gold light. The sound the fold-line makes when it hums. The faces of the people on the other side who had waved to me, once, through the almost-open gate, believing we were keeping faith with them.

I fought it. I want that recorded somewhere besides my own ink. I fought it, and lost, and in the losing I did the one unforgivable clever thing left to me: I poured what remained of myself into my maps. All of them. Every chart and margin note and smuggled sketch I had ever drawn, compressed into a single master copy, because a man who cannot remember can still *record*, and recording is a kind of defiance.

Then Halloway put a knife in me, and the tide came in, and that should have been the end.

Except. My apprentice found the map. A courier boy I had been training — quick, stubborn, too curious for his own safety, the exact qualities that get couriers killed. He carried the map for eleven years, moving it, hiding it, learning its secrets, until the Guild caught up with him in an alley off the counting quarter with a blade in his gut and enough life left for one decision.

He chose you, Ansel. Out of everyone in that crowded street, bleeding out at the weighing hour, he pressed my map into the hands of the guild's own ledger-keeper. I have wondered why. I think — I think he read people the way I read coastlines, and he saw in you the same thing I see now. Someone who balances accounts because she believes accounts *should* balance. Someone who would notice that the guild's books have been lying for two hundred years, and would be unable, physically unable, to let it stand.

"You're describing a fool," Ansel said, but her voice had gone rough.

"I'm describing a keeper," I said. "The covenant needs one. It has needed one for eleven years. Possibly longer."

She looked down at her hands — ink-stained, precise, an accountant's hands — as if seeing them for the first time as instruments rather than habits.

"The first sigil," she said at last, in her practical voice, the one she used to keep herself from feeling things. "Under the harbor. In the debtor's prison. Past a warden who buys truths."

"The drowned archive. Third aisle from the west stair. Ask for the cell with the warden's name scratched off the door."

"When?"

"Soon. The tide serves you twice a day and the Guild serves you never. And Ansel—" I let the ink brighten along the map's edges, the closest I could come to taking her hand. "When you bargain with the warden, don't rehearse. Whatever truth you spend down there, spend it raw. Polished truths are counterfeit, and counterfeit gets you thrown out — or worse, kept."

She nodded, snuffed the candle, and stood in the dark a moment longer than she needed to.

"For what it's worth," she said to the room, to me, to no one, "I'm sorry they took your colors."

Eleven years dead, and that sentence undid me more thoroughly than the knife had. Ink, as it turns out, can blur.

By dawn she was gone — down toward the harbor with my map against her ribs and her notebook full of my ghosts, walking toward a flooded prison to buy a stone with a piece of herself. I settled into the calfskin and did the only work left to the dead.

I waited. And I hoped. And I began, in the smallest corner of the margin, to draw the route she would need next — because whatever the warden's price, whatever Odo's sorrow, whatever waited in the water below the city —

she was going to need to know the way back out.
