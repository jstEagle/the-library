## 7. The Drowned Archive

The harbor prison had no name that anyone still used. On Ansel's map — on Ilex's map, she corrected herself, though the ink had begun to feel like hers — it was marked only with a small blue sigil and a line of cramped script: *The Drowned Archive. Bring something true.*

"Bring something true," Ansel repeated, standing at the edge of the old ferry landing as gray water lapped at pilings furred with weed. "That's not ominous at all."

"You say that," said the map, from inside her coat, "as though you have never once in your life brought something false anywhere."

"I'm an accountant."

"You're a fugitive who stole from her own guild and is currently impersonating a fishmonger's widow." The ink swirled thoughtfully. "I merely observe that truth may be a novel currency for you."

Ansel pulled her hood lower against the drizzle. Three days since the rooftops, two days since she'd found the smuggler's skiff Ilex had directed her to, one day of poling through the harbor's drowned quarter where the sea had swallowed streets whole a generation ago and nobody had ever quite gotten around to grieving them properly. Her arms ached. Her boots had given up any pretense of dryness somewhere around dawn.

"I was honest," she said. "Painfully honest. Ask anyone who ever received my quarterly reconciliations."

"Oh, I believe you're honest about *numbers*," Ilex said. "Numbers are easy. Numbers don't look at you while you lie to them. Corvane will ask you about yourself, and that, my dear ledger-keeper, is a different arithmetic entirely."

The ferry landing ended at a doorway standing alone in the shallows — a stone arch, its door long rotted away, steps descending into green-black water. But the water didn't behave like water. It stood in the stairwell like glass in a window, flat and faintly luminous, holding back as if behind a pane.

"The Warden keeps the flood out by agreement," Ilex said, his voice quieter now. She'd noticed he did that near places that mattered, as if loudness might wake something. "The archive below is dry. Mostly. The bargain is the toll."

"And the toll?"

"That's rather the point of a bargain, isn't it? You find out when you pay it."

Ansel looked at the standing water for a long moment. Somewhere beneath it, in a prison that had drowned but refused to die, waited the second sigil — one of five pieces of a covenant older than the guild, older than the city, older, possibly, than the concept of things being older than other things, which Ilex insisted was itself a covenant-era invention.

She thought about turning back. She thought about it honestly, thoroughly, the way she'd once evaluated a suspicious column of figures — weighing the risk, the alternatives, the certain misery ahead.

Then she thought about the pale light rising from the sealed well beneath the guildhouse, and the masters drinking it, and the starving world on the far side of the gate, and she stepped down onto the first submerged stair.

The water parted around her ankles like a curtain drawn by a very polite servant, and closed behind her without a ripple.

* * *

The Drowned Archive smelled of salt and candlewax and old paper, which Ansel found, to her mild horror, comforting. She'd spent thirty years among ledgers; apparently even drowning couldn't kill the smell of them, or her love for it.

The great hall stretched farther than the prison's footprint should have allowed — rows of iron shelving rising three stories, every shelf crammed with books, scrolls, tablets, bundles of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a metronome. Lanterns burned in niches without visible oil, their flames burning low and blue-green, like the light she'd seen rising from the well.

At the far end, behind a desk built from what appeared to be an entire ship's prow, sat Warden Corvane.

He was not what she expected. She'd imagined something monstrous — the guild's stories of the harbor warden involved tentacles, or a face like a locked door, or both. Instead he was a spare, gray man of indeterminate age, dressed in a warden's coat so old its buttons had been replaced with coins from four different kingdoms. He wore spectacles. He was mending a book with thread and a bone needle, and he did not look up when her footsteps echoed across the hall.

"You're dripping on my floor," he said.

"There's an ocean outside your front door."

"Which is why the floor matters." He finished his stitch, bit the thread, and raised his eyes. They were pale, unremarkable eyes, and they went through her the way her own fingers went through a ledger — line by line, looking for the entry that didn't balance. "You came by the north stairs. Alone. No guards, no bribe-bearers, no guild colors. Which means either you're very brave, very foolish, or very poor. In my experience the categories overlap more than people admit."

"I've come for the second sigil."

"Everyone who comes here has come for the second sigil." He set down his needle. "You'd be astonished how few of them leave with it. Most leave with nothing. Some leave with less than nothing, which is a thing I can arrange, having had several centuries of practice." He gestured to the chair opposite his desk. It was a good chair. That unsettled her more than the tentacles would have. "Sit. Let's establish what you are."

Ansel sat. "What are the terms? For the sigil."

"Straight to commerce. Good. I hate the ones who want to chat about the weather first — we're under the sea, madam, the weather is a settled question." He laced his fingers. "Here are the terms, and they do not change, and they have not changed since before your guild had a name. I trade in truths. Not secrets — secrets are just lies wearing nice coats. *Truths.* Things the speaker knows to be true, has verified, has paid some cost to know. The price of the sigil is a truth of sufficient weight. Weight is determined by me. Appeals are heard by me. The court is me. Any questions?"

"What happens if I offer something insufficient?"

"Then you keep your truth, I keep my sigil, and you swim home." A thin smile. "I am not a robber, Keeper Winterbourne. I am a merchant. Robbery is for people without patience."

Her stomach dropped. "How do you know my name?"

"You've been carrying a dead cartographer's map around the harbor for two days, asking questions in fish markets. The fish talk. Everything talks, down here. It's the damp." He leaned back. "So. The Meridian Guild's chief ledger-keeper, wanted for theft, comes to my door asking after a piece of the covenant. I could sell that information to Captain Vess right now for a frankly embarrassing sum. Instead I'm hearing your offer. Curious, isn't it, what that tells you about my prices."

It told her that Corvane wanted something the guild couldn't give him. She filed that away the way she filed everything — carefully, in a drawer she could find later in the dark.

"I saw something," she began, then stopped. Started again. "Three nights ago I went down to return a map to the masters. Instead I saw—" And there it was, the wall. The well of pale light, the masters bent over it like drinkers at a trough, the sound they made — she had the whole of it in her memory, vivid and terrible, and she found she could not push it out of her mouth. Because saying it would make it real in a new way. Saying it to a stranger, aloud, in a voice, would mean she could never again pretend she'd misread the accounts.

Corvane watched her struggle with the expression of a man watching rain arrive exactly when the almanac promised.

"Take your time," he said. "Truth often gets stuck on the way out. Like a fishbone."

"Ilex," said the map at her breast, mildly, "told you. Fishbone."

"Hush," said Ansel and Corvane together, and then regarded each other with the first flicker of mutual respect.

* * *

She tried three times.

First she offered the fact of her flight — how she'd stolen the sigil-stone, escaped over the salt roofs, evaded Vess. Corvane listened, nodded politely, and said, "A fine adventure. Worth perhaps a cup of tea. Next."

Then she offered the map itself — its existence, its ghost, the courier's death. That earned a longer pause, and a gleam of genuine interest, and then: "Secondhand. You know it because Ilex told you, and Ilex is not here to stand behind it. Truths need witnesses, madam, or they're just well-dressed rumors. Next."

The third time she offered a truth about herself — that she had loved the guild, truly loved it, had believed its ledgers were the honest bones of an honest city. Corvane's eyebrows rose a fraction.

"Now that has weight," he admitted. "But it's weight in the wrong direction. Regret is cheap, Keeper. Everyone regrets; the market's flooded with it." He picked his needle back up and resumed mending, which she understood was a dismissal, and also a dare.

Ansel sat in the good chair in the drowned archive and did sums.

She was good at sums. All her life she had been good at sums, and she had told herself it was because numbers were clean, because a number never flinched, because in a world of merchants and masters and men with swords, the ledger was the one place where a small, plain woman with ink-stained fingers held absolute power. Every figure answered to her. Nothing got past her. That had been her pride, her armor, her entire self, folded neatly into a single professional virtue.

And underneath it — she saw it now with terrible clarity, the way you see the true total at the bottom of a column once someone else points at it — underneath it, the numbers had been a place to hide. Years sold. Names traded. Entries that shouldn't exist. She had seen them for years and she had balanced around them, adjusted, footnoted, told herself the masters must have reasons, that a ledger-keeper's job was accuracy and not judgment. She had been accurate the way a knife is accurate.

The truth she had to spend wasn't about the well. It was about herself.

"I knew," Ansel said.

Corvane's needle stopped.

"For six years, at least, I knew something was wrong in the accounts. Not the shape of it — I didn't know about the gate, or the draining, I swear that on whatever you hold sacred, which appears to be bookbinding. But there were entries. Payments marked 'maintenance' that went nowhere. Tithes recorded twice. Deaths entered as retirements. I saw them, and I reconciled them anyway, because balancing the book was my whole religion and I was its most devout idiot." Her voice shook. She let it. "I didn't lie. That was always my excuse — I never lied, I only declined to look. But a ledger-keeper who declines to look is worse than a liar, because a liar at least knows what they're doing. So there. That's my truth. I was the guild's best servant precisely because I was willing not to see."

The hall was silent except for the patient drip of water.

Corvane set down the needle. He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with great care, and put them back on, and Ansel realized she was watching him buy time because he was moved and didn't care to show it.

"Do you know," he said, "how many people have sat in that chair and offered me truths? Kings offering their conquests. Priests offering their doubts. Lovers offering their betrayals, wrapped up so prettily you'd want to frame them. And do you know what nearly all of those truths had in common?" He didn't wait for an answer. "They were all about *other people*. Everyone who comes to me has a magnificent inventory of everyone else's sins. It's the rarest thing in the world, madam, to meet someone carrying a truth about themselves that they haven't yet repackaged as somebody else's fault."

He opened a drawer. From it he lifted a small case of black wood, and from the case, a sigil-stone — twin to the one warm against Ansel's ribs, but this one carved with a spiral like water going down a drain, or a whirlpool, or a galaxy; she supposed it depended on whether you were an optimist.

"The second sigil," Corvane said. "Paid in full."

Ansel reached for it and stopped. "Why?" she asked. "You could have sold me out to Vess. You said so yourself. What does the guild's money not buy that my confession did?"

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then Corvane looked past her, at the endless shelves, at the drowned records of a drowned prison, and his voice went quiet in the way Ilex's did near important places.

"Four hundred years ago I was the warden of this prison when it was merely a prison, and I took a bribe to lose a prisoner's file, and a man went to the gallows whose pardon was sitting in my desk drawer the whole time." He turned the coin-button on his coat with one finger. "I've been underwater ever since. Chose it, mostly. The sea is very good at not letting you forget." His pale eyes came back to her. "I collect truths because I am owed one, madam, and no one has ever paid. Your confession doesn't settle my account. But it's the first entry I've seen in centuries that makes me think the ledger might someday balance."

He slid the case across the desk.

"Two stones now," he said. "Three to find, and the last one sits in the guildhouse vault, behind doors that eat thieves. And between you and all of it stands Captain Vess, who is, I'm told, two tides behind you and gaining." He smiled thinly. "I mention this not as warning but as commerce. Consider it a free sample. My prices go up when clients are surprised."

* * *

Ansel climbed the north stairs with the water parting before her and closing behind, two sigils against her ribs like coals, and the strange new feeling still settling over her shoulders — not lighter, exactly. Confession hadn't lifted anything. If anything she carried more now: the well, the masters, her own six years of willful blindness, all of it packed tight and permanent.

But it felt like *load-bearing* weight. Like a wall that held the roof up.

"Well," said Ilex, as she stepped out into the gray drizzle of the drowned quarter. "You gave away your worst truth and walked out owning two-fifths of a covenant. There's a lesson in economics there, but I'm too proud of you to teach it."

"Don't be sentimental, it's unsettling in a map."

"Noted. Also — and I say this with enormous affection — you should probably start running."

"Why?"

"Because," said the map, "the gulls on that roof behind you have been sitting very still for ten minutes, and gulls, my dear, are Vess's scouts, and she pays them in bread."

Ansel looked up. Four gulls regarded her with the flat professionalism of tax assessors.

She ran.
