## 8. Vess Closes the Net

Captain Sera Vess had pursued thieves through the salt roofs of Meridian City for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned one iron law of the trade: everyone leaves a trail. The clumsy left footprints. The clever left rumors. The very clever left nothing at all — which was itself a trail, if you knew how to read silence.

Ansel Winterbourne, it turned out, was a silence-reader's nightmare and a ledger-keeper's gift. She left *records*.

Vess stood in the back room of a chandler's shop on Rope Street at an hour when honest people were still asleep, holding a receipt.

It was a receipt for candles. That was not the interesting part. The interesting part was that Ansel Winterbourne — fugitive, thief of a sigil-stone worth more than the street it had been stolen over, wanted by four masters of the Meridian Guild — had *paid*. Full price. In guild scrip, which was nearly as good as coin and twice as traceable, and she had signed the chandler's book with her own name in her own neat, upright hand, the same hand Vess had spent three years approving on expense ledgers before either of them knew what the other was.

*One dozen tallow, two beeswax (for reading), paid in full. A. Winterbourne.*

"For reading," Vess said aloud, to the empty shop.

The chandler's apprentice, who had been pretending very hard to be asleep on his cot in the corner, gave up the pretense. "She said her eyes were going," he offered. "Said she needed proper light to work by. Worked all night, she did. Never touched the good candle, just the tallow. Left the beeswax burning down like she'd forgot it."

"Did she now." Vess turned the receipt over. "And where did she go?"

The apprentice shrugged with the elaborate innocence of a boy deciding how much trouble he wanted to be in. "Didn't say. But she asked after the harbor stairs. Asked whether the drowned archive flooded at spring tide or only at king tide." He paused. "Asked it casual-like. Nobody asks about Corvane's prison casual-like."

"No," Vess agreed. "Nobody does."

She thanked him, told him he'd been no help whatsoever, and pressed a coin into his hand anyway, because that was how you kept informants: you lied to them warmly and paid them honestly. Then she stepped out into the blue-gray hour before dawn, where the city smelled of salt and cooling bread, and allowed herself, for exactly ten seconds, to feel what she felt.

Which was this: she didn't want to catch Ansel Winterbourne.

That was a problem. Vess was very good at wanting to catch people. It was practically the whole of her personality, according to her lieutenant, who had once bought her a mug painted with the words WORLD'S OKAYEST HUNTER as a joke and had spent a week on latrine duty for it. Wanting to catch people was simple. They ran; you followed; the world stayed sorted into its proper columns.

But Ansel had run across the salt roofs with a stolen stone in her fist and something in her face that Vess kept seeing when she closed her eyes — not the look of a thief caught thieving. The look of a woman who had seen something in a cellar and could not unsee it.

*What did you see below the guildhouse?*

Vess had asked herself that question every night for six days, and every night she had answered it the way a good soldier answers: *Nothing that concerns me. The masters keep what they keep.* And every morning the answer fit worse, like a boot resoled too many times, lumpy where it should have been smooth.

* * *

The harbor stairs went down farther than most citizens ever thought about, past the fish markets and the customs house, past the level where the lamplighters stopped bothering, down to doors that were older than the city's name. The Warden Corvane kept a prison down there that was also an archive, because Corvane had concluded long ago — so the stories went — that information was the only sentence anyone truly served, and he might as well collect it while they served it.

Vess did not go down as herself. She went down as a debt-collector's clerk, which required only a borrowed coat, a forged tally-book, and the ability to look bored, all three of which she possessed in abundance.

Corvane received her in a room lit by green glass lamps, behind a desk made of a ship's stern, and looked at her for a long moment with eyes like coins left in water.

"You're no clerk," he said pleasantly.

"I'm a captain of guild enforcers," Vess said, "and I'd take it kindly if you didn't shout about it."

"And why would I?" Corvane folded his hands. "Guild business is my favorite kind. It always pays double." He tilted his head. "You're hunting the Winterbourne woman."

"I'm asking whether she came here."

"She came here." He let the silence do some work. "She came here and she sat where you're sitting and she offered me a trade. Do you know what she offered?"

"Tell me."

"A truth." Corvane smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "A true thing, freely given, witnessed and weighed. That is my currency, captain. Coin is for people who have nothing better. And do you know what truth she brought me?"

Vess's mouth had gone dry somewhere around the word *truth*. "No."

"That the masters of the Meridian Guild are drawing life out of the far side of the gate." He said it lightly, the way a man announces the weather. "That they have been doing it for forty years. That the covenant between the realms is failing — not fraying, not aging, but being *eaten* — and that every lamp in your fine guildhouse burns a little brighter for it while the world on the other side goes gray and hungry." He leaned forward. "She traded me that truth for a sigil-stone. And here is the remarkable part, captain. Here is why I tell you, knowing you'll carry it straight back to your masters."

"Which is?"

"It *weighed*." Corvane tapped the desk, twice, softly. "Truths weigh differently depending on the teller. A lie dressed as truth has no heft at all. A truth told grudgingly weighs light. But that woman set that truth on my scale and it sank like an anchor. She wasn't guessing. She wasn't bargaining with rumor. She had *seen*." His coin-bright eyes fixed on Vess. "So the only question that interests me is: have you?"

* * *

Vess had, as it happened, seen something. Six days ago, in the corridor below the counting room, she had seen the masters coming up from the sealed well with their sleeves rolled and their hands pale as peeled fruit, and Master Ellery-Quist had smiled at her and said, "Routine maintenance, Captain," and she had saluted and walked away, because that was what a good soldier did.

She had also, in the last six days, done something she had never done in eleven years of service. She had opened files she wasn't cleared for.

Not dramatically. Nothing so vulgar as breaking into the vault. Vess simply read everything that crossed her desk properly for the first time — the requisitions, the supply tallies, the medical reports from the gate-wardens' quarter — and then she read them *together*, the way Ansel Winterbourne would have read them, crosswise, looking for the column that didn't balance.

The lamp-oil was the first thing. The guildhouse burned more oil than any building in the city, including the cathedral, including the lighthouse. Where did it go? Downstairs. Every barrel, down the well-stair, month after month, decade after decade, entered in the ledgers under *maintenance*.

The second thing was the wardens. Gate-wardens served five-year postings. Their service records were immaculate. Their pension records were blank. Vess had pulled forty years of warden rolls and found that of the sixty-one men and women posted to the deep quarter, fifty-eight had died in service, and the cause of death, in every single case, was recorded in the same tidy hand with the same three words:

*Failed. Natural causes.*

Fifty-eight people did not fail naturally in identical ways. Vess had sat with those pages for a long time in the enforcers' ready-room while the night shift snored around her, and she had felt the iron law of her own life bend — the law that said the world was sorted into columns, hers and theirs, lawful and unlawful — and she had heard it creak.

Because there was a third column. There had always been a third column. It was the one where the people who wrote the ledgers decided what the columns meant, and everyone else saluted and walked away.

"Routine maintenance," she said aloud, to the sleeping room, and heard for the first time how it sounded.

* * *

The order came on the seventh day, delivered by a runner with clean boots and dirty eyes, sealed with the masters' triple-spiral wax.

Vess broke it in her office with the door shut.

*CAPTAIN VESS. The fugitive Winterbourne is confirmed in possession of two covenant sigils and intelligence damaging to guild security. Capture is no longer practicable. She is to be terminated on contact. Confirmation of termination to be rendered to the undersigned within the day. The guild thanks you for your long and faithful service.*

There was more, in the way such letters always had more — a line about discretion, a line about her pension, a line about the unfortunate necessity of certainty — but Vess read it three times and the letter never got past *terminated on contact*.

She put it down. She picked it up. She held it over the candle, and then she didn't burn it, because burning things was what guilty people did, and she was not yet sure what she was.

Instead she did the sensible thing, the thing she suspected Ansel Winterbourne would do, which was to file it. She copied the order into her casebook in her own neat hand, dated it, noted the runner's description, and locked the original in the strongbox under the false bottom where she kept the evidence against enforcers she hadn't yet decided what to do about. Then she sat back and looked at the ceiling and laughed, once, without much humor in it.

Eleven years. Eleven years of chasing cutpurses and smugglers and one truly memorable man who had stolen a bridge — not from it, the whole bridge, a story for another time — and the first order she had ever refused was the easiest one she'd ever received. Find the woman. Kill the woman. No trial, no weighing, no column for the masters' own accounts.

*Terminated on contact,* because Ansel Winterbourne had seen the cellar and told the truth about it, and truths, in the guildhouse, were the one commodity that had never once appeared in the ledgers.

Vess took out a fresh page. At the top she wrote, in the careful script she used for testimony: *Case of A. Winterbourne — personal notes, not for file.* Underneath, after some thought, she wrote:

*Day 7. Subject observed paying full price for candles. Subject confessed a true thing to Warden Corvane that weighed like an anchor. Masters have ordered her death rather than her capture. Conclusion: we are not hunting a thief. We are hunting a witness. Correction: I am no longer hunting anything. I am following.*

She looked at that last line for a long while. Then, because she was who she was, she added a fourth line:

*Also she owes the guild twelve tallow candles and two beeswax, per the chandler's book, and I intend to collect.*

It was not much of a joke. It was, however, the first joke she had made in six days, and it settled something in her chest, the way a keel settles a boat.

* * *

She found Ansel on the eighth day, or rather she found where Ansel had been, which was a marsh-edge ferryman's hut with a cold fire and a bandage wrapper in the corner spotted brown, and tracks leading west into the reed-flats toward the old causeway. Wounded, then. Moving slow. The net could close today, if Vess reported the trail.

She stood at the hut door with her whistle in her hand. One blast would bring the harbor patrol. Two blasts, the full cordon. Eleven years of instinct sat in her palm, small and silver and certain.

In the fire's ashes, half-buried, lay a scrap of paper — a page torn from a ledger, covered in that neat upright hand. Vess crouched and eased it free. It was an account, of a kind. A list of names. Wardens' names. Fifty-eight of them, each with a date, each with the same three words written beside it in the guild's tidy hand — and beside each, in Ansel's writing, a single added line: *Who mourns him? Who was told?*

Nobody. Nobody had been told. Vess had checked.

She put the whistle away.

Then she tore a page from her own casebook, and on it she wrote the only message she could think of that was both true and safe, which took some thinking, and propped it on the cold hearth where a careful woman would be sure to look:

*Winterbourne — Your candle account is outstanding. Settle it at the bell-tower landing, midnight tomorrow, alone. Bring the stones. I'm not bringing the patrol. Ask yourself what I'd want with dead wardens' names and decide whether you trust me. You paid full price for candles. I'm betting you pay full price for everything. — V.*

She weighted it with a pebble, stepped back out into the gray morning, and stood a moment at the treeline, listening to the reeds talk to each other about the wind.

Somewhere west, a wounded woman was walking toward a causeway with two sigils and a ghost in her map, and somewhere east, the masters were waiting for confirmation of a killing that was not going to arrive. Between the two, walking neither direction yet, stood a captain with a bent iron law, a strongbox full of evidence, and — she admitted it now, quietly, the way you admit a debt — a growing, inconvenient, entirely unsoldierly hope that she would reach Ansel Winterbourne before the guild's other hunters did.

Because the runner with the clean boots had not come alone. Clean-boots runners never did.

Far off across the flats, faint on the wind, Vess heard a whistle blow twice — the full cordon — and began, without hurrying, to run.
