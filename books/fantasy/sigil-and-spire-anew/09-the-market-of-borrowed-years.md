## 9. The Market of Borrowed Years

The map had gone quiet since the causeway, but it still gave directions in the way a dying man gives directions — reluctantly, and only when you jostled him.

"Third sigil," I murmured to the ink that night, huddled under the eaves of a chandlery where the tar smell covered my own. "Where."

The lines of the harbor district crawled and rearranged themselves until one street stood out, dark as a vein: Gullsway Row. Beneath it, a single word surfaced in Ilex's careful hand, written small, as though he hoped I wouldn't read it too closely:

*The Tallow Market. Don't go hungry. Don't go honest.*

"Ilex," I said, "those are contradictory instructions for me specifically."

The ink shimmered, which was as close as he came to laughing anymore.

* * *

Getting into the Tallow Market turned out to be the easy part. The hard part was getting in *correctly*.

I had imagined a cellar, something furtive, men in oilskins whispering over contraband candles. What I found beneath Gullsway Row was a cathedral of commerce. The smugglers had hollowed out an old cistern and filled it with stalls, lanterns, cook-smoke, and noise — hundreds of people trading in the one commodity the Meridian Guild had taught this city to crave above all others.

Time.

Not the abstract kind. The kind you could hold. At the first stall, a woman with jeweled fingers decanted years from stoppered flasks into brass hourglasses while her customers watched with the particular hunger of people who have been told they are running out. Beside her, a clerk weighed grayish crystals on tiny scales — *harvested hours*, the placard read, *ethically sourced* — and I thought of the pale light rising from the well below the guildhouse, and had to stand very still and breathe through my nose until the fury settled somewhere I could carry it.

Because here was the thing the guild's ledgers had never shown me, not once in eleven years of balancing impossible accounts: the other end of every trade. In the counting room, years appeared as entries. *Received: forty annum-equivalents, provenance sealed.* Provenance sealed. Of course it was sealed. Nobody wants to know what a year looks like when it's been taken instead of given.

It looked like the boy at the third stall down.

He couldn't have been more than ten, sitting cross-legged on an overturned crate behind a tray of small clay vessels, each one labeled in a child's uneven hand: *GOOD HOURS. WARM ONES. SUNNY MORNING, SLIGHTLY USED.* He had a salesman's patter already worn smooth with use.

"Hours! Fresh hours! Grandmother's afternoon nap, barely touched!" He caught me looking and his face did something complicated — the professional smile staying put while his eyes took my measure, my guild-trained posture, the burn scar on my wrist from the salt roofs. "You're not buying," he concluded.

"I'm browsing," I said.

"Browsing is free," he allowed, magnanimous. "Buying costs money. Stealing costs fingers." He wiggled his own ten fingers at me as proof of his professional standards. "What are you, then? You've got clerk hands. Ink under the nails. But clerks don't come down here unless they're off the books, and off-the-books clerks always look like they're about to be sick."

"I'm not sick."

"You're green around the gills, miss. That's sick, or that's guilty." He tilted his head. "Which?"

"Tam!"

The barker from the next stall — a broad woman with forearms like ship rope — leaned over and cuffed the air near his ear without touching him. "Leave the lady be. And if she *is* buying, mind your prices, we talked about this."

"He's robbing himself," I said.

"He's robbing himself *enthusiastically*," she agreed, and went back to her customers.

Tam studied me a moment longer, then patted the crate beside him. An invitation. I sat, because my legs were tired and because there is no safer place in any city than beside a child who has decided you're interesting rather than dangerous.

"You want the third sigil," he said, quietly enough that the words didn't travel past our two sets of knees.

Every muscle I owned decided to become stone at once. I made them wait. "Why would you think that?"

"'Cause the last three people who asked me that exact question in that exact voice were guild spies, and they all bought the same fake directions, and none of them came back." He shrugged. "You haven't asked yet. So. Do you want it?"

"How do you know what I want?"

"Everybody down here knows what the sigils are." He said it simply, the way you'd say *everybody knows water's wet*. "The market runs on time, and the sigils are what time *is*, sort of, underneath. Old story. My gran used to tell it before—" He stopped. Picked up one of his little clay pots. Put it down again. "Before."

The word *before* sat between us like a coin neither of us wanted to pick up.

"They harvest here," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd seen it already — the alcoves behind the stalls where people sat in rows, plugged by thin glass needles to the flasks, their faces slack and older than their clothes suggested. Voluntary, the signs claimed. *Fair rates. Sell your surplus.*

"Some of 'em," Tam said. His voice went flat and old, a ledger voice, an accounting voice. "Some sell a bit when rent's due. Honest trade, mostly, that lot. But there's a crew works the north tunnels — the Gray Tithe, folks call 'em. They don't ask first." He looked up at me, and his eyes were doing that thing children's eyes do when they have seen far more than they should and have organized it into something useful so it can't hurt them quite so much. "You're going after the Tithe, aren't you. Or the ones above them. Same thing, really. Everyone upstairs thinks everyone downstairs is the problem."

"And what do you think?"

"I think," Tam said, "that upstairs and downstairs both drink from the same well, and nobody ever asks the well." He held up one of his pots. "These are real, by the way. Hours I gathered fair — mornings, mostly, good ones, from folk who wanted to give. Gran taught me gathering before she taught me anything else." He smiled suddenly, and it transformed his whole sharp little face. "She said a stolen hour sours. You can taste it. A given hour keeps forever."

*Ilex,* I thought, *you absolute ghost, you've been dead sixty years and your friends' grandchildren are still selling your theology in a hole under Gullsway Row.*

"I need to reach the bell tower," I said. "The third sigil hangs in the great bell. I'm told the way is watched."

"The way is *tunneled*," Tam corrected. "Everything under this city is tunneled; the guild just pretends otherwise so people keep paying bridge tolls like mugs." He was quiet a moment, doing arithmetic behind his eyes. "I know the tunnels. I also know what happens to guides who help strangers. Ask the last guide."

"Who was the last guide?"

"Exactly."

* * *

We walked the market together while he thought, or perhaps while he let me think he was thinking, and what I saw made the fury settle deeper into its carrying place, heavier now, familiar as a pack strap.

An old man sold thirty years in one transaction and walked out of the alcove with white hair and a springy step, delighted, having understood exactly nothing about what he'd done. A young mother haggled fiercely over a single flask — *one winter, that's all I need, just to get through the winter* — and the merchant quoted her a price with a straight face that should have been illegal. In the corner, a guild factor in undyed wool — deliberately plain, deliberately anonymous, I knew the costume, I'd helped write the dress-code memo — bought bulk at a discount, his ledger open on his knee, his pen moving with the same small competence mine moved with, once, when my worst sin was rounding errors.

That was the part that finally lit the furnace properly. Not the cruelty. Cruelty I had budgeted for. It was the *bookkeeping*. Somewhere above us, in a clean room smelling of cedar and sealing wax, these transactions were being entered in columns I might once have balanced. *Received: one winter, maternal, distressed seller, rate standard.* And some version of me — some Ansel Winterbourne who had never pressed a dying courier's map against her ribs — would have checked the sums, found them correct, initialled the page, and gone home to supper feeling that the world was in order.

"Miss," Tam said, watching my face. "You've got your furious look on."

"I have a furious look?"

"Oh, a proper one. Like a kettle deciding." He nodded approval. "Good. Kettles get things done. Sad teacups just sit there."

Despite everything — genuinely despite everything — I laughed. It came out cracked from disuse, but it was a laugh.

"Right," he said, as though my laughter had settled some final term of the contract. "Tunnels. Two conditions."

"Name them."

"One: you take me with you as far as the Bell-Keeper's stair, then I go home, and you tell no one I helped you. Not the tribunal-types you're clearly headed toward becoming, not anyone."

"Why the secrecy? Helping me could make you a hero someday."

"Heroes get named." He said it with the weary wisdom of someone quoting a lesson learned at cost. "Named people's sisters get visited by men with questions. Condition two." He picked up one of his clay pots — the smallest, the plainest, unlabeled — and pressed it into my hand. It was warm, absurdly warm, like a stone left in sunlight. Inside it, I could feel something patient turning over, the way bread rises. "A given hour. For the road. Gran's rule: never walk into the dark on empty time. When you're spent — really spent, not tired, *spent* — unstop it and breathe deep. Don't waste it on a bad day."

"Tam—"

"It's not a gift," he said sternly. "Gifts create debt, debt creates ledgers, and I'm not letting you owe me anything, you're obviously the owing type. It's a *trade*. You're giving me something too."

"What am I giving me?"

"A story." His grin flashed. "When this is over — whatever 'over' means for people like you — you come back and tell me how it ended. Every bit. Even the embarrassing bits. *Especially* the embarrassing bits."

"That's a terrible price. I accept."

* * *

He led me down through the market's bones, past the alcoves and the flasks and the factor with his tidy pen, through a door marked *SPARE CANDLES — NO ADMITTANCE* that had clearly admitted many people many times, and into the tunnels. Down there the city's noise became a heartbeat overhead, and the lamp-glow of the Tallow Market fell away behind us, and it was just Tam's small sure footsteps and the dark and my own breath.

At a junction marked with chalk arrows in a child's hand — he had been mapping these passages himself, I realized, for years, the way other children collect marbles — he stopped.

"Bell-Keeper's stair is two turns past the flooded cistern," he said. "And miss?" All the salesman's shine was gone. What was left was younger and much braver. "The Keeper takes memories as payment. Everybody knows that. So before you go up those stairs, decide which one you can live without. Really decide. Because if you decide up there, at the top, tired and scared—" he tapped my satchel, where the map slept, where Ilex whispered — "you'll pick wrong. Pick with a cold heart. Warm hearts spend stupid."

For a moment I almost told him. Almost said: *I've been paying all along, Tam. I paid a brother's face once already and I couldn't even tell you what it bought, because the buying took the knowing too.*

Instead I said, "Thank you," which is what brave people say when the true answer is too heavy to lift.

"Go on, then." He shoved my shoulder gently, a dockworker's gesture learned from someone big. "Kettle."

I went. Behind me, his footsteps pattered away toward home, toward a sister he'd mentioned exactly once and guarded with every other word.

Two turns past the flooded cistern, I found the stair — and found, wedged into the crack of the lowest step, a scrap of paper in a handwriting I had hoped never to see again outside my nightmares. Neat. Official. Guild-issue block capitals.

*THERE IS A CHILD GUIDING HER,* it read. *FIND THE CHILD FIRST.*

The map burned hot against my ribs. Far below the city, faint as a memory of light, I heard the great bell begin to toll — and with it, fainter still, Ilex's voice, worn down to thread: *the Keeper remembers everything, Ansel. Choose before you climb.*

I had one cold-hearted decision to make, and now I had to make it twice: which memory to feed the Keeper, and how fast to climb before men with questions reached a boy on Gullsway Row.

I chose the stairs. I ran.
