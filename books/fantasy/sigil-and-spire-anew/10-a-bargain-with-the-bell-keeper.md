## 10. A Bargain with the Bell-Keeper

The Belltower of Saint Verity had no stairs.

That was the first thing Ansel learned about it, standing in the shadow of its base at dawn, craning her neck until the tower's crown dissolved into river mist. The second thing she learned was that Tam had known this and had said nothing, because Tam's philosophy of information was that it was like bread — you shared it when someone paid you, or when they were bleeding, and not one moment before.

"No stairs," Ansel said flatly.

"No stairs." The boy grinned up at her, all elbows and satisfaction. He'd led her through half of Meridian's underside since the market — through cisterns that smelled of old rain and newer secrets, past doors that only opened for people who didn't look like they needed them to. "There's a handhold every arm's length apart, all the way up. Old as the tower. You climb."

"Who built it?"

"Nobody remembers. That's the fun part." He said this with the particular relish of a child who found history delicious precisely because everyone else found it annoying. "The keeper pays me a copper for every climber who doesn't fall. So far it's been a very cheap arrangement for him."

"How many climbers?"

"Two. This year."

"And how many fell?"

"One," Tam said cheerfully, "but he was already dead, so the keeper argued it shouldn't count. They're still arguing. It's been four years."

Ansel looked at the tower again. It rose out of the oldest quarter of the city like a needle pulled from a wound, its stones black with centuries of soot and pigeon opinion. Somewhere near the top hung the Great Bell — Verity herself, the city called her — cast in an age when, if Ilex was to be believed, the covenant between the two realms still ran strong and bright through everything the founders built. The third sigil did not merely hang in the bell. According to the map's ghostly cartographer, the sigil *was* the bell's heart, the reason Verity's voice carried farther than any bell had a right to, the reason people swore oaths beneath her toll and mostly kept them.

"Remind me," Ansel said, "why we can't simply wait until the keeper goes out for groceries."

"The keeper hasn't gone out for groceries in living memory." Tam held out his palm, and Ansel dropped two coppers into it — one for the guidance, one for the silence he would now maintain about where she'd gone. "Also, I asked around. There were enforcers asking after a tall woman with ink-stained fingers at the harbor gate last night. Asking *politely*, which is worse."

Ah. Yes. Vess. Ansel flexed her bandaged forearm where the causeway ambush had left its mark — shallow, but a reminder that the captain of guild enforcers was not a woman who wasted movements. Vess had let her go on the causeway. Or failed to catch her. Ansel genuinely could not tell which, and the uncertainty itched more than the wound.

"Well then," she said. "Up I go."

* * *

The climbing took the better part of the morning, and it gave Ansel far too much time to think, which was the one activity she'd been avoiding with real dedication.

She thought about Warden Corvane in the drowned archive, weighing her confession of what she'd seen below the guildhouse — the masters drawing pale light from the sealed well, the gate itself, drinking life from a starving world — and finding it worth exactly one sigil. She thought about how telling that truth had felt less like losing something and more like setting down a weight she hadn't known she carried, and how suspicious that felt, because honest things were supposed to cost.

*They do cost,* Ilex had murmured from the map that night, his voice thinner than it used to be, ink fading like tea left too long. *You've just been paying in a coin you couldn't see yet.*

She thought about her brother, which was dangerous, so mostly she didn't.

By midday her arms burned and her palms were printed with the pattern of ancient handholds, and she hauled herself over the final lip into a chamber that smelled of dust, beeswax, and — unexpectedly — chamomile.

The top of the Belltower of Saint Verity was a single round room, open to the sky on all sides by arches hung with moth-eaten curtains. The Great Bell filled most of it: fifteen feet of green-black bronze, scarred and magnificent, hanging from a beam of wood so old it had gone the color of iron. And beneath the bell, in a chair that might once have been a throne and had definitely spent some recent decades as a laundry rack, sat the keeper.

She was small. That was the first shock. Ansel had imagined someone vast, ancient, carved from the same stone as the tower. Instead she was a tiny woman wrapped in three shawls of clashing patterns, with white hair cropped short and hands folded on her lap like a grandmother waiting for a bus. A teapot sat on a brazier beside her, and two cups waited, already poured.

"You took the long way up," the keeper said. Her voice was dry and pleasant, like pages turning. "Most do. The short way involves the outside wall, and I don't recommend it before lunch."

"There's a short way?"

"There is now. I invented it in 1189. Nobody uses it." She gestured at the second cup. "Sit. Tea first. Bargaining on an empty stomach is how wars start."

Ansel sat, because it seemed unwise not to, and because her legs had opinions of their own. The tea was chamomile, slightly too sweet, exactly the temperature of forgiveness.

"You know why I'm here," Ansel said.

"I know why you *think* you're here." The keeper sipped. "You think you're here for the third sigil. You are, technically, but that's like saying a drowning man is at the lake for the view."

From inside Ansel's coat, faintly, the map stirred. *Ask her name,* Ilex whispered. *If she tells you freely, the bargain will be fair. If she refuses—*

"She won't refuse," the keeper said, without looking at the coat. "My name is Odile. It has been Odile for a hundred and forty years, and I am tired of it in the way you get tired of your own hands. Tell your friend in the ink that I remember him. Tell him the fourth cup is still his, whenever he grows a body again."

The map went very quiet. Ansel felt it trembling against her ribs, the way a person trembles when they are trying very hard not to feel something.

"He can't come," Ansel said softly.

"None of us can come back. That's rather the point of the whole arrangement, isn't it?" Odile set down her cup and folded her hands again, and something in her face shifted — the pleasantness remained, but beneath it appeared the thing that had kept this tower for a century and a half. "So. You want Verity's heart. You have two sigils already; I can smell them on you, salt and drowned paper. You want the third, and you want it without paying full price, because that's what everyone wants, and it's why the world is in the state it's in."

"I'll pay," Ansel said. "What's the price?"

"Everyone always asks that way." Odile clicked her tongue. "*What's* the price. As though the price were a number. A memory, child. One memory, freely given, and it must be a load-bearing one — a memory that holds up part of who you are, so that when I take it, something in you settles differently. Small memories are no use to the bell. Verity rings true or she doesn't ring at all, and she hasn't rung true in sixty years because the last fool who climbed up here paid me with the memory of a dog. A lovely dog! A golden dog! Worthless. The bell wouldn't take it. I've been holding it in escrow ever since." She pointed at a small jar on the shelf behind her, which did indeed seem to contain a faint golden glow shaped vaguely like a wagging tail.

Ansel stared at the jar despite herself. "What happens to the memories? After."

"They become the bell's voice. Every true toll of Verity is made of what people gave up to keep her ringing. When she tolled the plague-year's end, that was a woman's memory of her wedding. When she tolled the firebreak, that was a mason's memory of his master's hands teaching his." Odile shrugged, shawls shifting. "Nothing is lost, exactly. It's just... repurposed. Like me."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave the way you came, and the sigil stays, and the covenant fails, and both worlds go gray and hungry, and eventually somebody braver or stupider climbs up here and pays properly." Odile smiled gently. "No pressure. There never is. That's the whole cruelty of it — free will is only bearable if nobody reminds you it's operating."

*Choose,* Ilex breathed. *But choose knowing. Ask what she took from the others.*

"The courier," Ansel said. "The one who brought me the map. Did he climb here?"

Odile was silent for a moment. The wind moved the curtains; somewhere below, the city went on being the city, unaware.

"He tried to pay with his memory of me," she said at last. "His teacher. His friend. Sentimental boy — thought it would be the largest thing he owned." Her eyes crinkled. "It wasn't accepted either. Too fresh, too raw, not enough *structure*. In the end he paid with the memory of the day he first understood what the guild was doing to the other side. The day his innocence died. Verity took it gladly. He walked down those handholds lighter and colder, and he spent the rest of his life trying to buy back the feeling of not-knowing, and failing, and doing the work anyway." She looked at Ansel steadily. "That is what a load-bearing memory costs, Keeper-to-be. Not tears. Architecture."

*Ilex,* Ansel said silently, *what do I—*

*It must be yours alone,* the ink-voice said, and it was barely a voice now, more of a warmth with words in it. *I cannot choose for you. No one can. That is the entire meaning of a keeper.* A pause, long as a held breath. *But I will say: whatever you give, give it wholly. Half-surrendered memories fester.*

Ansel looked at her hands. Ink-stained. Ledger-keeper's hands, made for columns that balanced, for debts recorded and paid. She had spent her whole adult life believing that everything could be entered fairly in a book — that every loss had an offsetting gain somewhere, if you were careful enough, thorough enough, *good* enough at the work.

And underneath that belief, warm and worn smooth as a river stone, there was Alden.

Her brother. Four years older, forever laughing at his own jokes before the punchline, forever dragging her onto roofs and out onto the frozen canal against their mother's express instructions. Alden, who had taught her double-entry bookkeeping as a game when she was seven, debits and credits as a secret language only the two of them spoke. Alden, who had died eight years ago of the fever winter, and whose face —

Whose face.

Ansel reached for it and found it whole: the crooked front tooth, the eyebrows that went up independently like punctuation marks arguing, the specific way his left eye crinkled higher than his right when he laughed. She found the memory of the last time she'd seen him alive, propped in bed, demanding she read him the guild's quarterly reports because he refused to die without knowing whether barley futures had recovered. They had. He'd been insufferably smug about it for eleven more hours.

She found all of it, and understood, with the terrible clarity of the belfry light, that this was the load-bearing wall. Not grief itself — grief would remain, grief always remains — but his *face*, the living image of him, the architecture of her brotherhood. Take that away and she would still love him. She would love a shape with no features, a warmth with no face, an entry in a ledger with the description column blank.

"That's the one, isn't it," Odile said quietly. Not triumphant. Sad, in the practiced way of someone who had watched this exact moment a hundred times and never gotten used to it. "I'm sorry. I'm always sorry. It doesn't help, but I say it anyway."

"Yes," Ansel said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of the moment, and then she decided it wasn't — it was a ledger-keeper's voice, and this was, in the end, a transaction, and transactions deserved dignity. "Yes. This one."

"Say it plainly. Freely given. Verity will know if you lie."

Ansel closed her eyes. "I give the memory of my brother Alden's face. All of it. Freely given, to the Bell of Saint Verity, in payment for the third sigil of the covenant." She swallowed. "He'd have hated that I'm giving anything away. He kept everything. Ticket stubs, broken compasses, letters. He was a magpie of a man." Her breath caught. "Freely given."

The air changed.

It was not dramatic. There was no flash, no toll, no wind. There was simply a sensation, brief and total, like the moment of stepping into deep cold water — and then Ansel opened her eyes and knew, with absolute certainty, that her brother Alden had existed, that she had loved him, that he had died in the fever winter, and that his face was a door in a corridor she could walk down forever and never find again.

She was crying before she understood why. That was the worst of it — the tears arrived with no address attached, grief pouring through a hole where the picture used to be. She wept for a man she could describe perfectly in every detail except the one that mattered.

Odile put a shawl around her shoulders and let her cry, and refilled the tea, and said nothing, because there was nothing that helped, but presence was at least a kind of company.

*Oh, little keeper,* Ilex whispered, and his voice cracked like drying ink. *Oh, brave, foolish, honest woman. I am so sorry. I am so proud. Both at once — that's what this work is.*

After a while — she never knew how long — Ansel wiped her face and stood. Above them, the Great Bell hummed. It was a faint sound, a bronze throat clearing after decades of phlegm, and then it gathered itself and rang.

Once.

The toll rolled out across Meridian and kept rolling, farther than sound should travel, and Ansel felt it pass through her chest and recognized, dimly, something in the note — a wedding, maybe, or a pair of teaching hands, or a golden dog — layered under something new and clean and hers. Alden's laugh, stripped of his face, became pure music. Somewhere below, the whole city stopped to look up, because Verity had not rung true in sixty years, and everyone had forgotten what it did to you, hearing it.

In the bell's dark mouth, something glinted. Odile reached up with a hooked pole and drew it down: a disc of hammered metal the size of a saucer, etched with lines that moved slowly, like frost forming in reverse. The third sigil. It was warm.

"It likes you," Odile said, wrapping it in cloth and pressing it into Ansel's hands. "Verity. She rang for you specifically, which she does perhaps once a generation. Don't let it go to your head; she also liked a goat in 1402." The old woman studied her with faded, formidable eyes. "One piece of advice, keeper-to-be. You'll want to mourn what you gave up. Don't. Mourn the man, not the missing picture. The picture was never the point. Love isn't stored in faces." She smiled, and for a heartbeat she looked a hundred and forty years old and unbearably kind. "Now go. Quickly."

"Why quickly?"

"Because," Odile said, turning her head toward the eastern arches, where the morning haze lay thick over the rooftops, "your captain has been climbing the short way for the last hour, and she is much better at it than you were."

Far below, faintly, boots scraped stone.
