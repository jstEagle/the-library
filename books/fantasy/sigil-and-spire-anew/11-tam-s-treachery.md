## 11. Tam's Treachery

Tam Vetch had three rules for surviving the Market of Borrowed Years, and he recited them every morning like a prayer his mother never taught him.

One: never sell your own hours, no matter how hungry you get, because a boy who sells tomorrow can't be trusted to show up today. Two: never let anyone learn where you sleep. Three: never, ever get caught caring about somebody, because in the Market, caring was just another currency, and currencies existed to be taken from you.

He was eleven years old and he'd broken rule three twice in one week, which by his own accounting made him either the bravest or the stupidest person in Salt Row, and he genuinely could not decide which.

The first time had been the woman herself — Ansel Winterbourne, who walked into the Market of Borrowed Years wearing a scholar's coat two sizes too fine for it and looking at the hour-sellers like they were people instead of inventory. She'd bought nothing. She'd asked questions. And when the toll-keeper tried to charge her double for being a stranger, she'd paid it without haggling, which in Tam's experience marked her as either very rich or very new to the city, and it turned out she was neither. She was something better. She was *interesting*.

The second time had been worse. The second time was his sister.

* * *

They kept Pip in a room above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, and Tam knew this because he'd spent four days finding out, and because the man who told him — a soft-voiced enforcer called Dree with hands like folded paper — wanted him to know. That was the whole point of telling him. A secret location is only a weapon if the other person knows you have it.

"She's comfortable," Dree had said, in the voice people used for talking about weather or meat prices. "Fed. She asked about you. Children ask such persistent questions, don't they? We've been answering them honestly, more or less." He'd smiled then, and Tam had learned something important about Dree's smile, which was that it never reached anywhere near his eyes, the way a rumor never reaches the truth. "All we need from you, Tam, is a little information. Where the ledger-woman goes next. Which door she'll use. Nothing you wouldn't know anyway, since she seems to have adopted you."

"She hasn't adopted me," Tam had said, with the flat dignity of a merchant refusing a lowball offer. "I'm showing her the tunnels for a fee. Standard rate."

"Of course you are." Dree had produced an apple — a whole apple, unbruised, red as a festival lantern — and set it on the table between them. In the Market of Borrowed Years, an unbruised apple cost an hour of good daylight, easy. Tam's mouth had watered and hated itself for it. "Think of it as a retainer."

That had been yesterday. Today the apple sat on Tam's windowsill where he could see it, because he hadn't eaten it and couldn't bring himself to throw it away either. It was the most honest object in his room. It said exactly what it was: a bribe, fresh and shiny, waiting to rot.

Tam lay on his cot and stared at the ceiling and rehearsed arguments.

*She's a stranger. You've known her six days.* True, but weak.

*She's going to get herself killed anyway, walking into the bell-tower business with a map that talks.* Also true, but somehow weaker, because it wasn't really an argument against helping her, it was an argument for it.

*If you don't tell Dree, they'll hurt Pip.* And there it was. The argument with teeth. Every other thought scattered from it like pigeons from a cart-horn.

Pip was eight. Pip had a laugh like a kettle coming to boil and a habit of naming things that didn't need names — she'd once spent an entire afternoon naming the gulls off Harbor Wall (Snatcher, Big Snatcher, Snatcher's Wife) — and Pip had been taken because of him, because he'd been careless enough to matter to someone important enough to hunt. Rule three, broken, and the bill come due.

Tam got up, took the apple, and went to find Dree.

* * *

He told himself he would only give them the small thing. The route, yes, but not the timing; the door, but not the key. He'd hand over a coin clipped at the edges and call it whole. He was good at that. He'd been clipping truths since he was seven.

Dree received him in the chandler's back room, among barrels of tallow that smelled like old candles dreaming of being burned. And through the doorway, past a curtain of oiled canvas, Tam heard Pip's voice — high, indignant, alive — arguing with someone about whether the moon was better than the sun. Pip maintained it was, on the grounds that the moon worked nights and deserved the credit.

"She's fine," Dree said, watching Tam listen. "You see?"

"You said honest answers, more or less." Tam kept his voice level, trader-flat. "Which parts were less?"

"The parts where we told her you'd be along soon." Dree spread a map of the city on a tallow barrel — a real map, guild-issue, the streets inked fine as hair. "Now. The ledger-woman. She means to go for the sigil in the great bell. Everyone with sense knows the Bell-Keeper doesn't trade. So how does she mean to manage it?"

And here was where Tam's plan of giving only the clipped coin ran straight into the wall of what he actually knew, which was everything. Ansel trusted him. That was the horrible engine at the center of all this: she trusted him, a child seller of stolen hours she'd known less than a week, because she was the kind of person who did that, and the guild had built its whole machine out of people exactly like her trusting exactly the wrong souls.

So he gave them the route. The under-gallery behind the ropewalk. The stair that came up inside the bell-frame's shadow. The hour after the lamplighters finished, when the watch changed and the tower wardens ate. He gave it clean, unclipped, because he understood suddenly that clipped information was the cruelest kind — it got people half-rescued, half-warned, half-alive.

Dree wrote it all down without hurry. Then he looked up, and for one moment the folded-paper hands and the weather voice dropped away, and Tam saw the actual creature underneath, the one the guild paid to do arithmetic on children.

"Good lad. Your sister will be released by week's end."

"By week's end," Tam repeated. "Not now."

"Arrangements take time." The smile again, rumor-thin. "You understand arrangements."

Tam understood arrangements. Arrangements meant the coin you were promised stayed on the far side of the counter while the other party found reasons the counter should keep moving. He walked out of the chandler's shop into the gray afternoon with the apple still in his pocket, untouched, heavier than any hour he'd ever carried.

* * *

That night he sat on the roof of the rope-walk with his knees pulled up and conducted the hardest negotiation of his career, and the parties at the table were both him.

On one side: Pip. Pip fed and questioned and waiting, Pip whose release lived in Dree's pocket like a coin he'd keep flipping forever. On the other side: Ansel Winterbourne, who had crouched down to his eye level in the Market and asked his name like names mattered, who had bought a stolen hour off him and then — this was the part that kept snagging in him like a fishhook — given it back. Told him to spend it on something that made him glad. Nobody in the history of the Market had ever done that. Hours were for selling. That was the entire architecture of the place. And she'd handed his own hour back to him like it was obviously his, like of course it was his, like the whole idea that a child's time belonged to strangers was a thing so absurd she hadn't even noticed she was breaking it.

Rule three, broken twice. Caring about the woman and caring about what happened to the city she was trying to save, because he'd listened to the map talk — Ilex, the dead cartographer with the tired gentle voice — and he'd heard what the guild was doing to the other side of the gate, and once you'd heard that, you couldn't unhear it, and Tam suspected that was true even for people who weren't listening directly.

"They'll take her at the bell-tower," he said aloud to the roof tiles. "Or they'll take her on the way back, on the causeway, when the tide's in and there's nowhere to run. Dree likes causeways. Dree likes anything with one road in and one road out."

The roof tiles said nothing, being roof tiles, but saying it out loud made it real, and real things could be traded with.

Because here was the thing Tam knew that Dree didn't, the asset sitting in his inventory that he'd been too frightened to price: Dree had asked him for the route *in*. Nobody had asked him about the route *out*, because nobody thought the ledger-woman would have an exit worth guarding, because nobody thought a scholar and a child and a talking map amounted to a force requiring an exit strategy.

But Tam had shown her the tunnels. All of them. Including the one thing he'd held back even from her, professional habit, rule two: the flood-cut beneath the harbor road, the old storm channel that surfaced on the tidal flats near the causeway's midpoint, behind a grate he'd picked open himself two winters ago and relocked so nobody else would find it. If she came back across the causeway — and she would, because the marshes lay that way, and the map was pulling her toward the fourth sigil like a fish on a line — then the grate was the one door in the city the guild didn't have on its maps.

One door. One chance. And a message that had to reach her before the enforcers did.

* * *

Getting the message to Ansel Winterbourne turned out to be the easiest part, which Tam found insulting on behalf of everyone who'd ever told him things were hard.

She was lodging — if you could call it that — in the loft of a cooperage off Brine Street, sleeping in sawdust like a very tall, very tired cat. Tam climbed the drainpipe at dawn, which was nothing, drainpipes were stairs for the ambitious, and found her already awake, sitting cross-legged with the map spread before her. The ink moved when it spoke. He'd seen it twice now and it still raised the hair on his arms, though he'd have bitten off his tongue before admitting it.

"You're up early," Ansel said, and smiled at him, and the smile went all the way to her eyes, which after Dree's smile felt like stepping from a cold room into sun.

"I'm always up early. Early hours fetch a premium." He crouched at the edge of the lamplight, suddenly aware of the weight in his pocket. Not the apple. He'd left the apple on Dree's doorstep overnight, punctured neatly with a knife, so it would be found soft and browning by morning — a message in fruit, the only language the Market respected. What weighed on him now was a scrap of oilcloth, wrapped around a bent nail, tied with waxed thread.

"Tam?" Ansel had gone still, the way she did when the map told her something she didn't like. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. Something's *about* to be wrong, which is different, and cheaper if you buy it now." He tossed her the bundle. She caught it one-handed, unrolled it. His handwriting was cramped but legible; he'd written it four times to get it right, and burned the failures in the chandler's yard.

*They know about the bell. They'll let you take the sigil — taking it isn't the trap. The trap is the causeway after. Tide comes in at dusk. They'll have the far end and the near end and boats on the flats. Don't go by the road. Midway, seaward side, below the high-water line — iron grate, storm-cut, runs under to the saltmarsh path. I picked the lock myself. It opens.*

Ansel read it twice. When she looked up, her face did something complicated — gratitude and grief and a question all fighting for space.

"How long have you known?"

"Since yesterday."

"And you came anyway. At dawn. Why not last night?"

"Because last night I was still deciding whose side I was on," Tam said, "and I don't work cheap, so the decision took a while." It was a joke. It was also completely true, and they both knew it, and Ansel had the grace to laugh anyway, short and startled, before her face went serious again.

"Pip," she said quietly. "The guild has your sister. That's why they needed you."

"Don't," Tam said, sharper than he meant to. "Don't do the face. I know what I did. I sold your route to save my sister and I'm not sorry about the saving part, I'd do that part again, but I'm sorry about the selling part, and the difference between those two sorries is basically my whole life right now, so please don't make it bigger."

Ansel considered him for a long moment. Then she said, "You'd do the saving part again," and nodded slowly, as if confirming a figure in a ledger. "Then we're going to get her back. Both of you. After the marshes, before whatever comes next — Ilex says the fourth guardian owes debts, and I intend to collect on several." She folded his note carefully and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart, which was such an Ansel place to put it that Tam's throat did something embarrassing. "Thank you. And Tam — what will they do to you, when they learn the grate is open?"

Tam shrugged with the elaborate carelessness he'd practiced in mirrors. "Nothing they can prove. I gave them the route they asked for. Everything in my note is stuff I never told them." He grinned. "Rule two of the Market: never let anyone learn where you sleep. Rule two-and-a-half, which I invented last night: always keep one door off every map, including the ones you draw yourself."

"And the grate?"

"That one I'm keeping off my map too. From now on it belongs to you." He was already backing toward the window, because dawn was spreading and Dree's people watched Brine Street in daylight. "Bell-tower at dusk-lamplighting. Grate at mid-causeway, seaward side. And lady—" he paused, half through the window, silhouetted against the graying sky, "—the tide tables say it comes in fast tomorrow. Faster than the almanac says. Dree's arranged that too. Whatever you do on that causeway, don't stop to argue with anybody. Not even the map. Especially not the map."

The ink on the parchment behind her rippled, and a dry voice like wind through an empty library said, "I heard that, young man."

"Good," said Tam, and dropped into the alley, and was gone.

* * *

He made it three streets before the fear caught up with him, which was actually a personal record.

It caught him outside the chandler's shop, of all places — he'd gone there without deciding to, drawn the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth — and he stood in the shadow across Candlewick Lane and looked up at the window where a candle burned late, and heard, faintly, Pip's voice arguing with her keeper about the moon again. Still defending the night shift. Still certain the world undervalued its quiet workers.

They'd release her by week's end, Dree had said. Arrangements take time.

But Tam had grown up in the Market of Borrowed Years, and if the Market taught one lesson deeper than all others, it was this: *never trust a debt the debtor gets to define.* Dree's promise was a promissory note drawn on a bank that burned its own ledgers. The moment Ansel was taken — or drowned, or lost on the flats — Tam's usefulness ended, and end-of-usefulness in the guild's arithmetic had only one column.

Unless.

Unless the ledger-woman won. Unless she reached the marshes and came back with allies and proof and that terrible patient way she had of reading things until the truth fell out of them like coins from a torn pocket. Then the tribunal everyone whispered about would want witnesses, and witnesses with sisters locked in chandler's shops would be worth protecting, and Pip would walk out of that room not as a hostage released but as a wrong avenged.

Tam stood in the dark and understood, with the clarity that arrives only after you've already done the brave thing and can't take it back, that he hadn't chosen between Pip and Ansel at all. He'd chosen between two futures for Pip: one where she was a bargaining chip until she was nothing, and one where she was a reason the city changed. There was only one version of that trade where everybody walked away whole, and he'd just bet everything he had on it.

"Snatcher," he said to a gull perched on the eaves above him, using Pip's name for it. "Big night tonight."

The gull screamed once, which Tam chose to interpret as encouragement.

Across the lane, the candle in the chandler's window guttered and steadied. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the great bell hung silent in its tower, holding its sigil, holding its price. And somewhere between here and the marshes, a tidal causeway waited under a false almanac and a rising sea, with enforcers at both ends and one iron grate in the middle that a boy had opened and a woman might live to find.

Tam pulled his cap down and turned toward the harbor, toward the flats, toward the one thing left he could do from the outside: watch the watchers, and count their boats, and if the numbers went wrong — if there were more of them than his note had warned her about — find some way, any way, to scream the difference across the water before the tide closed over the road.

He was small. He knew that. But he'd spent his whole life learning what small things were worth in a big market, and he knew a secret the guild masters had never bothered to learn:

Everything, priced honestly. Even him.
