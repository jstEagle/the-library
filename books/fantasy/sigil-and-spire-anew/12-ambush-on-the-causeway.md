## 12. Ambush on the Causeway

The tide had turned against her, and Ansel could not help but feel it was personal.

She stood at the landward end of the causeway as the light failed — a long gray spine of stone running out toward the marsh islands, slick with weed, half-drowned already at its far reaches. The sea came in from the east in long patient lines, filling the channels on either side, and by full dark the whole road would be under a hand's depth of water. She had perhaps an hour. Maybe less.

"An optimistic crossing time would have been useful," she muttered, checking the map one more time.

The map lay across her palms, and where once it had been ink on vellum it now looked like something a candle had dreamed of — scorched at the edges, its lines glowing faintly gold where they should have been black. It had been burning itself slowly since the ambush began three days ago, spending its own substance to keep her alive, and she had stopped asking it to stop because Ilex had told her, in that dry cartographer's voice, that arguing with a dying map about conservation was like arguing with a tide.

"You could have mentioned," she said now, "that the causeway floods."

"I did mention." His voice came thin from the parchment, thinner than yesterday, thinner than the day before. "I said *mind the water*. You were busy being shot at."

"That was two days ago and you know perfectly well I meant—"

"Ilex," she said, and then couldn't find the rest of the sentence.

A pause. When he spoke again the dry humor had been set aside, carefully, like a tool no longer needed. "I know what you meant. Cross quickly, keeper's clerk. The marshes will take us in. They always took me in, when I was a man with legs instead of a man with margins."

She started out onto the causeway.

Her left side hurt with every step — a shallow cut along the ribs from the enforcer's blade at the tollhouse, not deep, but deep enough to remind her with each breath that she was a ledger-keeper playing at fugitive and losing. Three days of running. Three days of the map flaring gold to blind a crossbowman, to turn a blade, to show her a smuggler's stair when the streets closed around her. And each flare cost it. Each flare cost *him*.

She had done the arithmetic, because arithmetic was what she had. The map had been drawn with perhaps a hundred burnings in it. They had spent sixty-one.

*Sixty-one*, she thought, and her breath caught on the number, and she made herself keep walking.

* * *

Halfway across, the bells of the city rang the evening hour behind her, and beneath their sound she heard another bell — a single flat note from the watchtower at the causeway's near end.

The alarm note.

She didn't look back. Looking back was for people with time. She ran instead, or did the thing that was nearest to running that her ribs permitted, boots skidding on wet stone, spray coming up cold around her ankles where the sea was already claiming the road.

"They've found us," Ilex said. Not a question. He could feel it too, somehow — the map knew when guild eyes were upon it, the way a scar knows rain.

"How many?"

"The tower shows six. Two will hold the landward end. Four are coming out onto the causeway."

"Can we go around? The channels—"

"In this tide, with that wound? You'd drown inside four minutes, and I'd drown with you, and I have already drowned once and found it overrated."

Ahead of her, out of the failing light, the four came.

They walked in a line across the narrow stone, unhurried, which was worse than running. Enforcers of the Meridian Guild did not hurry; hurry implied doubt. The foremost carried a net-weighted chain that pooled and shifted in his grip like something alive. Behind him, a woman with a hooked staff she rested on her shoulder as though out for an evening stroll. Ansel recognized none of them by name, which meant nothing; Vess trained them all to be nobody.

She stopped. There was nowhere to stop *to* — the causeway was eight feet wide, water rising on both sides, the marsh still a dark smudge too far ahead. Behind her, two more silhouettes had appeared at the landward end, closing the door.

"Well," she said. "This is tidy."

"Tidiness is a guild virtue," Ilex murmured. "You used to sign off on it quarterly."

* * *

The leader of the four stopped ten paces away. Up close, in the last gray light, Ansel saw he was young — younger than her, certainly, with the smooth unlined face of someone who had never yet been asked to think about what he enforced.

"Ansel Winterbourne," he said. "By order of the Masters of the Meridian Guild, you are taken for theft, sedition, and traffic in forbidden sigils. Surrender the document and come quietly, and the tribunal may show mercy."

"The tribunal," Ansel said, "is a locked room and five men who drink the lives of another world through a hole in the floor."

Something flickered across the young enforcer's face — quick, quickly suppressed. Good. Somewhere under the training there was still a person who had heard rumors, who had wondered why the masters never seemed to age.

"You'll come with us," he said, less steadily.

"No," said Ansel, "I don't believe I will," and she felt, absurdly, like a clerk refusing a bad invoice: polite, immovable, doomed.

The woman with the hooked staff sighed and stepped forward. "Kestrel's mercy, Doran, talk to her on your own time." The staff came off her shoulder. "Document first. Then the girl. The Masters want the map whole."

*Whole*, Ansel thought. *They want him whole.* Because a burned map could be read by anyone, but an intact one might still hold whatever craft had let Ilex split the covenant's secrets in the first place.

Ilex understood it in the same instant. She felt it in the way the gold lines brightened against her palms.

"Ansel," he said quietly. "Listen to me. Whatever happens next — do not drop me into the sea. Promise me. If I go into salt water unfinished, everything I am goes out like a lamp. No voice, no imprint, nothing for whoever finds the vellum after. I would rather spend myself shielding you than be drowned twice."

"I'm not going to let anything—"

"*Promise.*"

The enforcers were moving. The chain-man swung his weight in a slow gathering arc, and the woman's staff-hook gleamed, and behind them both the landward pair had begun to walk out onto the stones.

"I promise," Ansel said. "Now do something clever."

"Oh," said Ilex, "I intend to be *extremely* wasteful."

* * *

The map caught fire in her hands.

Not the slow candle-eating burn of the past three days — this was a conflagration, gold-white, roaring up her arms without heat, and through the blaze she heard Ilex laugh, a real laugh, the laugh of a man spending everything at once because the account was closing anyway.

Light poured off the causeway like water off a weir. The chain-man threw up an arm and the weighted chain went slack and dead, its sigil-work blanked. The woman staggered back a step, hand across her eyes. Even the two at the landward end, fifty yards off, stumbled as the glare washed over them.

And Ansel ran.

She ran through the heart of her ally's funeral pyre, along the wet stones, past the blinded enforcers, her shadow thrown huge and flickering against the eastern clouds. The map's fire streamed backward off her hands like a banner. She heard shouting behind her, the young one — Doran — calling for order, the woman snarling something about *the Masters will hear of this*—

Then the light guttered.

It went out all at once, the way a lamp goes out when the oil is truly gone, and the darkness that followed was so complete that Ansel lost the causeway under her feet and went down hard on her knees in six inches of freezing seawater, the cut in her ribs tearing open with a white flash of pain.

She got up. She had to get up; the alternative was the channel. She got up by telling her body it was a staircase, one step, another step, and she ran blind with her arms out, and the sea came up around her calves now, and somewhere behind her boots were splashing — pursuit, gaining—

"Left," whispered a voice, so faint she felt it more than heard it, a breath against the soaked vellum clutched to her chest. "*Left, Ansel. The old pilings.*"

She veered left. Her shin cracked against submerged timber — pain, but *timber*, structure, a way — and she hauled herself along a row of rotting pilings that ran at an angle off the causeway into the dark, some ancient pier drowned a century ago, its stumps just breaking the surface in a broken line toward the marsh.

"Right at the third post. Mind the netting."

She minded the netting. Something tore at her sleeve instead of her face. The water deepened, then shallowed, then gave way to sucking mud, and then her boots found real ground — marsh ground, peat and sedge, rising in a low black hummock crowned with wind-bent grass.

Behind her, torchlight bloomed on the causeway. Voices. Someone wading out along the pilings, cursing the dark, the tide, the orders that had put them here.

Ansel crawled up the hummock and lay flat in the wet grass, bleeding, shaking, and pressed the map to her ear like a child listening for a heartbeat.

* * *

"Ilex?"

Nothing.

"Ilex. We're out. We're in the marshes, you did it, you magnificent, impossible, *wasteful* man, we're out—"

Still nothing. The vellum lay across her knees, and in the moonless dark she could see almost nothing of it, only that it was lighter than it had been — translucent now, thin as onion skin, its edges gone entirely. When she dared strike a match from Tam's stolen tin, the little flame showed her the truth.

Three-quarters of the map was simply *gone*. Burned away to nothing, not even ash clinging. What remained was a ragged western fragment: a corner of coastline, the marshes themselves inked in fading gold, and one small careful drawing of a hut with a chimney, marked in a spidery hand she recognized as his — *an old friend keeps a poor fire here; tell him the fourth debt stands*.

And in the remaining margin, letters so faint they might have been memory rather than ink:

*Forgive the expense. I find I mind it less than I expected to. Keep me dry, keeper's clerk. There is a little of me left, and I mean to see how you end.*

The match died. Ansel sat in the marsh grass with the wounded remnant of her friend cupped in both hands, and the tears came at last — not gracefully, not the way grief was described in novels, but in ugly gulping sobs that shook her torn ribs and made her gasp. She cried for the sixty-two burnings. She cried for the thirty-eight that remained. She cried because a man who had been dead for forty years had just died again, a second and smaller death, buying her forty yards of rotten pilings and a mouthful of seawater, and because she had promised not to drop him in the sea and she was going to keep that promise if it was the last covenant she ever kept.

When she had finished — because ledgers must balance, even grief, eventually — she wiped her face on her sleeve, folded the fragile fragment with the tenderness she once reserved for first-edition folios, and tucked it inside her shirt, against her skin, where it could stay warm and dry.

Then she looked up.

The marsh spread before her, black and whispering under the first stars, miles of sedge and tidal pools and drowned alder. And there, impossibly, absurdly, exactly where the map's last surviving drawing said it would be: a single point of light. A window. Firelight, small and orange and human, burning steady in all that desolation.

An old friend kept a poor fire.

Ansel got to her feet, swayed, steadied. Her ribs throbbed in time with her pulse. Somewhere behind her the enforcers were regrouping on the causeway, and somewhere ahead of her a hollowed man waited with a confession forty years old, and between the two lay only cold mud, dark water, and whatever strength she had left.

"Well," she said aloud, to the fragment against her heart, and fancied she felt the faintest warmth answer. "One more chapter. Try to stay with me for it."

She began to walk toward the light.
