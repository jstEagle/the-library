## 14. Vess Turns Her Coat

The summons came at the hour when the guildhouse lamps were trimmed low and the corridors smelled of cold tallow and older secrets. Captain Sera Vess had been expecting it for three days, which was why she had spent those three days doing something she had never once done in eleven years of service: lying.

She had falsified patrol reports. She had misfiled a requisition for marsh-boat fuel. She had told Enforcer Dree that the fugitive Winterbourne's trail went cold at the causeway, when in truth Vess herself had stood in the reeds an hour after the ambush and watched a wounded woman limp south with a dead man's map clutched to her chest like a child's doll.

*Trail gone cold,* the report said. *Recommend standing down.*

She had written *recommend standing down* with her own hand. Eleven years of hunting thieves and oath-breakers, and now she was forging documents like a smuggler.

"You wanted me, Masters."

The council chamber above the weighing hall had no windows — windows admitted light, and light admitted scrutiny, and the Meridian Guild had never cared for either. Seven chairs on a raised dais, six occupied. Master Corvel sat in the center, a small man made smaller by the enormous chain of office he wore, as though the guild had tried to weigh him and found him wanting and dressed him in metal to compensate. To his left, Master Ivenne turned a signet ring around her finger, around again, around again. The seventh chair stood empty. It had stood empty since before Vess joined the enforcers. Nobody ever explained why, and nobody ever asked, which told Vess more about the guild than any ledger could have.

"Captain." Corvel's voice was soft. He was always soft. Men who shouted wanted to be heard; men like Corvel wanted to be obeyed without the indignity of asking. "Your report on the Winterbourne woman."

"She's lost in the marshes, wounded. The marsh will do our work."

"Perhaps." Ivenne stopped turning her ring. "Or perhaps you'll find this clarifying."

She slid a folio across the table. Vess did not move to take it. She had learned long ago that in this room, whatever was offered was a test, and tests were graded by refusal.

Corvel smiled faintly, as if she'd passed something. "Three days ago," he said, "a warden at the drowned archive reported that the prisoner Corvane released a sigil-stone to a woman matching Winterbourne's description. In exchange for — what was the phrase?"

"A confession," Vess said. "He trades in truths."

"Yes. And what truth did she give him, Captain?"

Vess kept her face still. Below the guildhouse, behind the sealed well, she had seen the pale light being drawn up in long silver threads, and she had told herself it was maintenance, ritual, the ordinary machinery of an institution too old to explain itself to its servants. She had been telling herself that for eleven years.

"I couldn't say, Master."

"No," Corvel agreed pleasantly. "You couldn't. Because you didn't ask her. You let her go." He folded his hands. "We reviewed your pursuit record, Captain. Meticulous. Thorough. Right up until the causeway. After that — nothing. A woman carrying three covenant sigils vanished from the custody of our finest hunter, and your report reads like a eulogy for a trail that was never followed."

"The causeway flooded—"

"The causeway floods twice a day. You've crossed it in worse."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the great gate hummed its note that only the walls could hear, the sound Vess had slept beside for a decade and never thought of as hunger.

Ivenne leaned forward. "Here is what happens next, Captain. Winterbourne is gathering the sigils. We don't know yet how much she understands, but we must assume the worst: everything. That she has spoken to the map. That she knows what the well is for." She said it flatly, without shame, and that flatness frightened Vess more than any confession could have. "You will find her. And this time you will not bring her back."

Vess heard the words arrive one at a time, each with its own small weight, like coins counted into a palm.

"Kill orders are not issued lightly," Corvel said. "But the covenant is a bookkeeping problem, Captain, and Winterbourne has become an unbalanced entry. We correct entries. You know this. You've corrected many."

*I've arrested people,* Vess thought. *Dragged them here in irons. Watched them go down the stair toward the weighing hall and never asked where the stair ended.* Eleven years of not asking, stacked up behind her eyes like unsolved sums.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Corvel looked genuinely puzzled, the way a man might look at a scale that refused to tip. "Refuse? Captain, you have served with distinction. Your name is in the rolls. Your pension is in the vault. Refusal would be — arithmetic." He smiled his soft smile. "You won't refuse. You're the last honest person in this building."

That, Vess thought later, was the moment. Not the kill order. That sentence. Because he believed it, and because it was true, and because he had just handed the last honest person in the building an order to murder a woman for reading a ledger correctly.

"Understood, Masters," Vess said. "I'll need my field kit. And access to the evidence vault — I want the courier's recovered effects. There may be a scent-trace, papers, something to narrow the search."

"Granted," said Ivenne. "Efficiency. Good."

What she actually took from the evidence vault, twenty minutes later, while Dree signed the chit without looking up, was a lockbox marked *COURIER, DECEASED / PERSONAL EFFECTS / DO NOT CATALOGUE*. Inside: a broken compass, a tin whistle, and forty pages of correspondence between a cartographer named Ilex Marrowgate and four friends, discussing — in code so old-fashioned Vess had needed an hour to crack it — exactly where five sigils had been hidden, and exactly what the well beneath the guildhouse had originally been built to contain.

And a copy of a flow-ledger. Forty years of withdrawals from the far side of the gate, entered in the masters' own hands, balanced against names in the city registry. Names of people who had died young. Names of children born gray-eyed and slow. An entire column of the city, siphoned through a pipe, to keep seven chairs warm.

Vess read it twice by lamplight in the stable loft, then packed it beside her rations, mounted the ugly marsh pony nobody would miss, and rode south into the dark, committing treason at a trot.

* * *

She found the fire by its smoke, which was careless of Winterbourne, and then found the woman by the fact that she was sitting up against a deadfall with a knife across her knees, which was not careless at all.

"Don't," Ansel Winterbourne said, when Vess was still ten paces out. Her voice was rough. Her left arm was bound in strips of shirt, badly, and there was marsh mud to her jaw, and she looked like a woman who had been losing arguments with a landscape for two days. "I know that walk. I've been running from that walk for a week."

"If I wanted to take you, I'd have waited until you slept."

"You did wait until I slept. I'm not asleep. I haven't slept since your people burned the map's edge off." The knife didn't waver. "Say what you came to say, Captain, and then decide whether you can survive me saying no."

Vess dismounted slowly, keeping her hands wide. "I brought proof," she said. "Of the draining. The masters' own figures. Forty years of it."

Something flickered across the woman's face — hope, quickly strangled, the way a careful person strangles hope. "Prove it's not bait."

"It was bait until yesterday. Then Corvel gave me a kill order and called me honest, and I discovered I liked being honest better than I liked being theirs." Vess crouched, unhurried, and set the lockbox on the ground between them. Then, deliberately, she took out the flow-ledger and pushed it across the mud. "Read it. Check my arithmetic while you're at it. I'm told you're good."

Winterbourne didn't touch it. "Why should I believe anything you say?"

"You shouldn't. Belief is for priests and fools. Read the numbers instead. Numbers don't take sides." She paused. "That's what I used to think, anyway. Before I learned who was writing them."

For a long moment there was only the marsh talking to itself — water moving under reeds, some bird complaining about the century. Then Winterbourne reached out, kept her eyes on Vess, and drew the ledger into her lap.

Vess watched her read. It was, she thought, like watching someone pray, if prayer involved lips moving and occasional sounds of disgust. The woman's finger tracked columns. Stopped. Went back. Stopped again.

"These dates," Winterbourne said quietly. "This one here. Ninth of Deepmonth, thirty-one years ago. My father was a tally-clerk. He died that winter. Gray-eyed, they said at the funeral. Slow, at the end." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious in the firelight. "They put him in a column."

"They put everyone in a column," Vess said. "That's rather the point of ledgers. And rather the point of killing them."

Winterbourne laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, or maybe all of it. "Careful, Captain. That almost sounded like a joke. I thought enforcers were grown in barrels."

"We are. This one leaked." Vess sat down across the fire, finally, and began unbuckling her sword belt — slowly, showing every motion, laying the blade on the ground beside her knee rather than at her hip. It cost her something to do it. It cost her less than she expected. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to use me. Point me at things I can carry, fight, or burn. Trust can catch up later."

"That's the most sensible offer anyone's made me since a dead man started talking to me out of an ink pot."

"Is he still talking?" Vess nodded at the map, wrapped now in oilcloth, its burned corner visible even so. "The courier's master. Marrowgate."

Winterbourne went very still. "How do you know that name?"

"He wrote half the letters in your lap, Captain Winterbourne. I've read them. All of them. I broke his cipher over a stable loft dinner of dried eel, which is the sort of sentence I never imagined saying aloud." She fed a stick to the fire. "He hid the sigils with four friends. One of them is still alive, out here in the marshes somewhere — the letters call him the Warden of Reeds. If your map hasn't mentioned him yet, it will."

The silence changed quality. Vess had interrogated enough people to hear it: suspicion shifting, grudgingly, into alliance. Not friendship. Alliance. Friendship could come later or never; tonight, alliance was enough.

"Why?" Winterbourne asked at last. It wasn't hostile. It was the question of a bookkeeper who had found a figure that wouldn't balance. "Eleven years, Vess. You hunted me across half the city. Why turn?"

Vess considered several answers. The true one was simplest.

"Because I checked the sums," she said. "My whole life, that's all I ever did — check other people's sums and arrest whoever's didn't add up. And then I found out mine didn't add up either. Everything I enforced, all those years — it was arithmetic for people who were eating the city." She poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. "Corvel said I was the last honest person in the guildhouse. I've decided to be the first honest person outside it."

Winterbourne studied her for a long time. Then, carefully, she tore a strip from her last clean bandage, dipped it in the melted fat at the edge of the fire, and held it out.

"For your arm," she said. "You're favoring your left side. Have been since you dismounted. Marsh riding tears something in the shoulder — Ilex's letters mention it. Apparently he complained about it constantly."

"He would," said a dry voice from the oilcloth bundle, faint as breath on a window. "He complained about everything. It was his best quality."

Both women froze. The fire popped. Somewhere in the reeds, the bird resumed its complaints about the century.

"It talks," Vess said.

"It complains," Winterbourne corrected, wrapping the bandage around Vess's shoulder with brisk, ungentle competence. "There's a difference, and he'll explain it at length if you let him." She tied it off, sat back, and looked at Vess across the flames — the hunter and the hunted, the keeper of laws and the breaker of them, both of them traitors now, both of them free in the way that costs everything.

"So," Ansel said. "The Warden of Reeds. How far?"

"Two days' ride. Less if the tide cooperates." Vess flexed her bandaged shoulder, winced, and decided not to mention it. "And Winterbourne — when we find him, and the last sigils, and we go back to that gate — the masters will send everyone. Dree. Halvard. People I trained. Some of them are good."

"Then we'd better make sure the truth reaches them before the swords do."

Vess almost smiled. Almost. "You sound like a recruiter."

"I keep ledgers, Captain. Recruitment is just accounting with more shouting."

Above them the clouds thinned, and for the first time in days the stars came out — all except one patch of sky, low in the north, over the distant spire of the guildhouse, where the darkness seemed thicker than it should be. Where a gate that must never open fully was quietly starving a world.

Ansel saw Vess looking at it.

"Two days," Vess said, and threw another branch on the fire, and settled in to keep the first watch she had ever kept for the right side.
