## 15. The Gate That Should Stay Shut

The last stretch of the road to the gate was not a road at all, but a spine of black stone that rose out of the marsh like the back of something sleeping. Ansel had walked three days on Vess's directions, past reed-beds and drowned orchards, past villages where people watched them pass with the flat, careful eyes of those who had learned not to hope. Now the stone rose before them, slick with salt spray, climbing toward a sky gone the color of old pewter.

"Is it always this cold here?" Tam asked, huddled in the coat Vess had given him two days ago. It was too big for him; he wore it like a small, indignant bear.

"The gate drinks warmth," Ilex said.

Ansel flinched. She still wasn't used to hearing his voice come from the folded square of parchment tucked inside her breast pocket — thinner now, frayed at the edges where the map had burned itself to save her on the causeway. The burn had taken most of the eastern territories, the trade routes, the little annotations in his cramped hand. What remained of Ilex was mostly voice, and even the voice had developed a habit of fading mid-sentence, as though he were falling asleep between words.

"Drinks it how?" Tam pressed. He was fourteen and had never once let an unanswered question go by, which Ansel suspected was why he'd survived the market of borrowed years.

"Slowly," Ilex said. "For sixty years. A sip at a time."

They climbed. The stone was carved — Ansel realized this about halfway up, when her fingers found grooves too regular for erosion. Sigils, thousands of them, worn shallow by weather but still legible if you knew how to read. And she did know how to read; she had spent eleven years reading the Meridian Guild's ledgers, and ledgers were only another kind of covenant, promises set down so they could be kept.

"These are terms," she said aloud, wonder creeping into her voice despite everything. "It's a contract. The whole causeway is a contract."

"You're the first person in forty years to say that out loud," Ilex said, and there was something in his voice that might have been pride, or might have been grief wearing pride's coat.

Vess climbed behind her, silent as ever, one hand never far from her blade out of habit even though there was nothing left to fight up here but wind. She had changed since the marsh fire. Not softened, exactly — Ansel doubted anything could soften Vess short of divine intervention or a very persuasive accountant — but something in her had stopped bracing against the world. She walked like a woman who had already lost everything she'd been afraid of losing, and found she could still put one foot in front of the other.

The hollowed hermit — Aldous, though he flinched from the name — came last, moving with the slow care of a man carrying something breakable inside himself. Which, Ansel supposed, he was.

At the top of the spine, the world ended.

Not dramatically. There was no chasm, no wall of fire. There was simply a place where the black stone widened into a platform, and on the platform stood a doorway.

Ansel had imagined the gate a hundred times since the night a dying courier bled into her ledgers. She had imagined iron bars, arcane locks, a maw. What stood before her instead was almost modest: an arch of white stone, perhaps twelve feet tall, its surface covered in the same contract-script as the causeway, dense as rain. At its center hung a membrane like the skin atop cooled milk — pale, faintly luminous, trembling when the wind touched it.

Through the membrane, she could see the other realm.

She had expected darkness. She had expected monsters. Every story the Guild told, every warning painted on the guildhouse walls, every lesson drilled into apprentice keepers — all of it described the far side as hunger, as teeth, as the thing the covenant existed to hold back.

What she saw was ruins.

Gray towers, slender and impossibly graceful, their spires snapped off like winter branches. Terraces of gardens gone to ash-colored moss. A city built along a coastline that gleamed silver under a sun that seemed very far away — a pale, apologetic sun, the kind you see through fog. And moving through the streets, small as insects at this distance, figures. People. Walking slowly. Gathering at the base of broken towers. Some of them appeared to be waiting in lines.

"They're queuing," Tam said, pressing his face nearly to the membrane. "Why are they queuing?"

"For rationing," Ilex said quietly. "Or for news. Or for the gate to open. They've been doing it for sixty years. It's become a custom. Waiting is what they do now."

Ansel's hand rose to the membrane without her deciding to send it. The surface was cool, then warm, then — she gasped — *familiar*, the way a word in your own language sounds when you hear it spoken abroad. Something on the other side pressed gently against her palm. Not pushing. Resting. Like a hand laid over hers across a table.

"There are children," Vess said. Her voice was level, but her knuckles were white on the arch's edge. "Look. By the fountain. Those are children."

Ansel looked. They were. Small figures clustered around a dry fountain, and one of them — she would remember this until the day she died, whatever else the days took — one of them was holding up a paper lantern toward the gate. Holding it up the way children do, showing something precious to someone on the far side of a window.

*We made this,* the gesture said, in any language. *Look what we made.*

"I read their names," Ansel whispered. "For eleven years. In the transfer ledgers." Her voice cracked on the word *transfer*, because she finally understood what the columns meant, what the elegant abbreviations meant, what the neat quarterly totals meant. Vitality received. Yield per annum. Draw rate, sustainable. She had balanced those books. She had been proud of her penmanship. "I thought I was recording trade goods."

"You were recording them," Ilex said. "That's the horror of good bookkeeping. It makes anything legible. Anything tolerable. A number has no face until you stand in front of the window."

Aldous sank down onto the stone at the edge of the platform, his back against the arch, and put his face in his hands. He had been one of the five who split the sigils. He had been the one who argued, sixty years ago, that the gate should be watched rather than sealed, that vigilance was safer than trust — and then he had watched the first siphon being installed and had run, and had kept running for six decades, hollowing himself out with guilt the way a river hollows a bank.

"I told myself they were dangerous," he said, to no one, to the stone. "I told myself whatever we did to them, they'd have done to us. You can make yourself believe anything if you rehearse it often enough. I rehearsed it until it was true."

"It was never true," Vess said. Not unkindly. "You just needed it to be."

"Yes," Aldous said. "That's what guilt is. A lie you maintain on purpose because the truth costs more."

Tam had gone quiet, which for Tam was remarkable enough that Ansel noticed. He was staring at the lantern-child, and his jaw was set in a way she recognized from the market — the look he got when he saw someone smaller than himself going hungry.

"My sister," he said abruptly. "When the guild agents took her hours, she went gray. Not sad-gray. Actual gray. Like ash. For weeks." He looked up at Ansel. "That's what we've been doing to them. All of them. All the time."

"Yes," Ansel said. Because he deserved the truth, and because lying to Tam felt suddenly impossible, here, in front of the window.

The wind picked up, moaning through the contract-script, and for a moment Ansel could have sworn the arch itself shuddered — a deep, structural tremor, like a building settling, or a body in pain. The membrane flickered. Through it, the pale sun stuttered.

"It's failing faster than Ilex said," Vess said sharply. "You felt that?"

"I felt it." Ansel pressed her palm flat again. Beneath the skin of the gate, she could feel the covenant's rhythm — and it was wrong, arrhythmic, a heartbeat with a murmur. Sixty years of siphoning had not merely weakened the seal. It had wounded the thing the seal *was*. "How long?"

"With four sigils?" Ilex's voice wavered, thinned. "Days. Perhaps less. The fifth is still locked in the guildhouse vault, and the masters will have doubled the guard since Vess left. They know what you carry. They know what you intend."

"Then we don't have time to stand here feeling things," Vess said, but she said it gently, and she didn't move to leave, and neither did anyone else. For a long moment the five of them simply stood at the threshold of a world their city had been eating, and no one spoke, and the lantern-child held up her light.

Then Ansel did something she hadn't planned. She took out her ledger — the new one, the honest one, begun the night she fled the guildhouse with nothing but a map and a stolen stone — and she opened it on her knee, right there on the cold stone, and she began to write.

"What are you doing?" Tam asked.

"Recording," Ansel said. Her pen moved steadily. "Date. Location. Observed condition of the far realm: famine, structural collapse, civil order maintained through ritual waiting. Number of persons visible awaiting relief: uncountable. Conduct of persons observed: one child presented a lantern to the gate in greeting." Her throat tightened. "Note: they greeted us."

She wrote until her fingers ached, and then she wrote the entry she had been circling all morning, the one that mattered:

*Debt acknowledged. Creditor: the far realm. Debtor: Meridian. Terms of repayment: everything.*

She closed the book. When she looked up, Vess was watching her with an expression Ansel had never seen on her before — something that on a softer face might have been called moved.

"The masters always said you were the best keeper they'd ever trained," Vess said. "They were right. They just never understood what a keeper is *for*."

"To keep accounts," Ansel said. "And to keep faith. Preferably both at once."

Ilex laughed — a small sound, quickly spent, like the last coin in a purse. "Ansel. Listen to me. When we reforge the covenant, it will need ink. Living ink. A memory given wholly, of both realms, freely—" His voice caught, frayed, and for a moment there was silence from the parchment, and Ansel's heart clenched with a fear she refused to examine.

"Ilex?"

"I'm here," he said faintly. "Barely. Ansel — there's something you need to understand about the price. About what the reforging asks. I've known since the archive. I should have told you sooner, and I didn't, because I am at bottom a coward who died once already to avoid a difficult conversation—"

"Ilex." She lifted the map from her pocket, unfolded it carefully on her knee beside the ledger. The remaining ink swam, gathered, formed his familiar cramped script, then dissolved back into a voice. "Tell me."

"The covenant demands a keeper's full memory of both realms," he said. "As binding ink. That's what the original five swore and couldn't pay — none of them had seen both sides whole. But you have. You've seen ours, and now you've seen theirs. You're the first in sixty years who could actually sign."

The wind dropped. Even the sea below the causeway seemed to hold its breath.

"And whose memory?" Ansel asked, though some part of her, the part that had spent eleven years finding the terrible sum hidden at the bottom of every column, already knew.

"Mine," Ilex said softly. "I'm the only one here who belongs to both. Let me be the ink, Ansel. It's what I came back for."

Behind her, Tam made a small wounded noise. Vess's hand closed on Ansel's shoulder — steadying her, or steadying itself.

And through the membrane, faint and clear, the lantern-child's light flickered — once, twice — like a signal. Like a question. Like someone on the far side of a window, asking whether anyone out there had finally come to answer.

Ansel looked at the map, at the ghost of a man who had died once for this covenant and was offering to die again, and she felt the truth settle over her with the weight of a final column, the one no ledger ever wants to reach.

"No," she said quietly. "Not you. I've read enough books that end with someone else paying my bill."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the arch, because there was nowhere else to go and no strength left to go anywhere. Vess built a fire from driftwood that burned green and strange, and Tam fell asleep mid-sentence, mid-complaint, curled against Aldous's side like the old man was furniture, which Aldous tolerated with the bewildered tenderness of someone who had assumed he would die alone and unforgiven.

Ansel sat last watch, the ledger on her knees, the map open beside her.

"Ilex," she whispered. "Are you awake? However that works for you."

"A keeper never sleeps," he murmured. "It's in the job description. I wrote part of it."

"When the time comes — at the gate, with all five sigils — I need you to promise me something."

"Anything."

"If I try to take the price myself, stop me." She stared into the green flames. "Because I know myself, Ilex. I balance books. And somewhere in the back of my mind there is already a column adding up what each of us can afford to lose, and my name keeps drifting to the top of it, and I don't trust my own arithmetic anymore."

There was a long silence. When Ilex spoke again, his voice was warmer than she had ever heard it, and sadder.

"That," he said, "is the first fully honest entry you've ever made."

Above them, the membrane shimmered against the stars, and far away on the other side, in a gray and starving city, someone lit a second lantern.
