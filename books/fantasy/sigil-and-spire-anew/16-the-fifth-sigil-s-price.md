## 16. The Fifth Sigil's Price

Captain Sera Vess had walked the Meridian Guildhouse's upper corridors for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once thought of them as a maze.

Now, crouched in the shadow of a rain barrel behind the tanner's row across the street, watching her own former home glow amber and smug against the dusk, she understood that it was one. Every corridor turned back on itself. Every stair was guarded by someone whose name she knew, whose daughter she had once fished out of the harbor canal, whose wedding she had attended with a gift of good knives. That was the problem. You could not sneak past people you loved. You could only betray them, or be betrayed by them, and Vess had spent three days discovering which side of that ledger she now stood on.

"You're doing the face again," Tam said beside her.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic and the numbers are losing."

"I am always doing arithmetic," Vess said. "It is my best quality."

Ansel Winterbourne, crouched at Vess's other shoulder with the scorched remnant of Ilex's map cradled in her hands like a wounded bird, made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Three days ago Vess would have arrested her for it. Two days ago Vess had stood in front of the guild masters and refused an order to kill this exact woman, and had watched the door close behind her on everything she had built — rank, rooms, reputation, the small brass plaque on her desk that read *V., Captain, First Watch* and which she now realized she would miss more than the rank itself.

"Say it plainly," Ansel said. "The vault."

"The vault is under the counting room," Vess said. "Where you used to balance ledgers, before you became a fugitive and a nuisance."

"I was never a nuisance."

"You were a nuisance the moment you started being right." Vess settled back against the wall. "Here is the shape of it. The vault door answers to two keys: the masters' seal, which we do not have, and the captain's ring, which I am still wearing because nobody has asked for it back. The door also answers to blood-oath confirmation — the ring must be worn by the current captain, in person, speaking the closing words of the watch. *The day is counted; nothing owed goes unrecorded.*"

"Which you said every night for nineteen years," Ansel said quietly.

"Every night. It's a good oath. I meant it every time." She flexed her hand. The captain's ring sat heavy and cold, a band of gray iron with the guild's meridian line etched around it. "They'll know by morning. Possibly sooner. There will be no coming back from this, Winterbourne. Whatever happens tonight, I walk out of that building having burned the only life I knew how to live."

Tam, who was eleven and had the tact of a pickpocket, said, "Was it a good life, though? Be honest."

Vess looked down at him. He looked back up with the flat, unblinking honesty of children who had sold stolen hours in alleys and therefore had opinions about what time was worth.

"No," she said. "It was an orderly one. Those aren't the same thing."

"There," Tam said to Ansel, with the air of a man concluding a business negotiation. "Told you she'd come round. That's another copper you owe me."

"I don't recall agreeing to pay you per epiphany," Ansel said.

"That's because you weren't listening during the agreement, which is your fault, not mine."

Vess almost smiled. Almost. Then her eyes went back to the guildhouse, to the lit windows of the masters' hall where she had dined a hundred times on roast fowl and self-satisfaction, and the smile died without issue.

"One more thing," she said. "When we go in — when I open that door — you'll see things in there I've seen my whole career and explained to myself my whole career. Ledgers with names struck through. Jars. Don't ask me about the jars yet. I'll tell you everything, but not while I'm standing in it. Understood?"

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. In the map-light leaking between her fingers, her face was tired and resolute and younger than her reputation.

"Understood," she said. "We all have corridors we'd rather not walk twice."

"Yes," said Vess. "And tonight I'm walking mine in the company of a thief, a child, and a dead man's ghost. My mother would be so proud."

* * *

They went over the wall at the laundry gate, because Vess had reported its broken latch four times in six years and been ignored four times in six years, and there was a particular satisfaction in treason accomplished through bureaucratic neglect.

Inside, the guildhouse smelled as it always had: lamp oil, ink, cold stone, and beneath it all that faint sweetish wrongness Vess had never named until three days ago, when Ansel had described the pale light rising from the sealed well below and Vess had realized she'd been smelling the far realm's slow death her entire adult life.

She took them through the servants' passage behind the kitchens, past the wine stores (Tam made a noise of profound personal loss when they left them behind), up the back stair to the first watch gallery. And there, exactly as she had feared, stood Brannick.

Brannick was twenty-six, earnest, built like a door, and had been Vess's protégé since he was seventeen and she'd caught him crying in the armory after his first duty shift. He held a lantern in one hand and a halberd in the other, and when the light found her face, his expression went through surprise, joy, confusion, and horror in the space of a single breath, like a man reading a very short book with a very bad ending.

"Captain?" he said. "Captain, they said — they said you'd gone traitor. They said—" His voice cracked. "Is it true?"

Vess had planned for many contingencies. She had planned for alarms, for ambushes, for the masters themselves. She had not planned for Brannick's face.

"It's true," she said.

Behind her, she heard Ansel shift — heard, rather than saw, the ledger-keeper's instinct to intervene, calculate, negotiate — and Vess lifted one hand slightly. *Mine,* the gesture said. *This debt is mine.*

"Why?" Brannick whispered.

"Because I checked the accounts," Vess said. "You remember what I taught you. First lesson. When something doesn't add up, you don't stop asking until it does. Well. It didn't add up, Brannick. It hasn't added up for forty years, and the men upstairs have been cooking the books since before either of us could hold a pen. They've been taking years off people. Off people over there—" she gestured vaguely eastward, toward the wall, toward the gate, toward the gray country beyond it, "—and spending them here. And they ordered me to kill the woman standing behind me for finding out."

Brannick's gaze went past her shoulder to Ansel. To Tam. To the faint, drifting shimmer where the map's remnant hung in the air like heat-haze given opinions.

"The courier," Brannick said slowly. "The one who died in the counting room. That was real. We were told it was a robbery."

"It was a murder," said the shimmer, in a dry, papery voice that made Brannick take a full step backward and knock his lantern against the wall. "Though I appreciate the euphemism. Very guild. Very tidy."

"Don't mind him," Tam said cheerfully. "He's dead. He gets like that."

"Ilex," Vess said, with the patience of a woman who had learned in three days that the dead cartographer treated conversation as a competitive sport, "please. Not now."

"Now is precisely when. The boy with the halberd is deciding whether to raise the alarm, and I have watched a great many decisions get made badly in silence. Boy. Brannick, is it? Your captain taught you arithmetic and honor, and she is currently risking both to fix a theft so large it has its own weather. Ask yourself what the men upstairs would have you do to her, and then ask yourself why."

Silence. The lantern guttered. Somewhere above them, a floorboard creaked — a master walking his comfortable floor, thinking his comfortable thoughts.

Brannick lowered the halberd until its butt rested on the stones.

"I never saw you," he said hoarsely. "I saw — I saw a shadow. I chased it toward the east stair. It got away from me. Shadows do that."

"They do," Vess said, and her throat was tight in a way she decided to attribute to the dust. "Brannick. When this is done — if I'm alive, if any of us are — find me. I'll want to hear how you explain 'a shadow' to the tribunal. It should be educational."

"Captain—" He stopped. Started again. "The jars. In the vault. I did inventory once. I never told anyone what I thought they were."

"I know," Vess said gently. "I know. Go chase your shadow."

He went, and his boots faded down the east stair, and Vess stood a moment in the dark letting herself feel the specific, complicated grief of a teacher whose student has just outgrown her lessons faster than she outgrew hers.

"Well," said Ilex into the quiet. "That went better than my last encounter with guild personnel. Though admittedly the bar is subterranean."

* * *

The counting room at midnight was a country Vess did not recognize.

By day it was Ansel's kingdom — rows of desks, the tick of pens, the great brass weighing-scales at the center where impossible things were measured against each other with perfect, terrible fairness. By night it should have been empty. Instead, lamps burned low at the masters' dais, and two figures sat there: Master Corvel and Master Ives, playing at tiles, their rings glinting, a decanter of amber spirit between them.

Vess pressed herself and her companions into the alcove of the great scales and did her arithmetic fast.

Two masters. Not five. The others would be elsewhere — sleeping, or watching the gate, or whatever it was masters did at midnight that she had never been invited to and had never, until recently, wanted to be invited to. Two was manageable. Two was even, in a grim way, convenient.

"Change of plan," she breathed. "I can't reach the vault stair unseen. So I'm going to do the opposite of unseen."

Ansel stared at her. "That's your plan. Loudness."

"Loudness is a tool, Winterbourne. You of all people should respect it — you're about to make the loudest noise this city has ever heard, and you're planning to do it with paperwork." Vess straightened her collar, settled the captain's ring on her finger, and allowed herself one long breath. "Keep to the dark. If it goes wrong, take Tam and run. The sigils matter more than I do."

"That's not true," Ansel said immediately.

"It's excellent tactics."

"It's not true," Ansel repeated, with the mild immovability of a woman who had balanced ten thousand entries and knew a false one on sight. "Come back. That's an order."

"I believe," said Ilex's shimmer, drifting up near the ceiling with the smugness of a fog, "that I am the senior officer present, having died in service, and I concur with the ledger-keeper. Come back, Captain. Dead men make poor eulogists, but I am willing to try."

Vess did not answer. She stepped out of the shadows into the lamplight, boots ringing on the marble, and walked toward the dais with nineteen years of authority in her spine and nothing at all left to lose, which she was discovering was the most dangerous substance in the world.

Masters Corvel and Ives looked up. For one heartbeat, their faces performed the beautiful theater of men seeing a ghost — and then Corvel's face remembered itself and hardened into something older and colder.

"Vess." He set down his tile with a click. "You have some nerve returning."

"I have some nerve," Vess agreed, "and a ring, and the closing words of the watch, and you have a vault downstairs containing the fifth sigil, several hundred stolen years, and enough evidence to hang every master in this house twice. I've come to open it."

Ives laughed — a short, disbelieving bark. "With what army, Captain?"

"With none," Vess said. "With the truth. Which, I'm told, travels faster than armies these days. You should read the broadsheets. Oh — wait. You can't. They're about you."

Corvel rose. Up close, Vess could see what three days of distance had let her finally see: the fine tremor in his hands, the too-bright eye, the flush of a man drinking from a well he shouldn't. Years not his own, spent like coin.

"You were the best of them," Corvel said, and there was something almost like sorrow in it, which was the most obscene thing yet. "Nineteen years. I signed your commission myself. All of this—" he gestured at the guildhouse, the city, the whole shining edifice, "—rests on what's below. You think we wanted it? The covenant was failing anyway. We merely chose who starved. Would you rather it had been us? Them?" He flung a hand eastward. "They're hardly people anymore. Half of them are memories."

"Then we owe them a debt," Vess said quietly, "and this guild has always claimed to pay its debts."

She raised her ringed hand, and spoke the closing words of the watch — the ones she had spoken ten thousand times at ten thousand midnights, and which had never once, until now, been a weapon:

"*The day is counted. Nothing owed goes unrecorded.*"

The ring flared white. The floor beneath the dais shuddered, and the hidden stair unfolded from the marble like a sentence completing itself — and Corvel lunged for her, and Ives shouted for the guard, and from the darkness of the great scales came a sound Vess would carry to her grave with fondness: the unmistakable clang of a brass weighing-pan swung with intent by a former archivist, catching a master neatly across the knees.

"Sorry!" Ansel called, not sounding sorry at all. "Force majeure! It's in the ledgers!"

* * *

The vault stair went down farther than Vess had ever been permitted to go, past the level she knew, past the level she had pretended not to know about, down to a door of black iron banded with silver script. Her ring touched it, and the script drank the light, and the door opened on a cold that had nothing to do with temperature.

She had promised herself she would not describe the jars. Standing in the doorway with Ansel and Tam and the fading shimmer of a dead cartographer arrayed behind her, she found she couldn't keep the promise and didn't entirely want to. Some things deserved witnesses.

Rows of them. Glass and lead, ranked on shelves cut into living rock, each one holding a curl of pale light — a year, a decade, a life's worth of mornings — and each one labeled in a neat clerk's hand. Names. Dates. Deductions. Forty years of meticulous, unforgivable bookkeeping.

Ansel walked the shelves slowly, reading. Vess watched her stop at one jar, and go very still, and Vess knew without looking that the label bore a name from the far realm — someone who had once had mornings, and afternoons, and a voice, and had been poured out shelf by shelf to keep the masters' lamps burning.

"This one," Ansel said at last, and her voice was steady in the way of things that have decided not to break yet. "This is the fifth sigil?"

"At the back," Vess said. "In the warded case. The masters kept it closest, because it's the keystone — the one that binds the other four. Without it, your reforging is four fingers and no hand."

They crossed the vault together. The case stood where Vess remembered it, black iron and silver script, and her ring opened it as it had opened the door, and inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a stone no larger than a plum, carved with a spiral that seemed to turn when you weren't looking directly at it.

Tam reached for it. Vess caught his wrist. "Warded. Watch."

She set her ring in the case's slot and spoke the closing words a third time, and the wards sighed and died, and the fifth sigil lay bare.

Ansel lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and the moment it cleared the case, the other four sigils — sewn into her coat lining, wrapped in cloth, carried against her heart for weeks — answered. A chord sounded through the vault, through the rock, through the whole buried bones of the guildhouse: five notes finding each other after forty years apart.

And every jar on every shelf flickered. Just once. Like candles hearing their own names called.

"Oh," said Ilex softly, from somewhere above them all. "Oh, they *felt* that. Ledger-keeper. Winterbourne. Do you understand what you're holding? The covenant isn't a lock. It never was. It's a promise, and promises are made of attention, and for forty years no one on this side has paid any." The shimmer brightened, unsteady, urgent. "The gate. Tonight. Before the masters regroup — before they decide to spend everything that's left. Tonight, while the jars still remember what they are."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone, came the tolling of the alarm bell — and beneath it, worse, a deep groan of machinery waking, of the gate's seals being forced open from the masters' side.

"They're opening it," Vess said. "Partially. To pull one last draught before we can stop them."

Ansel tucked the fifth sigil against her heart with the others, and looked up the long stair toward the shouting and the light, and Vess saw on her face the expression of a woman reading the final page of an account and knowing the sum must be paid in full.

"Then we'd better go settle it," Ansel said. "All of it. Down to the last entry."

She took the stairs two at a time, and Vess — traitor, captain, keeper of a ring that no longer belonged to anything — followed her up into the noise, carrying nothing but the truth and the closing words, and finding, to her astonishment, that it was enough to run on.
