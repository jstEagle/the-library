## 2. Night Without Stars

Midwinter came to Corvane the way debt comes to a debtor — quietly at first, then all at once.

Isandro Galloway had spent the day of it doing what he always did in the week before the Long Dark: selling small mercies out of a shop that officially did not exist. His ledger — a plain thing, water-stained, kept in a cipher of his own devising — recorded no names and no prices, only debts owed to him in favors, information, and silence. That was how he'd survived twenty years in a city where magic carried a pyre's sentence: by never letting anything he did look like magic from the outside.

The charms looked like nothing. A knotted cord that kept frost from a window, sold as "old rope, good luck." A pressed sprig of rowan sewn into a coat lining, sold as "my grandmother's remedy for bad air." A coin with a hole bored through its center, sold as a curiosity, which would keep its bearer's name out of the mouth of anyone meaning them harm for one turn of the moon. Small things. Splinters, though Isandro did not know yet what they were splinters *of*. He thought of them as drippings from a fire he tended without understanding — the gift Aldous had left him, or perhaps the gift Aldous had *made* him, the one that let him see through ink into places ink had no business reaching.

That was the secret beneath the secret: not the charms, but the maps. When Isandro drew, truly drew — quill moving without his full consent, lines finding themselves the way rivers find the sea — he could see realms that appeared on no chart in the Spire's archive. Vhalen, mostly. Always Vhalen, at the edge of every page, like a country standing just behind his shoulder. He had never told a living soul. He had barely told himself.

On the last afternoon of the old year, a girl bought a knot of red thread from him for two coppers and a lie about her mother's health.

She couldn't have been more than nine, all elbows and soot, hair cropped short against lice, standing on the balls of her feet at his counter as if the floor might be pulled away at any moment — which, in the Warrens, it occasionally was. Her name was Pip, or she offered something like Pip, and she said her mother coughed at night in a way that frightened her, and she'd heard — everyone heard things, in the Warrens; rumor was the only currency that never devalued — that the map-seller on Candle Lane could fix things that weren't sickness.

Isandro should have turned her away. He turned most people away; it was the price of staying small. But she set the coppers down with the terrible solemnity of children paying with money they needed elsewhere, and she looked up at him with eyes too old for the face around them, and he thought of himself at that age, standing in another shop, buying hope from another man who dealt in it illegally.

"Red thread," he said, and wound three turns of it around his little finger while her attention drifted to the jars behind him. Under the counter, where she couldn't see, he breathed the smallest working into the knots — not a cure, because he could not cure, but a holding. A charm against collapse, against falling beams and rotten stairs and the thousand ways the Warrens killed the poor in their sleep. It would buy time. Time was the only honest product he sold.

"Keep this near her when she sleeps," he said. "Under the pillow. Don't tell her what it's for. Tell her it's for luck."

"Luck isn't real," Pip said, pocketing it with a merchant's efficiency.

"No," Isandro agreed. "But neither is the cold, if you're warm enough."

He watched her go through the fogged glass of his door, a small dark shape swallowed by the gray press of Candle Lane, and felt the familiar quiet dread settle over him like snowfall. Twenty years. Twenty years of small mercies and smaller risks, each one a coin slipped into a slot somewhere he couldn't see, feeding a machine whose purpose he had never learned. Survival has always had a price. He had simply been fortunate enough, until now, to pay it in installments.

He closed early. The sky was already going the bruised color it went before deep cold, and there was a wrongness in the air he couldn't name — a pressure, like the moment before a sneeze, or before a wall gives way. He banked his stove, checked his wards — the real ones, woven into the doorframe and the chimney and the loose flagstone under the counter — and sat down at his drafting table with a fresh sheet of paper, because the wrongness was worse when his hands were empty.

What he drew that evening, he did not choose to draw.

His quill moved and he let it, the way a man lets a sleepwalker relative wander the house. Coastline first — no, not coastline, boundary-line, the long seam where Corvane ended and Vhalen began, rendered in his private shorthand of hatching and stippling that no other cartographer alive could read. The line shivered under his hand. He frowned at it. In twenty years of drawing that border, it had never once wavered. It was the one fixed thing in his sight, more fixed than Corvane itself, more fixed than the sun's arc. Wards held it, old ones, older than the Spire, older than any institution still standing, and in all his life of seeing, they had never so much as flickered.

They were flickering now. Flickering the way a candle does when someone has opened a door in a far room.

Isandro put down the quill. Picked it up. Drew again, faster, the line coming out ragged, and where it ragged he saw — not with his eyes, with whatever it was that lived behind his eyes — a darkness pressing against the wards from the other side. Patient darkness. Starless darkness. Not attacking. *Waiting.* As if it knew something he didn't.

Outside, a dog began to howl, then stopped mid-note, as if it had been unplugged from the world.

Isandro stood so quickly his chair went over backward. The pressure in the air had doubled, tripled, become a weight on the chest, and every hair on his arms rose, and somewhere across the city a church bell began ringing with no one pulling the rope — he knew this because he could hear it, faintly, absurdly, tolling out of rhythm, tolling the way bells ring when their towers are shaking.

Then the stars went out.

Not clouded over. Not veiled. *Out* — all of them, everywhere, between one heartbeat and the next, as if some vast hand had pinched the sky's wick. Isandro was standing at his window when it happened, and he saw it directly: the whole bowl of heaven going black in a single instant, the winter constellations — the Plowman, the Sisters, the pale scatter above the Spire's tallest tower — erased like breath wiped from glass.

The silence afterward lasted four seconds. He counted them. Four seconds of absolute stillness, the whole city holding its breath without knowing why, and in those four seconds Isandro understood, with the calm clarity that sometimes arrives in place of terror, that everything he had built was provisional. Every charm, every mapped line, every careful year. Provisional, all of it, rented rather than owned, and the landlord had come.

Then the wards shattered, and the sound arrived like breaking glass heard through bone.

It did not come through the ears. It came up through the floor, through the soles of his boots, through the marrow of him, a crystalline shriek that ran along structures no anatomy acknowledged. Glass broke in his windows — actual glass, all of it, every pane slumping outward in glittering sheets. Jars shattered on their shelves. The inkwells burst, black spatter crossing the room like thrown knives. And beneath it all, underneath the physical ruin, Isandro heard the true sound: the border-wards of Vhalen dying, all of them at once, a chord of a thousand struck bells snapping off one by one, a sound with a shape, and the shape was a word, and the word was —

He didn't hear it. He felt it branded into him instead, because at that moment the sigil on his palm woke for the first time in twenty years.

It had been there since he was eleven years old. Aldous had pressed his thumb to the boy's hand one night by the workshop fire, an odd, formal gesture, half blessing and half apology, and left a mark like a compass rose drawn in old scar tissue. It had never done anything. Isandro had long ago filed it among the unexplained costs of being raised by Aldous Fenwick, alongside the lessons in dead languages and the questions never answered. Now it blazed. Now it *burned*, white-hot, a brand renewed, and light poured off his own hand and lit the wrecked shop in stark relief, and through the pain he felt it pulling.

Not metaphorically. Pulling. His whole body listed toward the north wall like a plant toward sun, and the pull had direction, and the direction was *outward*, toward the edge of the city, toward the fallen ward-line, toward the place where the sound had come from. The sigil wanted him to walk. The sigil had apparently always wanted him to walk, and had merely been waiting for the world to break first.

"No," Isandro said aloud, to his own hand, which was ridiculous, and then the second wave hit.

*

Fire took the workshop in minutes.

It started, he would swear ever after, in the drafting table itself — in the map, in the ragged border he had drawn with a shaking hand, the lines smoldering where his quill had pressed hardest, ink igniting without flame touching it. By the time he grabbed the ledger and the go-bag he kept packed for exactly the future now arriving, heat was peeling paint from the walls, and the smoke was the wrong color, greenish, shot through with motes of light that moved against the draft. Magic burning. His own work, twenty years of it, combusting into the winter air over Candle Lane, and every wisp of it a confession written in fire for anyone with the training to read.

He got out through the cellar. Of course he got out through the cellar; he'd built the escape route himself years ago and told himself it was paranoia, and paranoia, it turned out, had simply been foresight wearing a disguise. He came up through a coal chute two streets away, into air so cold it burned the throat, and stood in the alley with the ledger against his chest and watched the glow of his own life rising over the rooftops.

People were in the streets. That was the horror of it, and the strangeness. Midwinter night, the deepest cold of the year, starless, and Corvane had emptied itself outdoors. Old men in nightshirts stood in the middle of intersections staring straight up. Children cried, or laughed — both, unpredictably, the way children respond to the end of the world. Somewhere a priest of the Spire rang a warning bell badly. Somewhere else, farther, a building screamed — a tenement, timber cracking, stone shifting — and even from here Isandro could hear the particular groan of a structure deciding to die.

He thought of Pip. Two coppers and a lie about her mother's health. Red thread under a pillow, somewhere in the Warrens, in a district full of leaning buildings that had just lost whatever invisible architecture had been helping hold them up.

He ran. He told himself it was instinct, duty, the debt every seller owes every buyer; he told himself a great many things in the eleven minutes it took him to cross to the Warrens at a dead sprint, lungs full of broken-glass air. What he did not tell himself was the truth, which was simpler: the sigil was pulling him northward, and the Warrens lay northward, and he followed the burn in his palm like a compass needle and called the following something nobler than obedience.

He found the tenement by its dust cloud.

Three stories of rot and poverty, folded down into itself like a dropped cake, a blossom of plaster powder still drifting over the street. Neighbors were already clawing at the rubble with bare hands, and the cold was turning their breath to ghosts, and the noise they made was the worst sound Isandro had ever heard — worse than the wards dying, because the wards had died cleanly, instantly, and this was slow, and human, and wet.

"Pip!" Someone was shouting the name. It took him several seconds to realize the voice was his. "Pip! Girl in the green coat! Green coat!"

They found the mother first, or what the mother had become, and Isandro looked away and made himself look back, because he had sold the girl that thread and he would not insult the dead by flinching. Then a neighbor woman gave a cry and dug fast, frantic, and lifted out — alive, filthy, bleeding from the scalp, curled into a ball around a scrap of red thread clutched in one fist — the child.

Pip's eyes found him through the dust. Too-old eyes, wide now with a child's proper terror, and she held up the thread like evidence, like proof of a miracle, and mouthed something at him through the ringing chaos: *it held.*

Isandro's legs nearly went out from under him. He knelt in the wreckage-adjacent snow and let the neighbors pass the girl hand to hand to a healer, and he stayed kneeling long after, palms flat on the frozen ground, shaking. She lived. One small mercy had held, one knot of thread had done the one thing it was made for, and it should have been comfort, and instead it tasted like ash, because he understood suddenly and completely what he had never allowed himself to understand:

The charms worked *because* the covenant held. Whatever pact Aldous had bound him into, whatever ancient machinery kept Vhalen anchored and the borders sealed and the small magics of Corvane limping along in the dark — it had been bearing his weight all along, all their weights, all the hidden workers' and hedge-witches' and desperate sellers' weights, silently, for centuries. And tonight it had failed. Tonight the bill had come due for everyone, retroactively, with interest, and the red thread had held only because it was spending the last reserves of a structure already collapsing.

Everything he'd built was provisional. Everything everyone had built. The stars were out, the wards were down, and the darkness he'd seen pressing patiently against the border of his maps was no longer pressed against anything at all.

The sigil burned. Northward. Outward. Toward the ward-line.

He should stay. Help dig. There were voices still under the rubble, and hands enough to find them if he added his. It was the right thing, the decent thing, the thing Aldous would have —

No. Aldous would have gone north. Everything about tonight, every clue, every waking instinct in his scarred palm, said that Aldous had known this night was coming, had spent decades preparing a boy for it, and had vanished rather than be present for it. If Isandro wanted answers, they lay along the fallen border, not under Corvane rubble. And if he wanted to survive the week, the same was true, because when the Spire's inquisitors came — and they would come, before dawn, drawn to magical fire like crows to a battlefield — they would find a burned shop and a ledger's ashes and a trail leading out of the city, and they would follow it.

Terror and exhilaration arrived together, twins, indistinguishable, sharing one heartbeat. He recognized them both. Terror was familiar; he'd lived beside it twenty years. But the other feeling — the hot, shameful lift in his chest, the sense of a door opening after decades of corridors — that one frightened him worse, because it felt like coming home to a house he'd never lived in.

Isandro Galloway wrapped his ruined scarf over his face, clutched the ledger to his chest, and walked north out of the only city he had ever loved, past the staring crowds, past the weeping rubble, past the last lamplight of the mortal world. Behind him his workshop burned green. Above him the sky was a lid of perfect black. Ahead of him, beyond the hills, the ward-line lay broken and singing, and the sigil in his palm pulled like a hooked fish.

At the northern gate — open, unguarded, its watchmen staring upward at the starless dark — he paused once, and looked back, and spoke to the burning glow on the horizon as if the city could hear him.

"I'll come back," he said. "Whatever I am when I get there."

The wind off the borderlands answered in a voice almost like breaking glass.

He walked into the dark, and the dark, patient no longer, walked toward him.
