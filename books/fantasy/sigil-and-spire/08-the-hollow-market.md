## 8. The Hollow Market

Corvane had always smelled of tallow and river mud, but the city that rose out of the fog that night smelled of something else — something scorched and sweet, like sugar burned in a pan. Isandro stood at the edge of the Graywater district with his hood up and his new face — the one Maren's glamours had given him, a broad peasant jaw, a broken nose, eyes the color of dishwater — and felt his stomach turn before he'd taken a single step.

He had told himself he was coming back for a book. One book. Aldous's grimoire, hidden behind the loose brick in the workshop chimney, or under the floorboards, or wherever else the old man had stashed it in one of his paranoid moods. A simple errand. In and out before dawn.

But Corvane had changed, and the change had a smell.

The stars were still gone. That was the thing no one in Vhalen's borderlands could quite grasp when he tried to describe it: the sky over Corvane hadn't merely darkened, it had *emptied*, as if someone had reached up and scooped out every point of light and left the black behind. People had stopped looking up. That was the first sign of rot, Maren had said. When people stop looking up, they start looking sideways — at their neighbors, at their doors, at what they can grab.

The second sign of rot was the market.

* * *

He found it by following the light.

Not lamplight — there was barely any of that anymore; the Spire had rationed oil since the blackout, and whole streets went dark at dusk like drowned men. This light came from below, from the sunken cisterns beneath the old tannery quarter, where the city's founders had once stored water for sieges that never came. Now the water was gone and something else was stored there instead.

Isandro descended the rusted ladder into a cavern of murmuring voices and blue-white glow, and stopped on the last rung, and forgot how to breathe.

Charms. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They hung from wires strung between the cistern pillars like laundry, like lanterns, like fruit ripening on a vine — little glass vials and knotted cords and pressed-tin medallions, each one pulsing with a soft, sourceless radiance. He recognized the craft immediately. Of course he did. He had invented half of it.

But these were wrong. These were *hollow*.

His own charms had always been splinters of the covenant — small, honest fragments of a vast old pact, shaped into luck-wards and fever-knots and finding-stones. They carried power the way a cup carries water: borrowed, contained, returnable. What hung in the Hollow Market carried power the way a wound carries infection. The sigils on them had been scraped off other workings, peeled from ward-stones and stolen relics and — his throat tightened — from the collapsed border wards themselves, the great dying lattice that had once held two realms together. Someone was harvesting the corpse of the covenant and selling it by the ounce.

"First time down?" said a voice at his elbow.

The speaker was a woman with a merchant's smile and a moneylender's eyes, wearing a coat lined with more vials than any sane person should carry against their skin. She looked him up and down with professional efficiency and apparently decided the broken-nosed peasant was worth her time.

"Looking for something specific? Health? Luck? Love?" She leaned closer, dropping her voice to a confessional murmur. "I've got protection charms straight off the Spire's own boundary stones. Genuine article. The conclave's been pulling wards all winter — don't ask me why, ask them — and every stone they strip leaves... leavings." Her fingers walked along a cord of glowing beads. "The city's frightened, friend. Frightened people buy."

Isandro kept his voice low and stupid. "Is it safe? The stuff."

Her smile didn't waver, but something behind it flickered — the look of a woman who had answered this question many times and had refined the answer into a weapon. "Safe as anything is now. Safer than the streets above, where the Spire burns people for a birthmark. Down here we're honest about what we are." She pressed a warm vial into his palm, uninvited. "Fever-charm. For your wife, your mother, whoever. First one's free. The second one costs."

The charm hummed against his skin, and Isandro felt it the way a musician feels a false note. The power inside it was real but ragged, torn from its context, its edges fraying already. In a week it would decay into something worse than useless. In a month, depending on what it had been scraped from, it might curdle into something hungry.

He knew all this because he had spent twenty years learning exactly how much power a small vessel could hold. And he had sold his charms anyway, night after night, telling himself each one was honest, each one measured, each one safe — while the market around him grew fat on the idea that magic could be bought in handfuls by anyone with coin and desperation.

"How long has this been here?" he asked.

"Since the sky went out." She shrugged, as if describing weather. "Before that, folk were afraid of magic. Now they're afraid of everything else, and magic looks like the lesser fear." She patted his cheek with two ringed fingers, an oddly maternal gesture that made his skin crawl. "Welcome to the new Corvane, friend. Everybody's a sorcerer now. Nobody's a good one."

* * *

He should have gone straight to the workshop. He told himself so, twice, three times, walking through the tannery district's crooked lanes with his collar up and the free fever-charm burning a hole in his pocket like a coal. But the market had loosened something in him, some seal he'd kept on his own guilt for twenty years, and when he heard a familiar voice — thin, wheezing, unmistakable — coming from a doorway near the tanner's row, his feet turned before his mind gave the order.

Old Tam Vetch sat on a stoop in the lee of a shuttered chandlery, wrapped in three coats despite the season, haggling with a hollow-market runner over a knotted cord.

Tam. Who had bought a hearing-charm from Isandro four years ago because his ears were going and his trade depended on overhearing the right conversations at the right auctions. Who had paid too much and gotten a discount anyway because Isandro had liked him, genuinely liked him, in the way you can only like someone you see six times a year and never have to know. Who had once told him, pressing a pastry into his hands like a bribe, *"You're a good lad, Galloway. Whatever it is you're not telling me about these trinkets of yours, I can tell you make them with care."*

With care. As if care were the same as safety. As if love excused the arithmetic.

"—no, no, the fever one," Tam was saying, his ruined voice cracking. "My granddaughter's got the winter sickness, the bad kind. The apothecary wants eight crowns just to look at her. Eight! So it's the charm or nothing, isn't it, and nothing's not—"

"That's a fever-knot," the runner said, bored, holding up a cord that made Isandro's skin crawl. He could see the working inside it, or rather the absence of a working — a scraped sigil, a hollowed shell, power sloshing around inside it with no shape to hold it. "Strong stuff. Off a Spire ward-stone, genuine. Ten coppers."

"It won't hurt her?"

"Friend, it's *medicine*."

It will hurt her, Isandro thought. It might kill her. That knot was scraped from a boundary ward, and boundary wards are built to repel, to burn, to hold a line against things that want to cross — put that intent against a sick child's fever and you might as well press a hot iron to her skin and call it a poultice.

"Wait," Isandro heard himself say.

Both of them looked up. The runner's eyes narrowed — a big peasant man, hooded, alone, interfering with a sale — but Isandro was already digging in his pocket, already holding out the free fever-charm the merchant woman had given him. Even that one was better than the knot. Even that hollow, decaying scrap was kinder than what the runner held.

"Take this one instead. It's paid for. It's gentler."

Tam squinted at him. Four years of aging had bent the old man nearly double, but the squint was the same, and for one horrible moment Isandro saw recognition move behind it like a fish under ice — the voice, maybe, or the way he held out his hand.

"You sound like—" Tam began.

"Take it," Isandro said quickly. "Please."

Tam took it. The runner spat, muttered something about lost commissions, and melted into the dark with his poisoned cord. Isandro helped the old man to his feet, feeling how light he had become, how the coats hung off him like sails on a becalmed ship.

"The fever-charm's good for three days," Isandro said. "Keep it against her chest, under the shift, and don't let anyone tell you to buy a second one. If she's not better in three days, get the apothecary. Find the money somehow. The charms—" His voice caught. He steadied it. "The charms run out. Only people last."

Tam looked at him for a long moment, and then did something worse than recognize him. He smiled, patted Isandro's arm with a hand like a bundle of twigs, and said:

"Bless you, son. You remind me of a young fellow used to sell me charms. Good lad. Made them with care." He chuckled, wet and rattling. "Wonder what happened to him. Probably burned, like the rest. Whole city's gone to hell, hasn't it?"

"Yes," Isandro said. "It has."

* * *

The workshop was ash.

He had known it would be — he had watched it burn from a rooftop on the night the stars went out, had felt the fire take his twenty years of work and eat them in an hour — but knowing and standing in it were different countries. The walls stood to shoulder height, blackened. The roof was a lattice of char. Snow fell through where the ceiling used to be and hissed on the embers that still, impossibly, smoldered underneath, five weeks later, as if the fire refused to admit it was finished.

And the chimney still stood.

Of course it did. Aldous had built it, and Aldous built everything twice: once for use and once for hiding. Isandro climbed over the fallen beam, boots crunching on the bones of his own life, and put his hand into the flue where a child who had once been him had watched his mentor hide things a hundred times. Third course of brick, left side, behind the soot shelf.

His fingers found the loose brick. Found the cavity behind it.

Found nothing.

For a moment he simply knelt there in the snow and ash, hand buried in a dead chimney, and let the cold climb up his arm and into his chest. Then he made himself think. Aldous never hid anything in only one place. *A cartographer keeps copies,* the old man used to say. *Never trust a single line to hold your whole map.* There was a second cache — there had to be — and Isandro closed his eyes and let himself remember the workshop as it had been, wall by wall, beam by beam, hunting for the detail he'd noticed a hundred times and never questioned: the floorboard under the drafting table that sat a finger's width higher than its neighbors, worn smooth on one edge as if lifted often.

He dug through the ash until he found the joist line, and pried, and the board came up with a groan of wet charcoal, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth sealed with wax that had kept the water and fire both at bay, lay a flat iron box.

Inside the box: the grimoire. Aldous's true grimoire, not the teaching copy Isandro had studied as a boy but the working text, bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its pages dense with the old man's cramped, slanted hand. And beneath it, folded twice, a letter bearing Isandro's own name — his real name, written in ink that seemed to drink the light.

He didn't open the letter. Not yet. Not here, kneeling in the ruin with the Spire's patrols surely circling the district and the Hollow Market's merchants knowing his old route home. He wrapped the box again, strapped it beneath his coat against his chest, where it lay like a second heart, heavy and beating with somebody else's secrets.

He was halfway across the workshop floor when he heard the shouting from the direction of Tanner's Row.

* * *

He told himself it wasn't Tam's street. He told himself the crowd meant a raid, a patrol, a fight over market territory — any of the hundred miseries a starless city produced nightly. He told himself all of this while his feet carried him toward the noise faster and faster, until he was running, until he broke through the ring of gawking neighbors into a circle of torchlight where a door stood open on a stairwell, and a girl of perhaps nine was screaming down into the dark, and men were carrying something up the steps wrapped in blankets.

They laid it — him — on the stoop, and Isandro saw the three coats, and the twig-bundle hands, and the face gone grey as the ash in his workshop.

"Fever-charm," a woman was saying, the neighbor from downstairs, wringing her hands. "He bought it for the little one, put it against her chest like the seller said, and within the hour she was screaming and he took it off her and held it himself to draw it out, the fool, the dear old fool—"

The charm. The one Isandro had given him. The gentle one. The better one.

Isandro pushed through and knelt, and took Tam's wrist, and felt for a pulse with fingers that had drawn a thousand precise lines and could not find one true beat. The old man's eyes fluttered open, unfocused, swimming. His lips moved. Isandro bent close.

"There y'are," Tam whispered, and there was no confusion in it at all, no mistake, only a tired old man greeting a ghost he'd always half-suspected. "Knew it. Knew your voice. Good lad." A breath, rattling like wind through the burnt workshop. "Made 'em with care, didn't you."

"I'm sorry," Isandro said. The words came out cracked, childish, useless. "Tam, I'm sorry, I didn't know it would—"

"'S all right." The old hand twitched in his, patting, patting, the same gesture from the stoop, from years of pastries and bargains and small kindnesses traded across a counter built on secrets. "Everything runs out. Only people last. Told you." The eyes drifted toward the screaming girl, now quieting in her grandmother's arms, alive, alive because a dying man had pulled a poisoned charm against his own chest. "She'll do. You see she does."

Then the hand stopped patting, and the rattle stopped rattling, and Old Tam Vetch, who had trusted a good lad with careful hands, died on his own stoop under a sky with no stars in it.

Isandro stayed kneeling until the neighbors gently pulled him back, until someone covered the face, until the crowd began to murmur and shift the way crowds do when grief turns to suspicion. *Who sold the charm? Where did it come from?* He slipped away before the questions found him, moving through the dark lanes on legs that belonged to someone else, the iron box pressed against his chest like a stone.

* * *

Dawn found him on the city wall's northern stair, where the watch never climbed anymore because there was nothing left to watch for — no ships, no signals, no stars. Below him Corvane sprawled in the grey half-light, chimneys smoking, markets opening, the Hollow Market's blue glow long since banked but still present somehow, a stain under the skin of the city. Somewhere down there a nine-year-old girl was breathing because her grandfather had died of a kindness Isandro had handed him like a gift.

Every shortcut he had ever sold came back to him at once. Every fever-knot and finding-stone and hearing-charm, every measured, careful, honest splinter of a stolen covenant, placed into desperate hands because desperate hands paid and he needed to eat and it was only a little, only a trinket, only ever a little. He had told himself his charms were cups, not wounds. But he had never asked where the water came from. He had never asked what it cost to keep the well full, or who was paying, or what happened when the well ran dry and the whole thirsty city learned to dig for itself with broken tools.

Maren had told him once, in the early days of his exile, with that terrible clarity of hers: *You think you sold trinkets. You taught a city to smoke opium and called it medicine.* He had hated her for it. He hated her less now. He hated himself in exact proportion.

The letter in the iron box pressed against his ribs. *Isandro Galloway,* in ink that drank the light. Aldous's hand. Aldous, who had raised him and used him, who had hidden him and hunted him, who had known — must have known — that the wards were failing, that the market would bloom in the rot, that a boy with careful hands would sell whatever the desperate would buy.

He opened the box one last time, took out the letter, and broke the seal.

The first line read: *If you are reading this, then the stars are gone, and I am sorry, and you are going to hate me, and you should.*

Below it, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, almost a prayer, was a sentence that stopped Isandro's breath:

*What I bound, I bound unwilling. Ask the woman of the Broken Coast what the covenant asks of its keepers — and then decide, boy, whether you mean to pay it, break it, or rewrite it entirely.*

Maren. The letter named Maren.

Isandro stood on the wall with the paper trembling in his hands, watching the sun rise over a city that had learned to eat its own magic, and understood that the grimoire in his arms was not an answer. It was a question — and the woman who wanted the covenant destroyed had been asking it far longer than he had.
