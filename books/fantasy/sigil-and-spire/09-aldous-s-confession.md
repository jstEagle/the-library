## 9. Aldous's Confession

The grimoire did not want to be opened.

Isandro felt it the moment he lifted it from the false bottom of Aldous's traveling chest — a resistance like wet wool, like a hand pressing gently but firmly against his sternum. The book was bound in gray leather gone soft with age, its spine stitched with thread that gleamed faintly silver when it caught the lamplight. Silver thread. He had seen that stitching before, on the border wards, on the sigil now living in his palm like a second heartbeat.

Outside the shuttered room, Maren's exiles kept their watchfires low. Somewhere below, a kettle whistled and was hastily muffled. The Broken Coast camp was quiet in the way of people who had learned that noise invited attention.

He set the book on the table between them and did not touch it again for a long moment.

"You knew what this was," he said.

Maren sat across from him, her hands wrapped around a clay cup of tea she had not drunk. Firelight moved along the planes of her face, catching the old scar at her jaw — a thin white line he'd never asked about. "I knew what Aldous was," she said. "That's not the same thing."

"It might be."

She set down the cup. "Open it, then. Or don't. But open it knowing that whatever's written in there was written by a man who loved you, and loved you *wrong*, and those are often the most dangerous confessions to read."

Isandro thought of twenty years of mornings: Aldous at the workbench grinding pigments, humming off-key, teaching him how to hold a pen so the ink flowed without trembling. *A steady hand, boy. A map is a promise. Never make one you can't keep.* He thought of the old man's death — sudden, unremarkable, a heart giving out in a chair by the window — and how Isandro had grieved cleanly, simply, without suspicion.

The grief curdled now into something with edges.

He opened the book.

* * *

The first pages were ordinary enough — supply lists, pigment recipes, marginal sketches of ward-stones. Aldous's handwriting, cramped and precise. Then, forty pages in, the script changed. It grew larger, looser, as though the pen had been moving faster than the man could think, and the ink shifted color depending on the angle of the light: brown, then black, then a deep arterial red that made Isandro's stomach tighten.

And the voice changed too. This was no ledger. This was a confession, written the way some men write letters they intend to burn.

*I write this because someone must know, and I cannot say it aloud. To say it aloud would be to hear it.*

*They teach in Corvane that the covenant between our realm and Vhalen is ancient — older than the Spire, older than the city, a treaty signed by wise kings under benevolent stars. I know because I helped forge that story. I helped forge the covenant itself, thirty-one years ago, and it was neither ancient nor benevolent, and there were no kings, and the stars had nothing to do with it.*

*There was a god.*

Isandro's breath went shallow. Beside him, Maren had gone very still, the stillness of someone bracing against a blow she has already taken once.

*Not a demon, whatever the Spire's catechisms say. Not a devil out of the pit. A god of Vhalen — the god, perhaps; the land itself given will and voice, the thing that made the other realm breathe. When the boundary between worlds first thinned, it did not come through as an invader. It came through as a supplicant. It wanted passage, commerce, communion. It offered terms.*

*We refused its terms. They were generous, which frightened us more than cruelty would have. So we took it instead. We bound it beneath the gate at the world's seam, and we made its own power the mortar of the wall that held it. Every ward, every charm, every blessing poured out of that captivity. The stars over both realms burn on borrowed fire — its fire. The covenant is not a treaty. It is a cage, and we built it, and we called it peace.*

Isandro turned the page with fingers that did not feel like his own.

*A cage requires a key. A binding requires a binder. The rite demanded a mortal anchor — a living mind to hold the pattern steady, to be the hinge between the god's captivity and the world's continuation. The anchor must consent. That was the one term even we could not violate; the magic would not take an unwilling vessel. It would only hollow them, uselessly, and the binding would fail.*

*So we asked. We always asked. That was our great self-forgiveness — we asked, and someone always said yes, because we chose the desperate and the dying and the ones who could be made to believe their sacrifice was holy. Three anchors in thirty-one years. Each one gave their memories, their name, their self, into the pattern, and each one dissolved into the ward-line like sugar into water. The realm stayed anchored. The stars stayed lit. And I told myself every time that it was necessary, that the alternative was the sundering of two worlds, and perhaps it was true, and perhaps truth is just the lie you've stopped examining.*

*The last anchor is failing. I have felt it fraying for a year, the way you feel a tooth loosen. And I have found the next.*

Isandro stopped reading. His eyes had snagged on the sentence the way a boot snags on a root, and he had to go back and read it twice before the words would admit their meaning.

*The next anchor must carry the sight — the gift of seeing unmapped realms through ink — or the pattern cannot pass through them. Such gifts appear perhaps once in a generation, and rarely in anyone willing. I searched for eleven years. Then I found a boy in the Lamplight District who drew maps of places he had never been, and drew them true.*

*His mother owed me money. It was easy. It was all so easy, and I despise myself, and I did it anyway.*

The room tilted. Isandro gripped the table's edge. Twenty years. Twenty years of apprenticeship, of belonging, of being *chosen* — and here it was in Aldous's own hand: purchased. A debt collected. A boy acquired the way one acquires a bolt of cloth or a jar of pigment, and raised not out of love but out of *husbandry*, the patient tending of a crop intended for harvest.

"Isandro." Maren's voice came from very far away. "Breathe."

"He bought me." The words came out flat, stripped of anything. "My mother's debt. He—"

"Keep reading." Her voice cracked on it. "You need to keep reading. Because if you stop there, you'll hate him, and you should, but you'll miss the part that matters."

* * *

The confession ran on through the small hours. Isandro read until his eyes burned, and Maren read over his shoulder, and neither of them spoke except when one of them needed the other to confirm that yes, the words said what the words said.

The mechanics of the renewal were laid out with Aldous's terrible precision. The anchor must travel to the gate at the seam of worlds. Must stand upon the anchor stone. Must speak the covenant aloud, line by line, while surrendering into the pattern everything that constituted the self: memory first, then name, then the deeper strata beneath the name — the loves, the griefs, the private weather of a soul. What remained was not a corpse and not a person. It was a hinge. A living hinge, aware in some dim vegetable fashion, holding the door shut for as long as the pattern held.

The previous anchors had lasted decades each. Their minds eroded slowly, spread thin across the ward-line like butter across too much bread.

"There's a clause," Maren said quietly, near dawn. She reached past him and touched a passage near the end. "Here. Read it again."

*One mercy we built into the rite, whether from conscience or cowardice I no longer know: the sacrifice must be willing at every moment of the giving. If the anchor's consent fails — if, at any point in the dissolution, the self recoils and refuses — the rite halts. The partial memories already surrendered do not return. But the remainder remains, and the person walks away diminished, incomplete, missing pieces they can name but never recover.*

*Three anchors have begun the rite. None has refused. I do not know whether this proves their faith or my skill at choosing those with nothing left to lose.*

"I had nothing left to lose," Maren said.

Isandro looked up. The lamp had burned low; her face was half shadow, and in the shadow she looked younger and infinitely tired.

"You've read this before," he said. "That's why you knew about the vessel. That's why you—" He stopped, because the sentence finished itself in his head with a clarity that felt like falling. "It was you."

"My name wasn't Maren then." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the shape of humor worn smooth by years of handling. "Maren is a name I took off a drowned girl on the Broken Coast, because she didn't need it anymore and I did. Before that I was called something else, by a man who found me in a debtor's row in Harrowgate, sick and starving and — importantly — gifted. He fed me. He taught me. He told me I was chosen." Her mouth twisted. "Does any of this sound familiar?"

Isandro couldn't answer.

"Aldous was kind to me for six years," she went on. "Kind the way a farmer is kind to a field. And then the failing anchor began to fail faster than expected, and one night he sat me down at a table much like this one, with a book much like this one, and explained with tears in his eyes what a holy thing I was about to do." She turned her cup slowly on the table, around and around. "And the worst part — the part I've never told anyone — is that I almost said yes. I wanted to say yes. He had made himself so dear to me that refusing felt like murder. I got as far as the gate. I stood on the stone. I began the rite."

"What happened?"

"I remembered my sister's face." Simple as that. She said it the way one reports the weather. "Whatever else the rite strips away, it goes in order — recent memories first, oldest last — and somewhere in the first minutes of dissolution, it reached back far enough to show me my sister teaching me to swim in the Graywater shallows, and something in me dug in its heels and *refused*. The rite broke. I walked away with holes in me the size of a childhood." She finally drank the cold tea, grimacing at it as if surprised by its taste. "Aldous didn't pursue me. That's the other thing you should know about him. He let me go. He wrote me a letter, once a year, every year, asking nothing. I burned them all unread for the first five years. Then I started reading them. Then I stopped receiving them, because he died, and I learned of it four months late like a stranger."

The fire had burned down to coals. Isandro became aware of his own body again — the ache in his neck, the cold in his feet, the sigil in his palm pulsing with a rhythm slightly slower than his heart, as though it were patiently waiting for the rest of him to catch up.

"Why tell me this?" he asked. "Why give me reasons to run? You want the covenant destroyed. An anchor who refuses serves you better than one who consents."

"Because I'm done doing to people what Aldous did to me." She said it fiercely, leaning forward. "I brought you here to offer you truth, and truth means all of it — including the parts that argue against me. You deserve to choose with your eyes open. That's the whole difference between us and the Spire, Isandro. That's the *only* difference, and if I lose it, I'm just another Aldous with worse manners."

He looked down at the grimoire, at the red-brown-black ink, at the handwriting that had taught him his letters and now confessed to purchasing him like livestock. Horror and grief sat in his chest side by side, and beneath them, unwelcome and persistent, something that was not either of those things: understanding. Aldous had bound a god and sacrificed three souls and groomed a child, and had done all of it believing — genuinely, desperately — that the alternative was the death of two worlds. The road to the anchor stone was paved with exactly that reasoning. Isandro could feel its pull even now, the seductive arithmetic of necessity.

Both his mentors had faced this choice. Both had chosen survival — the world's survival, their own principles' survival — over principle itself.

And now the choice sat in front of him wearing his own face.

"The stars are going out," he said slowly. "The wards are broken. If I refuse, what happens? Truly. Not the way Aldous framed it. Truthfully."

Maren was silent for a long time. When she answered, her voice had lost its fierceness and become something rawer.

"Truly? I don't know. The realms won't tear apart tomorrow. Maybe not this year. Things cross over — you've seen them. People die. Borders blur. Perhaps the two worlds learn to live loose against each other, the way I've always dreamed. And perhaps it ends in slow ruin, famine and madness bleeding through a wound that never closes, and everyone who dies of it dies because I convinced a cartographer to be proud instead of careful." She met his eyes. "I've had thirty years to make peace with that possibility. You've had one night. So I won't push you tonight. Tonight I'll say only this: the rite as written is a cage built on a cage. Even if you consent, even if you pay everything — you'd be renewing a prison and calling it salvation, and the god underneath would still be screaming."

"Then what's your answer? Break it? Free it?"

"That's *my* answer. I need it to be yours, or it's worth nothing."

Isandro closed the grimoire. His palm throbbed. Through the shutters, the sky beyond the coast was beginning to pale toward a starless gray dawn, and he realized with a lurch that he had spent the whole night reading and had not once looked up, had not once checked — out of some superstitious dread — whether the darkness above still held no lights at all.

He rose and pushed open the shutter.

Black sky. Empty black sky, vast and blind, the sea beneath it reflecting nothing but their own small fire. Somewhere out in that darkness, sealed behind a failing gate, the stars of two realms waited like coins in a locked room.

And somewhere south, he knew without knowing how — the sigil told him, a cold pull behind his navel — the Spire was moving. Armies did not mobilize in secret forever. Whatever the conclave intended to do about the blackout, it would do it soon, and it would do it at the gate.

"Maren," he said. "How far is it to the seam?"

Her expression sharpened. "Four days' hard walk. Why?"

"Because I'm not deciding anything in this room." He held up his palm, the sigil dark and patient. "I'm going to stand where Aldous stood. Where *you* stood. I'm going to look at the gate and the god beneath it with my own eyes before I let anyone — Aldous, the Spire, or you — tell me what I'm worth."

For a moment something flickered across Maren's face — fear, or hope, or the place where those two things blur together. Then she nodded once, sharply, and reached for her cloak.

"Then we leave within the hour," she said. "Before the Spire gets there first."

Far to the south, faint as a bruise on the horizon, the sky had begun to glow — not with returning stars, but with something redder, and nearer, and deliberate.
