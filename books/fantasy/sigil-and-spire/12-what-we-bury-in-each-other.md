## 12. What We Bury in Each Other

The Broken Coast did not have weather so much as moods. That night it sulked — a low gray sea breathing against black rock, fog rolling in like something that had died and not yet admitted it. Maren stood at the mouth of the sea cave where they'd made camp and watched Isandro Galloway draw.

He didn't know she was watching. Or perhaps he did; he had a way of seeming unaware of things he had already measured. He sat cross-legged on a flat stone above the tideline, a scrap of vellum braced on his knee, his pen moving in small sure strokes. Even by the light of a single guttering charm-lamp — one of hers, one of the few honest things she owned — she could see the coastline assembling itself under his hand: the cave, the shingle beach, the leaning stack of rock the exiles called the Old Woman because she looked like she was waiting for someone who would never come.

As he drew, the coast *agreed* with him. She could feel it, that faint subsonic hum beneath the world's skin, the sound of the borderlands rearranging themselves to match ink. A channel that had been silting shut for thirty years would be scoured open by morning. A shelf of rock would stand a handspan higher against the tide. Small things. But nothing here was small anymore. Not since the stars went out. Not since the wards broke and the land began listening to whoever spoke its language loudest.

*Mine*, she thought, watching him. *Aldous found another one. Aldous always found another one.*

She had told herself she wouldn't care. She had told herself a great many things over forty years, and most of them had rotted like fish left on this same shore.

"You're staring," Isandro said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing an asset."

"Is that what we're calling each other now." He set down his pen and flexed his fingers, and she saw the sigil on his palm catch the lamplight — the brand that had woken when the wards fell, the mark of a keeper, the mark she had once worn herself in a dream that never quite ended. "You could just say you wanted to talk."

"I could." She came down the rocks with the ease of long practice, boots finding holds her feet had memorized years ago. "But talking is how Aldous got everything he ever took from anyone. He talked beautifully. You'd have liked him."

"I did like him." Quietly. "For eleven years. Before I knew he'd been keeping me like a pig for slaughter."

Maren sat beside him on the cold stone, close enough to feel the warmth off the charm-lamp, far enough that the warmth couldn't be mistaken for anything else. The tide came in below them, patient as arithmetic.

"Then you know," she said, "how good he was at making sacrifice feel like love."

* * *

She hadn't meant to tell him. That was the thing about the Broken Coast: it pulled confessions out of people the way the moon pulled water. There was nowhere to put anything here except into other people. No walls to write on, no trees to carve, no city records, no graves even — the exiles burned their dead and scattered them seaward, because burying anything in ground that reshaped itself weekly felt like a lie.

So she told him. It came out in pieces, between the waves.

"I was nineteen when the Spire's purges reached Vhalen's edge," she said. "You know that part. Everyone knows that part. What nobody knows is that I wasn't fleeing the purges. I was fleeing *him*. The purges were just the excuse my legs needed."

Isandro said nothing. He had learned that much from her, at least — when to let silence do the work.

"Aldous found me in a smuggler's den in Graywater, half-starved, selling little charms worse than yours. Yours actually work. Mine back then were mostly wishful thinking wrapped in wire." She smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "He bought one. Paid triple. Then he sat down across from me and asked me to show him how I made it, and when I showed him, he wept. Actual tears. I thought: this man sees me. This old, kind, brilliant man sees exactly what I am, and instead of burning me, he weeps."

"And?"

"And he did see me. That's the horror of it, Isandro. He saw me precisely, completely, the way you see a vein of ore in a cliff face. He wasn't weeping for me. He was weeping because he'd spent twenty years searching for a vessel the covenant would accept, and there I was, selling tin charms in a den full of lice."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere out in the fog, one of the starless things cried — a sound like a gull and a grief having an argument. The sentries on the ridge shifted; Maren heard the soft click of a crossbow being readied, then relaxing. It passed over. They always passed over the camp now, since Isandro's maps had thickened the borders of their little territory. One more debt she hadn't asked him for.

"He trained me for six years," she went on. "Taught me the deep craft — real magic, the kind the Spire burns children for. And all the while he was teaching me, he was measuring me. Testing the fit. There are words in the covenant rite, words only the keeper hears, and he taught them to me early, casually, the way you teach a child a song. I sang them in my sleep for years before I understood what they were for."

"What are they for?" Isandro asked. His voice had gone careful, the way voices go around wounds.

"They're the shape of the door," Maren said. "When the covenant renews, the keeper walks through that door and doesn't come back. Not dies — dies would be clean. The rite *unmakes* the person and uses the material. Your memories become the mortar. Your name becomes the seal. Everything you loved becomes fuel for the fire that keeps two realms stitched together. Aldous explained it to me the night before the renewal was due, standing on a ward-stone with the stars shining down like witnesses, and he held both my hands, and he said, 'Maren, you will be remembered as long as Vhalen stands.'"

"And you ran."

"And I ran." She laughed, short and ugly. "I ran so fast I left my shadow in his camp. I've been trying to grow it back for forty years. Exile isn't a place, Isandro. It's an amputation you keep feeling."

* * *

The tide turned. It always turned at the worst moments, as if the sea had a sense of theater. Maren got up and walked to the waterline, because sitting still had become impossible, because the story was in her now and it wanted out, all of it, the parts she'd never told anyone.

"I hid in the Border Marches when the renewal came due without a vessel. Do you understand what that means? The covenant strained. The gate held, but barely, and every year after that it held less. Aldous spent those decades searching for a replacement — and he found you. A boy who drew maps of places that didn't exist yet. A boy whose ink moved. He must have thought the gods were apologizing to him."

"He raised me," Isandro said. There was no anger in it, which was somehow worse. "He fed me and housed me and taught me letters. Whatever else he planned, that was real."

"Yes. And the executioner feeds the prisoner well, too. Both things are true. That's the trick of it — that's the whole trick of power. It never asks you to choose between love and use. It makes them the same act." She turned to face him. The charm-lamp threw her shadow huge against the cave wall behind him, a hunched giant with hollow eyes, and she thought: *there she is. The shadow I left behind.* "I loved him. I want that written somewhere, even if it's only in the sand tonight. I loved him the way you love a sunrise — helplessly, stupidly, knowing it will burn off the fog and leave the plain exposed. And when I finally understood what he intended, I discovered that loving him and hating him required no effort at all to do simultaneously. They lived in the same muscle."

Isandro rose and came to stand beside her. Not touching. He was good at not touching; she'd noticed that about him in the weeks since the exiles had pulled him half-drowned from a river net. A man who kept himself carefully unassembled, so no one could take him apart.

"Maren," he said. "Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because tomorrow you leave for the gate."

"We haven't decided—"

"You have. You've been deciding since the night your workshop burned. Every map you've drawn since has been walking you there. I've watched you redraw three miles of coastline in a fortnight, Isandro. You're not gathering information. You're gathering courage." She faced him fully. "So I'm going to say the thing I brought you here to hear, and then you're going to refuse me, and then we'll both stop pretending this alliance is going anywhere."

"Don't," he said softly.

"The covenant should die." The words came out of her like arrows, like the ones she'd loosed at Spire riders forty years ago, drawn from the same quiver, fletched with the same feathers. "Not renewed. Not rewritten. *Killed.* You've seen what it costs. You've seen what it took from every keeper before you — the god showed you, didn't it? In the visions. It shows everyone eventually. Generations of people unwound into mortar, and for what? So two realms can stay stapled together by a pact written in blood by a man who bound a captive god and called it order?"

"The alternative is chaos. Starless things in every village. Two realms bleeding into each other until neither survives."

"Then let them bleed!" Her voice cracked across the shingle, and a sentry turned, and she lowered it, but only a little. "Let them bleed until they learn to clot. Freedom isn't tidy, Isandro. Consent isn't tidy. You want to rewrite the pact — bind power to consent, make the god a partner instead of a prisoner. Beautiful. Noble. And it will fail, because pacts always fail, because any structure that requires one person to pay everything so everyone else pays nothing is a machine for manufacturing martyrs, and I have watched that machine run for four hundred years. Break it. Let the realms stand or fall on their own. At least then whatever survives will be *true*."

"And if nothing survives?"

"Then nothing deserved to!"

The words hung in the salt air. Below them a wave gathered itself, considered, and collapsed.

Isandro looked at her for a long time. And she saw it happen — saw the exact moment his answer arrived, before he spoke it, in the settling of his shoulders, in the way his hand closed gently over the sigil on his palm as though sheltering a coal from wind.

"No," he said.

"Maren. My mother sold matches in Corvane's lowest quarter. I don't remember her face anymore — I checked, last winter, and it was gone, worn smooth like a coin carried too long. I have spent twenty years selling splinters of a covenant I didn't know I carried, to people drowning quietly, because it was the only mercy the law allowed me. If I break the pact, every one of those people drowns louder. The child whose tenement I saved. The old man with the cough. The whole gray city, full of people who never signed anything and never hurt anyone. You're asking me to burn two realms to prove a principle, and I won't. I *can't*. Principles are what people have instead of faces, and I've already lost too many faces."

"So you'll erase yourself instead." Her voice came out flat. "Renewal. The door. You'll walk into Aldous's machine and call it virtue because you chose it and I didn't."

"Maybe. Or maybe I'll find the third path the god offered. I don't know yet. But I know I won't decide by killing everyone first and calling the ashes freedom."

"That's not—" She stopped. Breathed. The fog pressed close, wet wool against her skin. "That's not what breaking it means."

"It's what it *costs*, Maren. It's what it costs, and you know it, and you've known it for forty years, and you built a life on this coast out of the wreckage of not knowing it." He stepped closer, and for one terrible moment she thought he might touch her, and she didn't know which outcome she feared more. "You loved him. You ran. And you've spent your exile telling yourself the running was principle. Some of it was. But some of it — the part that matters, the part that hollowed you — some of it was just fear wearing principle's coat. And I'm sorry. I'm sorry, because I can see it, and seeing it doesn't help either of us."

The slap, when it came, rang off the cave walls. His head turned with it; he didn't raise a hand to his cheek; he simply looked back at her with an expression that was not anger and not forgiveness but something older than both, something like recognition.

"You don't know anything about me," she whispered.

"I know exactly enough about you to be certain you were the bravest coward I'll ever meet."

* * *

He left at dawn.

She watched from the Old Woman's shoulder as he climbed the goat track eastward with a pack on his back and Serah Voss's warning letter — the inquisitor had ridden in two nights past, mud-spattered, half-dead, carrying word that the conclave was moving on the gate with an army and a stolen rite — folded against his heart. The exiles who chose to go with him numbered nine. The ones who stayed, believing in her gospel of unbinding, numbered thirty-one. She counted twice. Numbers were the only honesty left to her.

Fen, her lieutenant, a woman with a shipwreck voice and a smuggler's patience, came up beside her. "You could stop him. The pass narrows at Graywater. Twelve archers."

"I know."

"You could take the sigil off his corpse and break the covenant ourselves. You've said it often enough."

"I know what I've said." Maren's hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook, somewhere below the waterline, in the flooded holds where she kept everything she'd buried. "Leave him be."

Fen studied her with the frank appraisal of someone who had followed her for fifteen years and earned the right. "You love him."

"I loved a man once who used that word as a leash," Maren said. "I'm not going to spend the rest of my life mistaking the collar for kindness. He goes. We go our own road."

"And what road is that?"

She looked east, where the dawn was doing its poor gray best, where the mountains over the gate stood black against a sky that still had no stars in its memory. Somewhere up there, a god waited in chains, and a young man walked toward it carrying her oldest wound like a lantern.

"The same road," she heard herself say, and hated the words even as they left her, and knew they were true anyway. "It always was. I just took the long way."

Below, the tide came in over the place where they'd sat, smoothing the sand, erasing nothing that mattered. Out past the breakers, faint as a scar healing, the first pale suggestion of light gathered in the east — and for one heartbeat, one only, Maren could have sworn she saw a star burning there, low on the horizon, stubborn as a promise.

Then the fog rolled in, and she turned inland, and began to walk.
