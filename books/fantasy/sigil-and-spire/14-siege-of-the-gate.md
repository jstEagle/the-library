## 14. Siege of the Gate

The sky over Graywater had been wrong for three days before the conclave arrived.

Isandro felt it first in his ink. The vials on his workbench — he still thought of it as a workbench, though it was a plank across two barrels in Maren's camp — had gone still and gray, as if the pigment itself were holding its breath. He'd stood there at dawn with a brush loaded for mapping and watched the color refuse to flow, pooling instead like something cowering.

"The boundary's thinning," Maren said from the tent flap. She didn't look at him. She hadn't looked at him properly since they'd parted bitterly outside the god's gate, and now that she'd come back to fight beside him, she spoke to him the way one speaks to a bridge under load: carefully, without weight.

"I know."

"You don't. Not yet." She turned then, and her eyes were rimmed red from sleeplessness or something older. "When it goes, Isandro, you'll feel every mile of it. The wards remember you now. You're written into them."

He wanted to say *that was Aldous's doing, not mine* — but the words had died somewhere between the grimoire and the anchor stone, between learning what the covenant cost and choosing to pay it anyway. He was written into the wards. He had signed himself in ink and intent, and there was no unsigning except the one that would take his name with it.

The horn sounded an hour past noon.

It wasn't a war horn. That was the horror of it. It was the Spire's summons-bell, the same brazen triple note they rang in Corvane to call inquisitors to a burning, carried out here to the borderlands on instruments of gilded bronze, as if the conclave could not conceive of doing anything — even this — without ceremony.

Isandro climbed the ridge above the camp and looked east, and his stomach turned over.

They came in column along the old ward-road, perhaps four hundred strong: conclave knights in white-and-gold, battle-priests with censers swinging, and behind them, borne on a litter of black iron like a relic or a prisoner, the ritual engine. Even at this distance he could see it — a great ring of interlocking sigil-plates, each one tall as a man, turning slowly around a hollow core. Stolen script crawled across every plate. He recognized some of it. He had drawn some of it, years ago, sold as charms to men who wanted luck with dice. The conclave had taken his small honest lies and scaled them into a machine for cracking open the world.

"They're going to set it on the gate itself," Serah said beside him. She had come up the ridge without sound, which was a habit of hers he'd stopped finding unsettling and started finding useful. Her inquisitor's coat was gone; she wore exile-gray now, but she still stood like the Spire, spine straight as a verdict. "The anchor stone is the keystone. Break the ritual against it, force the covenant to accept new terms at sword-point, and the gate opens under their control. Permanently."

"And everything on the other side comes through," Isandro said.

"Everything on the other side has been *waiting* to come through." Serah's jaw tightened. "I read the conclave's own surveys, before I burned my name. The starless dark isn't empty, Galloway. It's hungry. It's been pressed against the glass for nine hundred years."

Below them, Maren's exiles were forming up — barely sixty, armed with pikes and stolen steel and the kind of desperate competence that came from having nothing left to lose twice. Maren herself stood at their head with her hoarded power uncoiling around her like smoke from a doused fire, and even from the ridge Isandro could feel the heat of it, the terrible banked furnace of everything she'd refused to spend since fleeing Aldous.

She caught him looking. She did not smile. But she raised one hand — *go*, the gesture said, *do what you have to do* — and turned back to her people.

*Courage matters less than timing,* Isandro thought, and the thought was not his own but the land's, the covenant speaking through him in Aldous's borrowed cadence, and for once he did not flinch from the dead man's voice. *Time is bleeding out. Go.*

He went.

* * *

The path to the anchor stone ran down through a fold of hills that no longer appeared on any map — because he had unmapped them himself, two nights ago, drawing the terrain into a maze of false valleys to slow the conclave's approach. His drawings reshaped the world. He had known it intellectually since Ink and Iron, since the night a sketch of a ford became a real ford by morning. Knowing it and feeling it were different things. Each step he took now crossed ground he had authored, and the hills knew him, leaned toward him like wheat toward sun.

It made the ambush worse, somehow. To be betrayed on your own paper.

The starless things came out of the thin places at dusk — not through the gate, not yet, but through the hairline cracks the failing wards had left all along the border, seams in the world wide enough for the small horrors first. They poured from a fissure in a hillside he had drawn only yesterday, and his guilt arrived a half-second ahead of his fear: *I made this valley. I made its shadows deeper.*

They were wrong in the way dreams are wrong. Roughly man-shaped, roughly beast-shaped, but rendered by something that had never seen light and was guessing. Their surfaces drank the last of the sunset rather than reflecting it. Where they walked, frost formed in midsummer patterns — spirals, sigils, the negative image of writing. And they made a sound, low and continuous, like a page being slowly torn.

"Contact!" someone shouted below. "Left flank, the draw — *gods, what are they—*"

Pikes leveled. Maren's voice cut across the panic like a blade through cloth: "Steel slows them! Fire and ink stop them! Hold the line, hold the *line*—"

Isandro dropped to one knee in the scree, tore the stopper from his inkhorn with his teeth, and drew.

He drew fast and ugly, no artistry, just function: a line of warding-script across the draw, charged with the covenant's authority that lived now in his branded palm. The sigil flared blue-white where the starless things touched it, and they recoiled with a sound like steam hitting iron, their tearing-page cry rising to a shriek. One of them pushed through anyway — they learned, he realized with a lurch, they *learned* — and took a exile's pike through the chest without slowing, and would have taken the man too if Serah hadn't put a knife of consecrated steel through the thing's skull from the side.

"It doesn't die," she said, wrenching the blade free, black ichor smoking off it. "Nothing about it dies. It *unravels*. Buy minutes, not victories."

"Minutes are all I need." Isandro was already running, downhill, toward the gate.

Behind him the line bent but held. Ahead of him, lit by the first stars of the ritual engine — false stars, gold and obscene, conjured by the conclave's stolen script — the anchor stone rose from the earth like a broken tooth.

And the conclave's vanguard reached it first.

* * *

There were perhaps fifty of them at the stone, kneeling in a crescent while the ritual engine was hauled into position behind them, plates turning, gathering power. Their commander was a tall figure in white whose face Isandro couldn't see — but he knew the posture, the absolute certainty of it, and when the figure raised a hand and the air itself seemed to salute, Isandro understood that this was the conclave's leader, the one Serah's ledger had named, the architect of the manufactured crisis.

The leader saw him coming. Of course he did. There was nowhere to hide on a battlefield you were walking across openly.

"Isandro Galloway." The voice carried impossibly, the way voices do in dreams. "Last signatory. We've been looking for you since the stars went out. You could have saved us all this trouble by simply *coming home*."

"I'm not yours to summon."

"No one is anyone's, boy. That's the disease of this age." The leader gestured, almost gently, at the anchor stone, at the seamed and bleeding sky above it. "The old covenant bound power to blood, and blood failed. What we offer binds it to *order*. To hierarchy. To the Spire, which endures. Kneel, sign your inheritance over, and we will hold the gate together. Refuse—" the golden false-stars flickered, "—and we will hold it without you, over your corpse if necessary. The realms will be ruled either way. Choose who rules."

Every word was reasonable. That was the trap of it, Isandro thought. Every word was the kind of thing Aldous might have said, might have believed, standing before a captive god with a quill in his hand and mercy in his heart and chains in the other. *Someone must hold the power. Better us than chaos.* The oldest lie, wearing the newest uniform.

"You drained the wards," Isandro said. "You starved the border for a year so the crisis would deliver the covenant to you. Serah's ledger proves it. Your own scribes' hands prove it."

Something moved behind the leader's composure — not shame. Irritation. "Ledgers burn," the leader said. "So do traitors. So, regrettably, do cartographers. *Begin.*"

The ritual engine sang.

It was the worst sound Isandro had ever heard — the breaking-glass-through-bone chord of the wards shattering, sustained, amplified, weaponized. The anchor stone lit up with forced script, conclave script, terms being dictated to the covenant at the point of a siege engine, and the boundary above the stone tore like wet paper.

The sky opened.

Not to stars. To the *absence* of stars, a darkness so total it had texture, depth, appetite — and through the wound came the things that had waited nine hundred years against the glass. Not the small scouts from the hillside now. These were vast, slow, patient horrors, shapes like drowned cathedrals dragging themselves through a keyhole, and where they passed, the grass grayed and the wind forgot how to blow and one of Maren's exiles simply sat down in the middle of the battle and began weeping and did not stop.

"HOLD THEM!" Maren's voice, raw now, blazing. She had come up the slope with her people and she burned — actually burned, corona-light pouring off her, spending the hoard she'd guarded since fleeing Aldous, each spell a coin from a purse that could not be refilled. A wall of white fire met the first of the great shapes and staggered it. "ISANDRO! THE STONE! *GO!*"

He went.

* * *

The last hundred yards to the anchor stone were the longest of his life.

The conclave's kneeling crescent broke apart as the exiles crashed into it, and then it was not a battle but a storm of separate desperations — steel on steel, fire on shadow, Serah fighting through the press with two knives and thirty years of institutional murder in her muscle memory, cutting down the very knights she'd once commanded, her face wet and utterly expressionless. Isandro stopped seeing individuals. He saw gaps. Openings. Seconds.

A knight barred his path; Isandro slammed his palm against the man's breastplate and let the covenant's authority discharge through the sigil-brand, and the knight flew backward as if the land itself had rejected him. Another came from the left; one of Maren's exiles — a woman named Petra who'd once bought a hearing-charm from him in Corvane, who'd recognized him on sight weeks ago and said nothing — stepped into the blade meant for him and went down, and Isandro had no time to do anything but keep moving, because courage mattered less than timing, and time was bleeding out of the world through a wound in the sky.

The anchor stone was hot. Not warm — *hot*, feverish, the conclave's dictated script crawling across its surface in gold, rewriting the covenant's terms line by line while the ritual engine howled. Isandro pressed his branded palm flat against the stone and felt the old pact answer him like a dog recognizing its true master through a stranger's leash.

*I'm here,* he told it. *I'm the signatory. Hear me and not them.*

The stone shuddered. The gold script flickered. For one heartbeat the tearing chord of the ritual faltered—

—and the conclave's leader was suddenly there, close enough to touch, having crossed the battlefield the way powerful men cross rooms, untouched, escorted by certainty. Up close, at last, Isandro saw the face beneath the white hood, and it was ordinary. That was the final obscenity of it. It was the face of a tax assessor, a grain merchant, a neighbor.

"You're killing yourself for a system that ate your mentor," the leader said, over the roar. "That ate six keepers before him. That will eat whatever comes after you. Help us *manage* it instead. Keep your memories. Keep your name. Let the realms rot slowly, together, in order. That is the best bargain on any table, cartographer, and you know it."

And Isandro — bleeding, deafened, his mother's face already waiting behind the rite like a debt collector — looked at the wound in the sky, and at Maren burning herself down to buy him seconds, and at Serah fighting her own past hand to hand, and at Petra lying still in the grass with a hearing-charm in her pocket that would never chime again.

"No," he said. "I'm done with bargains where other people pay."

He opened the grimoire to the page he'd prepared. He pressed his palm to the anchor stone. And he began, aloud, in the covenant's living language of ink and intent, to speak the terms of renewal —

— as the first tendril of the starless dark came down out of the torn sky directly above him, and the ritual engine, sensing its rival, screamed louder, and somewhere behind him Maren shouted his name in a voice he had never heard her use, and the stone pulled him in.

The last thing he heard from the world was the tearing of pages.

Then there was only the rite, and the price, and the work.
