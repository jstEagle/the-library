## 15. Maren's Choice

The gate was screaming.

Maren had heard a hundred terrible sounds in her long, hollow life — the crack of pyres, the wet collapse of wards failing, Aldous's voice the night she fled him, saying *stay* in a tone that made staying sound like drowning. None of it prepared her for the sound the boundary made now: a note too low for ears, felt in the teeth and the marrow, as though the world itself were a struck bell with a flaw running through its heart.

She stood on the ridge above Graywater with the wind tearing at her coat, and below her the conclave's army moved like a wound across the land. Ten thousand torches. Banners of white and gold, the Spire's colors, marching in ranks so disciplined they looked less like men than like stitches — thread being pulled through the fabric of the borderlands by some vast and patient hand. At their center, on a platform of black iron, the ritual engine turned. She could see it even from here, could feel it even through the deadened place where her power usually lived: a great wheel of sigils grinding against the covenant's old lines, prying them open the way a crow pries open a mussel.

And inside that opening, things were coming through.

Starless creatures. She'd seen one up close three nights ago, when the first of them crossed at the ford — a thing shaped like a deer if a deer had been dreamed by something that had never seen light. It had no eyes. It had found her anyway, turning its faceless head toward the warmth of the power she kept banked behind her ribs, and it had made a sound like a word being unlearned.

Now there were hundreds of them, pouring through the tear the conclave's engine had widened, flowing down into the valley like smoke poured from an overturned jar.

"They're not even fighting us," Serah Voss said beside her. The former inquisitor's voice was flat, but Maren had learned to read the tremor underneath it — the sound of a woman watching everything she'd burned for reveal itself as ash. "Look at the formations. They're not here for the exiles. They're here to hold the perimeter while the ritual finishes."

"Of course they are." Maren pulled her gloves tighter. Her hands were cold; her hands were always cold now, had been for thirty years, since the night she ran from Aldous's tower with half a covenant's worth of stolen fire sealed in her chest like swallowed lightning. "We're not the enemy. We're weather. You don't aim your army at weather."

Below them, Isandro's line was breaking.

* * *

She had argued against this. She wanted that remembered, whatever else happened — she wanted it carved somewhere, even if the only place it survived was her own dissolving mind. Three days ago, when Isandro had spread his maps across the exile camp's long table and traced the route to the anchor stone, she had told him plainly: *You cannot reach it while the engine turns. The rite needs stillness, needs hours, and we have minutes at best.*

"I know," he'd said.

"Then you know it's impossible."

"I know it's impossible the way we've been doing things." He'd looked up at her then, ink-stained fingers resting on the map of the valley he'd drawn from memory — drawn, she now understood, into being — and there had been something in his face she recognized with a grief so old it had worn smooth, like sea glass. "You told me once that every pact is a chain. Fine. Then let me be the one who breaks this one properly. But I need time, Maren. Time is the only coin I don't have."

She had hated him a little, in that moment. She had hated him because he sounded like Aldous, and because he didn't, and because both of those things hurt.

Now she watched his ragged line of exiles and defectors and two dozen Corvane deserters hold the narrow ground before the anchor stone's approach, and she counted the minutes he'd asked for, and she understood what she had been saving herself for all these years without ever letting herself say it aloud.

* * *

The power was still there.

That was the joke of it, the cruel, elaborate joke she had built around her own life. Thirty years ago she had fled Aldous Galloway's tower rather than become the covenant's vessel, and she had run carrying the very thing she'd been running from — a reservoir of raw, unbound magic, siphoned from the covenant's channels during the months she'd served as Aldous's apprentice, hoarded in secret the way a starving woman hoards bread she cannot bring herself to eat. She had told herself it was insurance. Leverage. A weapon for the day the Spire came for the exiles.

But she had never spent it. Not once. Not when the inquisitors burned the Broken Coast settlement. Not when winter took four of her people and she could have warmed them with a thought. Not when Isandro lay fevered in her tent after the crossing at Graywater, shivering toward death, and every instinct she had screamed at her to pour a little of that fire into him and pull him back.

Because spending it meant beginning. And beginning meant ending. And she had built her whole self around the principle of never ending — of running fast enough, hiding deep enough, keeping enough in reserve that no debt could ever come due.

*You are the most careful coward I have ever loved,* Aldous had told her, the last night. He hadn't said *loved* then. But she had heard it anyway, in the space under the words, and she had hated him for making her hear it, and she had run.

The wind off the valley carried screams now. One of the starless things had reached the exile line — she saw it as a ripple in the torchlight, a place where the firelight bent wrong, and then a man was simply not there anymore, his spear clattering to the stones with nothing left holding it.

Maren closed her eyes.

Inside her chest, behind a door she had sealed with thirty years of discipline, the hoarded power stirred like something waking from a long sleep. It did not feel like fire anymore. It felt like a tide. Like the whole drowned weight of a sea held back by a door she had nailed shut with her own fear, and the nails were pulling free one by one, and the door was groaning, and beneath it the water pressed and pressed and pressed.

*If I open it,* she thought, *I die.*

Not quickly. That was the horror of the forbidden arts — they didn't kill you the way a blade killed you, clean and done. They consumed you the way a candle consumes itself, wick and wax and the light all one process, and the process had already begun the moment she reached for it. Every second she drew on the hoard, more of Maren became fuel. Her memories would go first — she knew this from the texts Aldous had made her copy, back when copying them was just penance and she'd read them without understanding. Then her senses, her name, her shape. What remained at the end was power with a fading outline of a woman around it, burning brighter and thinner until nothing was left but light and heat and a scorch-mark on the world.

*Thirty years of survival,* she thought. *Spent in how many minutes?*

She opened her eyes and looked for Isandro.

He was easy to find, even in the chaos — he fought the way he drew, precise and unhurried, his cartographer's sigils flaring blue-white wherever he struck, holding a seam in the line near the old waystone. Beyond him, up the slope, the anchor stone glimmered faintly through the dark, a tooth of pale rock crowned with the ghost of Aldous's oldest work. Between Isandro and the stone, the conclave's vanguard was pushing hard, a wedge of armored casters driving straight for the gap in the exile line, and behind them the ritual engine turned and turned, and the sky above the gate was a blackness so complete it seemed less like night than like a hole cut out of the world.

Isandro needed twenty minutes. Perhaps thirty. The rite of renewal — or whatever new thing he intended to write in its place, because of course he intended to rewrite it, because he was the kind of stubborn, maddening, luminous fool who looked at a covenant written in blood and thought *draft* — needed time, and time was bleeding out of the valley faster than the blood was.

Maren walked down the ridge.

* * *

Serah caught her at the bottom. "What are you doing?"

"What does it look like?"

"It looks like you're walking toward the vanguard alone."

"It looks exactly like that." Maren smiled, and was surprised at how easily it came. "Keep my people alive, inquisitor. That's the whole order. Keep them alive and keep them falling back in good order, and don't let anyone follow me, because what I'm about to do doesn't have room for witnesses."

Serah's jaw tightened. Maren watched the woman war with herself — the old discipline against the new doubt, the ledger-keeper's precision against the traitor's vertigo. Finally Serah said, "Whatever you're planning, the odds—"

"The odds are terrible. They always were. That was never the point." Maren pulled off her gloves, finger by finger, and dropped them in the mud. Her bare hands looked absurdly ordinary — a sorceress's hands should have blazed, she thought, should have announced themselves. Hers were just a woman's hands, scarred at the knuckles, ink-stained from decades of maps she'd drawn of places she could never go home to. "There's something I need you to do instead. If it works."

"Name it."

"When this is over — if there's an over — find him. Isandro. Whatever he becomes, whatever's left of him after the stone, find him and make sure he eats. He forgets. He forgot to eat for three days once over a map of the fenlands, and I had to put bread in his hand myself." She heard her own voice crack on the last word and was furious about it. "Promise me."

"Maren—"

"Promise me, Serah. You owe the world a debt. Start here."

Something moved behind the inquisitor's eyes — the ghost of a sister, maybe, a face from a pyre long ago. "I promise," Serah said, and Maren believed her, which was its own small miracle, and turned away before either of them could make it into more than it was.

* * *

The vanguard saw her coming and laughed.

She heard it over the din — the conclave's casters, confident behind their shields and their numbers, watching a single woman in a travel-worn coat walk toward them across the broken ground with her hands empty. One of them shouted something she didn't catch. A jeer. An invitation to surrender, perhaps. The Spire's people had always loved the theater of mercy.

Maren stopped forty paces out, planted her bare feet in the frozen mud, and opened the door.

There was no incantation. There was no gesture. There was only consent — the one thing the old covenant had never required, the one thing she had withheld for thirty years, given now freely and completely and all at once, to no god and no pact, but to herself. To the girl she had been in Aldous's tower. To the woman she had become on the Broken Coast. To every careful, hoarding, terrified year between.

*All of it,* she thought. *Every drop. Take me.*

The tide broke.

Light came out of her the way dawn comes out of the horizon — not gradually, but as a decision. The valley went white. She felt the shockwave roll outward from her body and heard, distantly, the vanguard's laughter turn to screaming, and then she stopped hearing anything at all, because her ears were among the first things the fire took.

It did not hurt. That surprised her most. The texts had promised agony — the price of the forbidden arts, the body's protest at being unmade. But there was no pain. There was only subtraction, gentle and relentless, like snow melting off a roof in spring sun. Her hearing went, and with it the battle's noise, and she discovered she didn't miss it. Her sense of smell went next, then taste, then the long catalog of small chronic aches she'd carried since the Coast — the knee, the shoulder, the old scar along her ribs. Her body was being paid out like rope, and each length that burned made the light burn brighter, and the brightness was hers, finally, wholly hers, not lent by any covenant or any man.

She raised her hand — she still had a hand, mostly — and the sky answered.

* * *

What Maren unleashed on the valley of Graywater had no name in any of the Spire's registers, because the Spire had spent three centuries ensuring no one lived long enough to name it. It was not destruction, though destruction followed it the way shadow follows a runner. It was *unbinding* — the raw grammar of magic before the covenants taught it manners, the language the world spoke before anyone agreed on what the words meant.

She swept it across the vanguard like a hand clearing a table.

Their shields, woven from sanctioned sigils, unraveled at her touch — not shattered, *unraveled*, threads of power picking themselves apart into their component intentions and drifting away confused. Their armor went soft as wax. The iron platform of the ritual engine shrieked as her wave grazed its edge, and a whole section of the conclave's great wheel simply ceased, its sigils erased as cleanly as ink lifted from wet paper.

The starless creatures turned toward her light and recoiled. That was the discovery that almost made her laugh, there in the white heart of her own consumption: they feared her. These things from beyond the gate, these children of the sealed dark, looked at what she was becoming and flinched — because she was, for these few minutes, the opposite of everything they were. She was a star. She was the thing the night had been missing.

So she gave them more of it. She stretched her light across the breach in the ward-line, a wall of dawn standing where the wall of stone had failed, and the starless things piled against it and broke like waves against a cliff, and behind that burning wall, the path to the anchor stone lay open and clear.

Through the roar of her own diminishing, she saw Isandro look up.

* * *

He was fifty yards away, at the head of the exile line, and when the light broke over the valley he turned as if someone had called his name — and perhaps someone had, in the only language left to her. His face, when she saw it, was the reason she had come. Not duty. Not the covenant, which she still believed deserved to die. Not the realms, vast and abstract and none of them ever having shown her a kindness worth the name.

His face. Ink-smudged, bloodied at the temple, wide-eyed with a horror he wasn't letting himself feel yet because feeling it would slow him down.

*Maren.* She couldn't hear him say it, but she saw it. She had spent thirty years learning to read mouths across crowded markets and silent rooms, and she read it now: *Maren, what are you doing, stop, you'll—*

She smiled at him. She hoped it reached him through all that light. *Go,* she mouthed back. *Write your map. I'll hold the door.*

He didn't move. Of course he didn't move. He started toward her instead, scrambling down from the line, and she felt something in what remained of her chest twist — fury and tenderness in equal measure, because of course he would try, because he was exactly the kind of man who would try, and she had known that since the day she'd fished him out of the Graywater reeds and decided, against every scrap of her hard-won wisdom, to care what happened to him.

*Don't you dare,* she thought at him, and pushed.

Not at him. Never at him. She pushed the light outward, a ring of radiance expanding from her position, and it passed over Isandro like a warm tide — and in passing, it showed him. That was the gift she hadn't planned, the thing the forbidden power did that no sanctioned art could: it stripped away every veil between what people were and what they meant. For one suspended moment, standing in the wash of her burning, Isandro Galloway saw all of her. The girl in the tower who had copied forbidden texts by candlelight and dreamed of a life with open windows. The woman on the Broken Coast who had buried friends and called it resilience. The coward, yes — he saw the coward, thirty years of careful retreats, and he saw the cost of every one of them, and he did not look away.

And she saw him. All of him. The boy chosen before he could choose. The man who sold charms at a loss because desperation deserved discounts. The heir who never wanted a throne, only a workshop, only a quiet life of honest maps — and who was walking, minute by minute, toward a stone that would take even the memory of wanting those things.

They saw each other, whole and unhidden, in the space of a single heartbeat.

No words. Words had failed them from the beginning — her ideology and his hope, her chains and his covenants, thirty years of argument neither of them had ever won. There were no words for this. There was only what passed between them in the light: *I see you. I see what it costs. I am choosing this, and I am choosing it for you, and you are allowed to let me.*

Isandro stopped running toward her. His fists closed. His jaw set in the particular way she had come to think of as *his* — the cartographer accepting a survey he couldn't refuse. And then he turned, and he ran, not away from her but toward the anchor stone, toward the rite, toward the future she was buying him with everything she had hoarded and every year she had refused to spend.

Trust, she thought, as the light took her hair, her name, the color of her own eyes. Trust was just love with the arguments removed.

* * *

She held the wall for eleven minutes. She knew because she counted, the way she'd once counted coins, the way she'd counted winters — the last habit of a hoarder, spending even her counting. Eleven minutes of burning, of the starless things breaking against her like black water on white rock, of the conclave's second wave advancing into her light and stumbling back out of it blinded and broken. Eleven minutes in which she felt herself grow simpler and simpler, memory by memory, until what remained was not Maren of the Broken Coast but something older and cleaner: a woman standing in a doorway, holding it open, refusing to close it.

Near the end, she had almost nothing left but the doorway itself.

And in that last thin clarity, she understood something she wished she could carry with her, and knew she wouldn't: that Aldous had been right, and she had been right, and they had both been wrong. The covenant was a chain. Love was a chain. Running was a chain. Everything true bound, and everything binding could break, and the only question that had ever mattered was not *whether* to be bound but *to what, and by choice*. Isandro had seen it first. Isandro, with his drafts and his redlines and his stubborn belief that any contract could be renegotiated by honest parties. He would bind the power to consent or die trying, and the world after him would be poorer in wonders and richer in freedom, and she —

She would be light on the water. She could live with that. She could not-live with that. Both, at once, gladly.

The last thing she spent was not power. It was attention. She gathered the final flicker of herself and sent it up the slope, after the small running figure with the ink-stained hands, a single thread of warmth across the distance between the doorway and the stone:

*Finish it. Finish it and don't you dare mourn longer than a season. And eat something.*

Then the door was empty, and the light went out, and the valley rang like a bell.

* * *

On the slope below the anchor stone, Isandro Galloway stumbled as the warmth touched him — a pressure between the shoulder blades, brief as a hand, gone as breath. He turned. Far below, where the wall of light had stood, there was only darkness again, and drifting motes like the last sparks of a bonfire, settling over the frozen mud.

The starless things hung motionless at the edge of the scorched ground, eyeless faces turned upward, toward the stone, toward him. Waiting. The conclave's engine still turned beyond them, grinding at the wounded covenant, and its leader stood on the black iron platform with his hand raised, and the sky above the gate was still a hole cut out of the world.

Isandro closed his fist around the sigil branded in his palm until it burned, and climbed.
