## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

There are three rules to selling charms in a city that has outlawed magic: never let them see your hands shake, never charge more than the lie is worth, and never — *never* — make one that works.

I had kept all three for twelve years. I'd like to say it was discipline. Mostly it was terror, which in my experience lasts longer.

The morning the trouble started, Vellharrow was wearing its winter the way old women wear their good shawls — pulled tight, slightly moth-eaten, and smelling faintly of something that died years ago. Frost had climbed the shutters of the Saltmarket overnight and turned them white as bone. My breath came out in little ghosts, and I stood at my stall on Cobbler's Row with my gloves buttoned to the wrist, arranging luck-knots in a basket like they were eggs I was afraid of cracking.

They were good knots, if I'm honest. Red cord, three loops, tied in a pattern my mentor Aldous once called "a prayer with its teeth pulled." He said it fondly, back when he still had teeth of his own and I still had a name anyone would speak aloud. The knots did nothing. That was the point. A charm that does nothing can't be confiscated, and neither can you.

"Kestrel! Two fever-charms, and don't skimp on the rosemary this time."

Mistress Hobb, who ran the pie stall two down and believed rosemary could cure everything short of bankruptcy. I wrapped her charms in brown paper, took her coppers, and gave her the smile I keep folded away for exactly this purpose.

"You'll boil them in wine," I called after her. "Not ale. Ale's for drinking."

"Ale's for living, girl." She waved without turning around. "Wine's for dying people."

Vellharrow wisdom. There was a reason the city had survived two sieges and a plague while grander places burned: nobody here expected anything to work.

The market filled slowly, the way it does in deep cold — vendors hunched over braziers, breath rising in columns, everyone performing the great civic theater of pretending commerce was worth leaving the fire for. I sold a luck-knot to a carter heading north on the ice road. I sold a charm against bad dreams to a widow who bought one every month and swore every month that they worked, which worried me more than she knew. I sold nothing at all to a boy who stood across the row for the better part of an hour, watching my hands.

People watch my hands. They're scarred from wrist to fingertip — pale branching lines, like frost ferns, like lightning frozen mid-strike. Storm-born, Aldous used to call them. Marked by the sky itself. In the Stormhold they'd been a badge. Here they were evidence, so I wore gloves year-round and told curious customers I'd been scalded as a child, which had the double virtue of being boring and mostly true, depending on how you define *scalded*.

The boy watching me couldn't have been more than seven, wrapped in a coat that had been his brother's and before that possibly a tent. He had the particular stubbornness of children who want something and have decided asking is beneath them.

Eventually he crossed the row and planted himself in front of my basket.

"How much for a wish?"

"Wishes are free," I said. "It's the string that costs."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My da's boat didn't come in. Three days."

Ah. There it was. The cold under the cold. I'd heard it all winter — the herring fleet scattered by the early storms, boats overdue, women standing at the seawall doing arithmetic with their faces. Vellharrow's magic ban hadn't stopped anyone from wanting magic. It had only stopped anyone from admitting it.

"Three days isn't long," I said, which was a kindness and also possibly a lie, and I've never been able to tell those apart cleanly.

"The Merrow's Luck came in yesterday. Half her crew drowned." His voice didn't waver. Children here learned early not to waver; wavering cost extra. "Gran says the old gods are waking up. She says that's why the storms are wrong."

"Your gran sells you stories instead of breakfast, does she?"

"She prays," he said, simply, the way you'd say *she breathes*. "She says praying's not magic. Praying's just talking to somebody who might listen."

And then — because children are sent into the world specifically to destroy the careful architecture of adults — he closed his eyes, right there at my stall, in front of my basket of harmless knots, and prayed.

*Please. Please bring Da home. Please.*

I want to be clear about what happened next, because I have spent a great deal of time since trying to explain it away, and honesty requires me to fail.

The wind moved.

Not the market wind — the flat, greasy gust that comes off the harbor carrying fish-rot and coal smoke. This was different. This came from nowhere, from between things, a thread of air that slipped through the frost-bound street like a finger through a keyhole. It touched the boy's hair. It stirred the cords in my basket, all of them at once, so that forty luck-knots lifted and settled again like a flock of small red birds deciding against flight.

And it whispered.

I won't pretend I heard words. What I heard was older than words — a pressure, a *leaning-in*, the sensation of something vast turning its attention toward a very small voice. Like standing in a cathedral and hearing someone clear their throat in the dark.

Then it was gone, and the market went on being a market, and the boy opened his eyes and grinned at me with a gap where his front tooth should be.

"It heard me," he said.

"No," I said. My voice came out strange. I coughed and tried again. "No, it didn't. Wind's just wind. Here." I pressed a luck-knot into his mittened hand and refused his coin. "For the string. Free wishes, remember?"

He ran off happy. I stood behind my stall with my heart going like a smith's hammer and my hands — hidden, always hidden — shaking inside their gloves.

Because I knew that wind.

Twelve years is a long time. Long enough to bury a name, build a new one out of cord and rosemary and small honest lies. Long enough to almost forget what storm-light looks like through a tower window, or the sound a man makes when the sky takes him. Almost. But some things you don't forget; you just stop visiting them, and they wait in the ground like seeds.

That whisper was a thing I had felt exactly once before, on the worst night of my life, when I knelt in the ruins of what I'd done and prayed — *prayed*, the way the boy prayed, simple and stupid and desperate — to gods everyone knew were dead.

Everyone knew the old gods were dead. That was the first lesson of the Stormhold and the last law of Vellharrow both: the divine age ended four hundred years ago, sealed away by sacrifices whose names we use as curses. What remained was craft, and craft was licensed, taxed, and eventually banned outright, and now there was nothing at all except weather and luck and whatever comfort you could tie out of red string.

Dead gods do not lean close to hear a child.

I packed up early. Mistress Hobb accused me of avoiding the tax collector, which was true on most days and a mercy today, since it meant she didn't look closely at my face. I walked home the long way, along the seawall, because apparently I wanted to suffer.

The harbor was gray iron to the horizon. Boats sat at anchor like patient animals. Out past the breakwater, the sea breathed slow and cold, and I stood there in the knife-wind telling myself the obvious: it was a draft. A trick of the frost. Forty cords moving on a thermal off the water. I was a woman who sold lies for coppers; surely I could afford one for myself.

But underneath the sensible voice, another voice — one I hadn't let speak in twelve years — said: *you felt it. You know you felt it. You know that leaning-in. Something heard him.*

And something else, quieter, worse: *something heard him because someone left the door open. And you know whose hands built doors like that.*

Mine. Symbolically. Historically. Mine.

I went home to my room above the chandler's shop — one bed, one stove, one window that rattled in any wind above a murmur, which had been a source of nightly complaint until tonight, when it became a source of nightly listening. I lit the stove. I took off my gloves.

In the firelight, the scars ran silver-pale from wrist to fingertip, branching, forking, the map of a night I'd spent twelve years refusing to reread. Aldous's voice came back to me unbidden, the way dead men's voices do, warm and infuriating: *Storms don't come for no reason, little kestrel. Weather remembers. Weather keeps accounts.*

"Weather," I told the empty room, "can go to hell."

The window rattled. Just the window. Just the wind.

I slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of a tower and rain and a man falling, which was also normal. What wasn't normal was waking before dawn with frost on the *inside* of the glass, spread in patterns too deliberate to be accidental — spirals, whorls, and at the center of the pane, unmistakable, a shape I knew better than my own face.

A sigil. My sigil. The mark that had been burned into my skin the night the storm answered me, the mark the Stormhold judges had stared at while they pronounced my exile, the mark I had covered with gloves and silence ever since.

It faded as the stove warmed, melting away like it had never been. But I sat on the edge of my bed with my heart in my throat, and I made myself say it plainly, because lying to yourself is the one charm that never pays:

Something was happening. The prayers were working. And whatever door had opened — I had the sick, crawling certainty that it had opened *through me*, that twelve years ago I hadn't just killed a man and lost a home, I had cut the first notch in a lock that someone, somewhere, was now patiently picking the rest of the way open.

Outside, the city woke. Carts groaned over frozen mud. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, hoarse with cold. Ordinary sounds, ordinary world.

And threading through all of it, so faint I could almost pretend otherwise, the wind coming off the sea was whispering — not to me, not yet, but to anyone who cared to pray.

I dressed. I buttoned my gloves. I went down to open my stall, because a woman has to eat, and because the best place to hide is in plain sight among things that don't work.

But I caught myself, twice before noon, glancing at the sky.

And when the boy came back that afternoon — grinning, gap-toothed, eyes shining — and grabbed my sleeve and shouted that his father's boat had come in that morning, every man alive, *every one*, and that Gran was lighting candles at the old shrine on Tanner's Lane, the one that had been empty for four hundred years —

— I smiled for him, and took his copper for a knot he didn't need, and felt the ground tilt quietly under everything I had built my life on.

The past I buried had begun breathing again.

And I was fairly sure I was the lungs.
