## 2. Prayers with Teeth

By the third morning, Vellharrow had stopped pretending nothing was wrong.

I knew it the way you know a storm is coming before the clouds bother to show themselves — not by any one sign, but by the texture of the silence underneath. The city's silence had always been a particular thing: flat, sealed, a lid screwed down over something that used to boil. For twelve years I'd lived under that lid like it was furniture. Now there was a draft coming through it.

"Kestrel Underhill." A woman at my stall — Maren Ostler, baker's widow, three fingers missing from her left hand and a mouth like a dropped anvil. She slapped a copper down on my boards. "The fever-charm. Another one."

"Maren, I sold you one yesterday."

"And it worked." She said it flatly, daring me to laugh. "My boy's fever broke at dawn. Broke clean. He was gray as ash when he went to bed and pink as a ham this morning, asking for breakfast like nothing happened. So." Slap. Another copper. "Another one."

I looked at her hands. At the copper. At the frost melting off my awning in long, deliberate drips, which it should not have been doing, because the morning was cold enough to crack a bucket.

"Maren," I said carefully, "charms are knots and thread. What they do is remind you to hope, and hope reminds you to boil water and open windows. That's all. That's ever been all."

"I know what they are," she snapped. "I've bought them for six years. They're string. But yesterday your string *worked*, girl, so either you've been swindling us all along or something's changed, and frankly after six years of string I don't care which."

She took her charm and went. I stood there holding two coppers that suddenly felt heavier than they were minted to be.

Because here was the thing about selling harmless charms in a magic-free city: you had to be harmless yourself. Thoroughly, boringly, provably harmless. Warden Maerin Vale walked the market rows every week, and while he had never once looked at my stall with anything but the polite blankness of a man auditing turnips, I had spent twelve years making sure that if he ever did look properly, he would find nothing but a tired woman and good knotwork. No sparks. No whispers. No weather.

And now my luck-knots were working, and I hadn't done a single thing to make them work, and every instinct I owned — instincts that had once been very expensive to trust — stood up and started screaming.

*It began breathing again,* I thought, and hated myself for thinking it. Because that was exactly what it felt like. Something buried. Something I'd helped bury. Breathing.

* * *

The stories came in with the fish carts, as stories do.

Tomas Brack, drowned off the Gullspit rocks on Tuesday — his boat found hull-up, his wife already wearing black — walked out of the surf Wednesday night, dripping, blue-lipped, and entirely alive. He said he remembered cold, and then he remembered a voice telling him *not yet*. He could not say whose voice. He said it twice anyway, because everyone made him, and each time he said it the candles nearest him guttered, just slightly, just enough.

A child on Candlemaker's Row prayed for rain for her father's dying garden, and got rain — a small, tight, furious little cloud that hung over one garden like a guilty secret until the beans stood up straight, then dissolved without wetting the street.

An old man at the Almshouse of Saint Verren — and note, please, that Vellharrow has no saints, only the Silence, only the great sealing of magic two generations back, but old men will invent saints the way dry ground invents cracks — an old man prayed himself out of a deathbed and into the taproom, where he drank four pints and told anyone who'd listen that he'd heard singing under the floor.

Under the floor. Under the stone. Under everything.

The city didn't know what to do with it. You could see it working through people like drink through a thin man. Some wept in the streets. Some locked their doors. The temples of the Approved Faiths — tidy, sanctioned, thoroughly unmagical institutions — held emergency councils, and their ministers emerged with faces like men who have been assured for decades that the cellar is empty and have just heard footsteps.

And the wardens posted notices. *Report manifestations. Report answered prayers. Do not pray, if you can help it.* Which is rather like posting a notice that says *do not think of eels.*

By Thursday evening, half the city was praying and the other half was listening for it, and I sat behind my shuttered window with a cup of tea going cold and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Maren Ostler slammed her coppers down.

Answered prayers need a door. That was the first law Aldous ever taught me: *power doesn't knock, Kestrel. It seeps. Your job is to know where the walls are thin.* The gods of the old world hadn't gone anywhere when the Silence fell — you can't kill things like that, only shut doors on them — and doors stay shut unless someone opens one.

Someone had opened one. In my city. Near my stall. Possibly, said a voice in my head that sounded unpleasantly like my own guilt, possibly *because of* my stall.

There was a prayer whispered at my counter on Monday. A child's prayer. I'd heard it, felt it go past me like a cold current, and I'd told myself it was nothing, memory playing tricks, frost on the ears.

Time to stop lying to myself. I'm bad at it; it curdles.

* * *

I went at night, because whatever I was walking toward, I preferred to meet it without witnesses.

The old shrine sat below the Tidegate, in the crook of the harbor wall where the masonry had crumbled into something between architecture and gravel. It predated the Silence, predated the Approved Faiths, predated probably the harbor itself in spirit. Once it had been a place where fishermen asked the sea's older tenants for safe passage, back when asking worked. After the sealing it became a place where teenagers went to be transgressive and drunkards went to argue with nothing. The altar stone was a slab of gray granite furred with salt-lichen, worn smooth in the middle by centuries of anxious palms.

I'd passed it a thousand times. It was furniture too.

Tonight the air around it moved.

Not wind — I want to be precise about this, because wind is my native language and this wasn't it. Wind comes from somewhere and goes somewhere and has opinions about flags. This was more like the air remembering how to breathe and doing it tentatively, the way you move a limb that's been asleep. The lichen on the altar glistened. Frost ferned across the stone in patterns that were almost — almost — letters, and then weren't, and then almost were again.

"Well," I said aloud, because talking to haunted shrines is less embarrassing if you commit to it. "Let's see what you are."

I put my gloved hand on the altar.

Cold shot up my arm — not temperature-cold, but the other kind, the kind with intent behind it, the kind I hadn't felt since the Stormhold, since the last time I stood in a circle of power and everything went wrong in the space between two heartbeats. I snatched my hand back. My breath came out white and hung there far longer than breath should hang, curling, considering.

And then the frost finished its work, and I saw the mark.

It burned up through the granite from underneath — no, that's not right either. It didn't burn. It *surfaced*, the way a body surfaces, the way truth surfaces, slow and inevitable and worse than fire. Lines of pale light drawing themselves in the stone, and I knew them before they finished drawing, knew them the way you know your own signature, your own face, the scar on your own palm.

A circle, broken at the north point. Three lines falling through it like rain through a window. A spiral at the heart, turning inward, turning down, a storm drawn in cross-section, the eye of it rendered as a single perfect dot.

My sigil. My mark. Not the sigil of storm-callers generally — theirs is a triangle in a ring, tidy, communal, taught to children with chalk. This one was mine alone. I had drawn it once, in blood and lightning and grief, on the night the sky over the Stormhold tore open and Aldous —

I didn't finish the thought. I never finish that thought; it has no bottom.

But my hands finished it for me. They shook, and under the shaking they *remembered*, the way hands remember knots. Because here is what nobody tells you about being a living sigil — well, here is what nobody told *me*, because nobody knew, least of all me: a sigil isn't a thing you draw. It's a thing you *are*. Every line of it runs through you like grain through wood. And when the conditions are right — when the pressure builds, when the walls are thin, when some desperate fool prays hard enough into the dark—

The dark answers through you.

"That child," I said to the shrine, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Monday morning. She prayed at my stall."

The frost said nothing. Frost is smug that way.

But I could feel it now, with my palm hovering an inch above the stone: the shape of it, the mechanism of it, laid bare like a dissected bird. Her prayer had gone out — small, hopeless, aimed at nothing, because she'd been born in a city where prayer goes nowhere and she prayed the way you hum, absent, half-believing. And it had hit me. Passed through me. Through the mark I carry in my marrow. And the mark, starved for twelve years, sealed and silent and *hungry* — the mark had opened. Just a crack. Just enough for something on the other side to lean against the door and whisper *not yet* to a drowning man and *rain* to a child and *sing* beneath the floors of almshouses.

I did that. Me. Not the Thorn Court, not some rogue godling slipping its leash — me, with my guilt and my gloves and my twelve years of careful harmlessness, I had been the thin place in the wall all along, and I had never known, and now the wall was leaking.

I sat down on the harbor stones, because my legs had resigned without consulting me, and I laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, the laugh of a person discovering the fire she thought she escaped has been sleeping in her pocket the whole time.

Twelve years ago they exiled me for killing my mentor with a storm. Twelve years I believed it — believed I was the disaster, the accident, the girl whose power ran ahead of her sense. I wore that guilt like a coat; it fit; I stopped noticing its weight. And now here was proof of a different order of disaster: not that I'd once lost control, but that I *was* a control loss. Standing. Breathing. Selling luck-knots.

Every prayer answered in this city had gone through me first.

Which meant every warden in Vellharrow was hunting a miracle, and the miracle was sitting on cold stones in a stolen hour, and when Maerin Vale traced the thread — and he would, because tracing threads was the one thing that man did better than breathe — the thread would end at my stall. At my hands. At me.

"Right," I told the frost. "Good chat."

I got up. My knees cooperated on the second attempt. And then I did the thing I would spend a great deal of later time regretting or not regretting, depending on the day: instead of walking away, instead of going home and packing and running — twelve years of exile had made running a fluent language for me, I could have been over the Tidegate and gone by dawn — instead, I reached out and touched the sigil one more time. Deliberately. With my bare hand, glove stripped off, scars and all.

I wanted to know who was leaning on the other side of the door.

The cold took my hand like a handshake from something with too many joints. And for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat, no more — I heard it. Not words. Words would have been merciful, words you can refuse. This was older: a vast, patient attention turning toward me through the crack I'd opened, the way a whale turns toward a swimmer, the way winter turns toward a lit window. It knew me. That was the horror of it, the true horror, the part I'd keep behind my ribs for chapters of this story yet unwritten: it wasn't surprised to find me. It had been waiting for me. Perhaps it had always been waiting for me. Perhaps the exile, the storm, the whole ruinous arc of my life had been less an accident than an *appointment*.

Then it let go, and I stumbled back, and the frost on the altar slid away all at once like a curtain drawn, and the shrine was just a shrine again — dead stone, salty lichen, teenage graffiti from forty years past (*VELLHARROW RULES, MAGIC DROOLS*, carved by some ancestor of every idiot I went to school with).

I stood in the dark with my bare hand burning and my heart going like a drum in a war song, and I understood, finally, completely, the shape of the trap I'd been living in:

I couldn't report this. Reporting meant revealing myself, and revealed sigils get harvested — that's not paranoia, that's history; the old courts kept sigils like orchards.

I couldn't run. Running meant leaving the crack open behind me with no one watching it, and whatever leaned against that door would keep leaning.

And I couldn't fix it. I had looked at the mechanism with my own eyes and my own hands, and the mechanism was *me*.

Somewhere across the city, a bell rang the hour, and under the bell — faint, unmistakable, unmistakably real — something sang beneath the stones.

I pulled my glove back on over my scars, and I went home the long way, and I did not run.

But come morning, when the wardens came — and they would come, they were already coming, boots on frost-slick cobbles — I would be at my stall, selling string, harmless as ever.

And the most dangerous lie in Vellharrow would be the truth standing right behind my counter.
