## 3. The Warden's Warrant

There are two kinds of trouble in Vellharrow: the kind you can arrest, and the kind that files paperwork. I have spent eleven years learning to tell them apart, and I will tell you now what took me a decade of frostbitten mornings to understand — the second kind is always worse.

The drowned fisherman was the first sign. Not that he'd drowned; men drown in Vellharrow's harbor every winter, because the sea does not care for our ordinances against it. The trouble was that he *un*-drowned. Tam Brask walked out of the water on the morning of the ninth, coughing up brine and prayers in equal measure, alive as a tax collector and twice as smug about it. By noon there were four hundred people at the old shrine on Candlemaker's Row, kneeling in the snow, asking the dead gods for things.

By evening there were six hundred, and I had a headache the size of the harbor master's ego.

"Miracles," said Sergeant Odo, who has the imagination of a turnip and the loyalty of a very good dog. "Actual miracles, Warden. My gran used to say the gods went quiet when the walls went up. Maybe they've come back."

"The gods did not go quiet," I said. "They were silenced. There's a difference, and the difference is my job."

Odo nodded the way he nods at everything, which is to say he agreed without understanding a single word, and we went down into the crowd together to do something about it.

* * *

You should understand something about Vellharrow before you judge us. Two hundred years ago, this city watched its neighbors burn themselves hollow on divine favor — kings crowned by whispering gods, harvests blessed until the blessing rotted, wars fought over whose thunderhead had the truer voice. So our founders did the only sensible thing left to frightened people. They built the Silence. No temples. No prayers answered. No magic at all, not so much as a hedge-witch's fever charm, and the Wardens' Office to keep it that way.

It worked. It worked so well that most people under thirty think magic is a story grandmothers tell to make porridge seem interesting. And then a fisherman walked out of the harbor, and the stories got teeth.

The shrine on Candlemaker's Row had been a shrine once, back before the Silence, and then a tannery, and then nothing, which is the natural career arc of buildings in this city. Someone had cleared the snow from its steps. Someone had set candles in the crusted ice along the altar stone, dozens of them, guttering in the wind like a congregation of small cowards. And someone — many someones — had been praying.

I felt it before I saw anything. That's the part I don't put in reports. Eleven years of purging charms and burning grimoires, and I have never once felt magic, because feeling it is supposed to be impossible for anyone without the talent. But standing on those worn steps, the hair on my arms lifted, and the air tasted the way it tastes before summer lightning — green and sharp and wrong for midwinter — and somewhere behind my ribs an old instinct said, very quietly: *run.*

I didn't run. I'm a warden. Running is for the other kind of trouble.

The altar stone was the source. I knew it the way you know a lie in a testimony — not by evidence but by the shape of the silence around it. Frost had grown across the granite in patterns too regular for weather, spirals and interlocking lines, and at the center, where the candle smoke blackened the rock, there was a mark burned deep into the stone itself.

I crouched over it for a long time. Long enough that Odo asked me twice if I was well.

I was not well. I was looking at a sigil I recognized, and recognizing it made no sense whatsoever, because sigils are drawn, deliberately, by trained hands, and this one had been *burned* — seared into granite by something hotter than any candle, in a single furious instant. And I knew the hand. Not the hand that drew it. The hand that wore it.

Kestrel Underhill sells luck-knots from a stall on Tallow Street.

* * *

Let me tell you about Kestrel Underhill, since apparently I am going to be honest in this report whether the Office likes it or not.

She has held the same corner stall for nine years, between the chandler's and a pie shop that would be a crime if bad pastry were illegal. She sells luck-knots braided from dyed cord, fever-charms that are just dried yarrow and good intentions, carved door-spirits for new houses, little tin bells "against ill luck," which is a scam so ancient it's practically heritage. Every warden knows her. Every warden has checked her stock a dozen times and found exactly what she claims: string, herbs, tin, and a smile with a great deal of weather behind it.

She is not what you'd call warm. She's quick and dry and she haggles like the haggling is the actual product, and she has never once, in nine years, given me cause to write her name in a ledger. I have admired her for approximately that long, in the way a man admires a lighthouse — regularly, from a respectful distance, telling himself the admiration is purely professional.

I told myself that again, walking to Tallow Street with an arrest warrant folded in my coat like a knife I hadn't decided to use.

The market was thin that morning, the cold keeping all but the desperate home. Her stall was open anyway, awning frosted, cords swaying. She stood behind it with her hands bare — I noticed that first, the bare hands, because she always wears gloves, even in summer, and I had long ago filed it under *eccentric* rather than ask — wrapping a luck-knot with the fierce concentration of a woman defusing something.

"Warden Vale." She didn't look up. "Come to confiscate my tin bells? They're decorative. I say so on the sign."

"Your sign says 'against ill luck.'"

"Ill luck is also decorative. Mostly theoretical. Like your job."

Odo made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. I let him live.

"Kestrel." I kept my voice level, conversational, the voice I use for people I don't want spooked. "Where were you two nights ago?"

"Nowhere. Here. Where everyone is." She finished the knot, bit the cord, looked up. Her eyes are grey, storm-grey, and I have thought that exact pretentious thought perhaps four hundred times and never once said it aloud. "Why?"

"A man came back from the dead."

Something crossed her face. Not surprise. Recognition, arriving late to a party it had been dreading. "Tragic for him," she said. "Or wonderful. Depends on his debts."

"There were answered prayers at the shrine on Candlemaker's Row. Real ones. Old ones." I set my hand flat on her counter, over the luck-knots, as if pinning them down would pin down the conversation. "And at the center of it, burned into the altar stone, a sigil. A storm-caller's binding mark. Yours."

The market noise seemed to step back. Even the wind gave us room.

"That's not possible," she said quietly.

"It's your mark. I've seen it — you press it into the wax seals on your better charms. You think nobody notices."

"I haven't pressed a seal in twelve years." Her hands had gone still on the counter, and I saw then what I had spent nine years not looking for: scars climbing out of her collar, pale as lightning-wood, branching up the side of her throat. "Maerin." She almost never used my name. When she did, it landed like a dropped coin. "If that sigil is active — if prayers are being *answered* — then something has unsealed it. Something outside these walls. Because I sealed it shut myself, the night everything ended, and I would know if—"

She stopped. Her jaw did something complicated.

"If?" I said.

"Nothing." She straightened. The merchant's smile came back up like a shutter. "So. Are you here to buy a bell or arrest me? Because I've got customers coming, and either way you're blocking my light."

I had the warrant in my pocket. I had had it since dawn, signed by Captain Ilvane, countersigned by someone from the Magistrate's office with initials I didn't recognize, ordering me to detain one Kestrel Underhill on suspicion of unlicensed thaumaturgy pending inquiry. It was the most straightforward order I had received in years. Magic in Vellharrow. A sigil-burner. An open-and-shut case, a career case, the kind of clean arrest wardens write memoirs around.

And I stood there in the cold doing arithmetic I hated: nine years of harmless knots. Twelve years since she'd pressed a seal. Bare hands in winter, always bare, hiding branch-scars like the ones storm-callers get when the power takes them young. A woman who flinched — actually flinched, a small involuntary thing, quickly murdered — when I said the word *answered*.

"You know what happens if I walk away," I said. "Another warden comes. One who doesn't know your stock. One who reads the warrant out loud in front of your customers."

"I know how warrants work, Maerin. I've had one before." She said it lightly, and it was the least light thing she'd ever said.

"Stormhold," I said. "Twelve years ago. The storm that killed Aldous Crane. They exiled you here instead of executing you, and nobody at the Office could ever tell me why the sentence was commute—" I stopped, because her face had gone white as the frost on her awning, and some questions you realize you're asking a moment too late.

"You've read my file," she said.

"I read everything."

"Then you know it doesn't add up." Her voice had gone very quiet and very steady, the way voices go when the person behind them is holding on with both hands. "A journeyman storm-caller calls a squall that kills one man and levels one tower, and the Stormhold — the Stormhold, Maerin, which executes for lesser crimes — sends her to a city with no magic and lets her sell *string*. Ask yourself why. Ask yourself who wanted me here, alive, quiet, and close to nothing." She leaned across the counter, close enough that I caught woodsmoke and yarrow and underneath it something electric, like the air off a struck flint. "Ask yourself who benefits from a storm-caller buried in the one city where no one would ever notice her power waking up."

The wind moved through the market and every tin bell on her stall rang at once, though none of them touched.

We both looked at them. We both pretended we hadn't.

"Kestrel," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended, "if I leave here without you, the next man won't ask questions. He'll bind your hands and march you through the Row where half the city is praying, and they'll see a witch dragged past their miracle-shrine, and I cannot protect you from what a frightened crowd does with a story like that. If I take you in — quietly, tonight, through the gaol's side door — you'll be in a cell, but it'll be *my* cell, under *my* watch, and I'll have time to pull the thread you just handed me. The file. The sentence. Whoever countersigned my warrant with initials that shouldn't exist."

She stared at me. Somewhere under the wariness there was something else, something startled, the look of a woman who has spent twelve years being nobody's problem and has just discovered she is somebody's.

"You'd arrest me," she said slowly, "to protect me."

"I'd detain you. Formally. It sounds better in the ledger."

"And if the Office decides I'm guilty of whatever's happening at that shrine?"

"Then I'll have made a terrible mistake," I said, "and I'll spend the rest of my career undoing it. I've done worse with less reason."

For a moment — one moment, no longer — her hand moved across the counter toward mine. It stopped halfway. Bare fingers, scarred knuckles, trembling slightly with cold or with something that wasn't cold. Then it withdrew, and she began stacking her luck-knots into a basket with brisk, businesslike violence.

"Close the stall," I said. "Now. Before the crowd on Candlemaker's Row hears there's a charm seller on Tallow Street and puts the two miracles together."

"They already have." She nodded past my shoulder, and I turned, and my stomach dropped.

They were coming up Tallow Street in ones and twos — the praying kind, the hopeful kind, faces pink with cold and faith, carrying candles stubbed from the shrine. And at their head, walking with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been told no, came three riders in grey cloaks, and each of them wore a mask of woven black thorn.

I had seen that mask exactly twice before, both times in sealed archives, both times in documents about a court that officially did not exist.

Behind me, Kestrel Underhill went very still, and said, in a voice stripped of all its dryness:

"Maerin. Whatever you were planning — it just got shorter."
