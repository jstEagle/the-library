## 4. Twelve Years of Silence

They took me at dawn, which I suppose was considerate of them. No crowd. No spectacle. Just six wardens in grey wool and iron buttons, standing in my little stall on Tanner's Row like a funeral party that had lost its corpse.

"Kestrel Underhill," said the one in front. He had a voice like a door closing. "You're detained under the Act of Quiet, on suspicion of unlicensed working."

I looked past him at the frost on my awning, at the luck-knots swaying gently in a wind that had no business being gentle in Vellharrow in midwinter, and thought: *well. That didn't take long.*

"You have the wrong woman," I said. "I sell string. String is legal. There's an entire guild devoted to it."

The warden's eyes went to my hands — gloved, always gloved — and then to the knot-work on the table, and something flickered across his face that wasn't suspicion at all. It looked almost like apology.

"I know," he said quietly. "But I have a warrant."

And that, more than the irons, more than the six of them, told me how much trouble I was actually in. Because Warden Maerin Vale did not say *I know* lightly. I'd watched him for twelve years from behind my charm-stall, the way you watch a lighthouse: constant, distant, useful for orientation. I knew he gave beggars his supper ration and arrested hedge-witches with their hands bound in silk so it wouldn't chafe. I knew he hated his job the way honest men hate necessary things.

He hated this. You could see it in how carefully he didn't look at me.

* * *

The gaol in Vellharrow is built into the old city wall, all granite and iron and the particular silence of places where people are kept against their will. They put me in a cell with a barred window facing north, toward the hills, toward the Stormhold I hadn't seen in twelve years. Someone up the chain of command had a sense of humor.

Maerin came himself, after sundown, carrying a lamp and two cups of tea. He set the second cup on the floor inside the bars, which was either kindness or interrogation technique, and sat down on the corridor side with his back against the stone.

"You don't have to talk," he said.

"But you have to ask."

"It's procedure." He turned the lamp down a shade, so the light went soft. "Witness account. For the record." A pause. "For what it's worth, the record probably won't survive the week. But I'd rather it existed."

I wrapped my hands around the cup through the bars. The tea was terrible. Gaol tea always is; they brew it the way they build walls, for endurance rather than pleasure.

"Ask your questions, Warden."

"When did you first feel the change?"

That question has no honest answer that isn't a story, so I told him the story.

* * *

I was nine years old when the Stormhold found me, which is young even for them. Most storm-callers come into it at fourteen, fifteen — the temper years, when the blood's already half lightning. Mine came early and came loud. There was a hailstorm over the Weald that summer that killed three cows and a chapel roof, and every diviner within two hundred miles pointed northeast and said *that girl.*

Aldous Merren came for me himself. Later I understood what an honor that was; at the time he was just a big, tired man in a rain-grey coat who crouched down in my mother's doorway and asked if I'd ever felt angry enough to break something far away.

"All the time," I said.

"Good," said Aldous Merren, who was, I would learn, the sort of man who thought grief and fury were raw materials rather than problems. "Then we've something to work with."

Twelve years I trained at the Stormhold. And I want to be fair to that place, because fairness is a thing it never got and rarely gave. It wasn't cruel. It was worse than cruel — it was certain. Certain that weather was a language and we were its native speakers, certain that power was a duty, certain that the storms answered us because we deserved to be answered. When you're raised inside certainty, you don't notice the walls. You think the whole world is a room.

Aldous taught me everything. How to read cloud-fronts like handwriting. How to hold thunder in your teeth without swallowing it. How to stand in a gale with your arms open and *ask*, because commanding a storm was arrogance and storms punished arrogance, but asking — asking was conversation. He used to say I had the cleanest calling-voice he'd heard in forty years, and he said it gruffly, looking away, the way he said everything kind.

He also taught me the rule. The only rule that mattered:

*A caller never calls in grief. Grief has no shape, and the sky will give it one anyway.*

* * *

"He was my mentor," I told Maerin. "Twenty-two years my senior, forty years a caller. He pulled me out of a farm village and made me what I was."

"And what happened?" Maerin's voice was careful. Flat, the way men flatten things they don't trust themselves to say with feeling.

"The trial record says I called a storm in defiance of orders. That it struck the tower. That Aldous died holding the wards closed while I ran." I drank the terrible tea. "That part's true, Warden. Every word. I've never once lied about what happened."

"But not about why."

I looked at him. In the low lamplight his face was all planes and patience, a face built for listening, and I understood suddenly why the hedge-witches never feared him. Some people arrest you like they're taking something from you. Maerin Vale arrested you like he was carrying something for you and hated the weight.

"No," I said. "Not about why."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, the wind moved along the wall, and I felt it the way you feel a hand pass close over the hairs of your arm — not touching, but near. Near, and *interested*. Maerin felt it too. I saw him glance at the ceiling.

"Tell me why," he said.

* * *

It was autumn, my twelfth year at the Hold. I was twenty-one and so full of my own gift that I mistook it for wisdom, which is the standard ailment of the gifted.

There was another student. His name doesn't matter now; the Court scrubbed it from every record, and I've half-scrubbed it myself, the way you sand a scar to make it lie flat. What matters is that he climbed the west spire in a squall to prove he could call lightning to hand, alone, unsanctioned, and the storm — storms are not servants, whatever the Hold taught, they are animals, vast and indifferent — the storm took him off the tower like a gull off a rail.

I saw it happen. From the east gallery. Forty feet of open air between us and I stood there with a caller's voice and did nothing, because Aldous had drilled discipline into me until it was bone, and discipline said *wait for sanction, wait for the circle, wait.*

So I waited. And the boy fell.

They fished him out of the ravine at dawn. And I went to Aldous — I remember the corridor, the torchlight, the smell of wet stone — and I told him it was my fault, that I'd frozen, that I'd let discipline be the thing that killed instead of the thing that saves, and do you know what Aldous Merren said to me?

He said: "Yes. And you'll carry it. That's what the gift costs. Carry it well."

Carry it well. As though guilt were cargo. As though it weighed nothing.

* * *

"That night," I said to Maerin, "I went up onto the tower roof. Alone. Which was itself against every rule we had." My hands had gone cold around the cup; I couldn't tell if it was the cell or the memory. "And I didn't call anything, Warden. I want that on your record, whatever happens to it. I didn't raise a finger of craft. I just — prayed. The way the old folk pray. The way people prayed before the gods went quiet. I stood in the wind and I said *if anyone is listening, look what your silence cost.*"

The wind along the wall had stopped. The whole gaol had that held-breath quality, the hush before weather breaks.

"And the sky listened," Maerin said. Not a question.

"The sky listened." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Three days later. I'd almost convinced myself I'd dreamed it. Three days later a front came down off the mountains that no chart predicted, no caller sanctioned, nothing. A black wall of weather with a shape to it, Warden. A *purposeful* shape. It hit the Hold at dusk."

"Aldous was on the tower. He'd gone up to work the anchor-wards, routine maintenance, and the storm came for the tower like it had my name written in its heart. Because it did. I'd signed it. A prayer is a signature, if anyone's listening." I made myself keep talking, because stopping meant drowning. "He saw it coming. He had time to get clear. Instead he opened the wards wide and started pulling the storm's spine, bending it away from the dormitories, and he held it for eleven minutes. Eleven minutes is a lifetime, in a storm. Long enough for everyone to reach shelter. Everyone but him."

"And you tried to help."

"I ran for the roof through wind that peeled slate off like bark. I got as far as the anchor-ring and I reached for him — I *reached*, the way you reach for a hand over a drop — and the storm took my reaching and made it a hook. My own calling-voice, turned against him. I didn't strike the tower, Warden. I *was* the tower. The storm bent around me and broke over him." The tea had gone cold in my hands. I set it down because otherwise I'd have crushed the cup. "He was still alive when I got to him. Half a minute. He looked at me and he wasn't angry. That was the worst of it. He looked *relieved*, like he'd finally paid off a debt, and he said—"

I stopped. Twelve years, and I'd never said the last part aloud to anyone.

"What did he say?" Maerin asked. Softly. Almost against his will.

"He said, 'Now we're even.'" I pressed my glove-covered knuckles to my mouth for a moment. "I never knew what he meant. I still don't. But the trial didn't care about any of it. The tribunal had the report written before I'd finished crying. *Unsanctioned calling, reckless grief-working, death by negligence.* Exile. Stripped of name and craft-rights. They put me on a cart south and told me if I ever worked so much as a fever-charm again they'd hang me, and I believed them, and I obeyed them, for twelve years I sold *string*, Warden, harmless knots and lucky thread, and I thought I'd buried it deep enough."

"But it wasn't buried," Maerin said. "It was planted."

I looked up sharply. That wasn't a witness-account question. That was a man thinking out loud in a dangerous direction.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

He was quiet a long moment. Then he reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper — not the warrant, something smaller, older, softened at the creases — and unfolded it halfway, and stopped, and looked at me with an expression I'd never seen on that patient face. Doubt. Not of me. Of everything else.

"The tribunal record from the Stormhold," he said slowly. "I pulled it yesterday, before the arrest. Standard procedure — verify the original crime before processing the new one." He turned the paper over without quite showing it to me. "The signature of the tribunal clerk matches no clerk ever registered at Stormhold. The seal impression is Thorn Court wax, but dated four years *before* the Thorn Court had authority to countersign northern trials. And there's a line item in the evidence inventory — 'recovered prayer-scroll, subject's own hand' — that appears in the inventory and nowhere else. Not read into evidence. Not described. Just... listed. Like someone wanted proof it existed without letting anyone see what it said."

The wind woke again along the top of the wall, and this time it wasn't passing near me. This time it came *down*, through the arrow-slit of my window, a thin cold ribbon of air that stirred my hair and carried a smell I hadn't smelled in twelve years — high mountain, ozone, the inside edge of a storm.

Maerin stood up very slowly. He was watching the slit in the wall, where nothing was visible but starlight, and his hand had gone to the lamp as if light might explain it.

"Kestrel," he said, and it was the first time he'd used my name, "what did your prayer say? Exactly. Word for word. What did you ask them for?"

The wind curled around my wrists like fingers checking a knot.

"I didn't ask for anything," I whispered. "I accused them. I said — *look what your silence cost, and if you're ever going to answer anyone, answer me.*"

Above us, far off beyond the granite, thunder rolled across a winter sky that should have been empty as a swept floor.

Maerin blew out the lamp.
