## 5. What the Cell Remembers

I have stood watch over murderers who snored like contented hogs. I have stood watch over smugglers who sang sea shanties in their sleep, off-key, with feeling. In eleven years as a warden of Vellharrow I had catalogued every sound a prisoner makes in the dark, and none of them had ever frightened me.

Then Kestrel Underhill began to talk.

Not to me. Not to anyone. She talked in a language I did not know and yet recognized the way you recognize a face from a nightmare — syllables like struck flint, vowels that bent wrong in the throat, words that seemed older than the word *word*. The gaol's night clerk, a pious man named Odo who crosses himself against anything more exotic than rain, refused to sit her corridor after the first hour. He left muttering about devils. I stayed, because staying was my job, and because — though I would not have admitted this under torture, which is ironic given where I was — I wanted to understand her.

The cell block of Vellharrow Gaol is stone on stone on stone, laid by masons two centuries dead who believed, rightly, that a city without magic must compensate with very thick walls. Nothing blooms in it. Nothing grows. Frost requires moisture, and moisture requires weather, and weather is something that happens elsewhere, politely, behind glass.

At half past the second bell, frost bloomed through Kestrel's cell wall like ink dropped in water.

I watched it happen. That's the part I keep returning to, when sleep declines to visit. I wasn't dozing; I was standing four feet away with my hand on my baton, doing my duty with exemplary dullness, when the grey stone under her barred window went white at its heart — a single fern-shaped crystal, then another, then a whole garden of them unfurling across the wall as if winter had remembered an old address. The temperature dropped enough that my breath showed. The lamp guttered but did not die.

And she kept talking. Asleep, wrists chained, face slack with exhaustion, she spoke in the old tongue with the fluency of a native and the grief of a mourner. Once — only once — I caught a phrase I could translate, because my grandmother used to sing it over the wash: *sleep, sleep, the storm is far away.* A lullaby. She was singing someone a lullaby in her sleep, in a language banned for three generations, while the walls grew ice around her like flowers growing toward a sun that wasn't there.

She stopped mid-verse. The frost stopped too, hanging on the stone, refusing to melt even when I held the lamp close enough to warm my own knuckles. It didn't melt for six hours. When the day shift arrived, Sergeant Brannick looked at the wall, looked at me, looked back at the wall, and said, "Vale, I don't get paid enough to write this up."

"I'll write it up," I said.

"Don't," he said, with the wisdom of a man twenty years my senior and half my integrity. "You write it up, it goes to the Office of Records, the Office of Records sends it to the Thorn Court liaison, and then we all spend a month answering questions from gentlemen whose faces I am not paid enough to describe." He tapped the frosted stone once with his knuckle. It rang like a bell. "Clean it, file nothing, and Vale — if she does it again, tell me before you tell anyone else."

Which told me Brannick had seen something like it before. Which told me there were things my sergeant knew that I did not. Which, in hindsight, should have been the first domino.

* * *

I cleaned the frost myself, scraping it into a bucket with a flat iron, and it came away in shapes — feathers, mostly, or leaves, or things between feather and leaf that I decided not to look at too closely. Kestrel slept through all of it. When she finally woke, near midday, she looked at the damp patch on her wall and then at me, and her expression was one I hadn't seen on her before: not fear, not defiance. Recognition. The look of a woman checking whether the trap has sprung.

"How much?" she asked.

"Enough," I said.

"Odo ran, didn't he."

"Odo ran."

"He always was a delicate sort." She flexed her chained hands, winced. "Warden. You should know that whatever your records say about what I am, they're incomplete."

"They say storm-caller. Exiled. Twelve years clean." I sat down across from the bars, on the stool Odo had abandoned, and made a show of settling in. "They also say you killed Aldous Merrin of the Stormhold. That part, I confess, I've read several times. It doesn't quite add up."

Her eyes came up sharp. "What doesn't?"

"The arithmetic. One apprentice, one master, one storm. Stormhold records say the storm was called at dusk. The witnesses' testimony says dusk. But the burn patterns on the tower — I pulled those from the archive two years ago, out of boredom, if you want the truth of it — show a strike pattern radiating outward from inside the structure. Called storms come down from above, Underhill. They don't come up from below." I let that sit. "Unless someone lit a fire first and blamed the sky."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she laughed, short and bitter, the laugh of someone finding a knife in a loaf of bread. "Twelve years," she said. "Twelve years I built my whole life on top of that arithmetic, and no one ever checked it."

"You never asked?"

"You don't ask questions about the thing that ruined you. You just carry it." She looked at her hands. "You're telling me the record shows the storm rose from inside the tower."

"I'm telling you the record has been sitting in a drawer marked *sealed* since before either of us could write."

That got her attention fully now, the way a hook gets a fish. "*Sealed*? By whom?"

"That," I said, "is what I intend to find out tonight. Try not to grow any more gardens."

* * *

The Office of Records occupies the basement of the Warden's Hall, which tells you everything about how much Vellharrow values its history. It is administered by Mistress Corvel, a small, dry woman of indeterminate age who has worked there so long that the dust has begun to regard her as senior staff. She owes me a favor dating back to the incident with the flooded annex and the forty-year-old tax ledgers, the details of which are sealed under a different arrangement entirely.

"Merrin trial materials," she said, when I gave her the request slip. "Stormhold jurisdiction, twelve years past. Hmm." She peered at me over her spectacles. "You're the fourth person to ask after that file, Warden Vale."

My stomach performed a slow, cold roll. "Who were the other three?"

"Two clerks from the Thorn Court liaison office, some years ago. And last week, a gentleman I'd never seen before. Grey cloak, no insignia. He didn't leave a slip." She sniffed. "He didn't leave, full stop, until I explained that sealed materials require a warrant countersigned by the Chief Warden, at which point he smiled at me in a manner I can only describe as botanical, and departed."

"Botanical."

"There were thorns on his mask, dear. Subtle ones. One learns to notice." She was already moving down the stacks, keys singing on their ring. "Here. Drawer nine. Though I warn you—" She stopped.

I stopped behind her. The drawer slot was empty. Not locked — empty, the little brass plate still screwed to the wood reading MERRIN / STORMHOLD / TRIAL OF, and beneath it, nothing but a rectangle of cleaner wood where a file had rested, undisturbed, for years.

"That's not possible," Corvel said, in the tone of a woman betrayed by furniture. "That file has been here since I filed it. I filed it personally. I remember the string on it, it was the red-and-black court string, very distinctive—"

"When did you last open the drawer?"

"A week ago. Inventory." Her voice climbed slightly. "It was here a week ago, Warden. I signed the inventory sheet myself."

"And your botanical visitor came—"

"Four days ago." She turned to look at me, and for the first time in our acquaintance, Mistress Corvel looked old, and frightened, and small in the way that people become small when the ground shifts under institutions they trusted. "Someone took a sealed trial file out of my office without breaking the lock, without disturbing the dust, and without leaving so much as a courtesy note. What kind of person does that?"

"The kind," I said, "who knows the lock better than you do."

* * *

Here is the thing about discovering your institution may be a criminal: it doesn't arrive as thunder. It arrives as bookkeeping.

I spent the rest of that day and most of the evening pulling threads, and every thread led somewhere worse. The Merrin trial file existed — I found references to it in three separate indexes, each citing a different drawer number, none of which contained it, which meant someone had been *editing the indexes*. The witness roster for the trial listed seven witnesses. I cross-checked against the Stormhold's own muster rolls from that season and found eleven names absent — including two junior wardens stationed at the tower that night who had transferred, within a month of the trial, to postings so remote that one of them was essentially guarding a lighthouse that hadn't been lit since the war.

The transfer orders were countersigned by the Thorn Court liaison. All of them. Every irregularity, every gap, every convenient absence in the record of that night carried the same quiet signature, the bureaucratic equivalent of a hand over a mouth.

And then there was the verdict itself. Trial transcripts in Vellharrow are copied thrice — one for the accused's jurisdiction, one for the Office of Records, one for the Court of Appeal. I sent a runner to the appeal court archives with a plausible story and a coin, and he came back at the ninth bell with a copy of the transcript that made me sit down on a crate of confiscated dice and read it twice.

The transcript of Kestrel Underhill's trial contains a statement from Aldous Merrin himself. Dated two days before the storm. In it, the master storm-caller formally requests that his apprentice be reassigned out of the Stormhold — not punished, *reassigned* — on grounds he describes, in language the transcript scrupulously preserves, as follows:

*"There are parties who have expressed interest in my student. Their interest is not academic. I have reason to believe her value to these parties lies in precisely the qualities her exile would cultivate. I will not deliver her to despair to suit their ledger. If the Stormhold cannot protect her from its friends, let it at least lose her honorably."*

Aldous Merrin had known. Two days before he died, he had tried to send her away, and the request was denied — denied by whom, the transcript does not say, but the denial is initialed in a hand I recognized, because I see it every day, on requisitions and rosters and the annual budget: the Chief Warden's own.

I sat in that dusty archive for a long time with the transcript in my hands. Somewhere overhead, Vellharrow went about its evening — bells, cartwheels, the clatter of a city that believes its laws are load-bearing. And I thought about a woman in my gaol who sang lullabies in a dead language while frost grew gardens on stone, and about twelve years of exile built on an arithmetic nobody was allowed to check, and about a dead man's last recorded act being an attempt to save her from exactly what happened anyway.

They hadn't punished her. They had *made* her. Exile as a forge. Guilt as fuel. Some part of me — the part that had spent eleven years believing the warrant in my pocket was justice rather than stationery — understood it before I let myself say it aloud:

Somebody needed Kestrel Underhill broken in a particular way. And the machinery I served had done the breaking, and then filed the receipts, and then somebody had come with a thorned smile and taken the receipts away.

* * *

I went back to the gaol past midnight, carrying the transcript copy sewn into my coat lining and a decision I hadn't finished making.

Kestrel was awake. Of course she was. She sat on the cot with her chains pooled in her lap, watching the door before I opened it, as if she'd heard my thoughts coming up the stairs.

"You found something," she said.

I hesitated. There is a version of me — the warden of two days ago — who files this conversation, redacts it, reports it upward to superiors who are, I now strongly suspect, the problem rather than the remedy. That version of me is honest, diligent, and thoroughly played.

I passed the transcript through the bars.

She read it the way people read wounds — fast, then slowly, then the same line again and again. Her breathing changed at Aldous's request. At *"let it at least lose her honorably"* she made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, small and involuntary, like a rib cracking.

"He tried to give me away," she whispered. "He tried to send me somewhere safe, and they wouldn't allow it, and then—" Her hands closed on the paper. Frost crackled across its surface, delicate and instant, feathering out from her fingertips, and she didn't seem to notice. "Then they killed him and wrote my name on it. Twelve years. Twelve years I prayed every night for forgiveness for a murder I didn't—" She stopped. Went very still. "Warden. What did you say happens to answered prayers in this city lately?"

"Underhill—"

"Because mine were *answered*." The frost was climbing the bars now, threading silver between the iron, and her eyes when they came up were wide and terrible with understanding. "Every guilt-wracked prayer I ever whispered into that shrine — every night for twelve years, begging the old gods to prove I wasn't what they said I was — someone was *listening*. Someone arranged all of this so that my despair would be exactly the right shape. And now the prayers are working, Maerin, and I don't think that's mercy." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I think I'm the key. I think I've been the key since the day they exiled me. And whoever holds the lock is coming to turn it."

The frost reached my side of the bars. It touched my sleeve and bloomed — a single perfect crystal flower, opening, impossibly alive.

Above us, faint through three floors of stone, the gaol's front bell began to ring. Not once. Not the change-of-watch pattern.

Continuously.

Boots on the stairs. Many boots. And underneath them, thin and wrong, a smell of green sap and cut stems drifting down through the dark, like a garden forcing its way indoors.

"Underhill," I said, drawing my baton and stepping between her cell and the stairwell, "how attached are you to staying arrested?"

She looked at the frost-flower on my sleeve, then at me, and despite everything — despite the terror and the grief and the twelve wasted years in her eyes — something at the corner of her mouth moved.

"Warden," she said, "are you offering to break the law?"

"I'm offering," I said, as the boots reached our floor, "to amend it."
