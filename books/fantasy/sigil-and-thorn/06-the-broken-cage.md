## 6. The Broken Cage

The cell had a view of exactly nothing, which I was beginning to consider a personal insult.

Three days of gray stone, a straw pallet that smelled of other people's regrets, and a slot in the door through which Maerin Vale delivered meals with the enthusiasm of a man feeding a debt he couldn't name. He'd stopped asking questions after the second day. Not because I'd answered them — I hadn't, not really — but because somewhere between the fever-charm confession and the drowned fisherman, we'd both realized the questions were bigger than the room.

I was picking at the mortar between two stones — not escaping, just bored, there's a difference — when the lamps in the corridor guttered.

All of them. At once.

In a city where magic had been illegal for twelve years, you learn to read the absence of it the way sailors read weather. And what I felt crawl up through the floor of that cell wasn't absence. It was *arrival*.

"Vale," I said to the empty corridor. "Whatever you're doing out there, stop."

The dark answered with thorns.

They came through the gaol's front doors without breaking them — which should have been my first clue these weren't constables. Thorn-masked figures, six of them, moving like water finding cracks, and where they walked the frost didn't melt, it *organized*, climbing the walls in patterns I recognized with a sick lurch of my stomach. The same geometry as the sigil on the shrine altar. My mark, or something wearing its shape.

The night warden got one shout out before a thorn-masked man touched his brow and he folded like wet paper. Alive, I noted distantly. They wanted no corpses tonight.

They wanted me alive too. That was somehow worse.

My cell door didn't open so much as *unmake* itself, iron unraveling into rust and memory, and a figure stood in the frame — tall, masked in woven blackthorn, voice like dry leaves when it spoke.

"Kestrel Underhill. The Court requests your presence."

"The Court," I said, backing against the wall. "Funny. I don't remember applying."

"You were invited twelve years ago." The mask tilted. "You are late."

*You were invited.* The words went through me colder than the frost on the stones. Twelve years ago. The storm. Aldous. The trial that had felt less like justice than a machine grinding toward a conclusion someone else had written—

No. Later. Panic later. Survive now.

I did the only reasonable thing: I grabbed the chamber pot and threw it at him.

To his credit — or the mask's — he caught it without looking. Which gave me the half-second I needed to dive past him into the corridor, where I immediately ran into a wall of Maerin Vale.

He was out of uniform. No warden's blue, no warrant chain, just a dark coat and a sword he was holding wrong for ceremony and right for killing. Behind him, down the corridor, two more thorn-masks lay crumpled, and there was blood on his knuckles that wasn't his.

For one absurd moment we just stared at each other — the prisoner and her jailer, both armed with nothing but bad decisions.

"They're not here to arrest you," he said. His voice was steady but his eyes weren't. "They're here to *collect* you. There's a difference, and I've spent three days reading the sealed files to understand it." He shoved something into my hands — my confiscated gloves, my coat, a knife. "Your trial records were tampered with. Sealed by an authority above the Magistry. Above the Crown, maybe. Whatever took your mentor, whatever put you here—" his jaw worked, "—it wasn't justice. It was *husbandry*."

"Husbandry."

"They've been keeping you. All this time. Exile, poverty, guilt — like aging wine." He said it flatly, but I watched the disgust move through him like a swallowed stone. "And I signed the warrant."

Somewhere behind us, stone screamed. The remaining thorn-masks had stopped being polite.

"I can't turn you over to them," Maerin said. "And I can't protect you in here. So." He looked at me, and for the first time since I'd known him — all those years of buying fever-charms he pretended not to notice — the duty-bound Warden Vale looked genuinely, terrifyingly free. "Run with me or run without me. Choose fast."

The old Kestrel — the one who'd spent twelve years trusting no one, least of herself — would have run alone. Would have told him exactly where he could sheathe his conscience.

But the thorn-mask was rising behind him, frost blooming from its fingertips in my own signature, and the arithmetic was simple: alone, I was a parcel being delivered. With him, I was at least a moving target.

"With you," I said. "But if this is a trap—"

"It's a catastrophe," he corrected. "Traps have plans."

We ran.

* * *

The back of Vellharrow's gaol is a drainage gate built for corpse removal, which tells you most of what you need to know about the city's relationship with its dead. Maerin had the key because, apparently, wardens who spend years quietly suspecting their institution also memorize its embarrassments.

We came out into the frozen canal district with alarms finally waking behind us — real ones, human ones, bells and shouting, the city's ordinary machinery grinding awake far too late.

"This way." He pulled me left, away from the watch posts. "The marsh gates. Nobody follows into the salt marshes in winter."

"Because people die in the salt marshes in winter."

"Because people die *everywhere* in winter," he snapped. "At least in the marshes it's honest."

Behind us, at the mouth of the alley, a thorn-mask stepped out of shadow without hurrying. Then another. They weren't chasing. They were *herding* — angling us, patient as sheepdogs, toward some route of their choosing.

Maerin saw it the same moment I did. I watched him do the math and hate the answer.

"They want us going east. Toward the coast road." He grabbed my wrist. "So we go west. Into the flats."

"The flats flood with the tide—"

"Not till dawn. Six hours." He was already running. "Six hours is a lifetime if you spend it right."

I'd have argued, but the alley behind us filled with cold that made my teeth ache, and arguing is hard when your lungs are crystallizing. We went west.

Here's what nobody tells you about fleeing for your life: it's mostly bookkeeping. Where to step, when to breathe, how long you can hold a stitch in your side before it becomes a knife. The salt marshes outside Vellharrow in deep winter are a gray labyrinth of frozen channels and dead reeds, moonlit and utterly silent, and we ran through them until the city shrank to a smudge of orange behind us and the thorn-masks — I checked twice, three times — did not follow.

They stopped at the marsh line. Stood there in a row, five of them, motionless as fence posts, watching us go.

"Why aren't they following?" I gasped.

Maerin turned, looked back, and I felt him arrive at the same conclusion I did, the one sitting in my chest like swallowed ice.

"Because they don't need to chase you," he said slowly. "They know where you're going. You just haven't figured it out yet yourself."

*You were invited twelve years ago.*

I sat down hard on a frozen tussock and laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, the hysterical edge of it scraping out of me into the dark. Twelve years of exile, and every road I'd ever walked had been a corridor. Every choice, a door someone else had left unlocked.

"Hey." Maerin crouched in front of me. Up close, in the moonlight, he looked younger and older at once — the exhaustion of a man whose entire architecture of belief had collapsed three days ago and who hadn't yet decided whether to fall with it. "Kestrel. Look at me. Whatever they planned — plans break. I've spent my career breaking them."

"You broke warrants," I said. "Not gods."

"No." He held out his hand. Bare, gloveless, calloused from sword work, offered into the freezing air between us like a question. "But I'm told storms do."

I looked at that hand for a long time. Hunter's hand. Jailer's hand. The hand that had slid bread through my door slot every day without commentary, that had lingered a half-second longer than regulation required, that I had pretended not to notice because noticing would have meant hoping, and hope was a luxury item in my particular tax bracket.

Twelve years ago I had trusted someone — Aldous — and the storm had taken him for it. Trust had a body count, in my experience.

But the alternative was sitting alone in a salt marsh, waiting for the tide or the thorns, whichever arrived first.

I took his hand. His grip was warm and careful, like he thought I might shatter, and I hated how much I didn't hate it.

"Don't make me regret this, Warden."

"Maerin," he said. "If we're running from the law together, you may as well use it against me properly later."

"Maerin, then." I let him pull me up. My legs had opinions about standing, all of them negative, but the horizon east was graying toward the Stormhold ruins — toward answers, toward whatever I'd been *ripened* for — and the horizon behind us held nothing but a city that had never wanted me anyway.

"We'll need shelter before dawn," Maerin said, scanning the reed-banks. "And food. And a plan."

"I have a plan."

"Do you."

"Yes. Step one: keep walking. Step two: survive. Step three:" I glanced at him sideways, and despite everything — the frost, the fear, the fact that my own signature was blooming across the country like a wound — I found the ghost of a smile. "Figure out why a hundred-year-old cult spent twelve years seasoning me like a festival ham. And then introduce them to the storm they ordered."

Ahead of us, far off across the flats, lightning flickered inside a cloudless sky. Once. Twice. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Like something answering.

Maerin's hand tightened around mine, and neither of us pretended it was for balance.

"That's not natural weather," he said quietly.

"No," I agreed, watching the light die. "It never is, around me. Walk faster."
