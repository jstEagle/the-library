## 7. Road of Ashes

**Kestrel**

The salt marshes ended a day and a half behind us, and what came after was ash country — old burn fields from some war nobody sang about anymore, where the trees stood up black and fingerless against a sky the color of dishwater. Charming scenery. If I'd been selling postcards, I'd have called it *Atmospheric*.

Maerin walked like a man who thought walking was a moral exercise. Straight back, even stride, boots striking the frozen ruts in metronome time. I walked like someone who'd spent twelve years sitting at a market stall and had opinions about it.

"You're limping," he said, without looking at me.

"I'm *strolling*. There's a difference."

"Your left knee disagrees."

"My left knee and I have an arrangement. It complains, I ignore it, we both maintain our dignity."

He stopped then, which meant I had to stop too, which was probably the point. He crouched by my boot with the grave efficiency of a man defusing something, ran his thumb along the laces, and tightened one that hadn't needed tightening. His hands were careful in a way that made me unreasonably angry. Wardens weren't supposed to be gentle. Gentle wardens were how you got ambushed, or so I assumed; I'd never actually been hunted by an ungentle one to compare.

"It's swollen," he said. "We stop early tonight."

"We've made four miles."

"We'll make four more tomorrow on two good knees instead of six today on one bad one." He straightened. "Arithmetic, Underhill. You sell charms. You must know some."

"I sell *luck-knots*, which is arithmetic for people who don't trust numbers."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. On anyone else I'd have called it a smile. On Maerin Vale it was more of a structural event — a small crack in official masonry — and I looked away before it could make me feel anything inconvenient.

That was the trouble with him, I was beginning to understand. Not the arrest. Not the warrant with my name inked on it like a price tag. The trouble was that when he broke me out of the gaol — when thorn-masked things with too many joints came through the wall and he chose *me* over his own uniform — he hadn't hesitated long enough to make it dramatic. He'd just done it, the way you'd pull a stranger out of a river, and now we were both fugitives sharing a road and neither of us knew what to do with our hands.

The ash fields gave way to scrub pine near dusk, and we made camp in the lee of a fallen trunk. Maerin built the fire small and shielded, because he was Maerin, and I sat as far from it as warmth allowed, because I was me.

"You can come closer," he said.

"I'm fine."

"The frost is forming on your sleeve."

"That's a fashion choice."

"Kestrel." My name in his voice did something unfair to my spine. "Come closer to the fire."

I came closer to the fire. Not because he told me to. Because my knee staged a coup.

We ate hardtack and smoked fish he'd bought off a marsh eeler with coins he'd had sewn into his coat lining, which told me he'd planned his treason longer than he'd admitted. I chewed and watched the flames and tried not to think about the other thing we weren't discussing: the frost.

It had started on the second night. Little blooms of white ferning across the ground around wherever I slept, spreading slow, like the cold was taking dictation from my dreams. And tonight, sitting this close to Maerin, I could feel it again — a pressure under my sternum, a whisper of static in my teeth, the old storm-calling stirring like something rolling over in its sleep.

"Move back," I said suddenly.

He blinked. "What?"

"From me. Move back. Now."

He didn't ask questions — another thing about him that kept wrong-footing me — he just shifted three feet down the log. A heartbeat later, frost spidered across the space between us, silvering the ash and dead needles in a perfect fan, pointed exactly where he'd been sitting.

We both stared at it.

"That's new," I said, when my voice worked.

"How long?"

"Since the gaol. Since—" I flexed my gloved fingers. The scars beneath them ached, the old Lichtenberg lines Aldous's death had burned into me, branching like frozen lightning under the skin. "Since your superiors' friends tried to put a sack over my head, if we're being precise. Which apparently frightens me into casting. Wonderful. My magic has developed anxiety."

"Or it's responding to proximity," Maerin said quietly. "To people."

"To *you*, you mean." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Congratulations, Warden. Twelve years of exile, twelve years of nothing — no storms, no whispers, not so much as a stiff breeze — and three days next to you and I'm frosting the countryside. Either you're very calming or very much the opposite."

He looked at the fire for a long moment. "My mother used to say weather follows feeling," he said. "She was a hedge-wife, out past the Northgate. Nothing licensed. She'd read the sky like tea leaves." A pause. "She was taken when I was fourteen. That's why I joined the Wardens. I thought if the rules were enforced *fairly*—"

"They'd stop being rules that break people."

"I was young."

"You were young," I agreed, softer than I meant to. The fire cracked. Somewhere off in the dark, a marsh bird made a sound like a hinge complaining. "Aldous used to say something similar. That power isn't the danger. Power is a hammer; guilt is the hand that swings it." I pulled my knees up, bad one and all. "He said it the morning he died, actually. I've had twelve years to appreciate the irony."

Maerin didn't offer comfort. He just moved back to his original spot on the log, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, inside the radius where the frost should have reached him.

It didn't reach him.

I noticed. I decided not to notice. Some discoveries are better saved for daylight.

* * *

**Maerin**

She talked in her sleep.

Not words, at first — just breath, quick and shallow, and the fire burning lower than it should, flames leaning away from her like courtiers backing from a temper. I fed it a branch and sat up properly, because sleeping on watch was a habit I'd never managed to acquire and wasn't about to start.

Then she said a word. One word, in the old tongue — the god-tongue, the tongue the Wardens made us memorize only so we'd know it when we heard it and report it when we did. I knew it the way you know a scar. *Vess-ka-orun.* The first syllable was the name stitched inside every Thorn Court seal I'd ever broken open in the archives.

Frost bloomed outward from her shoulders in a slow ring, delicate as lace, and stopped precisely at the edge of my blanket. As if the cold knew whose territory ended where.

I should have written it down. I should have logged it, catalogued it, done all the tidy institutional things that had been drilled into me since I was a boy watching my mother's cart roll through the Northgate with her hands bound. Instead I sat there in the dark listening to a wanted woman dream in a dead language, and I felt — there was no honest word for it except the soft one — *tender*. Which was a problem. Tenderness toward the subject of a warrant was how wardens ended up in their own cells.

The warrant was ash in my coat pocket. I'd burned it the night we fled, and I still caught myself reaching for it, the phantom weight of paper that would tell me who I was. Without it I was just a man in the woods with a storm-caller, and the strange thing — the thing I turned over like a coin with a worn face — was that it didn't feel like falling. It felt like setting something down.

She woke near dawn, sudden, whole-body, the way soldiers wake. Her eyes found me first, then the frost ring, and her face did something complicated.

"How much did I say?"

"One word. *Vess-ka-orun.*"

Her breath went out of her slowly. "That's the name on the seals. The Court's seals." She rubbed her arms, glaring at the frost as though it were a dog that had misbehaved. "I don't speak the old tongue, Maerin. Nobody speaks it. It died with the shrines."

"Then something is speaking *through* you."

"Yes, thank you, I'd arrived at the terrifying conclusion independently." She got up, stamped feeling back into her bad knee, and refused the hand I offered, then accepted it half a second later, which I chose to interpret as progress. "The Stormhold's two days north, if the maps in my head haven't rotted. Aldous will be dead, most likely, or worse than dead, but there are records. Trial records. Someone tampered with my exile and I want names."

"And after names?"

"After names?" She looked at me sideways, windburned and defiant and so tired it leaked through the defiance like water through a roof. "After names, Warden, I decide whether I'm a weapon or a person. I'd think you of all people would want to know how that turns out."

* * *

**Kestrel**

The riders found us on the afternoon of the second day, and I will go to whatever grave I eventually occupy insisting that it was not entirely my fault.

In fairness, the fault was distributed. Maerin's fault was choosing a route along the ridgeline, visible for a mile in every direction, because "the low ground floods." My fault was arguing about it loudly enough to drown out — ha — the sound of hooves.

"There." He said it flat, no drama, hand already closing on my wrist. Four shapes on the southern rise, black cloaks, and where faces should have been, woven masks of briar and bone. Thorn-masked. The same make as the things that had torn open the gaol wall.

"Riders," I said stupidly.

"Run."

We ran. Or rather, we ran, stumbled, slid down a scree slope that tried to murder my knee as a professional courtesy, and cut into a ravine choked with dead bramble. Behind us the hoofbeats didn't speed up. That was the worst part. They didn't need to. They rode at a walk, unhurried, the way you follow something you're certain of.

"They're herding us," I gasped.

"I know."

"Where's the exit from this ravine, Maerin? Tell me there's an exit from this ravine."

"There's a streambed. Half a mile."

"Half a *mile*?" My lungs were filing formal complaints. "I sell charms! My primary physical activity is haggling!"

"Less talking."

The pressure under my sternum had been building since the masks appeared, static crawling up my arms, and I realized with distant horror that fear was feeding it — that the storm in me smelled the hunt and liked it. Frost skated ahead of my boots on the stones. The air went sharp and metallic, the taste of a sky deciding.

"Kestrel." Maerin had stopped. He was looking at my hands, where light — actual light, blue-white and branching — was leaking between my glove seams like the world's worst lantern. "Your gloves."

"I know."

"Take them off or they'll burn."

"If I take them off I might burn *everything*—"

The lead rider crested the ravine lip above us and raised something that whistled — a horn, or a bone flute, a thin ugly note that went through my teeth and *hooked* into the thing under my sternum and pulled—

And the storm answered.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the gloves charring at the fingertips, remember Maerin shouting my name, remember the sky over the ravine going the color of a bruise in the middle of a cloudless afternoon. Lightning came down the ravine walls in sheets, not striking us, striking *around* us, a corridor of thunder walking backward up the slope, and the horses screamed and the masked riders scattered like crows off a corpse, and I stood in the middle of it with my bare hands out and my scars lit up like veins of silver, laughing or sobbing, possibly both.

Then it stopped. All of it. The sky healed itself sheepishly. I collapsed onto a rock.

Silence. Ringing, smoking silence.

Maerin crouched in front of me. His eyebrows were singed. His expression was doing several things at once, and the one that won was not fear.

"That," he said, "was the most controlled loss of control I have ever witnessed."

"Don't flatter me, I'm having a moment."

"Twelve years," he said slowly, "you told me you couldn't call weather anymore. That the Stormhold took it out of you when they exiled you."

"They did. They *tried*." I stared at my hands. The scars had faded from silver back to pale, ordinary old-hurt white, and I hated how much I already missed the light. "Apparently it grew back. Apparently it grew back *wrong*, or right, or — Maerin, I killed a man with less than that. Less than a tenth of that. Aldous died under a storm a fraction this size, and I just — I just did that on purpose, or near enough, and it felt—"

"Like what?"

I looked at him. Honest question, no ledger behind it, no warrant. Just a man kneeling in scorched bramble asking a witch how her lightning felt.

"Like coming home," I said, "to a house that burned down."

He held out his hand. After a moment — after deciding, consciously, with witnesses present — I took it. His palm was warm. The frost didn't touch him. I was starting to build theories about that, and I filed every one of them under *later*.

"Two miles to the Stormhold," he said. "Can you walk?"

"I can limp with tremendous dignity."

"That's the spirit."

We came around the last shoulder of the ridge as the sun went down, and there it was at last: the Stormhold, black against a bruised-orange sky, its towers snapped off like rotten teeth, its gates hanging open on darkness. Twelve years since I'd seen it. Twelve years, and my body remembered every stone.

And standing in the gate — small with distance, unmistakable with everything else — a figure. Leaning on the doorpost. Waiting.

Watching him raise one hand, slow, in greeting, I felt the ground tilt under everything I believed about the worst day of my life.

Because the figure in the gate of the ruined Stormhold was old, and bent, and gray —

and it was Aldous.
