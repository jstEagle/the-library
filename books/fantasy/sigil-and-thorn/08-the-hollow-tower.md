## 8. The Hollow Tower

The Stormhold had been a legend when I was a girl, and legends do not age well.

We saw it first as a smudge against the grey sky, a black tooth growing out of the mountain's shoulder. By midday it resolved into walls — or what remained of them. The towers still stood, mostly, but their crowns were broken like snapped fingers, and the great storm-vane that had once crowned the central spire hung twisted on its pinion, creaking in a wind that came from nowhere and went nowhere.

"That's it?" Maerin said. He'd been quiet for most of the morning, which for Maerin Vale was roughly equivalent to another man's silence for a week. "The famous Stormhold."

"It was taller," I said. "Before."

"Before what?"

"Before me." I didn't look at him when I said it. Some truths you hand over face to face; others you toss behind you and keep walking.

He let it lie there in the snow between us, which was becoming a habit of his I neither thanked him for nor trusted. Twelve years of warden training teaches a man to leave bait untouched until he understands the trap. I'd have respected it more if it weren't so unnerving to be the bait.

The road up was a road no longer — just a scar of old paving stones shouldering through the drifts, each one carved with a lightning-sigil worn nearly smooth. My boots knew the way anyway. That was the worst part. Twelve years, and my feet still remembered the count of steps from the gatehouse to the courtyard, the way they remembered a lullaby.

"You're shaking," Maerin said.

"I'm cold."

"You weren't shaking an hour ago."

"Then I'm colder now." I climbed faster, because if I slowed down he'd put his hand on my shoulder, and if he put his hand on my shoulder I would do something unforgivable, like lean into it.

* * *

The gatehouse stood open. Not broken — *open*, doors swung wide, hinges oiled. Snow had drifted only ankle-deep in the entry hall beyond, and someone had swept a path through it.

Maerin's hand went to his sword. Mine went to the charm at my throat, which was a knot of braided horsehair that did absolutely nothing except remind me of home, and which I gripped anyway, the way a drowning man grips a plank he knows is rotten.

"Someone lives here," Maerin murmured.

"Someone *keeps* here," I corrected. There's a difference. A thing lived-in has crumbs and clutter. This hall was immaculate. The flagstones gleamed. The banners of the Stormhold — grey silk shot with silver thread, storm-crow sigils staring down from every wall — had been laundered. Recently. The silver hadn't even tarnished.

Nobody launders banners in an abandoned fortress. Nobody sweeps snow in a ruin. Unless the ruin is not abandoned. Unless the ruin is being *maintained*, the way a stage is maintained between performances.

"Oh," I said, very quietly. "Oh, this is wrong."

"What?"

"This place is dressed." My voice came out flat and strange. "It's been kept ready. Like a house where the family is expected back any day."

"Expected by whom?"

I didn't answer, because at that moment the doors at the far end of the hall opened, and something shuffled out into the light.

* * *

I want to tell you that I recognized him instantly. That some deep chord of love sounded in me and I wept and ran to him. That is what happens in ballads, and I have made a modest living writing things that never happen.

What actually happened was this: an old man came toward us, tall once and stooped now, wrapped in a storm-caller's grey robe gone yellow at the seams. His hair had gone entirely white, and it hung past his shoulders in fine tangles, and his hands — his beautiful hands, hands that had taught mine to shape lightning out of raw air — trembled constantly, like leaves deciding whether to fall.

He stopped ten feet away. He looked at me. And his eyes, pale as winter water, went soft and puzzled and pleased all at once, the way an old man looks at a granddaughter he can't quite place.

"There you are," Aldous Vane said. "You're late for your lesson."

* * *

The sound I made was not dignified. I'll spare you the details. Maerin caught my elbow, whether to steady me or restrain me I couldn't have said, and probably couldn't have distinguished himself.

"Aldous." My voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "Aldous, you're — everyone said — I *saw*—"

"Saw what?" He smiled, and half his teeth were gone, and the smile was still, impossibly, his smile. "Saw the tower fall? Saw the lightning? Child, I've been struck by lightning eleven times. It's rude to assume the twelfth took."

"But the funeral—" My mind was scrabbling at a wall, looking for handholds. "They burned a body. I watched them burn a body. They gave me the ashes."

"They gave you *ashes*," Aldous agreed cheerfully. "Very tidy stuff, ashes. Cheap, too, if you know a butcher." He turned, robe swishing, and beckoned with one trembling finger. "Come in, come in. You've let the cold in. Your friend too — yes, yes, I see the sword, boy, you can put it away, nobody's going to hurt her in front of me. Or me in front of her. Probably."

Maerin looked at me. I looked at Maerin. Somewhere in that exchange we agreed on a treaty whose terms were: we go in, we stay armed, and if either of us says the word *run*, we run.

We followed the dead man into the heart of the Stormhold.

* * *

He led us to the old lecture hall, where I had spent six years of my youth learning the grammar of storms. Someone had lit a fire in the great hearth — a real fire, laid that morning, the wood dry. The benches were dusted. On the master's dais, chalk still sat in its tray beside a slate covered in equations of pressure and charge, written in a shaking hand.

Aldous settled onto his stool like a king taking a throne that had shrunk around him. Then he looked up at me again, and this time the puzzlement in his face sharpened into something else. Something that hurt worse than the madness.

"Kestrel," he said. "Kestrel Underhill. Oh — oh, forgive me, I thought—" His face crumpled and reassembled itself, weather changing. "They told me you'd come. They said she'd come when the frost was deep enough. I've waited such a long time for my lesson, Kestrel. Did you bring the answer? To the question I asked you? The night before?"

"The night before the storm," I whispered.

"The night before the storm," he agreed, delighted, as though I were a clever student finally catching up.

I sat down on a bench like my strings had been cut. Maerin stayed standing, planted between me and the door, doing his warden arithmetic: exits, threats, odds. There were no exits except the way we'd come. The only threat was an old madman by a fire. The odds were terrible anyway.

"Aldous," I said carefully, the way you speak to a spooked animal, or a spooked god. "Who told you I would come?"

"The grey people." He waved a hand vaguely. "Thorns on their faces. Very polite. They bring me bread and chalk and new robes, and they sweep the halls, and they say, 'Master Vane, she will come when the frost is deep enough.' And I say, good, good, because I have a question, and she never answered it, and a teacher cannot die with a question unanswered. It's untidy."

The fire popped. Somewhere high above, the broken vane groaned against its pinion.

"How long?" I asked. "How long have they kept you here?"

He frowned, counting on fingers that wouldn't hold still. "Since the storm. Since the twelfth lightning. Or since the eleventh. Time is slippery in this old tower, child; it runs through the cracks like water. But they are *very* punctual about the bread."

Twelve years. Twelve years they had kept my mentor alive in a swept and polished tomb, fed and clothed and tended, waiting for me to walk back through the door. Keeping him the way you keep a lure fresh.

And I had spent those twelve years carrying his ashes in a lead box under my bed, praying forgiveness to a ghost who was never there to receive it.

Something in my chest went very quiet and very cold, and I recognized the feeling, because it was the same feeling that had preceded every storm I ever called.

* * *

"Aldous." I knelt in front of him, took his trembling hands in mine. They were ice-cold even beside the fire. "The question. Tell me the question. Please."

His eyes focused — truly focused — for the first time. And underneath the white hair and the ruined mind, I saw a flicker of the man who had dragged a half-feral dockside witch-girl into the Stormhold and told her she was worth teaching.

"The night before the storm," he said, "you asked me why the gods went silent. Do you remember?"

"Yes."

"And I told you the truth, and you didn't believe me, because the truth was ugly. So I asked you the counter-question. The examination question. The one that determines everything." His grip tightened on mine with sudden, shocking strength. "I asked you: *what if the silence isn't the gods abandoning us, Kestrel? What if it's the gods being held?*"

The fire crackled. Maerin had gone very still by the door.

"And your answer," Aldous pressed on, relentless now, the lecture-hall cadence rising in his ruined voice, twelve years of waiting pouring out of him, "your answer, child, was *then someone should set them free*. You said it angry. You said it grieving. You said it to the sky, on the tower top, with your hands full of storm. And the sky *heard* you."

"No." I pulled back. "No, that was just — I was angry, I didn't mean—"

"Meaning is for priests!" He laughed, wild and bright. "The sky doesn't take *meaning*, it takes *shape*. And you, Kestrel Underhill, you are not a storm-caller. Storm-callers borrow. You — you are the door itself. A living sigil. Born once a century, marked at birth, sealed at trial, opened by grief. Why do you think your little charms worked? Why do you think the drowned fisherman walked home? Every prayer in this godforsaken city passes *through* you, and you have been wide open since the night you called the storm and wept and begged the silent heavens to wake up!"

The room tilted. I heard myself breathing. Maerin's voice came from far away: "Kestrel. Kestrel, look at me."

But I was looking at Aldous, and Aldous was looking at me with terrible, lucid pity.

"They didn't exile you for killing me," he said gently. "Oh, child. They exiled you to *season* you. Grief is the key that turns the sigil, and they needed you ripe. Twelve years of guilt, twelve years of prayers — you've been marinating in exactly the despair required. And now the frost is deep enough. Now you've come home. And somewhere south of here, the people who swept these floors are smiling, because the door is walking to the gate all on its own."

I stood up. My legs held, which surprised me.

Every mile of exile. Every charm sold for coppers while I flinched from my own reflection. Every night I'd lain awake rehearsing apologies to a corpse. Every prayer — every single desperate, guilty, begging prayer — catalogued and counted and *used*.

They hadn't taken my life. They'd *farmed* it.

"Kestrel," Maerin said again, and this time I heard him, and what I heard in his voice frightened me more than anything Aldous had said, because it wasn't suspicion anymore. It was alarm. For me.

I turned to the old man who had died for me twice — once in fact, once in fiction — and found my fury had nowhere to land, because he was already gone again, smiling his gap-toothed smile, patting my hand.

"There you are," Aldous Vane said warmly. "You're late for your lesson. But that's all right. We have time before the frost finishes deepening."

Outside, the wind changed direction, and began to blow from the north — from the Thornmarch — and it carried, faint and rhythmic, the sound of hooves.

Many hooves. Riding hard.
