## 10. Thornmarch

The pass had a name older than any of us, which in my experience meant it had a body count to match.

Thornmarch. The locals on the mountain's far side called it that because of the blackthorn hedges that grew wild along its throat, twisted into shapes no honest wind would make. I'd have preferred a pass named something cheerful. Buttercup Hollow. The Gentle Slope. But geography, like the gods, has never cared much for my preferences.

We'd been climbing for two days since leaving the Stormhold's ruin behind us. Aldous walked at the front because he insisted, and because arguing with him about walking was like arguing with weather — technically possible, deeply pointless. He was thinner than I remembered him being, and slower, and there were moments when his eyes went somewhere else entirely, some room inside his own skull where the Thorn Court had kept him for twelve years and presumably hadn't tidied up before leaving.

"Your breathing's loud," he told me, without turning around.

"You're ninety years old climbing a mountain," I said. "My breathing is the least loud thing here."

"I'm seventy-one."

"That's basically ninety."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't rusted out of him somewhere in captivity. Maerin glanced back at me from the rear — he always took the rear, because he was constitutionally incapable of letting anyone guard his back — and gave me one of those looks. The ones that said *I'm worried about the old man too* without saying anything so soft aloud. Warden training, probably. Feelings are contraband in Vellharrow; you learn to smuggle them in glances.

We made camp that night in the lee of a leaning cairn, and Aldous talked more than he had in all the days since the tower. Not about the Court, not about what they'd done to him. He talked about the old lessons. About how I used to cheat at grounding exercises by humming, because the vibration helped me hold still. About how he'd once caught me stealing honeycakes from the refectory with a wind-sigil and made me return every single one, personally, to a cook named Brannit who then fed me three more out of respect for the theft.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked, when he paused.

"Because," he said, looking into the fire with eyes that were clear for once, "you should know which parts of me were real. Before tomorrow decides which parts of you get to be."

I didn't sleep much after that. Neither did Maerin. I could hear him not sleeping across the fire, the particular silence of a man listening hard, and around the third hour he moved closer without pretending otherwise and sat beside me with our shoulders almost touching. Almost touching is a language we'd been fluent in lately.

"The pass," he said quietly. "It narrows to maybe ten paces wide. If riders come, we can't outrun them uphill."

"So we don't get caught downhill of them."

"That's the plan?"

"That's the plan. You're a warden. Improvise the rest."

His mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling. "You know," he said, "twelve years ago I'd have arrested you just for talking to me like that."

"And now?"

"Now I'm thinking about how badly the arrest would go."

* * *

They came an hour past dawn, and of course they came at the worst part of the pass, because the Thorn Court has never once in its long scheming history done anything inconvenient to itself.

We heard the horses first — that deep drumroll through stone — and then the blackthorn hedges along the pass walls began to move. That was the part that turned my stomach cold. Hedges don't move. These did, branches unknotting themselves, thorns lengthening like fingernails, and through the gap between them came riders in masks of woven briar, six of them, then four more behind, lances couched, moving at that unhurried trot of people who know exactly how many exits the room has.

"Down!" Maerin shoved me against the cliff wall as the first lance came low. It missed me by a hand's width and buried itself in the cairn-stone behind, splitting rock like rotten fruit.

Aldous didn't take cover.

He stepped out into the middle of the pass — seventy-one years old, half-mad, starved down to sinew and stubbornness — and raised both hands, and the air *changed*. I felt it in my teeth before I understood it. Old storm-craft. The real kind, the kind they taught at Stormhold before the Stormhold was ruins: not charms, not knots, but the raw argument between pressure and sky.

The lead rider's horse screamed and reared as a gust like a thrown door flattened everything ahead of Aldous. Two riders went down. The hedge-thorns whipped toward him and he burned them back with a crackle of static that lit his white hair blue.

"Aldous!" My voice cracked on his name. "What are you doing—"

"What I should have done twelve years ago." He looked back at me over his shoulder, and he was smiling, and I hated him for it. "There's a side path, forty paces back, behind the split boulder. It climbs to the ridge. It's steep and it's ugly and you'll hate every step. Take it. Don't look back."

"No." The word came out flat and immediate, the way truth does. "No, we go together—"

"Kestrel." And now his voice was the voice from the refectory, from the lessons, from the thousand small mornings of my childhood — patient, immovable, warm underneath like a banked coal. "I spent twelve years as bait. As a hook with your name on it. Let me spend five minutes as something better. Go."

Maerin grabbed my arm. Behind Aldous, the fallen riders were rising, and more thorn-shadows were boiling up the pass from below — the whole hedge-line alive now, a green-black tide. There wasn't time for the conversation we needed to have. There would never be time for that one.

"I'm not forgiving you," I said, because apparently that was what I chose to say with possibly the last words I'd ever speak to him. "Whatever this is. Whatever you think it buys. I'm not — I haven't decided anything."

"I know." His smile didn't waver. Thunder was gathering above the pass, actual thunder, answering him, and he stood in the middle of it like a man greeting an old dog. "That's why I'm asking anyway. Forgiveness isn't a debt, girl. It's a door. Leave it unlocked. That's all. Just — leave it unlocked."

Then he turned his back on me, which no mentor should ever have to do, and walked toward the riders, and the sky came down to meet him.

* * *

Maerin hauled me up the side path and I let him, mostly, though I remember fighting him for the first few steps out of pure animal refusal — my feet finding reasons to stumble, my head craning back, my hands clawing at his grip not to escape but to *stay*. Because staying meant watching. And watching meant —

The lightning hit the pass mouth in a column of white. I felt it through the rock under my palms, a shudder like the mountain flinching. When my vision cleared, two riders and most of the hedge were burning, and Aldous was still standing, arms spread, hair whipping, laughing — actually laughing, a ragged joyful sound that carried up the cliff face to me like a gift I hadn't earned.

"GO, YOU STUBBORN GIRL!"

So I went.

The side path was everything he'd promised and worse. It switchbacked up a chimney of broken stone, loose scree sliding under every step, wind trying to peel us off the wall. Maerin climbed behind me with his hand fisted in the back of my coat whenever the grade pitched past vertical, and neither of us spoke, because speaking required breath and breath was budgeted elsewhere. Below and behind us, thunder rolled again and again — Aldous spending himself coin by coin, buying each minute of our climb at the only price he had left.

At the third switchback I stopped being able to feel my legs and started being able to feel something else.

It started in my sternum. A heat, or a pressure, or a tuning — like someone plucking a string inside my chest that vibrated outward through bone. Every crash of thunder below sent a corresponding pulse through me. Every flare of lightning painted the backs of my eyelids silver. And with each pulse, something in me *opened*, a hairline crack widening, and through the crack came a taste of what I'd been before Vellharrow, before exile, before gloves — the storm not as something I summoned but as something I *was*, vast and indifferent and awake.

"Kestrel." Maerin's voice, very close, very careful. "Your hands."

I looked down. Frost feathered off my fingers in spirals, and where my boots pressed the stone, the rock had gone glassy-smooth, melted and refrozen. Above us the clouds had stopped drifting and started rotating, slowly, around a point directly overhead. Around *me*.

"It's fine," I said, in the tone people use when it is not fine.

"It's not fine. Kestrel, look at me." He caught my face in both hands, gloved against the frost, and made me meet his eyes. Grey eyes, steady, terrified in the way he never allowed his voice to be. "Whatever's happening — whatever he's giving you down there — you hold it. You hear me? Not forever. Just until we're over the ridge. Then you can fall apart as thoroughly as you like, and I'll be there for all of it. But right now you hold."

"He's dying." The words scraped coming out. "Maerin, he's — I can feel it, every strike costs him, he's spending *himself*, I can feel him getting smaller—"

"I know."

"We could go back. We could fight—"

"With what? Six against two?" His thumb brushed my cheekbone, rough and brief. "He made his choice. Make yours. Hold."

I held.

I don't know how to describe holding without describing what it held back. Imagine standing in a doorway with a hurricane on the other side and your shoulder against the door, except the hurricane is also *grieving*, also screaming a dead man's name in a voice that sounds like yours, and every second the door strains harder against your shoulder blade. The ridge came. We dragged ourselves over it into a high grey bowl of wind-scoured snow, and below us, far below, the thunder stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. The way a heart stops.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

* * *

We didn't stop moving until dark, because Maerin wouldn't allow it and because I couldn't have borne stopping. Stopping meant sitting with it. Moving meant at least the grief stayed windblown, smeared thin across miles, diluted enough to carry.

We sheltered finally in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Maerin built a small fire from the emergency kit he carried like scripture, and I sat with my knees pulled up and watched the flames do their ordinary flame things and thought about doors left unlocked.

"He asked me to leave the door unlocked," I said, eventually. To the fire, mostly. Maerin had learned when I talked to fires that I was also, incidentally, talking to him.

"And did you?"

"I don't know. I told him I hadn't decided. Which is — that's not an answer, is it? Twelve years I built my whole self out of what he did to me. The storm. The death. Exile. Guilt. All of it, load-bearing walls." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "And now it turns out even that was taken from me. Even my crime was furnished by the Thorn Court. So what am I supposed to do with all this grief, Maerin? It doesn't have anywhere to *go*. There's no villain in my story anymore. There's just an old man who loved me and got used for it, and he died buying me forty minutes up a cliff, and the last thing I said to him was *I haven't decided*."

Maerin was quiet for a while. He fed the fire a stick with the deliberateness of a man buying time to say something true.

"My father died when I was nineteen," he said. "Badly. I was on shift. I could have been there an hour sooner if I'd bent the rules, and I've spent eleven years deciding whether I did the right thing by following them." He looked at me across the fire. "You never finish deciding. That's not how it works. Grief isn't a question you answer. It's a weight you redistribute."

"That's very warden-ish wisdom."

"Yes, well. It's the only kind I stock."

Something in my chest cracked then — not the sigil, not the storm-thing, the other thing, the human one — and I leaned sideways into him before I could talk myself out of it. He absorbed the impact the way he absorbed everything, solidly, without complaint, and after a moment his arm came around my shoulders, and we sat like that while the fire ticked and the mountain wind mourned in its own dialect.

That was when I noticed the snow.

It had begun falling — ordinary-looking snow, fat flakes wheeling down past the overhang. Except every flake, for about thirty feet in every direction around me, hung in the air for a full heartbeat before remembering to fall. The whole hollow was suspended in a held breath. And the clouds above the ridge, which had been streaming east all evening, were circling. Slowly. Patiently. Around a point directly above my head.

Waiting to be told what to do.

"Maerin," I whispered.

"I see it." His arm tightened around me. He didn't pull away. That mattered more than I can say — that the frost bloomed off my skin and the sky orbited my grief and he *stayed*. "Is it you?"

"I don't know. Maybe. Maybe he — maybe whatever he spent down there didn't vanish. Maybe it went *somewhere*." I lifted my hand, and a thread of lightning answered it, delicate as a spun wire, arcing between my fingers without heat, without pain. Beautiful. Wrong. Mine. "Aldous said the sigil opens through despair. Vessia's whole design — season me with guilt, harvest me at midwinter. Well." I watched the light gutter and die between my fingertips, and felt the enormous attention of the sky settle back into waiting. "Congratulations to Lady Vessia. Her crop is coming in early."

Maerin shifted so he could see my face. In the firelight he looked older than his years and steadier than any man had a right to.

"How much control do you have over it?"

"Some. Not enough. And it's growing every hour, and I can feel—" I stopped. Started again. "There's something north of here. Past the ridge. I've been feeling it since the tower — a pull, like a current under ice. Aldous mentioned a shrine in the glacier, before the end. He said if I wanted answers about what I really am, the old prayers would know."

"The god-tongue singer Maerin — sorry, the singer I recruited in my head — knew songs about a glacier shrine," Maerin said dryly. "If she's still alive. She's the type to be alive purely out of spite."

"Then we go north." I stared into the fire, and the fire stared back, and somewhere in its heart I thought I saw the shape of an old man laughing in the rain. "Before whatever's opening in me opens all the way. Because Maerin — I felt it, when the thunder stopped. For one second, the storm and I were the same size. And it liked it."

Far to the north, beyond three ridges of blackening snow, something ancient shifted in its sleep beneath the ice — and every prayer ever whispered in Vellharrow trembled on its string, waiting to see which way the wind would turn.

We broke camp before midnight. North, into the white country.
