## 11. The God Beneath the Ice

I have served the Wardens of Vellharrow for eleven years, and in that time I have learned that most disasters announce themselves politely. A warrant arrives. A door is kicked. Someone screams. The bureaucracy of catastrophe is very orderly.

The glacier shrine did not announce itself at all. It simply appeared out of the white like a debt come due.

We had been walking for two days since Thornmarch — two days of Kestrel silent as a closed ledger, her hands still crusted with Aldous's blood no matter how many times she scrubbed them in snow. She hadn't spoken a word about the pass. She hadn't spoken much of anything, except once, at dusk on the first day, when she'd said, "He asked me to forgive him," and then walked faster, as if the sentence were something chasing her.

So when the storm came down off the icefield like a landlord with an eviction notice, I was almost grateful. At least it gave us something to argue with.

"There," I shouted, pointing through the white roar. A seam of blue light, faint and wrong-colored, glowing in the face of the glacier ahead. Not firelight. Firelight is orange and honest. This was blue the way a bruise is blue.

Kestrel squinted against the wind. "That's not a camp."

"No."

"That's not *natural*."

"Neither are you," I said, "and yet here we are, walking together through a blizzard like old friends."

She gave me a look that would have curdled milk, which I counted as progress. Two days ago she'd been giving me looks that would have curdled blood.

The seam turned out to be an entrance — a cleft in the ice, wide enough for one person, its edges worn smooth as if by centuries of shoulders. Carved above it, half-erased by wind, was a line of script in the old tongue. I knew enough of it from my training to read maybe one word in five, and the word I could read was *prison*.

Cheerful place, this.

Kestrel stood before the script for a long moment, snow gathering on her lashes. Then she said, quietly, "It says *they sleep beneath*. It says *do not wake them*." She glanced at me. "Your Warden training cover this?"

"Wardens don't train for gods," I said. "Wardens train for hedge-witches selling love charms at inflated prices."

"And how's that going for you?"

"I arrested one. She escaped. Repeatedly." I gestured at the blue-lit cleft. "After you."

"After me," she repeated, and there was something bitter in it, and something tired, and she went in.

* * *

Inside, the shrine opened into a hollow cathedral of ice, and I will admit — because no one else survived to contradict me — that my breath left my body entirely.

The walls glowed from within, that deep bruise-blue, and set into them were figures. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Human shapes frozen mid-motion, suspended in the glacier like flies in amber: a woman reaching upward, a man kneeling, children curled small, soldiers with their spears still raised. All of them facing inward, toward the center of the chamber, where a floor of black stone rose out of the ice like an island.

An altar. And on the altar, carved so deeply it might have been cut with lightning rather than chisels, was a sigil.

I had seen that sigil twice before. Once in the sealed records of the Warden archive, where it was labeled only *pre-ban iconography, do not reproduce*. And once burned into the altar stone of the shrine in Vellharrow, in Kestrel's own hand, without her knowing it.

"It's everywhere," Kestrel whispered. Her voice echoed strangely, doubled, as if the ice were repeating her. "It's on every wall. Maerin, it's—"

"You," I finished. "It's you."

"Don't say it like that."

"How would you like me to say it?"

"I'd like you not to say it at all," she snapped, and then pressed her gloved hand over her mouth, because the echo came back wrong — deeper, layered, like three voices wearing hers like a coat.

We stopped talking after that. Wise, under the circumstances.

We made camp on the black stone island, because it was the only surface that wasn't actively glowing, and I got a fire going with the last of our dry tinder while Kestrel explored the chamber's edge. I watched her move between the frozen figures, touching none of them, reading them the way I'd once watched her read customers at her stall — quickly, kindly, cataloguing griefs.

"They're not dead," she called back suddenly. "Maerin. They're not dead."

I joined her. Up close, I could see what she meant. The figures weren't corpses preserved in ice. Their chests moved. Impossibly slowly — one breath per minute, perhaps less — but they moved. A soldier's fingers twitched around his spear shaft. A woman's lips shaped some endless, glacial syllable.

"Sacrifices," Kestrel said. Her voice had gone flat, which I had learned was worse than her shouting. "This is what the imprisonment was. Chapter whatever-it-was of the shrine texts, the part everyone forgets. The gods weren't locked away by heroes with swords. They were locked away by *this*. By people. Thousands of people, given to the ice, alive, forever, to be the lock."

"The histories say the gods fell in battle," I said. "The Silence War. That's what they taught us."

"The histories were written by the winners' grandchildren." She touched the ice beside the kneeling man — not on him, near him, the way you touch a coffin lid. "Look at their faces. Nobody looks like this in battle. They look like this when they've been *promised* something. Salvation. That their deaths would hold the world shut." She laughed, one short ugly note. "And it worked. For a thousand years it worked. And now the winter's getting longer and colder every year, and everyone blames the weather."

I thought of Vellharrow's frost-bound markets. Of the harbor iced over since early autumn. Of the official explanation, issued by the Council, which was, essentially, *winters happen*.

"The seal is failing," I said.

"The seal is failing," she agreed. "Because the lock was made of people, and people run out. Or—" and here she looked down at her own gloved hands, and I saw her arrive at the thought the way you watch someone step off a ledge, "—or because someone opened a door in it. Twelve years ago. In a storm. With a prayer she didn't know was a prayer."

"Kestrel."

"Aldous told me once that guilt is the loudest prayer there is," she said. "I thought he was being poetic. He was being *technical*." She pulled off one glove, and there it was — the sigil, faint silver lines across her palm and wrist, brighter than I'd ever seen them, pulsing gently in time with the blue light of the walls. In time, I realized, with the breathing of the frozen sacrifices. "Every answered prayer in Vellharrow. Every miracle. That's not the gods waking up because they're strong. That's the lock rattling because I'm standing on it. My sigil isn't just a key, Maerin. It's a *crack*. And every prayer that slips through widens it."

The fire behind us popped. Somewhere in the ice, distantly, something exhaled.

"So we walk back," I said. "We find another way. There's always another way; I've spent eleven years learning that warrants can be lost, records can burn, orders can be misfiled—"

"Maerin." She turned to face me fully, and the light from the walls made her eyes look like they belonged to one of the frozen figures. "There's a war-god under this ice who eats despair for breakfast, and a Court full of thorn-masked fanatics who want to pour me down his throat at midwinter. There are nine days until midwinter. What exactly is your other way?"

I didn't have one. I am a dutiful man; I am very good at following procedures that exist. When procedures do not exist, I tend to stand there looking like a man who has been handed a form written in a language that hasn't been invented yet.

Which is why I was almost relieved when the shrine decided to provide us with a procedure.

* * *

It began with the cold changing. Not getting worse — getting *attentive*. The temperature dropped, but it dropped with intention, the way a room drops when someone important enters it. The blue light in the walls pulsed faster. The breath of the frozen sacrifices quickened, one breath per minute becoming two, becoming ten, and the sound of it filled the chamber like a tide coming in.

Kestrel grabbed my arm. "Get behind the altar."

"What is it?"

"I don't know. Get behind the *altar*, Maerin."

I got behind the altar. She planted herself in front of it, arms slightly out, the way she'd stood between me and the thorn-masked riders at Thornmarch, and I remember thinking, with the specific clarity of imminent disaster, that this woman made a habit of putting her body between me and things that wanted to kill us both, and that I had never once managed to return the favor properly.

The ice at the far end of the chamber — the wall opposite the entrance, the deepest wall — began to clear. Not melt. *Clear*, frost receding like a curtain drawn back, revealing a shape beneath.

A face.

It was vast — forty feet of it, maybe more — and it was beautiful in the way avalanches are beautiful, all symmetry and total indifference. Eyes like frozen lakes opened beneath the clearing ice. They were not cruel. That was the worst part. They were not cruel or kind. They were the eyes of something that had been asleep for a thousand years inside a lock made of dying people, and had just felt a draft.

Its voice, when it came, did not use the air. It used the ice, the stone, the bones of my skull.

***LITTLE CRACK.***

Beside me, Kestrel went rigid. "I'm not—"

***YOU OPENED THE DOOR. YOU ARE THE DOOR.***

"I didn't mean to—"

***NO ONE EVER MEANS TO.*** Something that might have been amusement moved through the glacier, a shudder of blue light. ***THE ONES WHO BUILT MY CAGE DID NOT MEAN TO DIE IN IT EITHER. THEY MEANT TO SAVE THE WORLD. IT IS ALWAYS SALVATION. IT IS ALWAYS SOMEONE ELSE'S BODY.***

"What are you?" I demanded, from behind the altar, feeling extremely heroic.

The lake-eyes slid toward me. I have faced down armed smugglers, rogue storm-callers, and one memorable bear, and I have never felt as thoroughly *catalogued* as I did in that moment.

***A WARDEN. HOW DELIGHTFUL. YOUR ORDER WAS FOUNDED BY MY JAILERS, LITTLE MAN. EVERY LAW AGAINST MAGIC, EVERY PURGE, EVERY BAN — ALL OF IT DESIGNED TO KEEP MORTALS FROM PRAYING TOO LOUDLY AND RATTLING MY CHAINS. YOU HAVE BEEN GUARDING MY PRISON YOUR WHOLE LIFE AND CALLING IT PEACEKEEPING.***

I thought of the Warden oath. *To keep the silence.* I had always assumed the silence meant safety. Standing in that blue-lit tomb, listening to ten thousand hearts beat once per minute, I understood that the silence had never been peace at all. It had been a gag order.

"Why show yourself?" Kestrel asked. Her voice shook, but she kept it pointed at the god like a blade. "If you're waking up anyway, why talk? Why not just break out?"

***BECAUSE I CANNOT BREAK OUT. NOT YET. NOT ALONE.*** The eyes narrowed, and for the first time I heard something underneath the vastness — something smaller, and older, and horribly familiar. ***AND BECAUSE I REMEMBER WHAT IT IS TO BE USED.***

The god told us the rest, and I will set it down here as plainly as I can, because if anyone reads this afterward, they should know we were not deceived. We were offered a choice, and we saw the trap in it, and we took it anyway, because the alternative was worse.

The gods — all of them, the whole quarreling pantheon of the old days — had grown fat on worship and thin on wisdom, and their war had cracked the sky. So mortals had ended it the only way mortals end anything: they paid. They built the gate beneath the ice and fed it with generations of the willing, and they bound the gods behind it, and they founded the Wardens to enforce a world of quiet, and they called it the Silence, and they called it peace.

But locks made of sacrifice need tending. Need renewal. And somewhere along the centuries, the tending became ritual, the ritual became rote, the rote became myth, and the Thorn Court — originally the lock's keepers — decided that what the lock really needed was not maintenance but a *master*. A war-god crowned and wearing mortal flesh, to rule the long winter forever. Vessia's midwinter rite wasn't a restoration. It was a coup.

And the sigil-born — a living key, born once a century, whose power opens only through despair — well. You cannot crown a god without a key.

***SHE WILL USE YOUR GUILT TO TURN THE LOCK,*** the god said to Kestrel, and its voice was almost gentle, which frightened me more than the thunder had. ***AS THEY USED THE ONES IN THESE WALLS. AS THEY USED ME, WHEN I WAS YOUNG AND HUNGRY AND LET THEM POINT ME AT THEIR ENEMIES. I AM NOT OFFERING YOU SALVATION, LITTLE CRACK. I KNOW BETTER THAN TO DRESS UP A LEASH. I AM OFFERING YOU ARITHMETIC.***

"Say it plainly," Kestrel said.

***IF VESSIA POURS YOU THROUGH THE GATE AT MIDWINTER, THE WAR-GOD RISES CROWNED, AND THE WORLD FREEZES UNDER HIS QUIET FOREVER. IF THE GATE IS DESTROYED — UNMADE, NOT SEALED, FOR SEALING ONLY BREEDS ANOTHER COURT OF THORNS — THE GODS END. ALL OF US. THE WINTER BREAKS, BECAUSE THERE WILL BE NOTHING LEFT TO IMPRISON. AND THE KEY THAT DOES IT SPENDS ITSELF ENTIRELY.***

The chamber was very quiet. Ten thousand slow heartbeats. One fast one — mine.

"You're asking her to die," I said.

***I AM TELLING HER THE PRICE. ASKING IS VESSIA'S TRADE.*** The eyes returned to Kestrel. ***YOU HAVE NINE DAYS. THE GATE'S HEART IS BENEATH THE CITADEL AT THORNMARCH — WHERE THE FIRST STONE OF IT WAS QUARRIED. GO OR DON'T. BUT DECIDE SOON, LITTLE CRACK. THE ICE IS THINNING, AND I AM SO VERY TIRED, AND TIRED GODS MAKE POOR PATIENTS.***

The light sank. The breaths of the frozen slowed again, one per minute, ten thousand clocks winding down. The face faded back beneath the frost, and the last thing visible before the ice closed over it was one eye, watching us go with what I swear — and I know how this sounds — what I swear was pity.

* * *

We didn't speak for a long time after that. I rebuilt the fire. Kestrel sat with her bare palm open in her lap, staring at the sigil as though it were a stranger's handwriting.

Finally she said, "You should go back."

"No."

"Vessia wants me alive. If I turn myself in, the rest of you—"

"There is no 'the rest of you,' Kestrel. There's you, and me, and nine days, and a god who just told us the price of everything." I fed the fire another stick, mostly to give my hands something honest to do. "Besides. I burned my warrant outside Thornmarch. I'm not a warden anymore. I'm just a man with strong opinions about tyrants and terrible survival skills."

She looked at me then — really looked, the way she hadn't since Aldous died — and something in her face shifted, some load redistributed. "You could still walk away. The marshes. The coast. No one would follow you."

"Yes, they would," I said. "They'd send me. I'm very good at finding people. Ask anyone I've arrested." I paused. "Ask you."

A sound escaped her that was either a laugh or the beginning of one, strangled at birth. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist and said, "Nine days."

"Nine days."

"To get to Thornmarch, past the Court's riders, into a citadel full of fanatics, and unmake a god's prison with a power I barely control, using myself as the fuse." She flexed her bare hand, and the sigil pulsed silver, and far above us, through a hundred feet of ancient ice, I heard — faintly, patiently, terribly — the sound of something beginning to thaw.

"Well," Kestrel Underhill said, and reached for her gloves. "I've had worse plans. I just can't name one right now."

Outside the shrine, the blizzard had stopped. The sky over the icefield was clear and black and full of stars, and every single one of them, I could not help noticing, looked like a countdown.
