## 12. An Honest Lie

I have told a great many lies in my life, most of them small and merciful. *The fever will pass. The knot is only string. I don't know what you mean.* But the lie I told Maerin Vale was the largest and least merciful of all, and I told it while he slept.

Let me be honest about the dishonesty, at least. That's the trouble with being a liar with a conscience — you end up narrating your own crimes to no one, in the dark, like a penitent who's misplaced her god.

* * *

We had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, three days out from the glacier shrine, two days — by my reckoning and Maerin's grudging agreement — from the Thornmarch citadel where Vessia waited for midwinter with her thorn-masked choir and her ledger of my blood. The fire was small. Maerin had built it small on purpose; he built everything small and deliberate, as if the world might be persuaded to order itself if he just set the example.

He was sharpening his sword. He'd been sharpening it for an hour, which meant he wasn't really sharpening it at all. He was thinking, and his hands needed somewhere honest to be.

"You're doing it again," he said, without looking up.

"Doing what?"

"Planning something. You get a line between your brows. Like a knot someone tied too tight."

I touched the spot, betrayed by my own face. "Maybe I'm cold."

"Kestrel." He set the whetstone down. In the firelight his eyes were that steady grey-brown that had unnerved me since the gaol — the look of a man who reads warrants for a living and has never once been fooled by fine print. "Whatever it is, we do it together. That was the arrangement."

"That was *your* arrangement."

"It was ratified," he said, "by both parties. Under open sky. With witnesses." He nodded at the horse, which was asleep standing up, because even our horse had given up on dignity days ago.

I laughed — I actually laughed — and hated myself for how easy he made it, and how much easier that made what I was about to do.

"Maerin." I pulled off my glove. The sigil-mark on my palm had been darkening all day, a bruise-colored spiral, warm as a held coin. It pulsed when I thought about the gate. It pulsed when I thought about him. Lately those had become alarmingly similar sensations. "If the ritual needs me — my blood, my despair, whatever recipe they're following — then every mile we walk toward Thornmarch together is a mile closer to handing them both of us. They don't want you. They'll use you. And you'll let them, because you're constitutionally incapable of letting someone else bleed first."

"And you're constitutionally incapable of telling me the whole plan instead of the speech version," he said. "So. The actual plan?"

I looked at the fire. I looked at him. I lied.

"I'm going to sleep on it," I said. "And tomorrow we'll go over the ground, and I'll show you where the shrine drains into the meltwater, and we'll find the stupidest possible way in, because stupid is the one thing the Thorn Court won't expect from a warden."

It was a good lie. It had specifics. It had his own voice folded into it. He almost smiled, and something under my ribs tore along a seam I hadn't known was there.

"Sleep," he agreed. "I'll take first watch."

"No — take second. I want first." I wanted the watch because I wanted the leaving, and I wanted the leaving done while his breathing went slow and trusting, because apparently I was also a coward, and a cruel one, and there was no charm for sale that fixed either condition.

* * *

He fought sleep longer than I expected. Warden's habit, or something else — some animal part of him that had spent twelve years tracking storms and knew this one was going to change course. Twice his eyes opened and found mine across the fire, and twice I gave him the same look I'd given customers buying fever-charms for dying relatives: gentle, certain, false.

The third time, he didn't wake at all.

That was the worst part. Not the lying. The *trusting*. He had burned his warrant for me. He had committed treason with open eyes and a straight back and exactly one blanket, half of which he'd insisted I take. And I repaid it by dosing the last of our tea with crushed poppy-heads I'd bought in a market town a lifetime ago, back when my crimes were limited to selling luck to people who didn't need it.

I knelt beside him in the dark. His sword lay within reach of his hand even unconscious, which said more about his childhood than any confession could have. Frost had silvered his lashes. I brushed it away with my bare thumb — the marked hand, the sigil hand — and felt the power stir under my skin like a dog hearing its name, wanting to be useful, wanting to be *enormous*.

"Not tonight," I whispered to it. "Tonight I need to be nobody."

Because here is what I had worked out, alone, in the arithmetic of the small hours: Vessia did not want me dead. Dead sigils are worthless sigils. She wanted me delivered — willing if possible, broken if necessary, but *delivered*, and before midwinter, or the ritual window closed and her war-god slept another century. Which meant that if I walked into Thornmarch myself, alone, unforced, she would have no reason to keep hunting anyone. No reason to send riders down the white country after a warden who'd gone traitor. No reason to burn smugglers' villages or squeeze hedge-priests or do any of the quiet monstrous accounting the Court had been doing in my name since before I knew I had a name worth stealing.

They needed a sigil in despair. Fine. I would bring them a sigil in despair — and underneath the despair, hidden so deep even I had to squint to find it, a plan.

It was not a good plan. Let the record show. My plan consisted of: surrender, survive, smile, learn the ritual's shape, and sabotage it from inside the one place in the world I'd sworn never to be again — the center of someone else's design. Aldous had died holding a pass so I could run. I was going to honor him by walking straight back through it, which I suspect would have made him either furious or proud, and with Aldous those were frequently the same expression.

But the other reason — the true reason, the one I only admitted to the fire — was simpler and smaller and wore a warden's coat.

Every day Maerin rode with me, the Court's list of his crimes grew. Every village that sheltered us, every ally he'd gathered, every smuggler who'd winked at him across a fire — all of it was evidence now, kindling with his name written on it. I had already cost one man I loved his life to a storm I couldn't control. I would not stand in the wreckage of a second one and call it destiny.

So I took his cloak — his *cloak*, because I am sentimental trash even mid-betrayal — and folded it over him properly, tucking the edges the way you'd swaddle something breakable. I took nothing else. Not the flint, not the dried meat, not the horse. A thief leaves with goods; I left with the debt.

At the edge of the firelight I stopped. Old habit. You always check your work before you leave a stall, my mother used to say — count the charms, count the coins, count the lies.

"An honest lie," I told the dark. "That's all it is. The truest thing I know how to give you."

The dark, notably, did not answer. Gods were mostly gone from the world by then, and the ones remaining had terrible manners.

I walked north, toward Thornmarch, and I did not look back, because I knew — I *knew* — that if I looked back I would see him sitting up in the ashes, and I would turn around, and then everyone I loved would burn on schedule after all.

* * *

Maerin woke to cold ashes.

I know this because he told me later, in the flat voice he used for things that had cut too deep for anger. He woke the way he always woke — instantly, completely, a man surfacing from water with his boots already on — and the first thing he noticed was the silence. Not the ordinary silence of a winter camp, which ticks and settles and breathes. A *shaped* silence. The silence of a space where a person used to be.

The poppy tea told him the rest before the empty bedroll did. He held the cup, sniffed it, and sat very still for a long moment, and I am told — by him, much later, in a tone that dared me to laugh — that his first coherent thought was not *she's gone* but *she drugged me, the absolute —*

He never finished that sentence. Even in retrospect, even married-adjacent, even years on, he refused to finish it, on the grounds that finishing it would make it funny, and he was not ready for it to be funny. I respect that. Grief should get to keep its dignity however long it wants.

What he did next, he did fast. He read the camp the way he read everything — tracks, ash, absence. I'd left on foot, northward, alone, taking nothing. A woman traveling light and fast toward the one place in the world she should never go. He stood in the middle of our ruined little household of two and understood, with the particular clarity of the betrayed, exactly what I'd decided: that his life was a price I'd pay anything to avoid, including his trust.

"She thinks she's protecting me," he said aloud, to the horse, who flicked one ear. "She hasn't noticed she's protecting herself. From being loved badly. From needing anyone." He saddled up. "Well. She can be wrong in comfort, at least. I'm coming anyway."

And here is the cruelty of it — mine, I mean, though the morning deserves a share. I had planned my surrender down to the bootlace. What I had not planned, because I genuinely believed it impossible, was that a man whose entire identity was duty would commit treason a *second* time, faster than the first, with no warrant to burn and no institution left to defy. I had calculated everything except the obvious: that Maerin Vale did not follow orders, or warrants, or even me.

He followed storms. He always had. He'd simply been polite enough, all these years, to wait until the storm asked to be followed.

* * *

By full dark of the next day I could see Thornmarch against the sky — a black molar of rock with the citadel crouched on top, windows burning gold, smoke rising straight as plumb lines in the still air. Below it, the road climbed through stands of thorn-trees that had no business blooming in deep winter, and yet bloomed, white flowers opening in the frost like the world's most ominous welcome.

I stopped at the treeline and did the last of my preparations, which were few. I put my gloves back on to hide the sigil — no. I took them *off*. If I was surrendering, I would surrender honestly: here is the mark, here is the storm, here is everything you engineered, Lady Vessia, and I am walking into your hands with my eyes open, because the alternative is watching yours close over someone I love.

One more thing. I unpicked the luck-knot from my collar — the first knot I'd ever tied, ugly, lopsided, the one I'd kept through exile and gaol and marsh and mountain — and I tied it instead to a low branch of the nearest thorn-tree, where a certain stubborn man, riding north at a pace that would kill a lesser horse, could not possibly miss it.

Not a message. Messages can be ignored. A *knot*. He'd know what it meant. He'd spent enough nights watching my hands to know that I tie knots for two reasons only: to hold things together, and to leave a trail.

Hold things together. Leave a trail. Both, this time. An honest lie, and a promise tucked inside it like a blade in a sheath.

Then I walked out of the trees, onto the open road, raised my empty hands, and said to the masked riders who rose from the snow on either side of me like thorns given legs:

"Lady Vessia is expecting me. Tell her the sigil came willingly —"

And behind my teeth, where no one in the Thorn Court could hear it, where only a warden reading my trail north could ever find it, I added the rest:

"— and she'd best pray her ritual holds, because I've come to take it apart from the inside."

The masks turned toward me. Somewhere above, in the citadel, a bell began to ring midwinter's first hour.

I smiled the stall-keeper's smile, gentle and certain and false, and walked into the thorns.
