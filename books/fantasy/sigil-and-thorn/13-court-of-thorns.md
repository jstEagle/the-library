## 13. Court of Thorns

They took my boots at the gate.

I want it noted that of all indignities — the hood, the wrist-irons carved with silence-sigils, the three days in a rattling cart through country so white it looked like the world had given up — losing my boots was the one that nearly broke my composure. A woman can endure a great deal with warm feet. Without them she is simply cold meat waiting to be seasoned.

The Thornmarch citadel rose out of the snow like a fist that had forgotten what it was holding. Black stone, thorn-crowned spires, windows lit a sickly amber that I chose not to think about too closely, because everything else in my life that glowed amber had eventually tried to kill me.

"Kestrel Underhill," said the gate-warden, reading from a ledger as if I were a parcel. "Storm-born. Sigil-bearer. Surrendered voluntarily."

*Voluntarily.* There's a word that will follow me to my grave wearing a smug little hat. Yes, I surrendered voluntarily, in the sense that I slipped away from a sleeping man I loved and walked barefoot across frozen marshland so the people hunting me wouldn't hunt *him*. Voluntary like an avalanche is voluntary.

I'd rehearsed this on the road. Three nights in a shrine made of ice, staring at the ceiling while Maerin slept — no, don't start on Maerin yet, we'll get there and then I'll have to stop writing for a while — I had rehearsed the shape of myself they needed to see. The Court didn't want a fighter. They wanted exactly what twelve years of exile had supposedly manufactured: a guilty woman, hollowed out, grateful to be wanted by anyone at all. Guilt was their harvest. Aldous had told them — or rather, I had deduced from what Aldous told me before he died holding a mountain pass for strangers — that a sigil's power opens only through despair. They had spent over a decade seasoning me like winter ham.

So I gave them despair. The best performance of my life, and I've lied to wardens, smugglers, and one extremely persistent goat.

* * *

They led me down. Of course they did. Villainous orders never keep their prisoners in pleasant rooms with views; there's apparently a rule about it. The stair wound into the citadel's roots, past doors that breathed cold, past a corridor where the walls were veined with something black and branching that I pretended very hard was mineral deposits and not roots of the thorn itself.

The thorn was everywhere, once I let myself see it. In the ironwork. In the grain of the doors. In the frost patterns on the stone, which did not branch the way frost branches but grew the way a bramble grows — deliberately, hungrily, toward things.

The door at the bottom was white birch, which was somehow worse than black iron would have been. Someone had wanted this room to look gentle.

Lady Vessia sat behind a table so long it could have hosted a treaty signing. Or a last supper. She was smaller than I expected — I had built her, over the road, into something towering and antlered, a winter queen out of a cautionary tale. Instead there was a neat woman in dove-grey wool, silver hair pinned with what looked suspiciously like a thorn of living wood, hands folded, face arranged into an expression of patient kindness that I recognized immediately because I wore its twin every market day for charm-buyers who couldn't afford charms.

"Kestrel," she said warmly, as if we were old friends resuming a correspondence. "Thank you for coming."

"I was carried," I said, and then remembered my role, and let my shoulders curve inward, and dropped my eyes to the table's scarred surface. "I mean — thank you. For receiving me." My voice cracked on the right syllable. Twelve years of practice cracking voices.

Something flickered behind her eyes. Satisfaction, maybe. Or assessment. With women like Vessia those are the same expression.

"Sit, please." There was one chair, positioned before the long table like a penitent's stool. I sat. The stone was cold through my stocking feet, and I used that — let the shiver come honestly, let my breath fog, let myself look small. It wasn't hard. Half of it was even true.

"You've been through a great deal," she said. "Aldous Fenwick. I knew him, you know. Before."

My heart performed a complicated maneuver. "You knew him?" I let grief roughen it. This part wasn't acting; grief for Aldous was a well I could fall into without trying.

"He taught here once. Long ago. He had such hopes for you." She tilted her head. "Such terrible hopes, as it turned out. The storm at Stormhold — twelve years ago. You called it?"

"I called it." Flat. True. Let her taste the flatness; guilt should taste flat, like old coins. "It killed him. I've paid for it since."

"And now you've come to pay more."

Now. Careful now. A broken penitent doesn't ask questions. But a broken penitent *does* flinch when the bill is mentioned, and I flinched, and watched her note the flinch with the tenderness of a farmer noting a ewe's weight.

"I came because I'm tired," I said. Which was true, which was the dangerous thing about good lies — you build them out of true lumber. "Because whatever I am, whatever was done with me — the sigil, the gods waking — I can't carry it alone anymore. I did a terrible thing once by accident. I won't do another by refusing to help."

Silence. The candles along the table guttered though there was no draft, flames leaning toward me like they were listening.

Then Lady Vessia smiled, and it transformed her face entirely, and not in a comforting direction.

"There she is," she murmured. "There's the honest creature under all that market-stall humility. Do you know how many years we waited for this conversation? How much patience it took, letting you sell your little knots and fever-charms, letting you believe you were harmless?" She rose, and the wool dress moved wrong, hem swaying against the windless air. "You were never harmless, Kestrel. You were *ripening*."

* * *

She walked the length of the table as she talked, and I kept my eyes lowered and counted her footsteps and built my panic into a small, dense ball somewhere below my sternum where it couldn't show on my face.

Panic, incidentally, was the enemy. Not because fear would give me away — a broken woman has every right to be afraid — but because despair was the key they wanted turned, and terror feeds despair beautifully. Every minute in that room I had to feel afraid and refuse to *dwell* in it, hold the emotion like a hot coal in cupped hands, present but not consuming. I thought of Maerin's hand around mine in the glacier shrine, callused and too warm, absurdly ordinary, and I set that memory between myself and my fear like a brick wall.

"The gate lies beneath us," Vessia was saying. "It has always lain beneath the Thornmarch — why do you think we built here? Not sentiment. Geography. The gods were sealed by sacrifice, an age ago, by frightened mortals who mistook silence for peace." Her footsteps paused behind my chair. I felt her presence like weather pressure. "But seals are made of belief, and belief rots. The winter thins. The prayers leak through. And once a century, the seal itself takes flesh — a sigil, born walking, carrying the gate's own mark in the blood."

"You want my blood," I said quietly.

"I want what your blood does." She circled back into view, and knelt — actually knelt, bringing her face level with mine, close enough that I could see the grey of her eyes held flecks of black arranged in branching patterns. "At midwinter, when the veil is thinnest, your blood opened upon the gate will unmake the lock. And what comes through must wear a vessel. That vessel will be me. I have prepared for forty years, child. I am not a usurper. I am a *doorway*, properly fitted."

"And the god that comes through," I said, keeping my voice small, awed-broken, the voice of a woman being swept downstream. "What is it? What will it do?"

"What it was always meant to do before the frightened men locked it away." She said it with such serene certainty that I understood, with a lurch, that she wasn't lying to me. She believed every word. Fanatics make the most dangerous liars precisely because they aren't lying. "War, Kestrel. Purpose. An end to this long grey crawling age where mortals huddle behind wards and pretend the divine never existed. The world will be remade. And you — " she touched my chin, one finger, cold as a key — "you will be remembered as its mother. No more exile. No more guilt. Everything Aldous died believing about you, corrected."

And there it was — the hook baited with the exact worm that would catch me. Forgiveness by proxy. Meaning manufactured from my worst night. If I hadn't spent twelve years learning the weight of that particular bait, if I hadn't already been offered it by my own reflection a hundred 3 a.m.s running, I might have bitten.

Instead I let tears come — real ones, grief does that, grief is a generous collaborator — and whispered: "He died thinking I was a monster."

"He died thinking you were a weapon," Vessia corrected gently. "Which is kinder, in my experience. Weapons get cleaned and honored. Monsters get hunted." She stood, smoothed her wool skirt, and became again the tidy administrator of apocalypse. "You'll be kept comfortably until solstice. Six days. Eat, rest. Despair needs strength, and yours has been... traveling. We'll feed it back into you."

* * *

The cell they gave me was, insultingly, nice. A real bed, a brazier, a window slit showing a stripe of iron sky. Comfort as a form of husbandry. You fatten the calf.

I sat on the bed and did inventory, because inventory keeps panic in its cage.

Six days. Solstice rite beneath the citadel, at the gate. My blood, opened — cut, I supposed, or worse, wept out through genuine despair, which was why they'd bothered with the kindness and the history lessons and the Aldous-shaped hooks. Vessia as vessel. A war-god in a fanatic's skin, with a century of preparation behind her and an entire order of thorn-sworn faithful at her back.

Against that: me. Barefoot. One luck-knot braided into the fringe of my shawl, which the searchers had dismissed as sentimental rubbish, and which was neither luck nor rubbish but a signal worked in knot-code that Maerin Vale had spent three weeks on the road learning badly, mostly by arguing with me about whether the fourth crossing meant *danger* or *wait* or possibly *soup*.

If he'd found the shawl missing. If he'd read the knot. If he'd chosen treason with his whole stubborn duty-bound heart.

If, if, if. Hope is just anxiety wearing its Sunday clothes.

I lay back and stared at the ceiling and let the performance drop off my face for the first time in six hours. What remained underneath was tired and twenty-eight years old and so frightened that the frost on the window slit began, faintly, treacherously, to grow in patterns.

I stopped it. Clamped down. Because here was the arithmetic I hadn't shared with anyone, the one I'd figured out in the ice shrine while Maerin snored: they needed my blood *opened by despair*. But despair wasn't the only thing my blood could do. I was the gate's mark. Its lock. And a lock, Aldous had once told a classroom of storm-calling children, remembers every hand that has ever turned it.

If they were going to use me as a key, then for six days I intended to study the door.

* * *

On the second day, they sent a priest to prepare me — hedge-tongue prayers, thorn-blessings, gentle questions about my childhood designed, I was fairly sure, to locate and irrigate the wellsprings of my misery. I gave him a flood. My mother's indifference. The first time I called lightning and wet myself with fright. Aldous's face in the storm-light. I rationed truth and embellishment in equal measure, and by the end he was weeping softly into his sleeve, and I hated myself a little, and did it anyway, because every tear he reported upward was proof to Vessia that the fruit was ripening on schedule.

On the third day I heard, through the window slit, far off across the white country, a sound I had been listening for without admitting it.

Not a horn. Not a war-drum. A whistle — three notes, descending, deliberately tuneless, the kind of whistle a man makes when he's pretending to be casual and failing completely. Maerin Vale whistled like a man arresting someone even when he was just walking beside you. I had teased him about it for two hundred miles. I would have given both boots — both *boots* — to hear him argue about soup-knots one more time.

Three notes, descending. Then silence, wind, the creak of the citadel settling on its thorn-roots.

I pressed my forehead to the freezing stone of the window slit and laughed, silently, helplessly, tears freezing in the corners of my eyes, because he had actually come. The man who burned his warrant for me — I didn't know that yet, wouldn't know for years, the way he stood in his office and fed his whole lawful life into a brazier — had crossed the white country after a woman who left him nothing but a knot meaning *danger* or *wait* or possibly *soup*.

Four days to solstice. Four days to keep the mask on, eat their food, weep their tears, ripen convincingly — while somewhere below, a man who was terrible at knots and magnificent at showing up began, I could only pray, to be an idiot about walls.

Vessia wanted a doorway. Fine.

Doors, as any charm seller knows, open from both sides.
