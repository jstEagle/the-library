## 14. The Man Who Followed Storms

I have burned exactly one document in my career as a Warden of Vellharrow, and I want it noted — for whatever record survives the winter, if any record survives the winter — that I did it sober.

Mostly sober.

The warrant had my own signature countersigned beneath Captain Doss's seal, and Doss's seal beneath the Chancellor's, and the Chancellor's beneath a smaller mark I hadn't noticed until that night in my office, when I held the paper to the candle and read it for the hundredth time: a thorn coiled around a ring. The same mark that had been pressed into wax on the orders I'd carried out for eleven years. The same mark Kestrel Underhill had described to me through the bars of her cell, half-frozen, sleep-talking in a language older than the city walls, while frost bloomed up through stone like something climbing toward the light.

*They put you in this uniform,* she'd said once, not unkindly, which was somehow worse than if she'd spat it. *They just let you think you chose it.*

So. The warrant went into the brazier, and I watched twelve years of my life curl up like a moth's wing, and then I packed a bag, because treason is a great deal more manageable with dry socks.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about leaving an institution that raised you: it doesn't fight you. It simply stops existing behind you. I walked out of the Wardens' Hall at dawn with my badge left face-down on Doss's desk like a bad coin, and no one shouted, no one barred the gate, no one even looked up from their tea. Vellharrow has survived gods going silent, plagues, three fires, and one memorable year of carnivorous pigeons. It can survive losing me. That should have been comforting. Instead it felt like discovering your own funeral had already been scheduled and catered.

The trail was easy to follow, which frightened me more than a hard trail would have. Kestrel had slipped her cell during the Thorn Court's attack on the gaol — I'd broken her out myself, though "broke" is generous; mostly we ran — and she'd gone north into the white country without covering her tracks, because she wanted me to follow or she didn't care whether I did, and I genuinely could not decide which possibility hurt worse.

Her tracks led through the salt marshes, where the reeds stood black and brittle against the ice, and out onto the high moor where the snow lay undisturbed except by wind and by one set of boot prints walking with the particular stubborn stride of a woman who has decided the world owes her answers and intends to collect.

I followed. Obviously. A man who burns his warrant might as well commit fully to the bit.

* * *

I found my first ally in a smuggler's den under the drowned chapel at Graymire, and I want to be clear that I found him; he did not find me, whatever he tells people now.

Brannic Hale — smuggler, fence, occasional rescuer of orphaned lambs when the mood strikes him, which is rarer than you'd hope — had been running goods across the frozen marshes since before I earned my badge. We had crossed paths professionally perhaps forty times. He had escaped me perhaps thirty-eight of those. When I dropped through his ceiling hatch into the lamplight, he didn't even reach for a weapon. He just sighed the sigh of a man whose luck has finally, comprehensively run out.

"Warden Vale," he said. "Come to arrest me at last? I'll confess to everything. Even the pigeons."

"I'm not a Warden anymore."

That got his attention. Brannic Hale has the kind of face that does its thinking in public — eyebrows first, mouth trailing after — and I watched both features conduct a brief emergency meeting.

"You're either lying," he said, "or you've done something spectacularly stupid, and either way I'm buying."

"I need passage north. Fast. And I need you to not ask why."

"Everyone who says 'don't ask why' is about to ruin my life." He poured two cups of something that smelled like it could strip barnacles. "Drink. Then tell me why anyway, because I'm going to ask regardless and it'll save time."

So I told him. About the answered prayers. About the sigil burned into the shrine altar in Kestrel's own hand. About the Thorn Court's riders in their thorn-masks, and the gaol assault, and the records I'd found sealed behind the Chancellor's authority — records showing a trial tampered with, a sentence engineered, a girl exiled into precisely the shape of despair that some old ritual required. About the war-god sleeping under the ice, and midwinter coming like a door swinging open.

Brannic listened with his cup halfway to his mouth and never completed the journey.

"The Thorn Court," he said finally. "The Thorn Court is real. I thought they were what grandmothers invented to make children eat their supper."

"They're real. They killed a fisherman's family in Marsh End last week to leave a message. They're going to kill a great many more unless we stop them."

"And the storm-caller. The one you were ordered to arrest." His eyebrows performed again. "You broke her out."

"Yes."

"Because of duty?"

"Because it was right."

"Hm." He drained his cup at last and grimaced as though betrayed by it personally. "Wrong answer. If you'd said love, I'd have charged you double. Right gets people killed slower." He was already pulling charts from under a loose floorboard. "Graymire to the Stormhold road, four days if the ice holds. I know the crossings. And I know three other people who hate the Court enough to help, assuming they haven't been eaten."

"Why would you help?" I asked. "You don't know me. You especially don't know her."

He looked at me for a long moment, and the smuggler's mask slipped just far enough to show the man underneath, who was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"My sister prayed," he said. "When the fever took her boy, back before the gods went quiet. She prayed every night for a month. Nothing answered, and the boy died, and she stopped speaking for two years. Last week —" his jaw worked — "last week she told me she'd prayed again. Just to test it. Just once. And the candle lit itself." He shrugged, elaborately casual, fooling neither of us. "She laughed for the first time in twenty years. Whatever's waking up out there, Warden, it gave my sister back a piece of herself. Somebody's going to try to steal that. I'd rather it wasn't stolen."

* * *

The hedge-priest came next, and she was less negotiable than the smuggler, which surprised me, since I'd expected holiness to be softer than crime.

Sera Ondine — though she insisted her true name was known only to the roots — lived in a hollowed oak outside Thistlefen and had spent thirty years tending shrines the Wardens were legally obligated to demolish. I had demolished two of them personally. She recognized me on sight, which made our introduction bracing.

"You," she said, opening her door exactly wide enough to fit one word through it.

"Me."

"You pulled down the shrine at Cobble's Rest. Old Nan had tended that stone sixty years. She cried into her apron."

"I remember."

"She cursed you, you know. With the full rites. Your left knee was supposed to wither."

"It did ache for a season," I admitted. "I assumed it was the stairs."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth that in a younger woman might have become a smile. She opened the door another inch. "Why are you here, fallen Warden?"

I told her the truth, because lying to hedge-priests seemed unwise on general principle, and also because I was tired of carrying it any way but whole. When I finished, Sera Ondine looked past me at the grey sky for a long time, lips moving, taking counsel with roots or saints or possibly just the weather.

"The god-tongue singer," she said at last. "You'll need him. If the girl is truly the living sigil, she'll be hearing the old words in her sleep, and she won't understand them, and they'll frighten her into mistakes. Ilvane can translate. Ilvane can also be insufferable, so pace yourself."

"Where do I find him?"

"Wherever he was thrown out of last." She fetched a bundle of dried herbs and pressed it into my hands like a woman arming a soldier. "For frostbite. And listen — Warden. Maerin Vale." Her eyes were pale as birchwood and twice as steady. "You served the law that buried the gods' names. Now you serve the girl who dug them up. Don't pretend those are the same work. One of them requires you to kneel."

"To whom?"

"That," she said, closing the door, "is the question, isn't it."

* * *

Ilvane we found — as prophesied — freshly ejected, this time from a tavern in Coldharbor Row, on account of singing verses that the owner claimed "made the beer go sour and the customers remember things they'd paid to forget."

He was sitting in the snow with a lute and a look of profound philosophical resignation, as though exile were a weather he'd grown accustomed to. He was maybe fifty, wrapped in three coats of clashing colors, with silver rings on every finger and eyes the color of a thawing pond.

"You're the Warden," he said before I'd said anything. "Or formerly. You have the walk. Wardens walk like the ground owes them money." He squinted up at me. "You smell of burnt paper and bad decisions. Warrant?"

"Burned."

"Splendid. Then you're no one, and no one is my favorite sort of person." He patted the snow beside him. "Sit. Tell me about the sigil."

I sat. Snow soaked through my trousers. I was beginning to understand that alliance-building in the white country involved a certain amount of wet.

"How did you know I was—"

"Ondine sent a bird. Also, you're the third person this week asking after the storm-caller, and the only one not wearing a thorn mask, which narrows the field considerably." He tuned a string without looking at it. "Ask your question, former Warden. You've been holding it since the marshes."

"What is she becoming?"

Ilvane stopped tuning. For a moment all the performance drained out of him and left someone older, and sadder, and much more careful.

"The god-tongue has a word," he said quietly. "*Vess-hara.* It means 'the door that walks.' Once a century, sometimes longer, a person is born with the gate of the divine written into their blood — not a priest, not a vessel, a door. What comes through depends entirely on who's holding the handle." He looked at me. "Right now, everyone holding the handle is terrified or ambitious, and the girl herself hasn't touched it at all. Do you understand what that means?"

"That she's dangerous."

"That she's *alone.*" He said it like a diagnosis. "The Court wants to use her. The gods want to pour through her. The law wanted to bury her. And the one man following her across the ice—" he pointed the tuning peg at my chest "—hasn't yet decided whether he's chasing her or keeping her company. Decide soon, Vale. Doors don't wait."

* * *

We caught up to her on the fourth day, at the edge of the Ashvale flats, where the snow lay thin over black earth and the wind came off the glacier fields with a sound like slow breathing.

I saw her before she saw us — a small figure against all that white, hood up, walking with that same stubborn stride, and something in my chest did a thing I refuse to dignify with description. Eleven years I had watched her from across market squares and warrant ledgers, admiring the neatness of her charm knots, the steadiness of her hands, the way she spoke to frightened customers like they were the only commerce that mattered. Eleven years of telling myself it was professional observation.

Treason, at least, was honest.

She heard us at the last moment — of course she did; she'd probably heard us for a mile — and turned with her hand already raised, frost crackling faintly around her fingers, and then she saw who it was and the frost died and something else crossed her face instead, quick and complicated, gone before I could name it.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"You burned your warrant." It wasn't a question. Smoke-smell travels; apparently so does news of it.

"Thoroughly. There were witnesses. Brannic will tell anyone who stands still."

Behind me, Ilvane strummed a chord of greeting that was almost certainly sarcastic. Brannic waved cheerfully, the way men wave from the deck of a ship they've decided to scuttle. Sera Ondine had stayed behind at Thistlefen — "the roots need me, and besides, someone must pray for you lot, and none of you are capable" — but her herbs rode in my pack, and her curse-knee ached companionably in the cold.

Kestrel looked at the four of us — three, plus me — and I watched her do the arithmetic of it, the terrible arithmetic of a woman who had spent twelve years believing she was worth nothing except as fuel.

"Why?" she asked. Simply. As if the whole white country had narrowed down to that one letter.

"Because the trial was rigged," I said. "Because your mentor is alive and being used as bait. Because a woman named Vessia intends to open a war-god with your blood at midwinter. Because—" and here I faltered, because the honest answer was standing right there in my throat and had been for eleven years, and I was a dutiful man even in treason, and duty demanded I say it plainly. "Because I read your file a hundred times looking for the monster they described, and she wasn't in it. Only a girl who loved her teacher too much to let a storm take him without trying to stop it. I've been following that girl for years, Kestrel. I just didn't have the courage to admit it until I'd committed arson."

The wind moved. Somewhere under the ice, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the snow around her boots lifted a half-inch and hung there, glittering, undecided.

"You understand," she said quietly, "that I'm the most dangerous thing in this country. That everyone who follows me ends up used, or dead, or worse."

"I understand that Aldous held a pass alone so you could live," I said. "I understand that you walked into the Thorn Court's citadel planning to lie to tyrants rather than let them hunt me. I understand that you keep choosing other people's safety over your own and calling it guilt." I stepped closer, close enough to see that her eyes were red-rimmed and her jaw set like masonry. "I'm not following the storm, Kestrel. I'm following you. There's a difference, and I intend to spend whatever's left of winter proving it."

For a long moment she said nothing. The suspended snow settled. Then she snorted — actually snorted, inelegant and human and so thoroughly unlike the living sigil of prophecy that Ilvane made a small delighted noise behind me.

"You're insufferable," she said. "You know that."

"Frequently informed."

"The Stormhold's three days east. Aldous said—" her voice caught on the name, and she pushed through it, brave and rough. "Aldous said the gate opens at midwinter. Five days. We'll be cutting it close."

"Then we'd best start walking," said Brannic, consulting the sky with a smuggler's instinct for weather. "Storm coming. A real one, not—" he gestured at Kestrel apologetically "—no offense."

"Some taken," she said, and almost smiled.

And so we went east across the Ashvale flats, five traitors and a door-that-walks, our shadows long behind us in the failing light. I marched at the rear, as wardens habitually do, watching our backtrail — and it was there, in the last violet hour of dusk, that I saw them: riders on the southern horizon, six of them, seven, moving without hurry, without torches, without any need of light at all.

Thorn masks catch the dusk like oil catches flame.

They weren't chasing us. That was the worst part. They were pacing us, patient as winter itself, herding us east along the exact road we had already chosen — toward the Stormhold, toward the gate, toward midwinter.

Kestrel turned to see what I was looking at. The wind rose around her, unbidden, and far away under the ice, something ancient turned over and began, very softly, to laugh.

"They know where we're going," she said.

"Yes," I said, and drew my sword, because some habits survive even treason. "Which means we'd better arrive first."
