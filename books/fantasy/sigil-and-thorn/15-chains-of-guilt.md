## 15. Chains of Guilt

They took my boots first.

I want to say I understood the strategy immediately — that I catalogued it like a charm-seller appraising a rival's stall — but the truth is I spent a full minute staring at my own bare feet on the black stone floor and thinking, *of all the indignities*. Cold stone. Thorn Court dungeons apparently skimped on hearths the way Vellharrow's tax collectors skimped on mercy.

The boots were practical, of course. Everything Vessia did was practical. Bare feet couldn't run far. Bare feet reminded you that you were meat and weather, not a sigil, not a weapon, not a woman with a plan.

I had a plan. It was a terrible plan. It consisted of *smile, kneel, confess, lie through your teeth until midwinter, then ruin everything.* I'd rehearsed it in the cart for three days while thorn-masked riders watched me with the patient interest of men guarding a prize pig. Broken penitent. That was the role. I'd had twelve years of practice looking broken; guilt is an excellent costume once you've worn it long enough to forget it's a costume.

So when they brought me before Lady Vessia — not in a throne room, disappointingly, but in a round chamber walled in living hawthorn, branches woven so tight the torchlight couldn't escape — I gave her exactly what she wanted.

"I'm sorry," I said, and knelt on the cold stone, and let my shoulders curve the way Aldous's had curved after the storm, the way mine had curved every day since. "I never wanted any of this."

Vessia sat in a chair of blackthorn that looked grown rather than built, and studied me the way I studied suspicious dice.

"No," she said. "You didn't. That's what makes you useful."

She was smaller than I'd imagined. That unsettled me more than the thorns did. In my head she'd been a tower of a woman, all winter and iron, but the real Vessia was grey-eyed and neat, with ink-stained fingers and the mild face of someone who balanced ledgers. She looked like she should be selling me flour. Instead she was selling me a god.

"You've been cooperative," she went on. "You came willingly. You knelt prettily. And yet." She tilted her head. "Your sigil hasn't opened. Three days in my keeping, and the gate is still shut. Do you know why?"

"Because I don't know how to open it?" I offered.

"Because you're lying to yourself," she said pleasantly, "and the gate only opens through truth. Despair, specifically. Real despair. Not performance." She rose, and her shadow moved wrong across the hawthorn walls — too many branches, too many angles, as if the room itself leaned in to listen. "Guilt is a wall you've lived behind so long you've mistaken it for a house, Kestrel Underhill. I'm going to take the house down."

I should have been afraid. I was afraid. But some stubborn merchant's corner of my brain noted, with grim professional admiration: *she talks like a fortune-teller at a harvest fair.* All mystery and inevitability. The difference being that fortune-tellers' prophecies cost a copper and Vessia's were going to cost me everything.

Two attendants — silent, thorn-masked, their masks grown onto their faces by the look of them — took my arms. I didn't fight. Fighting was for later, or for never, depending on how the next hour went.

"Where are we going?" I asked.

"To remember," said Vessia. "That's all this is. Memory. Nothing I do will touch you. You'll simply be asked to look at what you've spent twelve years refusing to look at."

*Nothing I do will touch you.* Every liar's best line. I'd used it myself, selling fever-charms: *the charm does nothing, madam, it merely reminds your body of what it already knows how to do.*

* * *

The chamber they took me to was round, windowless, and floored with a single slab of pale stone — and carved into that slab, deep enough to have been cut by something patient and enormous, was a sigil I knew better than my own handwriting.

Mine. My mark. The spiral-and-fork that had burned itself into the shrine altar in Vellharrow the night a child's prayer answered itself, except here it was the size of a millwheel, and here it was *waiting*, its channels empty, dry as old blood.

"Stand in the center," Vessia said.

I stood in the center. Because that was the plan, and because my feet had decided obedience was safer than courage, and because — I'll admit it — some part of me wanted to see it. Twelve years of pretending the storm hadn't happened, and underneath the pretending, always, the horrible curiosity: *what am I?*

The thorn-masks withdrew. Vessia remained at the edge of the sigil, and in her hands she held a small thing that caught the light: a ring of braided rowan wood, cracked down one side.

Aldous's luck-knot. The one he wore on his wrist the day I killed him.

My breath went out of me like a door kicked open.

"Where did you get that?"

"He carried it into the storm," Vessia said softly. "It came back to us afterward. Things touched by a sigil's making tend to... return. Like water finding low ground." She turned the broken knot over in her fingers. "Now. Let's begin. Tell me about the day Aldous died."

"I've told it a hundred times. To wardens, to judges, to myself—"

"Not once honestly." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; nothing about her rose, everything *descended*, gentle as snowfall. "You told it as a confession. A confession is a story with the truth cut out. So. Again. From the beginning. And this time, Kestrel, you'll be inside it."

She set the luck-knot in the outermost channel of the sigil.

The world folded.

* * *

I was twenty-three again, and it was raining on Stormhold's terraces, and Aldous was laughing at me.

"You call that a wind-catch? My grandmother could out-gesture you, and she's been dead thirty years."

"It's a *warm-up*," I said, grinning, rain in my teeth. "Everyone needs a warm-up."

"Everyone needs humility, is what everyone needs." He flicked his fingers and the rain bent around him in a courteous arc, because Aldous never got wet if he could help it; he said weather ought to treat its elders with respect. He was sixty-one and broad as a doorway and his beard had gone the color of the granite cliffs, and he was the closest thing to a father the Stormhold had ever given me, which is to say he was sharp-tempered, generous-handed, and impossible to impress.

I knew what came next. I knew it the way you know a stair has a missing step — the knowledge arrived a half-second too late to matter. Because this wasn't memory anymore. This was the chamber, and Vessia's voice threading through it like wire:

*You're angry with him. Why are you angry with him?*

"I'm not—" 

*Look again.*

And I looked, and there it was, the thing I had sanded off the memory for twelve years: I wasn't just grinning. I was *hungry*. I'd been passed over for the rite of storm-binding that spring — too young, too wild, Aldous himself had argued against me before the Circle — and I'd come out onto that terrace intending to show him. Intending to make him eat his caution. The warm-up was a challenge thrown down. The laughter had a blade in it.

*You wanted him to watch,* Vessia murmured. *You wanted him to admit he'd been wrong about you.*

"Yes," I said, and hated how easily it came out, how the chamber peeled the lie off like wet paper. "Yes. All right. Yes."

The scene shifted. The rain thickened. Somewhere beyond the cliffs the pressure dropped, and every storm-caller alive felt it — a great slow turning of something vast and half-asleep out past the edge of the sky. An honest storm. A once-in-a-generation storm. The kind you're supposed to report to the Circle so the wardens can anchor it.

Aldous turned toward the tower to send word.

And I — gods help me, I remember the exact shape of the thought, small and bright and rotten as a cherry pit — I thought, *if I anchor it myself, they'll have to give me the binding rite.*

I called it down.

*There it is,* Vessia whispered, tender as a knife. *Say the rest.*

The rest was this: the storm was bigger than anything I'd ever touched, and my anchors were pride instead of skill, and for ninety seconds I held the most beautiful thing I have ever held — lightning walking on the sea like a god testing its legs — and then it noticed me noticing it, and it *leaned*, and everything after that was noise. I remember Aldous shouting. I remember throwing the storm's weight off the terraces where the younger students slept, because whatever else I was, I was not that — I remember *choosing*, in the white heart of it, to take the strike path through the east cliff rather than the dormitories. I remember Aldous following the storm to pull it off me, because that was who he was, he'd have pulled a storm off a stranger. I remember the last thing he said.

Not, as the official record had it, *Kestrel, release it.*

What he actually said, in the instant before the lightning found him wearing my face as its road home, was: *"Proud girl — let go — I've got you—"*

He thought he was saving me. Right up until the end, he thought I was the one in danger, and he died reaching for my hand.

The chamber let me stay in that moment. That was Vessia's craft, I understood dimly, somewhere above the drowning: other torturers use pain, but pain ends, pain burns out, the body simply refuses. She used the moment itself. She let me stand on that terrace forever, rain in my teeth, watching Aldous's hand close on air where mine should have been, and she whispered, *you could have taken it. You could have reached back. You chose the storm.*

"I was nineteen feet away and the air was solid lightning," I snarled, or sobbed, or both. "Nobody could have—"

*Nobody tried,* she said. *You didn't even try. You watched him die and part of you — the part that wanted the binding rite — was relieved. No witness. No one left who knew how badly you'd erred.*

"That's not true."

*Isn't it? What did you feel when the storm ended?*

Silence. Because I knew. I had always known. When the wind died and the rain ran red off the terraces and they pulled me out from under a fallen arch, the first whole feeling that formed in me, before grief, before horror, was a terrible lightness. *No one saw.* And then, a heartbeat later, the guilt had arrived like an avalanche to bury that lightness, and I had lived under the avalanche for twelve years, and I had called the burial penance, and I had called the penance virtue, and I had built a little shop and sold little charms and told myself the story of the reckless girl who made one mistake—

One mistake. As if pride were a mistake. As if hunger were weather.

The sigil beneath my bare feet began to drink.

I felt it happen. Felt the despair go out of me like heat out of an open door, drawn down into those carved channels, and each channel that filled lit faintly, a slow wheel of blue-white light turning around me, and with every spoke that lit I felt *bigger*. Not stronger — bigger. As if the walls of Kestrel Underhill were thinning and something behind them was pressing its face against the glass. The gate. Their gate. The war-god's door, and I was the key, and despair was the turning of the lock.

*Almost,* Vessia breathed, and for the first time her ledger-keeper calm cracked enough to show the furnace under it. *Almost. One more. Give me the last of it, Kestrel. Give me the moment you understood no one would ever love you—*

And the chamber, obligingly, offered up a memory for that too.

Maerin Vale, standing outside my cell in the Vellharrow gaol, lamplight on his tired face, telling me he'd read my trial records twice and neither version matched. Maerin breaking me out of a burning gaol because his duty said surrender me and his hands said otherwise. Maerin in the salt marshes, soaked to the ribs, saying *I'm not doing this because I pity you* and then, two weeks later, on the ash road, catching my hand when I slipped on scree — and not letting go. Walking the whole afternoon like that, both of us staring rigidly ahead, two people holding hands the way soldiers hold a rope over a drop, except there was no drop, there was just a road, and neither of us mentioned it, and at dusk when we finally had to let go to make camp my hand stayed warm until midnight.

*No one will ever love you,* Vessia said. *Look at what you are. Look at what you've done. Even your warden—*

"He burned his warrant for me," I said.

The chamber stuttered.

"He tracked me across the white country," I went on, and my voice came out rough and steady, both at once, the way voices do when they've stopped caring how they sound. "He gathered smugglers and hedge-priests and a god-tongue singer who thinks I'm a bad influence, and she's right. He's coming here. He might already be dead trying. And you think the memory I'll trade for your god is Aldous dying?" I laughed — actually laughed, kneeling in a wheel of stolen light with my feet bleeding cold. "Lady, I've been guilty for twelve years. Guilt is my *native language*. You can't drown a woman who's been swimming since she was twenty-three."

The sigil flared, furious, hungry, one spoke short of full.

*Then hold it shut,* Vessia said, and now there was nothing pleasant in her at all, nothing mild, just the vast patience of a thing that had waited a hundred years and would wait a hundred more. *Hold it with your love, then. Hold it tonight, tomorrow, every night until midwinter. Love is heavy, Kestrel. It will break your back before it breaks my gate.*

* * *

They put me back in my cell. Boots returned, which I chose to interpret as a courtesy rather than a message, though with Vessia it was surely both.

I sat on the cot with my back to the wall and my hands flat on my knees, and I did the only work left to me. I held the gate shut. Not with strength — I had none, the sigil had drunk me down to the dregs — but with weight. With the specific gravity of one afternoon on an ash road, a hand in mine, warm past midnight.

Every time the light under the door pulsed — and it pulsed, slow and patient, a heartbeat in the stone — I pressed my thumb against my palm where his hand had been, and I counted. One afternoon. Two weeks of bickering over firewood. The way he said my name like it was a word in a language he was still learning. The way he looked at me in the glacier shrine, awed and appalled in equal measure, and didn't look away.

Love is heavy. She was right about that. I'd carry it gladly until my back broke.

Outside, far off, muffled by thorn and stone and winter, something began to burn — a sound like a city-sized bonfire, and under it, faint but unmistakable, a battle-horn blown ragged and defiant by someone who had absolutely no business blowing battle-horns.

I stood up. I smiled at the door, at the pulsing light, at the hundred-year patience on the other side of it.

"Hold on, warden," I told the dark. "I'm done being the key. Come midwinter, I'm going to be the lockpick."

The gate under the floor shuddered — and somewhere above, the horns came closer.
