## 16. Midwinter Eve

*Kestrel*

The Thornmarch citadel had been built by people who wanted you to feel small before they even asked your name. Black stone, spires like needles, windows narrow as knife-slits. It sat on its crag above the frozen river the way a spider sits in the middle of a web — patient, ornamental, and absolutely certain of what was caught in it.

I was the thing caught in it. I'd walked in through the front gate two nights ago with my hands bound in silk instead of iron, because Lady Vessia had a flair for theater, and now I stood in the great hall of the solstice rite wearing white robes that some well-meaning servant had probably spent hours pressing, and I thought: *if I die tonight, at least I'll die dressed for my own funeral.*

The hall was carved from the mountain itself, older than the citadel around it, older than Vellharrow, older probably than the concept of doors. The gate stood at its far end — not a door, nothing so kind. A seam in the world, tall as three men and thin as a wound, veined with light the color of deep ice. The old gods' prison. My blood was supposed to open it.

Around the seam, the Court had arranged everything with the loving precision of people who had never once been interrupted in their lives. Candles in their hundreds. Choirs of hooded singers whose voices made my teeth ache. Thorn-masked riders lining the walls like furniture that could kill you. And at the center, on a dais of black glass, Lady Vessia in silver, radiant as frostbite, watching me with the fond attention of a gardener regarding a particularly promising weed.

"Kneel," she said, when the choir finished.

I knelt. Not because I wanted to — because kneeling put my hands near the floor, and the floor near the ritual circle, and the ritual circle was where I needed to be. Three days of playing the broken penitent. Three days of flinching at shadows and weeping on cue and letting them believe despair had done its work. Vessia thought she'd hollowed me out.

She hadn't. She'd just given me three days to memorize the shape of the ceremony.

"The sigil opens through sorrow," Vessia said, lifting a ceremonial knife that looked expensive enough to have opinions. "Through guilt made flesh. Through the moment a soul understands exactly what it is." Her voice carried beautifully; I'll give her that. "Tonight, midwinter, when the veil is thinnest, we wake what sleeps. Tonight the silence ends."

*Yes,* I thought. *That's rather the problem.*

Because here's what nobody in this room knew except me, and one smuggler named Brannic who was at this very moment crouched in a drainage culvert half a mile away with forty other lunatics, waiting for a signal: a living sigil doesn't only *open*. It can also *turn*. Aldous had told me that much, in the Hollow Tower, in the last lucid hour he had. *The gate answers the hand that bleeds on it, girl. Not hers. Yours.*

They were about to use my blood to crown their war-god.

I intended to use theirs to break the lock.

Vessia took my wrist. Her fingers were cold as the glacier shrine where Maerin and I had learned the truth — that the gods were prisoners, that the winter was a failing seal, that every answered prayer since autumn had been a crack widening. She drew the knife across my palm, and my blood fell onto the black glass, and the whole hall leaned in like an audience at an execution.

The gate shivered. Light crawled along its seam. Somewhere beneath the mountain, something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to smile.

And that was when the roof came off.

Not literally. Though honestly, given what happened next, I think the roof got off lightly.

* * *

*Maerin*

I had burned my warrant eleven days ago, and I still caught myself reaching for it the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore. Old habits. Twelve years of being Warden Vale, of believing the ban was mercy, of telling myself that a city without magic was a city without monsters. Then I'd read the sealed records. Then I'd learned what my own order had done to a frightened nineteen-year-old girl to season her like meat.

Treason, it turns out, sits easier than hypocrisy. That's not something they teach at the Academy.

"Forty seconds," Brannic said beside me, pressed flat against the frozen ditch below the citadel's service road. Around us, our army of the improbable — smugglers, hedge-priests, Sera Vane the god-tongue singer who hummed to herself like a woman remembering a song from before the world — waited in the dark with more courage than equipment. Half of them had sharpened farming tools. One man had brought a boat hook. To a siege. I'd decided not to ask.

"You're certain the wards will fall?" I said.

"I'm certain the hedge-priest swore they would," Brannic said, "and I'm certain he's either a genius or a liar, and either way we'll know in forty seconds."

Above us, the solstice rite was beginning. We couldn't see it, but we could feel it — a pressure in the ears, a wrongness in the cold, the particular silence that comes before something ancient wakes up. Every prayer answered since autumn had been leading here. Every miracle, every drowned fisherman walking home, every child's wish granted by wind — all of it cracks in a dam, and tonight the Thorn Court meant to knock the whole thing down and call the flood a coronation.

Kestrel was in there.

I kept trying not to think that, and failing, the way you keep trying not to look at a wound. She'd slipped away from our fire nine days ago with nothing but an honest lie and a kiss on my forehead like a benediction, and I had woken to cold ashes and understood two things at once: that she loved me, and that she loved me in the most infuriating way possible, which was by deciding my life mattered more than her opinion of my feelings.

So here I was. Committing treason against the institution that raised me, in the company of a boat hook, to interrupt a divine coronation, because a charm seller with storm-scars under her gloves had decided to sacrifice herself alone rather than let anyone else pay her debts.

Thirty seconds.

"Hedge-priest," I called down the line. "Whatever you're doing. Do it."

Old Tamlin — seventy if he was a day, knees like rusted hinges — pressed both palms to the frozen ground and began to recite. Not the sanctioned liturgies. The *old* words, the ones the ban had outlawed for a century, the ones Sera Vane had spent three nights teaching him. The snow around his hands went grey, then clear, then simply *stopped being snow*, melting into black earth in a spreading circle, and above us the citadel's ward-lights flickered like candles in a draft.

Twenty seconds.

The wards didn't fall so much as *blink* — confused, briefly, by prayers arriving in a dead language from underneath their own foundations. And in that blink, Brannic stood up, bellowed something about owing the Thorn Court money, and led forty improbable people up the service road toward a postern gate that a bribed kitchen-hand had promised to leave unbarred.

It was barred. Of course it was barred. But one of the smugglers had a ram, and faith, it turns out, is mostly stubbornness with better posture, and thirty seconds after that we were inside the belly of the spider, running toward the great hall while alarms shrieked overhead and thorn-masked riders poured out of barracks like wasps from a kicked nest.

"Fen!" I shouted. "Priests — take the choir gallery! Brannic, the riders, hold the west arch! Nobody lets them restart whatever's happening in that hall!"

"And you?" Brannic grinned through the smoke of the first torch he'd thrown.

"Me," I said, drawing steel, "am going to interrupt a ceremony."

* * *

*Kestrel*

Here is what I had learned about ceremonies in three days of pretending to be broken: they are rituals, and rituals are just habits with incense, and habits can be *broken* if you hit them hard enough in the right place.

The right place was the third verse.

The choir sang the waking-song in four movements — severance, summons, surrender, crowning. Everyone in the hall assumed the sequence was fixed, because it had been sung the same way for a thousand years. But I'd listened. Really listened, the way you listen when your life depends on the lyrics. And in the third movement, there was a rest — a held silence, eight heartbeats long, written into the music a millennium ago because the original singers had feared the gate too much to sing straight through it.

Eight heartbeats of silence, at the exact moment the gate stood most open and least commanded.

Vessia's knife had opened my palm. My blood was on the black glass. The gate was awake, straining, half-open, drinking the despair out of the air like wine. And when the choir reached the third movement and the whole hall held its breath —

I spoke into the silence.

Not the waking-word. The *other* word. The one Aldous had taught me at nineteen, back when I thought it was just an exercise, a storm-caller's discipline: the word that takes power already moving and asks it a question. *Whose?*

Storm-light doesn't answer questions politely. Storm-light answers them *catastrophically*.

The gate screamed. There's no better word for it — a sound like a glacier calving inside a cathedral, a sound I felt in my sternum and my teeth and somewhere behind my eyes. Light the color of deep ice poured along the seam and then *outward*, off the script, wild, and the black glass dais cracked from end to end, and every candle in the hall went sideways in a wind that had no business existing indoors.

The choir scattered. The thorn-masks turned toward me, and I saw, for the first time, uncertainty in the set of their shoulders. They had rehearsed a coronation. Nobody had rehearsed *this*.

"SEIZE HER," Vessia roared, and her composure — that beautiful, terrible frost — cracked for just a moment, and underneath it I saw something that almost made me laugh: fear. Genuine, human, ordinary fear. She'd planned for a penitent. She'd planned for despair. She had not planned for a woman who'd spent twelve years selling luck-knots to children and had gotten very, very good at small, precise work.

Riders came at me across the shattered dais. I had no weapon. I had a bleeding hand and a stolen education and the gate of the gods howling at my back like a struck bell.

I raised my ruined palm, and the storm in the seam looked at me — I don't know how else to say it — looked at me the way a horse looks at the person holding the reins, testing, asking *really?*

"Really," I said.

Then the west doors exploded inward, and Maerin Vale walked into my apocalypse with a sword and a face like a man arriving late to his own wedding, and I have never in my life been so relieved to see someone breaking approximately forty laws.

* * *

*Maerin*

Later — much later, when I had time to be anything but terrified — I would try to describe the hall to people, and fail. Words are built for weather, not for the inside of a storm. There was light without source. Wind without sky. The gate at the far end had gone from a seam to a *presence*, vast and hungry and singing, and the whole chamber tilted around it like a ship in a swell.

And in the middle of it, standing on a cracked altar with her white robes whipping around her and her scarred hand raised like a torch, was Kestrel Underhill — exiled charm seller, confessed storm-killer, the woman who'd left me cold ashes and a lie to keep me safe — holding the wrath of imprisoned gods on a leash made of one word.

She saw me. Even across that chaos, even with riders converging and the ceiling shedding stone, she saw me, and her face did something complicated that I chose, in the moment, to interpret as *you absolute idiot, I love you.*

"Maerin!" she shouted over the roar. "The choir — the third verse — I've turned it, but I can't hold it and fight —"

"So don't fight," I said, putting myself between her and the nearest three riders. "Hold. I'll do the fighting. It's the one thing I'm actually qualified for."

The riders were good. I'll grant them that. They were also fighting a man with nothing left to lose and a recently discovered talent for insubordination, which is a worse combination than any sword technique ever devised. Behind me, Brannic's smugglers flooded the west arch; ahead, Fen and the hedge-priests had reached the gallery and were doing something to the choir's remnants that made the waking-song collapse into discord. The ceremony was dying. I could feel it dying, feel the gate's song going ragged, feel the whole elaborate machine of the Thorn Court's thousand-year plan grinding against a gear it never accounted for:

A charm seller who'd learned to listen.

But Vessia wasn't finished. I should have known. People like her are never finished; they're just between plans. I saw her on her dais, silver robes scorched, silver face set, and I saw her look at the half-turned gate, at the ritual half-spent, at the storm-light running wild and masterless —

And I saw her understand, before any of us did, that a half-finished rite is still a rite. That the gate didn't need a perfect ceremony. It needed *blood and intent*, and she had both, and she had never once in her life required permission.

"No," Kestrel screamed, seeing it too. "No — the vessel — she's going to —"

Vessia stepped off her dais into the light of the gate itself, spread her arms like a woman welcoming rain, and *drank*.

* * *

*Kestrel*

I felt it happen the way you feel a change in pressure before a squall — a dropping in the ears, a hush, a wrongness. The storm-light that I'd turned, that I'd been holding, that had finally begun to answer to *me* — it stopped answering. It found a new mouth.

Lady Vessia stood in the heart of the gate's radiance, arms wide, head tipped back, and the light poured into her the way water pours into a crack in stone — and she was the crack. I watched silver bleed out of her hair. I watched her shadow grow even as her body stayed perfectly still. I watched her mouth open in something that was either a scream or a hymn, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, exactly what we had done.

We hadn't stopped the coronation.

We'd knocked over the table at the wedding feast and left the crown lying on the floor — and she'd picked it up.

The hall went quiet in the way that only true catastrophe manages. Riders froze mid-strike. Brannic lowered his torch. Somewhere in the gallery, Sera Vane's humming stopped, and the silence she left behind was worse than any noise.

Vessia opened her eyes.

They weren't eyes anymore. They were weather.

And the last thing I registered, before the world tilted and the temperature fell and something vast began to unfold itself out of a woman-shaped container far too small for it, was Maerin's hand finding mine in the dark — bleeding palm to calloused palm, storm to steel — and his voice, very calm, very close:

"Kestrel. Tell me you have another word."

I didn't.

But I was about to need one.
