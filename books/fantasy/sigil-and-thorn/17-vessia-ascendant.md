## 17. Vessia Ascendant

I have been shot at, stabbed, cursed by hedge-witches, and once bitten by a customs official's badger, and I can report with some authority that none of it prepared me for watching a woman drink a god.

The hall of the Thornmarch citadel was coming apart around us — Kestrel's stolen storm-light still tearing ribbons out of the ceiling, banners burning where they hung, the great solstice mirror cracked down its center like a lie caught mid-telling. Somewhere behind me, smugglers were fighting wardens who had, until an hour ago, been their enemies' enemies, which in my experience is how most allegiances die. The exiled singer was screaming a word that made the stones weep frost. And at the heart of it all, on the dais beneath the half-ruined gate, Lady Vessia of the Thorn Court stood over the spilled ritual chalice with blood on her chin and an expression I can only describe as *appetite*.

"Kestrel," I said, because apparently I am the sort of man who gives warnings to people already standing in the fire.

She was on her knees at the base of the gate, one hand pressed flat against the stone, and she didn't look up. "I know," she said. "I know. Keep talking, though. Your voice is doing something useful."

"It is?"

"No idea what. But keep doing it."

Vessia lifted the chalice — or what remained of it, a crumpled thing of black silver, half its contents soaked into her sleeve, half still sloshing — and looked at us both with the mild patience of someone who has read the last page of every book in the library.

"You've made such a mess of my ceremony," she said. Her voice carried through the chaos without effort, the way a knife carries through bread. "Twelve years of cultivation. Twelve years of grief, watered daily, pruned carefully. Do you know how difficult it is to grow despair properly, Warden Vale? It wants exactly the right amount of hope. Too much and it flowers into defiance. Too little and it rots into numbness." She smiled at Kestrel. "You were my finest garden."

And then she drank the rest.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is all I have left of it, and because later — if there is a later — someone will ask me, and I refuse to be the witness who flinched.

The ritual had been meant for midwinter midnight, sung by a full court, fed by a century of prepared prayers. It got instead a broken rite, a shattered mirror, and perhaps a third of its measure. That is the only reason any of us survived.

Vessia went up like smoke going up a chimney in reverse. There was a sound — not loud, worse than loud, a sound like the world clearing its throat before saying something final — and the light in the hall changed color. Not brighter. *Deeper*. Every shadow grew roots. The frost on the windows turned black. And Vessia unfolded.

That is the only word. She unfolded, the way a map unfolds, except the map kept unfolding past the edges of itself, and what stood where a woman had stood was tall as the gate and wrong as a reflection that moves a half-second late. She had too many joints in her arms. Her face was still Vessia's face, mostly, the way a portrait is still a person, and her eyes were the eyes of something that had been locked under ice for so long it had forgotten whether it was prisoner or jailer.

"*Yes,*" she said, in three voices, none of them hers alone. "*Oh. Oh, this is what hands feel like.*"

The nearest thorn-masked rider knelt. Then the next. Worship spreads faster than fire; I have seen both, and fire at least has the decency to consume things evenly.

"Maerin." Kestrel's hand found my wrist. Her fingers were freezing. They were always freezing, and I had spent two weeks on the road pretending I hadn't noticed, the way you pretend not to notice a wound until it's the only thing you can see. "The gate. If I close it now — while it's open, while *she's* half-poured into it —"

"You'll lose your magic." I said it because we had agreed to say the true things out loud, Kestrel and I, ever since the marshes, ever since she told me about Aldous with rain running down her face like she couldn't tell it from tears and didn't try to. "All of it. Forever."

"Yes."

"And if you take it—"

"I become *that*." She nodded at Vessia, who was learning to walk, each step cracking the flagstones in a spreading web. "Or worse. Better at it. I'm the sigil, Maerin. The gate was built for me. Vessia's just — borrowing the door."

There are moments when a man understands his own life in a single glance backward. Mine came then: twelve years of warrants and purges and quiet admiration from across a market stall, all of it funneling toward this frozen hall, toward a woman on her knees asking me nothing at all, because she had already decided, and was only telling me so I wouldn't waste my last minutes arguing.

"No," I said.

"Maerin—"

"No. Listen to me. You don't get to do the noble thing alone anymore. That was chapter one. We're well past it." I drew my sword, which against a nascent god was roughly as useful as the badger, and stepped between her and Vessia anyway. "Close the gate. I'll buy you the time."

"That's not time you can buy. That's time you can *die* in."

"Well," I said. "I've been meaning to use my leave."

* * *

Here is what they don't put in the warden's manual: courage is mostly bookkeeping. You run the sums — what stands between the thing you love and the thing that will destroy it — and then you place yourself in the difference and stop thinking. Thinking is for people with margin.

Vessia came toward us the way winter comes toward a lamp-lit window: inevitably, and with a kind of distant curiosity about what all the warmth is for. The kneeling riders parted for her. The singer's word-song broke off into coughing. Behind me I heard Kestrel begin to speak in the old tongue, low and steady, the syllables landing like stones dropped down a well, and I understood that whatever she was doing, it would take every second I could give her.

So I gave.

I am not going to romanticize the fight, because there wasn't one. A fight implies two parties with comparable prospects. What there was, was a man with steel and training standing in front of a creature wearing a lady's face like a borrowed coat, and the creature found him mildly interesting for approximately nine seconds.

Her hand — the first one, the one that still had fingers in reasonable proportion — closed around my blade and snapped it. The second hand closed around me.

Pain, when it finally arrived, was almost a relief after the cold. Her grip went through my coat, through the mail I'd stolen from a dead warden outside the walls, through skin and muscle, and I felt ribs go the way green wood goes, splintering rather than breaking clean, and I heard myself make a sound I will deny to my grave. She lifted me off the floor. My boots dangled. Through the red haze I could see her studying me, head tilting, those deep-light eyes narrowing with something almost like recognition.

"*Warden,*" she said. "*You smell of duty. Duty is such a small cage. I lived in one for a thousand years.*" Her grip tightened, and the world went white at the edges. "*Shall I show you what's outside it?*"

"Pass," I wheezed.

And then — this is the part I will think about for the rest of my life, however long the rest of it runs — Kestrel stopped chanting.

I felt it more than saw it. The air changed pressure, the way it does before a summer storm, and every candle in the hall leaned sideways. Vessia turned her head, and in that half-second of distraction her grip loosened just enough, and I dropped — four feet, ten feet, I don't know, I landed on stone and the stone was kind enough to knock me unconscious for what the singer later swore was only a moment.

What I missed, I have reconstructed from witnesses and from the marks left on the world:

Kestrel Underhill stood up from the base of the gate with storm-light running off her like rain off oiled canvas, and she did not attack Vessia, and she did not run. She walked to me. She knelt in the wreckage of my ribs and put my head in her lap, and she said my name twice, and then she looked up at the thing that had been Lady Vessia and said, in a voice that was not raised and yet reached every corner of that vast cold hall:

"He stays alive. Whatever else happens tonight — he *stays alive*, or I swear by everything you're trying to steal from me, I will burn the gate down with all three of us inside it, and your god can starve in the dark another thousand years."

And Vessia — vast, wrong, half-divine Vessia — hesitated.

Because here is the thing about gods and near-gods and the mortals who feed them: they understand hunger perfectly, and hunger understands threat. Kestrel was the living sigil. The gate answered her blood before it answered anyone's rite. And in that moment, kneeling over a broken warden with frost blooming from her fingertips in patterns no Thorn Court scholar had ever recorded, she was not a penitent, not a weapon, not a garden grown for harvest.

She was the lock, and she was informing the key that she could swallow herself whole.

* * *

I woke properly to snow falling indoors.

It fell through the shattered roof in slow spirals, catching the storm-light, and it did not melt on the flagstones, and it did not feel cold — or perhaps I simply no longer had the means of comparison, being as I was a sack of cracked ribs and internal bleeding arranged loosely around a stubborn heartbeat. Kestrel was still holding me. Her gloves were gone. I had never seen her bare hands in twelve years of knowing her, not once, not even at the stall in high summer, and now the storm-scars stood out on her palms like lightning fossilized in bark, and she was looking at them as if meeting old acquaintances under unfavorable circumstances.

"The gate's open," she said quietly. "Really open. Vessia poured most of herself through and the rest of her is — I don't know. Standing very still. Listening." She swallowed. "Maerin, I can hear it. On the other side. All of them. The ones they imprisoned. They're not — they were never what the Court says they are."

"Kestrel." Blood made my voice thick. I said her name again until she looked at me. "Whatever you're about to do. Do it fast. I'm leaking."

"Don't joke."

"I never joke about logistics." I tried to sit up and my body filed a formal complaint, so I settled for gripping her wrist. Her pulse was hammering. Mine, I suspected, was filing for bankruptcy. "Listen. You asked me once why I burned my warrant. I told you it was because the Court lied. That was half of it. The other half—" The words cost more than the ribs did. "The other half is that I'd rather serve one stubborn charm-seller than a thousand clean orders. So whatever you choose in there — close it, open it, set it on fire and dance on the ashes — choose it as yourself. Not as their sigil. Not as your guilt. As the woman who shortchanges children on luck-knots because she thinks they don't notice."

"They notice."

"Of course they notice. That's why they come back."

Something moved through her face — grief and fury and terror and, underneath all of it, the thing I had been watching kindle since the salt marshes: a terrible, magnificent clarity. She pressed her forehead to mine, briefly, snow settling in her hair.

"If I close it forever," she whispered, "I lose everything I am. And if I take it, I lose everyone I love. And if I do nothing, Vessia finishes pouring and the world gets a war-god with a grudge and excellent posture." She laughed, wet and unsteady. "Twelve years ago I'd have picked the option that hurt me most and called it penance."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to go find out what's actually on the other side of that door." She laid me down with a gentleness that frightened me more than Vessia had, because it was the gentleness of someone setting down something they may not pick up again. "Watch him," she told the singer, who had limped over and was pressing something foul-smelling against my ribs. "If I'm not back by dawn—"

"Dawn's a long way off," the singer said. "Gods keep their own hours."

Kestrel stood. The storm-light bent toward her like wheat toward wind. At the foot of the gate she paused, one bare scarred hand rising toward the stone, and the last thing I saw before the light took her — before the hall filled with a whiteness that was not light at all but *attention*, the attention of everything that had ever been imprisoned behind the world — was her shoulders squaring the way they did before she told a customer an honest price.

Then the whiteness closed over her like water.

The snow kept falling indoors. Vessia's remnant stood motionless at the edge of it, three voices silent, waiting. My blood spread warm under my back, and I counted Kestrel's breaths in absent memory because I could no longer see her chest rise, and I thought, with the peculiar calm of a man who has run out of everything except conviction:

Come back. Come back, or I will follow you into that light on broken ribs and a dead warden's warrant, and I will be *insufferable* about it.

The gate began, very softly, to sing.
