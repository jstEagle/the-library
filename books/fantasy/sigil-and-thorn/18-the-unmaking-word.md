## 18. The Unmaking Word

The gate did not open so much as *admit* me, the way a wound admits a blade — reluctantly, and with the full knowledge that something was about to be lost.

One moment I was kneeling in the snow before Vessia's half-spent ritual, Maerin bleeding out ten feet away, his breath fogging in shallow silver puffs. The next, the storm-light swallowed everything, and I stood in a place that had no weather at all.

No. That wasn't true. I *was* the weather.

I looked down at my hands and they were made of cloud-shadow and lightning-veins, vast as river deltas, small as a girl's palms. Both at once. The sigil on my skin — the mark I'd carried since birth without knowing it, the brand the Thorn Court had spent twelve years seasoning into an instrument — blazed across every surface of this place like a map drawn in fire.

"Ah," I said, to no one. "So this is what I've been for."

My voice echoed strangely, off nothing, off everything. This was the inside of the gate. The divine realm. The place the old gods had been locked behind when mortal sacrifice sealed them away, back when the world was young enough to believe gods could be caged by arithmetic and blood.

I had expected a throne room. A battlefield. Something with columns.

Instead there was a long corridor of ice, and along its walls, pressed like flowers between pages, were the gods themselves.

* * *

They were not what the hymns promised.

I had grown up on Stormhold stories — Aldous's voice rumbling through the tower halls, telling me of the Thunderer whose laughter split mountains, of the Pale Mother who wove winter from her hair, of the war-god Vessia wanted to crown herself with, whose name had been eaten by time because even speaking it was a kind of surrender.

The things in the ice were none of those beings. Or rather, they were, but the way a drowning man is the same person he was on dry land — technically, tragically, unrecognizably.

The first one I came to was small. That was the horror of it. A god the size of a child, curled fetal against the ice, its storm-heart flickering like a guttering lamp. As I approached, it turned its head, and its face was every face I'd ever prayed to and none of them: my mother's, Aldous's, the fisherman who'd walked home drowned, the child at my stall whose whispered wish had started all of this.

"*You came,*" it said. Its voice was wind through a keyhole. "*The sigil. You came.*"

"I didn't mean to."

"*None of us meant anything,*" it said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked a glacier somewhere in the waking world; I felt it shiver up through my borrowed body. "*That is what they never understood. We did not fall. We did not rebel. We were* put *here. And the lock they used—*"

Its flickering eyes found the sigil blazing on my skin.

"*—was you.*"

"I'm not a lock," I said. "I'm a person. My name is Kestrel Underhill. I sold charms. Bad ones, mostly. I once cheated a widow out of three coppers for a fever-knot that did absolutely nothing, and I still feel bad about it."

The god blinked. For a moment, something almost human moved behind its face — surprise, maybe, or the ghost of amusement.

"*Yes,*" it said softly. "*That is why you are the lock. Not despite the coppers. Because of them.*"

* * *

I walked the corridor. There was nothing else to do, and besides, some part of me — the part that had spent twelve years selling luck to people who needed stories more than magic — understood that you don't rush past suffering just because it's inconvenient to look at.

There were hundreds of them. Thousands. Pressed into the ice like specimens, each one dimmer than the last. A sea-god whose beard had become actual kelp, crusted with barnacles that wept brine. A hearth-goddess whose flame had shrunk to an ember no bigger than a spark off a flint, cupped in hands gone translucent from disuse. A trickster whose mouth had been sewn shut with its own laughter — I could see the stitches moving, still trying to tell jokes to no one.

And at the far end, largest of all, wrapped in chains of frozen prayer: the war-god. The one Vessia wanted. It was awake. Of course it was awake. It had been awake for a thousand years, listening to mortals whisper its name in desperation, feeling every battle fought in its honor like a hand on the back of its neck.

It saw me coming and did not struggle. That frightened me more than struggling would have.

"*Sigil,*" it said. Its voice was deep enough to have foundations. "*You are here to open the door.*"

"No," I said. "I'm here to close it. Forever."

Something moved across its ruined face. It took me a moment to recognize it, because I had only ever seen it in mirrors, on the worst nights, after the nightmares: relief.

"*Oh,*" it said. "*Oh, thank the nothing that listens.*"

"You want to be unmade?"

"*I want to stop,*" it said, with the terrible simplicity of truth. "*Do you know what we are, little lock? We are not tyrants. We were never tyrants. We are* containers. *Your ancestors were dying of our attention — every prayer a hook, every sacrifice a leash, every temple a fist around our throats pulling us toward their hungers. They built us to hold their wars and their harvests and their griefs, and then they realized what they'd built, and they could not unbuild it. So they sealed us. And the seal required a soul shaped exactly like ours: a thing of enormous power and enormous guilt, born once a century, taught by suffering to keep the door shut from the inside.*"

"That's monstrous," I said.

"*Yes,*" it agreed. "*Mortals are monstrous. It is their finest quality. But consider, sigil: they did not make us monsters. They made us* mirrors. *Everything we ever did to them, they had already done to themselves, and to us, and asked us to bless it. We are the shape their cruelty took when they gave it a name and knelt to it.*"

I thought of Vellharrow. Of the magic ban, and the wardens, and Maerin burning his warrant in the snow. Of Lady Vessia, who had looked at a living lock and seen a key. Of myself, twelve years ago, kneeling in the ruins of the Stormhold with Aldous's blood on my hands and a storm screaming out of my chest, praying to gods I'd been told were dead — *please, please, take it back, undo it, I'll give anything* —

And the gods, half-dreaming in their prison, hearing the sigil's despair like a bell through fog, answering.

"Oh," I said. "It was me. At the beginning. My prayers opened the crack."

"*Your guilt rang the bell,*" the war-god said. "*Vessia's ambition pulled the rope. But yes. The door has been opening for twelve years, one answered prayer at a time. Every miracle in Vellharrow was a splinter working loose. And now you stand inside the gate, holding all your power, with the choice your ancestors designed you for.*"

"Which is?"

"*Open, and let us pour through. Close, and keep suffering. Or—*" and here its voice dropped, and all down the corridor, the other gods stirred, pressing closer to the ice, thousands of ruined faces turning toward me like flowers toward a sun that had finally remembered them, "*—or speak the word. The word that was written into the foundation of the world and then forgotten, because remembering it would have been mercy, and mercy is the one thing power cannot afford.*"

"What word?"

The war-god smiled. It was not a frightening smile. That was somehow the worst part.

"*You already know it. You have known it since you were nine years old, kneeling in the rain, wishing with everything in you that you had never been born with this. Say it, sigil. Say what you said to the storm.*"

* * *

I stood in the corridor of ice for a long time. Time works strangely in divine places; it may have been a heartbeat or a century. Somewhere outside, Maerin was bleeding, and Vessia was becoming something vast and wrong, and the winter was either breaking or swallowing the world, and none of that mattered here, and all of it mattered completely.

Because here was the thing. Here was the terrible, clarifying, awful thing:

If I closed the gate — if I did what I was made for, kept the lock shut, held the door with my guilt and my scars and my despair until I died of it — the gods stayed imprisoned. Suffering, but contained. The world went on. Vessia failed. Maerin lived, probably. And I spent whatever remained of my life as what everyone had always said I was: a weapon someone else had forged, pointed at someone else's enemy, forever.

If I opened it — if I let Vessia's ritual complete, if I gave the Court their war-god — the world ended in fire and silence, but it would be *my choice*, at least. My treason instead of my obedience.

Or.

Or I could say the word.

And the word was not a spell. That was the joke of it, the cruel cosmic joke that Aldous would have appreciated, that I would laugh at later, alone, in a market stall in Vellharrow, selling knots that did nothing: the word was simply the truest sentence I knew.

*I wish I had never been made for this.*

Not *I wish I had no power*. Not *I wish I were someone else*. Those were wishes of escape, and escape was just another form of imprisonment. No. The word — the unmaking word, the one written into the world's foundation and forgotten — was the wish that the whole architecture of purpose-built suffering had never existed. Mine. The gods'. The Thorn Court's. The entire ledger of blood that power had been keeping since the first mortal knelt to the first storm and asked it to be something other than weather.

To say it was to unmake the divine realm. All of it. Every god, every prison, every prayer ever answered or refused. And with it, the source of all magic — because magic, I understood suddenly, standing in that corridor, was never a human gift at all. It was leakage. It was the sound of gods breathing through the walls of their cell, and every storm-caller and hedge-witch and thorn-priest who ever lived had been drinking from the cracks.

Unmake the prisoners, and the walls come down. Unmake the walls, and the leaks stop.

Forever.

I thought about my hands. These vast, veined, terrible hands full of thunder. The only thing I had ever been proud of and the only thing I had ever hated, often in the same breath, usually in front of other people.

I thought about Maerin, who had followed me across the white country not because I was powerful but because I was *mine* — stubborn, wry, cowardly at the right moments, brave at the wrong ones. He had never once looked at me the way people look at power. He'd looked at me the way you look at a person you're arguing with and losing to and enjoying it.

He deserved a world where I was a woman, not a sigil.

And the gods — the gods deserved to stop.

"*Well?*" the war-god asked, gently, the way you ask someone you love whether they're ready for the surgeon. "*Lock? Sigil? Kestrel Underhill, seller of bad charms and worse poetry? What will you do?*"

I looked down the corridor one last time. Thousands of faces. The hearth-goddess's ember. The trickster's stitched mouth. The child-sized god who had spoken to me first, watching me with eyes like the last light of a very long day.

"You know," I said, "in Vellharrow they teach children that the gods abandoned the world. That's why there's no magic. That's why prayers go unanswered. Everyone believes they left."

"*We never left,*" the small god whispered. "*We were never anywhere. We were only ever the shape of your reaching.*"

"Then I'm going to do the kindest thing anyone has ever done for any of us," I said. "I'm going to stop reaching."

I closed my eyes. I found the memory — not the one Vessia had tortured me with, not Aldous's death, not the storm. The other one. The one I had clung to in the dark of the Thornmarch cells while despair tried to finish the sigil's work: Maerin's hand in mine, salt-crusted and cold, on the night we fled into the marshes. The way he hadn't let go. The way he still hadn't.

I took a breath I didn't need anymore.

And I spoke the unmaking word.

* * *

It sounded like this: "*Enough.*"

The corridor did not explode. Nothing so dramatic. The ice simply *exhaled* — a sigh ten thousand years held — and began, from the far end, to come apart. Not shattering. *Melting.* The gods did not scream. They *sagged*, the way a tired traveler sags into a chair, the way a held breath sags out of the chest, and their faces — every one of those thousands of ruined faces — turned toward me with expressions I will carry to my own grave, which will now be an ordinary grave, in an ordinary ground, with no divinity anywhere near it.

Gratitude. Terrible, bottomless, relieved gratitude.

The war-god went last. Of course it did. It stood in its dissolving chains and looked at me with something that was almost paternal, and it said, "*You were the best of us, little mirror. You were the only one who ever chose to be a person.*"

Then it was snow. Then the snow was light. Then the light was—

* * *

I woke on my back in the snow of the Thornmarch courtyard, and the first thing I noticed was the silence.

Not the silence of eternal winter. The silence of a room after a clock stops. The silence of something that had been humming underneath the world since the beginning of the world finally, mercifully, stopping.

I sat up. My hands were just hands. Chapped, frostbitten, ink-stained at the cuticles from years of cheap charm-work. Human hands. I stared at them and felt — I don't know how to describe it except as the sensation of setting down a weight so heavy you had forgotten you were carrying it, so that for a moment you can't stand upright, because standing was something you did *around* the weight.

The storm was over. The real storm, the one Vessia's ritual had torn open — gone, unraveled, its thread cut at the source. Above me the sky was doing something it hadn't done in twelve years: clearing. Actual stars, shy and astonished, peeking through the tearing clouds.

"Kestrel."

Maerin. Alive — I could have wept, and possibly did, though my face was so numb I couldn't tell — propped against a fallen pillar, one hand pressed to his side, his warrant-burned fingers shaking. He was looking at me the way people look at the site of a miracle or a disaster, which in my experience is the same look.

"It's done," I said. My voice came out rusted. "Whatever it was. It's done."

"What did you do?"

I considered the question. Considered lying, which was my native language, and decided — for once, at the end of everything — against it.

"I unmade the gods," I said. "All of them. And the gate. And—" I flexed my fingers, watched them shake, felt for the storm that had lived under my skin since birth and found only quiet, deep and clean and absolute, like a house after the last guest leaves. "And me. The version of me that was ever going to matter to history."

Maerin was quiet for a long moment. Snow drifted down through the starlight, fine and ordinary, the kind of snow that means nothing except that it's cold and it's winter and it will end.

"Good," he said finally.

I laughed. It hurt my ribs. I laughed anyway. "Good? I just committed deicide on a scale the hymns don't have words for, and you say *good*?"

"They were prisoners," Maerin said simply. "I read the records, Kestrel. Whatever they were — whatever they are now — they were suffering." He winced, shifted his grip on his wound. "Someone finally let them stop."

Somewhere across the courtyard, Vessia screamed — a raw, human, hollowed-out sound, the sound of a woman who had drunk half a god and been left holding the empty cup. Her own Court was converging on her, and I found I couldn't summon anything about it. Not pity, not satisfaction. Just a distant, exhausted recognition: she had reached for power, and power had turned out to be a door, and doors only lead somewhere if someone built a room behind them.

There were no rooms anymore. Just weather. Just the world, ordinary and sufficient, spinning on without supervision.

Maerin struggled upright. I went to him, put his arm over my shoulders — my weak, human, gloriously unremarkable shoulders — and together we started toward the gates of the Thornmarch citadel, where fires were burning low and survivors were stumbling out into the falling snow.

"So," Maerin said, as we walked. "What does a former living sigil do for work?"

"I hear charm-selling pays terribly," I said. "But the hours are good."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh, and then his knees buckled, and the last thing I registered before the exhaustion took us both was the snow falling soft and meaningless and beautiful on the ruins of the divine war — and, far to the south, past the marshes, past the walls of Vellharrow, the faintest smell of thaw.

Winter was ending. Not the season. The *sentence*.

And somewhere in the wreckage behind us, a woman named Vessia was learning what it cost to reach for a god and find only sky.
