## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Names

The rain came at the Gildergate like it had a grudge.

Coral Hartwell had kept the ledger desk for nine years, and in that time she'd learned the rain's moods the way other people learned their lovers'. This was its mean one—the kind that found every seam in the old stone, pooled under the door, and turned the gutter-lamps to smears of gold on wet black glass. She sat with her sleeves pushed back, her pen moving, her presence folded down small. That was the trick of gatekeepers: you didn't hide, exactly. You simply became part of the furniture of the place, so unremarkable that even the guild's own clerks walked past her without seeing her face twice.

Invisible. Reliable. Unwritten.

She was entering the night's trades into the ledger—three bottled griefs from a widow in Lowmarsh, a decade of borrowed years against a cooper's unborn son, one name sold outright by a man who wanted to forget he'd ever been called Aldous—when someone began hammering on the door.

Not knocking. Hammering. The kind of sound that meant *run* rather than *open*.

Coral's pen stopped. The guild's rules were simple and she knew them like her own pulse: the Gildergate opens for trade hours, dawn to dusk. No exceptions. No favors. And above all—she was never, ever to admit anyone after the bell rang, no matter what they said, no matter how they begged. The last keeper who broke that rule had left nothing behind but a smear of ink on page one and a vacancy Coral had been hired to fill.

The hammering stopped. Then started again, weaker.

"Please." A voice through three inches of oak and iron, cracked down to almost nothing. "Keeper. Please. I know you can hear me."

Coral looked at the ledger. Looked at the door. The candle flame leaned in some draft she couldn't feel.

"I'm sorry," she said, and her voice came out flat, professional, the voice of the desk and not the woman behind it. "Trade hours are ended."

"Trade—" A coughing fit, wet and terrible. "I'm not here to trade. I'm here to *return* something. That has to count. Returning always counts. You know it does."

She did know. It was carved over the arch inside: *What is given back is forgiven.* Old law, older than the guild itself, and even the magistrates beneath Mount Vael honored it, or so the stories said. Her hand was already on the bolt before she remembered she'd decided not to move.

That was the thing about invisibility. It worked fine until something made you visible to yourself.

She opened the door six inches. Rain blew in and hit the ledgers; she snatched the nearest one away with a clerk's reflexes before a single drop touched the vellum.

The man on the step was half-dead already. She could see it in the gray of his lips, the way his weight hung off the doorframe like laundry off a line. Courier's leathers, guild-mark burned out of the shoulder—burned out deliberately, gouged with a knife, which was its own kind of message. His left side was soaked darker than the rain could account for.

"You're the keeper," he said, as if confirming it to himself. "Good. Good. They said there'd be a keeper who sees things."

"Who said?"

He fell forward instead of answering. She caught him—caught his full weight, which surprised her, because she'd spent nine years convincing herself she wasn't strong enough to hold anything—and got the door shut behind them both, because leaving it open felt worse than breaking rules.

Inside, under the lamps, he was older than she'd thought, and younger than he should have been. He pressed something into her hands with the desperation of a man handing off a lit fuse: a map, folded small, stained across one corner in blood that was still warm.

"Vael," he whispered. "Beneath Vael. The court. My daughter—Wren—they took her soul, keeper. Sold it, bound it, put it in the fire-hold with the others. I carried this map nine days. Nine days running." His eyes found hers and held on with terrible focus. "Her name is Wren. Wren Halloway. Check your oldest book. Check it and you'll believe me."

"I'm not authorized to—"

"*Check it.*"

There are moments when refusing costs more than obeying. Coral set the map down, wiped her hands on her apron out of pure habit, and pulled the oldest ledger from the shelf—a tome so old its spine had gone soft as cloth, its pages the color of weak tea. The guild's first book. The one she'd been told to maintain but never, in nine years, to *read*.

She opened it near the front, where the entries were written in a hand more careful than any clerk used now, each trade recorded like a confession.

And there it was. Not Wren. Not yet.

*Coral Hartwell, age six. Traded by next of kin. Consideration received: two debts forgiven, one silence purchased. Bound beneath Mount Vael, fire-hold. Status: unredeemed.*

The letters swam. She read it again. And again. Her own name, in ink dried decades ago, in a book she had dusted every week of her working life without once opening past the ribbon.

"That's why they let you keep the gate," the courier murmured, sliding down the wall to sit on the cold flags. Almost gentle, now, the way people get when dying. "Gatekeepers. Did you never wonder why the guild only hires the traded? Children nobody paid enough to redeem? We're cheap, keeper. And we don't ask questions about doors we remember being carried through ourselves."

Coral stood very still. Somewhere in the building, water dripped. Nine years of her life rearranged themselves quietly around the fact of her own name, the way furniture rearranges around a hole in the floor.

"Wren," she said finally. "Show me where Wren is."

He smiled—or tried to. "You'll go, then. Knew you would. You've got the look." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a second thing, smaller: a scrap of veil-cloth, gray as ash, that seemed to drink the lamplight. "Find Brannoc. Sigil-smith. He owes the guild ink—he'll hate that, use it. He can work this. Tell him Tam sent you. Tell him I finally paid my debt."

His breath went ragged, then shallow, then stopped altogether with a sound too small for such a large quiet after.

Coral knelt beside him for a long moment. She did not cry. Gatekeepers weren't built for crying; they were built for standing between.

But when she rose, she did not reach for the ledger to record his death. Instead she picked up the map, and the veil-scrap, and her oldest, softest, most forbidden book, and she looked at the door she was sworn to keep shut.

It occurred to her, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had spent nine years believing herself invisible when all along she had simply been *hidden*—filed away, shelved, an entry awaiting redemption in a court beneath a mountain. The guild hadn't hired a keeper. They'd parked an asset at the door and called it a career.

Rain hammered the roof. The candle steadied.

Coral Hartwell took her cloak off its hook, wrote one line in the day-ledger in her own neat hand—*Gate closed. Keeper absent.*—and stepped out into the dark to find a disgraced sigil-smith who owed the guild a debt of ink.

Behind her, on the desk, the oldest ledger lay open to a page bearing her name.

She intended, before the end, to burn it.
