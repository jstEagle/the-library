## 2. What the Veil Chooses to Hide

Coral had buried the courier at dawn, in the strip of scrubland behind the Gildergate where the guild's failures went. No stone, no name. Just a shallow cut of earth and rain still coming down like it had a grudge.

The map stayed dry. She'd wrapped it in oilcloth and kept it against her ribs all night, and now it lay open on her worktable between the inkwells and the ledger she was no longer sure she could trust.

*Wren,* he'd said. *My daughter's name is Wren.*

And there it was, three lines from the bottom of the oldest page: *One girl-child, soul intact, sold to the Court Beneath Vael. Buyer's mark: the magistrate's own seal.* The seller's name was smudged—deliberately, she suspected—but the date was clear enough, and the date meant nothing to her except that she'd been alive when it happened. Keeping the gate. Writing down trades. Invisible.

She'd written this entry herself? No. Older hand. But someone had signed it off, and someone above that someone had approved it, and the whole chain led back through nine years of Coral Hartwell writing down other people's losses in a fair, tidy script and never once asking what happened next.

The knock came at midmorning. Three raps, then two more—the old sign for *debt collector*, though nobody had used it since before her time.

She opened the door on a big man with soot worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like shadow, and a satchel slung across his chest that clinked when he breathed wrong.

"You're the keeper," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the door."

"Brannoc Vey." He said his own name like he was spitting out something bitter. "Sigil-smith. Formerly. I'm told you have need of my trade, and I'm told I have a debt of ink the guild means to collect whether I pay it or they do." His eyes went past her shoulder to the map on the table. "So we're going to be honest with each other, you and I, or this doesn't work."

"Come in before someone sees you," Coral said, which wasn't honesty but was close enough to start.

* * *

Brannoc spread the veil across her table like a dead thing.

It was cloth, technically. Grey-green, fine-woven, older than either of them, and it drank the lamplight instead of returning it. When Coral looked directly at its center, her eyes slid off, found the edge of the table, moved on.

"It hides what it chooses," Brannoc said. "That's the flaw. The guild wove it to protect couriers, and couriers only ever needed to hide their cargo—a vial, a letter, a stolen year. It decides what's important and covers it." He tapped the fabric. "Under Mount Vael, everything down there can smell intent. We don't need it to hide our cargo. We need it to hide *us*. Both of us, wholly, all the way down."

"And you can rework it?"

"I can rework anything." Pride and shame in equal measure, braided tight. "I wove sigils into the guild's veils for eleven years. Then I wove one that let a widow keep her dead husband's name without paying tithe on it, because I thought—" He stopped. Started again, flatter. "The guild disagreed. There's a price schedule for mercy. I couldn't meet it. So now I owe them a debt of ink, and debts of ink get collected by putting the debtor to work until the work kills him."

"So this is your payment."

"This is me choosing which debt destroys me." He met her eyes. "You should know what reworking costs before you say yes. The veil takes memory as thread. Not random memory—it takes what you'd least afford to lose. That's how it binds. A hiding-place built from something precious holds better than one built from dross."

Coral looked at the veil. At the map. At the ledger, open to the page with Wren's name on it.

"What did you plan to feed it?" she asked.

"My mother's hands," he said simply. "Kneading bread. That's the memory. Everything else about her is gone already—I sold pieces of myself for years to cover guild fees, standard practice, don't look at me like that—and her hands are the last thing left. I've been saving them." He rolled up his sleeves. "But it'll take two threads minimum, one from each of us, or the weave won't hold a person whole. So. Your turn."

* * *

She almost lied to him.

She had a lie ready—she always had lies ready, nine years of them, polished and stacked like coins. She could offer him the taste of rain in Gildergate Square, or the sound of the gate bell, or some small bright nothing dressed up as sacrifice.

Brannoc watched her decide not to use it. Later, he would tell her that was the moment he trusted her—not the memory itself, but watching her choose the true one over the easy lie.

"There's only one thing I have left," she said. "My mother's face. I don't remember her name. I don't remember her voice. I've been keeping her face the way you keep a coal alive—cupped, careful, fed on nothing. And I don't know why it matters so much, because I don't even know if I loved her. That's what being invisible does. It eats outward from the middle."

She laid her palm flat on the veil.

"Do it."

"Coral." First time he'd used her name. "Once it's in the weave, it's in the weave. You won't grieve it, because you won't know there's anything to grieve. You'll just be a woman walking toward a mountain with a hole where her mother used to be."

"Yes," she said. "And somewhere under that mountain there's a girl who's a hole in somebody's life right now, and I helped put her there. Do it, Brannoc."

* * *

He worked by candlelight, needle-thin stylus moving through the veil's warp like it was water. He took his mother's hands first—he insisted, said the smith pays first—and Coral watched him go somewhere far away while the memory left him, watched his jaw set and his eyes go wet and empty at once, and when he came back he didn't say anything, just held out the stylus to her.

Her turn was quieter. There was no dramatic severing, no pain. Just a moment where she reached inward for her mother's face and found smooth, warm dark, like a room after the candle's out. Somewhere in the weave of an old grey-green cloth, a woman was smiling. Coral would never see it again. She knew that, distantly, the way you know the weather in a country you'll never visit.

What frightened her wasn't the loss.

It was that she felt lighter. That the hole fit her like a coat.

"There," Brannoc said roughly. He folded the veil and pressed it into her hands. "Two threads. Yours and mine. Whatever else happens down there, that cloth knows us now. It'll hide us like it hides itself." He paused. "I haven't made anything that mattered in six years. In case you were wondering whether I'm doing this for the debt."

"Are you?"

"I'm doing it because you paid a worse price than mine and didn't flinch." He shouldered his satchel of tools. "Mount Vael is four days north. The court sits behind a gate of dragon-bone, and the fire that lights it has been guttering since before either of us was born—which matters, though I'll explain why on the road. We leave within the hour, before the guild notices its keeper's gone missing."

At the door, Coral stopped. On the table behind her, the ledger lay open, and her eye caught the smudged seller's mark beside Wren's name—the one she'd assumed was deliberate erasure.

In the candlelight, angled right, she could see it plainly.

It wasn't smudged. It was *hers*. Her own childhood handwriting, looping and uncertain, the signature of a girl who hadn't yet learned to make herself invisible.

The seller's name on Wren's contract was *Coral Hartwell*.
