## 3. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain swallowed them whole.

Coral had expected darkness. What she got was fire—great ribbons of it pouring from the throat of a chained dragon at the far end of the cavern, feeding a court lit entirely in ember-orange and shadow. The beast hung in iron harnesses thick as ship's masts, its flame siphoned through copper throats into braziers, sconces, a chandelier the size of a fishing boat. It breathed because it could not stop breathing, and everything beneath Mount Vael burned on its suffering.

"Keep your hood up," Brannoc murmured beside her. "Let the veil choose."

The veil chose. Courtiers in bone-white masks turned their heads as Coral passed and found nothing worth seeing—a servant, a draught, a flicker of heat off the braziers. Nine years of being invisible at Gildergate, and here she was invisible again, but this time by craft instead of habit, and it felt different. Worse, somehow. A choice.

They descended steps cut from black glass, down into the court proper, where the magistrate held audience.

He sat beneath the chandelier on a throne of fused coins—melted together, worthless and inseparable, which Coral suspected was the point. He wore no mask. His face was ordinary, kind even, the face of a village schoolmaster, and that frightened her more than any monster would have.

"You've come for something," he said, before they'd reached the bottom step. "They always come for something."

"I've come to make a trade," Coral said. Her voice didn't shake. She'd spent nine years learning not to let it.

* * *

The souls were kept behind him.

She saw them when he gestured—rows upon rows of glass vessels arranged on iron racks like wine in a cellar, each one holding a soft grey-gold light that pulsed slow as sleeping breath. Hundreds of them. More than hundreds. The racks ran back into the dark farther than the dragon-fire reached.

"They're comfortable," the magistrate said, following her gaze with something like pride. "Warm. Fed on flame-light. Better housed than most of the living, I assure you."

"One of them is Wren Calloway," Coral said. "Sold against her will. Her father died getting me that ledger line."

"Her father *sold* her, madam keeper, same as anyone sells anything. That she objected afterward is between her and her buyer." He spread his hands. "But you know how these transactions work. You keep the books."

That landed like a blow. She did keep the books. She had recorded trades like this one in a fair hand for nine years and told herself she was only ink, only witness, only the gate.

"The trade," she said. "Name your price."

He named it, and it was ruinous—not coin, but years, memories, the whole of her time at Gildergate, every entry she'd ever written, offered up blank. Brannoc made a sound low in his throat. The veil cost her her mother's face already; this would take everything else.

"No," she said.

"Then we're at an impasse." The magistrate smiled his schoolmaster smile. "Unless—" He tilted his head. "You could walk away. I'll even unbar the door for you. You seem like a sensible woman, Keeper Hartwell. Sensible women don't burn down cellars full of fine wine over one bottle."

"How do you know my name?"

His smile widened. "Your name has been in our ledgers far longer than you've been keeping anyone's. Shall I read it to you?"

And he did.

He recited it the way she wrote entries: date, goods, parties. A child of four years, sold by the family Hartwell, purchased by the Gildergate itself—for what purpose, she couldn't hear anymore, blood roaring in her ears—held in trust, never yet delivered. Never yet *collected*. Her own soul, sitting on a rack somewhere back in the dark, waiting out nine years of rain-lashed nights while she catalogued other people's griefs in bottles.

"That's why the veil hides you so well," the magistrate said gently. "It recognizes stock."

* * *

Brannoc found her at the base of the throne steps, her hand pressed flat against cold glass, looking at a small grey-gold light that pulsed quick as a sparrow's heart. Beside it, another light—smaller, dimmer, bound in older glass.

"Wren," she said. And then, barely a breath: "Me."

"Coral." Brannoc crouched beside her. His hands were ink-stained to the wrist; they always were. "We can still walk out. The veil holds. Nobody under this mountain knows your face except a magistrate who wants payment, not war."

"She's four years old in there."

"She's also you."

"Yes," Coral said, standing. "Both things are true."

She thought about the courier dying on the Gildergate floor, the map soaked through with his blood, the word *please* worn down to almost nothing. She had refused him. He had died anyway. She would not stand in front of two small lights and refuse again.

"Brannoc. How much fire does it take to break a binding?"

He went very still. Then he looked up—at the dragon, at the chandelier, at the great guttering heart of flame that fed the whole court—and she watched him understand.

"It doesn't take much," he said. "It takes *unbinding ink*. Ledger-fire. You'd need the original page, and you'd need to feed it to the source flame itself, and you'd need—" his jaw tightened, "—someone holding the gate open behind you, because the moment those bindings break, this entire court comes apart like wet paper. Every debt in the room gets called at once."

"Can you hold a gate?"

"I'm a sigil-smith who owes the guild a debt of ink," he said. "I can hold anything for about ninety seconds. After that, I make no promises and neither does the mountain."

"Ninety seconds." Coral pulled the map from her coat—the courier's map, bloodstained, folded soft as cloth from handling—and beneath it, wrapped in oilskin, the page she'd torn from the guild's oldest ledger three nights ago, the night she stopped being only ink. Her own entry, in her own fair hand, recording her own sale. "That's enough."

"It really isn't."

"It's what there is."

* * *

The magistrate saw her coming and rose from his throne of worthless coins, and for the first time his kind face looked afraid.

"Keeper. Be sensible."

"I've been sensible for nine years," Coral said. "It nearly killed a girl."

She walked past him—past the masked courtiers who turned and, veiled or not, finally *saw* her, felt her coming like weather—up onto the dais where the great siphon-throat channeled the dragon's fire into the chandelier. The heat hit her like a hand. Behind her, Brannoc was already carving sigils into the air with both bleeding hands, building a door out of nothing where the mountain's throat had sealed shut, shouting something she couldn't hear over the roar of the flame.

Coral unwrapped the oilskin. Her own handwriting stared up at her, neat and damning: *one soul, aged four, sold by the family Hartwell.*

"Not sold," she said aloud, to no one, to everyone, to the four-year-old somewhere back in the dark. "Returned."

She fed the page into the dragon-flame.

It caught all at once, white-hot, and the burning wasn't quiet—it was a *sound*, a chord struck through the whole cavern, and every vessel on every rack flared bright as dawn. Glass cracked in rows like frost breaking off a windowpane. The grey-gold lights rose—hundreds of them, thousands, streaming upward past the chandelier, past the masked courtiers who were screaming now, past the magistrate on his knees among his fused worthless coins—and the mountain began, very slowly, to come apart.

"CORAL!" Brannoc's voice, raw, from the doorway of light he was holding open with his whole body shaking. "NOW!"

She ran. Past the racks, past the shattering glass, and she snatched two small lights out of the air as she went—one that pulsed like a sparrow, one that pulsed like her own heartbeat, which was the same thing—and the ceiling came down behind her in sheets of black glass, and the last thing the court beneath Mount Vael ever showed anyone was the dragon closing its eyes.

Then: night air. Rain. Grass. Brannoc face-down on a hillside, laughing and coughing blood and laughing, the gate dissolving into sparks above them, the mountain groaning closed forever behind them.

In Coral's cupped hands, two small lights beat against her palms like birds deciding whether to stay.

Above them, on the ridgeline, a rider in guild grey sat motionless, watching.

Word would travel by morning. Word always traveled.

Coral closed her fingers gently around the lights, stood, and started downhill toward whatever life would take them back—the first visible woman in Gildergate's history, marked, hunted, and, for the first time in nine years, entirely, dangerously *seen*.
