## 4. A Door Left Open

The souls came up out of the mountain like smoke finding a chimney.

Coral stood on the scree slope below Vael's broken gate and watched them go—hundreds of pale lights rising through the rain, scattering across the valley in every direction. Some moved fast, eager, streaking toward distant towns where bodies waited or where bodies had long since stopped waiting. Some drifted slow, circling the peak as if they'd forgotten what they were climbing toward. One paused beside her, close enough that she felt it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder, and then it was gone, southward, toward whatever life would take it back.

Brannoc sat on a flat stone with his burned hands wrapped in strips of his own shirt, watching the sky with an expression Coral had never seen on him before. Not grief. Not relief. Something closer to a man counting coins he'd been sure were counterfeit.

"Three hundred and eleven," he said. "I counted."

"You counted."

"Habit." He flexed his fingers inside the bandages and winced. "Nine years of ledger-keeping teaches a person to count. Or so I'm told."

She sat down beside him. The rain had thinned to a fine grey mist, and the dragon-fire behind them had guttered to nothing—a red glow deep in the mountain's throat, then less than that, then dark. Somewhere below, the magistrate's court was rubble and screaming, or silence. She found she didn't care which.

"Wren," she said.

"The girl?" Brannoc nodded down the slope. "By the stream. She hasn't moved since we came out."

Coral looked. Wren stood at the water's edge, small and soaked and utterly still, watching the last of the freed lights cross the valley. Waiting, Coral realized, for one of them to be hers. For a mother or a father or anyone at all to come walking up the road with open arms.

No one came. The road stayed empty. The lights went out over the hills, and Wren kept standing there, patient as only a child who has learned patience in a bad place can be patient.

Coral got up. Her knees cracked. Nine years of sitting behind a desk she'd told herself was a duty, and now her body remembered it had legs.

* * *

The guild's mark arrived three days later, at the inn in Lowmarsh where they'd stopped to let Brannoc's hands heal.

It came as a letter sealed in black wax, and Coral knew what it was before she broke it, because the courier who delivered it wouldn't meet her eyes. He set it on the table, touched two fingers to his brow—the old gatekeeper's salute, the one she'd given ten thousand times and never once received—and left without a word.

Inside, a single line in the guild-master's hand:

*Your name is struck from our ledgers. You are nothing to us. Go where you please; no door of ours will know you.*

Coral read it twice. Then she laughed—not because it was funny, but because it wasn't, and the alternative was something worse.

"They've made me visible," she said, sliding the letter across the table to Brannoc. "That's their punishment. Everywhere I go now, people will see me. Remember me. I'll have to be *someone*."

"Monstrous," Brannoc said dryly. "A whole life, with witnesses. However will you survive it."

But his hands weren't steady when he passed the letter back, and Coral understood why. She'd been invisible by trade. He'd been invisible by disgrace—struck from the sigil-smiths' rolls so long ago that even he'd stopped looking for his name anywhere. The guild hadn't just punished her. It had reminded both of them what they were: unlisted. Unclaimed. Men and women the world's great books declined to record.

Except.

The second letter came that evening, and it came by no courier at all. It was simply there when Coral opened the shutters at dawn—folded into the frame, weighted with a river stone, written in the cramped hand of someone who'd spent decades keeping records nobody official counted.

*Sister,* it began. *We saw what you did beneath Vael. We saw what you refused.*

*You should know there are more of us than the guild admits. Keepers of gates the ledgers never mention—ferry crossings, mountain passes, the door in the alms-house wall, the stair behind the chandlery in Gildergate itself. We keep thresholds. We decide, each of us alone, who passes and who is turned away, and we answer to no master's ink.*

*We do not ask you to join us. Joining is a word for guilds. We only tell you: the next time you stand at a door, you will not stand there alone.*

*The gate at Millbrook needs a keeper. It opens both ways. Come see it, if you like.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Coral read it standing in the cold morning light with mist on her eyelashes, and felt something settle in her chest that she had no ledger-entry for—a weight leaving, or a weight arriving; she genuinely could not have said which.

* * *

Millbrook was four days' walk north, a village of slate roofs and one stubborn bridge, and the gate was exactly as promised: a low wooden door in the side of the tollhouse, older than the tollhouse, older probably than the bridge, opening onto a lane that had no business being there and yet had clearly always been there. A threshold. Nothing grander than that.

Coral stood before it for a long time on her first morning as its keeper.

Behind her, Wren was arguing with the tollhouse cat about jurisdiction. The girl had talked almost nothing for the first week—watched everything, flinched at raised voices, slept with her back to a wall—and then somewhere on the second day of walking she'd begun, tentatively, to speak, as if testing whether her voice still worked outside the mountain. It did. It worked very well. She had opinions about cats, about porridge, about the correct way to fold a map, and about Brannoc, whom she had decided was funny in the way that grumpy things are funny, and whom she followed around asking questions until he surrendered and started teaching her sigils just to have something to occupy her hands.

"She'll want to know if the door opens today," Wren called now, appearing at Coral's elbow with the cat draped over one arm like a stole. "People are already lining up. There's a woman with a goat."

"A woman with a goat."

"For the market on the other side. She says the lane saves her half a day." Wren peered up at her. "Do we let goats through?"

"I don't know. Let's find out together."

They opened the door together, too—Wren's hand on the latch under Coral's, because Coral had decided, in the four days of walking, that this was not a thing she would ever do alone again. The lane beyond was bright with morning. The woman with the goat clucked her thanks and passed through, and after her a tinker, and after him two sisters carrying baskets of late apples, and each of them saw Coral plainly, met her eyes, said good morning to her by name within the hour, because that was how it worked when you were visible: people learned you.

Brannoc arrived at midday with his hands mostly healed and his satchel full of inks, took one look at the queue forming, and sighed the sigh of a man accepting a job he'd pretend to resent.

"I'll need a workbench," he said. "And the girl stops asking questions about binding-sigils until she can write a straight line."

"She writes straighter than you do."

"That is slander, and I will be lodging a complaint with whoever keeps records here."

"That would be me," Coral said. "Complaint noted. Filed nowhere. Struck from all ledgers."

And he grinned—actually grinned, the disgraced sigil-smith, the man who'd owed the guild a debt of ink since before she'd ever sat behind her desk—and went inside to argue about benches.

* * *

Evening came down soft over Millbrook. Coral closed the gate at dusk, as keepers did, but she left the latch loose—the way the letter had described, the way the network's doors all worked. Openable. From either side. By anyone with the courage to knock.

Inside the tollhouse, Wren had done the thing she did every night now: climbed onto the chair, and hung the map on the wall. The courier's bloodstained map, creased and torn and mended with Brannoc's careful sigil-work along the folds, framed in scrap wood Wren had painted blue because blue was, she announced, the color of doors that open. Beneath it Coral had pinned a single sheet of paper, blank except for one line at the top, in her own hand:

*Ledger of the Millbrook Gate.*

Below the line, the first entry, dated this morning:

*One woman. One goat. Both returned by nightfall, the goat slightly fatter.*

Wren blew out the lamp. In the dark, Coral listened to the house settle around them—the creak of Brannoc's cot, the cat's purr, the girl's breathing going slow and even—and understood, finally, what she'd been keeping all those years at Gildergate. Not a gate. Not a ledger. A vigil. A long, cold watch for a door that would never open toward her.

This one did. This one creaked on its hinges right now, in fact, because Wren had kicked it open on her way to bed and couldn't be persuaded to close anything properly, and Coral lay listening to it swing gently in the night wind and thought: *let it.*

Somewhere far off, past the edge of sleep, she heard the latch lift—softly, politely, the way the network's messengers came and went—and a folded paper slid through onto the floor.

She'd read it in the morning. Whatever it asked, wherever it pointed, she would not go alone.

The door, left open, breathed the night in and out, like a house learning to live.
