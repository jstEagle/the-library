## 3. The Crown That Wears the Man

Brannoc had sworn an oath to this city once, and the city had answered by forgetting him. Now he walked its streets again with a hood drawn low and a child on his shoulders who smelled of woodsmoke and rain, and he found that the forgetting ran both ways. The cobbles did not know his boots. The gate-guards did not mark his face. Even the gulls crying over the fish-market seemed to wheel past him as though he were made of the same gray air as everything else.

He had spent six years learning to be unremarkable. It sat poorly on him still, like armor sized for another man.

"Down," the child said—not in the valley's tongue, not in any tongue the capital would know, but in that older speech that moved through Brannoc's chest like a hand through water. *Down. I want to see.*

"You'll be seen," he rumbled back, in the same language. His mouth remembered it better than his mind did; the words came out shaped wrong, but shaped enough. The child had been patient with him, correcting his grammar with the grave authority of someone perhaps seven years old.

*Seen is not the same as caught,* the child said, and slid down his cloak to stand in the mud, small hands fisted at her sides, staring up at the palace.

It rose above the city like a jaw of white stone, all spires and buttresses, banners snapping in the wind off the sea. Brannoc had knelt in its great hall once, young and stupid and full of the old king's name, before the old king stopped being anyone at all. He had watched Aldric crowned—had sung the coronation hymn with a voice cracked from shouting—and he had been one of only three men in that hall who saw what settled onto Aldric's brow along with the gold. Something had leaned down out of the rafters' shadows. Something had poured itself into the crown like wine into a cup, and the cup had drunk the man.

The other two witnesses were dead. Brannoc had only been exiled, because he would not stop saying what he'd seen, and a knight who remembers too much is more dangerous to a throne than a knight who draws steel.

Behind him, Silas drew her hood tighter. She had been silent for two days—not her usual silence, which was watchful and wry, but a silence with weight in it, like a woman carrying something breakable up a long stair. Since the border. Since the magic had come back into her like a flood into a dry riverbed and she had fallen to her knees in the wet grass and wept without sound.

"It's worse than I thought," she said now, quietly, in the trade-tongue. "The wards around the palace. Look at them."

"I don't see anything."

"That's rather the point." Her eyes moved across the air above the gates, tracking lines only she could perceive. "They're not walls. They're funnels. Everything the wards catch—prayers, blessings, stray magic, the little fires people light for their dead—it all drains inward. Toward the crown." She paused. "Toward the child."

The child looked up at him. In the gray light her eyes held that banked ember-glow that no human child's should hold, and Brannoc felt the old oath stir in him like a dog hearing its name after years.

"We go in tonight," he said.

* * *

They went in through the cisterns, because Brannoc remembered the cisterns—he remembered everything, that was his curse and his single usefulness—and because the wardens' patrols had thinned since the king stopped holding court. That was the thing about a hollowed king: the rot spread downward through the whole apparatus of rule. Servants wandered the corridors with unfocused eyes. Guards stood post and dreamed. Somewhere above them, the crown fed, and everything within its reach grew a little dimmer, a little more obedient, a little less itself.

Silas led them through the last galleries by the light of a sigil she'd drawn on her own palm—a small burning script that hurt Brannoc's eyes to look at directly, written in that pre-kingdom hand. She had grown steadier with each day of travel, the power settling into her like water finding its level, and she had begun, hesitantly, to read the archive fragments she'd carried out of the temple sewn into her coat.

Now, in the antechamber behind the throne room, she stopped so suddenly that the child walked into her legs.

"What is it?" Brannoc kept his voice low. His hand was already on his sword. Six years of exile had taught him that silence in a palace meant teeth somewhere near.

Silas didn't answer. She had pulled one of the folded vellum sheets from her coat, and she was holding it up against the wall of the antechamber, against the faint tracery of old carvings beneath centuries of gilt and paint—and the shapes matched. Line for line. Curve for curve.

"No," she said softly. "No, no, no."

"Silas."

"This is the binding." Her voice had gone strange, flat, the voice of a scribe reading her own death warrant. "The ritual that locked the crown to the king. I've been reading these fragments for days thinking they were a warning, a history, someone else's work. They're not. They're a draft. This—" she pressed her palm to the wall, and the sigil on her skin flared, and beneath the gilt the ancient carving woke and glowed in answer, "—this is the finished version. Cut into the stone before the palace was even crowned with its roof."

The child tugged Brannoc's sleeve. *She's shaking,* the child observed, with a child's terrible clarity.

"I can see that."

"The handwriting," Silas whispered. She had turned the vellum over, though there was nothing on the reverse, though there had never been anything on the reverse. "I told you I recognized the dialect from the sealed archives. I lied. Not knowingly—I don't think I knew I was lying. But I've seen this hand before. I've *copied* this hand. For years, in the scriptorium, my teachers praised how naturally it came to me." She laughed, one broken note. "Of course it came naturally. It's mine. It has always been mine. I wrote the binding, Brannoc. Or I helped write it. And then I paid the cost of it, and the cost was myself."

The torches guttered. Somewhere far above, iron rang against iron—the changing of a guard that no longer changed anything.

"Tell me the rest," Brannoc said. It was not a kindness to ask, and they both knew it, and she answered anyway, because that was who she was: a woman who could not leave a record false, even the record of herself.

"There were three of us. Three scribes, in the time before kingdoms, when the dragons were dying and the fire had to go somewhere. We wrote a vessel-ritual—to hold the last dragon-fire safe until hands worthy of it came. But the crown got there first. It learned the shape of our working and inverted it. Instead of a vessel, a prison. Instead of keeping the fire safe, feeding on it, king after king, hollowing them out." Her fingers traced the carved lines. "And we couldn't undo it without unmaking the vessel too. So we did the only thing left. We hid the fire. Passed it down, person to person, generation to generation, each bearer giving it up before the crown could find them. And we erased ourselves from every record so the crown could never trace the working back to its authors and tear the counter-spell out of us."

She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake.

"I didn't flee debt, Brannoc. I fled *myself*. Every record I ever falsified, every name I scratched out of every ledger—I was finishing the job. I thought I was protecting the world from knowing me. I was protecting the crown from finding the last piece of the unbinding." A breath. "Forgetting was never innocence. I just buried my guilt where I couldn't reach it and called it peace."

The child reached up and took Silas's ink-stained hand in both of hers. She said something in the dragon-tongue, soft, and though Brannoc's grasp of it was poor, he caught the shape of it: *You came back. That is the part that matters.*

Silas closed her eyes. When she opened them, the shaking had stopped.

"Then we finish it," she said. "Tonight. Before the crown senses how close the fire has come. I need the throne room. I need the original matrix under the dais—my matrix—and I need time to read my own name back into the world." She rolled up the vellum with hands that no longer trembled. "And I need you to keep me alive while I do it, wolf-knight, because the moment I begin, every thrall in this palace will know exactly where I am."

Brannoc drew his sword. The blade sang faintly, remembering old oaths.

"That," he said, "is the first order anyone's given me in six years that I'm glad to follow."

They pushed open the doors to the throne room together—and stopped.

The hall blazed with candlelight, hundreds upon hundreds of candles, and every throne-side pillar was hung with chains, and hanging from the chains, swaying gently, were the wardens of the valley temple. Alive. Waiting. And on the white throne sat King Aldric, upright and immaculate and empty-eyed, the crown upon his head burning with a cold blue flame—and the crown turned, slowly, like a head waking, toward the small glowing child at Brannoc's side.

"*There* you are," it said, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "I have been so hungry."
