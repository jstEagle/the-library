## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The candles in the Amber Hall were lit at dusk, all ninety of them, and Isandro Okonkwo counted every flame as they always did — because a flame that guttered wrong could mean a draft, and a draft could mean an open window, and an open window on the night of the Autumn Concord banquet could mean anything at all.

Ninety flames. All steady.

Isandro stood at the edge of the serving corridor with their hands folded and their face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a servant who thought about nothing but supper. It was a face they had practiced for eleven years, since the day they'd arrived at the palace of Vhalen's Rest with a taster's certificate forged by a man who owed them a debt and a tongue that was not entirely their own accomplishment. The court believed Isandro Okonkwo had been born with a cast-iron stomach and a lucky constitution. The court believed many things.

The truth sat coiled beneath their skin like a second heartbeat: a gift of sigil-craft, old as the mountains, older than the crown that now employed them to swallow its dangers.

"Isandro." The steward's voice, clipped as ever. "Her Majesty's table is set. You'll take your place."

They took it. They always took it.

* * *

The Amber Hall smelled of beeswax and roasted quail and the particular sweetness of pears poached in spiced wine, and beneath all of it — faint as a whisper under a door — the copper-and-rain scent of something that should not have been there.

Isandro had learned to trust that whisper above every other sense they owned. Poison announced itself differently to different noses; to theirs, after years of deliberate, methodical, terrifying self-education, it came as a color behind the eyes. Arsenic was green. Hemlock was grey-blue, like dusk over water. The compounds of the Thornfell smugglers — the ones that killed slow and left no mark for any physician to find — tasted of iron and old snow.

What drifted from the third course, from the covered dish of glazed eel destined for the queen's own plate, was none of these and all of them at once. A blend. Someone had blended.

Isandro moved through the ritual as though nothing lived inside their skull but hunger and duty. They tasted the soup before it left the kitchen pass — clean. The bread — clean, though the butter carried a bitterness that was only rind, only honest. The wine for the queen's cup, decanted and breathing — clean, and they let themselves breathe too, one measured breath, because the eel was coming and they would need their hands steady.

Behind them, the hall filled with music and the rustle of silk. The queen sat beneath the amber canopy with her rings stacked three deep on each finger, laughing at something the ambassador from Corvess had said, her laughter bright and rehearsed and utterly without warmth. Isandro had watched that laughter for years. It never reached her eyes, and her eyes missed nothing — least of all her food-taster standing motionless beside the service door with a silver spoon held to the light.

The eel arrived on a platter of black lacquer, glazed until it gleamed like riverstone, garnished with candied ginger cut into thin coins.

Isandro lifted the tasting fork.

Under the honey and the smoke, under the skillful mask of the glaze itself — which was clever, so clever, someone had used the glaze to carry it — the poison bloomed. Not arsenic. Not hemlock. Something compounded, layered, built to wake slowly in the blood so that Her Majesty would retire feeling perfectly well and die comfortably in her sleep, and no physician in Vhalen's Rest would ever name the cause. The kind of poison you bought from a smuggler who knew the high passes. The kind that cost more than a servant earned in ten lifetimes.

Someone wanted the queen dead tonight, and had paid a fortune for the privilege.

Isandro's mouth flooded with the taste of it, and their body — trained, disciplined, betrayed by nothing — did not so much as blink. They set down the fork. They inclined their head toward the steward.

"The glaze is excellent," they said clearly. "Her Majesty may be served."

And then, in the four seconds it took for the platter to travel from the warming table to the queen's elbow, Isandro Okonkwo committed treason.

Their sleeve fell forward — a small thing, a servant's careless gesture, brushing the edge of the lacquer platter as if steadying it. Beneath the cuff, inked into the skin of their inner wrist where no one ever looked at a food-taster, lay a sigil they had drawn that morning in the dark of their narrow room: a closed circle crossed by three descending lines, the old sign of stilling, of quieting, of things made harmless. It woke at the touch of their pulse against the platter's rim. Isandro felt it go out from them like a stone dropped into deep water — felt the poison's color dim, gutter, drown. The compound did not vanish; that was beyond even their gift, and they had never dared test how far beyond. It simply went to sleep. Dormant. Inert. Sleeping in the queen's bloodstream like a seed in frozen ground, harmless unless something woke it.

Nothing would wake it. Isandro would make certain of that tomorrow, when they prepared the queen's morning tisane with the counter-agent they kept hidden in a hollowed heel of cheese.

The platter settled before Her Majesty. She took up her knife. She ate.

Isandro exhaled through their nose, slow and silent, and watched the candle flames again. Ninety. All steady. Their own heart was not.

* * *

"You look like death warmed twice," said Sefa, appearing at their shoulder with a stack of dirty plates and the particular gracelessness of a woman carrying more than she should.

"Sefa." Isandro stepped back from the service door, out of the sightline of the hall, letting the blankness fall away for exactly as long as it was safe to let it fall. With Sefa it was safe more often than it should have been. That was the danger of her, and the whole reason Isandro kept coming back to it anyway. "I look like a man who has eaten fourteen meals today and enjoyed perhaps two of them."

"Fourteen?" She shifted the plates to her hip and grinned up at them — Sefa was small and quick and brown as a chestnut, with flour permanently ghosted into the creases of her knuckles no matter how she scrubbed. "You're wasted on the crown, Okonkwo. Any sensible person would have died of pleasure by the sixth course."

"The sixth course was pigeon. There is no pleasure in pigeon."

"There's pleasure if you make the sauce right." She bumped their arm with her elbow, an intimacy no one else in the palace dared, and lowered her voice. "You didn't touch the eel."

Isandro's blood went very quiet.

"I tasted it," they said. "It passed."

"You barely wet the fork. I was at the pass when you did it. I've watched you eat whole legs of lamb for this job, whole fish, once an entire oyster pie that Master Corvin swore was off." Her dark eyes searched their face with a directness that had unsettled better liars than Isandro. "Barely wet the fork. And then you stood there looking like a woman watching a hanging."

"A woman watching a hanging," Isandro repeated mildly, "would be a remarkable sight in Vhalen's Rest."

"Don't do that." Sefa's voice dropped further, and the playfulness drained out of it, leaving something older and more careful underneath. "Don't do the thing where you turn everything into a joke until I forget what I saw. I forget plenty, Isandro. I forget most things. But I don't forget that."

The corridor noise swelled around them — servers passing, the steward's voice calling for the sweet course, somewhere a dropped spoon and a hissed curse. Ninety candles burning steady in the hall beyond. Isandro looked at their oldest friend in the world, the girl who had shared stolen plums with them in the market alleys of Lowbridge before either of them had a trade or a title or a secret worth killing for, and felt the familiar exhaustion settle over their shoulders like a wet cloak.

Eleven years of vigilance. Eleven years of tasting other people's deaths so the queen wouldn't taste her own. Eleven years of sleeping with one hand curled over their wrist, guarding the ink, guarding the gift, guarding the self that no law and no master and no friend was permitted to see.

And here was Sefa, holding a stack of dirty plates, telling them she had seen.

"I don't know what you think you saw," Isandro said quietly.

"I think," said Sefa, "that my friend has a mark on her wrist that she covers even in summer, and that she talks to her food sometimes under her breath like she's arguing with it, and that three years ago when the whole kitchen came down with flux and you didn't, and last winter when Lord Ammerel's household lost four servants to bad mushrooms and his taster died and ours didn't — I think I add numbers, Isandro. I'm a kitchen maid, not a fool. Adding numbers is half the job."

The words landed in Isandro's chest like stones into still water, and the ripples went out and out and out.

She didn't know. Not truly — couldn't know the shape of it, the sigils, the years of poisoning herself by careful degrees until her blood became a fortress, the gift that ran in her family like a vein of ore through worthless rock. But she knew something. She had known, perhaps, for years, and had said nothing, and had gone on sharing plums and gossip and the small warm hours between services as though knowing changed nothing at all.

That was the part that nearly undid them. Not the fear — fear was an old companion, worn smooth as a river pebble from handling. It was the tenderness that ambushed them, sudden and total, standing in a serving corridor smelling of grease and beeswax: *she knew, and she stayed.*

"Sefa," Isandro began.

"Sweet course!" barked the steward, sweeping past. "Move, both of you, move — Her Majesty is waiting."

Sefa hoisted her plates. For a moment her hand found Isandro's forearm and squeezed, brief and fierce, right over the cuff, right over the hidden circle of ink. Then she was gone into the steam and clatter of the kitchens, and Isandro stood alone in the corridor with their heart hammering out a rhythm that had nothing to do with poison.

* * *

The banquet ended past midnight. The queen retired unpoisoned, laughing her bright rehearsed laugh, and the hall emptied by degrees until only the candles remained, burning down in their ninety small vigilances.

Isandro walked the long way back to the servants' wing, as they always did, past the gallery where the portraits of dead monarchs watched with painted patience. Their wrist throbbed faintly where the sigil had spent itself. By morning the ink would need redrawing; the gift consumed its marks the way a fire consumes its fuel, and Isandro consumed ink the way other people consumed bread.

In their narrow room they sat on the narrow bed and did the accounting they did every night. The eel: neutralized, follow-up required at breakfast. The source: unknown, and troubling — palace kitchens were sealed tighter than the treasury, which meant the poison had come in through a channel Isandro did not control, and channels they did not control were the whole reason they had taken this miserable post in the first place. The risk: contained. The exposure:

They flexed their hand. Sefa's squeeze still lived in the skin of their forearm like a brand laid cool against the ink.

*She knows something,* Isandro told the dark. *Knowing something is not knowing everything. She is safe. You are safe.*

But the thought arrived unbidden, treacherous, wearing the voice of the criers who would soon enough be shouting in the squares: *safe for how long?*

There had been rumors all autumn. Whispers from the capital, from the queen's inner council, from the magistrates who smiled too much lately. Talk of a purge. Talk of the gifted — the sigil-makers, the shifters, the hedge-witches and soul-readers, all the people born with the old blood in them — being named enemies of the crown. Talk of rewards for informants. Isandro had told themselves it was politics, the usual seasonal fever, the kind that burned hot and broke by midwinter.

Tonight, watching the queen eat a poisoned dish and smile, Isandro had understood something new: the crown already lived among enemies. Perhaps it wanted more of them. Perhaps it needed them — needed the fear, needed the hunting, needed something to feed.

They drew the blanket over their shoulders and pressed their thumb to the fading sigil on their wrist, feeling the last warmth of it like a coal wrapped in wool.

Outside, the first wind of true autumn came down off the Thornfell mountains, rattling the shutters, carrying the smell of snow that had not yet fallen but was already thinking about it. Somewhere in the dark city below, someone had paid a fortune in smuggler's gold to kill a queen, and had failed, and did not yet know it.

And somewhere closer than that — two floors down, in a cot behind the kitchen scullery — a small brown woman with flour in her knuckles was sleeping soundly, keeping Isandro's secret without ever having been asked to carry it.

Isandro closed their eyes. Tomorrow there would be the tisane, and the counter-agent, and the long careful work of finding whose hand had seasoned the eel. Tomorrow there would be Sefa's knowing eyes across the kitchen, and the exquisite, exhausting labor of pretending not to know that she knew.

For tonight, they let themselves have the memory of the squeeze on their arm, the warmth of it, the weight of being seen by one person and surviving it.

It was enough. It had always been enough.

It would not be enough for much longer.
