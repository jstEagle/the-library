## 2. Edicts in Ash

The bells began before dawn.

Isandro heard them from the narrow room above the tallow-chandler's shop where they lodged — three strokes, a pause, three more — and lay still beneath the rough blanket, counting. The palace rang its bells for coronations and funerals and the changing of the season's guard. It did not ring them before dawn unless something had changed.

By the time they reached the kitchen of the Queen's Own household, the change had a name, and the name was being shouted in every square in Vellamarch.

*The Purge of Gifts.*

The criers stood on their stones with parchment scrolls in their fists, and their voices carried over the morning market like smoke over water: *By decree of Her Radiant Majesty, all persons gifted with the old arts — sigil-craft, soul-sight, hedge-healing, beast-speaking, and every art unnamed — shall present themselves to the magistrates within thirty days. Those who confess shall be marked and registered. Those who conceal shall be seized. Those who aid the concealed shall share their sentence. Informants will be rewarded in silver and standing.*

Silver and standing. Isandro had learned long ago that those two words bought more knives than any assassin's guild ever could.

They kept their face arranged into the mild, slightly dull expression that had served them through seven years of palace service — the face of a man who thought mostly about his next meal, which was, in fairness, not entirely a lie. They took up their station by the queen's breakfast table, and when the first dish came up from the ovens — honeyed oats with cream and crushed hazelnut — they tasted it with the small silver spoon that never left their belt, held each mouthful on their tongue for the count of ten, and waited for their body to tell them what their eyes could not.

Nothing. Almond, cream, the faint bitterness of hazelnut skin. Safe.

The queen swept in wrapped in grey fox fur, her face like a coin left too long in rain, and Isandro bowed with everyone else and tasted everything she touched and did not let their hands shake.

But underneath the plainness, under the servant's stoop and the taster's silence, something was screaming.

*Thirty days.* Thirty days to decide whether to walk to the magistrates and be marked like a branded ox, or to run, or to burn everything they were down to the ash and hope the fire reached deep enough.

---

The gift had come to them at nine years old, in the season their mother died, though they had never been able to decide whether those two facts were cause or coincidence. Their mother had been a hedgewife in the Thornfell foothills, one of the quiet practitioners who mended fences with a chalked sign and drew fever out of children with a knotted thread, and she had taught Isandro the first letters of the sigil alphabet the way other mothers taught prayers — half-whispered, half-ashamed, absolutely certain.

*It is not wicked,* she used to say, warming their cold hands between hers. *It is only old. The crown is new. New things are frightened of old things.*

She had been dead eleven years now. The crown had grown no less frightened, and Isandro had grown very good at hiding.

Their tools lived in a false bottom beneath the floorboards of their rented room: a bundle of bone styli worn smooth by their mother's thumb; three cakes of binding-ink made from oak-gall and iron salt; a strip of cured hide covered in practice glyphs, each one annotated in their own careful hand; a ring of lead tokens stamped with warding marks, good for an hour's protection against scrying, less against anything with teeth. Seven years of a double life, folded into a space the size of a loaf of bread.

That evening, after the queen's supper had been tasted course by course — the pike in sorrel sauce, the quail, the spiced pears, all of it clean, all of it safe — Isandro climbed the stairs to their room, barred the door, and knelt on the floorboards with the loose plank lifted and the whole secret life of J. M.— of Isandro Okonkwo laid out in the candlelight.

They told themselves it was caution. If the purge came to door-to-door searches, if informants' silver loosened landladies' tongues, then the tools could not be found. Burning them was survival. Survival was the whole discipline of their existence, the grammar of every day since they'd walked out of the foothills with nothing but their mother's styli and a forged letter of recommendation.

They built the fire up high. They fed the bone styli into it one at a time and watched the flame take the smooth places their mother's thumb had polished, and told themselves the ache in their chest was only smoke.

The binding-ink went next, the cakes cracking and hissing, releasing a smell like burnt autumn. The practice-hide curled black. The lead tokens softened and slumped into bright droplets among the coals, and Isandro sat back on their heels and watched seven years of careful, patient, terrified work become light and heat and nothing.

Then they held out their right hand, palm up, over the fire, and looked at it.

Because here was the truth the edict had not accounted for, the flaw in the queen's arithmetic that made Isandro laugh — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. The tools were not the gift. The ink was not the gift. You could burn every stylus in Thornfell and the gift would remain, sitting in the blood and the bones like a second heartbeat, patient as winter. Isandro could draw a warding sigil in spilled wine with their little finger. They could write a binding in the frost on a windowpane. They had once, in desperation, sealed a leaking roof-tile with a mark traced in candle soot that held through a three-day storm.

The gift was not in the tools. The gift was in *them*, and there was no fire hot enough, no magistrate sharp enough, no confession thorough enough to cut it out.

*Those who conceal shall be seized.*

Isandro looked at their hand — a taster's hand, scarred faintly at the fingertips from years of pricking and probing, unremarkable in every way but one — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they had just burned their tools for nothing. Not for safety. For ceremony. A funeral for objects, because they could not hold a funeral for the self.

The candle guttered. Down in the street, a crier's voice rose and fell, rose and fell, wearing the words smooth as river stones: *silver and standing, silver and standing.*

Isandro put out the fire, raked the ashes fine, and sat in the dark until the bells rang midnight, doing the only thing they knew how to do with fear, which was to make lists. Routes out of the city. Gates and their guards. Which magistrates took bribes and which took pleasure. The smuggler paths through Thornfell, every switchback and cache, mapped in memory because paper could be seized and memory could only be taken with the head entire.

And threaded through every list, unwelcome as a splinter, one name: *Sefa.*

---

Sefa found them the next morning in the palace kitchens, and she did not find them the way she usually did — with flour on her arms and some complaint about the pastry cook and a joke already loaded behind her teeth. She came through the steam and the clatter quiet, which for Sefa was like coming through a storm quiet, and she set herself against the chopping block beside Isandro as if she'd been assigned there, and said nothing for a long moment while around them the kitchen roared with panic dressed up as industry.

Everyone was talking about the edict. Everyone was talking too loudly, the way people do when silence would force them to think. The under-cook had a cousin in the provinces who could read clouds, apparently, and was this true, and would he have to register, and someone else's brother-in-law swore the reward for informing was fifty crowns now, fifty, enough to buy a shop —

"Walk with me," Sefa said. "The cellars need taking stock."

No one stopped them. In the upheaval, a maid and a taster descending into the wine cellars with a lantern raised no eyebrows at all.

The cellars ran back into the hillside beneath the palace, older than the palace, older than the crown maybe — vaulted stone going dark and cool and smelling of dust and dregs and the green ghost of long-empty barrels. Sefa led them past the racked wine, past the cheese loft, past the pickling shelves, all the way to the far end where the last barrel stood in shadow and the lantern-light barely reached, and there she turned and looked at Isandro with an expression they had never seen on her face before.

Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, something steadier. Something that had clearly been waiting a long time for its turn.

"You burned your tools last night," she said.

The cellar went very still around them. Somewhere above, muffled through feet of stone, the kitchen went on shouting about fifty crowns.

"I don't know what you mean," Isandro said, and heard how thin it sounded, how rehearsed, how exactly like the voice they used for magistrates and guards.

"Smoke," Sefa said simply. "Your chimney smoked wrong last night. Greasy, sweetish — that's binding-ink burning, oak-gall and iron salt. My grandmother rendered ink for a hedge-witch two valleys over. I know the smell." She folded her arms. "And you've been smoking it once or twice a year for as long as I've known you, always late at night, always after some edict or procession or visit from the Magistry. I used to think you were destroying letters. Then I paid attention."

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. Seven years. Seven years of the mild face and the dull eyes and the careful, practiced nothing, and the whole time —

"How long," they managed.

"Since the third month you worked the tasting table. You neutralized something in the regent's tribute-wine — I saw you do it. A flick of your finger in the spill when you wiped the table, and the wine that should have put the ambassador's whole retinue to sleep did nothing at all. I didn't understand it then. I do now." Her voice dropped lower, though there was no one in the world down here but the two of them and the dark. "You're gifted, Isandro. You've been gifted this whole time, hiding in plain sight, and you are either the bravest person I know or the biggest fool, and I have decided it doesn't matter which."

"Why?" The word came out cracked. "Why say nothing? Why not — " and here their mind supplied the ugly arithmetic automatically, the way it supplied antidotes and poison names — *fifty crowns, silver and standing* — "why not sell me?"

Something moved across Sefa's face, quick as a fish under ice — hurt, Isandro realized, actual hurt — and then it was gone, and what remained was fierce.

"Because when my mother died, four winters back, I had nothing. No family, no coin, no claim on anyone. And you — " she stopped, steadied. "You sat with me the whole night watch. You brought food you'd tested yourself, off your own plate, and you said, *no one has touched this but me, I promise you, eat.* Do you know what that is, to a girl who trusts nothing? That's a wall built around her out of somebody else's own body. You made yourself my proof that the world had one honest thing in it." She shook her head slowly. "I wasn't going to sell the one honest thing for fifty crowns. I wouldn't sell you for five hundred. I wouldn't sell you for the queen's whole purse."

Isandro discovered that they could not speak. The vigilance of seven years — the counting, the tasting, the mild face worn like armor until they'd forgotten there was skin underneath — all of it had been built on a single assumption: *no one knows, and if anyone knows, I die.* And here in the dark under the palace, the assumption lay broken on the flagstones, and instead of death pouring through the crack, what poured through was warmth. Terrifying, unguarded, unbearable warmth.

"You knew," they said finally, uselessly. "All this time, you knew."

"I knew." Sefa's mouth twitched toward her usual crooked smile, though her eyes stayed wet. "And you never once poisoned me, which in this city counts as devotion."

A laugh broke out of Isandro — real, startled, half a sob — and echoed off the vaulted dark, and for one moment the cellar was not a cellar but a place outside the edict, outside the purge, outside the reach of criers and magistrates and queens: just two people laughing in the smell of old wine because the truth, spoken aloud at last, weighed less than the lie had.

Then the moment passed, as moments do, and the weight came back with interest.

"They'll come for the registers first," Isandro said, and their taster's mind was already moving, cold and fast, sorting threats the way it sorted flavors. "Then the hedge-folk in the low quarters — easiest to find, least able to run. Then door-to-door. Sefa, listen to me. When they ask you questions, you know nothing. You've never smelled anything on any chimney. You've never seen anything at any table. You're a kitchen maid with a dead mother and bad knees, and you will live a long boring life, and that is the whole of your story."

"No."

The word landed flat and final as a cleaver.

"Sefa — "

"No." She stepped closer, and the lantern threw her shadow huge against the barrels. "You don't get to hand me a boring life like alms. I'm not a thing you protect by lying to. I'm your friend, and friends don't stand in doorways while the house burns." Her chin came up. "Besides. You'll need help. You can't run alone — you're a taster, not a fighter, and half this city would sell your name for a copper now that there's a price on it. You need someone who knows the servants' gates, who knows which guards drink, who knows which carts leave before dawn unchecked. You need me."

"I haven't said I'm running."

"You burned your tools." Sefa's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "People burn their tools when they're deciding. And Isandro — " she reached out and took their scarred right hand, the gifted hand, and held it in both of hers, warm and flour-rough and utterly unafraid — "whatever you decide, decide it fast. Because I watched the criers this morning, and I watched the faces of the people listening, and it isn't fear I saw. It's appetite. Thirty days is not a deadline. It's an opening of the hunting season."

Isandro looked down at their hand in hers. Above them, muffled and distant, the palace bells began to ring the hour, and beneath the bells, faint but unmistakable, came the crier's cadence again, drifting down through stone and earth like water through a grave:

*— all persons gifted with the old arts — those who conceal shall be seized — informants rewarded in silver and standing —*

Silver and standing. Fifty crowns. A shop of one's own. A life bought with a neighbor's name.

And somewhere beyond the city walls, north and east, the Thornfell mountains stood up white-shouldered against the morning, holding in their deep places the old roads, the smuggler caches, the doors that predated the crown — and, if half the drunk soldiers' stories were true, a court beneath the mountain that bought and sold things no market should touch.

Isandro closed their fingers around Sefa's hand.

"Not yet," they said quietly. "Not tonight. Tonight we taste the queen's supper and smile at everyone and learn who's been promoted and who's been paid. But Sefa — pack a bag. Small. And keep it where you can reach it in the dark."

Sefa searched their face for a long moment, and whatever she found there, she nodded once, slow and solemn as an oath.

"And Isandro? Whatever it is you're planning—" the crooked smile surfaced again, scared and bright, "—you're not planning it alone anymore."

They climbed back up into the noise and the steam and the hunger of the palace kitchens, two servants among forty, invisible, ordinary, armed.

And in the street outside, a crier nailed a fresh copy of the edict to the post, and the wind off the mountains stirred the parchment like a living thing, and the thirty days began quietly counting themselves down.
