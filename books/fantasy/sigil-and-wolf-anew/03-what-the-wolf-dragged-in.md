## 3. What the Wolf Dragged In

Bren had been running since moonrise, and the city had finally stopped making sense.

Streets that should have run straight bent back on themselves like question marks. Doors he'd marked as exits opened onto courtyards walled with new brick. The city of Vhalen did not want him to leave, and it was telling him so in the language of stone and mortar, patiently, the way a spider tells a fly.

He pressed himself into the shadow of a tannery's overhang and let his lungs burn. The wound in his shoulder — a crossbow bolt, taken at the river gate, snapped off and left to fester — pulsed in time with his heartbeat, which was too fast and knew it.

They had come for him at dusk. Not because of anything he'd done. Because of what he *was*.

The first sweep, the criers were calling it. Purge of Gifts. Every shifter, every hedge-witch, every child born with frost on their breath or whispers in their teeth — seized, catalogued, and carted away to places no one named twice. Bren had watched them take the cooper's daughter from her father's arms, the girl screaming a word that made the soldiers' torches gutter blue, and he had understood with animal clarity that the word would save no one. It hadn't saved the cooper's daughter. It wouldn't save him.

So he ran. And the city ran with him, closing its streets like a fist.

*Think,* he told himself. *You're still a wolf even when you're a man.*

He closed his eyes and breathed through his nose. Under the tallow-stink of the tannery, under the ash-smoke and the fear-sweat of a thousand hiding people, there were other smells. River water. Wet wool. And beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, rising from somewhere ahead and below —

Bread. Yeast and warm grain and the deep mineral breath of ovens.

Kitchens. Kitchens meant cellars. Cellars meant doors, and doors meant out.

Bren pushed off the wall and followed his nose into the dark.

* * *

The servants' gate to the palace kitchens stood open, because gates always stood open where food came and went; hunger kept better hours than any guard. Bren slipped through sideways, one shoulder leading, and found himself in a corridor that smelled of marrow-bones and bay leaf, lit by a single lamp burning low.

His legs nearly failed him there. He caught himself against the wall and stood, breathing, listening.

Voices. Two of them, low, from the kitchen proper. One belonged to someone moving — the small sounds of work, a knife against a board, a pot lid set down carefully so it wouldn't clatter. The other voice wasn't speaking yet. It was just breathing, and then it stopped breathing, and Bren knew he'd been heard.

He could have run. His body wanted to run; every muscle sang with it. But his shoulder was pouring warmth down his arm, and the city outside was a closed fist, and running was how wolves died alone.

Instead he stepped around the corner with his hands open and empty.

The kitchen was vast and dim, hung with copper. At the far end, near the banked ovens, stood a figure in servant's grey — slight, plain-faced, hair bound back without vanity, holding a carving knife in a grip that said they knew exactly where to put it. Behind them, half-hidden by a flour shelf, a young woman in a scullery apron stared at Bren with eyes gone wide as coins.

"You're bleeding on my floor," the knife-holder said.

It took Bren a moment to place the voice — the queen's food-taster, the one the kitchen girls called the Grey Ghost, who ate death three times a day and never blinked. He'd seen them once across a banquet hall, unremarkable as furniture.

"I'll bleed quieter," Bren said, "if you'll point me at a door."

"There are guards at every door."

"Then I'll bleed slower."

Something moved behind the taster's face — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. They glanced back at the scullery girl. "Sefa. The morning loaves won't watch themselves."

"They're watching *him*," the girl — Sefa — whispered, but she went, though she went slowly, pausing at the door to look at Bren with an expression he couldn't read: pity, or recognition, or both. "Don't die in here," she told him. "It's bad luck for the bread." Then she was gone, and her footsteps faded up the stair, and Bren and the taster were alone with the ovens' dying heat.

The taster set down the knife. Deliberately. Where Bren could see it.

"My name is Isandro," they said. "You're the wolf they pulled out of the fish market an hour ago. Half the garrison is drawing your ears from memory."

"Flatterers. They're better than that." Bren eased himself down onto a flour barrel before his knees decided the matter for him. "Isandro. That's a southern name."

"It's a dead name, if the criers are to be believed." Isandro crossed the kitchen, unhurried, and knelt to look at the wound with a professional eye. Their hands were cool and certain. "Crossbow?"

"River gate. I was in a hurry."

"You were shot."

"I was in a hurry *and* shot. The two aren't mutually exclusive." He hissed as Isandro probed the entry. "Careful. That shoulder has to last me."

"It has to last you longer than the night, or nothing I do matters." Isandro rose, fetched a basin, clean linen, a clay pot whose smell hit Bren like a memory — yarrow and honey and something sharper underneath, something that made the pain recede before it ever touched skin. "Hold still."

"You're good at this."

"I'm good at a great many things I'm not supposed to be." Isandro worked in silence a moment, cleaning, packing, binding, and Bren watched their hands and thought: *these are not a servant's hands.* There was a precision in them that belonged to scribes and surgeons and, he realized, looking at the faint ink stains along the fingers, to something else entirely.

"You've got sigil-ink on you," he said quietly.

Isandro's hands did not stop. Did not even slow. But something in the air between them changed temperature, the way a room changes when a door opens onto winter.

"You mistake me," they said.

"Wolves don't mistake smells. That's wormwood ash and silver-gall and about six other things I've smelled on charms my grandmother used to draw. You reek of craft, friend. Beautifully hidden, but I've got a nose the crown would pay fifty crowns for." He held very still. "And here we are. Both of us what the purge is for, sharing a kitchen at midnight."

For a long moment Isandro said nothing. They tied off the bandage with a surgeon's knot, tucked the end flat, and sat back on their heels. In the oven-light their face was unreadable, which was itself an answer — only people who had practiced being unreadable for years achieved that particular blankness.

"If I gave you to the guard," Isandro said at last, "they'd be here in four minutes. I know because I've timed everything in this palace for eleven years. If I hide you instead, and anyone learns of it, they hang us side by side in the market square." They looked up. "Give me one reason."

Bren considered lying. He was good at lying; shifters learned early that truth was a luxury for those with pack protection, and he'd had none for years. But lying to this person felt like lying to a mirror, and mirrors had a way of answering.

"Because you already decided," he said. "You bandaged me before you asked. People don't do that for strangers they mean to sell."

Isandro exhaled — a long, controlled breath, the kind that had probably saved their life a hundred times in a hundred poisoned dining rooms.

"Sefa will bring broth," they said. "Drink it. Sleep in the cold cellar behind the salt racks; the draft covers scent, and the guard dogs hate the cold. At dawn I'll decide what kind of fool I am."

"And if you decide you're the sensible kind?"

"Then you'll have had a meal and a clean bandage, which is more than the market square would give you." Isandro stood, gathered the bloody linen, and paused. "What's your name, wolf?"

"Bren."

"Just Bren?"

"For now. Names are heavy things to carry alone." He met their eyes. "Yours too, I think. Isandro."

Something flickered there — the ghost of the smile again, or its opposite. Then the taster doused the lamp and left him in the dark with the smell of bread and yarrow, and for the first time since dusk, Bren slept without dreaming of running.

* * *

He woke to Sefa's boot prodding his ribs and a bowl of barley broth steaming in her hands.

"Up," she whispered. "Trouble. Sweep party at the servants' gate. Six of them and a sniffer-hound, and the hound is *very* interested in the corridor."

Bren was on his feet before the sentence ended, shoulder screaming, mind already mapping. "Back way?"

"Through the larder, past the ice house, down the drain stairs. But the yard beyond opens onto the guard court — there's no cover till the coal sheds, and the coal sheds are forty feet of open ground." She thrust the bowl at him. "Eat while you think. Fainting wolves get caught."

He drank. It was good broth, salted right, and he hated how much comfort he took from that.

Boots above. The scrape of the gate. A dog's whine, high and eager.

And then Isandro was there, materializing out of the dimness with a tray in their hands and a look on their face that Bren would remember for the rest of his life: calm as deep water, and underneath it, something moving fast.

"The coal sheds," Isandro murmured. "I can't get you past the yard. But I can make the yard not matter." They set the tray down and began, with terrible steadiness, arranging things on it: a covered dish, a wine flask, two cups. "Sweep parties take refreshment. It's regulation. They'll want to search the kitchens properly, which takes an hour, and hungry men search badly."

"You're going to feed them," Sefa said.

"I'm going to *taste* for them. It's what I'm for." Isandro's voice didn't waver, but their hands, lifting the wine flask, trembled once — a single fine tremor, gone as fast as it came. "There's valerian in this wine. Enough to slow a hound's nose and soften six men's attention. Not enough to harm. I've measured poisons for eleven years; I can measure mercy."

"That's the gift," Bren said softly. "That's what you do with it. You hide it inside service."

"I hide it inside *survival*. Service is the shape the hiding takes." Isandro met his eyes, and Bren saw it plainly now — the exhaustion under the calm, years deep, the vigilance of a person who had never once been allowed to be what they were. "If this works, you owe me a door out of this city. Can you find one?"

"I can find one," Bren said. "The river culverts under the tannery quarter. Old smuggler runs. My nose knows them even if my eyes don't."

"Then here's my price." Isandro drew a breath. "Not coin. Not favor. Information. You're mountain-born — I can hear it in your vowels, and you flinched when the criers mentioned Thornfell. Everyone flinches at Thornfell. But you flinched like someone who'd *been* there." Their voice dropped. "The court beneath the mountain. The one that buys things. Do you know its doors?"

The question landed in Bren's chest like a dropped stone. He thought of his grandmother's stories, told low by winter fires: the doors older than the crown, the regent who paid in wishes and collected in souls. He thought of why he'd really fled the high country, and the pack that had branded him traitor in absentia, and the sister whose name he hadn't spoken aloud in six years.

"I know some of them," he said. "Old ones. Sealed ones. Doors the crown forgot and the mountain didn't."

"Then we have a bargain to make after all." Isandro picked up the tray. "Survive tonight, wolf. We'll talk prices at dawn."

They walked toward the light of the gate, spine straight, tray level, the perfect servant carrying refreshment to tired men — and Bren watched them go and understood that he was looking at the bravest coward he had ever met, or the most cowardly brave man, and that the difference didn't matter because both of them were already damned.

The sweep party drank the wine. The hound circled the corridor twice, whined, and lay down with its head on its paws. Six men searched the kitchens for an hour with the thorough, amiable slowness of the pleasantly drunk, and found nothing but flour and order.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the ice-house vent, and with it came Isandro, carrying a lantern turned low and a face that had made its decision overnight.

"The culverts," they said, without preamble. "Show me."

They went together — Isandro, Bren, and Sefa, who refused with quiet ferocity to be left behind, walking the drain stairs down into the dark where the city's old bones showed through: brick laid by masons three kings dead, arches furred with moss, water talking to itself in the black.

At the third junction Bren stopped and breathed. Rat, rot, river — and under it, faint as a prayer, the green smell of open country. North. Toward Thornfell.

"This way leads out," he said. "Two days' walk underground, then the fells." He turned. "But hear my whole price before you step another foot, because it isn't information anymore."

Isandro waited.

"When I'm out of the city, I'm going north anyway. To the mountain. There's—" His throat tightened. "There are souls in that court that don't belong to it. Mine knows two of them by heart. I was always going. But alone, I'm a wolf with a hole in his shoulder knocking on doors older than kingdoms. With you—" He gestured at them, at the ink-stained fingers, the careful hands, the whole buried architecture of their hidden self. "With you, I might actually get one open."

"And what do I get?" Isandro's voice was steady, but Sefa had gone very still beside them, and Bren saw that the taster's hand had found hers without either of them seeming to notice.

"A road only I can smell. Doors only you can read. And when we come out the other side — whatever we come out as — a witness." Bren held their gaze. "You've spent eleven years being no one. I'm offering you the chance to be someone *somewhere no one's watching*. That's worth more than any door, and I know it, because I lost mine."

Silence, except the water. Somewhere far above, the city woke to another day of edicts and informants and fear, and none of it reached down here.

Then Isandro laughed — short, startled, real — and it transformed their whole face, and Bren thought: *there you are.*

"A wolf and a taster," Isandro said. "One hunted for his blood, one for her craft, walking into the one place in the world that eats both." They extended their hand, ink-stained and steady now. "My grandmother used to say the mountain keeps what the valley throws away. Perhaps it's time we introduced ourselves."

Bren took the hand. Her grip was stronger than it looked.

Behind them, Sefa cleared her throat. "I hate to interrupt the dramatic vows," she said, "but the guard rotation changes in twenty minutes, and Rook Vane's men use this culvert on Thursdays."

Both of them stared at her.

"Rook *Vane*?" Bren said. "The smuggler? You know Rook Vane?"

"Everyone in the kitchens knows Rook Vane," Sefa said, with the mild tone of someone stating that everyone knew the weather. "He bribes the pastry cook weekly and flirts terribly. And if you're going north through Thornfell, you'll need him, because the passes are already snowing and his are the only maps that don't lie." She folded her arms. "So. Are we fetching him, or are the two of you planning to smell your way over a mountain in winter?"

Bren looked at Isandro. Isandro looked at Bren.

Above them, muffled by thirty feet of stone, a crier began the morning proclamation — the purge list, the rewards, the names — and the sound slid down through the dark like a blade finding its sheath.

"We fetch him," Isandro said.

And Bren, turning north into the black water's smell, felt for the first time since the bolt took him in the shoulder that the running had stopped — that it had become, instead, a hunt, and that for once in his long lonely life, he was not the one being chased.
