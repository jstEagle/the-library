## 4. Sefa Taken

The city smelled of ash for three days after the edict.

Not the honest ash of hearth-fires — that smell meant bread, warmth, someone's supper waiting — but the thin, chemical reek of burned paper and burned things that had once been precious. It drifted down from the Temple Quarter where the criers had built their pyres of confiscated books, sigil-boards, painted charms, a gifted child's wooden alphabet blocks with letters that rearranged themselves when no one watched. The wind carried it into the palace kitchens, into the sculleries, into every corner where people like me had learned to make ourselves small.

I carried the smell with me into the third morning, along with everything else I could not put down.

Sefa's door was on Tallow Lane, in the crooked shadow of the tannery wall, behind a courtyard where someone had tried to grow beans in a barrel and failed. I knew the way there the way I knew the paths through Thornfell: by heart, by habit, by the particular rhythm of my own feet on stones I had walked a hundred times before dawn. I went before the city woke properly, hood up, hands in my sleeves, one of the last three sigil-tools I had not burned hidden against my ribs where it lay like a coal that refused to go cold.

A stylus of blackthorn wood, no longer than my smallest finger. Worthless to anyone who couldn't use it. Everything to me.

I told myself I was going to say goodbye.

That was a lie, and I was good enough at tasting lies to know my own when it sat bitter on my tongue. I was going to ask her to run. South, past the salt marshes, over the border into Vessany where the queen's writ thinned to nothing. I had coin saved against exactly this catastrophe — eleven years of wages hoarded in a hollowed-out cheese wheel under my cot, because a food-taster who might need to flee at any hour learns to keep her purse light and her escape funded. I would give her all of it. I would tell her about Bren, hidden in the old ice-house beneath the kitchen gardens, about Rook Vane and the mountain roads, about the plan already assembling itself in the dark corners of my mind like frost forming on glass.

And if she refused — if Sefa, stubborn as her namesake saffron, expensive and impossible to cultivate — then I would stay another day. One more day. Just until she was safe.

The lie tasted so sweet going down that I almost believed it.

* * *

Tallow Lane was wrong before I reached it.

I registered the wrongness the way I registered poison: not as a thought but as a flinch, a tightening behind the teeth, the body knowing before the mind consented to know. Too quiet. The tannery yard should have been loud by now — the scrapers out at first light, the dogs complaining, the tanners' boys hauling buckets of lime water that stank to the second story. Instead the lane held only fog off the river and a silence so complete it had texture, like felt laid over the world.

Then I saw the door.

Sefa's door was green. Had been green for as long as I'd known her — a bad green, a green mixed from whatever pigment the chandler's son had left over, peeling in long curls that Sefa kept promising to scrape and never did. She said the ugly color kept away tax collectors and suitors alike, and she was probably right; nothing about that door invited approach.

It stood open now. Not broken — open, politely, deliberately, the way a door stands when its owner has been escorted through it and has not been permitted to close what she leaves behind.

On the threshold, weighted with a stone, lay a sheet of vellum.

I should have walked away. Every instinct honed across eleven years of banquets and slow poisons and the long education of staying alive screamed at me to turn around, to fold myself back into the fog, to become again the plain-faced servant whom no one troubled to remember. The edict was four days old. Informants were being paid in silver by the pound. Any figure lingering at a seized doorway was a figure worth reporting.

I picked up the paper.

The seal was the crown's — the crowned tower, pressed deep and slightly crooked, the seal of a clerk who worked late and cared little. But the hand beneath it was not a clerk's formality. It was a magistrate's hand, fat and confident, each letter fed and groomed, and I recognized it because I had seen it before on documents that crossed the queen's table at the autumn assizes. Magistrate Corvel Hane. Assessor of Debts and Forfeitures, Third Circuit. A man whose jowls I had once watched tremble over a plate of quail I had tasted for him personally.

*By order of the Assessor of Debts,* the document began, *and pursuant to the Crown's outstanding obligations to the Court Beneath the Mountain, the following person is taken into custody as surety—*

I read it twice. Three times. My lips moved on the fourth reading, though no sound came, because some part of me believed that if I spoke the words aloud they would become real, and some other part believed that if I could not speak them they were not.

*—one Sefa Anouk Ilves, kitchen-woman, of no recorded gift, assessed at fair market value per Schedule Nine, her soul held in trust as collateral against the Crown's winter debt until such time as payment is rendered or the thaw releases obligation.*

No recorded gift.

She wasn't seized under the Purge. She wasn't taken for magic, for sigils, for anything she was. She was taken as *currency*. Weighed on a schedule, priced like grain or tin, her soul — her laughing, scheming, saffron-stubborn soul — entered into a ledger as a line item against debts the crown had run up buying weapons it was too proud to admit it needed.

The paper shook in my hands. I let it shake. There was no one in the fog to see, and for once I did not care who might.

At the bottom, in smaller script, the terms: *Surety to be conveyed to the Court Beneath the Mountain before first frost. Release upon settlement or seasonal opening, whichever comes later.* Whichever came *later*. The gates sealed for a generation — everyone in the trade roads whispered it, Rook Vane had confirmed it with the flat certainty of a man quoting freight rates. If the crown did not pay by first frost, Sefa's soul would sit in a lantern-glass beneath the mountain until the seals broke, decades hence, and souls dimmed in captivity. Everyone knew that too. The smugglers' songs said the lanterns went dark like lamps running out of oil, and what woke at the end of a generation was not always what went in.

First frost was nineteen days away.

* * *

I do not remember crossing the courtyard. I remember the bean barrel, tipped over, its failed crop scattered and frozen brown. I remember the door standing open onto a room stripped with terrible efficiency — not ransacked, *inventoried*. Her mother's quilt gone from the bed. The copper kettle gone from the hook. Even the little clay pot of saffron she kept on the sill, grown from threads she'd smuggled home in her cheek for two years, stolen from the palace kitchens thread by golden thread — even that was gone, logged somewhere, valued, scheduled.

What remained told the story better than the magistrate's hand ever could. A chair set neatly back under the table. A cup washed and dried, left upside down on the shelf, because Sefa could never leave a cup dirty even while being arrested. And on the floor by the cold hearth, overlooked or deemed worthless, a single shoe — her left, the one with the mended strap, the one she always complained pinched.

I picked it up and held it and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that I had done this.

Not Corvel Hane with his fat confident hand. Not the crown with its schedules and circuits. Me. Because the purge had come and I had thought of survival first — mine, then Bren's, then the grand architecture of flight — and Sefa had been folded into the plan as an afterthought, a stop to make on the way out of the city, a goodbye to say before the road took me. I had spent four days building escape routes and had not spent one hour asking what the purge would mean for a woman with no gift to hide, no sigils to burn, no reason to think the storm would want anything from her.

The law had found a use for her anyway. The law always finds a use.

Justice would have said: this woman owes nothing, took nothing, harmed no one; leave her be. Justice was not consulted. Justice had not been consulted in this kingdom for a very long time, and I had known it — I had *tasted* it, course after course, year after year, watching men like Hane grow fat on the queen's table while the ledgers underneath grew red — and I had told myself that a food-taster's silence was neutrality, was professionalism, was simply the shape my survival took.

My silence had a price too. It was written on Schedule Nine. It was sitting in a lantern-glass under a mountain, dimming.

I sat down in Sefa's chair, at Sefa's table, in Sefa's stripped and orderly room, and I let the grief come the way I let nothing else come: completely, silently, without taste or measure. I wept until the fog outside the window turned grey to white to grey again, and when it was done I was empty in the particular way that is not emptiness at all but a clearing — ground burned back to soil, ready or not for whatever was planted next.

Then I stood, and set her shoe gently on the table beside the magistrate's crooked seal, and made a vow with no ceremony at all, because ceremony was for people whose vows might be witnessed.

*I will bring you home. Before first frost. Whatever it costs, whoever it costs — including me.*

* * *

Rook Vane kept his accounts in a room above a dye-house in Saltcote, where the reek of mordant covered every other smell and no one asked questions about visitors who preferred it that way. I had leverage on him filed like knives: three routes through Thornfell he did not want the crown to know he ran, and my eleven years of knowing it. Blackmail is an ugly word. I preferred to think of it as calling in a debt, which was, I was learning, the only language this season spoke.

He opened the door himself, small and quick and grey as driftwood, and read my face in the single glance of a man who survives by reading faces.

"No," he said immediately.

"You don't know what I'm going to ask."

"I know what you look like." He stepped aside anyway, because men like Rook always step aside in the end; calculation only delays them. "Come up, then. And wipe your boots."

In the lamplit room above the dye-house, surrounded by ledgers, I told him everything. The edict. Bren in the ice-house. The magistrate's seal and Schedule Nine and the nineteen days. He listened the way smugglers listen, motionless, eyes doing the arithmetic his mouth refused.

"The court beneath the mountain doesn't sell those souls back," he said quietly. "You know that. Collateral isn't ransom. They hold it until the debt clears or the holder stops caring, and crowns stop caring the moment the ledger turns."

"Then I'll take it back."

His laugh was short and had no humor in it anywhere. "With what army? With what gift?" His eyes flicked to my hands, quick as a pickpocket's, and I realized too late that grief had loosened something in me — that I had spoken of sigils and theft in the same breath, and Rook Vane missed nothing that touched his trade. "Ah," he said softly. "So that's what you are."

I said nothing. Eleven years of saying nothing. But the silence had changed weight, and we both felt it.

Rook turned to his shelves, ran a finger along the spines of his false-ledgers, and stopped. When he faced me again his face had closed like a door — but doors, I was learning, close over rooms, and rooms hold things.

"Your friend's soul won't be the only lantern down there," he said. "There's one with my brother's name on the glass. I left him to it eight years ago because the arithmetic said one life against a whole route, and I've done that sum every night since and gotten a different answer every time." He pulled a ledger free and dropped it on the table between us with a slap like a struck bell. "Nineteen days. First frost. You get me through the passes, taster, and I'll get you through the doors. That's the bargain. That's the whole of it."

Outside, far off toward the palace, a crier began the evening proclamation, the words dissolving into ash-smell and fog before they reached us. Somewhere beneath a mountain, a lantern dimmed a fraction more.

I put out my hand, and Rook Vane — who trusted no one, who had taught me half of what I knew about distrust — shook it.
