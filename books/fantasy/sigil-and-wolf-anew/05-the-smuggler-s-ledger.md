## 5. The Smuggler's Ledger

The Saltcellar smelled of tar and old lies.

Isandro had not been inside it in three years — three years of careful distance, of keeping Rook Vane's name out of their mouth the way one keeps a splinter unprobed — but the smell had not changed. Pitch and brine soaked into black oak beams. Tallow smoke with a thread of something sweeter beneath it, poppy resin maybe, or the burnt sugar Rook liked to chew when he was thinking. The lamps were still shuttered green, so that every face in the room looked drowned. Somewhere behind the bar a kettle muttered to itself like an old woman who no longer expected anyone to listen.

Three years ago Isandro had walked into this room as a stranger with a ledger's worth of knowledge in their head. Tonight they walked in as a fugitive with ash still under their fingernails from burning their own tools, and the difference felt smaller than it should have.

Rook sat at his usual table in the back corner, the one with sightlines to both doors and the cellar hatch. He was a big man gone soft in the wrong places and hard in the surprising ones — a belly like a flour sack over forearms that could crack a walnut between finger and thumb without looking. His beard was oiled to a point. Rings on six fingers, none of them precious metal, all of them valuable. He was playing cards with two men Isandro didn't know, losing on purpose, which meant he was buying something from them: information, patience, or silence.

He saw Isandro before they'd crossed half the room. Of course he did. Rook Vane missed nothing that happened inside the Saltcellar; it was the only country he fully ruled.

"Well," he said, and laid down his hand face-up, folding without shame. "The queen's own tongue walks into my parlor smelling of woodsmoke and fear. Sit down before someone remembers your face."

"Someone will remember my face regardless."

"Then sit where I can watch them remembering." He kicked a stool out from under the table with one boot. To the card players: "Out. Take your losses and your gossip to the docks, both are stale." They went, one of them giving Isandro a long look that Isandro catalogued and dismissed — a dockhand, third shift, more interested in coin than crowns. Not a threat tonight. Possibly a threat tomorrow. Everything was possibly a threat tomorrow now.

When they were alone, Rook poured two fingers of plum brandy without asking whether Isandro wanted any. He knew they didn't. He poured it anyway, because the ritual mattered to him, because a man who ran contraband through nine mountain passes needed rituals the way a ship needed ballast.

"So," he said. "They've taken Sefa."

The name landed like a fishhook behind Isandro's breastbone. They kept their face still. Years of tasting for a queen who poisoned rivals over dessert had taught them stillness the way other people learned prayers.

"You know that already."

"I know everything that moves through this city worth knowing, little taster. A magistrate named Corvel signed her transfer papers four days ago. Debt settlement, crown to mountain. Very tidy. Very legal." He said *legal* the way other men said *syphilitic*. "Her soul goes under Thornfell as surety until spring thaw. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement."

"Then you know what I'm going to ask you for."

Rook drank. Over the rim of his cup his eyes were small and brown and patient as a spider's. "I know what you're going to *offer*, is what interests me. Because you didn't come here to beg. Begging isn't in you. You came here to hold something over me, and I confess I'm curious which knife you think you've got." He set the cup down. "So show me the blade, Isandro Okonkwo. I'll tell you if it's sharp enough."

Isandro reached into their coat and drew out a folded paper, worn soft at the creases from years of carrying. They did not open it. They simply laid it on the table between the brandy bottle and Rook's rings, and let him look at it, and watched his face do the small terrible arithmetic it had been waiting three years to do.

It was not a map. Maps could be burned, maps could be denied. It was better than a map. It was a list — forty-one entries in Isandro's neat, servant-invisible hand, each one a date, a shipment description, and a route reference keyed to numbers only Isandro could read. Forty-one times Rook Vane had moved goods through the Thornfell passes that no smuggler was supposed to find twice. Forty-one times he had bought his safety not with bribes but with a single payment made once, long ago: the promise that Isandro would keep silent about how he found his roads.

And beneath the forty-one entries, in the same hand, a single line:

*Routes reconstructed in full. Originals destroyed.*

"Where did you—" Rook began, and stopped, because he already knew. He had known for three years. That was the poison in it. He had paid Isandro's silence knowing the silence was only ever rented, never sold.

"The passes aren't hidden," Isandro said quietly. "That's the thing you never understood. They're *smelled*. Wolf-paths, old shifter roads, ways that follow water and wind instead of stone. You can't map them because they move with the seasons. But they can be learned. And I have spent seven years learning them, Rook, because learning things is what I do. What I was made to do. What I am."

The green lamplight made his face swamp-colored. For a long moment the only sound was the kettle muttering and, distantly, rain beginning against the shutters.

"You're gifted," Rook said. Not a question. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man stepping across ice. "That's why the criers can't hang you yet. That's why you're sitting in my parlor instead of a cell. You hid it well enough to taste for the queen herself." He laughed, short and without humor. "Gods. All those banquets. All those dishes you waved away. Half the court thought you were fastidious."

"Half the court thought I was a eunuch with a delicate stomach. Let them."

"And now you want passage through Thornfell. My passes. The ones you're holding hostage with that paper." He tapped the list once, lightly, as if it might bite. "Blackmail. From you. I'd be hurt if I weren't so impressed."

"It's not blackmail." Isandro turned the paper around and slid it toward him. "Read the last line again. *Originals destroyed.* I'm not selling you silence, Rook. I'm spending it. Once we're through the high pass and behind the sealed gates, that paper is worthless to anyone but you — proof of nothing, leverage over no one. I'm handing you back your own secret as payment. That's not a knife. That's a receipt."

Rook stared at them. Then he did something Isandro had seen perhaps twice in seven years of acquaintance: he laughed properly, from the belly, loud enough that the bartender flinched behind his taps.

"A receipt," he wheezed. "Oh, you cold-blooded little serpent. Seven years I wondered what I'd gotten myself into, hiring a food-taster's discretion, and it turns out I hired the most honest extortionist in the Nine Cities." He wiped his eyes. "Fine. Fine. You want the wolf-roads, you get the wolf-roads. There's a cache at Greyfall Bend, storm-proof, stocked. There's a toll-keeper on the Serpentwater you'll need to treat gently. And there's—"

He stopped.

It was a small stop, a hitch no one who hadn't spent seven years reading faces across banquet tables would have caught. But Isandro caught it, and leaned forward, and did the thing they did best: tasted the air of the conversation and found the flavor underneath.

"There's something you want too," Isandro said. "You didn't agree that fast out of admiration. Admiration doesn't pour brandy. Talk."

Rook's jaw worked. He looked at the green lamps, at the rain, at his own six ringed fingers spread on the table like a fortune-teller's spread. When he spoke again the performance had drained out of his voice, and what remained was older and tireder.

"My brother," he said. "Tammas. Tammas Vane."

Isandro waited. Waiting was a skill. Most people couldn't bear four seconds of it.

"He was gifted. Nothing dramatic — he could make plants grow out of season, coax a garden through frost. Green-thumb work. Village magic. The kind the old laws ignored." Rook's thumb moved over one of his rings, around and around. "First year of the queen's reign, before the Purge had a name, there was a levy. Crown debts, mountain debts, the whole rotten ledger nobody talks about. They took people from the villages. Quietly. Paid the magistrates in tax forgiveness and called it resettlement. Tammas was twenty-two. He grew roses in December. Somebody talked."

The rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked and both of them went still, then eased.

"They took him under the mountain," Isandro said. It wasn't a question anymore either. The shape of it had been assembling itself since the moment Isandro had found Sefa's empty cot, since Bren had spoken of the court beneath Thornfell in a voice like a man describing a dream he wished he'd never had. Souls as currency. Debts paid in persons. A kingdom's rot running downhill into the dark.

"Fourteen years ago," Rook said. "I was nineteen and I did nothing. I told myself he was dead, that the mountain eats what it takes, that a man with one brother and no gift should keep his head down and his routes profitable. And I have kept my head down for fourteen years, taster, and I have been *very* profitable." He looked up, and in the drowned green light his eyes were wet and furious and ashamed, all three at once, which was its own kind of honesty. "And then a kitchen maid gets taken the same way, by the same machinery, and a poison-taster walks into my parlor holding my life's secrets in one hand and offering them back to me like they're nothing, and I think: fourteen years. Fourteen years of telling myself the mountain can't be robbed."

"Can it?"

"No idea." He smiled, and it was a bad smile, a cracked-door smile. "But I know this: the court buys souls because souls hold value. Value means accounting. Accounting means ledgers. And I am very, very good with ledgers." He pulled the list toward himself, unfolded it, and studied Isandro's neat hand for a long moment. "If Sefa's soul is collateral, it's written down somewhere. If it's written down, it can be found. If it can be found—"

"It can be stolen," Isandro finished.

"*Recovered,*" Rook corrected. "Let's use the pretty word while we're still above ground."

* * *

They worked through the night, and the work was its own strange comfort.

This was the part Isandro understood — the part that fit their hands like a well-worn spoon. Lists within lists. Rook produced a second book, older, bound in grey leather gone bald at the corners, and together they cross-referenced: Rook's routes against the seasons, the cache locations against the snowline, the toll-points against the days when the river spirits were traditionally placated and the days when they were traditionally hungry. Isandro memorized faster than Rook could write, which annoyed him enormously and which he pretended not to notice.

"Wolf-paths first," Rook said, tapping the high passes. "Your shifter friend — and yes, I heard about the wolf you dragged out of the kitchens, the whole street heard about it — he'll smell the turns before you see them. Trust the nose over the eyes. Eyes lie in the mountains. Noses don't."

"Bren knows the court's old doors," Isandro said. "He said there are ways in that predate the regent. Ways the wardens have forgotten."

"Wardens forget nothing. Wardens just get lazy, and laziness looks like forgetting after a hundred years." Rook blew out a breath. "Greyfall Bend cache has rope, iron rations, and a coat that will fit your friend if he's not too broad through the shoulders. Wolves usually are. After that—" his finger traced a line up the Serpentwater gorge, over the toll-mark, into a blank space where even his ink gave up, "—after that you're past anything I can draw for you. Past anything anyone's drawn. You'll be navigating by whatever gift you carry and whatever luck you've hoarded."

"I don't hoard luck."

"No," Rook agreed. "You hoard secrets. Same currency, better exchange rate."

Near dawn the rain turned to sleet, ticking against the shutters like small impatient fingers, and Rook called for food — real food, bread and hard cheese and pickled onions, brought up by a boy who looked at nothing and was tipped generously for it. Isandro ate mechanically, tasting each thing out of habit, finding nothing in any of it but food. It was the first meal in seven years they had eaten without checking. The realization arrived strangely: not freedom exactly, but the absence of a weight they'd carried so long they'd mistaken it for their own bones.

"Question," Rook said around a mouthful of cheese. "The paper. Your list. Why give it back? You could have held it forever. Kept me leashed for life. That's what I'd have done."

Isandro considered the question honestly, because Rook had earned one honest answer and because lying to a business partner before entering a mountain together was poor hygiene.

"Because I spent seven years being a hidden thing," they said. "Hidden in plain sight, hidden in habit, hidden even from the people I—" They stopped. Sefa's name rose in them like floodwater. "Hidden from everyone. And hiding works, right up until it doesn't, and then it kills you along with everyone you were hiding for. I'm done paying rent on my own life, Rook. The list goes back to you because keeping it would just be another way of staying buried."

Rook chewed on that longer than he'd chewed the cheese. "Sefa," he said finally. "She's the one. The reason you're doing all this."

"She's the reason I'm still a person. Everything else is logistics."

"Hm." He gathered the grey book, the list, the maps, squaring their edges with the fussiness of a man arranging his own courage. "My mother used to say the mountain court runs on debts the way a mill runs on water. Every soul that goes in turns a wheel. Every wheel grinds something." He stood, joints cracking, and looked down at Isandro with the expression of a man about to do something unforgivably stupid and fully aware of it. "Time to go see what our souls are grinding."

"One condition," Isandro said, rising too. "In the mountain — if it comes to choosing between Sefa's lantern and Tammas's—"

"Don't." Rook held up a ringed hand. "Don't insult us both. We choose when we're standing in front of them, not before. That's the only honest arithmetic there is." He pulled his coat from a peg, heavy as a burial shawl, and settled it on his shoulders. "Greyfall Bend is four days' walk if the weather holds and six if it sulks. We leave within the hour, ahead of the patrols, and we don't stop to be heroes on the way."

Outside, the sleet had thickened toward snow — early, too early, the first white breath of a winter arriving ahead of schedule, as if the mountain already knew they were coming and had begun closing its accounts.

Rook locked the Saltcellar door behind them with the tenderness of a man locking a coffin, and the three of them — taster, smuggler, and the wolf waiting shadowed in the alley mouth with snow already gathering on his shoulders — turned north, toward Thornfell, toward the doors beneath the root, and toward the long ledger of the mountain court, where somewhere in its pages a kitchen maid's name was written in ink that Isandro intended, whatever it cost, to burn.
