## 6. Into Thornfell

The first snow came down like a held breath finally released.

Bren smelled it before he saw it — that iron-and-clean scent rolling off the high crags, the smell of the world being washed and buried at once. He stood at the treeline where the last of Thornfell's foothill pines gave way to scree and shadow, and he let it settle on his shoulders, on his bare hands, on the old scar across his collarbone where a wolf's tooth had gone in and a man's knife had finished the argument.

Behind him, Isandro crouched over their pack, checking straps for the third time. Rook Vane leaned against a boulder with the expression of a man who had been cheated by every landscape he'd ever crossed and expected this one to be no different.

"You're smiling," Isandro said without looking up. "That worries me."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your voice is smiling. It's very unsettling coming out of a face like yours."

Rook snorted. "Leave him be. Wolves grin when they're about to do something stupid. It's how you tell them apart from sensible animals."

Bren turned his face up into the falling snow and let it melt there. *Stupid.* Perhaps. The mountain ahead of them was a country of teeth — purge patrols in the low passes, bounty-men in the valleys, and above all of it the cold itself, patient as a debt-collector. The maps called these peaks impassable past first frost. That was precisely why he'd chosen them. Maps were written by people who walked roads, and roads were written by people who wanted to be found.

"We go up through the Hollow Spine," he said. "There's a path the maps don't have. My nose knows it even if my eyes don't anymore." He tapped the side of his head, where the pack-scent-memory lived, layered like sediment: pine-musk and blood and his mother's ruff, the whole archive of a life he'd been exiled from. "Wolf-paths. They don't follow water or stone the way men think. They follow *smell*. Wind-carries, game-trails, the warm seams under the rock where the earth breathes out. A shifter can read them. A man can't. And a patrol—" he smiled properly now, all teeth, "—a patrol walks where its horses can."

"And if your nose is wrong?" Rook asked.

"Then we die somewhere beautiful and unmapped, and no one ever finds our bones. There are worse fates. I've been threatened with several."

Isandro stood, shouldered the pack, and looked up at the mountain the way Bren had seen them look at the queen's banquet table: cataloguing, measuring, unimpressed. "Then let's be about it. Every hour we stand here, Sefa's lantern dims a little more."

They climbed.

* * *

By the second day, Bren understood that he had brought two entirely different creatures into the high country, and that both of them were going to surprise him.

Rook Vane complained. That was expected; complaining was the man's native tongue, and he spoke it fluently in three registers — grievance, lamentation, and prophecy of doom. What was not expected was how *well* he moved. For all his grumbling, the smuggler's feet found purchase where Bren's eyes found nothing, and he read weather off the wind with an accuracy that made Bren's hackles prickle with something adjacent to respect. Twice Rook redirected them around cornices that groaned under the snow-load, and twice the cornices came down behind them in long white thunder.

Isandro, though. Isandro was the one who unsettled him.

The taster climbed like someone who had decided, calmly and in advance, that exhaustion was a thing that happened to other people. They ate little, slept less, and watched everything — not with fear, but with the particular hunger of a person memorizing a place. On the second evening, while Rook cursed a frozen bootlace and Bren scouted the ridge, Isandro knelt at the base of a split boulder and simply *looked* at it for a long time, snow gathering in their dark hair, and when Bren came back they were tracing something in the air above the stone. Not touching. Just following lines only they could see.

"What is it?" Bren asked.

"A door," Isandro said, "that isn't a door. Or a door that hasn't decided to be one yet." They stood, brushed the snow from their knees, and said nothing else about it.

Bren did not press. He knew what it was to carry a self you couldn't show anyone — he'd spent four years doing exactly that, folding his wolf down small inside his skin, walking through cities on a man's legs with a wildness beating against his ribs like a bird in a box. He recognized the posture. He recognized the loneliness underneath it.

But he also noticed what Isandro didn't say, which was: *I can't use it here.* Not *won't*. *Can't.* As if the gift weren't a tool but a wound, and opening it in front of witnesses would bleed them both.

He filed that away, the way wolves file things away, and kept climbing.

The third day, the world went white.

Not snowfall — fog, a living fog that poured down the pass like milk into tea, erasing the ridgelines, the sky, the difference between up and down. Sound went strange and close. Bren stopped relying on his eyes entirely and dropped to one knee, pressing his palm flat to the cold stone, letting his nose do the work it had been born for.

"This way," he said. "Off the trail. Down and left, along the warm seam."

"The what?" Rook demanded.

"The mountain breathes. Feel it." He took Rook's hand — the smuggler flinched, then allowed it — and pressed it to a crevice in the rockface. From deep within came a faint exhalation, air warmed by whatever furnace the mountain kept beneath its bones, carrying the mineral smell of deep places. "Every range has them. Cracks where the heat leaks out. Game follows them in winter. So did my pack, before—" He stopped. Before the purge. Before he'd run and left them to it. Before the word *traitor* got carved into his name in a language only wolves could read.

"Before," Rook finished for him, and for once didn't joke.

They moved into the seam-country, a labyrinth of stone ribs and breathing cracks where the fog thinned and the snow lay shallow because the ground refused to let it sleep. Bren led with his chin up and his mouth slightly open, tasting the air the way Isandro tasted wine: for what shouldn't be in it.

Which was why he caught it, half a day later, while the other two still believed themselves safe.

Woodsmoke. Leather. Horse sweat and oiled steel and — underneath, faint but unmistakable, carried on a wind-shift from the south — *iron filings and consecrated ash*, the specific stink of purge-charms, the talismans the queen's witch-finders wore to make gifted flesh itch and burn.

Bren froze so abruptly that Isandro walked into his back.

"What?"

"Patrol." His voice came out flat and low, the wolf's register, and he felt both of them go still behind him. "Six, maybe seven. Below us, maybe half a mile, moving up the main pass. They've got hounds."

"Hounds," Rook repeated. "Of course they have hounds. Why would the crown send anything into mountains without dogs? Dogs find everything."

"Not me." Bren bared his teeth at the fog. "Dogs are afraid of wolves. Even city-bred curs carry the memory. But they're not afraid of *you* two, and your tracks are all over this seam like a confession."

The wind shifted again, and this time it brought something worse than smoke: a horn-note, thin and clear, rising from the pass below. Then another, answering from the east. Coordinating. Sweeping.

"They've picked something up," Isandro said quietly. "Maybe just spoor. Maybe us."

"Then we move." Bren turned downhill — not toward the patrol, but obliquely, cutting across the slope through a field of broken slabs where their scent would tangle and scatter. "Fast and quiet. If we reach the ridge before they close the net—"

The net closed first.

It came out of the fog like the fog deciding to have edges: a mounted rider, then two more, materializing twenty yards downslope with the terrible casualness of men who had done this many times. Chainmail under grey cloaks. Crossbows, already levelled. And at the lead rider's throat, glinting dully, a witch-finder's charm — a little cage of black iron with a sliver of something inside it that *hurt* to look at, that made the air around it feel like a struck match held near the skin.

Bren heard Isandro's breath catch — heard the small involuntary sound of a nerve being touched — and understood in one cold flash that the charm was working. That Isandro, standing right beside him, was feeling exactly what the queen's instruments were designed to make gifted people feel.

"*Halt!*" the lead rider called, muffled and enormous in the fog. "In the queen's name! You three — names and business!"

Rook's hand drifted toward his belt-knife with the smoothness of long habit. Bren's muscles began the old familiar rearrangement, bones remembering their other shape, the wolf rising in him like water finding its level—

And Isandro stepped forward.

Later, Bren would try to reconstruct those few seconds and never quite manage it. He remembered the fog, thick as wool. He remembered the crossbows, three black mouths. He remembered thinking, with absurd clarity: *they'll shoot, they'll shoot, why are they walking TOWARD them—*

And he remembered the light.

It wasn't firelight. It wasn't lightning. It was smaller than either and stranger than both — a sigil drawn in the air with two fingers, quick as a signature, glowing the pale gold of lamplight through honey. Isandro's hand moved and the light *moved with it*, hanging in the fog like a second moon no bigger than a coin, and the witch-finder's charm at the lead rider's throat began, softly, to sing — a high, keening note, the sound of iron being unmade.

The crossbow bolts never flew. The riders' weapons sagged. The lead man clawed at his charm as it came apart in his gauntlet like wet paper, and his horse screamed and wheeled, and in the space of three heartbeats the patrol's discipline dissolved into the fog from which it had come, hooves receding downslope, horns blowing confused and contradictory signals into the white.

Silence. Falling snow. The little gold light guttered, dimmed, and went out.

Isandro stood with their hand still raised, fingers trembling — Bren could see the tremor even at ten feet, could hear the heartbeat hammering in their chest the way he could hear a rabbit's across a meadow. Their face was very pale. Their eyes were wide open, fixed on their own hand as though it belonged to a stranger.

"Well," said Rook Vane, after a moment. "That's going to complicate the ledger."

* * *

They didn't stop until dusk, when the fog burned off into a sky of bruised violet and the temperature fell like a verdict. In the lee of an overhang, Rook built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and they ate cold sausage and hard bread and drank melted snow, and nobody spoke for a long time.

Bren watched Isandro across the flames.

They sat with their knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, staring into the fire with an expression he recognized from mirrors — the look of a person taking inventory of damage. When they finally spoke, their voice was quiet enough that a human ear might have missed it. His didn't.

"I've used that sigil maybe six times in my life," Isandro said. "Always hidden. Always in the dark, always alone, always cleaning up after someone else's poison. I have spent eleven years making sure that no living soul saw me do that." A pause. "And today I did it in front of a purge patrol, a smuggler, and a wolf-shifter, and I don't know whether I want to laugh or be sick."

"Both," Rook offered. "I find both works. Preferably with drink."

"Why?" Bren asked. Not *how* — the how was craft, and craft could wait. *Why.*

Isandro was silent long enough that the fire popped twice. Then: "Because the charm was hurting you."

"It wasn't pointed at me," Bren said. "Witch-finders don't hunt shifters. We're not on the list. Beasts, they call us. Vermin category."

"I know what the list says." Isandro's jaw tightened. "I also know what a purging-charm feels like when it's burning near someone it considers *unclean*, and I felt it hit you like a hot wire the moment that rider cleared the fog. You were about to shift — I could see it starting, your shoulders, your hands — and if you'd shifted inside a charm's radius with seven armed men watching, they wouldn't have taken you alive. Nobody takes a shifter alive. So." They shrugged, a small motion, as if the largest decision of their life had been arithmetic. "The math was simple."

Bren stared at them. Something moved in his chest that had nothing to do with the wolf — something older and more dangerous, the thing that made packs out of strangers and families out of the hunted.

"You exposed yourself," he said slowly, "to save a beast."

"To save *you*." Isandro looked up, and the firelight caught the fury in their face, banked and steady as coals. "Don't you dare quote their categories at me. I've spent eleven years tasting poison so a woman who burns children's letters could sleep soundly. I know exactly what her lists are worth. They're worth the paper she'll hang people from." They looked back at the fire. "Sefa knew what I was for years and never sold me. She used to leave extra honey on my tray on festival days, and neither of us ever said a word about why. That's what loyalty looks like. Not the list. This."

The wind moved through the pass above them, sifting snow through the dark like flour through a sieve. Somewhere far off, a horn sounded again — fainter now, searching in the wrong valley. Rook fed another stick to the fire and said nothing, which from Rook was practically a blessing.

Bren found that his hands had gone still. The wolf, which had been pacing inside him since the patrol, had settled — sat down, almost, ears forward, watching Isandro with the same attention it gave to the one creature in a forest that mattered.

"When we get Sefa back," he said at last, "you should draw that sigil again. Where we can see it. Not for hiding."

Isandro laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty with disuse. "You realize what you're asking. If I'm seen using it openly, every informer between here and the capital will have my description by spring. My name. My face."

"Your face is already known to everyone in this company," Bren said. "And none of us sold you." He held their gaze across the small fire. "That's the pack rule. What's shown is shared. What's shared is defended. I learned it before I lost the right to it. I'm giving it back to you."

For a moment Isandro's composure cracked — not much, just a hairline, just enough for Bren to see the exhausted, terrified, fiercely alive person underneath the servant's plainness. Then they nodded, once, and reached into their coat, and drew out a strip of leather Bren hadn't seen before: a wrist-band, plain and worn, marked with a single faded sigil in old ink.

"Sefa gave me this," they said. "Years ago. She bought it off a peddler who claimed it was a luck-charm. It's not — it's nothing, just ink and hide. But I've worn it every day since, under my sleeve, where no one could see." They fastened it back around their wrist, outside the sleeve this time, in full view of the fire and the dark and the two people watching. "There. Done."

Rook raised his tin cup in a mock toast. "To bad decisions made bravely."

"To honey on festival days," Isandro said.

Bren lifted his cup too, and drank, and let himself — for one breath, one heartbeat — believe they might actually make it.

Then his nose twitched.

He went rigid, cup halfway to his lips, head turning slowly northward into the dark. The wind had shifted again, and threading through the cold and the pine and the distant woodsmoke of the retreating patrol came a scent he had not smelled in four years and had never once forgotten. Musk and juniper and old blood-kinship, carried down from the high pass like a message written in a language older than words.

Wolves. Real ones. Many.

And among them, unmistakable even across miles of night air, a scent he knew better than his own heartbeat: the pack. *His* pack. Moving down out of the heights, straight toward the seam-country, straight toward them.

"Bren?" Isandro said. "What is it?"

He set down the cup. His hands were shaking, and this time it had nothing to do with fear.

"We're about to have company," he said. "And they remember my name."
