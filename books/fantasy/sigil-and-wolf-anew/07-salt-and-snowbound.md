## 7. Salt and Snowbound

The storm found them an hour after dark, the way storms always did in Thornfell — not arriving so much as *remembering* them, as if the mountains had been waiting all along for three fools to climb high enough to be worth burying.

Rook Vane smelled it coming before he heard it. Twenty years of running contraband through these passes had taught his nose more than any weather-witch's glass: a smell like iron filings and old pennies, like the sky sharpening a knife. He'd been about to call a halt anyway when Bren stopped dead on the trail, lifted his head, and said, "Snow. Heavy. Now."

"Then we move," Isandro said.

"No," Rook said, and pointed east, off the trail, toward a wall of granite furred with pine. "We *hide*. There's a cache."

Bren's ears — the wolf ones, the ones that came and went with his moods like weather of their own — pricked forward. "A cache?"

"A smuggler's cache." Rook was already moving, boots finding the invisible path, the one that wasn't a path at all until you knew where to put your feet. "Every man who runs Thornfell keeps one. A hole in the rock with a door that doesn't look like a door and provisions that don't look like provisions. Mine has stood since before the queen's grandmother learned to smile for crowds."

"And you're just telling us this now?" Isandro's voice was flat, but Rook had spent enough years reading voices to hear what lived under the flatness: exhaustion, mostly, and under that the particular fear of a person who had just used magic in front of witnesses and hadn't yet decided how to feel about it.

Three days ago, when the purge patrol had come up the switchbacks below them with lanterns and leashes and that horrible singing net they used for gifted folk, Isandro had stepped out from behind a boulder and drawn a sigil in the air with two fingers, and the patrol's lanterns had gone dark as drowned eyes, and the dogs had forgotten what they were smelling, and the whole squad had turned around and marched home convinced they'd seen nothing worth reporting. Rook had watched it happen. He'd watched Isandro's face while it happened, too — the terror first, then something else, something almost like joy, quickly strangled.

He hadn't mentioned it since. Neither had Bren. There was an unspoken agreement among hunted things: you didn't poke at each other's wounds. You just kept walking.

The cache door was a slab of granite that looked exactly like every other slab of granite, except that Rook knew which end to lift and where the counterweight hid. It swung inward on oiled hinges — he oiled them every pass-through, twice a year, whether he needed the cache or not; a smuggler who neglected his caches was a smuggler who died in one — and revealed a room perhaps twelve feet by fifteen, cut into the mountain's bones by hands long dead. Somebody had lined the walls with slate against damp. Somebody had built a bench into the stone, and a hearth with a flue that vented through a crack no smoke could betray, because the crack breathed outward only, some old trick Rook had bought from a dead man for a keg of brandy and never questioned.

Inside: salt pork in waxed cloth. Hard cheese going pleasantly sharp. A sack of dried apples. Oatcakes sealed in tins. A jug of plum spirit strong enough to strip varnish. Blankets that smelled of cedar and years. And, because Rook Vane was Rook Vane, a small locked box he did not open in company.

"Well," Isandro said, looking around with the expression of someone reevaluating him upward by several degrees. "You weren't lying about being prepared."

"I never lie about preparation," Rook said. "Lies are for prices."

Bren had already dropped onto the bench, and the moment he sat, the sitting became a collapse — the big shifter folding down onto his forearms, shivering, the wolf-shape flickering at the edges of him like a candle in wind. Shifting cost heat. Shifting in snow cost double. Rook threw him a blanket without comment and got a grunt that might have been thanks.

Isandro, meanwhile, was doing what Isandro always did: working. Building the fire small and clean, setting water to warm, slicing the salt pork thin enough to stretch, laying out oatcakes with the economy of a person who had fed themselves carefully in dangerous places for a very long time. Rook watched those hands and thought, not for the first time, that everything about the crown's food-taster was a study in controlled surfaces. The plain face. The servant's stoop. The voice kept deliberately unremarkable. Even now, even three days into flight, even after the sigil, Isandro moved like a person trying to take up less space than a body required.

The storm arrived in earnest while the food warmed — a sound first, a vast white roar against the mountainside, then a pressure, then a cold that crept through the slate walls like a debt collector. Outside, the world ceased to exist. Inside, the little room filled with the smell of frying pork and cedar smoke and wet wool, and for a while nobody talked, because there is a kind of silence that only happens between people who are all too tired to pretend.

It was Bren who broke it, eventually, around a mouthful of oatcake. "How far tomorrow?"

"If the storm breaks by midmorning, the high shelf by dusk," Rook said. "Day after that, the Toll-Keeper's Bridge." He saw Isandro go still at the name and chose, generously, not to comment. Everyone who ran these passes knew about the bridge. Nobody liked talking about it.

"And after the bridge?"

"After the bridge," Rook said, "we stop being on any map ever drawn, and I start earning my keep." He took a swallow of the plum spirit, felt it light a line of fire from throat to sternum, and looked at the other two across the firelight. Bren wrapped in his blanket like a man-shaped bruise. Isandro with their back straight and their hands quiet in their lap, watching him with those careful, weighing eyes.

He should tell them. He'd known he should tell them since the second night, since Isandro had asked him — casually, too casually — *why does a smuggler volunteer to walk into the court beneath the mountain?* And Rook had given the answer he gave everyone, the easy one: *because the court pays in things better than gold.* Which was true, as far as it went. Most true things were.

But they were storm-bound now, sealed in a hole in a mountain with nowhere to be and nothing to do until the sky relented, and there is something about a storm that loosens the tongue. Maybe it was the way the wind screamed against the stone like something grieving. Maybe it was the plum spirit. Maybe it was simply that he'd watched Isandro draw that sigil and understood, finally, that he wasn't the only one in this company carrying a thing too heavy to set down alone.

"The court buys souls," Rook said.

The words came out blunter than he'd planned. Both of them looked up.

"You know that much already — everyone who runs Thornfell knows that much. Souls as currency. Payment for debts the crown can't settle in coin. What nobody tells you is *how* it works, because how it works is worse than the fact of it." He turned the jug in his hands. "They don't kill you. That's the thing people imagine — some altar, some knife, done. No. They *keep* you. Your body walks around afterward. Eats, sleeps, works. But the part of you that wanted things, that remembered why mornings mattered — that goes into a lantern-glass and sits on a shelf in the dark, and your body learns to live without it, the way a house learns to live without a roof. Rain gets in. Everything rusts."

Isandro's face had gone very still. "Sefa."

"Sefa," Rook agreed quietly. "And others. Dozens every winter. Hundreds over the years. The regent calls it *collateral*." He spat the word like gristle. "I've carried soul-ledgers myself, early days, before I knew what the sealed boxes were. Paid me triple rates to run them up the mountain and never ask. I asked anyway, once. Just once." His mouth twisted. "That's how I learned the rest."

Bren had stopped eating. In the firelight his eyes had gone amber-bright, the wolf close under the skin. "The rest being what?"

"The rest being Tam." Rook heard his own voice go strange, distant, the voice of a man reciting something memorized against his will. "My brother. Younger by four years and stupid with it, the way younger brothers are. We ran the eastern passes together, him and me, after our mother died. He was the fast talker. I was the one who counted. Worked well enough for six years." He took another drink. The fire burned it away too fast. "Six years ago the crown's debts came due — the real debts, the ones behind the pretty ledgers, the ones the queen signs with a seal nobody's allowed to see. The magistrates started collecting. Not taxes. *Names.* Gifted names, mostly, but they'd take anyone whose soul appraised high, and Tam's appraised high, because Tam was — " His voice caught, and he hated it, and pushed through. "Tam was *luminous*. Everybody said so. You'd walk into a room and he'd already made it warmer. That's not poetry. That's what the appraisers wrote down. *High warmth. High resonance.* Like grading a ham."

The silence in the cache was total except for the storm.

"They took him in autumn," Rook said. "And I — " He set the jug down very carefully, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw. "I was in Vell's Crossing with a full load and a buyer waiting. I knew where they were taking him. I knew the road. I could have been ahead of the wagon in half a day. And I sat in Vell's Crossing and I drank and I told myself the arithmetic. One soul in the hand versus two lost on the road. Cargo before blood. Every rule I'd ever lived by, lined up in a row to tell me it was fine, it was *sound*, it was the smart play." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "So I made the smart play. And my brother's been on a shelf in the dark for six winters, and I've walked past the door of that court eleven times since, carrying goods, carrying *other people's* goods, and every time I told myself next year. Next year, when the route's safer. Next year, when I've got leverage. Next year."

He looked up. Met Isandro's eyes. Met Bren's.

"That's why I'm here. Not for payment. Not for leverage. I'm here because I did the arithmetic once and it made me a coward, and I'd rather die in that mountain than do the arithmetic again."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The storm hammered. The fire popped.

It was Isandro who moved first — Isandro, who reached across the space between them and refilled Rook's cup without asking, which in the grammar of people who don't touch each other easily is very nearly an embrace.

"Thank you," Isandro said.

"For what? For telling you I left my brother in a cage?"

"For telling the truth badly." Something flickered at the corner of Isandro's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if it dared. "Most people tell it beautifully. It means nothing when it's beautiful."

Bren snorted — a rough, wet sound that might have been laughter or might have been the shifter equivalent of clearing his throat before saying something difficult. "Well," he said. "Since we're confessing."
