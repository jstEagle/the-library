## 9. Wolves of the High Pass

Bren smelled them before he heard them, and heard them long before Isandro or Rook could have.

It came to him on the wind off the glacier — a thread of musk and woodsmoke and old blood that his body knew the way a scar knows rain. He stopped mid-stride on the switchback trail, boots planted on stone glazed with rime, and let his nostrils flare until the cold ached in the roots of his teeth.

*Pack.*

Not just any pack. The wind carried names in it: Marrow's grey flank and her wheezing breath; the twins, Ash and Ember, who had been pups when Bren last ran beneath the pines of the Hollow Vale; and beneath all of it, heavier than iron, the scent-signature of Torv himself — second of the High Hollow pack, keeper of its law, hater of Bren's name since before the last purge had emptied the valley dens.

"Trouble?" Rook asked behind him. The smuggler had gone quiet-footed as ever on scree, his ledger-satchel hugged to his chest like a child. His eyes flicked up the pass, reading nothing, because there was nothing for human eyes to read yet — only snowfield, only the black fangs of rock, only the white silence pressing down from the col above.

"Worse," Bren said. "Family."

Isandro came up beside him, breath steaming, cheeks raw-red with three days of cold. They looked at him the way they always did — level, patient, waiting for the whole truth rather than the easy piece of it. It was one of the things Bren had come to trust about them, this taster who tasted nothing by accident, who walked through poison and lies and came out clean on the other side.

"The pack I left," Bren said. "When the purge came through the vale four winters back, the queen's hunters took six of ours. The alpha ordered us to scatter and hide — den-law, survival. But some of us wanted to fight." He flexed his hands inside their fingerless gloves, feeling the ghost of claws. "I stayed to fight. We lost anyway. And when it was over, those who'd hidden called me the fool who brought the fire down on us, and those who'd fought called me the coward who lived. So I left. Walking away from a pack is allowed. Walking away *during* a hunt—" he shook his head, "—that's another word."

"Traitor," Isandro said quietly.

"Traitor," Bren agreed. "And they've found my trail. They'll have been following us since the river, maybe since the cache. Torv doesn't lose a scent once he has it."

Rook swore under his breath in three dialects. "Can we go around?"

"There is no around. This is the only pass into the high country before first frost seals everything. North is the icefall, south is the patrol roads. We go through them, or we don't go at all."

"And if they don't want us to go through?"

Bren looked up the trail, where the wind was shifting, bringing the pack-scent closer, richer, threaded now with something new — excitement, the sharp coppery edge of a hunt turning deliberate. "Then we find out what I'm worth to them after four years away."

* * *

They came down out of the rocks an hour past noon, when the light lay flat and shadowless on the snow and every shape looked carved from bone.

There were nine of them. Eight wolves in winter coats — greys and rusts and one great white-shouldered male — and one woman walking upright at their center, which meant she was the alpha, which meant she had chosen to meet them as a person meets persons, law before tooth. Her hair was iron-grey braided with hawk feathers, and her face was seamed like old bark, and Bren felt his heart clench painfully at the sight of her, because four years had not dulled the memory of her voice saying *run, children, run* while the queen's crossbows sang.

Marrow. Alpha of the High Hollow. The one who had ordered the scattering Bren had refused.

The eight spread into a crescent without a sound, flanking the trail, cutting the way forward and the way back both. Professional. Old habit. Bren's own hackles rose in answer, a prickle along his spine where the wolf lived just under his skin, wanting out, wanting to answer posture with posture. He held himself still. Held his breath even. Made himself stand the way a man stands when he has done nothing wrong — though the pack's law did not care much for the difference between innocence and absence.

"Bren of the Hollow Vale." Marrow's voice carried across the snow without effort, the way voices do in high thin air. "You walk back into our mountains wearing a man's coat and leading strangers. Speak your business."

He made himself bow his head — not submission, exactly, but courtesy, the grammar of a language he hadn't spoken in four years. "Alpha. I carry two companions over the pass to the court beneath the mountain. Nothing more. We ask passage, not territory."

"You ask passage." Torv stepped forward from the crescent's right end, and the snow seemed to shrink around him. He was enormous even in man-shape, shoulders like a doorframe, a scar splitting his left brow where a hunter's bolt had grazed him the night Bren had stood and fought. His eyes were amber and utterly without warmth. "The traitor asks *passage*. As if he were still owed the roads of his birth."

"I never betrayed the pack," Bren said. "I bled for it. Ask the hunters' graves outside the vale how much I betrayed it."

"I asked the survivors instead." Torv's lip curled. "They say you shamed us. That when the alpha commanded dispersal, you defied her — and your defiance drew the hunters deeper into our range. Two more dead because Bren the Bold would not bend his neck. And then, when the fighting was done, you slunk away rather than live under the law you'd broken."

"The law you're quoting," Bren said, keeping his voice level with an effort that cost him, "is the law of hiding. I chose the law of standing. If that's treason, then every wolf who ever died facing a hunter is a traitor too."

A low growl moved through the crescent — not agreement, not denial, just the pack's unease rippling like wind through wheat. Bren felt it and hated how much he still cared. Four years alone, four years of city gutters and smuggler roads and sleeping in shapes that fit no den, and still the old hunger for their regard sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Marrow raised one hand, and the crescent stilled.

"There is a way this is done," she said. "Older than me, older than the vale. A wolf who leaves during the hunt returns under challenge or not at all. You will not walk this pass on my sufferance, Bren, because my sufferance is not mine to give — it belongs to the law, and the law belongs to all of us, or it is nothing." She turned her head slightly. "Torv. Second stands for the pack. As it has always been."

Torv smiled, and it was the worst thing Bren had seen in four years, because it was the smile of a wolf who had waited a long time for permission.

"Shifting forms," Marrow continued, "man to man, so no blood is spilled that can't be answered. First to yield, first to fall unconscious, or first to break — the fight ends. Winner's claim stands. Loser goes where the winner says." Her eyes found Bren's, and for one unguarded moment something moved behind them — grief, maybe, or memory of a gangly young wolf who used to steal fish from the smoking racks. Then it was gone, banked like a fire under ash. "Unless you refuse. Refusal is also an answer. You'd turn back down the mountain, and we'd forget we saw you."

Forget. As if a pack forgot anything. As if the word *traitor* would not follow him down every road for the rest of his life, whispered in dens he'd never enter, taught to pups he'd never meet.

Bren pulled off his gloves. Flexed his bare fingers against the cold.

Behind him, he heard Isandro shift their weight, and Rook mutter something that was probably a prayer to whatever gods smugglers kept. He didn't turn around. If he turned around and saw Isandro's face right now — calm, careful, already calculating odds — he might lose his nerve, and a man did not lose his nerve in front of Torv.

"I accept the challenge," Bren said.

* * *

They cleared a ring in the snow, the eight wolves pacing its edge, and the fight began without a horn or a count, because pack fights begin when the challenger stops talking.

Torv came in fast for a big man — faster than he had any right to be — and Bren barely got his forearm up in time to turn the first blow aside rather than eat it full in the jaw. The impact jarred him to the shoulder. Snow sprayed. He gave ground, circling, letting the cold air burn in his lungs, hunting for the rhythm underneath the rage.

Because Torv was fighting angry. That was the thing four years had taught Bren that his younger self never knew: anger was fuel, but it was also a smell, and a fighter who could smell it could step around it. Torv led with his right always, favored the scarred side's blind angle never, and burned his fury in bright hot flares that left gaps between them.

Bren weathered the second flurry, took a glancing hit across the cheekbone that split skin and filled his mouth with the copper taste of his own blood — *the taster's trade, rubbing off on me*, he thought absurdly — and then found his opening on the third exchange. Torv overcommitted, lunging for a finishing grip, and Bren dropped low, hooked his leg, and put him down into the snow with the whole mountain's weight behind it.

The impact knocked a grunt out of Torv and a growl out of the watching wolves. Bren straddled him, pinned the bigger man's wrist, cocked his fist—

—and the fight went strange.

Because Torv stopped struggling. Lay there in the churned snow, breathing hard, looking up at Bren with those amber eyes, and said, very quietly, so only Bren could hear:

"Do it, then. Finish it. Prove the vale was right about you — that you'd put your hands on your own blood for the sake of strangers."

And Bren understood, all at once, that this was the real fight. Not the fists. Torv didn't want to win. Torv wanted Bren to become the traitor the pack needed him to be — wanted the story settled, the ugly arithmetic of the purge given a villain, so that the survivors wouldn't have to sit with the harder truth: that hiding had saved some of them and killed others, and no one, not Bren, not Torv, not Marrow herself, had found a way to save them all.

His fist hovered. His knuckles ached with cold. Somewhere beyond the ring, Isandro stood absolutely still, and Bren could feel their attention on his back like a hand.

"No," Bren said.

He released the wrist. Stood. Stepped back from Torv, arms open, breathing ragged, blood running warm down his cheek into his collar.

"I yield the *fight*, Torv. Not the argument. Beat me bloody if the law needs it — I'll take it. But I won't make myself into your villain so the rest of you can sleep." He turned, slowly, to face the whole crescent, to face Marrow. "I left because staying meant becoming something I couldn't live as. I'm coming back through these mountains because people I love need me to. That's the whole of it. Judge it however the law requires. But I'm done apologizing for choosing to stand."

Silence, except the wind.

Then Marrow said, "The fight isn't finished. Torv is second. He rises or he yields — you don't get to walk out of the ring on a speech."

And Torv rose.

Bren saw it coming — saw the coiling in the big man's shoulders, the fury curdling into something past fury, the moment where a sparring match tips over into the other kind — and he braced for it, tired, half-blind from the cut, knowing he had perhaps one clean block left in him.

He never needed it.

A small figure crossed the snow-ring like a thrown knife.

Isandro — plain, quiet Isandro, who moved through the world like someone trying not to be noticed — stepped directly between Bren and Torv's charge, and did something with their hand. Bren caught only a glimpse: fingers splayed, a quick sigil-trace drawn in the freezing air, faint gold light guttering like a struck match.

Torv stopped.

Not gently. He stopped the way a wolf hits the end of a chain — full momentum arrested, thrown against his own halt, staggering, snarling, straining forward against nothing visible. The sigil held him like glass holds a moth. His eyes went wide, then wider, and the whole crescent erupted — eight wolves surging forward with their lips peeled off their teeth, and Marrow's voice cracking across the snow like a whip:

"*Hold!*"

They held. Barely. And in the ringing quiet that followed, Marrow looked at the small stranger standing in her pack's fighting ring, at the fading gold trace still hanging in the air, at Torv straining against a binding no wolf-craft could make — and her face went through recognition, disbelief, and something very close to fear.

"A gift-worker," she said. "You travel with a gift-worker, Bren. In the season of the Purge. On our mountain."

"They're not traveling with me," Bren said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "We travel together. There's a difference, and it's the whole point of everything I just said."

Isandro lowered their hand. The binding eased, and Torv staggered free, coughing, staring at Isandro with an expression that had passed through hatred into something more complicated — the look of a creature meeting a power older than the laws it had built its life on.

"You broke pack-law," Marrow said to Isandro. Flat. Final. "Interference in a ring-fight. By the old rules, the penalty is banishment of the offender and outlawry of whoever vouches for them."

"I know," Isandro said. Their voice was calm, and Bren marveled at it, at this person who had spent a lifetime being invisible choosing, here, now, on a mountaintop, to be unmistakable. "But your law assumes the fight was fair. It wasn't. Your second stopped fighting to win and started fighting to make a story. I stopped him from lying to you." They glanced at Torv. "No offense intended. But you know I'm right, and so does everyone smelling the shame on you right now."

Torv's ears — he was half-shifted now, without quite meaning to be, the wolf rising through the man — flattened. He said nothing. Which was itself an answer.

Marrow was silent a long time. The wind moved her hawk feathers. Behind her, the twins — grown now, Bren realized, grown and scarred and strangers to him — watched with young eyes that held neither the old grudge nor the old love, only curiosity, which might have been the most painful thing of all.

Finally the alpha spoke.

"The law says banish the interferer." She looked at Isandro. "The law also says a ring-fight ends when one party ceases to contend in honor. And I have eyes, gift-worker, whatever else I am." She exhaled, long and slow, a plume of steam dissolving into the white air. "So here is what the law and I have negotiated between us. The debt of interference is owed — and debts are the one currency this mountain respects. You will owe the High Hollow pack a service, to be named when I name it. And Bren—" her gaze swung to him, and this time she did not hide what was in it, the old grief, the old tenderness, worn smooth by four winters, "—Bren walks the pass. Under challenge answered, under law satisfied. What he is to us after that—" she paused, and Bren watched her choose the words with the care of someone setting down something fragile, "—that depends on what he does at the mountain court. Souls are stolen there. Ours among them, once. If you're going to that place, wolf, then go and see what you bring back."

She turned. The crescent folded around her, and the nine of them moved back up into the rocks, gray shapes dissolving into gray stone, until only the wind remained and the churned ruin of the fighting ring and the blood-dotting of snow where Bren's cheek had dripped.

Rook let out a breath he'd apparently been holding for ten minutes. "I have never," he said, "been so frightened by people walking *away*."

Bren didn't answer. He was looking at Isandro, at the small ordinary person who had just broken a thousand-year-old law for him without hesitation, without calculation, the way you'd swat a wasp away from a friend's neck.

"You shouldn't have done that," he said. "The debt. The exposure. Everything."

"I know," Isandro said. They tore a strip from their sleeve, wet it with snow, and reached up — they had to reach, Bren was half a head taller — to press it to his split cheekbone. Their fingers were steady. Their eyes were not quite. "Do it again tomorrow, though."

"Why?"

"Because you'd have done it for me." A small, crooked almost-smile. "That's the whole ledger, Bren. That's the entire account."

Above them, far up the pass, the wind changed direction — and Bren's nose, sharper than any human's, caught what it carried down from the heights. Stone dust. Cold metal. The deep mineral breath of rock that had been opened after a long time closed.

The gates beneath the mountain were stirring. And somewhere below, in the dark, Sefa's soul was burning down like a candle in a lantern-glass, and first frost was coming faster than any of them had planned for.

"We move," Bren said, wiping the blood from his face and turning up the trail. "Now. While the pack's watching — let them see which way I walk."

And he walked, and heard — soft, certain, unhurried — the footsteps of his found pack falling in behind him.
