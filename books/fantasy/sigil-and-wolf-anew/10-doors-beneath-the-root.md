## 10. Doors Beneath the Root

The snow stopped an hour before dawn, and in that hour the mountain went so quiet Isandro could hear their own blood moving.

They had climbed since moonset, following a seam of stone that Bren swore was a road and Rook swore was nothing but a goat's bad dream. The path ran along the shoulder of Thornfell's lowest peak, where the pines gave up and only lichen lived, gray-green crusts that glowed faintly when Bren's breath passed over them. Below, the world they'd left behind lay drowned in white. Above, the summit wall rose black against a sky rinsed clean of cloud, and set into that wall — when the first true light found it — was a door.

Isandro stopped walking without deciding to stop. Their body simply refused another step, the way a tongue refuses a spoiled dish.

"There it is," Rook said, and his voice came out smaller than he meant it to. He pulled his scarf down off his face. His beard was crusted with ice, and beneath the ice his skin had gone the color of old tallow. "Gods under everything. I'd half convinced myself I dreamed it."

"You didn't dream it," Bren said. The wolf-shifter stood with his weight on his good leg, the wounded one stiff behind him, and his nostrils flared at the air the way they always did before he said something he disliked. "I smelled it two turns back. Old iron. Old cold." He paused. "And something else. Something like — after rain. Like the air over a struck match."

"Ozone," Isandro said, before they could think better of it.

Both men looked at them.

"It's what the air smells like when sigil-work is fresh," Isandro said. Their voice sounded strange in their own ears, too steady, the voice they used at the queen's table when announcing a course. "Or very old. It's the same smell either way. Power doesn't rot. It just waits."

They started forward again, and this time their legs obeyed, though every instinct they had honed across nine years of tasting for a crown screamed at them to turn around. That was the thing about the gift no one warned you: it never told you *don't*. It only told you *careful*, in a voice like a mother's, like a poisoner's, like your own.

* * *

The door was not carved into the mountain. It was grown from it.

That was Isandro's first understanding, and it arrived whole, the way certain truths arrive — not reasoned out but tasted, known on the tongue before the mind can name them. The stone of the gate flowed in slow whorls, like wood grain seen through water, like the rings of a tree cut open. And at its center, where a door should have handles or hinges or a king's seal, there were sigils.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They covered the gate from the frozen ground to twenty feet overhead, fine as spider silk in some places, bold as brand-marks in others, all of them cut into the stone itself — no, not cut, Isandro realized, drawing closer, breath fogging, boots crunching on rime. Not cut. *Grown.* Each line of each sigil was a ridge of living rock, raised up out of the surface the way a scar rises out of healed skin, and every one of them still held power. Isandro could feel it the way they felt heat from a stove: a pressure against the gift, a hum in the teeth, a taste at the back of the throat like copper and juniper and something older than both.

They forgot Bren. They forgot Rook. They forgot the purge, the queen, Sefa's soul dimming somewhere behind all that stone. They forgot their own name.

What they did not forget — what they could not have forgotten if the mountain fell on them — was how to read.

Their grandmother had taught them, in a kitchen that smelled of rendered fat and dried thyme, tracing letters in spilled flour while the bread proved. *This is Vesh,* she would say, tapping a loop with a floury finger. *Vesh is the binding. This is Omma. Omma is the opening. Every working is a sentence, little taster. Every sentence is a promise. And every promise has a price written into it, if you know where to look.*

Isandro looked.

The gate's outer ring was warding — Vesh-work, dense and layered, generations deep, the kind of protective lattice that took a guild of makers years to raise. But beneath the warding, woven through it like thread through cloth, ran sentences of a grammar Isandro had never been taught and understood anyway, the way you understand your mother's language without ever having studied it. The forms were older. The loops closed differently. The promises were larger.

But they were the same promises.

*Here is the door. Here is the keeping. Here is the price.*

"Oh," Isandro said aloud. It came out as almost nothing, a ghost of sound, and yet in the silence of the high pass it rang like a dropped plate. "Oh, no."

"What?" Bren's hand was suddenly on their arm, warm even through three layers of wool. The shifter had gone tense, ears of him pricked toward the treeline below, but his eyes stayed on Isandro's face. "What do you see?"

"The same thing I've been running from my whole life," Isandro said. They lifted their hand — bare, despite the cold; they'd pulled the glove off without noticing — and laid their palm flat against the gate, an inch from the nearest sigil, close enough to feel the power standing off the stone like heat off a kettle. "This is guild-craft. Or — no. Guild-craft is *this*, worn thin and handed down until nobody remembers what the hands looked like before the gloves. This is the root. This is what we were copying, all those centuries, without knowing who we copied it from."

Rook had come up on Isandro's other side. The smuggler squinted at the sigils the way he squinted at forged bills of lading — professionally, hungrily, seeing none of it. "Meaning what? Meaning the mountain folk learned it from your lot?"

"Meaning," Isandro said slowly, feeling each word settle into place like stones into a wall, "my lot learned it from theirs. Or both learned it from something older than either. Look at the binding-forms. Vesh-loop, double-anchored, priced in blood-name — that's the exact structure of the ward I burned in the queen's kitchen last spring. The one that kept arsenic out of her wine. I drew it in chalk dust in four seconds because my grandmother taught me the shape when I was six years old. And whoever made this door spent — " Isandro's hand moved along the wall, following the whorl of a sentence forty feet long — "*decades* on this single panel. Same grammar. Same promises. Same prices." They laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "The queen calls the gift a blight. Calls us weeds in the field. And the seat of her oldest enemy is built out of the same weed, grown tall."

* * *

Bren circled wide, wolf-cautious, putting himself between the gate and the slope they'd climbed. Rook hung back near the rocks, hands jammed in his armpits, doing the arithmetic smugglers do — exits, timing, odds — and finding all the columns ugly.

Isandro read.

It was slow work. The cold crept up through their boot-soles and turned their fingers clumsy, and twice they had to stop and beat feeling back into their hands inside their coat. But the gate yielded to them the way an old lock yields to the right key — grudgingly, then all at once. The outer warding resolved into sense: *no passage uninvited; no unbinding within; the court sleeps sealed till thaw of the long winter.* Standard. Almost comforting in its familiarity.

The second layer was less comforting.

*Debts recorded. Debts honored. Debts collected.* The words repeated, spiraling inward, each iteration older than the last, the script growing stranger, more angular, until the oldest ring at the center was barely language at all — more like weather, like the pattern frost makes on glass. But the meaning survived every transformation, because meaning, Isandro thought, is the one thing that doesn't rot: *that which is owed will be paid. In coin, in labor, in years, in names, in souls.*

Souls.

Sefa's face rose in Isandro's mind — not the way it usually did, sharp with guilt, but soft, the way it had looked across the kitchen the night she'd said *I've always known about you, Issy, I've known since the flour incident when we were twelve, you think you're so subtle* — and Isandro's chest tightened until breathing became a deliberate act.

"She's in there," Isandro said. Not to anyone. To the door. To the grammar. To whatever maker had grown these sentences centuries ago and left them standing like a promise the world had forgotten it made. "She's behind this."

"The lanterns are kept deep," Rook said quietly. He had come closer without Isandro noticing. There was something raw in his voice that hadn't been there on the road — the voice of a man looking at the front of a house he'd once fled out the back of. "Vault level. My brother described it to me, the one time — " He stopped. Started again. "The one time he wrote me, before they took him. Said the vault sat under the roots of the mountain itself. Said the whole court was built on top of it like a lid on a pot."

"A lid on a pot," Isandro murmured. Their eyes were still moving over the sigils, and now they saw what they should have seen at the start — what part of them, perhaps, had been refusing to see. The innermost ring, the oldest script, the weather-pattern writing: it wasn't a ward at all. Wards keep things out. This kept something *in*. And the something it kept in was the same power that hummed in Isandro's teeth right now, the same power that had lived under their skin since birth, quiet as a swallowed coal.

The gate wasn't guarding the court from thieves.

It was guarding the world from what the court did with people like Isandro.

* * *

"We should camp," Bren said. "Eat. Sleep. Go in rested."

"No," Isandro said.

"Issy." The nickname, in Bren's low growl, landed differently than it did in Sefa's mouth — heavier, more worried. "You've been standing in the wind reading rock for two hours. Your lips are blue. Whatever's behind that door, it'll still be behind it tomorrow."

"That's exactly what frightens me." Isandro finally turned from the gate. The light had changed while they worked; the sun had cleared the eastern ridge and now struck the sigils full-on, and in direct light they *moved* — not visibly, not quite, but there was a shimmer in them, a suggestion of rotation, like a wheel seen through water. "Every day we stand here is a day closer to first frost. You heard the toll-keeper. You heard Rook. When the gates seal, they seal for a generation. Sefa spends thirty years in a lantern-glass, dimming. Rook's brother with her. And every day, the regent's leverage grows, because the longer winter runs, the fewer options we have and the more they can ask."

"So we go in today half-frozen and half-blind," Rook said. It wasn't quite a question and wasn't quite agreement. The smuggler rubbed his jaw, ice crackling in his beard. "Course, going in today means figuring out the door. And unless one of you speaks fluent mountain — "

"I do," Isandro said.

The words came out calm. Inside, everything was roaring.

Because here was the truth they had been circling since dawn, the truth the gate had pressed into them line by line, sentence by sentence, the way a signet presses into wax: the gift was not a curse. The gift was not even, properly speaking, *theirs* — not Isandro's alone, not the gifted of the crownlands alone. It was heritage. Old heritage. Shared heritage. The same root under two different trees, and one tree had grown in palace gardens where it was pruned and poisoned and purged, and the other had grown here, in the dark, fed on souls, grown vast and hungry and wrong — but rooted in the same soil, speaking the same first language.

Which meant the queen's purge was not just cruelty. It was amputation. A kingdom cutting off its own hand because it had forgotten what the hand was for.

And which meant — and here the roaring inside Isandro went quiet, went cold, went clear as high-altitude air — which meant that everything Isandro had ever hidden, every sigil wiped away before dawn, every tool burned in Edicts-in-Ash panic, every flattening of themselves into servant's plainness, had been done in service of a lie so large the whole kingdom believed it. The lie that the gift was foreign. Other. Blight.

It was not foreign. It was the oldest citizen in the room.

"I can open it," Isandro said again, steadier. "Not break it — you don't break work like this, you *answer* it. The outer ring is a question. It's asking for proof of craft. Proof of — " they hesitated, and then said it plainly, because they were done being plain in only one direction — "proof of the gift. Freely shown. That's the toll. Not blood. Not payment. Just *witness*. Someone standing at this door willing to be seen."

Bren was quiet a moment. Then: "The patrol on the pass. When you lit the sky up to shake the trackers off us." His amber eyes held theirs, steady, unflinching. "First time you used it in the open. How did it feel?"

"Like taking off a boot I'd worn so long I'd forgotten my foot hurt."

"And now?"

Isandro looked back at the gate. At the ancient sentences, patient as glaciers, waiting for an answer they had waited centuries to hear. Somewhere beyond them, down through the roots of the mountain, a kitchen maid's soul was guttering in glass, and a smuggler's brother with her, and gods knew how many hundreds more, harvested by a queen who called it justice and banked by a regent who called it commerce.

"Now," Isandro said, "it feels like coming home to a house that's been waiting for me. And I hate that. And I'm going in anyway."

They knelt at the base of the gate, where the oldest sigil sat half-buried in drifted snow, and swept the snow clear with their bare hand. Then they drew breath, and reached down into the place where the gift lived — the swallowed coal, the hidden hearth, the self they had spent nine years starving — and let it rise.

Power moved down their arm and out through their fingers, and where it touched the stone, the ancient sigil *woke*. Light ran along the whorled lines like fire along a fuse, climbing the gate, ringing it, the whole door suddenly blazing with sentences spoken aloud at last, and the mountain beneath them shivered — a long, deep tremor, like a sleeper turning over —

— and somewhere far above, on the ridge they had crossed at dawn, a sentry of the court uncrossed his arms and began, unhurried, to walk down toward the light.
