## 11. The Court of Debts

The gate beneath the root did not open so much as *admit* them, the way a throat admits a swallow — reluctantly, and with the sense of something larger deciding not to choke.

Isandro went through it bent under a clerk's burden: a ledger-box strapped to their back, ink-stained cuffs, a broker's seal of green wax hanging at their chest on a cord that Rook had sworn was genuine because he'd once sold it to a man who was now dead. The disguise sat on Isandro like a second skin stitched from someone else's life. Plain wool. A servant's stoop, practiced for years in the queen's halls and repurposed here without shame. Only the sigil-tools were gone — burned in a palace brazier two seasons ago, or so the world believed — and only Isandro knew how much of their craft lived now in memory and blood, needing no brass stylus at all.

Bren could not come. Wolf-shape would be scented in three heartbeats by whatever warded these halls, and man-shape was no better; the mountain court remembered its debts, and Bren's pack had left its mark on these stones long before the crown ever learned to spell the word *purge*. So Bren waited in the dark above the sealed door with Rook, both of them chewing silence, while Isandro walked down into the earth wearing a dead man's seal and their own heartbeat for company.

*You come back out,* Bren had said, gripping their shoulder hard enough to bruise. *That's the bargain. Everything else is decoration.*

*Everything is always decoration,* Isandro had answered, and hated how easily the lie came.

The passage beyond the gate descended in spirals cut by hands older than any kingdom Isandro had served. The walls were veined with sigils — real ones, deep-cut, glowing faintly like embers banked under ash — and Isandro made the mistake of reading them. It cost them four steps of staggered footing and a cold sweat that had nothing to do with fear of discovery. The script was a grandmother to their own hidden craft. Same grammar. Same logic of binding and release. Someone had taught this to human hands once, before the crowns and purges, before *gift* became a word men spat. Walking those corridors felt like walking through the house of a relative they'd been told was dead.

A warden met them at the third turning. Or Isandro assumed it was a warden; it had the shape of a tall person the way a coat has the shape of a body, grey robes moving a half-beat behind the thing inside them, face smooth as river-worn stone except where eyes should have been, which held instead two points of lantern-light.

"Purpose," it said. Its voice was the sound of a ledger closing.

"Debt-service," Isandro answered, pitching their voice low and bored, the voice of a man who counted other people's misery for wages. They unslung the ledger-box and opened it to the forged pages — Rook's best work, and Rook's guiltiest. "Accounts for the house of Vane. Collateral review. Winter assessment."

The light-eyes moved over the pages without appearing to read them. Perhaps they didn't need to. Perhaps the court tasted truth the way Isandro tasted poison, and what it tasted in Isandro was boredom, tedium, the flat mineral flavor of clerical drudgery — because Isandro had spent the whole descent building that flavor around themselves like insulation, thinking of ledgers, of mildew, of chilblains, of the thousand gray grievances of a clerk's winter.

"Follow," said the warden.

They followed.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was beautiful the way a debt is beautiful: perfectly balanced, immaculately kept, and rotten all the way down into the bedrock of what it meant.

It opened suddenly out of the spiral stairs into a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some buried organ, and every one of them dripped slow light — pale gold, honey-thick — onto terraces of black stone carved into tiers, galleries, colonnades. Figures moved among them: courtiers whose skins shimmered faintly with borrowed luster, servants who did not shimmer at all, wardens drifting between like punctuation marks. Music came from somewhere, thin and sweet, a melody that seemed to have been going on since before the mountain had a name and intended to continue after everything above ground had forgotten itself.

And everywhere — everywhere — the lanterns.

They hung in chains from the cavern roof, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a glass vessel the size of a fist or a heart, each one holding a soft coiled radiance that pulsed. Not flame. Flame flickers. These glowed with the steady patience of something alive and unable to leave. As Isandro crossed the floor behind the warden, the nearest lantern pulsed a little faster, and Isandro felt it — actually felt it, a tug behind the breastbone, faint as a fish testing a line.

They did not look up again. Looking up was how clerks got noticed. But their skin crawled with the arithmetic all the way across the hall: *how many souls does it take to light a cavern? How many Sefas?*

The regent received petitioners in a rotunda at the cavern's far end, seated on a throne of salt-white stone that might have been grown rather than carved. Isandro had expected a monster. What sat on the throne instead was worse: an elegant figure of indeterminate age, robed in winter-grey, with a face like a portrait left too near a fire — handsome features just beginning to soften and run at the edges, as though beauty itself were a debt the regent could no longer service. Its eyes were the same lantern-light as the wardens', but older, and tired, and terribly patient.

"Vane's accounts," the warden announced, and stepped aside.

Isandro bowed. Clerk-deep. Eyes down, box presented, voice sanded flat. "Winter review, lord. Interest accrued against collateral held."

"Collateral." The regent turned the word over like a coin bitten for gold. "Your master sells me safe-passage debts, and I hold his paper against souls. Yes. I know Vane." Something moved at the corner of the ruined-handsome mouth — amusement, or its fossil. "He thinks I do not know why he comes himself, this year. He thinks I keep poor books." The lantern-eyes fixed on Isandro, weightless and enormous. "Do you know what we keep, little clerk?"

"Souls, lord." Flat. Bored. A fact among facts.

"Show them."

* * *

There was no cruelty in how it was done. That was the horror of it. A warden led Isandro along a gallery hung with glass, past soul after soul after soul, and consulted a slate with the courtesy of a librarian. Isandro kept their breathing even and their mind gray and their eyes lowered, and inside, behind the clerk-mask, something screamed in a register no one in this place could hear, because everyone here had heard it too long to hear it anymore.

Sefa's lantern hung at shoulder height, third row, marked with a tag of pale bark bearing her true name in script that hurt to glance at directly.

Isandro looked anyway.

She was dimmer than Isandro had imagined souls ought to be. When Sefa laughed in the palace kitchens — flour on her knuckles, stealing candied ginger with the shameless grace of a cat — she had burned brighter than anyone Isandro had ever known, and they had known gifted people, real ones, before the word became a death sentence. Her light here was a coal wrapped in ash. It pulsed when Isandro came near. Pulsed again, quicker, and Isandro felt the tug behind the sternum sharpen into something like recognition, and had to lock every muscle in their body to keep from reaching out.

*She knows me.* The thought arrived whole and terrible. *Some part of her knows me, and I am standing here holding a dead man's ledger.*

"Winter assessment," the warden recited, tapping the glass with a grey fingernail. The sound rang thin, like ice on a pond. "Reserve declining. At current draw, collateral value insufficient by first thaw. Standard remedy applies."

"What remedy," Isandro said, and made it a clerk's question, disinterested, pen poised over an invisible page.

The warden looked at Isandro with the light-eyes, and for a moment Isandro understood that it was studying *them* — weighing, appraising, tasting. Then it said: "Trade-up. The court permits substitution. A stronger soul retires the debt entire. Your master may bring one, or—" the grey head tilted, "—the court sometimes finds buyers prefer to settle in person."

Isandro wrote a meaningless stroke in the ledger. Their hand did not shake. Years of tasting poison in front of queens had built that hand, stroke by steady stroke, and Isandro thought, distantly, that they owed the queen that much: the steadiness was hers, purchased with every dish they'd survived for her.

"I'll convey the terms," they said.

* * *

The regent dismissed the other petitioners with a gesture and kept Isandro standing below the salt-white throne, and Isandro realized only afterward that this was deliberate — that the audience had been arranged from the moment the warden tasted them at the gate, or perhaps from before, perhaps from the moment a magistrate in the lowlands sold Sefa's name downward and the mountain had begun, in its patient way, setting its table.

"You are not Vane's clerk," the regent said conversationally, the way one remarks on weather.

"Lord?"

"The ledger is competent forgery. The seal is correct. The boredom is exquisite — truly, I have rarely been so well-insulated against interest." The ruined-handsome face inclined. "But you flinched at the gallery. Once. At the third-row lantern. Every soul in this court flinches somewhere in that gallery, little clerk, but yours flinched at *her*, and your grief has a signature." The lantern-eyes half-lidded. "I collect signatures. It is the family business."

Isandro's mind ran the exits and found them all bricked. So they did the other thing — the taster's thing, the dangerous thing, the thing that had kept them alive through seven years of a queen's enemies: they stopped hiding and started *negotiating*.

"If you knew what I was," Isandro said, "you'd have taken my soul at the gate."

"At the gate? No." Genuine surprise flickered across the softening features. "Souls taken by force sour. Like meat kept wrong. We buy. We trade. We extend credit." A pale hand gestured at the glittering dark above, at the orchard of glass and captive light. "Every lantern up there was *agreed to*. That is what your queen has never understood, and what makes her such a profitable partner — she believes she is harvesting. She is merely brokering. The gifted sell each other to her with whispers and rewards, she sells them to me with paperwork, and everyone signs, and everyone is shocked, shocked, at the terms." The regent sighed, and the sigh fogged faintly in air that had no right to be cold. "You, though. You came down here *unsold*. Do you know how rare that is? An unsold gift, walking in on its own feet, wearing a clerk's boredom."

"I came for the third-row lantern."

"Yes." The regent smiled, and the smile ran slightly at one corner, like wax. "Her name is Sefa. Kitchen maid. No gift at all — did you know that? Ungifted souls are poor currency. Dim. She was sold against a debt she never incurred, which even by our standards is inelegant." The lantern-eyes narrowed to slits of patient gold. "But she is *yours*. I can taste it. Grief with a signature, and loyalty underneath like a wire through the wax. And so I will make you the offer I make so rarely, because rare things are the only ones worth having."

The rotunda went quiet. Even the distant music seemed to lean in.

"A stronger soul retires her debt entire," the regent said. "Yours is — forgive the appraisal — magnificent. Taster-trained, poison-tempered, sigil-hidden, self-made. Decades of discipline folded into a single flame. She walks out of here tonight, whole, scarred a little, alive. And you stay." The hand opened, generous, terrible. "One lantern for another. The fairest trade this court has offered in a century. Think of it, taster. She goes home to spring. You go into the glass, and in a hundred years, when they hang you in the great gallery, they will say of your light what we say of the finest: *steady*. Nothing burns you. Nothing dims you. You will be the most beautiful thing in this mountain."

And the whisper rose in Isandro then, the temptation they would deny ever hearing for the rest of their life: not fear, but *arithmetic*. Sefa's laugh. Sefa's flour-dusted knuckles. Sefa waking whole in the sun while Isandro's vigilance, Isandro's hiddenness, Isandro's long gray labor of a self finally came to rest in glass. It would be easy. It would be *fair*. Somewhere far above, Bren and Rook waited at a sealed door for a friend who would never climb back out, and grief would find them, and time would sand it smooth, and the world would go on owing nothing.

*Steady,* the regent had said. *Nothing dims you.*

"That's a lovely speech," Isandro said. Their voice came out low and even, a taster's voice, a voice trained to deliver verdicts over soup. "You've clearly given it often."

"Often enough to mean it."

"Then let me counter-offer." Isandro lifted their head fully for the first time since entering the court, and let the clerk fall away like a dropped cloak, and looked the regent of the mountain dead in its lantern eyes with the eyes of a person who had decided, in this moment, underground, to stop being anyone's disguise. "I'm not selling. I'm not trading. I'm here on *collection*."

The regent went very still. Above them, in the vast dark, ten thousand lanterns pulsed — once, together, like a single held breath — and Isandro would spend years afterward wondering whether the mountain had heard them, or whether it had merely, at last, begun to listen.

"And what," said the regent softly, "do you imagine I owe *you*?"
