## 12. A Taste of Regent

The summons came on a plate.

Not a letter, not a herald's cry — a plate, carried up through the root-hewn corridors by a warden whose face was smooth as river stone and whose eyes never blinked quite enough. The plate was black glass, and upon it, written in something that gleamed like frost but was warm to the touch, were the words:

*THE REGENT DESIRES THE TASTER'S TONGUE AT TABLE. COME HUNGRY. LEAVE FEAR AT THE DOOR — IT SPOILS THE PALATE.*

Isandro read it three times while the warden watched with that unblinking patience, and understood it for what it was: not an invitation but a summons, not courtesy but a leash dressed in silk. In the crown's court, such a message would have arrived sealed in wax and witnessed by three clerks. Here, beneath the mountain, power did not bother with paper. Power simply said *come*, and you came, or you learned what refusal weighed.

They came.

The corridor to the regent's hall was long enough that Isandro counted their own heartbeats against their steps and lost track somewhere past four hundred. The walls wept mineral light — veins of pale blue luminescence running through stone so old it had forgotten being mountain and become something more like bone. Warm air moved against their face, carrying smells that made the taster in them sit up and take notice: roasting marrow-sweet roots, game bird fat with high-meadow seeds, honey dark as tar, and beneath it all a thread of something metallic and wrong that Isandro could not yet name and refused to forget.

Bren walked at their shoulder until the last turning, where two wardens stepped into the passage like doors closing. His hand found Isandro's sleeve — a brief pressure, wolf-warm even through cloth.

"I'll be near," he murmured. "If the walls start moving wrong, shout."

"They always move wrong here."

"Then shout anyway." His eyes held theirs a moment longer than comfort allowed, and then the wardens ushered him aside, and Isandro went on alone into the regent's hall.

* * *

It was not a hall so much as a throat — a vast chamber carved in curves, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor polished to a black mirror that gave back every candle-flame doubled. A single table ran down the center of it, long and low, set for two. Two chairs. Two places. The regent sat at the far end, and Isandro was given the near one, so that the whole length of the table lay between them like a negotiated border.

The regent was not what Isandro had braced for. They had imagined vastness — a thing of antlers and shadow, all the mountain's malice given shape. Instead there sat a figure of indeterminate years wrapped in grey, with a face like weathered limestone: patient, fissured, impossible to read. Its eyes were the only vivid thing about it, a deep amber, and they fixed on Isandro with the particular attention of someone who has already decided how a meal will go and is merely waiting for the guest to agree.

"Taster," the regent said. Its voice was dry stone rolling downhill. "You saved my clerk three days of paperwork by not dying in my corridors. Sit. Eat. I am told your tongue is worth more than the queen's whole kitchen."

"The queen would agree," Isandro said, and sat. "She pays it accordingly."

"And resents every coin." Something that might have been amusement moved in the limestone face. "We know a great deal about her resentments down here. We hold receipts."

Servants emerged from alcoves Isandro had not seen — or perhaps they had been standing there all along, motionless as furniture. They wore no livery, only grey, and their faces had the same too-smooth quality as the warden who had brought the summons. Soulless, Isandro thought, and the word landed in their chest like a dropped stone. Not dead. Not empty either. Just... spent. These were what remained when a soul lived elsewhere, in glass, in a vault, in debt.

The first course came: a broth the color of dark amber, smelling of smoked meat and mountain herbs and, faintly, of iron.

Isandro did not touch the spoon.

"You hesitate," the regent observed. It had not begun eating either. "I am told that is your profession."

"My profession is hesitation," Isandro agreed. "Done quickly and convincingly."

They lifted the spoon. Broth is the hardest thing in any poisoner's repertoire — flavors layered over flavors, heat masking bitterness, fat carrying what water will not. But broth also tells truths to a trained palate if you know how to listen to it, and Isandro had spent eleven years learning to listen. They let the liquid rest on the tongue without swallowing. Closed their eyes. Let the taste unfold the way a story unfolds: first the smoke, then the marrow, then the bitter-green edge of some herb that grew only where snowmelt ran —

— and there, underneath, thin as a hair drawn through silk: *bittercress root*. Harmless in a crown kitchen. In a mountain court, where the soil chemistry turned every common plant slightly strange, bittercress root became something else entirely. Isandro knew this because they had read the smugglers' ledgers Rook Vane had once sold them access to, entries in a cramped hand noting which lowland simples *changed character* past the Thornfell line. Bittercress, past the line, stopped being food and started being a slow fire in the blood. Not lethal tonight. Lethal in nine days, when no one would ever think to look backward at a feast.

Someone in this court wanted the regent dead slowly, deniably, and had arranged it through the kitchen.

Isandro swallowed, opened their eyes, and found the amber gaze waiting.

"Well?" said the regent.

"It's excellent," Isandro said. "The smoke is alder, the marrow is from a beast grazed on the south meadows, and the green note is bittercress root — which, below this mountain, is not a seasoning but a sentence. Nine days to death, disguised as a wasting illness. Whoever seasoned this broth wants you gone by the next dark moon, and wants the mountain to believe you simply faded."

The silence that followed had texture. The servants had gone utterly still. Somewhere far above, water dripped with the regularity of a clock.

The regent lifted its own spoon, considered the broth within it, and set it down again without drinking.

"Continue," it said. "Taste everything. Tell me everything."

* * *

So Isandro tasted.

Seven courses, each stranger than the last, each interrogated on the tongue like a witness under examination. Roast bird whose fat carried a resin that would cloud the mind by morning — deliberate, this time, and cruder; a second hand at work, or a second faction grown impatient. A honey-cake dusted with pollen that was clean, actually clean, the first honest sweetness of the evening. A wine-dark liquor that Isandro refused outright, setting the cup down untouched, because beneath its plum-and-iron perfume lay something that did not belong to any plant or mineral Isandro knew — something that tasted, unmistakably, of *magic bent wrong*, of a gift used as a vehicle rather than a weapon. Sigil-work dissolved into drink. Someone in this court was poisoning with craft itself.

That one they reported last, and watched the regent's limestone face change for the first time — a fissure widening, the amber eyes going flat and bright as coins.

"Craft," the regent repeated softly. "In my wine."

"In your wine. Drawn by someone gifted, and recently — the residue still hums. I'd guess less than a day old." Isandro folded their hands on the table and made themselves meet that gaze without flinching, though every instinct honed across eleven years of hidden life screamed at them to look down, defer, disappear. "I can tell you the flavor of the poison. I cannot tell you the hand that drew it. But whoever it was knows sigil-craft well enough to hide it in a vintage, which means they are not a servant. Your rival sits close to you."

"You speak of my court as though you know it."

"I know courts," Isandro said. "They differ in architecture, not in appetite. Above the mountain, the queen purges the gifted and calls it justice. Below the mountain, apparently, the gifted poison each other and call it politics. I have eaten in both houses now, and the menu rhymes."

For a long moment the regent said nothing. The candles burned. The mirror-black floor held their reflections upside down, so that it seemed to Isandro they were looking at the true version of themselves — the one who had spent a lifetime inverted, hidden, swallowed.

Then the regent laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry and grinding, like a rockslide heard from very far away, and it went on long enough that the soulless servants shifted in their alcoves.

"A taster with a philosopher's tongue," the regent said. "The queen wastes you. Or perhaps she doesn't — perhaps she keeps you precisely because you see clearly, and hates you for it. Yes. That sounds like her." It leaned forward, and the candlelight moved strangely across its face, filling the fissures with shadow. "You want something from me, taster. No one crosses Thornfell in the first snows, walks into my halls wearing a broker-clerk's disguise so poor it insults us both, and tastes seven courses of my table out of professional devotion. You want a soul out of my vault. The maid's. The one the magistrate sold me against the crown's debts."

Isandro's heart struck their ribs once, hard. They kept their hands still. Eleven years of practice: keep the hands still, keep the face plain, let nothing show but what you choose.

"Yes," they said.

"And you imagine I will simply give it back."

"I imagine," Isandro said carefully, "that you invited me to this table for a reason of your own, and that reason was not the pleasure of my company. So let us do what courts do. Let us bargain."

The regent studied them. The amber eyes seemed to weigh, to measure, to price.

"Here is what I know," it said at last. "I know the queen owes this court a debt older than her reign, and I collect it in the only currency she has that cannot be counterfeited. I know her purge fills my vault faster every winter, and that she calls it law while I call it trade, and that neither of us is lying exactly. And I know that tonight, at my own table, in my own hall, someone tried to kill me with my own kitchen and my own craft — which means my court is rotting from inside, and a rotting court cannot afford mercy." It spread its grey hands. "So. Why should I release one small maid's soul and weaken my collateral, taster? Give me a reason with weight."

Isandro had prepared answers on the long walk to this hall. Arguments about honor, about law, about the difference between debt and theft. All of them now felt like copper offered for gold.

What came out instead was the truth, plain as salt.

"Because in nine days, if I hadn't spoken, you'd have been dead, and your vault would belong to whoever poisoned you — and I promise you, whoever poisons a regent slowly and with craft does not intend to honor your debts. They'll free the souls they like and sell the rest to someone worse. Sefa's soul is safer with you alive and bound by your own bargains than with whatever comes after you." Isandro leaned forward. "You asked for weight. There it is. My tongue just bought you your throne for another season. Price it however you like — but price it fairly, because I will know if you don't. I always know."

The hall was silent again. Above them, the darkness of the ceiling seemed to lean closer, listening.

And the regent — the ancient, patient, limestone regent of the court beneath the mountain — inclined its head, slowly, the way one power acknowledges another.

"Fairly priced," it said. "Very well, taster. Here is my offer, and hear all of it before you speak. The maid's soul may be ransomed — but souls in my vault are not freed by kindness. They are freed by settlement. Bring me what settles her account, and she walks out of my mountain whole." It paused, and the amber eyes glittered. "Or. There is another currency I would accept in her place. A stronger soul. A *gifted* one. Yours has a flavor I can smell from here, taster, no matter how plainly you dress it. One soul for one soul. Walk away rich, and leave the maid to her winter."

The words settled over the table like frost.

Isandro thought of Sefa's laugh in the palace kitchens, flour on her cheek. Thought of a lantern-glass dimming in a vault of debts. Thought of Bren bleeding somewhere in these corridors, and Rook counting the hours to his brother's lantern, and the gates that would seal for a generation when first frost came.

"One more course," Isandro said quietly. "Before I answer."

The regent raised an eyebrow of weathered stone.

"What is it?"

"Tell me," Isandro said, "who taught your kitchen to use bittercress. Because the hand that seasoned that broth knew this mountain's secrets intimately — and I don't believe your rival is the only traitor at your table. I believe someone let them in. And I think you know it too. Which means, Regent, that you didn't invite me here to test my tongue at all." They held the amber gaze. "You invited me because you need a taster you can trust. And there is exactly one person under this mountain whose loyalty isn't for sale — because everyone here is already bought, and I'm the only one who came from outside."

For the first time that night, the regent looked genuinely surprised — a fissure opening all the way through.

And far off, deeper in the mountain than sound should have traveled, something began to ring: a low, urgent tolling, wardens' bells, getting louder.

The regent rose, all patience gone.

"It seems," it said, "your friends have found my vault."
