## 13. Rook's Reckoning

The lantern hall smelled of cold iron and old grief.

Rook Vane had smuggled salt past customs men who could smell a lie through three layers of oilcloth. He had walked contraband over ice bridges that groaned like dying cattle. He had once spent nine days in a root cellar beneath a magistrate's own house while the man drank wine ten feet above his head. Fear was a country Rook knew the way other men knew their mother's kitchen.

But nothing had prepared him for the smell of the lantern vault's antechamber: wax and grave-dirt and something sweeter underneath, something that made the back of his teeth ache, like standing too close to a beehive full of sleeping souls.

Because that was what they were. Sleeping souls. Hundreds of them.

He stood in the shadow of a buttress carved to look like a rib of the mountain itself and counted the lights drifting in the dark below — small, slow, patient flames suspended in glass, each one burning its particular color. Amber. Green. A blue so pale it looked like remembering rather than light. They hung from chains of black iron that rose into a ceiling lost in gloom, row upon row upon row, an orchard of the stolen, tended by wardens who moved among them on silent feet with long brass poles, trimming wicks no living hand should touch.

*Somewhere down there is Tam.*

Rook pressed his back against the stone and did the thing he had done all his life, the thing that had kept him alive when braver men were dead: he calculated.

Isandro and Bren were elsewhere in the court — the taster gone toward whatever ledger-room held the queen's filthy secrets, the wolf limping but growling, buying them time with his blood. The plan had been simple, the way all good smuggling plans are simple: get in, get Sefa's soul, get out, and let the mountain swallow the rest. Rook had agreed to it with a nod and had not told them — had not let himself fully admit even to himself — that his feet would carry him here instead. To this hall. To this orchard.

*Tam Vane. Sold to the court eleven years ago for a debt of four hundred crowns, which is what a man's life costs when the crown decides his life is collateral.*

Rook had signed the papers himself. That was the arithmetic he'd never spoken aloud. He'd been twenty-two and drowning, and the magistrate had laid the choice out neat as coins on felt: your brother's soul, or both your necks. And Rook — clever Rook, careful Rook, the one who always found the third road — had taken the first road put in front of him and called it survival ever since.

Eleven years of telling himself there was no way in. Eleven years of learning every smuggler path through Thornfell *except* the one that mattered, keeping his maps deliberately blind, because a man can bear anything if he never looks directly at it.

Then Isandro Okonkwo had sat across from him in a dockside tavern with poison under their tongue and truth in their eyes, and said: *Your brother's soul is still burning down there. I've seen the debt rolls.*

Still burning. After eleven years.

Rook breathed out slowly and watched his breath curl away into the cold.

* * *

He had gotten this far by theft and nerve. The warden's cloak sat heavy on his shoulders, its collar stiff with someone else's sweat; the brass pole in his hands was warm where the dead warden's grip had warmed it before Rook left him folded behind a cistern. He had learned two things since entering the court: the wardens did not speak to one another, and they flinched — actually flinched, a small shudder at the shoulder — whenever they passed the row where the oldest lanterns burned.

The oldest lanterns burned green.

Rook moved down the rows with the unhurried gait of a man doing tedious work, because that was the whole secret of walking anywhere you didn't belong: never look like you're escaping, look like you're bored. He passed lantern after lantern and made himself look at none of them, because each glass held a face if you let your eyes settle — not literally, the flame had no face, but the mind supplied one anyway, some stranger's mother, some drowned child grown up somewhere without them, some brother with a gap between his front teeth and a laugh like a cart going downhill.

*Row forty. Row forty-one.* He had bribed a junior clerk three days' wages for the number, back when he'd still pretended to himself this was reconnaissance. *Row forty-two.*

There.

The lantern hung low, near the end of the row, as though even the court's filing system had given up on it. Small glass, dull chain, and inside — a flame the color of river water at dusk, brown-gold, unglamorous, stubborn. It guttered as Rook stopped before it, then steadied, leaning toward him the way a candle leans toward a draft.

Toward him.

Wardens trimmed these flames. Wardens fed them something from little brass cups. But no one had stood in front of Tam Vane's soul in eleven years, and the flame leaned toward Rook like a dog hearing footsteps on a road it had waited beside.

"Oh," Rook said, and his voice came out cracked down the middle. "Oh, you stupid, loyal—"

He caught himself. Looked up. Down the long aisle, a warden paused at its trimming, head tilting with the slow attention of a heron deciding whether the water moved.

Rook lowered his eyes, gripped his pole, and became furniture. Boredom. Tedium. The walk of a man whose feet hurt and whose shift would never end.

The warden moved on.

Rook stood there shaking, and hated himself for shaking, and did the arithmetic again because arithmetic was the only prayer he knew.

*Options.* Break the glass: noise, yes, but how much? These weren't thin market glasses; they were ward-glass, thick as a knuckle, and the chains were iron worked with sigils he could feel prickling the hairs of his arms at six feet distant. Take the whole lantern and run: heavier to carry, slower, but silent until the alarm — except the moment it left its hook, something would notice. Everything about this place noticed. The walls here had a pulse. He'd felt it since the gates, a heartbeat in the stone slower than any animal's, patient as debt.

And beneath the options, quiet as silt, the real calculation. The one he'd been running since the tavern.

*If I take Tam's lantern, I doom the mission. Isandro gets caught, or slowed, or killed. Sefa stays in her glass until spring — or forever. Three lives weighed against one, and one of them mine already half-spent.* Clever Rook. Careful Rook. The mathematics of cowardice dressed up in a smuggler's coat.

But there was another column in the ledger, the one he never let himself read.

*Tam took the blame for me once, when we were boys, for the broken mill-gate. Took a beating with a strap that split his back open, and never said my name. Not because he was brave. Because he loved me, and love does sums differently than fear does.*

Rook looked at the little brown-gold flame leaning toward him, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had spent eleven years doing sums.

* * *

He didn't decide. That was the strange thing, the thing he would tell Isandro later by firelight and never quite believe himself. There was no moment of resolve, no grand speech inside his skull. What happened was smaller and worse and better: his hands simply stopped listening to him.

They went to the chain first. His fingers, numbed by cold, fumbled at the hook, and the iron sigils bit him — little white sparks, a smell like struck flint — and he hissed and kept working, and the biting got worse, and he kept working, because pain was just information and information was just weather and he had walked through worse weather for less pay.

The lantern came free of its hook with a sound like a held breath released.

Nothing screamed. No bell. For three heartbeats the hall stayed vast and silent, and hope — stupid, fatal hope, the emotion that had killed better smugglers than him — lifted in Rook's chest like a bubble through dark water.

Then the flame inside the glass stood straight up.

All down the rows, in every direction, hundreds of flames stood straight up. Every soul in the orchard turned its color toward Tam Vane's lantern at once, amber and green and pale remembering-blue, a field of flowers opening toward one sun, and the light of them doubled and doubled again, and the dark at the top of the ceiling filled with a hum like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

The pulse in the stone stumbled. Skipped. Then began to race.

"No," Rook whispered. "No no no—"

A warden at the far end of the hall turned its head. It did not run. That was the horror of them — it simply *turned*, the way a door turns, and began to glide down the aisle with its brass pole held level as a lance, and from the gallery above came the soft ring of more hooks being drawn, more poles lifting, a sound like a hundred fishing lines cast into black water.

Rook ran.

He ran with the lantern clutched to his chest like a child, and the flame inside it burned hot enough now to feel through the glass, hot as a hand pressing back against his, and he thought absurdly, wildly: *he knows me, he knows me, he's been waiting eleven years and he knows me* — and then a pole swept out of a side aisle and he dropped, rolled, came up with his knife out because the cloak was no use now, the game was up, the only law left in this hall was weight and edge.

The warden loomed. Beneath the hood there was no face Rook could have named — a smoothness, a suggestion, like a thumbprint pressed into wet clay — and the pole came down and Rook did the ugly arithmetic of close quarters and drove his knife up under the robe's edge, into whatever passed for the soft parts, and the warden folded around the blade without crying out and went down like a tent.

More coming. The aisles filling. The bell-hum climbing toward a shriek.

Rook ran, and the lantern ran with him, its heat against his sternum like a second heart, and somewhere in the warren beyond the vault doors he heard the thing he'd been dreading and praying for in equal measure: a howl. Wolf-deep, ragged, furious. Bren. Alive, then, still fighting, still holding the rearguard they'd sworn to hold.

And threaded through the alarms, high and clear and impossibly calm, a voice calling names down the shifting halls — Isandro's voice, the taster's voice, pitched to carry:

*"ROOK VANE! If you've done what I think you've done, you'd best come find us — and bring the light!"*

Rook laughed. It came out of him like blood from a wound, helpless and wild, and he clutched his brother's soul to his chest and ran toward the voice, toward the wolf, toward the woman who had seen through him from the start and never once said *coward* aloud.

Behind him the great bell finished its ring at last, and every lantern in the orchard dimmed a fraction — the court, wounded, drawing its strength home to defend it.

Ahead of him, the halls began to move.
