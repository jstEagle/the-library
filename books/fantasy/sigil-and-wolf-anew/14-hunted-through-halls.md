## 14. Hunted Through Halls

The corridors of the mountain court did not stay where you left them.

Bren had understood this the way he understood most things — with his nose first. The air in the halls was old stone and cold iron and something sweeter beneath it, like sap running under bark, and that sweetness *moved*. It pooled in one passage and drained from another. It told him where the walls had been an hour ago, and where they would be by morning, and it told him — this was the part he did not like — that the walls were not walls at all, but something living, something grown rather than built, and grown things could be *grown elsewhere*.

He said as much to Isandro, low, as they crouched in a junction where three corridors met like the prongs of a trident.

"They shift," Bren said. "The whole place breathes. When the alarm went off in Rook's wake, I felt it through my paws before I heard the bells — a shudder, like a hillside deciding to move."

Isandro's face, in the lantern-glow of the corridor's veins of pale light, was doing the thing it always did: going very still, very careful, the servant's blankness pulled on like a mask even now, even here, a thousand feet under a mountain with no crown left to fool. But their eyes gave them away. They always did. They were reading the walls the way Bren read scent-trails.

"Then we don't follow the route we came," Isandro said. "We follow the breathing."

"That's what I'm telling you. Listen—" Bren flattened his ears. Somewhere behind them, down the corridor they'd come by, there was the sound he had been dreading since Rook's brother's empty lantern began to scream light into the dark: boots. Not many. Warden-boots made a particular sound, soft-soled but heavy-strided, iron at the ankle, and they did not hurry, because wardens never hurried. Hurrying meant fear, and fear meant the hunted might be worth fearing. These walked at the pace of men who knew the corridors would do their work for them.

"The vault is east," Isandro said. "Rook went west after his brother — gods keep him — and Sefa's lantern is—"

"I know where it is." Bren did, too. He'd marked it the way wolves mark everything: by smell. The lantern-hall smelled of glass and cold and, underneath, faintly, horribly, of *Sefa* — of her soul, which had a scent like bread and lavender and river-mud, and which had been growing thinner every hour they'd been inside. That thinning frightened him more than the wardens. Souls, it seemed, could starve.

"Then hear me," Isandro said, and took his wrist — his human wrist, because Bren had shifted back to run-and-talk, fur traded for fingers, and his skin still prickled with the wanting of the other shape. "When the wardens close, I need time. Not much. A hundred heartbeats, maybe two hundred, to reach the lantern and start the unbinding. You cannot be there for that. What I have to draw, I have to draw alone."

"You said that about the bridge toll, too."

"And I was right about the bridge toll." Isandro's grip tightened. Their hands were small and scarred from years of kitchen-heat and taster's needles, and they were stronger than they looked, and Bren loved them for both facts equally. "Bren. Look at me. If it goes wrong in there — if the unbinding calls something, if the regent comes, if the mountain itself wakes — I need to know that you are between me and it. That is the only way I can do this with a steady hand. I have spent my whole life keeping my hand steady while other people tried to kill me. Don't make tonight harder than it has to be."

It was, Bren thought later, a strange kind of request — asking to be guarded by naming it plainly, stripping away all the pretense that sacrifice is ever accidental. Wolves didn't ask. Wolves simply put themselves in the jaws when the pack needed it, and no one thanked them, and no one needed to. But Isandro was not a wolf. Isandro was a person who had hidden so long and so completely that even their courage came disguised, wrapped in practical language, offered as logistics. *I need you between me and it.* As though it were a matter of positioning. As though Bren couldn't hear, underneath, the same words every wolf-pack spoke without speaking: *don't let me die alone; don't let me die for nothing.*

"Two hundred heartbeats," Bren said. "Count them slow."

* * *

They ran.

Not the flat-out sprint of prey — the mountain court punished panic, he was sure of it, sure the corridors tasted fear in the sweat of intruders and grew thorned accordingly — but the long loping run of wolves covering ground, heads low, lungs working, choosing turns by instinct and Isandro's sigil-sense together. Bren led with his nose where the passages narrowed; Isandro led with whatever sense let them feel the sigils sleeping in the stone, waking as they passed, humming like struck bells that hadn't finished ringing. Twice Isandro stopped dead and pressed a palm flat to the wall, and twice the wall's slow grinding halted — held, Bren guessed, by some wordless bargain between the gift in Isandro's blood and the older magic in the mountain. Kin recognizing kin. It made the hair along his spine stand up every time.

The third time, the wall did not stop.

It opened instead — swung inward with a groan of living stone into a gallery Bren had no map for, tall and ribbed like the inside of a throat, and standing in it, lamplight pooling silver around their feet, were six wardens.

Six. He counted twice out of disbelief. Wardens traveled in threes; three was doctrine, three was enough for any intruder, three kept each other honest. Six meant they had merged two hunting parties. Six meant someone above — the regent, or worse, the regent's rival faction, the poisoners whose work Isandro had foiled at the feast — wanted this corridor sealed with bodies in it.

The wardens were not what Bren had expected. He'd imagined crown-men, the queen's grey-cloaked hounds, all sneer and steel. These were quieter creatures. Robes the color of deep water. Faces masked in smooth bone-white plates carved with sigils Bren's eye slid off of, the way his gaze slid off ice on a river — *do not look directly*, the carvings seemed to say, *you will not like what sees you looking back*. And they carried no swords. They carried staves tipped with lantern-glass, small ones, and the glass was not empty.

Bren smelled what was in them. He wished he hadn't. Each little lantern held a soul — dim, guttering, terrified — and the wardens used them the way a man uses a torch: souls burned as fuel, souls spent as light. One of the six lanterns flickered in a rhythm Bren recognized, a stutter-pulse like a heartbeat missing every fourth beat, and his stomach turned over, because he knew that rhythm. He'd slept beside it for three nights in the smuggler cache while its owner told stories to keep them all from despairing.

Rook Vane's brother.

Rook had freed him — or thought he had. The lantern Rook shattered had been real; Bren had smelled the ghost of the brother's soul scatter into the dark like smoke. But a soul, once loosed in this place, did not escape. It drifted. It drifted until a warden caught it, as a fisherman catches fish, and bottled it again, and here it was, four galleries away, burning in a stranger's staff so that strangers could hunt more comfortably in the dark.

Something in Bren's chest went hard and hot and stayed that way for the rest of the night.

"Run," Isandro said, and did not wait, and Bren did not argue, because arguing was a luxury of people who were not being hunted through shifting corridors by six soul-lantern bearers. They ran *through*. The gallery was wide enough for wolf-speed, and Bren put himself ahead of Isandro as they passed the wardens — not attacking, not yet, just *between*, the oldest trick in the pack-book, the blocker's position, the one who takes the hit so the runner doesn't have to.

The wardens let them pass. That was the trap, and Bren saw it a half-second too late, the way you always see traps a half-second too late. Behind them, the gallery mouth they'd entered by ground shut like a jaw. Ahead, the far arch — the one leading toward the lantern-vault, the only way forward — began to grind closed, slow as a glacier, patient as winter.

And the wardens turned, unhurried, and began to walk.

Not run. Walk. Because they did not need to catch anyone. They only needed the door to finish closing, and doors here closed at the speed of mountains, and mountains were faster than grief.

Bren stopped in the middle of the gallery and turned around.

There was a moment — he would carry it to his grave, however long that was — where he stood on the cold stone with the six white masks walking toward him and the arch closing behind him and Isandro sprinting for the gap, and he felt the whole shape of the choice settle over him like snowfall. Quietly. Completely. There was no drama in it. Wolves do not dramatize. The pack-of-three needed Isandro alive and free and at that lantern, and the pack-of-three had exactly one creature in it built to hold six wardens in a throat-shaped room, and that creature was currently wearing skin and finding it inconvenient.

He shifted.

It took four heartbeats, the full change, muscle and bone remembering themselves, man folding down into wolf the way a tent folds into its bag — and he used those four heartbeats well, roaring the change loud, letting the wardens see it, because a wolf-shifter fighting in the half-shape was a legend even wardens had heard of, and legends hesitate. Hesitation bought heartbeats. Heartbeats bought Isandro distance. Everything, tonight, was measured in heartbeats.

By the fifth beat he was fully wolf, and by the sixth the first warden had raised its staff, and by the seventh the little lantern at its tip flared, and the soul inside it screamed.

Bren had heard wolves howl. He had heard men scream. He had never heard a soul scream, and he hoped — with the fierce, futile hope of things already past hoping — that he would forget the sound by morning. It wasn't sound, exactly. It bypassed the ears and landed somewhere behind the sternum, a vibration of pure *wrongness*, and it did what it was designed to do: it stopped him mid-stride, staggered him, turned his legs to water for one precious second that the warden spent well, sweeping the staff around and loosing a lash of pale fire off the lantern's tip.

The lash caught him across the shoulder.

Fire made of soul-light did not burn like fire. It burned like *grief* — cold at the center, hotter than any forge at the edge, and it went through fur and hide and found the muscle beneath and bit, and kept biting, and Bren felt the wound open in a long diagonal line from shoulder to ribs and felt, worse, something in the wound that was not blood. The lash carried a little of the screaming soul with it. It left residue. It left *hunger* — a tiny cold seed of want planted in the torn flesh, whispering that flesh was fuel, that warmth was currency, that everything alive was something to spend.

He shook it off. Mostly. Enough. He was a wolf, and wolves know pain the way rivers know stones — intimately, constantly, and without being ruled by it.

The second warden came at him with the staff reversed, trying to club, and Bren went under the swing and up through the guard and took the warden's arm in his jaws. Bone-white robe, bone-white mask, and underneath, when his teeth found the forearm, flesh like anyone's flesh, warm and salt and afraid. He did not break it. He *shook* it — once, hard, the way a wolf shakes a rat — and the staff went spinning, and the warden went down, and the little lantern on the floor rolled, guttering, its captive soul flickering like a candle in wind.

Three heartbeats. Maybe four. He was aware, distantly, through the red haze at the edge of his vision, of the arch ahead, and of Isandro reaching it, and of the gap still open — a wolf's width, a man's width, narrowing — and of Isandro turning in the gap to look back at him.

Don't, Bren thought, with the whole force of his mind. *Don't you dare come back.*

Isandro stood in the closing arch for one heartbeat — one — face naked in a way Bren had almost never seen it, all the masks gone, all the hiding gone, just the raw expression underneath, which was love, plain and unarmored and useless as a spoon against a flood. Then Isandro did the hardest thing Bren had ever watched a person do, which was nothing. Isandro turned and went through, and the stone came down behind them like a verdict, and the sound of it was the sound of a mountain swallowing.

* * *

Five wardens left. He'd dropped one; the other five had learned caution, which was bad news dressed as good.

They spread. Of course they spread. Five points of a star, staves low, lanterns burning, and the gallery's throat-shape suddenly became a noose-shape, because the walls — the living, breathing, grown walls — had begun to move. Not fast. Nothing here was fast. But the ribbed stone was contracting by inches, patient as digestion, and Bren understood with a wolf's clean clarity that the room intended to be much smaller soon, and that everything in it would be smaller too.

His shoulder was bad. He catalogued it the way he catalogued everything, nose-first, pain-second: the gash was deep, the muscle beneath torn, the cold seed of soul-hunger still whispering in the wound, and blood was running freely down his foreleg, dripping from his elbow-joint onto the stone, and the stone *drank it*. He watched a drop vanish into the floor like rain into sand and understood that the mountain court fed on blood the way it fed on souls, on debt, on everything given unwillingly. Fine. Then it would get his blood willingly. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of him.

The first warden to press the attack came from his blind side, the wounded side, smart, and Bren let it commit — let the staff rise, let the lantern flare, let the soul-scream build to that staggering peak — and then moved *into* it instead of away. Every instinct said flee the scream. Instincts could be lied to. He charged through the sound like a wolf charging through a river in flood, took the lash across the flank rather than the face, and hit the warden at full weight, two hundred pounds of wolf and fury and grief for a soul screaming in a bottle, and they went down together in a tangle of robe and fur, and Bren's teeth found the mask and crushed it, and beneath the mask the warden had a face, and the face was young, and the eyes were frightened, and the eyes were the eyes of someone conscripted, someone taken the way Sefa had been taken, sold the way souls were sold, and Bren — this was the part he would think about later, in the bad hours — hesitated.

One heartbeat of hesitation. His blood on the stone, the walls closing, five enemies, and he spent a heartbeat noticing that his enemy was also someone's child.

The warden's companion did not hesitate. The lash came down across Bren's hindquarters and he spun, snarling, the world going white at the edges, and drove the second warden back with sheer committed violence, backing it against the moving wall until the wall itself pressed at its shoulders and it broke and fled down a side-gallery that swallowed it whole.

Four. Three on their feet, one crawling. The walls tighter. His legs heavier. Blood everywhere now, his own, a spreading map of him on the floor, and the mountain drinking it with an appetite he could feel through his pads, a slow pull, a tide.

He thought about Rook, who was somewhere west of here holding his brother's freed soul against his chest like a bird with a broken wing, running for gates that might already be sealing. He thought about Sefa, whose soul-starved scent had been getting thinner all night, bread and lavender fading toward nothing. He thought about the cache-storm, and the confessions, and the night three strangers became something like family — Rook saying *I abandoned him*, Isandro saying *my immunity was built on poisons meant for others*, Bren saying nothing at all because his confession was written into his body, into the brand the pack had seared into his shoulder for leaving during the last purge, the mark that said *traitor* in a language older than crowns. He had chosen the found pack that night on the high pass, broken pack-law to save Isandro, fought the pack's second and won and lost and won something better than winning. The pack-of-three. It had held through storms and bridges and courts. It would hold now, or it would hold broken, but it would hold.

The third warden was clever. It stopped attacking entirely. It set its staff upright on the stone, lantern glowing, and stepped back, and simply waited, and its waiting said everything: *the walls will do it, the blood-loss will do it, the hunger-seed in your wounds will do it. Why should we spend anything? We are owed, not owing.*

So Bren attacked *it*, and the attack was ugly and short and cost him more than it cost the warden, and it worked anyway, because commitment beats patience nine times in ten and the tenth time doesn't matter if you're already dead. He went through the staff-guard, took a lash across the neck — grazing, thank every god of fur and field, grazing — and bowled the warden over and pinned it and could not finish it, because his jaws were shaking now, trembling with blood-loss and cold, and because underneath the shaking he remembered the young one's frightened eyes and could not unremember them. He held the pin. Three heartbeats. Long enough for the warden to understand it had lost. Long enough for the warden to crawl backward out from under him, gather its staff, and retreat — and the retreat of the last two, together, down the gallery, unhurried, confident, was somehow worse than an attack. They weren't leaving. They were letting the room finish.

The walls were nearly closed now. A den's width of space, maybe less, ribbed stone pressing in from every side, and Bren stood in the shrinking dark on three good legs and one bad one, bleeding from shoulder and flank and neck, the hunger-seeds whispering in all three wounds, and he did the last thing left to do.

He howled.

Not a cry for help. The pack-of-three was beyond help; everyone knew it; help was a story for other nights. This howl was a report. It was the signal every wolf knows and no human ever quite learns — the long note that says *I am here, I am hurt, I am not done, and what I was guarding is guarded.* It went up through the stone and out through whatever cracks the mountain allowed, and somewhere east of him, beyond a sealed arch, he chose to believe it reached Isandro's ears, and he chose to believe Isandro understood it, because understanding it was the only payment he would ever ask.

Then the walls touched his fur, and kept pressing, gently, enormously, the way a hand closes around a moth, and Bren folded down among his own blood and lay still and let the mountain decide what it had caught.

* * *

It decided slowly. Mountains always do.

The pressure eased. The stone, having drunk its fill of his blood, seemed to lose interest in crushing him — or perhaps the wardens recalled it, or perhaps some older law of the court forbade wasting what could instead be *kept*. Bren felt himself lifted — not carried, *grown*, the floor rising under him like a tide coming in, stone flowing around his body in bands, snug at the barrel of his chest, snug at his flanks, holding him the way amber holds an insect. A niche opened in the wall and received him, and the wall closed over him to the depth of a wolf's coat, and he hung there, embedded, breathing, watching the corridor through a lens of stone ground thin as horn.

A display. He understood that much. The court did not waste examples, and a wolf-shifter — branded traitor by his birth-pack, hunted by the queen's purge, found defending a gifted sigil-smith in the deep halls — was worth showing to the next intruders, the next debt-brokers' clerks, the next fools who came down the root-doors thinking love was a key.

Fine. Let him be a lesson. Lessons bled too.

Time went strange in the stone. He slept and woke and slept, and the hunger-seeds in his wounds grew roots, and he dreamed in fragments — the high pass, the pack's second spitting his name like a curse, the toll-keeper's river asking for true names, Sefa's laugh in the kitchen doorway years ago, flour on her hands — and between dreams he listened, because listening was all he had left, and a wolf embedded in a living wall hears everything the wall hears.

He heard the corridors breathe. He heard boots, sometimes, passing, and once the unmistakable drag-step of Rook Vane's bad knee going west at speed, and his heart lurched and he could do nothing, nothing, nothing, and the nothing was the worst of it, worse than the wounds.

And then — hours later, or days, stone-time was weather-time, it changed without changing — he heard something else.

It came from the east. From the direction of the vault. It was not boots. It was not bells. It was a voice, Isandro's voice, pitched low and carrying the way voices carry through stone when the stone wants them to, and it was saying words Bren did not know, syllables in a rhythm older than the crown, older than the court, older maybe than the mountain — and with each repetition the walls around him trembled, fine dust sifting from the seams, the amber-grip loosening by hairsbreadths, the whole deep body of the mountain shivering like a horse feeling thunder under the horizon.

An unbinding. It had to be. Isandro had reached the lantern. Isandro was paying the price — blood and name, the full exposure, the claiming — and the mountain, which was built on bindings, on debts, on souls held in glass, was coming apart at the foundation, seam by seam, debt by debt.

The stone around Bren's chest cracked.

Not much. A line, thin as a hair, running from his shoulder to his ribs, right through the oldest wound. But through the crack came air — real air, moving air, smelling of stone-dust and ozone and, faintly, impossibly, of *Sefa*: bread and lavender and river-mud, fuller than it had been in days, a soul made whole again and breathing free somewhere above him.

Bren strained against the failing stone, three legs and a grudge, and howled — not the report-howl this time but the other one, the one wolves save for the moment the pack comes back over the ridge, the one that means *here, here, I am here, I knew you would come.*

Above him, through a mile of waking rock, he felt — actually felt, the way he felt weather in his bad shoulder — the mountain begin to close its gates.

And he felt, closer, nearer, coming down through the cracking dark with the surety of a promise, footsteps. Two sets. One limping.

One not.
