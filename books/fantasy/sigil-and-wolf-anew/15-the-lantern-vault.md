## 15. The Lantern Vault

The corridor let Isandro go the way a throat lets go of a swallowed thing.

One moment the walls were moving — stone grinding against stone with a sound like millstones the size of cathedrals, the warden-lights swinging their long shadows across the passage in slow, deliberate arcs — and then the rock simply opened before them, and there was a door, and the door was old.

Older than the crown. Older than the queen's grandmother's grandmother. The sigils cut into its frame were of the deep-cut style, the kind Isandro had only seen in the forbidden folios smuggled up through Thornfell in their apprentice years: strokes that predated the common script, that predated the *idea* of scripts, lines drawn by hands that had learned geometry from roots and riverbeds. And beneath those ancient marks, worn nearly smooth by centuries of touching, ran a second layer of carving — newer, cruder, crown-work — as if someone had tried to claim an older house by nailing their name over the door.

Isandro knew that impulse. They had spent thirty years nailing plainness over themselves.

Behind them, far back down the shifting halls, Bren was still fighting. Isandro could hear it even through the stone — not the howls now, those had gone quiet, but the wet, rhythmic sounds of a body being spent like coin, each one less than the last. Every step they took away from that sound was a step paid for in wolf's blood. *Hold,* Isandro thought at the stone, uselessly, the way you might think *hold* at a friend falling. *Hold on.*

Rook Vane pressed close at their shoulder, his brother's empty lantern-hook still swinging from his belt where he'd torn it free of the warden-rack, and his face in the dim light had the gray look of a man who had finally run out of calculations. "That door," he said hoarsely. "I've heard about that door my whole life. Smugglers sing songs about that door. Nobody who's gone past it has come back to correct the verses."

"Then we'll be the first correction," Isandro said, and put their palm flat against the stone.

The gift rose in them without being called — it had been rising all night, ever since the blood-sigil, ever since they had spoken their own name aloud in a dead man's voice and felt the mountain *hear* it — and the old carvings warmed under their hand like a hearthstone remembering fire. Not resisting. Recognizing.

*The court's magic shares roots with your own,* they had realized at the outer gate, and the realization returned now with its edges sharpened: this place had been built by gifted hands. Whatever the crown believed it had conquered, whatever the regent believed he ruled, the bones of this mountain were sigil-craft, and sigil-craft answered to its own.

The door opened inward, silently, like an eye.

* * *

Cold came out to meet them. Not the clean cold of the high passes they'd crossed with Bren leading them off every map — this cold had texture, weight, a flavor. Isandro's trained tongue identified it before their mind caught up: iron, and something sweeter underneath, faintly rotten, like fruit left too long in a sealed jar. The taste of preservation. The taste of things kept past their season.

They stood at the top of a stair that spiraled down into a vast round chamber, and the chamber was full of light.

Not torchlight. Not the blue warden-glow. Lanterns — thousands upon thousands of lanterns, hanging in tiers from invisible chains, rising in slow spirals toward a ceiling lost in dark, each one holding a soft, sourceless luminescence the color of a candle seen through milk. Some burned steady and bright. Some flickered. Some were so dim they were barely distinguishable from the dark between them, little dying stars strung through a man-made sky.

And some, here and there among the tiers, had simply gone out.

Rook made a sound low in his chest, half breath, half wound. "Souls," he said. "Gods below. It's true. It's all true."

Isandro had known. Had known since the toll-keeper's bridge, since Rook first spoke of soul-debt in the storm-bound cache with his hands shaking around a cup of melted snow. Knowing was one thing. Standing beneath a sky of harvested souls was another thing entirely, and Isandro stood very still at the top of the stairs and let it enter them the way they let poison enter them — deliberately, fully, because only by taking it into yourself could you learn what would kill you.

Somewhere in that luminous dark was Sefa.

Isandro descended. The stairs were cut in the same deep-rooted style as the door, and as they went down, the lantern-light touched their face, and they understood with a lurch why the air tasted of preservation: the light itself was patient. It did not flicker with any draft. There was no draft. This place had been sealed against time, and everything in it was being kept — slowly, gently, terribly — the way a taster keeps a sample dish warm until the moment comes to serve it.

They found her tier by feel more than sight. Isandro had spent years learning Sefa's presence in a crowded kitchen: the particular rhythm of her movements, the warmth she carried like a stove kept banked. That rhythm was here, faint as a heartbeat under wool, and Isandro followed it past lantern after lantern — each one they passed, they could not help looking, could not help seeing faces. Faces in the glass. Dim, dreaming, diminished, but *faces*, and some of them turned as Isandro went by, the way flowers turn toward a window.

A woman with river-gray eyes. A boy no older than fourteen whose lantern guttered when he saw Isandro looking, flaring once with something that might have been hope. An old man whose light was almost nothing, a coal's last memory of fire.

*The Purge is harvest.* The thought arrived whole, unbidden, terrible, and Isandro pushed it away because Sefa was near, Sefa was *here* —

— and then they found her.

Her lantern hung at chest height on a chain of black iron, and inside the glass her soul was a small warm gold, kitchen-fire colored, and it was dimming. Isandro could see it dimming, could see the way winter outside was drinking it through whatever conduit fed these cages, the gold thinner than it should be, thinner than the girl who used to slip Isandro honey-cakes and hum off-key and say *you work too hard, Iso, even your shadow works too hard.*

"Sefa," Isandro whispered, and put both hands around the glass.

It was warm. Warm as a living hand. And through the sigil-sense that Isandro could no longer fully suppress — the gift rising in them like floodwater against a door they had stopped bracing — they felt her, faintly, the way you feel sun through a wall. Not gone. *Kept.*

"I'm here," they said. Their voice cracked on it, and they let it crack; there was no one left in this mountain to perform steadiness for. "I'm here, and I am not leaving without you, and I am going to burn down whoever built this."

"Isandro." Rook's voice, strange and tight. "You need to see this."

He was standing at the tier's end, where the spiral of lanterns gave onto a gallery of shelving — and the shelves were full of books. Ledgers. Rows and rows of them, bound in leather the color of dried blood, spines stamped with the crown's thorn-and-tower sigil.

Isandro crossed to him slowly. Somewhere above, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, something boomed — the mountain's gates grinding, or warden-horns, or Bren's last stand becoming his last *last*. None of it mattered yet. Nothing mattered yet but the book Rook had pulled from the shelf and was holding out to them with both hands, the way you hand someone a live coal.

The cover read: *Receipts, Gifted Persons, Reign of Queen Marenna, Years One Through Nine.*

Marenna. The old queen. Dead forty years.

Isandro opened it.

The handwriting was clerk-perfect, column after column, and it did not take a gifted mind to read it — only a literate one, and a stomach. *Names.* Names of gifted people, listed with dates of seizure, dates of delivery, and prices. Not execution records. Not imprisonment rolls. *Prices.* Paid to whom — and there, in the column marked REMITTED TO, written again and again and again in the same tidy hand, year after year, reign after reign:

*To the Crown. To the Crown. To the Crown.*

"They weren't purging us," Isandro said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them; somewhere very deep, a part of them had decided that if it shook, they would fall apart, and they could not afford to fall apart in front of four thousand souls. "All of it. The edicts. The informants' rewards. The criers. The sweeps." They turned a page. Another page. Forty years of pages, and the columns never changed. "It was never justice. It was accounting. She's been selling us to pay her debts for longer than I've been alive, and the Purge—" The word came out of them like a tooth. "The Purge is harvest. She planted fear so we'd hide, and hiding makes us easy to count, and counting makes us easy to *sell*."

Rook said nothing. He was looking at the ledger with the expression of a man watching a map of his whole life redraw itself — every route he'd ever run, every cargo he'd pretended not to notice, every magistrate who'd looked through him with that particular knowing indifference. His brother's name would be in these books. Isandro watched him understand that, watched him find the shelf for his brother's era with his eyes, and watched him choose — visibly, deliberately — to set the grief aside and keep working, because that was what family looked like down here: love postponed until it could be useful.

Isandro did not postpone theirs. Isandro let it become fuel.

"The ledgers are the court's own," they said, thinking aloud now, fast, the way they thought when tasting a dish with too many ingredients. "Crown-stamped, regent-witnessed. This isn't evidence I could forge or that anyone could call forgery. This is the mountain testifying against the queen." They pulled volume after volume, checking dates, checking seals. "Every crier in the lowlands reads from sheets the palace writes. Fine. Then we write new sheets. We copy these pages and we send them everywhere — garrisons, guildhalls, rival houses, every market square with a post to nail them to. Let her purge turn around and look at her."

"You're talking about the fall of a crown," Rook said.

"I'm talking about receipts," Isandro said. "She always taught us to follow the money. So we'll make everyone else follow it too."

They worked quickly, tearing blank leaves from the back of a spare ledger, copying in the swift clerk-hand they'd learned precisely so no one would ever suspect them of anything — the irony of it sat in their chest like a swallowed coal. Name, date, price, remitted-to. Name, date, price. Each line a person. Each person a lantern overhead. Isandro copied until their fingers ached, and as they copied, the rage in them stopped being a wildfire and became something better: a forge. Shaped. Contained. Pointed.

Purpose, they thought. Rage with a job to do.

It was Rook who noticed the last thing. He had drifted along the shelves while Isandro wrote, and suddenly he made a sound — small, punched-out — and said, "Isandro. The most recent volume. Look at the projections."

The newest ledger lay open on the shelf, its final pages filled not with completed entries but with estimates, forward-looking sums, neat annotations in a different, finer hand. Projected yields for the current Purge. Quotas by province. And at the bottom of the final page, underlined twice in the regent's own ink, a single line that Isandro read three times before its meaning finished arriving:

*Balance due to the Court, payable in souls, at first frost — OR renewal of the Treaty of Roots, terms per original grant.*

"Treaty of Roots," Isandro breathed. "The gate sigils. The crown didn't conquer this place. It *contracted* with it." They looked up, past the tiers of lanterns, toward the dark where the regent waited somewhere with his courteous voice and his offer of a stronger soul for a weaker one. "The queen owes debts to the mountain. The mountain holds the paper. And the paper says the payment is *us*."

Above them — distant, enormous, final — the mountain groaned like a ship changing course, and every lantern in the vault flickered at once, four thousand small lights shivering together, and in the glass at Isandro's back, Sefa's gold flame dipped low.

And then the temperature of the room changed. Not colder. *Attended.* The particular pressure of a presence that has been listening for some time, the way Isandro had felt a thousand times in the queen's banquet hall — the sense of being tasted, now, instead of tasting.

At the head of the stair, framed against the spiraling light, stood the regent of the court beneath the mountain.

He was not armed. He did not need to be. He folded his long hands and inclined his head with terrible courtesy, and his voice, when it came, was warm as a dish served at the perfect temperature.

"So," he said. "The taster has found the pantry."
