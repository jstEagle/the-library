## 16. Bargain with Winter

The vault had no ceiling that Isandro could find. The lantern-light went up and up into stone that darkened by degrees to something like night, and somewhere in that false darkness the mountain breathed — a slow, mineral exhalation that stirred the flames of a thousand soul-lanterns without moving their glass. Each flame burned its own color. Amber for the old ones. A sickly green for those taken recently. And one small blue-white light, three shelves down, guttering like a candle left out in wind.

Sefa.

Isandro stood before her lantern with the court's ledger open against their forearm, the ink still wet enough under their thumb to smear, and listened to the sound of the regent's approach.

It was not footsteps. The regent of the court beneath the mountain did not walk so much as *arrive*, each step a small concession the stone made to her presence. The air changed first — colder, then warmer, then cold again, as though winter itself were being argued with. Isandro did not turn around. They closed the ledger, pressed it flat beneath their palm, and watched Sefa's flame shiver.

"You have been reading," the regent said.

Her voice was the voice of a woman who had once been young in a country that no longer existed. It carried frost in it, and underneath the frost, something older and wearier — the sound of a debt collector who had grown tired of counting.

"I read everything I'm served," Isandro said. "Old habit."

Behind them, at the vault's mouth, Bren lay propped against the doorframe where they had dragged him, one arm pressed to his ribs, his breathing a wet, labored rasp that Isandro heard the way they heard poison in wine — constantly, helplessly, cataloguing every wrongness. Rook stood over him with a stolen warden's blade held low and badly, the grip of a man who had never fought anything he couldn't outrun. Two freed souls drifted near his shoulders in their lantern-glass, dim as foxfire, waiting to be carried home.

And between them and the door, the regent unfolded herself from the dark.

She was tall the way a stalactite is tall. Her gown was the grey of deep snow at dusk, and her face was beautiful in the manner of things carved rather than born — symmetrical, patient, wrong. Her eyes were the color of the lantern-flames around them, all colors at once, and none.

"Three intruders," she said. "One dying. One thief. One clerk who is not a clerk." She tilted her head, and the motion was slow as ice forming. "You took the ledgers from my own shelf, taster. Do you know what happens to thieves in my court?"

"They're given positions of trust," Isandro said, "if they're useful."

Something moved across the regent's face that on a living woman might have been surprise, or might have been appetite.

* * *

They had walked into this vault knowing it might be a trap. That had been the plan's whole architecture, such as it was — walk in, take what was true, and let the truth be armor. But plans made in smuggler caches by firelight always looked different under the actual weight of the thing they meant to move, and the regent's attention was a weight like a glacier's.

"Here is what will happen," the regent said. She drifted closer, and where she passed, the nearest lanterns leaned toward her like flowers toward sun, hungry and involuntary. "The first frost is four days gone. You felt it on the stairs — the cold coming down through the root-stone. When the last frost-seal closes, these gates shut for a generation. Twenty years, taster. Your wolf will be dust in twenty years. Your smuggler will be an old man telling lies about the time he almost robbed a mountain." She paused before Sefa's lantern, and her shadow fell across the little blue-white flame, dimming it further. "Leave her. Walk out while the doors still answer you. Go home. Burn your evidence or keep it; I care nothing for the queen's politics above. The dead above ground are not my concern."

"And if we stay?"

"Then you stay forever, and your souls join my treasury, and in twenty years some other desperate creature comes down looking for *you*."

The words fell into the vault and rang there. Isandro let them ring. This was the other old habit, the one even older than reading everything served: when a dish was set before you, you did not eat at once. You looked. You smelled. You waited to see what the cook wanted you to taste, because whatever the cook wanted you to taste was never the whole meal.

The regent wanted them afraid. Fear made people leave things behind. Fear had made Rook leave his brother, once; fear had made half the souls in this room leave their bodies upstairs in the world, walking around hollow, paying debts they never borrowed. The regent's whole economy ran on fear, and here she was, minting more of it, holding it out to them like coins.

Isandro looked past her at Sefa's flame.

It had dimmed since the banquet hall, since the feast where Isandro had tasted henbane in the regent's cup and swallowed the warning along with the wine. Three days ago, maybe less. Souls in glass did not burn steadily; they burned *down*, the way a lamp burns down, and the court called that interest, and the world above would call it murder, and both would be right.

"You're offering us the door," Isandro said slowly, "because you think we can't take what we came for."

"I am offering you mercy, which is rarer than gold down here."

"No." Isandro turned fully to face her at last, and let the ledger show in their hand, let the regent see that they knew exactly what they held. "Mercy would be giving her back. What you're offering is a discount. You'd rather lose one soul than let three thieves start pulling threads in your vault. Because if we leave with her — with proof, with names, with dates going back forty years — then every gifted soul above ground stops being currency and starts being *evidence*. The harvest ends. Not because anyone beat you. Because you got caught."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like flour sifting through fingers. Somewhere far above, water found a new path through stone.

"You are either very brave," the regent said at last, "or very stupid, and my long experience has taught me that the two are indistinguishable right up until the moment of death."

"Then it's fortunate," Isandro said, "that I'm neither. I'm a taster. My entire life is the space between brave and stupid. It's a very narrow space. You learn to live in it."

Bren made a sound against the doorframe — half laugh, half cough, blood in it. "She does this," he rasped. "At toll-bridges. At pack-fights. She just — stands there and *talks* until the world gives up—"

"Hush," Isandro said, without turning. "Save your breath. You'll want it for the argument later about who carried whom."

* * *

The regent circled them. It was not a threat-display, or not only that; there was something assessing in it, the way Isandro themselves circled a dish of unknown provenance. Around and around, her grey hem whispering over stone, the lanterns bowing as she passed.

"The wolf is dying," she observed. "Warden steel. There is no salve in my court for it, and none above ground either — the blades are quenched in cold that doesn't exist anymore anywhere else. He has perhaps two days of pain, then a week of fever, then an end. Unless." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Unless he stays. My physicians are old, but they are thorough. His soul could remain in his body *and* in my keeping — collateral, like the maid's. He would heal. He would simply not leave."

"And me?" Rook's voice cracked on the word. He had not spoken since the alarms, since the moment he'd smashed his brother's lantern-glass and the warden-horns had begun to sing through the halls. Now he lifted his chin, and Isandro saw him plant his feet the way he'd planted them on the Toll-Keeper's Bridge — a coward deciding, one more time, to stand anyway. "What's your offer for me? Let me guess. My brother goes back in the glass, and I carry him out, and I owe you—"

"You owe me nothing," the regent said. "Your brother's soul was bought honestly, from a magistrate, in the year of the last Purge. Debts honestly incurred are sacred in this court. That is the difference between us and the crown above, taster, and you may write it in your little book: we do not lie about what we are."

"No," Isandro agreed quietly. "You just make lying unnecessary. Everything here is written down. Every horror, signed and dated and filed. It's the most honest place I've ever stood, and I've stood in a lot of rooms full of poison."

The regent stopped circling. She came to stand directly before Isandro, close enough that the cold of her rolled off her like air off a winter river, close enough that Isandro could see that her eyes were not colorless at all but every color in the vault, drawn in, reflected — a thousand small flames swimming in the grey.

"So," she said softly. "We come to it. You have your evidence. You have your defiance. You have your dying wolf and your trembling thief and your guttering little maid, and four days of door. Tell me, food-taster, servant of a queen who would sell you as readily as she sold your friend — what do you imagine you have that I could possibly want?"

And there it was. The opening. Isandro had been watching for it the way they watched for almond-bitterness under honey, and now it lay on the table between them, and they picked it up with great care.

"A life," Isandro said. "Yours."

* * *

The vault went very still. Even the mountain seemed to stop breathing.

"Explain," the regent said, and the softness of it was worse than any roar.

"Three nights ago you sat down to a feast in your own hall. Seven courses. Wine from the deep cellars, poured by your own steward, decanted by your own seneschal, guarded by wardens at every step of the journey from cask to cup." Isandro held her gaze. "And in the third cup of the second course, henbane. Not enough to kill — you are old and deep-rooted and it would take a lake of henbane to kill you — but enough to slow you. To blur you. To make you *manageable*, for one evening, while certain parties among your own faction moved certain documents and spoke to certain allies about how the regency has grown *tired*, how the regent has grown *careless*, how perhaps it was time for younger hands on the older seals."

"You cannot know this."

"I tasted it," Isandro said simply. "I am the best tongue in two kingdoms, and I tasted it in the time it takes to swallow. Then I warned you. You lived. Your rivals spent that evening watching a regent who should have been dull and pliant and instead was sharp as broken glass, and they have spent three days since wondering how, and wondering who, and getting *frightened* — and frightened factions in courts like yours do stupid things, which is why your wardens' horns have sounded twice today already for reasons that have nothing to do with us."

The regent had not moved. But the temperature of the vault had dropped, degree by degree, frost feathering out across the stone floor from the hem of her gown in slow white ferns.

"You saved my life," she said. "Or so you claim."

"I don't claim it. I *state* it. And in your court — your honest, ledgered, debt-sacred court — what is a life owed?"

The frost reached Isandro's boots. Their breath came out white. Behind them, Rook had gone gray-faced, and Bren had pushed himself up off the doorframe despite everything, growl building low in his ruined chest, ready to spend the last of himself on this. Isandro raised one hand behind their back: *wait*. Wait. We are not done. We are not even close to done.

The regent looked at them for a long, long moment. The thousand flames swam in her eyes.

"A life-debt," she said finally, "is the oldest instrument in my treasury. Older than the lanterns. Older than the seals. It binds the debtor absolutely, and it binds the creditor to name a price the debtor can pay — for a debt beyond ability is no debt at all, only cruelty, and cruelty is the crown's trade, not mine." Her lips thinned, and Isandro understood suddenly, with a taster's certainty, that she was not angry. She was *delighted*, in the way of a woman who has played ten thousand years of solitaire and finally been dealt an interesting hand. "You learned our law faster than my own wardens. Who taught you?"

"A smuggler's ledger, a dead grandmother's grimoire, and twenty years of tasting other people's intentions," Isandro said. "I had good teachers. Most of them are dead. Some of them are in your glass."

"Name your price, then, creditor." The regent spread her hands, and the frost bloomed outward to the walls. "Within my power to pay. Choose carefully. I honor my debts to the letter, and the letter is a cage with fine bars."

Isandro had rehearsed this. In the cache, in the storm, in the dark hours when Bren slept and Rook pretended to sleep, they had turned the phrasing over like a coin, testing for clipped edges. Because the letter was a cage, and the regent would pay to the letter, and so the letter had to hold everything.

"Sefa Okonkwo's soul," Isandro said, "released from your keeping, unbound, whole, and free to leave this mountain with us — with the debt discharged fully, no residue, no lien, no interest outstanding. And safe passage for the four of us and any souls we lawfully carry, to whatever door stands open, before the frost-seal closes."

"Not the ledgers," the regent said at once. "The books stay."

"The books were never part of the price. I already have what I need from the books." Isandro tapped their temple. "Every figure. Every date. Forty years of the queen's signature in your margins. You can keep your paper, regent. Paper burns. Memory walks out your front door."

For the first time, the regent laughed. It was a terrible sound, thin and bright as ice cracking on a pond, and it echoed up through the vault and out into the halls where, distantly, horns answered it.

"Ohh," she breathed. "Oh, you are *wasted* on the queen. Very well. The maid goes free, the debt dies clean, passage granted. And the wolf?"

Isandro's throat tightened. Here was the bar of the cage they hadn't solved, the cost with no clever phrasing around it. "He comes with us. Wound and all."

"He dies on the stairs," the regent said flatly. "My medicine keeps him; the outside kills him. Choose: his soul stays as my collateral and his body heals, or his body leaves and his soul follows it into the ground within the month. Those are the letters available to me, creditor. I cannot pay what I do not have."

"Then we'll find another way to pay for him." Isandro's voice did not shake, and they were proud of that, and ashamed of the pride, because pride was cheap next to Bren's blood spreading slowly through the bandage at his side. "Passage and release for Sefa. That much, sealed now. The rest is ours to solve."

The regent studied them a moment longer — the way Isandro had seen cooks study a dish returned uneaten, trying to work out what the diner knew. Then she drew herself up, and the frost on the walls caught the lantern-light and threw it back in a thousand small fires, and she spoke words in a language older than the crown, older than Thornfell, older maybe than the mountain's hunger, and the words settled onto the air like a seal pressed into wax.

"It is witnessed," she said. "The maid is yours. Come, then, creditor. Take her glass from my shelf yourself — and know that taking it is not the same as *opening* it." Something flickered across her carved face, there and gone, and Isandro would spend the next hour understanding it as pity. "A soul bound into lantern-glass by a court's seal does not come loose with a key. There is an unbinding. There is always an unbinding. And the unbinding has a price of its own — one I cannot pay for you, and one your clever tongue cannot bargain away."

The regent stepped aside from the shelf, and gestured, and the little blue-white flame of Sefa's soul trembled in its glass, waiting.

"What price?" Isandro asked.

The regent smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the worst thing yet.

"Ask your gift," she said. "It already knows."

* * *

Isandro crossed the vault to the shelf. Their boots crunched frost with every step. Behind them Bren was saying their name — not *taster*, not *clerk*, but their name, the real one, the one only three living people knew — and Rook was swearing softly, steadily, a smuggler's rosary.

They lifted Sefa's lantern from the shelf. It weighed nothing and everything. The flame inside leaned toward their hands like a cat recognizing warmth, and through the glass, faint as a voice through a wall, Isandro thought they heard humming — off-key, tuneless, the way Sefa hummed over the bread ovens when she thought no one was listening.

The gift rose in them unbidden, the hidden thing, the buried thing, the thing they had starved and smothered for twenty years. It read the seal on the lantern the way their tongue read a broth: every layer of it, every binding-thread, every knot the court's makers had tied. And at the center of the knot, plain as henbane under honey, sat the price, and the gift showed it to them without mercy:

*A sigil of unbinding, drawn in the maker's own blood.*

*Signed with the maker's true name.*

*Spoken aloud, in a place where the mountain hears.*

Isandro stood in the frozen vault with their friend's soul warm in their hands, and understood that the regent had never offered them a choice at all. Leave Sefa and go free — or stay, and open the glass, and say the name. Say *gifted*. Say it in the hearing of the court, in the hearing of the wardens, in the hearing of whatever spies the queen kept even here, down in the dark, among the debts.

Twenty years of hiding, balanced against one small blue-white flame.

The frost crept. The horns sounded, far away. And Isandro Okonkwo, crown's taster, servant, ghost, began — very quietly, in a voice only the lantern could hear — to hum back.
