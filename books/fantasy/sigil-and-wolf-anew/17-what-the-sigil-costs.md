## 17. What the Sigil Costs

The vault breathed cold.

Not the honest cold of Thornfell's passes, which bit and then let go, but a cold that had been *arranged* — poured into the lantern vault like wine into a cellar, banked and tended by wardens who understood that souls kept best at a temperature just shy of freezing. Isandro felt it settle into their lungs as they descended the last stair, each step ringing faintly against stone that was not entirely stone. The walls were veined with sigils older than the crown, older than the queen's grandmother's grandmother, and they glowed with a pale blue light that did not flicker, because nothing down here was permitted to waver.

Behind them, Bren's blood marked the corridor they had come by. Isandro tried not to think of it. Tried, and failed, the way they always failed when it mattered most.

Rook Vane stood at their shoulder, his brother's freed soul-lantern cradled against his chest like an infant. The glass inside it was dark now — empty, spent, the soul within already fled to whatever country souls go to when their debts are paid — but Rook carried it anyway, as if the emptiness itself were something worth protecting. His face was gray. His hands did not shake. Isandro had learned, over these long weeks in the mountains, that this was how Rook grieved: completely still, like a man standing very quiet so that the thing hunting him would pass by.

"Where is she?" Rook asked. His voice came out sanded flat.

Isandro didn't answer with words. They walked.

The vault opened around them the way a cathedral opens — suddenly, all at once, the ceiling leaping away into darkness strung with chains. And from those chains, hundreds of lanterns hung, tier upon tier receding into the dark, each one holding a swirl of light that moved the way breath moves, the way a sleeper turns. Some burned bright gold. Some had dimmed to embers. One or two, near the far wall, had gone out altogether, and no one had taken them down.

Isandro found Sefa's lantern on the third tier, exactly where the regent's clerk had said it would be. The ledger-number etched into its brass base matched the entry Isandro had memorized three days ago while wearing another man's livery and smiling another man's smile: *Okonkwo, Sefa. Collateral against crown debt, autumn assessment. Value: sufficient.*

*Sufficient.* As if her laughter in the palace kitchens — the laugh that had once made a visiting duke spit his wine — could be entered in a column and summed.

"Sefa," Isandro said softly, and put their palm against the glass.

The light inside stirred. It pressed toward their hand the way a moth presses toward a window, and for one terrible moment Isandro felt the shape of her through the barrier — felt her confusion, her cold, her stubborn refusal to be afraid even now, even here. She had known about the gift since they were children stealing plums off the guildmaster's tree. She had never once been afraid *of* Isandro. It seemed unjust, obscene, that she should spend her fear on anything else.

"Tell me you know how to open it," Rook said behind them.

"I know how." Isandro drew back their hand. Their fingers had gone numb, and not from the cold. "That's not the problem."

* * *

The problem was written into the lantern's seal itself.

They knelt before it, Rook keeping watch at the stair, and read the binding with the part of themselves they had spent thirty years pretending did not exist. The sigil-work was exquisite. Whoever had forged this vault had understood unbinding — had understood it well enough to guard against it absolutely. Every lock Isandro knew had a key-shaped flaw somewhere, a seam where the maker's intent thinned. This one had none. The seal was a closed argument, self-proving, complete.

Except.

There was always an except; the regent had made sure Isandro saw it, had practically underlined it during that awful audience above. A sigil of unbinding, drawn in the maker's own blood, signed with the maker's own true name. Not a name given by parents. Not a name entered in a guild roll or spoken to a magistrate. The name underneath all of those — the one the gift answered to, the one that had never been said aloud because saying it aloud meant the hiding was over forever.

Isandro stared at the seal and understood, finally, what the regent had been doing. The trade offered — a stronger soul for Sefa's — had been mercy dressed as cruelty, or cruelty dressed as mercy; with the regent it was impossible to say. But this, the terms of the unbinding, was something else. The mountain court did not merely collect souls. It collected *truths*. It wanted Isandro's name the way a miser wants coin: not to spend, but to hold, to own, to weigh.

And if Isandro refused? If they turned back down the stair, out the gates, into the free cold air, leaving Sefa to dim here until spring took what winter spared?

Then the hiding would have cost everything after all. Every year of the taster's tongue, every poison swallowed in secret, every morning memorizing which smile went with which master — all of it spent to protect a life that would go on dimming in a glass jar because its keeper had flinched at the final price.

"Isandro." Rook's voice, low. "Wardens will come. Whatever you're going to do—"

"I know."

They looked at their hands. Taster's hands, the court physicians called them — steady, sensitive, unremarkable. Hands that had held a hundred cups of death and set them down untouched. Beneath the left thumbnail, a scar from the first sigil they'd ever cut, at nine years old, scratching a luck-mark into the handle of Sefa's favorite ladle so it wouldn't crack. She still used that ladle. She had probably been holding it when the magistrates came.

Isandro thought about the banquet hall where this whole story had begun to end. The poisoned dish. The hidden sigil beneath the tablecloth, worked in a heartbeat while the court laughed at a joke about northern barbarians. They had saved the queen that night. Saved her, and hated themselves for it, and gone home to a cot too small for their dreams and lain awake wondering which was the mask and which the face.

No more wondering. That was the price, and it was also, Isandro realized with a strange floating clarity, the *gift*. The seal demanded the truth. For once in their life, someone was demanding it.

"Rook," they said. "When I start, the vault will know. Everything in this mountain will know. You'll have maybe fifty heartbeats to get to the gates."

"And you?"

"I'll be right behind you." Isandro smiled, and it surprised them how easily it came. "I've lied to better people than a mountain."

* * *

They bared their left forearm and drew the knife.

The blade was smuggler's steel, Rook's gift, unlovely and utterly reliable. Isandro set its point against the skin below the elbow, where the veins ran close and blue, and paused — not from fear of the cut, but to gather the words. Because blood alone was only ink. The name was the writing.

*I am Isandro Okonkwo,* they began silently, testing it. *I am the queen's food-taster. I am a maker of sigils. I am the friend of Sefa Okonkwo, who is no kin of mine by blood and all kin by choice. I am the one who kept your secrets, mountain, and the one who is done keeping them.*

They cut.

The pain was clean, almost welcome — a single honest sensation after years of tasting lies. Blood welled dark and quick, and Isandro caught it on two fingers and began to draw.

The sigil of unbinding was not complex, but it was unforgiving. Every line had to be laid in a single stroke, no lifting the hand, no correction, because a bound thing recognized hesitation and clung to it. Isandro had practiced this pattern a thousand times in flour, in spilled wine, in the condensation on the palace windows — always in erasable mediums, always with a lie standing ready (*doodling, m'lord, idle hands*). Now they drew it in the one medium that could not be wiped away.

First the circle: the world entire, no exits. Then the four anchors, north and south and east and west of the seal's own geometry, each one a small act of defiance — *this line says the binding was made; this line says it can be unmade; this line says the maker answers for it; this line says the unmaker does too.* Last, at the center, the space reserved for the name.

Their blood was running low. Of course it was. The sigil drank it greedily, the way the vault drank warmth, the way the crown drank everything it touched. Isandro's vision fuzzed at the edges. Somewhere above, distantly, iron was beginning to ring — alarm bells, or the mountain's own pulse quickening. Fifty heartbeats. Fewer now.

They pressed their bleeding fingers to the center of the circle.

And stopped.

Because the name would not come out as a whisper. Isandro had imagined signing it the way they did everything — quietly, sideways, beneath the notice of the room. But the seal was drinking their blood and waiting, patient as winter, and it would accept nothing less than voice. The magic itself required it. The old makers had built honesty into the architecture of unbinding, and there was no smuggler's path around it, no servant's door, no hidden sigil beneath the tablecloth.

To free Sefa, Isandro had to stand in the heart of the enemy's house and say, aloud, in their own voice, *what they were.*

The bells rang on. Rook shouted something from the stair — footsteps, many, descending. Bren's blood was cooling in a corridor far above. And Isandro Okonkwo knelt in the lantern vault with their life in a thin red line on their arm and discovered that the last lock was not on the lantern at all.

It was in their throat.

Thirty years of silence broke like ice on a spring river.

"My name," Isandro said, and their voice cracked on the second word, cracked and kept going, "is Isandro Okonkwo. I am a maker of sigils. I was born gifted, in the salt-quarter of Corvane, in my mother's kitchen, and I have hidden it every day of my life since. I tasted the queen's poisons and I neutralized her enemies' poisons and I told myself it was survival, and some nights it was, and some nights it was cowardice wearing survival's coat. I am the one who signed the luck-mark on Sefa's ladle. I am the one who is taking her back."

They signed the name in blood across the center of the circle, stroke by stroke, and with every stroke the vault *heard*.

There was no other word for it. The hanging lanterns brightened by fractions, hundreds of imprisoned souls turning toward the sound of a true name spoken freely, the way flowers turn toward a sun they had been told was only a story. The blue sigils in the walls flared white, then violet, then a color Isandro had no name for and no longer needed one. The mountain knew them now. Let it. Let the whole buried kingdom know that a servant's child had walked into its vault and refused, at the final door, to be small.

"By my name and my blood," Isandro said, "I unmake what was bound. *Sefa. Come home.*"

The seal shattered.

Not loudly — that was the terrible beauty of it. It came apart like frost leaving a windowpane, like a held breath released, and Sefa's light rose out of the broken glass in a slow golden column and hung in the air before Isandro, pulsing, warm, unmistakably *her*. For a heartbeat it hovered. Then it flowed forward, past Isandro, up the stair, toward the body waiting somewhere in the regent's guest chambers — a soul going home to its own flesh with the whole mountain ringing its passage like a wedding bell.

Isandro stayed kneeling a moment longer, swaying, their arm sheeted red, watching the empty lantern swing on its chain.

Then the wardens arrived, and there was no more time for wonder.

* * *

They came down the stair in ranks of six, tall figures in gray lacquer, faces hidden behind masks of smoked glass, and the lead warden spoke in a voice like gravel sliding: *"The maker will remain. The vault claims the name that was spoken here."*

"The vault," Isandro said, rising, blood dripping from their fingertips onto the ancient floor, "can file a grievance with the crown. Oh — wait." They smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and they did not want it to be. "You *are* the crown's creditors. How convenient."

Rook was already moving, hurling a fistful of cache-powder — smuggler's flash, blinding white — into the foremost rank. Isandro's hands were moving too, faster than thought, drawing a shield-sigil in the air with lines of fire where lines of blood had been, because the gift, unhidden, needed no medium anymore. It needed only the maker, and the maker, at long last, was present.

The wardens broke against the shield like water against a pier.

"GO!" Isandro roared, and the word carried a sigil in it, and the stairwell itself seemed to lean them upward, toward the gates, toward the regent waiting at the top with the old life-debt between them like a drawn knife — toward Bren, wounded, waiting, *theirs*, the first person Isandro had ever chosen to show the truth instead of the mask.

They ran. The vault fell away behind them, its hundreds of lanterns burning brighter than they had in decades, lit by the echo of one name honestly spoken.

And Isandro Okonkwo — taster, smuggler, friend, maker — climbed toward the daylight with their arm bleeding and their head high, carrying nothing hidden at all.
