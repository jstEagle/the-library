## 18. The Mountain Wakes

Bren heard the mountain before he saw anything at all.

It began as a sound beneath sound — a groan in the roots of the world, deep enough that his ribs felt it before his ears did, the way a wolf feels thunder through the pads of its feet. He was lying where Isandro had left him, propped against a fallen pillar with Rook's coat wadded under his shoulder, and the stone floor beneath him was shivering like the hide of a horse bothered by flies.

Then the light came.

The lantern-glass in Isandro's hands flared white — Sefa's soul, unbound, burning like a struck match against all that dark — and Bren watched the color drain out of the vault around them. The sigil-lines carved into the walls, older than the crown, older than any kingdom's memory, lit up one after another like veins of fire racing through black marble. They had seen those lines dead and dormant on the way in. Now they woke, and waking, they *screamed*.

The unbinding tore through the court the way a river tears through an ice dam.

"Isandro—" Rook's voice cracked high. The smuggler was on his feet, soul-lantern clutched to his chest — his brother's, dim gold guttering inside the glass like a candle afraid of wind. "Isandro, the ceiling—"

Bren looked up. He wished he hadn't. Cracks were spreading across the vaulted dark overhead, slow as frost climbing a window, and from each crack sifted a fine gray dust that fell and fell and did not stop falling.

He tried to stand. His leg folded under him like wet rope, and the pain that went up through his hip was white and total, blotting out thought. The wardens' blades had found him twice in the corridors — once along the ribs, once deep in the thigh — and though the wounds had stopped bleeding freely, they had not stopped *talking*. Every heartbeat was a hammer-blow delivered to the inside of his skin.

Hands caught him under the arms. Isandro's hands — small, strong, smelling of blood and lantern-smoke and something green, some herb-musk that was purely them.

"Don't," Isandro said. "Don't try to stand yet."

"The gates." Bren got the words out through his teeth. "How far?"

"Far." Isandro's face was pale as tallow in the lantern-light, but their eyes were steady, and there was something new in them — something Bren had never seen there in all the weeks since the kitchens, since the bargain struck over flour sacks and fear. Isandro had always been careful. Careful was the armor they wore the way other people wore skin. Now the armor was gone, burned away by whatever it had cost to draw that unbinding sigil in their own blood and speak their own name aloud into a place that ate names. What was underneath was not softness. It was iron.

"They'll seal for a generation," Isandro said. "The regent told me true about that much. We have until first frost finishes falling on the mountain's crown, and no longer."

"Then we'd best be quick about it," Rook said, "because I can hear frost falling already, and I don't mean snow."

He was right. Beneath the groaning of the stone, beneath the shriek of waking sigils, there was a new sound now: a vast, rhythmic grinding, far away and getting less far, like millstones the size of hills turning against each other somewhere in the dark ahead of them. The gates. Doors of stone older than the crown, closing the way a fist closes.

The regent had promised to open them. Bren had not been conscious for most of that bargain — he had been bleeding in a corridor while Isandro ran — but he had heard the shape of it afterward, told in Isandro's flat, fast voice while they bound his wounds with strips of Rook's shirt: *the regent owes a taster's life-debt, and debts are the only law down here.* Whether the promise would hold with the whole court coming apart around its ears — that was another matter entirely.

A third sound joined the others. Boots. Many boots, ringing off stone, coming from the direction none of them wanted company from.

"Wardens?" Rook whispered.

"No." Isandro tilted their head, listening the way Bren listened — and for a moment Bren felt the strange doubling of it, two hunters' ears on the same silence. "Too few. Too even. That's an honor guard."

It was. They came around the corner of the shattered vault four abreast, tall figures in gray lacquered scale, and at their center walked the regent of the court beneath the mountain — robed in winter itself, it seemed, hem trailing frost-ferns that melted and reformed with every step. Its face was old the way mountains are old, and just as unreadable.

It stopped before Isandro. It looked at the lantern in Isandro's hands, where Sefa's soul burned bright and whole and *hers*, and then at the second lantern swinging from Rook's fist, and last — longest — at Bren, sprawled and bloody against the pillar.

"A debt is a debt," the regent said. Its voice was the sound a glacier makes when it calves, distant and enormous. "The taster saved my life at my own table. I do not forget. I do not forgive. But I do not forget." It turned. "Follow. The ways I open will stay open only while I walk them. Fall behind, and you will fall behind forever."

It did not wait to see if they understood.

Rook hauled Bren up over his shoulders in a fireman's carry that jolted a grunt out of him he wasn't ashamed of — much — and Isandro took his arm across their back besides, so that between the two of them he half-walked and half-floated through corridors that were actively trying to kill them.

And they were. That was the thing the stories never told you about dying places: they didn't go quiet. They went *busy*. Walls leaned. Floors tilted by degrees too subtle to see until your next step dropped an inch further than your leg expected. Dust became streams became cascades. And everywhere, everywhere, the sigil-lines blazed and crawled, the court's ancient circulatory system hemorrhaging light now that Isandro's unbinding had cut its heart.

Once, a whole stretch of corridor simply ceased to exist ahead of them — the floor gone, a chasm breathing cold up out of the depths — and the regent raised one hand without breaking stride, and a bridge of blue ice laced itself across the gap, singing faintly, and they crossed it with Bren's teeth clenched so hard his jaw ached worse than his leg.

Once, a section of ceiling came down behind them with a boom like a door slamming on the world, and the dust-cloud that chased them up the passage was warm and tasted of iron, and Rook said, very quietly, mostly to himself, "My brother used to say the mountain would get us both someday. He owed me a drink, the bastard. Still does." And he ran faster.

And once — only once — Bren looked back.

Sefa's lantern threw their shadows huge and wavering down the corridor behind them, three figures and a frost-crowned king, running through a collapsing cathedral of stone. But past them, deeper in, he could see the vault they'd left, and the other lanterns still racked there in their hundreds — souls the queen had sold, souls whose owners might still walk and breathe above ground, waiting for a rescue that was not coming, that could not come, that no one alive knew how to give.

Isandro saw him looking.

"I know," Isandro said. Low. Wrecked. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"You came for one," Bren managed. "You got two. That's two more than anyone's ever carried out of here."

"It isn't enough."

"No," Bren agreed. "But it's what we have. So we carry it."

* * *

They emerged from the deep ways into the approach hall — the vast throat of the mountain court, where the gate stood — and Bren, half-carried, half-dragged, finally saw the doors themselves.

He had known them once, long ago, in the years before the last purge, when he had been young and the pack had run these slopes and the old wolves still spoke of the under-court the way sailors speak of the sea: with respect, with dread, and with a great many lies. He had thought he remembered them. He had remembered nothing.

They were not doors. They were a cliff-face given intention — a hundred feet of black stone carved with spirals within spirals, sigils stacked on sigils in patterns that made his eyes slide off and his skull ache to follow. And they were closing. Even now, even with the regent walking toward them, the two halves were drawing together with that endless millstone grind, the gap between them narrowed to perhaps thirty feet, twenty-five, the darkness beyond already thickening like water freezing from the edges inward.

Beyond the gap, Bren could see the night. Actual night. Snow slanting through it, fat flakes catching the light of the waking sigils like sparks off a whetstone. Cold air poured through the narrowing seam, and it was the sweetest thing he had ever breathed.

"Hold," the regent said, and raised both hands.

The grinding slowed. Did not stop. Slowed.

"The debt carries you to the threshold," the regent said, without turning. "Understand this, taster. The court is dying because you unbound what was bound. The foundation was the binding; the binding was the foundation. What falls below will fall for a generation, and what wakes above—" it paused, and something moved through its frost-hem, a tremor, almost like laughter, almost like grief, "—what wakes above will be weather such as Thornfell has not seen in living memory. You have purchased lives with a winter. Spend them well."

"We intend to," Isandro said.

"Do you?" The regent turned its head, and its eyes were the gray of ice at depth. "The ledgers you carry name your queen. When you set them loose in the world, the world will burn, and the burning will not distinguish between the guilty and the gifted. You know this."

"I know it," Isandro said. "I'm carrying them anyway."

Something passed over the regent's face that on a human face might have been called respect. On the regent's, it was only a stillness, brief as a blink.

"The debt is paid," it said. "Go."

And it stepped aside, and the gates — slowly, grudgingly, with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat after a thousand years — began to open.

Twenty feet. Twenty-five. Thirty.

"Now," Rook breathed. "Now now now—"

They ran. Or Bren ran, insofar as running described what his body was doing — a lurching, staggering, agony-wrapped forward motion, Isandro under one arm and Rook's grip like an iron band across his chest, the two lanterns swinging and flaring, Sefa's bright and Rook's brother's guttering and steadying, guttering and steadying, as if the souls themselves were leaning toward the open air.

Behind them, the regent stood motionless amid its honor guard, watching them go, frost spreading from its feet across a floor that was beginning, at last, to shake in earnest.

Fifteen feet of gap left when they reached the threshold. Ten. Bren could feel the cold of the closing stone at his back like the breath of something enormous inhaling.

His leg gave out.

One stride it held; the next it didn't, and he went down across the threshold with the whole weight of himself, and Isandro went down with him, and the gap was six feet and closing, and Rook — Rook did the bravest coward's act Bren had ever witnessed or ever would: he turned back, into the closing dark, hooked his hands under Bren's arms, and *heaved*, screaming words Bren couldn't parse through the roar in his ears, dragging him bodily across the stone while Isandro shoved from behind and the gates ground shut with the patience of geology—

—and the dark took the last of the light, and the sound cut off like a head from a body, and there was nothing beneath Bren but honest frozen mud and nothing above him but snow falling out of a black sky, and he was laughing. He couldn't help it. It hurt to laugh and he laughed anyway, flat on his back in the drifts outside a sealed mountain, alive, with a soul-lantern glowing beside his face like a small captive dawn.

"Get him up," Isandro said. Their voice shook. "Get him up, we have to move, the whole slope is going to—"

The mountain finished the sentence for them.

It did not roar. It *sang* — one vast harmonic note rolling up out of the rock beneath them, through the rock, through their bones, as somewhere far below the court of debts collapsed into its own foundations, and the peak above them shed its snow in a single sliding sheet that came down the slopes like a white tide, and Bren, being dragged upright again, being half-carried down through the trees as the avalanche thundered past on their left and buried the gate and the path and the world they'd come from under forty feet of fresh powder, understood suddenly and completely that he would never see this place again, and that no one would, not for a generation, and that everything they had done was now sealed under the snow along with the regent and its honor guard and its terrible ledger-halls.

Except the evidence. Except the truth. Except the two souls swinging in glass, and the four of them, stumbling down through the pines with the mountain singing its long note behind them and the first true storm of winter swallowing the world ahead.

* * *

They made the tree line as full dark fell, and there they stopped, because Bren could not take another step and everyone knew it and no one said it.

Rook cleared a hollow beneath a granite overhang with the efficiency of a man who had slept rough in Thornfell for thirty years — cut pine boughs, a windbreak of stone, a fire ring already blackened by some earlier traveler whose luck had been better than his warmth. Isandro laid Bren down on the boughs with a gentleness that undid him more than the warden-blades had, and then knelt, and did not ask permission, and peeled back the ruined bandages to look at what the wardens had made of him.

"Well?" he said.

"You'll live." Isandro's hands were steady. Their voice was not, quite. "If infection stays away. If the cold doesn't finish what the steel started. If we can keep moving tomorrow, and the day after, all the way down to the lowlands." They looked up, and the firelight caught the dried blood on their forearm — the blood they had paid for the unbinding, the signature written in themselves. "I can't carry you, Bren. I'm sorry. I need you to walk."

"Then I'll walk." He said it simply, because it was simply true. "Wolves walk on worse than this. Wolves *die* on worse than this, sometimes, but mostly they walk."

Isandro huffed — half laugh, half sob — and bent their head, and for a moment pressed their brow against his shoulder, the way wolves do, the way pack does, and Bren closed his eyes and let it be true.

Rook set the two lanterns side by side on a flat stone where the firelight could reach them. In Sefa's glass the soul burned steady and strong, a flame with a person inside it, waiting. In the other, Rook's brother's light had gone from guttering to something calmer — dimmer than Sefa's, scarred by its long captivity, but *there*, turning slowly, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice.

"He'll wake when we're clear of the mountain's shadow," Isandro said quietly. "Soul and body find each other again, given distance and time. Sefa sooner — hers was taken recent. Rook's brother..." They didn't finish it. They didn't have to.

Rook nodded. He crouched by the lanterns a long moment, saying nothing, doing nothing, just keeping watch over that small gold light like a man standing vigil at a deathbed that had turned, impossibly, into a cradle. Then he cleared his throat roughly and went to see to the fire.

Later — much later, when Rook slept in fits and Isandro sat awake at the hollow's mouth with the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against their chest like a sleeping child — Bren lay staring at the snow falling past the overhang, fine and endless, erasing the world one flake at a time.

"Isandro."

"Mm."

"When we get down. When you send those pages out to every garrison and crier in the lowlands." He turned his head. Firelight moved on their face. "It won't end clean. You know that. Queens don't fall quiet. Half the country will call you a hero and half will call you a traitor, and both halves will want something from you, and neither will leave you alone."

"I know."

"And you'll be doing it *openly*. No more plain servant's smock. No more tasting other people's food and swallowing your own name. Everyone will know what you are."

Isandro was quiet a moment. Then they smiled — tired, crooked, real.

"Bren. I wrote my name in blood tonight in front of a king of the under-mountain and it *worked*." They looked down at their own forearm, at the scabbed line of the sigil. "I think I can survive being known."

Outside, the snow kept falling, and far above, the mountain's long note faded at last into the ordinary silence of winter — and somewhere down in the valleys, in the city where it all began, a queen who believed her harvest safely stored had no idea what was coming down out of Thornfell with the thaw.

Or rather, Bren thought, settling deeper into the boughs, letting the pain and the exhaustion pull him under at last — not with the thaw.

With the first hard riders of the storm. Because Isandro had already told him, before sleep took them, in a voice gone flat and certain as a drawn blade:

*"We don't wait for spring. We ride at first light."*
