## 19. First Frost

The light came first.

Rook Vane had spent thirty years learning to read the dark — the grey that meant dawn behind cloud, the amber smear of a lantern through fog, the blacker-than-black of a mountain's throat swallowing a man whole. So when the passage ahead began to pale, he did not trust it. He had learned in the court beneath the root that light could lie, could be glass and glamour and a soul burning itself out to make pretty shadows on a wall.

But this light was cold. That was how he knew it was true. It came down the corridor like water finding its level, thin and silver and smelling of snow, and it touched his face with fingers that had no warmth in them at all and he nearly wept right there, stumbling over Bren's dragging weight, because cold meant outside, cold meant sky, cold meant *out*.

"Almost," Isandro said behind him. Their voice was ash by now, scraped hollow from shouting sigils into stone that did not want to hear them. "Rook — almost."

The mountain was closing. He could feel it in his teeth, a deep grinding hum, the sound of doors the size of seasons swinging shut. Behind them the corridors were folding, walls sliding together like hands clasped in prayer or in strangling, he'd never known which, and the regent's last gift — the debt called due, the gates held open by a taster's saved life — was spending itself down to nothing. Rook did not look back. Looking back was for men who believed they might return.

He put his shoulder under Bren's arm and climbed.

* * *

They came out into a world gone white.

Not the white of the court, which had been the white of bone and lantern-glass. This was the white of snow falling through still air, fat flakes descending without hurry, as though the sky had all the time in the world and intended to use it. The pass opened before them like a bowl turned upside down, and above it Thornfell's peaks stood black against a grey sky, wearing their winter crowns already.

Rook set Bren down as gently as he could, which was not very. The wolf-shifter had taken a warden's blade across the ribs and another across the thigh, and though Isandro had bound the wounds with strips of Rook's own coat and whatever craft they could risk — small sigils, quick ones, drawn in frost-melt on skin — Bren had lost blood enough to turn his face the colour of old tallow. His breathing was shallow but even. Wolves, Rook thought, were made to survive things that should kill them. He clung to that.

Isandro stood at the mouth of the passage and watched it die.

Rook watched them watch it: the gate sealing itself shut, stone flowing over stone, the sigils older than the crown dimming one by one like lamps carried away down a long hall. There was no roar, no collapse. Just a slow closing, quiet as a book shutting, and then the mountainside was only a mountainside, blank and snow-dusted, bearing no door at all. A generation sealed. Perhaps two. Whatever else happened in the world above, the court beneath the root would keep its own counsel now, with its regent and its remnant faction and its ledgers full of bought souls, locked away from the queen who had fed it for decades.

"It's done," Isandro said. They said it the way a man says *it's done* when he has cut off his own hand to free himself from a trap — not triumph, not grief, just the flat accounting of a price paid.

Then Sefa stirred.

She had been carried these last miles wrapped in every spare layer they owned, light as a bundle of sticks, her body breathing but her self somewhere else — in the lantern, Rook understood, or what remained of the lantern after Isandro's unbinding sigil had cracked it open in a bloom of blood-written light. Her soul had come home to her scarred, the way a river comes home to its bed after drought, thinner than it was, cutting new channels. But it had come home.

Now her eyes opened, brown and unfocused, and found Isandro's face above her.

"You look terrible," she whispered.

Isandro made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and folded down beside her in the snow, and Rook turned away to give them the moment, because some moments are doors and you should not stand in them.

He looked instead at the thing in his hands.

* * *

He had carried the empty lantern out of hell.

That was the truth of it, though he'd have cut anyone who said it plain. Through the shifting halls and the warden-hunt and the race against the sealing dark, with Bren bleeding and Isandro hauling Sefa, Rook Vane — smuggler, coward, calculator — had kept one arm curled around a foot of glass and brass like a mother around a child. His brother's soul-lantern. Empty now. Tam Vane's soul had gone home to Tam's body three days dead in a smuggler's cache below the treeline, and no unbinding sigil could follow it there, and Rook had known that, had known it since the moment Isandro told him what freeing a soul meant when the body was already cold.

He had freed it anyway. He had smashed the warden-lock with a smuggler's pick and a smuggler's patience and let his brother's light out into the dark of the mountain, knowing it had nowhere left to land. Knowing it would simply — go. Scatter. Become whatever souls become when they are done being currency.

It had been the first thing in thirty years he had done without calculating the cost.

Now he knelt in the snow at the edge of the pass, where the stones gave way to the first stunted pines, and dug.

The ground was iron with frost. He dug with a knife and his bare hands and the knife broke on a buried root and he kept going with his hands until his fingers bled and stopped feeling, and the hole was not deep, would never be deep enough, but it would do. Snow fell into it and did not melt.

Tam had been the better brother. Everyone who met them both knew it inside an hour. Tam laughed easy and trusted easy and had followed Rook into the smuggling life because where else would he go, and when the mountain court's brokers had come collecting debts Rook owed, Tam had stepped forward and said *take mine instead, he has children to feed* — except Rook didn't have children, Rook had ledgers, and Tam had known that too and offered anyway, because that was Tam all through. And Rook had let him. Had stood in the doorway of their mother's house and let his little brother be taken in his place, and told himself for eleven years that the mathematics were sound, that a soul in a lantern felt nothing, that survival was its own justification.

The mathematics had never once been sound.

"I'm sorry," Rook said to the hole in the ground. His voice came out wrecked, a smuggler's voice run aground. "I'm sorry I was slow. I'm sorry I counted. I'm sorry you're not —"

He couldn't finish it. He laid the lantern in the grave instead — glass clouded, brass green with the court's damp, empty as a blown egg — and covered it with frozen earth and then with snow, and pressed his bleeding palms flat on top of the little mound as if he could warm it, as if warmth had ever been the problem.

And then Jorun Vane, who had not wept at his father's funeral nor when the crown burned his routes nor when they took his name off every guild roll in the lowlands, wept. Ugly and loud and kneeling in the falling snow, with a wolf dying gently ten feet away and a maid waking from death and a taster standing silent guard, Rook Vane wept for his brother, and the mountain took the sound and gave nothing back, which was right. Grief wasn't a trade. You paid it out and got nothing, and that was how you knew it was real.

A hand settled on his shoulder. Isandro's. They said nothing. Tasters, Rook was learning, knew exactly when not to speak — it was the same skill as knowing when not to swallow.

After a long time, Rook wiped his face on his sleeve, leaving streaks of soot and blood, and said, "We should move. Weather's turning honest."

Above the peaks, the sky had begun to lower itself onto the world, grey thickening to slate, and the wind had picked up a note that Rook's bones knew from twenty years of high passes: the long winter coming down. Not a storm. A season. The kind of winter the old songs sang about, when the passes closed and the courts slept and the lowlands huddled and waited. They had beaten the seal by hours. Maybe less.

"Can he walk?" Isandro asked, nodding at Bren.

"He'll have to." Rook got his shoulder under the wolf-shifter again. "Wolves don't die in snow unless they mean to."

Bren opened one eye. "Heard that," he rasped. "Insolent man."

"Alive, then. Good. Less carrying."

* * *

They went down out of the pass as the first true snowfall of the long winter came on, and it was slow, and it was terrible, and Rook would remember every step of it for the rest of his life.

Bren walked between them, half-carried, leaving a dotted line of red melting into white whenever he stumbled, which was often. Sefa walked too — refused to be carried, Isandro said, with a stubbornness that had survived even a soul-deep scarring — clutching Isandro's arm with one hand and pressing the other to her chest as if holding a door shut against wind. Sometimes she stopped and stared at nothing, and Rook saw her lips move, counting something, or naming something, keeping hold of herself the way a climber keeps hold of a rope. Once she asked Isandro, very quietly, whether she had been dreaming for eleven years, and Isandro said, "No. You were taken. Those aren't the same," and Sefa nodded and walked on.

They sheltered the first night in a smuggler's cache Rook had stocked seven years ago and written off as lost — a hollow beneath a leaning slab, dry, with a rusted tin of hardtack and a bottle of plum brandy he could have kissed. He fed Bren broth melted from snow and dried meat softened in it. He poured brandy for the others and drank none himself; his hands were not steady enough to waste good liquor.

Sefa sat close to the small fire and watched the flames with an expression Rook couldn't read, and finally said, "There was a woman. In the court. She used to hum, near my lantern. Every night. I think she was remembering a song she couldn't finish." Her voice was steady and that steadiness was the most frightening thing about her. "I want to find out who she was. When we're home. I want to know if anyone's looking for her."

"We'll find out," Isandro said.

"There will be others," Sefa said. "In those ledgers you took. Others with families who think they ran away, or died, or never loved them enough to stay." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were clear and hard and entirely awake. "You'll tell them. All of it."

"Yes," Isandro said.

Rook, sharpening his second-best knife by the fire, said nothing, and thought about ink, and paper, and how many copies a man could buy for the price of one soul.

* * *

On the third day the trees thickened and the air lost its knife-edge and they came down out of Thornfell proper into the foothill forests, where the snow lay shallower and the silence was the ordinary kind. Bren could nearly walk alone by then, moving with the careful economy of a convalescent wolf, and he ranged ahead a little each hour, nose working, until near dusk he stopped dead on a game trail and lifted his head.

"What is it?" Isandro asked.

"Woodsmoke," Bren said. "And bread." A pause. "And wolves. Old ones. Far off, north. Not my pack." Something in his shoulders eased as he said it. "My pack is south. I can feel where they aren't."

They found the cottage at the edge of a clearing: a charcoal-burner's hut, abandoned for the season, its owner gone down to the valley villages before the passes closed. The chimney still drew. There was split wood under the lean-to and a hearth that hadn't forgotten its shape, and Rook — who had broken into better houses with worse manners — got a fire going while Isandro settled Sefa on the charcoal-burner's cot and Bren collapsed onto the floor by the hearth like a felled tree, sighing the long sigh of a creature finally permitted to stop.

The storm arrived properly that night, the long winter making its entrance in earnest, snow sweeping past the little window in horizontal sheets while the fire roared defiance. Outside, far off, a wolf voice rose and was answered, and Bren's ears — his human ears, mostly human — tilted toward it and then deliberately away.

"Not yours?" Rook asked.

"Not tonight." Bren's eyes caught the firelight, gold-flecked, tired beyond measure. "Tonight I'm a house-wolf. Tonight I belong to the ones in this room."

Sefa, half-asleep on the cot, smiled at that. Isandro sat by the window with the court's stolen ledger pages spread on their knees, reading by firelight, copying names into a smaller book in a hand so neat it looked printed. Rook watched them work for a while — the food-taster, the hidden gifted, the friend who had walked into a mountain and unmade a soul-prison with their own blood — and felt the strange settling in his chest that he supposed other men called hope.

"They'll come for you," he said quietly. "When word spreads. The queen. She won't let a ledger like that walk around loose."

"I know," Isandro said, not looking up. "Let her send whoever she likes. I'm done tasting for people who poison the well."

Outside, the wolf-song rose again, closer now, many voices braided together on the wind — and among them, unmistakable even to Rook's dull human ears, a voice Bren knew. The wolf-shifter went rigid by the fire, head lifting, nostrils flaring, and his eyes when they turned to the window were wide with something between dread and longing.

"That's my pack," he said. "That's my pack, and they've crossed the border, and they're coming here."
