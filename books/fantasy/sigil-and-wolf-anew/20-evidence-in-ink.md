## 20. Evidence in Ink

The wolves came at moonrise, and they did not come for blood.

Isandro watched from the cache doorway as seven shapes resolved out of the snowfall — grey and silver and one pale as ash — and folded themselves around the cabin like a held breath. Bren went out to them barefoot in the cold, and whatever passed between him and the ash-pale she-wolf happened too low and too fast for human ears. When it was done, three of the pack remained at the treeline as escort and the rest turned back toward the high passes, and Bren came inside with frost in his beard and an expression Isandro had never seen on him: unburdened.

"They'll walk us to the border," he said. "No one will touch us between here and the lowlands. Apparently dying for a found pack counts for something, even among wolves."

"Apparently," Rook said, and raised his flask to the window as if toasting the treeline.

Sefa slept through all of it, curled by the fire with her soul-lantern's empty glass cradled in her hands like something she could not decide whether to keep or bury. She had woken twice in the night, both times asking where she was, both times reaching for Isandro's wrist to confirm it was real. Her soul was back in her — scarred, dimmed at the edges like cloth faded along its folds — but hers. She would carry a winter-coldness in her all her life. She had told Isandro this matter-of-factly, the way she'd once reported a burned batch of bread, and then asked if there was anything to eat.

Now there were ledgers to copy, and no more time than winter allowed.

* * *

They descended over four days, the wolf escort ghosting them through passes where the purge patrols had already gone home to their garrisons, their zeal frozen out of them by a cold that made even cruelty expensive. Isandro rode wrapped in a smuggler's oilskin with the court's ledgers bound against their chest beneath it, three volumes of vellum and iron-gall ink that weighed more than any book had a right to weigh, because debt always does.

They had taken the ledgers openly, in the end. The regent, honoring the taster's life-debt, had stood aside in the collapsing vault and let Isandro walk out with the court's own accounting — *take them*, the regent had said, in that voice like water moving under ice, *and show your crown what it has been eating*. Whether it was honor or spite or simply the mountain's long game, Isandro did not know and had stopped trying to guess. Debts were debts. The regent paid them. That was more than could be said for queens.

On the third night, camped in the ruins of a shepherd's hut with the wolves as shadows at the perimeter, Isandro opened the first ledger by firelight and read it straight through, though reading it felt like swallowing glass.

The entries began forty-one years ago. The queen's grandmother had started it — a single page, a single name, a gifted girl sold to settle a grain debt after a bad harvest. Then the grandmother's son had refined the practice, and then the current queen had industrialized it. There were columns. Isandro had spent years learning to read a kitchen's worth of small cruelties in a single glance at a table, and these tables were fluent in a language they knew intimately: quantities, prices, seasonal adjustments. Souls bought low in famine years. Sold higher to the court when the crown needed favors. And running through it all, in the queen's own hand, the justification repeated like a refrain:

*Purge of Gifts, Year One. Yield: nine souls. Debt service rendered.*

The Purge was not justice. It never had been. It was harvest. Every crier's proclamation, every reward for informants, every neighbor turned against neighbor — it had all been a net, and the crown had been selling the catch beneath the mountain to pay for its wars and its banquets and its pride.

Isandro closed the ledger and sat for a long time listening to the fire tick and the wind worry the broken roof.

"You're doing the face," Sefa said quietly from her bedroll. "The one you used to do when you tasted something bad but couldn't say so."

"I'm doing the face," Isandro agreed.

"What are you going to do?"

Isandro looked at the ledgers. Forty-one years of names. Somewhere in those columns, Rook's brother. Somewhere, Sefa. Somewhere, hundreds whose families still believed they had died of fever, of flight, of the purge's righteous fire.

"Every soul they took has a mother," Isandro said. "A village. A crier who read the queen's edict aloud to them. I'm going to send the truth home to all of them. At once. Everywhere."

Rook, sharpening his knives by the fire, didn't look up. "You understand what you're describing. Not one letter to one magistrate who can be bought or buried. Hundreds. Garrisons. Criers' guilds. The great houses that hate the queen anyway. You want to make it impossible to smother."

"I want to make it impossible to smother," Isandro confirmed.

"That's not justice," Rook said. "That's arithmetic."

"Yes," Isandro said. "Justice is what happens afterward, if we're lucky, in courts and years and other people's hands. This is just ink. But ink is the one poison I've never needed a sigil to deliver."

* * *

They came down out of Thornfell on the sixth day, past the last wolf-shadow fading into the treeline with something that might have been a farewell, and reached the river town of Marrowford two days after that — a smuggling town, a garrison town, a town built on the honest dishonesty of people who moved goods the crown preferred not to see counted. Rook Vane had called it home for twenty years, which meant he knew which innkeeper could be trusted, which clerk could be bribed, and which scribe worked nights in the back of a chandler's shop for coin and asked nothing about content.

His name was Pellam Ostrey, and he had ink-stained fingers and a stutter that vanished entirely when his pen touched paper. He looked at the ledgers, looked at Rook, looked at Isandro's plain servant's clothes and steady eyes, and said only, "How many copies, and how fast?"

"All of it," Isandro said. "And fast enough that no one can stop us before it's done."

It took eleven days.

Eleven days in a room that smelled of tallow and lamp-black, while outside the first hard frosts silvered Marrowford's gutters and the criers still called the queen's edicts in the square — *shelter not the gifted, inform and be rewarded* — and Isandro sat with a copying pen moving hour after hour until their hand cramped and Sefa pried the pen away and made them eat. They copied in shifts, all of them. Rook had surprising, careful script learned from years of falsifying manifests. Sefa's kitchen-trained hand was quick and neat. Even Bren copied, tongue between his teeth, letters the size of a child's, and refused all mockery with a look that ended the conversation.

They made thirty full copies of the damning pages — not the whole ledgers, but the heart of them: the queen's own annotations, the yield columns, the forty-one years of names matched against the purge records every garrison kept. Isandro wrote the covering letter once, and had Pellam copy it thirty times, and read it so many times they could recite it in their sleep:

*To the honorable reader: enclosed are pages from the ledgers of the court beneath Thornfell, taken from that court's own vault. They record the sale of souls to the mountain court by order of the crown, across forty-one years, and they name the Purge of Gifts as the means of harvest. Check the names against your own purge rolls. Count the missing. The law was never the point. The taking was.*

*We do not ask you to believe us. We ask you to read, and then to look at your neighbors, and ask who profits from your fear.*

*— A former servant of the crown, who tasted what was served, and will no longer swallow it.*

No signature. No sigil. Isandro had thought long about signing it — about claiming the gift in ink as they'd claimed it in blood before Sefa's lantern — and decided against it. This letter was not about them. Let the words stand alone. Their name would come soon enough, and loudly, whether they wished it or not.

Distribution was Rook's art. He laid it out on the chandler's floor like a battle plan, which, Isandro reflected, it was.

"Criers' guilds first," he said, tapping with a knife-point. "Marrowford, Duncastle, Greywater, the river towns. Criers repeat what they're given unless they're forbidden, and forbidding it proves it matters. Then garrisons — the junior officers, not the commanders. Commanders owe the queen. Junior officers have brothers-in-law who vanished in the purges." The knife moved. "Then the houses. House Aldery keeps a grudge older than the queen. House Vessin lost a shipping contract to the crown last spring and has been looking for a knife ever since. Give them each a copy with a different page marked, so we learn who reads fastest."

"And when the queen's people trace it back here?" Sefa asked.

Rook smiled without humor. "By then it'll be in forty hands in six towns, and burning one chandler's shop won't unring any bells. That's the whole trick of it. You don't hide the poison. You put it in everything."

Isandro met his eyes. "You've changed your tune."

"I've got a brother's empty lantern buried under a cairn in Thornfell," Rook said, and his voice went flat and quiet. "I did the math my whole life and the math never once saved anybody. Consider me reformed."

* * *

The first response came faster than anyone expected.

On the twelfth morning, before dawn, the criers of Marrowford took the square — and instead of the queen's edict, they read the letter. All of it. Isandro heard it from the inn's upper window, the crier's voice cracking across the frost-hard cobbles, naming the years, naming the yields, and then — this was the part Isandro had not predicted — beginning to read the names. Just the names, one after another, while windows lit up down every street off the square like embers catching.

Below, in the dark, people came out of their doors to listen. Nobody shouted. That was the frightening thing and the hopeful thing at once: nobody shouted. They just listened, and stood close together in the cold, and when the crier finished, someone began — softly, uncertainly — to sing a mourning song for the dead of the last purge, the one from thirty years ago, and others joined, and the sound rose up through the frost-air to the window where Isandro stood with Sefa's hand gripping their arm.

"They're not rioting," Sefa murmured. "They're grieving. In public. For gifted people." She laughed, wet-eyed and disbelieving. "Do you know how long I stood in kitchens listening to people say our kind deserved it? And now they're singing."

"Ledgers don't sing," Isandro said. "People do. We just gave them the reason."

By noon, the garrison commander had ordered the criers silenced, and by evening, three more criers had read the letter in defiance and been arrested for it, and by the next morning, criers in Duncastle and Greywater were reading it too, because silencing a crier in one town is the surest way to make every crier in every other town demand to know why. Rook watched the reports come in through his network of carters and boatmen and laughed until he had to sit down.

"It's arithmetic," he said gleefully. "I told you. You can bribe a man. You cannot bribe everyone who's already read it."

The great houses moved slower, as great houses do, but they moved. Within five days, House Aldery publicly demanded the crown account for the missing persons named in "certain documents circulating among honest folk." House Vessin went further, freezing crown credit — and a kingdom that cannot borrow cannot pay its soldiers, and an unpaid garrison reads whatever its junior officers put in front of it. The purge patrols in the lowland counties stopped reporting arrests. Then they stopped reporting. Then, in two garrisons, officers returned their purge commissions in writing, with copies sent — deliberately, pointedly — to the same criers' guilds.

The Purge did not collapse all at once. It collapsed the way a roof collapses: a groan, a sag, a rain of dust, and then all at once, irreversibly, down.

But roofs fall on the people underneath them. Isandro knew it, watching the news accumulate like snowfall, and on the thirteenth night in Marrowford, sitting alone over the last unposted copy of the letter, they admitted what they had been avoiding admitting since the first crier spoke: the queen would not fall quietly, and she knew Isandro's face. She had fed them from her own table for six years. Somewhere in the palace, a warrant was being drafted with a very specific name on it — or worse, no name at all, just a description and a bounty, loose in the world like anything else the crown let off its leash.

Bren found them at the window past midnight, where Isandro was watching the square out of old habit, tasting the dark air for trouble.

"You've got the look," he said. "The pre-battle look. You get very still and you listen with your whole skin."

"Do I?"

"Like a rabbit deciding which way to run. Except you never run." He leaned on the sill beside them. Below, the square was empty and frosted, the crier's platform standing bare under the moon. "Say it, then. Whatever it is."

"The queen knows me," Isandro said. "Not my gift — she never knew that. But my face, my post, my name. When the letters reach the palace, and they've reached it by now, she won't waste time wondering who did this. She'll send for her food-taster. And when she finds the food-taster gone, she'll send for the food-taster in earnest." Isandro turned the last copy of the letter over in their hands. "Everything we've set in motion protects the truth. Nothing protects us. The truth will win eventually — ink is patient. But she has soldiers, and wolves of her own kind, and she is cornered, and cornered queens burn things."

Bren was quiet a moment. Then: "So we don't be here when it burns."

"No," Isandro said. "We go north, up the valley, to the house above Greywater where my mother's people came from. There's a charter to write — a guild, for gifted healers and tasters, out in the open, under law if the law will have us and under each other's protection if it won't. And—" They stopped. Listened.

Down in the square, faint but unmistakable on the frozen air, came the sound Isandro had spent six years training their ear to catch before anyone else caught it: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast. Not the rhythm of a courier — the rhythm of a pursuit, riders fanned wide, coming up the river road toward Marrowford with the queen's colors somewhere among them, and leading them, by the banner-signal Rook had taught Isandro to read at a distance, a warrant-party with authority to take alive.

Or to stop pretending, if taking proved difficult.

Bren's head lifted. His nostrils flared, reading the wind the way he read everything. "Horses. Eight. Maybe ten. Half a night out." He looked at Isandro, and the wolf was very close behind his eyes. "How fast can you finish that letter?"

Isandro looked down at the last copy — the one addressed not to a crier or a garrison or a jealous house, but written in a steadier hand than the rest, to be delivered by their own mouth, in person, to the one place in the kingdom where it would cut deepest: the palace itself, into the queen's own hands, before her own court.

"Fast enough," Isandro said, and reached for the ink.
