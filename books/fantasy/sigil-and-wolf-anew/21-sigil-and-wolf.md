## 21. Sigil and Wolf

The snow had stopped three days before the soldiers came, and Isandro spent those days doing something they had never once done in all their years of service to the crown: nothing.

They sat in the small house above the valley — Rook's cache, once; a smuggler's bolt-hole with a hearth that drew well and windows that faced east, toward the lowlands, toward the city where the queen still sat her throne on a chair built of other people's debts. They sat by that hearth and mended a shirt. It was Bren's shirt, torn at the shoulder where the wardens' blades had found him beneath the mountain, and Isandro worked the needle through the wool with hands that should have been shaking and were not.

That was the strangest part. Not the ledgers, already copied and sent winging out with criers and peddlers and one very determined pigeon-fancier from the salt flats. Not the purge collapsing like a rotten roof, garrisons turning their officers out into the streets, rival houses reading Isandro's ink aloud in their halls until the words *the crown sells souls* stopped being treason and started being simply true. None of that shook them. What shook them was the quiet. The ordinary, domestic, unguarded quiet of a morning with no poison in it anywhere.

Bren lay on the long bench by the fire, his shoulder bound, his ribs strapped, a wolf too large for the room even in his man-shape. He had slept most of three days, waking to eat, to drink the willow-bark tea Isandro brewed, to say something dry and watch for their smile. Now he watched them sew.

"You're doing that wrong," he said.

"I have mended a thousand shirts."

"You've tasted a thousand dinners. Different skill." He shifted, winced, settled. "The seam'll pucker."

"Then I'll wear it puckered." Isandro bit the thread. "It's my shirt now anyway. You gave it to me when you bled on the old one."

"That's not how gifts work."

"It's exactly how gifts work. Ask any courtier."

He laughed — a low sound, careful of his ribs, but real. Outside, the thaw was beginning in earnest; water ran down the mountain in a hundred bright threads, and the smell of wet stone and early green came through the gaps in the shutters. Somewhere below, a cart creaked along the valley road. Ordinary sounds. Isandro kept cataloguing them the way they had once catalogued flavors — almond, always check for almond — and kept catching themselves at it, and kept putting the habit down like a knife no longer needed at table.

Sefa was asleep in the loft. She slept most hours still, her soul newly returned to her, scarred the way a tree is scarred by wire — growing around the wound, alive, changed. When she woke she would sit by the fire and hold a cup of broth in both hands as if remembering what warmth was for, and sometimes she would look at Isandro and say, without any particular expression, "I knew about the sigils since we were twelve, you know," and Isandro would say, "You never said," and she would say, "Neither did you," and that would be the whole conversation, and it would be enough.

Rook was gone to the lowlands with the last copies of the ledger, walking openly down roads he'd spent a lifetime keeping secret, because a man whose brother's soul had come home out of a lantern-glass no longer saw much use in hiding anything.

And so there were only three of them in the house above the valley, and the fire was warm, and the shirt was puckered, and Isandro thought: *this is what I hid from, all those years. This is what I thought would kill me.*

It nearly did, of course. But not this way.

* * *

They heard the horses first.

Not many. Six, perhaps eight, moving at a walk up the switchback road — no urgency in them, which was itself information. A raid comes fast. A summons comes slow. Isandro set down the shirt and stood, and Bren was already upright, already listening, his head tilted in that way he had, reading the world through ears older than his shape.

"Eight," he said. "Cavalry. Iron shoes, army farriers. They've stopped at the foot of the path." A pause. His nostrils flared. "Leather. Oil. Fear."

"Fear?"

"Wolves smell fear. Men are just wolves with worse manners." He looked at Isandro, and there was something in his face Isandro had learned to read under the mountain, in the dark, between one warden's horn and the next. "They don't want to be here."

Isandro crossed to the window and eased the shutter open a finger's width. Below, at the bend where the smuggler's path met the old drove road, eight riders had dismounted. Their armor was the queen's gray-and-gold, but it wore badly on them — buckled crooked, cloaks pinned wrong, the way armor sits on bodies that have stopped believing in what it's for. One of them held a scroll with the royal seal, and he held it the way you hold a thing you've been told is holy and have privately decided is not.

At their head stood a captain Isandro knew.

Captain Vess Marrow. She had served at the queen's left hand for eleven years. She had eaten a thousand meals Isandro had tasted first. She had once, at a midwinter feast, quietly pushed her wine toward Isandro and murmured, "This one smells off to me — do you taste it?" — and Isandro had tasted it, and saved her life, and neither of them had ever spoken of it again. Vess Marrow owed Isandro a life and both of them had pretended the debt did not exist, because pretending was how the palace survived.

She was not pretending now. She stood at the foot of the path with her helmet under her arm and her sword still sheathed, looking up at the small house above the valley the way a woman looks at a door she has been ordered to knock on and does not wish to open.

"They've come for me," Isandro said.

"Yes." Bren did not move toward his boots. That was the thing about Bren; every instinct in him said *run or fight*, and he had spent a winter learning a third option, and it cost him visibly every time. "And?"

"And nothing. Let them climb."

"Isandro." His voice dropped. "Eight soldiers. Your ribs aren't the ones strapped."

"My ribs are fine. Yours are the ones strapped, and you'll stay by that fire if I have to sigil you to the floor." They said it lightly, and then didn't, because the moment deserved better. "Bren. Look at them. Look at how they sit their horses. Those aren't hunters. Those are men who've read the ledger."

His jaw worked. "The ledger doesn't unmake an order."

"No. But it unmakes the belief behind the order, and belief is all an order ever was." Isandro pulled on their coat — the plain servant's coat, wool gone soft at the cuffs, the coat they had worn through banquets and purges and the whole long masquerade of their life. Then, deliberately, they reached to the shelf and took down the little kit they had rebuilt in the weeks since the mountain: bone stylus, iron gall ink, the sliver of mirror in which they had once practiced sigils by candlelight while the palace slept. They weighed it in their hand.

They did not hide it. That was the whole point. They hung it at their belt where anyone could see.

"The hidden life ends today," Isandro said. "One way or another. I'd rather it ended standing up."

Bren looked at them for a long moment. Then he took his boots from beside the hearth and put them on, and he took his sword down from over the door, and he did not buckle it on. He carried it bare in his hand, point down, the way a soldier carries a blade when he means to be seen holding it and not using it. And he stood at Isandro's side, at the top of the path, in the thin bright morning, in full view of eight of the queen's soldiers — a disgraced wolf-shifter, branded traitor by his pack, standing guard over a food-taster with a satchel of forbidden craft at their belt.

"Found pack holds," he said quietly.

"Found pack holds," Isandro answered.

* * *

Vess Marrow climbed the path alone.

That was the first sign. The second was that she stopped ten paces off — close enough to speak, far enough to be courteous, which in the language of soldiers meant *I am not here to take you*. The third sign was that she looked at Bren's bare blade and did not reach for her own.

"Taster Okonkwo," she said. Her voice was parade-ground steady and her eyes were not. "By decree of Her Majesty, I am instructed to arrest the fugitive known as Isandro Okonkwo, formerly of the palace kitchens, on charges of sedition, theft of crown property, and—" her jaw tightened around the word— "unlicensed gift-work."

"Unlicensed," Isandro said. "As opposed to licensed gift-work. Which the crown performed for decades, in bulk, beneath a mountain."

Something flickered across the faces of the seven soldiers waiting below. They had heard. Of course they had heard; the whole kingdom had heard; the criers had shouted it in nine cities and the salt-flat pigeon-fancier had nailed it to a barn door in a village so small it didn't appear on maps. *The Purge is harvest.* Every gifted soul sold to pay the crown's debts, dressed up in law's clothing and called justice.

"I know what the ledger says," Vess said. "I've read it twice."

"And yet you climbed the path."

"I climbed the path because I am still, God help me, a soldier of the crown, and someone had to bring you the order instead of letting them send men who'd have burned the house first and asked questions after." She lifted the scroll. "So. Here it is. The order. Read it yourself if you like. The seal's genuine. The signature's genuine. Everything about it is genuine except the reason, and we both know the reason died the day your ink hit the streets."

Isandro did not take the scroll. "What will you do, Captain?"

"That," she said, "is the question I climbed this mountain to answer."

Behind her, the seven soldiers had come up the path too — not in formation, not at command, just drifting upward the way men drift toward a thing they need to see. They stopped in a rough half-circle, helmets off, breath steaming in the cold bright air. Young ones, mostly. A couple grizzled veterans. One woman with a burn scar down her jaw whom Isandro recognized distantly from a feast two winters past — a kitchen guard, always kind to Sefa, always slipping the maids extra bread.

And Isandro felt it then, the way you feel weather change. Under the leather and oil and horse-sweat, under the iron and the fear Bren had smelled, there was something else rising off these eight like heat off stones. A scent Bren had caught and named *fear* and it was fear, yes, but fear of the wrong thing — not fear of Isandro. Fear of the order in the captain's hand. Fear of being the one who had to carry it out. And threaded through it, unmistakable to a taster who had spent a lifetime reading people the way they read soup:

Hope.

"Captain," Isandro said slowly, "may I ask you something? As one palate to another."

Vess blinked. "Ask."

"The midwinter feast, two years ago. The red wine from the southern cellars. You pushed it toward me and asked if it tasted off."

Color rose in her face. "I remember."

"It was bitter almond under the plum. Someone meant to kill you that night, Captain. I neutralized it with a sigil drawn in spilled wine while you watched me spit in the cuspidor and thought I was being delicate." Isandro let that stand in the cold air between them. "You have known what I was for two years. You drank from my hand afterward anyway. You ate everything I ever set before you. So tell me honestly — did my gift ever once frighten you before someone paid you to be frightened of it?"

The silence went on so long that the wind had time to move through the bare trees and come back.

"No," Vess said at last. It came out rough. She cleared her throat. "No. It didn't."

"Look at your people, Captain."

She turned. And Isandro watched her see what Isandro had already tasted in the air: the young soldier twisting his gloves, the veteran staring at the ground, the scarred kitchen-guard woman looking not at Isandro but at the sigil-kit hanging openly at their belt with an expression Isandro had seen a thousand times on a thousand faces across a lifetime of hiding — the look of someone recognizing a thing they thought they alone carried.

"Captain," said the scarred woman suddenly, voice cracking on it. "Ma'am. My mother could light candles with a word. They took her in the third sweep. They told us it was lawful." She swallowed. "We all know somebody. Don't we. Every one of us knows somebody."

Nobody spoke. Nobody had to. The half-circle nodded, one after another, the way a tide turns — not all at once, but all together.

Vess Marrow stood very still, holding the genuine seal and the genuine signature and the dead reason. Then she did the thing that Isandro would remember for the rest of their life, the thing they would tell Sefa by the fire that evening and tell the guild-charter signatories years after that.

She dropped the scroll in the snow.

Not threw it. Dropped it, deliberately, the way you set down a weight, and stepped back from it, and said, in a clear parade-ground voice that carried down the valley:

"The arrest cannot proceed. The warrant names a criminal. There is no criminal on this mountain. There is a taster who saved my life, a soldier who bled for strangers beneath a mountain, and a house full of people the crown owes more than it can ever pay." She looked at Isandro, and her eyes were wet and furious and free. "I'll bear whatever comes of it. I imagine I'm not alone."

The scarred woman laughed — a startled, broken sound. The young soldier sat down right there on the snowy path, as if his legs had simply resigned. The veteran took off his helmet entirely and tucked it under his arm like Vess's, and it was somehow that, more than anything, that made Isandro's throat close: eight soldiers of the queen, standing unarmed in the snow, refusing.

Bren exhaled beside them, long and slow, a wolf letting go of a held breath. "Well," he said. "That went better than the bridge."

"The bridge went fine."

"A river spirit tried to eat your name."

"And I won, if you recall."

"You cheated."

"Tasters don't cheat. We *prepare*."

* * *

Afterward — after the soldiers had warmed themselves at the hearth Sefa insisted on feeding them, after the scarred woman named Dalla had held her hands over the fire and said, very quietly, "My mother lit candles with a word," and Sefa had taken those hands and said, "Show me someday," after Vess had stood at the door and asked, formally, what Isandro intended to do now —

"Now?" Isandro had looked around the small warm room: Sefa scarred and stirring a pot, Bren sprawled by the fire with his ribs strapped and his sword finally hung properly on its peg, eight gray-and-gold cloaks drying on hooks like shed skins. "Now I stop tasting other people's food and start teaching people to trust their own tongues."

Vess had almost smiled. "The queen won't like it."

"The queen," Isandro said, "is going to have considerably larger problems than my charter within the month. The ledgers name her ministers, Captain. They name the magistrates who sold Sefa. They name the accounts. Justice that runs on souls can't survive daylight, and I intend to keep the sun high for a very long time."

Vess nodded slowly. Then she hesitated, and Isandro — who had spent a lifetime reading the small movements of people deciding whether to say a dangerous thing — waited.

"There's one more thing," she said. "A rider came to our column yesterday. Palace courier, sealed orders, marked urgent. Not for you." She reached into her cloak and produced a letter, its wax unbroken, the queen's seal pressed deep and clean. "For the captain commanding the arrest. Which is me. It instructs me that if the arrest fails, I'm to consider you... an asset rather than a fugitive. To offer terms. Protection. In exchange for your continued services." Her mouth twisted. "Her Majesty would like her taster back, Isandro. It seems she has dinners coming up that she's afraid to eat."

The room went quiet. The fire popped. Bren had gone very still, the way he went still before a fight, and Sefa had stopped stirring, and eight soldiers were watching Isandro with the particular attention of people witnessing history decide which way to lean.

Isandro took the letter. Turned it over once in their fingers. The wax was good work; it always was. They had tasted enough of the queen's world to know how good it was at sealing things in.

Then they handed it back to Vess, unopened.

"Tell Her Majesty," they said, "that the palace kitchens taught me one lesson above all others." They smiled, and it was not a servant's smile, and it was not a small one. "Never serve a dish you wouldn't eat yourself. She poisoned her own table for thirty years. Let her choke on it."

Vess bowed — low, deliberate, a bow between equals — and led her soldiers out into the bright cold morning, and down the path, and away toward the lowlands carrying a refusal instead of a prisoner, which was worth a great deal more.

Bren came to stand at Isandro's shoulder at the window, watching the riders shrink down the switchbacks.

"You know she'll send someone worse next time," he said.

"She'll try." Isandro leaned into him, wolf-warm, solid as the mountain at their backs. "But next time she won't be sending them to a hidden thing in a hidden house. Next time she sends soldiers, they'll find a guild hall, and a charter, and a line of gifted folk standing in the door who've stopped being afraid." They watched the sun climb over Thornfell, gilding the snowfields where a river spirit still held a wolf's true name somewhere in its bright cold depths, where a pack still howled about a traitor who had chosen differently, where a sealed gate slept beneath the roots of a shattered court. "Let her come. We'll be home."

Below them, far down the valley, the first spring carts were already on the road — and among them, Isandro noticed, a wagon with no markings at all, moving too fast for commerce and too straight for wandering, driving directly toward the capital with the kind of purpose that made a taster's trained eye narrow.

Someone else had read the ledgers. And someone else, it seemed, was hurrying to the queen before the truth arrived.

"Rook should see this," Isandro said softly. "Whoever drives that wagon isn't carrying grain."
