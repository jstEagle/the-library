## 22. A Table Set for Four

The thaw came to Thornfell the way forgiveness comes — slowly, then all at once, and with a great deal of water.

Isandro heard it first from the kitchen window of the small house above the valley: a sound like a thousand small bells, which was only the ice on the eaves letting go, drop by drop, into the mud below. Then came the smell. Winter had been a smell of stone and smoke and held breath; spring was loam and running water and the green insistence of things that had waited all season underground for their moment. Isandro stood at the window with flour to the wrists and let it all in through the open sash, breathing like someone who had only just remembered that breath could be taken for pleasure rather than survival.

For eleven years they had tasted everything twice — once with the tongue and once with fear. Even now, months after the mountain closed behind them, even now with the queen's criers reading retractions in three cities and the Purge collapsing like a bad soufflé, Isandro still caught themselves doing it: lifting a spoon, pausing, reading the steam for the faint bitter signature of arsenic or the almond ghost of bitter-cyanide or the oily sheen of something worse. Old habits sat deep as scars.

But this morning there was no poison anywhere in the house. This morning there was only bread proving by the banked oven, a haunch of lamb steeping in wine and juniper, honey melting off a warm knife, and four places set at a table that had never once, in its short life, been laid with anything meant to harm.

Isandro tested it anyway. Not out of fear — out of craft. A fingertip to the glaze of the sauce, tongue touched briefly to skin, and the whole flavor unfolded like a hand opening: sweet dark wine, resin, salt, the lamb's own richness underneath. Nothing hiding beneath. Nothing coiled in the finish. Just food, honest as a handshake.

They laughed — actually laughed, alone in the kitchen, at the sheer luxury of a clean taste — and went back to work.

* * *

The house had come to them by way of Rook Vane, who claimed he'd won it in a card game from a man who'd won it in a war, and who refused to say more because "a smuggler's provenance is like a lady's age: known to everyone, admitted by no one." It sat above the valley on a shelf of green hillside, stone-walled and slate-roofed, with a garden gone gloriously feral under Sefa's hands and a view down over the river where, if you knew where to look, you could see the old toll-bridge glinting in the sun. The river spirit still kept its bridge. Isandro still owed it nothing but courtesy, and paid that gladly.

Bren had carried the doorframe up the hill on his back when the frame cracked in transit, walking the whole way in human shape because "wolves don't do carpentry," though Isandro suspected he simply liked the look on the carter's face. He'd rebuilt it slightly too wide. When asked why, he'd shrugged and said, "Room for whoever comes." That was Bren entire: a creature who had been thrown out of one family for his loyalty and had answered by building a house with extra room in the doorway.

Sefa had planted the garden before the walls were properly mended. "Priorities," she'd said, dirt to her elbows, seeds in her fist like a gambler holding a winning hand. She had come back from beneath the mountain with her soul scarred — some days her eyes went distant and she'd lose the middle of a sentence, some nights she woke calling for a mother who'd died years ago — but she had come back *whole*, which the lantern-glass had promised no one. The healers said the scarring might fade. Sefa said she didn't want it to. "It's proof I was worth stealing," she told Isandro once, "and worth taking back."

Now her voice floated up from the garden, arguing cheerfully with Rook about whether peas could be trellised on smuggler's cord. They could not, Rook insisted. Sefa was doing it anyway.

Isandro rolled the dough thin, thought about nothing dangerous at all, and was happy.

* * *

The charter arrived by courier two days early, wrapped in oilcloth against the spring damp, sealed with the new sigil of the Guild of Gifted Healers and Tasters — a mortar and pestle crossed with a quill, drawn inside a circle that was deliberately, pointedly, unbroken. Isandro turned it over in their hands at the kitchen table and read it twice, slowly, the way one reads anything that will change a life.

*Be it known that those born with the gift of discernment — of poison and physic, of truth and falsehood, of the body's needs and the body's harms — shall practice openly, under law, under oath, and under each other's protection. No taster shall be required to prove loyalty by swallowing what another means to kill with. No healer shall be punished for knowing too much.*

No taster shall be required to swallow what another means to kill with.

Isandro read that line a third time and had to set the parchment down.

Eleven years. Eleven years of the guildhall's back stairs, of dosing themselves in secret until their blood was a pharmacopoeia, of smiling at nobles whose kitchens they could have emptied with a whisper, of lying awake cataloguing every poison they'd neutralized and every one they hadn't caught in time. Eleven years of being valuable precisely insofar as they were expendable. And here, in ink, in law, in plain language anyone could read: *never again.*

There was a space at the bottom for signatures. Isandro's would be the first — founder's privilege, the courier had said, along with a look of awe that Isandro was still learning how to be uncomfortable under. Below it, blank lines waited for others. There were supposed to be many others. The purge had taught the crown exactly how many gifted people it had been living among, and the answer had frightened the crown considerably more than the gifted ever had.

Isandro found the pen. Found the ink. And then, out of eleven years of habit, checked the ink.

It was fine. Of course it was fine. But checking had kept them alive, and they saw no reason yet to be ashamed of staying alive.

They signed: *Isandro Okonkwo, taster, sigil-maker.* Both names. Both trades. All of it, in the open, where anyone could see.

The pen made a small scratchy sound, very loud in the quiet kitchen, and when it was done Isandro sat looking at their own name for a long moment — the first document in their life that named the gift without shame attached — and felt something in their chest unclench that had been clenched so long they'd forgotten it was a muscle.

Then the smoke alarm that was Bren's nose went off, and Isandro rescued the bread.

* * *

They came up the hill in the gold part of the afternoon, when the light lay long across the grass and the thaw-water ran bright in every furrow.

Rook arrived first, naturally, because Rook treated punctuality as a form of reconnaissance. He brought wine — good wine, suspiciously good wine, the kind of wine that raised questions about certain ledgers — and a parcel he thrust at Isandro with elaborate casualness. Inside was a set of knives. Not smuggler's knives. Cook's knives. Beautiful ones, balanced, honed, with handles worn smooth by someone who had loved them.

"Found them in a cache," Rook said, not meeting Isandro's eye. "Figured you'd put them to better use than I would."

"You gave your brother's knives away?"

"I gave my brother's knives to the person who got him back." Rook's voice did something complicated on the last word. He buried it in a shrug. "He'd have liked you. He liked people who fed him."

Isandro touched the handles once, gently, and said nothing, because some gifts are too large for thanks and can only be carried forward.

Bren came up from the tree-line in wolf-shape, running the ridge with the pure stupid joy of an animal in spring, and shifted at the gate shaking off snowmelt like a dog — which he pretended not to be, and was, entirely. He'd filled out since winter. The wound in his side had healed to a pale seam he showed off to children in the valley as "the mountain's autograph." He smelled the lamb from thirty yards and his tail — absent in human shape, but somehow still implied — betrayed him completely.

"Juniper," he said, reverent. "You used juniper."

"Don't get sentimental."

"I'm a wolf. We're *all* sentiment. It's mostly what we're made of." He ducked through the too-wide doorframe without ducking, as it was built for him to need not to, and stopped dead at the table. Four places. Real cloth. Candles, because Sefa had insisted candles make any meal a ceremony, and Sefa was usually right about ceremonies.

"Four places," he said quietly.

"Always four places," Isandro said. "You know that."

Sefa came last, carrying peas — actual peas, first of the season, triumphant — and a smudge of dirt on her cheek that no one dared point out. She looked at the table, at the charter lying open beside the bread, at Isandro standing in their own kitchen with both names signed in their own hand, and her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding whether to cry or joke.

She joked. She was crying while she did it, but she joked.

"Look at you," she said. "Guild-founder. Charter-signer. Sitting at a table with no poison in anything. Who even *are* you?"

Isandro considered the question seriously, because Sefa deserved serious answers even wrapped in jokes.

"Someone who used to be two people," they said. "And is only one now. Sit down. The lamb's ready."

* * *

Dinner was, by any measure Isandro had ever applied to food, unremarkable — and therefore the finest meal of their life.

The lamb fell apart under the spoon. The bread was dense and good and slightly lopsided where Bren had shaped one loaf himself ("wolves aren't bakers either, but I contain multitudes"). The peas were sweet enough to make Rook suspicious, and he said so, and Sefa threw a pea at him, and he caught it in his mouth without appearing to move, which settled the question of whether smuggler reflexes atrophied in retirement. (They had not retired. Everyone knew this. Nobody pressed.)

They talked the way people talk when they have earned the right to be boring: about the garden, about whether wolves could be taught to herd geese (no), about whether Rook's card game winnings would survive an audit (they would not), about the young taster arriving next week from the capital — a girl of sixteen who could taste lies the way Isandro tasted poison, fleeing a household that had decided her gift was convenient rather than human. Isandro had written back: *Come. There's room in the doorway.*

"And the guild?" Sefa asked, seconding helpings around the table with the authority of someone who had once fed a hundred mouths a day on palace scraps. "When does it open?"

"When the charter has enough names," Isandro said. "Forty-one so far. Healers, tasters, two sigil-makers, one woman who can hear storms coming three days out and wants to teach farmers to listen." They turned their cup slowly on the cloth. "We'll train them properly. Openly. Oaths instead of secrets. And when the crown comes asking for tasters—" they smiled, thin and real, "—the crown can hire us like anyone else. At fair rates. With contracts."

"With *lawyers*," Rook said, delighted. "You've become terrifying."

"I learned from watching lawyers sell souls," Isandro said, and the table went quiet for a moment, the way it always did when the mountain surfaced between them — briefly, respectfully, and then sank again. They had agreed, the four of them, on one rule: the dead court could be spoken of, but never allowed to sit at the table. Tonight it stayed outside, in the cold, where it belonged.

Bren broke the silence the way he broke most things: gently and completely. He leaned over and bumped his shoulder against Isandro's, wolf-affection translated into human grammar.

"You could have stayed hidden," he said. "Even after. You had the ledgers. You could have sent them and vanished somewhere warm and safe and anonymous." His amber eyes caught the candlelight. "Why sign your name? Why stand in the doorway?"

Isandro looked down the table — at Sefa, alive, scarred, laughing at Rook; at Rook, who had broken his own cowardice like a seal on a letter and never quite gotten over being proud of it; at Bren, who had chosen them over a pack and never once made the choosing feel like a cost.

"Because I spent eleven years learning every poison," Isandro said, "and the deadliest one I ever swallowed was secrecy. Small doses. Daily. Seemed harmless." They lifted their cup. "I'm done drinking it."

Sefa raised hers. Rook raised his. Bren, who drank wine the way wolves drink water — enthusiastically and with spillage — raised his highest.

"To the found pack," Bren said.

"To room in the doorway," said Sefa.

"To clean tastes," said Isandro.

They drank. Outside, the last light slid off the mountains, and somewhere down in the valley the river ran loud with meltwater, carrying winter away to the sea.

* * *

Later — dishes done, candles guttering, Rook asleep in the chair he insisted wasn't a nap, Sefa humming over her seed-trays by lamplight — Isandro stepped out onto the doorstep with the charter under one arm and stood in the spring dark.

The stars over Thornfell were thick as spilled salt. Somewhere upslope, a wolf sang — not Bren, some wild cousin, a voice from the high passes — and Bren's answering note rose from inside the house, low and content, a conversation older than either of them.

Isandro looked at the signature drying on the parchment. Their own name, in their own hand, naming everything they were.

Below it, forty blank lines waited. And beyond the hills, in cities and villages and hidden kitchens, dozens more waited too — the gifted, the hunted, the ones who had survived the purge by burying themselves alive and didn't yet know the ground had thawed.

Word would reach them. Word always reached the hidden; it was the first thing the hidden learned to hear.

Isandro breathed the green night air, tasting nothing but spring, and went inside to add more places to the table.
