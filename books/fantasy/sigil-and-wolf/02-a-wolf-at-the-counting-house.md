## 2. A Wolf at the Counting House

The bell above the counting house door rang wrong.

Marguerite knew every sound the Vellum Guild made the way a mother knows her child's breathing. The morning bell was bright and brief. The noon bell had a flat note, struck by a clapper worn smooth on one side. But this — this was past closing, long past, when the lamps burned low and the clerks had gone home to their suppers, and the bell that rang now had a rasp in it, like a throat clearing before it spoke.

She set down her pen. The entry she'd been balancing — three bottled storms sold to a mining concern in the eastern hills, payment rendered in forty years of good luck, which the guild would collect with interest — stayed open on her desk. Ink dried slowly in the counting house. It liked to wait.

"Closed," she called, though she was already rising, already reaching for the iron key that hung beneath her collar. Guild policy said the night clerk handled walk-ins. Guild policy also assumed the night clerk hadn't resigned three days ago, and that Marguerite hadn't yet found his replacement, which meant the counting house's entire after-dark business — its ledgers, its vaults, its sealed gate humming beneath the floorboards like a sleeping animal — rested on her alone.

The man in the doorway filled most of it. Tall, but not broad; he had the lean look of something built for distance rather than strength. A traveler's coat, road-gray, with mud on the hem from streets that hadn't seen rain in a week. His hood was back, and his hair was dark going gray at the temples, and his eyes —

His eyes went around the room first. That was what she noticed. Not the merchandise, not the clerk behind the counter. The exits. The windows. The weight-bearing beams. He read the counting house the way she read a ledger: line by line, looking for the falsified entry.

"You're closed," he agreed. His voice was low and roughened, a voice used to being quiet. "I'll be quick."

"The guild trades by daylight. Appointments only."

"I don't make appointments." He stepped inside without quite asking permission, and the door swung shut behind him on its own weight, and Marguerite felt the floor hum — once, faintly, under her feet. The gate below had noticed him.

That should have frightened her more than it did. What frightened her instead was how *familiar* the hum felt, like a word half-remembered from a dream.

* * *

The trading floor of the Vellum Guild looked, to the uninitiated, like nothing so much as a bank. Marble counters. Brass fittings. Ledgers bound in calfskin stacked behind barred windows where tellers sat with ink-stained fingers. Only the inventory gave it away: the glass case of bottled weather along the east wall; the shelf of borrowed years, each one stoppered in amber vial and labeled in a hand so fine it looked printed; the cabinet of names, which Marguerite had never been permitted to open, locked behind three keys none of which she owned.

The man walked the length of the room like he was measuring it for a coffin. Then he stopped at the counter across from her station, reached into his coat, and set something down on the marble with a click that rang through the whole floor.

A sigil-stone.

Marguerite had catalogued four hundred and eleven sigil-stones in her nine years with the guild. They came in all sizes — some no bigger than a thumbnail, some the size of a fist — and they were the guild's most tightly controlled commodity, because a sigil-stone was a name given physical form. Press your palm to one and it would know you. It would remember you when you'd forgotten yourself. The guild bought them from desperate families and sold them to worse ones, and every stone that crossed her desk went into the ledger with its mark recorded in her own careful hand.

This one was old. Older than any she'd catalogued. It was the size of a hen's egg, cut from gray river-stone that should have been dull as dishwater but instead held light the way water holds a moon. And carved into its face, deep enough to have been worked with tools rather than magic, was a wolf.

Not a wolf the way a herald would draw one — rampant, decorative, tongue lolling in some dead duke's colors. This wolf was running. Head low, ears back, body stretched full out, carved by someone who had watched wolves run and never forgotten it. And beneath its forepaws, small as a signature, was a single mark: a vertical stroke crossed by two shorter ones, like a bird's footprint pressed into snow.

Marguerite's hand went to her collar before she could stop it. Beneath the linen, against her skin, the iron key she wore there seemed suddenly warm.

Because she knew that mark. She had known it since she was six years old, since the night her mother woke her and pressed her small palm against something cold and told her, in a voice cracked down the middle, *remember this, remember this, whatever else they take —*

She couldn't remember her mother's face. She couldn't remember her mother's voice except for that one sentence. But she remembered the mark. Two crossings over one stroke, burned into her behind her eyelids, older than any memory she could account for.

"How much?" the man said.

She dragged her gaze up from the stone. He was watching her watch it, and there was something in his expression she didn't like — not greed, not even caution. Recognition. The way you look at a stranger who is wearing your brother's coat.

"That depends on provenance," she managed. Her voice came out steady. Nine years of practice at steadiness. "Sigil-stones require documentation. Origin, lineage of ownership, the rite of severance. Without papers, I can't—"

"It doesn't have papers."

"Then I can't buy it."

"Yes, you can." He turned the stone slowly on the marble, and the lamplight slid across the running wolf, and Marguerite's chest went tight. "You're the keeper. I asked at three taverns before I found this place, and at every one they said the same thing: ask for the woman who keeps the ledgers. The one who can feel the gate." His eyes lifted to hers. "So. Can you feel me?"

The question was obscene and he knew it, and she hated him for asking it, and hated herself more for answering it honestly, because yes. She could feel him. Everyone registered on the gate's attention like pressure changes before weather — the clerks were barely ripples, the masters were slow heavy swells. But this man came through like a footstep on frozen ice. Sharp. Close. *Listening.*

"You shouldn't be able to feel anything," she said quietly. "The gate is sealed. Ordinary people pass over it their whole lives and never know it's there."

"I'm not ordinary."

"No," she said. "You're not."

* * *

They negotiated the way wolves circle — or so she imagined, having never circled anything but debt columns. He named an outrageous figure. She named an insulting one. He told her the stone had come off a corpse in the border fog, and she told him corpses in the border fog didn't carry sigil-stones because the fog ate them, and his mouth twitched at that, almost approval, and she realized with a jolt that she was enjoying this, and hated that too.

"Fifty crowns," he said finally.

"Twenty-five, and I record the sale honestly, which means recording that you refused to give a name. Anonymous sales draw questions. Questions draw the crown's assessors. You'd be doing yourself a favor."

"Thirty, and my name is Corren Ash, and you can write it in your pretty book, because I've been written in worse ones."

She wrote it. *Corren Ash.* The ink took cleanly, and she noted the date, the item, the price, and then — because the guild required it, because the ledger required *everything* — she dipped her pen again and began to sketch the sigil mark into the margin: the running wolf, the footprint beneath its paws.

Her pen stopped.

"What year was the stone cut?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"Older than the Correction. Maybe older than the kingdom." He shrugged. "The man I took it from said it was a family thing. Passed down. He said—" Corren paused, and for the first time since he'd walked in, he looked uncertain, and the uncertainty made him look younger, and dangerous in a different way. "He said there were others like it. A whole litter of them. Stones cut for children, so they'd remember who they were if anyone ever tried to make them forget."

The lamp flame leaned sideways. There was no draft. Below the floor, very softly, the gate pulsed — once, twice — like a heart picking up speed.

Marguerite put down the pen. She reached beneath her collar and drew out the iron key she had worn since she was apprenticed, the key the masters had given her, the key that opened nothing she'd ever found. Except it wasn't a key. She had known it wasn't a key for years, the way you know a lie you've agreed to keep. It was a stamp. A seal matrix. And stamped into its face, worn nearly smooth by decades of handling, was a mark.

One stroke. Two crossings.

Corren Ash looked at it, and then at her, and the wariness in his face broke apart into something rawer. "Where did you get that?"

"It was given to me. With my position." She heard her own heartbeat. "I was told it was a guild token."

"It's not a guild token." His voice had dropped, gone quiet and fast, a man talking near a sleeping predator. "I've got the same one. Burned into my shoulder when I was seven years old, by a woman who wasn't my mother, in a house I can't find anymore on a road that isn't on any map. There were six of us marked that winter. I'm the only one I've ever found." He leaned across the counter, close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and road dust on him. "Who are you? Before the guild. Before the ledgers. Who were you?"

"I'm Marguerite Blackwood."

"That's a name," he said. "It's not an answer."

And the terrible thing — the thing that kept her awake that night and every night after — was that he was right. She searched backward through herself the way she'd search a ledger for a missing sum, and the sums added up perfectly right up until they didn't. A childhood in the capital. A school. An apprenticeship. All of it neat, all of it documented, all of it beginning — she realized now, with the wolf-mark burning on the counter between them — beginning suspiciously, impossibly, exactly forty years after the Correction, like a story someone had written for her and told her she'd lived.

Below the floor, the gate pulsed again. Hungry. Patient.

Corren straightened, and the rawness shuttered away, and he was a smuggler again, a seller, a stranger. "Thirty crowns," he said. "We're done here."

"We're not done." She pushed the stone back toward him, though every part of her wanted to close her hand around it and never let go. "You didn't come here to sell this. Smugglers sell things they want rid of. You've carried this stone through the border fog and half the country to bring it *here*, to the one place in the world that keeps records older than the Correction." She met his eyes. "You came to ask me something. So ask."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the walls, the building settled, or pretended to.

Then Corren Ash smiled, and it was not a comforting sight — all teeth, no warmth, a flash of the feral thing she'd sensed under his skin since the moment the bell rang wrong.

"Not tonight," he said. "Tonight I sell you a stone for thirty crowns, and you write my name in your book, and we're strangers. But tomorrow, keeper — tomorrow I'm coming back with a question about a road that eats maps. And when I do, you're going to want to lock your door, sit down, and pour something strong. Because I think you and I were both erased from somewhere." He tapped the counter once, beside the running wolf. "And I think somebody downstairs knows exactly where."

He was gone before she could answer, the bell rasping behind him, and Marguerite stood alone in the lamplight with thirty crowns, a stolen relic, and a ledger whose next blank line seemed to wait for her name.

Above the counter, unnoticed by either of them, the ink in the open entry had begun — very slowly — to move.
