## 3. The Proclamation

Corren Ash had stolen things from every quarter of Vellamark — candlesticks off a bishop's altar, a sleeping countess's ring, a whole barge of saffron once, just to prove it could be done. He knew the city the way a wolf knows a forest: by scent, by shadow, by the small telltale sounds that meant someone was about to ruin his morning.

So when the bells began at dawn, all of them at once, wrong, he was awake before the first note finished dying.

He lay on the narrow bed in the rented room above the Saltcellar tavern and listened. Bells rang for fires, for coronations, for funerals of the great. They did not ring *all together*, in no pattern any ringer would choose, unless someone wanted the whole city's eyes open at the same hour.

Below his window, footsteps. Many. Moving with purpose.

He dressed in the dark, checked that the sigil-stone still sat snug against his chest where he'd sewn it into his shirt lining — the stone that had cost him three weeks of planning and one very unpleasant night in a smuggler's cellar to acquire, and which he intended to sell today to the Vellum Guild before anyone came asking after it — and went down.

The tavern keeper, a woman named Hess whose face was a topography of old fights, stood in the doorway staring out at the street. She didn't look at him when he came to stand beside her. She didn't look away from the street for a long time.

"Criers," she said finally. "At this hour."

In the grey light, a man in crown livery stood on the fountain's rim, unrolling a scroll with the crisp ceremony of someone who had rehearsed. Around him, the early market crowd had gone still in a way crowds almost never did. Fishwives with their baskets down. A milk cart abandoned mid-street, the horse dozing. Even the gulls had shut up.

The crier's voice carried the way trained voices do, flat and clear and reaching every corner without effort.

"By decree of the Crown, in the matter of unnatural gifts: all persons possessing or suspected of possessing magic are hereby summoned for examination. Examination shall be conducted by the Office of the Witchfinder General. Compliance is expected. Sheltering the gifted is treason. Reporting them is duty. The Crown thanks its faithful subjects."

A pause, exactly long enough.

"Examination is mandatory. Examination is final."

He rolled the scroll. Stepped down. Walked on to the next square, flanked by two soldiers who looked bored, and the crowd stayed frozen until he'd turned the corner, and then it did not erupt, because there was nothing to erupt *into*. It simply began moving again, faster than before, everyone suddenly very busy, very careful, eyes down.

Corren felt the cold start in his stomach and spread outward, the way it always did, an old familiar animal instinct: *run*.

Hess shut the door. Turned the lock, though no one had knocked. "Examination," she said, tasting the word like spoiled milk. "My grandmother could find water with a willow twig. Half the herb-wives in the Lowmarket can take a fever out of a child. Is that what they're calling in the bells for?"

"That's what they're calling the bells for." Corren kept his voice level, which took work. His hand had drifted, without his permission, to rest over the hidden sigil-stone. "It won't stop at herb-wives."

"No," Hess said. "It won't." She looked at him properly then, and he saw her doing the arithmetic every innkeeper in a port city could do in her sleep: what her lodger was, what sheltering him might cost, what her own risk ledger said. To her credit — or perhaps to her cunning; with Hess it was hard to say — she only jerked her chin toward the back stairs. "You owe me for two nights. Pay up and use the cellar door. And Ash? Whatever you're carrying that makes you touch your chest like that — sell it fast."

He paid her. He used the cellar door.

* * *

The streets of Vellamark taught him the shape of the proclamation as he moved through them.

It wasn't panic. That was the worst part. Panic he knew how to move through — panic was weather, and weather passed. This was something more orderly. At the corner of Rope Street, a woman was quietly taking down the charms hung over her doorway, one by one, wrapping them in cloth like laundry. Outside the chandler's, a boy of maybe ten stood very straight while his mother scrubbed at his forearm with a wet rag, trying to remove a birthmark shaped like a feather, scrubbing until the skin went raw and the boy didn't cry, because he'd understood already, at ten, what marks meant now.

Two men argued in low voices outside a shuttered shop — not whether to report their neighbor, but whether they'd be punished for *not* having reported him yet.

And everywhere, nailed to walls and doorposts and the posts of the fish stalls, the proclamations themselves, fresh ink gleaming:

**BY DECREE OF THE CROWN. EXAMINATION IS MANDATORY. EXAMINATION IS FINAL.**

Corren read the words four times across four districts and each time they landed the same way, a fist under the ribs. He'd grown up in the border fog, among people the maps had given up on, people whose gifts were as ordinary as chapped hands. He'd spent ten years in the city learning to pass — learning to keep the wolf-thing coiled tight beneath his skin, learning to walk like a man with nothing unusual about him except bad luck and good hands. He had told himself the passing was temporary. A strategy. Someday, he'd told himself, the country would catch up to what it actually contained.

The country had not caught up. The country had turned around.

By the time he reached Guildhall Hill, he had made himself a promise with the cold, clean simplicity of a blade: get the sigil-stone sold, get paid, get out of Vellamark before the examination wagons started rolling. The Vellum Guild bought impossible things; let them have this one. Let them worry about it.

He should have known better. He'd been inside the counting house once before. Nothing about the place had ever promised safety.

* * *

The Vellum Guild's counting house squatted at the hill's crest like a fat black ledger dropped from the sky — granite, windowless on the ground floor, its upper windows tall and narrow and always, Corren had noticed, curtained. Smoke rose from its chimneys even in summer. Nobody he'd ever asked knew what was burned there.

Today the smoke was thicker. Today there were carts.

Two of them stood in the yard behind the building, and guild clerks — the grey-robed, ink-stained, perpetually apologetic men and women who made the guild's gears turn — were carrying armfuls of books out the loading door and feeding them to a fire pit that had been dug, hastily, right there between the flagstones. Not a proper pyre. An emergency. Books went in, pages curled black, and the clerks stood watching with expressions Corren recognized from funerals.

He'd come around the back intending to find the trade entrance and negotiate with whoever handled acquisitions. Instead he stood in the shadow of the wall and watched the Vellum Guild burn its own memory.

"You're not on the list."

He turned. A clerk stood behind him — young, round-faced, holding a ledger against her chest like a shield. Her name, he vaguely recalled from his last visit, was Tally or Tabitha or something built to rhyme with *account*. Her eyes were red-rimmed.

"I don't need to be on a list," Corren said. "I'm here to sell."

"Sell." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Man, look around you. We're not buying. We're—" She gestured at the fire with her chin, unable to finish.

"What is it you're burning?"

"Everything." The word escaped her like something shoved too long behind a door. Then she caught herself, glanced at the yard, at the windows, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "Orders from the masters, since the bells. Every record of acquisition. Every provenance chain. Every client register." Her arms tightened around her own ledger. "Every name."

"Names of clients?"

"Names of *anyone*." She was shaking now, fine tremors, the kind that live in the bones. "Don't you understand what we kept here? Forty years of it. People who sold us things no one else would touch. People who *bought* them. People who came through the—" She stopped herself so hard he could see the effort, teeth clicking shut. "It doesn't matter. It's being destroyed. All of it. The masters say if it isn't written, it can't be found, and if it can't be found—"

"—it can't be examined," Corren finished.

She nodded. And then, because terror makes strange confidants, or perhaps because she'd been carrying it since dawn and had nowhere left to put it, she leaned closer. "There's a second fire," she whispered. "Inside. In the archive vault. I'm not supposed to know. But the masters went down at first bell with the heavy ledgers — the ones that never leave the vault, the ones older than the charter — and Master Ellery was crying. Master Ellery has never shown an emotion except satisfaction in eleven years of service. And what they're burning down there—" Her voice failed and rebuilt itself. "It's not records. Records have copies. What they're burning down there only exists once. You understand? It's *people*, in ink. Whole lives. And they're feeding them to the fire like yesterday's rubbish, and they're doing it *calmly*, and they've asked me to bring my own book, and I don't know how much longer I can pretend I don't hear what's happening under this floor."

Somewhere beyond the wall, another crier began the decree again, the same flat cadence, *examination is mandatory, examination is final*, and Corren watched the young clerk flinch at every word as though the crier were reading her sentence aloud.

"The gifted," he said quietly. "That's who's in those ledgers. Isn't it. Names of the gifted, going back decades."

"I didn't say that." She stepped back, mask slamming down over her face, clerk once more. "I said nothing. I'm saying nothing now. If you'll excuse me, I have books to carry."

She walked toward the fire pit with her ledger hugged to her chest, and Corren saw, in the last moment before she reached it, her fingers tighten on the cover — and then loosen, deliberately, one finger at a time, like a woman letting go of a railing over deep water. She fed it to the flames. Watched it curl. Did not cry, though it cost her everything visible.

Corren stood in the shadow a while longer, doing his own arithmetic, and the sum had changed completely since dawn.

The guild wasn't a buyer anymore. The guild was a witness destroying itself. Which meant the sigil-stone against his chest — a stone bearing a mark he'd seen once before, burned into a memory that wasn't his, on a girl's hand in a dream he'd had since childhood and never understood — was suddenly worth either nothing at all, or far more than money.

And there was a third possibility he didn't yet have a name for: that the stone, and the mark, and the girl he hadn't met yet, and the forty years of names going into the fire, were all threads of the same knot. That the Correction the old ones in the fog camps muttered about when they thought the young weren't listening — *the year the world forgot itself*, his mother had called it, and spat — had a ledger somewhere. And that ledger was burning.

He touched his chest, feeling the stone's faint warmth through the linen. It pulsed. Or he imagined it pulsed. With that thing, the distinction had never been reliable.

Above the yard, in a curtained upper window, a figure appeared — a woman, pale hair pinned severely, standing very still behind the glass. She was looking down at the fire. Even at this distance, even through curtain-shadow, Corren could see she was not watching the books burn.

She was watching the street. Watching, methodically, everyone who stopped to watch.

Her gaze swept toward the wall where he stood, and Corren — a man who had pickpocketed a bishop — stepped back out of sight with his heart hammering like a boy's, certain, without knowing why, that she had seen him anyway.

He needed to get inside that building. Not to sell. To learn what was burning below the floor, and why the stone on his chest had begun, very softly, to sing.

* * *

Getting into the Vellum Guild's counting house on a day when the guild was devouring itself proved easier than it should have been, which told Corren everything about how rattled the machinery was. The loading door stood unbarred. The clerks were too few and too grief-struck to challenge a grey-cloaked figure carrying a crate, and the crate — borrowed from a fishmonger's alley — gave him purpose and blindness in equal measure.

Inside, the air tasted of smoke and lamp oil and something older underneath, something like a cellar that had never dried. The main hall stretched up past galleries of shelving, and everywhere was the organized chaos of destruction: ledgers stacked for carrying, registers sorted by century, clerks moving with the grim efficiency of people dismantling a cathedral.

No one questioned him. No one questioned anything. That was the rot in it — he could see it on every face. These people had served an institution that traded in bottled storms and borrowed years, and when the crown's bell rang, the institution had not hesitated, had not bargained, had not even slowed. It had simply begun erasing itself, calmly, thoroughly, the way it must have done everything. As if betrayal were just another entry in the accounts.

He found the stair to the vault by following the smoke.

It rose from below in a thin grey ribbon, and with it came a smell that stopped him on the steps — not paper, not leather. Underneath the burning books was something sweeter and far worse, like hair, like skin, like a name being rendered down to ash. The sigil-stone against his chest grew warm enough that he pressed his palm flat over it, half to soothe it, half to hold it down.

At the bottom of the stair, a door stood ajar, and through the gap he saw firelight on stone, and shadows of robed figures moving with terrible patience, feeding the flames.

And he heard voices. One of them, dry and precise, belonged to a master he recognized from the yard — Ellery, the one who never showed emotion, whose voice now shook only slightly, the way a bridge shakes, structurally.

"We begin with the founding rolls," Ellery was saying. "Then the toll registries. Then the crossings."

Another voice, older, female, tired beyond tiredness: "The toll registries last. There may still be time to save the toll registries."

"There is no time, Master Ondine. There has been no time for forty years. We simply pretended otherwise, and the pretense was profitable." A pause, and the sound of a heavy book opening. "Page one. Shall I read the first struck name, so we remember what we are?"

Silence. Then, softly, from the old woman: "I have never forgotten it. That is rather the point of me."

Corren stood in the dark of the stairwell, smoke stinging his eyes, the stone singing against his heart, and understood three things at once, the way one understands a snare closing: the guild had not merely recorded the Correction. The guild had *made* it. The names in the fire were not casualties of some ancient catastrophe — they were the catastrophe, catalogued and cross-referenced, and the masters were burning the evidence not to protect their clients but to protect the crime.

And somewhere in those founding rolls, in the first struck name the old woman had never forgotten, was a thread that ran — he could feel it, the way wolves feel weather — straight to the mark on his stone, and to whatever pale-haired watcher kept vigil in the curtained windows above.

Behind him, at the top of the stairs, a floorboard creaked.

He turned, hand already finding the knife at his belt, and found himself looking at a young woman in clerk's grey — ink-stained fingers, a smudge of soot on one cheekbone, and eyes that went straight past his disguise as if cloth were glass. She stared at him. He stared at her. Between them, rising up the stairwell, the smoke of burning names curled and thinned.

"You're not a clerk," she said.

"No," Corren admitted.

She glanced back over her shoulder toward the hall, toward the masters, toward the fire below — and then, in a voice pitched for him alone, trembling with something that was not fear, or not only fear:

"Then you'd better come with me. Before they burn the rest of it."
