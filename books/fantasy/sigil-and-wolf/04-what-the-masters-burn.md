## 4. What the Masters Burn

The order came down at dawn, written in Master Ellery's own hand on a slip of vellum so thin it was nearly translucent: *All ledgers of the Third Vault to be burned by nightfall. No copies. No exceptions.*

Marguerite read it three times standing in the doorway of her counting room, the paper trembling slightly between her fingers — not because her hands shook, but because the slip itself seemed to shiver, as though it already knew what it was asking and was ashamed.

No copies. No exceptions. She had worked for the Vellum Guild for eleven years, and in eleven years she had never once seen those words applied to a ledger. Ledgers were sacred. Ledgers were *the point*. The guild traded in impossible things — bottled storms, borrowed years, luck decanted into stoppered glass — and every trade, every debt, every promise made across the gate beneath the counting house lived or died by its record. A ledger burned was a debt unmade. A ledger burned was a person unwritten.

She looked up at Master Ellery, who had delivered the order himself rather than sending a clerk, which told her everything about how much he trusted his clerks this morning.

"Third Vault only?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later.

"The Third Vault." Ellery was an old man built like a folded letter, all creases and sharp edges, and today every crease looked deeper. "The others will follow. Don't ask questions you'll regret having answers to, Mistress Blackwood."

"I always regret having answers," she said. "I ask anyway."

Something flickered behind his eyes — pity, maybe, or its close cousin, fear wearing pity's coat. He set one dry hand briefly on her shoulder, the closest thing to tenderness the guild masters permitted themselves, and said, "Burn them clean. Ash to ash. And Marguerite—" he paused at the door, not turning around, "—the fire is to be witnessed. I will witness it myself."

Then he was gone, and she was alone with eleven years of accounts and a brazier that hadn't been lit since the winter of the great frost.

* * *

The Third Vault occupied the lowest habitable floor of the counting house, one level above the sealed gate chamber, and it was always cold down there — not the honest cold of stone and depth but a colder cold, a cold with intent, as though the gate below were drinking the warmth out of the world through the floor. Marguerite had spent half her working life in that room and had long ago stopped mentioning the chill. The other clerks thought she preferred it. Solitude suited the keeper of difficult accounts, they said. They didn't know that she stayed because the cold was a conversation, and she was the only one who could hear it.

Today the gate was restless. She felt it before she reached the bottom stair — a pulse through the soles of her boots, slow and deep, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. *Hungry*, she would have called it, if she ever let herself use that word outside her own skull. The guild's official term was *resonance*. The guild had many beautiful terms for ugly things.

The ledgers filled six shelves along the eastern wall. Forty years of entries, bound in grey calfskin, spines stamped with the guild's sigil — a door, closed, above which hung a single star. She pulled the first volume down and held it a moment longer than necessary. *Borrowed Years, Accounts 1–400.* Inside were names. That was what no one outside these walls understood: the guild didn't trade in things. It traded in people. Every bottled storm had been bought from someone who'd weathered it. Every borrowed year had been taken from a life that would now end sooner. And every entry recorded both sides — the giver and the taker — in the guild's meticulous, merciless hand.

If these books burned, the debts didn't vanish. Only the memory of them did. The givers would go on giving, hollowed out, never knowing why their lives felt shorter than they should. The takers would keep what they'd stolen, scot-free.

Marguerite set the first ledger on the copying desk instead of the brazier.

She told herself she was only going to look at it one last time. She was a very good liar, even to herself; it was practically a professional qualification.

* * *

The copying took shape the way treason usually does — gradually, then all at once.

First she merely turned pages, refreshing her memory, saying goodbye. Then she noticed something she had catalogued a hundred times without ever truly seeing: the country's name. Every ledger page bore a header — the guild's standard form — and the header began with the realm in which the transaction occurred. *In the country of ______,* the form read, and the blank had been filled in forty years ago with a word that sat strangely on the tongue, a word she had read ten thousand times and never once spoken aloud, because when she tried to speak it her mouth produced nothing. Not silence exactly. A smooth, polished absence, like a tooth socket healed over.

She had assumed for years that this was some quirk of the old script. A ligature gone wrong. Ink faded past legibility.

She fetched a magnifying lens. She held the page under the good lamp. And she saw, for the first time, that the word had not faded. It had been *struck*. A single clean line drawn through it, in ink darker than the surrounding text, in a hand she recognized — everyone in the guild recognized it — the founding master's own. And beneath the strike, in smaller letters, cramped into the margin as if the writer had been afraid even of the margins: *the Correction, year one. Let no record bear the true name. What is forgotten cannot be reclaimed.*

Her skin prickled from wrist to shoulder. Below her feet, faint but unmistakable, the gate pulsed — once, twice — like something turning over in its sleep.

Forty years ago. The Correction. Everyone knew the Correction the way everyone knows the weather: there had been a war, or something like a war, and afterward the borders had been redrawn and the histories rewritten, and the crown had declared a new age of clarity and order. Schoolchildren learned it in a single afternoon. There was nothing before the Correction worth learning.

But the ledgers said otherwise. The ledgers said there had been a *name* — a true name for the country itself — and that it had been struck not by war but by *order*, deliberately, from every record, by the guild's own founding hand. The guild hadn't just witnessed the erasure. The guild had performed it.

Marguerite stood in the cold vault with a magnifying lens in her hand and felt her whole life tilt beneath her like a floor in an earthquake's first second. Eleven years of balancing accounts. Eleven years of believing she served a house of trade, neutral as water. And underneath, all along, the guild had been the knife.

She thought of the wolf-marked smuggler, Corren Ash, and the sigil-stone he'd tried to sell her two days ago, and the mark burned into it that matched the mark that lived in the oldest corner of her memory — a memory she had never been able to source, a memory of snow and a woman singing in a language Marguerite did not know and could not forget. She had told herself it was nothing. A dream. An inheritance of somebody else's story.

Now she wondered whose memory it actually was. Now she wondered if memories could be struck too — if the Correction had swept through minds the way the founder's pen had swept through pages.

The gate pulsed again, longer this time, and the lamp flame leaned toward the floor as if bowing.

"No," Marguerite said aloud, to the gate, to the room, to forty years of lies. "You don't get to hurry me."

And then, with the calm of a woman stepping off a cliff and discovering she has wings she doesn't yet trust, she sat down at the copying desk, uncapped her pen, and began to steal.

* * *

She worked the way she did everything: precisely, swiftly, without flourish. She could not copy six shelves of ledgers — there wasn't time, and bulk would kill her faster than discovery. So she took the spine of the thing. The headers. Every instance of the struck name, from the oldest volume forward, each one photographed onto the page of her memory and then onto loose sheets hidden inside the false back of her account-case, where she kept the guild's private reconciliation figures. Page after page, she transcribed the crime in the guild's own format, using the guild's own forms, so that anyone who read her copies would see not a traitor's screed but a ledger — clean, balanced, damning. If the guild taught its clerks anything, it was that the truth kept properly is more terrifying than any lie.

Her pen scratched. The brazier waited, unlit, patient as a dog. Twice she heard footsteps on the stair and stopped, heart slamming, pen flat against the desk, and both times the footsteps passed on — clerks carrying their own armloads of condemned volumes up to the yard, where the burning was already beginning. Through the high slit window she could smell smoke by midmorning, sweet and awful, the smell of calfskin and iron-gall ink surrendering.

Somewhere above, the guild was eating its own history. And she was crouched in its belly, taking notes.

There was a moment — she would remember it all her life — when she copied the final header of the final surviving Third Vault volume and found, tucked into the binding's gutter, a loose leaf that had slipped free decades ago and been pressed flat between pages like a flower. It was older than the rest, different paper, different hand. A single line:

*The door was not always shut. The door was not always ours. Before the Correction we kept it open, and the country had a name, and the name was—*

The rest was torn away.

Marguerite held the fragment until her hands stopped shaking, which took a while. Then she copied that too, every surviving word, and slid it into the false back with the others, and felt the weight of the case change — not heavier, exactly, but *denser*, the way a secret makes a pocket hang differently than coins do.

Below her feet, the gate went quiet. Utterly, unnaturally quiet, the way a house goes quiet when the argument upstairs has stopped being shouting and become something worse.

She didn't like the quiet. She liked the hunger better. At least hunger was honest.

* * *

By late afternoon the Third Vault was empty except for ash-smell drifting down the stairwell and one last ledger in her hands — the oldest, the one she'd started with, *Borrowed Years, Accounts 1–400*. Master Ellery would come at dusk to witness the final burning. She had promised him ash to ash.

She carried it to the brazier. She knelt. She arranged the coals and lit them with a taper and watched the flame take, and she fed the ledger in page by page, because a book burns badly all at once and Ellery would want it thorough. The names curled and blackened. Four hundred givers, four hundred takers, four hundred bargains dissolved into orange light and grey flakes that rose on the vault's cold updraft like inverted snow.

She watched every page burn. She memorized faces from the entries as they went — a storm-seller with a scarred chin, a woman who sold nine years to buy her son out of a debt-bond, a man who traded his talent for music and spent the rest of his life wondering why songs made him weep. She gave them that much: a witness. The guild had taught her that records were how the dead stayed owed, and if the records must die, then let the keeping pass to flesh.

When it was done she raked the ash fine, as instructed. Her knees ached. Her hands smelled of smoke and would for days, she suspected — a brand of her own, small and private.

Treason, she thought, kneeling in the ash of the only institution she had ever loved. It feels like bookkeeping. It feels like closing an account.

It felt, she admitted, thrilling — a bright wire pulled tight through her chest, terror and exaltation wound together so finely she couldn't have separated them with the finest lens in the guild. She had broken nothing, stolen nothing visible, harmed no one. She had only *kept* what she was ordered to destroy. And yet she had never in her life been so awake. Every sound was sharper. The cold had teeth. She was, at thirty-one years of age, for the first time entirely alive and quite possibly about to die, and both facts seemed to belong to the same moment.

She was still kneeling there, savoring it the way you savor the last swallow of something you know you'll never afford again, when the stairwell changed.

Footsteps — but wrong ones. Not the shuffle of clerks, not Ellery's dry precise tread. These steps were slow, unhurried, soft-soled, spaced like a metronome. Someone descending who had never been in a hurry in her life and never would be, because the world hurried toward her, never away.

Marguerite rose, dusted ash from her skirts, and composed her face into the mild professional blankness of a senior clerk. Her account-case hung at her hip with its false back full of the country's murdered name, and it suddenly weighed as much as an anvil.

Seraphine Vale came down the stairs the way fog comes over a harbor wall.

She was smaller than rumor painted her, neat and grey-eyed, dressed in crown charcoal with the witchfinder's silver pin at her collar — a closed eye, wrought in wire. She carried no weapon that Marguerite could see, which was somehow worse. Her gaze moved across the vault — the empty shelves, the raked ash, the dying brazier — reading it the way Marguerite read ledgers: completely, instantly, missing nothing.

"Mistress Blackwood," Vale said. Her voice was gentle. That was the famous thing about Seraphine Vale, the thing the gifted told each other in whispers: the gentleness. "I'm sorry to arrive early. I do hate disrupting a household's rhythm."

"The masters expected you tomorrow," Marguerite said.

"Yes." Vale smiled, small and pleasant, and looked directly at her — at her, specifically, out of all the vault's shadows — with an interest that had no warmth in it at all. "That's rather the point of arriving today. May I ask what you're kneeling in?"

"Ash," Marguerite said. "As ordered."

"Mm," said the witchfinder, and stepped closer, and breathed in once, delicately, like a hound. "Smoke, ash, and ink." Her grey eyes lifted. "So much ink, Mistress Blackwood. Burned books rarely explain why the air smells of *fresh* ink."

Below the floor, silent as a held breath, the gate waited to see what Marguerite would say next.
