## 5. Seraphine Vale Comes Calling

The witchfinder arrived without thunder.

That was the first thing wrong with her. Corren had expected the crown's hounds to come baying — soldiers in the street, a carriage with barred windows, men with pikes and no patience. Instead, an hour past dawn on the third day of the Purge, a plain black coach drew up before the Vellum Guild's counting house, and out of it stepped a woman in dove-grey wool with a ledger of her own under one arm and a smile that never quite reached her eyes.

She was small. That was the second thing wrong with her. Corren had spent his life reading danger by its size, its noise, its appetite for attention, and Seraphine Vale confounded every instinct he owned. She was perhaps five feet nothing, silver-streaked hair pinned with what looked like ordinary bone combs, gloved to the wrist in grey kid leather. She walked into the guildhall like a guest arriving slightly early for tea.

"Good morning," she said to the doorwarden, pleasantly. "I do apologize for the hour. I know how precious mornings are to people who work with ledgers."

The doorwarden, a big man named Hask who could crush walnuts in his fist, went pale as vellum anyway.

Corren watched from the gallery above, where the apprentices kept the copying desks, and felt his skin prickle in a way that had nothing to do with cold. He'd been sleeping in the counting house since the Proclamation — Marguerite had insisted, arguing that the safest place for a marked man during a purge was inside the institution doing the purging, hidden among its own paper. He wasn't sure he believed her logic anymore. He was sure he didn't like what he was seeing now.

Because Vale did not go to the masters' wing. She did not demand documents. She did not post soldiers at the doors, though two waited outside with the bored posture of furniture. What she did instead was worse.

She began shaking hands.

* * *

Corren saw it from above: Vale moving through the ground floor of the counting house, desk to desk, greeting each clerk and apprentice by name — *and she knew their names* — with that same soft apology for intruding. And each time she took a hand, she held it a moment too long. A breath. Two. Her gloved fingers pressed against the pulse point of the wrist, and her eyes went somewhere else entirely, unfocused, listening.

By the fourth handshake, Corren understood what he was watching, and his mouth went dry.

She was reading them.

Not their papers. Not their faces. Their *skin*. Whatever gift the crown had put into Seraphine Vale, it lived in her hands, and hands were patient things. She pressed a thumb to the inside of a wrist and waited, gentle as a nurse, while whatever truth lived in the blood rose up to meet her. Corren had seen gifted readers work markets and fairs — palmists, fortune-tellers, frauds mostly. This was not that. The frauds wanted coin. Vale wanted confession, and she got it the way a spider gets flies: by making the web feel like hospitality.

He watched a junior clerk named Pell — a boy of nineteen who copied shipping manifests and had once, drunk, shown Corren a trick where he could make a candle flame lean toward him like a curious dog — shake the witchfinder's hand and come away from it grey-faced, nodding at something she murmured, walking off to his desk, sitting down, and beginning to cry silently over the manifests while she moved on to the next hand, still smiling.

Pell wasn't dragged out. That was the third thing wrong with her. Nobody screamed. Nobody was chained. The boy simply wept at his desk, and two of Vale's attendants — quiet women in grey who had come in behind her like shadows — brought him a cup of water and sat on either side of him, and that was all. It was somehow more horrifying than irons. It said: *we have time. We have all the time in the world. Everyone confesses eventually, so why rush?*

"Master Ash."

Corren turned. One of the masters' clerks stood behind him, a nervous woman with ink-stained cuffs. "Mistress Blackwood asks that you wait in the negotiation room. She says—" the clerk swallowed, "—she says the witchfinder will want to speak with everyone. Alphabetically. You're near the end. A is early, though. I'm sorry."

*Ash.* Of course. Of course he was early. The alphabet was a trap built for him specifically.

He made his way down through the stacks, past shelves of bottled weather humming faintly in their lead-lined cases, past the vault door with its seven locks, and into the negotiation room where Marguerite already waited. She stood at the long table with her hands flat on its surface, and when she looked up at him he saw she'd been crying earlier — not recently; the evidence was scrubbed away, but the lids gave it away, the rawness underneath.

"They took Pell," she said quietly. "Out the back. No fuss. His chair is still warm."

"I saw."

"He told them everything in four minutes, Corren. Four minutes. He's not even strong-gifted. He leans candles." Her voice cracked on the absurdity of it and she hated herself for it; he could see her hating herself. "What happens when she touches someone who actually—"

"Don't," Corren said.

"—when she touches *me*?" Marguerite finished anyway, because she was Marguerite, and dread for herself always came dressed as dread for others. "My hands are on every ledger in this building. My gift is in the ink. If she reads my skin, she'll read the gate. She'll read everything beneath this floor."

"Then don't let her touch you."

"With respect," Marguerite said, with a ghost of her usual dryness, "one does not decline a handshake from the crown's witchfinder. One shakes the crown's witchfinder's hand, and then one is either innocent or one is a traitor, and both of those are decided in the space of about two seconds."

Corren leaned against the wall and crossed his arms, and became very aware of the brand.

It sat under his left sleeve, high on the inner forearm where the muscle folded — the wolf-sigil, burned into him years ago by a pack elder whose name he still couldn't say without his chest tightening. To anyone else it was an old scar, a bad decision from a wild youth. To a reader like Vale, pressed fingers and patient silence, it would be a shout. Sigils weren't just marks; they were *records*. The wolf-mark carried his whole history of the gift — every time he'd used it, every time he'd suppressed it, the shape of the thing itself written into his flesh like a watermark in paper.

And today he was going to sit across a table from a woman whose entire profession was finding watermarks.

Marguerite looked at him and said, "Your arm."

"I know."

"You've thought about it?"

"I've thought about nothing else since she arrived." He rolled his sleeve down and tugged the cuff over his knuckles. "Here's the story. When I was sixteen I fell into a fire at a tannery in Marrowgate. Bad burn, left forearm. There are people in Marrowgate who'll swear to it — the tanner's daughter, half the street. Scars tell stories, Mistress Blackwood. Mine tells a boring one."

"And if she reads deeper than the scar?"

"Scars don't have depth," Corren said. "That's the trick of them. They're dead tissue. Burned nerve ends don't carry anything — no memory, no gift-signature, nothing to read. The sigil sits *under* the burn. As far as any reader can tell, the mark is damage, not design." He paused. "In theory."

"In theory," Marguerite repeated.

"In theory," Corren agreed, and neither of them smiled.

There was a knock, soft as a moth against glass, and the door opened, and Seraphine Vale came in alone.

* * *

She closed the door behind her with exaggerated care, as if the corridor were full of sleeping children.

"Forgive the interruption," she said. "I'm working through the staff in order, and I find alphabetical order soothes me. Silly habit." She set her own ledger on the table — Corren noted it was bound in the same grey leather as her gloves, noted that everything about this woman matched, coordinated, *intentional* — and sat down across from them without being asked, which was its own small violence. In the Vellum Guild, you did not sit until invited. Everyone knew that. She knew that. She sat anyway, and dared them to object.

"Mistress Blackwood. Keeper of ledgers, I'm told. First among the accountants." Her voice was warm. Genuinely warm — Corren had heard enough false warmth to know the difference, and this wasn't it. Vale seemed to actually like people, in a distant, collector-ish way, the way a lepidopterist likes butterflies. "You balance impossible accounts. I confess I've always wondered what that's like. Bottled storms must be terribly difficult to price."

"We manage," Marguerite said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, flat on the table, were not; Corren could see the tendons standing in them like bridge cables.

"And Master Ash." Vale turned the warmth on him, and Corren felt it like a lamp being angled. "A relic-broker, currently consulting for the guild. Not on the permanent rolls. Interesting. That makes you the only person in this room I haven't read a file on." She tilted her head. "Tell me about yourself, Master Ash. I find files so dry. People are better."

"There's not much to tell," Corren said. "Tannery brat from Marrowgate. Ran cargo up and down the river roads for eight years. Sold the guild a sigil-stone last month. Now the borders are closing and I'm stuck indoors with accountants, which I'm given to understand is punishment for sins committed in a former life."

"A sigil-stone," Vale repeated, and her interest sharpened so fast it was almost audible, a blade coming off a strop. "How lovely. May I ask — what sigil?"

"Wolf," Corren said.

Something happened in the room. It was very small. Vale's smile stayed exactly where it was, but her eyes changed temperature, dropping several degrees, and Corren felt the change on his skin like a draft under a door. Beside him, Marguerite went very still.

"A wolf sigil," Vale said softly. "On a stone."

"On a stone. Just the stone. Stones can't be purged — no blood in them. Lucky thing, that." He shrugged, lazy, river-rat casual, and prayed to every god whose name had survived the Correction that laziness would hold. "I found it in a wreck cache upriver. Didn't know what it was. The guild paid me for it. End of story."

"End of story," Vale echoed. She opened her ledger, wrote something — three words, maybe four; Corren counted the pen-strokes — and closed it again. Then she stood, and said, "Mistress Blackwood, I wonder if I might impose upon you for a tour. The masters have been so protective of their floors, and I confess professional curiosity. And Master Ash —" she turned to him, and here came the trap, sprung with the gentleness of everything she did, "— I'd take it as a courtesy. A handshake, before I go. I make a point of greeting everyone I interview. It seems rude otherwise, doesn't it? To talk to a man and never properly meet him."

The room went silent.

Corren heard his own heartbeat. He heard Marguerite's breathing stop. He heard, beneath the floor, faint and far and hungry, the gate pulse once — he was almost certain Marguerite heard it too, because her lips parted around a breath she didn't take.

Vale extended her right hand. Gloved. Grey kid leather, immaculate. She held it there in the air between them, patient as winter.

And Corren Ash, smuggler, liar, wolf-marked traitor of the crown's new list, reached out and shook it.

* * *

Her grip was cool through the glove. She held for one breath. Two. Three — longer than she'd held any of the clerks downstairs, and Corren understood that too: she was digging. Her thumb settled into the inside of his wrist, right over the pulse, and her eyes drifted half-shut, and for three seconds that lasted a year, Seraphine Vale read Corren Ash's skin.

He gave her nothing. That was the discipline of it — the thing the pack had drilled into him until it lived in his spine. *Don't blank your mind. Blank minds look like locked rooms, and locked rooms invite crowbars. Give her a room, but fill it with furniture she expects.* So he filled himself with Marrowgate: the tannery yard, the reek of lime and hides, the fire — he pulled the memory forward, made it loud, made it *true*, because it was true, the fire had happened, the scar was real, only its meaning was a lie. He let the burn-memory flood his arm, flood the surface of his skin, flood everything her fingers touched. Here. This. Fire, pain, a boy screaming in a tannery yard, nothing else, nothing under, nothing beneath.

Vale's eyes opened.

"Well," she said, and released his hand, and smiled at him with genuine, unguarded pleasure. "You have a remarkable pain tolerance, Master Ash. Most men flinch when I read a burn scar. You didn't flinch at all."

"Told you," Corren said. "Boring story. I've told it so many times it's worn smooth."

"Mm." She gathered her ledger. "Thank you both for your time. Mistress Blackwood — the tour. Whenever you're ready."

Marguerite rose. Corren watched her do it, watched her square her shoulders and smooth her skirts and become, visibly, deliberately, the most boring woman in the building: Keeper of Ledgers, First Among Accountants, a creature made entirely of columns and sums. She walked Vale to the door. At the threshold, Vale paused.

"One more courtesy, if I may." She turned back toward Marguerite, hand extending. "Since we're touring together. It seems rude otherwise."

Corren saw it happen. Saw Marguerite's face perform a flawless sequence — polite surprise, mild acquiescence, the small social surrender of a woman who cannot refuse — while underneath, behind her eyes, something went white with panic. Her hand came up. Slowly. Like a drowning woman reaching for a rope she knows is cut.

And Corren did the only thing he could think of.

He sneezed.

Loudly. Catastrophically. Into the space between them, doubling forward, and as he did he stumbled — genuinely stumbled, catching Marguerite's raised hand in his own and pulling it down to steady himself, so that when Vale's hand arrived there was nothing to meet it but air, and Corren was holding Marguerite's fingers in his, apologizing, mortified, sneezing again for good measure.

"How embarrassing," he managed. "River dust. Gets in everything."

"So it does," Vale said. Her gaze rested on him for a long, long moment — on the man who had just, with theatrical clumsiness, interposed himself between the witchfinder and the keeper of the guild's most dangerous ledgers — and then she smiled, warmly, and said, "Well. I shall simply have to catch you another time, Mistress Blackwood. I do hate unfinished courtesies."

She left. The coach rolled away. The counting house exhaled.

* * *

They stood in the empty negotiation room for a full minute without speaking.

Then Marguerite sat down, abruptly, all the borrowed steadiness draining out of her at once, and put her head in her hands. "She was going to read me," she whispered. "Right there. I was going to give her my hand, Corren. I felt myself do it — I felt the politeness pick my arm up like a puppet—"

"I know. I saw."

"You sneezed on the witchfinder."

"I sneezed *near* the witchfinder. There's a legal distinction."

Marguerite laughed — a single cracked note, half hysteria — and then pressed her palms harder against her eyes. "Four minutes," she said. "Pell lasted four minutes. I wouldn't have lasted four seconds. And you—" she lowered her hands and looked at him, really looked, and Corren watched her arrive at something she hadn't let herself think before. "You sat there and let her dig into your arm for three breaths, knowing what's under that scar, and you gave her a tannery fire. How?"

"Practice," Corren said. "And spite."

"It's not enough, you know." She was serious again immediately, the fear reassembling itself behind her eyes. "She wrote something down when you said *wolf*. Three words. Maybe four. She'll check the stone, Corren. She'll trace the sale. And when she comes back — and she will come back, they always come back — she won't shake hands next time. She'll bring instruments. Or worse, she'll bring nothing at all, and just *stay*."

Corren looked at the door she'd left through. Looked at Marguerite, pale and shaking and still, stubbornly, magnificent. Felt under his sleeve the wolf-sigil sitting quiet against the burn scar, patient as its namesake.

"Then we're done waiting for her to finish her list," he said. "Alphabetically, she's only up to the C's. We have days, maybe. Less, if she reads faster than she lets on."

Marguerite lifted her chin. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning tonight," Corren said, "you show me those pages you copied. All of them. Because if we're running, we run with proof — and I know these streets better than anyone born inside them ever will."

Outside the tall windows, the fog was already gathering along the river, thick and early and wrong, creeping up the cobbles of the Forgotten Quarter like something remembering its way home.

Marguerite followed his gaze, and her face went the colour of old paper.

"She left the tour unfinished," she said softly. "But she didn't leave the building empty-handed. Did you see what her attendants carried out?"

Corren hadn't. He'd been watching Vale. But Marguerite had been watching everything else, the way keepers watch, and what she said next dropped the temperature of the room ten degrees.

"The crate from the vault," she said. "Sealed with the guild's black wax. The one containing the sigil-stone I sold you — no. The one containing *everything the stone was taken from*."

She stood, and her hand found the edge of the table, and beneath them, faint and unmistakable now, the gate pulsed twice — quick, urgent, like a heartbeat answering a knock.

"She has the stone's twin," Marguerite whispered. "And tomorrow, she won't need to shake anyone's hand at all."
