## 6. Flight Through the Forgotten Quarter

*MARGUERITE*

She did not sleep. Sleep required a belief that tomorrow would arrive in an orderly fashion, and Marguerite Blackwood had spent her whole life balancing books in which tomorrow was never guaranteed — only *entered*, provisionally, in pencil.

So she sat at her desk in the counting house with a single lamp lit, and she watched the crate.

The black wax seal held. Of course it held; guild wax was warded against everything except the hand that made it. But the sigil-stone inside it — or rather, whatever remained of the stone's twin, the shard Seraphine Vale had carried away in her gloved palm like a coin she intended to spend later — that was another matter entirely. Marguerite could feel it the way she felt the gate beneath the floor: as pressure. As appetite. A faint rhythmic tugging at the base of her skull, patient as tide.

*It knows I'm here,* she thought. *The stone knows its sibling is below us, and it wants to be whole.*

At some hour past midnight, Corren Ash came down the back stairs without making a sound, which should have been impossible for a man his size. He'd refused the guest room twice and then simply stopped answering when offered it, and now he stood in the doorway of her office wearing his coat still buttoned, as if he'd never intended to stay anywhere at all.

"You're sitting in the dark with a lamp," he said.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous habit. In my experience people who think this late either run or do something stupid."

"Which are you planning?"

He almost smiled. It didn't reach anything above his mouth. "Neither. I'm leaving before dawn. The stone's sold, the coin's spent, and this house is going to come down around your ears, keeper. I've seen it before. Guilds don't get visited by witchfinders out of curiosity."

"The guild has been here two hundred years."

"And the Correction was forty years ago," he said quietly, "and nobody remembers what came before it except you, apparently, because you keep the books." He crossed to her desk and set one finger on the ledger there — not touching it, hovering, the way a man hovers near a flame he respects. "You told me tonight you found pages where the country's name had been struck through. Forty years of struck-through names. You think Vale came here to buy a stone?"

Marguerite looked up at him. In the lamplight his face was all planes and shadow, and the brand on his wrist — hidden under his cuff, but she'd seen it, the wolf-mark, identical to the one that lived half-remembered behind her eyes — seemed to radiate heat.

"Why did you really come here, Ash? Not to sell a relic. Smugglers sell relics to fences in dockside taverns. You brought it to the Vellum Guild."

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Because the mark on the stone matched something I've been carrying since I was nine years old, and I wanted to know who else wore it." His jaw tightened. "And because every fence I've ever used has forgotten me within a season. Every contact. Every friend. There's a hole in the world shaped like wherever I'm from, and the only people who might know why were sitting in this building."

Above them, faintly, a floorboard creaked.

They both went still.

Then the bell began.

Not the counting-house bell — the *city's* bell, the great cracked bronze in the Belltower of Saint Verre that rang only for proclamations. Once. Twice. A third time. And then voices, human voices, spreading outward through the pre-dawn streets like spilled water finding every crack:

*"By decree of the Crown! All persons bearing the gift, the mark, or the touch — surrender yourselves for examination! Shelter them and be counted among them!"*

Corren moved first. He always would, she was beginning to understand — he was built of reflexes the way she was built of entries. "Back door," he said. "Now. Where does the informant sleep?"

"Informant?"

"Every counting house keeps one. Someone who carries word to the watch when the ledgers smell of treason. Who is it?"

And Marguerite, with a coldness blooming under her ribs, knew exactly who. *"Tam."* The junior clerk with the soft hands and the mother in debt. She'd covered his shortfalls herself, twice, out of pity. "Third floor. East room—"

Corren was already on the stairs.

She followed, lamp abandoned, and they found Tam's door open and the room empty — window wide, curtains lifting in the night wind, a rope of knotted bedsheets still swinging gently over the alley below.

"He's gone to the watchhouse," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded very far away.

"He's gone to whoever pays best. Same thing." Corren leaned out the window, reading the dark street the way she read a ledger column. "We have minutes. Maybe less. Where do you keep the pages?"

"What pages?"

"The ones you copied. Don't insult me, keeper — you copied them. A woman who balances accounts of bottled storms doesn't burn the only proof of the biggest lie in the kingdom. She copies it first."

She stared at him, and he stared back, and somewhere below them the city bells kept ringing, and she realized she wasn't afraid of him knowing. That was the strange part. After four days of suspicion sharpened into something almost like respect, being *seen* by Corren Ash felt less like exposure than like being read aloud correctly.

"Floor safe. Under the hearthstone in Master Ellery's study. Combination is the date of the Correction backwards."

His eyebrows rose. "You put it under the master's own chair."

"Nobody guards a secret better than a man who doesn't know he's keeping it."

* * *

*CORREN*

The safe opened at the second try, which Corren considered a personal failing — he liked locks to fear him on the first — and then Marguerite was pulling out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, thin as a hymnal, and pressing it inside her coat against her heart.

"That's everything?" he asked.

"It's forty years of everything." She was pale but steady, and he noted, with the detached admiration of a man cataloguing assets, that her hands weren't shaking. "Names. Trade routes. A map of the northern provinces from thirty-nine years after the Correction, where three towns exist that no census has ever listed. Tax receipts paid by villages the crown insists are moorland." She swallowed. "Ash, there are *receipts*. People have been paying taxes to a country that says they don't live there."

"Save it for running." He caught her elbow and steered her toward the servants' stair. "Tam will have named this house. When the watch comes for a witchfinder's errand, they come with fire."

They came with more than fire.

Corren heard them first — boots, many, on the cobbles outside the front of the building, and beneath the boots a sound he hadn't heard since childhood and had hoped never to hear again: the particular wet-humming silence of a null-wagon. Iron-banded wheels, lead-lined walls, built to carry things that screamed. Or things that made other things scream.

"They brought a wagon," he said. "For one clerk's testimony, they brought a wagon."

"Maybe Tam told them something true."

"Tam told them everything true. That's what makes him valuable." He pulled her into the scullery, past hanging copper pots, toward the delivery door — and stopped so hard she walked into his back.

Through the frosted glass of the door, lantern light swung. Voices. At least six men. And threading over them, clear as a knife drawn slowly across silk, a woman's voice — calm, warm, almost kind.

"—no harm needs to come to anyone tonight. Master Ellery, please, sit down. I only want the ledgers."

Seraphine Vale. Here. *Now.*

Corren turned in the narrow space, and Marguerite was close enough that he could see her pulse jump at her throat, and for one absurd heartbeat neither of them spoke, because both of them were listening to the guild masters — the great and terrible Vellum Guild, keepers of impossible trade — fold like merchants caught with false weights.

"The vault is sealed—" Ellery began.

"Master Ellery." Gentle as snowfall. "I have a warrant signed in the King's own hand and a wagon that holds eleven gifted souls already, and it is a cold night, and I am *tired*. Open the vault."

A pause. Then the groan of iron, and Corren exhaled through his teeth.

"Guild folds faster than I thought."

"They've folded for forty years," Marguerite whispered. "This is just the first time someone asked them to do it standing up." Her hand had found the oilcloth bundle over her heart. "The back gate. Through the yard—"

"There's a wall."

"There's a door *in* the wall. The old coal gate. It hasn't been opened since—" She stopped. Her eyes went distant, unfocused, the way they did when she was feeling something the rest of them couldn't. "Since before I was born. But it's there, Ash. Underneath everything, it's still there."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning this quarter is older than the Correction, and the Correction didn't finish its work here. Come on."

* * *

*MARGUERITE*

The coal gate was a door of blackened oak behind a curtain of ivy, and it opened at her touch — not unlocked, but *remembering*, hinges that hadn't swung in sixty years moving as though oiled yesterday, and Marguerite filed that away in the part of her mind that was already screaming. Things did not open for her. Ledgers balanced for her. Doors were doors.

But the Forgotten Quarter had begun at the guild's back wall, and the Forgotten Quarter was hungry, and perhaps hunger recognized hunger.

They came out into an alley that shouldn't have existed.

She knew the counting-house district the way she knew the backs of her own hands — every street surveyed, taxed, entered. This alley appeared in none of it. Cobbles underfoot worn smooth in a pattern no modern cart had recorded, shuttered windows with carvings above them — wheat sheaves, a rising sun — in a style forty years dead. And behind them, as they ran, the alley itself changed.

She heard it before she understood it. A sound like paper being turned. And when she glanced back — against every instinct, she glanced back — the mouth of the alley was *softening*, its edges blurring like ink touched by a wet thumb, the counting-house wall dissolving into a fog that was not weather.

"Ash." Her voice came out thin. "Look back. Don't stop walking. Just look."

He looked. He said nothing for ten strides. Then: "Is it following us?"

"No." She was beginning to understand, and understanding was worse than fear. "It's *forgetting* us. The streets we've left aren't there anymore. We passed through and the country closed the page behind us."

"So the Quarter hides us."

"The Quarter *erases* us. There's a difference, and the difference is whether we find our way out the far end." She grabbed his sleeve and pulled him left, down a passage between leaning houses whose upper floors nearly met overhead. Somewhere behind, muffled by fog and distance, came the crash of the guild's front doors and shouting — Vale's people in the building, Vale's voice cutting through it all, still terribly gentle. "Left again. There's a chandler's yard—"

"There's no chandler's yard. I cased this district yesterday."

"You cased the district that exists *now*." They burst through a gap between houses onto a lane, and there it was: a chandler's yard, stacks of tallow crates, a cart with one wheel missing, exactly where a map from thirty-eight years ago said it would be. Corren stared at it as if it had slapped him.

"That wasn't there."

"It was always there. It got corrected away. The Quarter keeps the old country underneath, like writing under writing." She was breathing hard now, the oilcloth bundle slick with sweat against her chest. "My whole life I've felt the gate pull beneath the counting house, and everyone said it was the vault, it was the wards, it was my imagination. But it isn't the vault, Ash. The vault sits on top of something. The *whole city* sits on top of something. And right now, out here where the Correction is thinnest, the thing underneath is waking up because we're running across it like feet on a sleeper."

Ahead, the lane forked. To the right, lamplight and the sounds of the watch — the normal city, the corrected city, hunting them. To the left, fog. Not natural fog; it lay in a perfect wall, waist-high and absolute, glowing faintly at its edges like a page held up to a candle.

Corren looked at the lamplight. Then at the fog. Then at her.

"I know which one you want," he said.

"You know nothing about me."

"I know you copy forbidden pages and hide them under your master's chair and talk to locked buildings like they're old friends." Something wild had come into his face, and for the first time she saw the wolf in it plainly, unhidden, and it did not frighten her, and that frightened her more than anything else that night. "Left or right, keeper."

Behind them, at the mouth of the lane, lanterns. A voice — Vale's voice, carrying impossibly through the fog, unhurried and warm as a hand on the shoulder:

"Miss Blackwood. Your colleagues were so helpful. Do come and tell me what you took."

Marguerite took Corren Ash's hand.

It was not a decision she made; it was simply what happened, the way entries happen, ink meeting line. His grip closed around hers — calloused, hot, steady — and together they stepped off the edge of the corrected world and into the fog.

The world went white. Then it went *wrong*. Sound stretched; the shouts behind them smeared into vowels with no words attached. She felt the fog move through her like a hand through water, searching, tasting, looking for her name to take — and she did the only thing a keeper could do. She held her name in her mind like a coin in a fist, and she *did not let go*.

Then grass underfoot. Cold air. Stars — real stars, arranged in constellations she half-knew from maps forty years old, constellations that no longer hung over any city she'd lived in.

They stumbled out onto a hillside dotted with ruins — foundations, a well, a boundary-stone carved with letters worn to ghosts. Behind them, the fog wall stood in the starlight, silent, patient, sealing the way back.

Corren dropped to one knee, breathing like a bellows. She sat down beside him because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

"We're outside the city," he managed at last.

"We're outside the *country*," she said, and heard the truth of it as she spoke. She pointed at the boundary-stone. He crawled to it, brushed the moss away, and went very still.

The carving was legible, barely. A name. The name of a province that appeared in no living record — except in her copied pages, struck through forty years ago, in the same ink, in the same hand, over and over and over.

"That's the name," Corren said quietly. "From your ledger pages. The one that got erased." He looked up at her, and the starlight made him look younger and more dangerous at once. "Keeper. What exactly did you steal from that guild?"

Marguerite pulled the oilcloth bundle from her coat. Her fingers were finally shaking now — delayed, obedient fear arriving on its own schedule. She unwrapped the top page and held it flat between them, and in the fog-light her copied map lay open like a wound: roads running north into blankness, towns marked in her own careful hand beyond the border, and at the center of the blankness, circled in red ink by whoever had annotated the original, a single notation she had not understood until this moment.

*A door was here. Find what it became.*

"Well," she said. "First we find out what a door becomes after forty years."

Behind them, deep in the fog, something howled — long, low, and unmistakably answering the brand on Corren's wrist.
