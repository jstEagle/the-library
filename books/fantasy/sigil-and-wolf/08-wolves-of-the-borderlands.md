## 8. Wolves of the Borderlands

The fog began three miles before the border did.

I felt it first in my teeth — that old ache I'd carried since I was nine years old and ran from a burning waystation with nothing but a stolen coat and a brand on my ribs. The border fog wasn't weather. It was scar tissue. The land's own skin trying to knit over a wound that had never been allowed to close, and every gifted thing within fifty miles could feel the stitching pull when the wind shifted wrong.

Marguerite felt it too. I watched her feel it.

She'd been quiet since the den beneath the tannery, since we'd spread her copied pages across a smuggler's table and watched roads eat themselves off maps drawn after the Correction. Quiet in the way a drawn bow is quiet. All morning she'd walked half a step behind me through the pine country, ledger pages wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart, and she hadn't asked where we were going until the trees started to thin and the light went strange — silvered, diffuse, as though the sun couldn't quite remember how to strike this ground directly.

"Corren." Not a question. A warning shot. "What is this place?"

"The thinnest part of the world," I said. "Or the thickest, depending on how you look at it. The erasure doesn't hold here. The Correction was done in the capital, in the guild's own chamber, and its power fades with distance — like a shout fading down a valley. Out here, at the far edge of what they rewrote, the old country leaks through."

"Leaks through how?"

"You'll see." I ducked a low branch heavy with mist-beads. "Keep your hands visible when we get there. And whatever anyone says to you — whatever name they use — don't correct them."

She stopped walking. "What name?"

I should have told her then. Some part of me knew it, the same part that had known since the counting house, since the moment I'd turned that sigil-stone over in my palm and seen the wolf mark and heard her breath catch across the table. But telling her would mean explaining things I didn't fully understand myself, and there's a particular cowardice in waiting for someone else to say the hard thing first.

"You'll see," I said again, and hated myself for it, and kept walking.

* * *

The pack's camp sat in a hollow where the fog pooled deepest, invisible from any ridge unless you knew to look for the absence of things — no birdsong, no deer trails, no frost on the bracken even in the coldest weeks, because something below kept the ground warm. I'd found it at fourteen, half-dead, following a sound I still can't describe except that it was like a voice calling a name I hadn't been given yet.

They had a sentry posted at the hollow's lip: a girl of maybe sixteen named Wren who could taste lies on the air the way other people tasted rain coming. She stepped out of the mist with a short spear held loose and easy, and her eyes went straight past me to Marguerite, and stayed there.

"You brought a stranger," Wren said.

"I brought family," I said, and watched Marguerite flinch at the word.

Wren's nostrils flared. Her gift worked close-range, skin to air, and I saw her reading Marguerite the way Seraphine Vale read people — except Wren read with hunger instead of cruelty, the hunger of someone starved for proof that the world outside still made people like us. "Ledger-keeper," she said slowly. "Ink under the nails. Paper smell. And underneath—" She frowned, tilted her head like a bird. "Underneath something old. Something crossed."

"Crossed?" Marguerite's voice came out thin.

"Over the line," Wren said, as if it were obvious. "Before or after?"

"After," I said quietly. "I think after. She wouldn't know."

Wren looked at me a long moment, then at Marguerite, and whatever she tasted in the air between us made her lower the spear. "Halla will want to see this," was all she said, and turned into the fog, and we followed.

The camp unfolded out of the mist the way it always did — gradually, reluctantly, as if the fog were deciding whether to permit it. Tents of waxed canvas and patched sailcloth. A cookfire whose smoke rose straight up and then bent sideways all at once, as if obeying a different sky. Racks of drying fish, a forge no bigger than a barrel, children — actual children, two of them, chasing each other between the tents with wooden swords, laughing, unafraid. In forty years the crown had never found this place. In forty years the crown had never once thought to look, because the Correction had written the borderlands out of its own attention. You cannot hunt what your map has forgotten.

Marguerite stood at the edge of it with an expression I'd only seen once before, the night she'd shown me the page where the country's true name had been struck through in four different inks by four different hands across four decades. It was the look of a woman watching a door open onto a room she'd dreamed about without knowing she dreamed.

"They're just — living," she said. "Out here. Openly."

"We're not openly anything. We're forgotten, which is better than open." I touched her elbow, gentle, guiding. "Come on. Halla's by the fire."

* * *

Halla Voss had been old when I joined the pack and seemed determined to be old forever, as a matter of principle. She was a small woman, broad-handed, with white hair cropped close to the skull and eyes the pale grey of river ice. Her gift was memory — not reading it, like Vale, but holding it. She remembered everything she'd ever heard or seen with perfect fidelity, which meant she remembered a country that officially had never existed, and she'd spent thirty years making sure the rest of us carried pieces of it too, so that no single death could take it all.

She was mending a net when we came up to the fire, and she didn't look up right away. That was Halla. She let you stand in her silence until you'd told her everything your posture knew.

"You're thinner," she said finally, to me. "And you're running from something with teeth. Both of you."

"The witchfinder found the camp's location once already," I said. "Not this one. The eastern one. They burned it in the spring. I—" I swallowed. "I should have come sooner. I'm sorry."

"You came when you came." She set down the net. "Sit. Both of you. Maren, broth."

A woman at the next fire moved to obey, and Marguerite sat stiffly on a log beside me, spine straight as a ledger's binding, hands folded in her lap like a woman in a magistrate's office. Around us the camp had gone quiet in that particular way — not hostile, but attentive. Curious. I counted faces: maybe thirty, all ages. A man with burn-scarred forearms whose gift was mending broken metal. Twin boys who shared dreams. An ancient fellow called Doss who could make himself unnoticeable, who was sitting in plain view precisely because he enjoyed the irony.

Halla took Marguerite's hands before the broth arrived. Turned them palm-up. Studied the ink stains, the callus along the middle finger from years of pens, and then — gently, with a thumb — pushed back the sleeve of her left wrist.

There, on the soft inner skin, faint as a watermark: the wolf mark. The same sigil I'd carried branded into my ribs since childhood, the same one cut into the stone Corren Ash had tried to sell at the counting house on the worst and best day of my life. I'd seen hers only once, briefly, by candlelight, and neither of us had spoken of it since. Some recognitions are too large for conversation.

Halla went very still.

"Halla?" I said.

She wasn't listening to me. She was looking at Marguerite the way a scholar looks at a page she's spent decades trying to find — and then her face changed, and it broke my heart a little, because the scholar became something older and sadder. She became a woman greeting a ghost.

"Saoirse," Halla said.

The word dropped into the fire-silence like a stone into a well.

Marguerite blinked. "I'm sorry — what?"

"Saoirse." Halla said it again, slower, tasting each syllable, and I heard now that it wasn't in any language I knew. It had a shape to it, a music — the accent of somewhere east of everywhere, the accent of a country that appeared on none of the crown's maps because the crown had eaten it. "That is your name. Your true name. Saoirse Blackwood — no." Her thumb moved over the wolf mark, and her voice roughened. "Not Blackwood. That name was given later, at the crossing, a toll-name, a shell. Before that you were Saoirse of the Vale of Aeron. Daughter of Eamon and Bríd. Born under the third moon of harvest in the year the old count called the Year of Two Autumns."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind us a tent flap snapped in wind that hadn't existed a moment before.

"That's not possible," Marguerite said. Her voice was perfectly level, which is how I knew she was drowning. "My name is Marguerite. Marguerite Blackwood. I have ledgers with my signature going back eleven years. I have a mother's ring with a B engraved inside. I was born in Carrowmere, I—"

"In Carrowmere," Halla agreed softly. "That is what was written. That is what everyone was made to remember, including you. But the mark doesn't lie, child. The mark is older than the Correction. The wolf sigil isn't a guild sign and it isn't a smuggler's brand — it's a road-mark. It's what the gatekeepers of Aeron wore, the ones who tended the door between what is and what was. Your family kept the door for nine generations." She released Marguerite's wrists, slowly, as if letting go cost her something. "When the Correction came, the keepers were the first taken. Most died. Some fled west, over the line, into the country that would become yours. And the crossing itself — the passage through the rewritten border — demanded payment. Memory. The toll of forgetting. Every family that crossed paid it. Yours paid heaviest, because yours had the most to lose."

Marguerite wasn't breathing properly. I put my hand on her back and felt the tremor running through her like current through wire.

"My gift," she said. "Feeling the gate. The hunger. That's—"

"A scar," Halla said. "The toll took your memories and left the wound that remembers for you. You've spent your whole life keeping a door you don't remember inheriting. Serving the very institution that erased the country your family died carrying out of the fire." She paused. "I'm sorry. There's no kind way to say it."

The broth arrived and nobody touched it.

I watched Marguerite's face do several things in succession — denial assembling itself, crumbling, reassembling in a new configuration, crumbling again — and underneath all of it, terrifyingly, recognition. Because she'd felt it. I'd seen her feel it from the first night: the way the sigil-stone had called to her across a counting-house table, the way the gate beneath the guild spoke to her like kin. She had spent years believing herself a servant of the Vellum Guild who happened to be cursed with sensitivity. Now a stranger with ice-grey eyes was telling her she was the heir to everything the guild had buried.

"How do you know my parents' names?" she whispered.

"Because your father came through here." Halla's voice gentled further, if such a thing were possible. "Twenty-nine years ago. With a babe in his arms and burns on his hands and no memory of why he was crying. He stayed with us one night. He couldn't stay longer — the forgetting pulled at him, drew him onward toward the life that had been prepared for him, the false one, the comfortable one. But before he went, he asked me to hold something for him. He said, 'Someone should remember my daughter's name, even if I can't.' So I have. Thirty years, child. I have said Saoirse aloud every morning of thirty years so that somewhere in the world, one person would know it." She reached into the collar of her shirt and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small carved bead, worn smooth. "He gave me this. Said it was hers. Said it was cut from the doorpost of the keeper's house in Aeron."

Marguerite took the bead in both hands like it was a live coal. And then she did something I will carry to my grave: she laughed. One broken, disbelieving note, half sob. "It's a button," she said. "It looks like a button. My whole life, my entire self, and it comes down to a button."

"It comes down to a name," Halla corrected. "Buttons are just how names travel when countries burn."

Around the fire, the pack had begun, quietly, to do something I hadn't seen them do for strangers in all my years. Maren had stopped pretending to cook. Doss had let himself be noticeable. The twin boys had crept close to stare at the woman with the wolf mark, and their mother hadn't called them back. Nobody said welcome. They didn't need to. Belonging, in the border fog, was offered the way water is offered to a traveler — silently, already poured, set down within reach.

Marguerite looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and grateful all at once, and she mouthed one word: *why*. Why hadn't I told her. Why had I let a stranger hand her her own name.

Because I didn't know, I wanted to say. Because I only ever knew the mark, not the meaning. But that wasn't entirely true, and her eyes said she knew it wasn't entirely true, and I looked down at the fire like a coward.

"Halla," I said, to break it. "The guild masters are hunting her. The witchfinder too. And the copied pages show the erasure accelerating — villages going to fog, whole parishes unwriting. We need to know about the original door. The gate before the guild built their counting house over it. Everything you know about Aeron. Everything about the Correction."

Something moved through the camp then — not wind, exactly. The fog at the hollow's rim thickened, curled inward, and for a heartbeat every head turned toward the treeline, every gifted nerve in the camp pricking at once. I felt it too, deep in the brand on my ribs: a pulse, distant and vast, like a great heart turning over in its sleep beneath the earth to the east. Toward the capital. Toward the gate.

Toward her.

Halla rose slowly, net forgotten, and her pale eyes had gone sharp as winter.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tonight you eat, and sleep, and let the dead rest. Tomorrow I'll tell you about the door." She looked east, at the pulse we all could feel, and her jaw tightened. "But hear this now, Saoirse of Aeron, because the fog brought it on the wind and the fog does not lie: the gate knows you've come home. And it has begun to call the others."

"What others?" Marguerite asked.

Halla turned back to her, and for the first time since I'd known her — through fires and winters and the loss of the eastern camp — Halla Voss looked afraid.

"Everyone the Correction erased," she said. "And child, they are *many*."
