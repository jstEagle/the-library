## 9. The Name I Don't Remember

The fog did not lift with the morning. It simply grew thinner, the way a held breath grows shallow — still there, still pressing, but permitting the world to exist inside it.

I woke on a bed of pine boughs under a shelter of stretched hides, and for three full seconds I did not know where I was. Then memory arrived in its usual disorder: the burning ledgers, Seraphine Vale's soft voice, Corren's hand around my wrist as we ran through streets that forgot themselves behind us. The camp. The wolves.

The woman called Sera-who-was-not-Sera sat at the edge of the shelter, mending a net that had no business being a net. It was woven from something pale and fibrous that moved when she wasn't looking at it, and I decided, in the interest of my own sanity, not to ask.

"You slept," she said. It wasn't a question or a greeting. It was an observation, delivered like an accountant's note.

"Barely."

"Good. Barely is how we sleep here. Fully is how we die." She bit a thread free and squinted at me through the grey light. "The elder wants you. When your legs work."

"My legs work."

"They'll need to." She rose, tucked the impossible net under one arm, and left me alone with the fog and the sound of the camp waking: low voices, a kettle, somewhere a dog that barked once and thought better of it.

* * *

The elder's shelter stood at the center of the camp, larger than the others, built from timber so old it had gone silver. Inside, the walls were hung with objects that made my ledger-trained eye ache: a clock with no hands, a mirror that showed the room from above, a jar of what I sincerely hoped was honey. Things traded across the border of sense. Things like the ones I had spent nine years recording in the Vellum Guild's books, except these were kept out of love rather than profit.

The elder's name was Maren. She was small and brown as a walnut, with hands knotted like tree roots and eyes the color of tea left too long. She sat before a low fire and did not look up when I entered.

"Sit," she said. "You stand like someone waiting for permission to leave. You've been doing it your whole life. Sit anyway."

I sat. Across the fire, Corren leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who has delivered a package and wants confirmation of receipt. Sera settled beside him.

Maren turned her palms toward the fire. "Show me your wrist."

My wrist. The left one. Where, for as long as I could remember, there had been a birthmark — a small, dark whorl of pigment shaped like nothing in particular, which my mother had called a beauty mark when I was young and which I had privately, superstitiously, always thought looked like a spiral drawn by someone trying to draw a circle and failing at the last moment.

I pushed back my sleeve. In the firelight, the mark looked darker than usual. Almost fresh.

Maren looked at it the way I looked at a ledger entry that didn't balance: long, patient, and increasingly grim.

"Corren," she said. "Where did you find her?"

"The counting house. She's the guild's ledger-keeper."

"I asked where you found her. Not where you stole her from."

He had the grace to look uncomfortable. "At the gate road. She was reading the fog."

"She was *listening* to the fog," Maren corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." She lifted her gaze to mine at last. "Girl. What do they call you?"

"Marguerite Blackwood."

"And who gave you that name?"

"The foundling registry of the Vellum Quarter. I was left at the guild's door as an infant. They named me themselves." I had said this sentence a hundred times in my life, in a hundred bureaucratic contexts, and it had never once hurt until now, in this silver-walled room, under this woman's tea-colored gaze. "It's a common enough practice. Orphans get guild names. Ledger names. Something tidy to file us under."

"Tidy," Maren repeated softly. "Yes. That's exactly what it is. A filing." She reached into the folds of her shawl and produced something wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapping it with the care of a surgeon. Inside lay a stone — no, a sigil-stone, like the one Corren had tried to sell, but older, cracked down one side, and carved with a mark I knew.

A wolf. Running, head thrown forward, tail streaming — and beneath its paws, a border line, and beyond the border, a second mark. A spiral. Drawn by someone trying to make a circle and failing at the last moment.

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the dog barked again and was silenced.

"That's—" My voice came out wrong. I started again. "That's my mark. On the stone. How do you have my mark on a stone?"

"It isn't yours," Maren said gently. "Yours is the stone's. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." She set it in my hands. It was warm. Sigil-stones aren't warm; I had handled dozens in the guild vaults, cataloguing them, and they were always cold as riverbed pebbles. This one was warm as a sleeping animal. "Forty years ago, this belonged to a family. A mother, a father, two daughters. They lived in a country that no longer appears on any map, because it no longer appears in anything else either. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," I said. And then, because lying to this woman felt like lying to gravity: "Yes. I think I'm starting to."

* * *

What follows, I will write down as I received it, because writing is the only way I know how to hold a thing without it slipping.

Maren told it plainly, the way you tell someone a diagnosis. There was no performance in it, no drama, and that was somehow worse than drama would have been.

Before the Correction, there had been another country east of ours. It had a name — she said the name, and the name went into my ears and slid out again like water off oilcloth; I heard it, I swear I heard it, and I cannot keep it — and it had borders, and cities, and forty thousand years of graves. The throne wanted it. Not its land; land can be taken honestly. The throne wanted its *absence*, because the country stood between the crown and the gate.

The gate. Our gate, the one beneath the counting house, the one I had felt pulsing under my ledgers for nine years like a heart under a floorboard. It had not always been sealed. It had been a door between what is and what was — between the world and everything erased from it — and the first guildmasters had learned to open it just wide enough to trade. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Names, when names were scarce.

But a door that opens is a door that hungers both ways, and the other country sat closest to it, and the throne feared what might come through. So forty years ago, the guild and the crown together performed the Correction. They did not conquer the neighboring country. They rewrote existence so that it had never been. Its people became citizens of our country with histories grafted onto them. Its roads became our roads, bending where they had never bent. Its name was struck from every record, every map, every mind.

Almost every mind.

"The crossing has a toll," Maren said. "Existence doesn't rewrite cheaply. Everyone who came out of that country paid it. They forgot their homeland, their language, their dead. Some forgot more." Her eyes went to my wrist. "Some paid in different coin. The gate marked those who crossed nearest to it, or heaviest, or youngest. It took a piece of them and left the mark in exchange. A scar of the toll. Your family crossed with two daughters. One was four years old."

The sigil-stone was warm in my hands. The spiral mark on my wrist seemed to pulse with it, in time, I realized with a lurch, with the faint deep rhythm I had felt beneath the counting house every day of my working life. The gate's pulse. I had thought I was feeling it through the floor.

I had been feeling it through myself.

"The elder daughter," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "What happened to her?"

"You're looking at her granddaughter's story and asking about her own," Maren said. "The elder daughter remembered. Children remember what adults are made to forget — the toll takes the deepest memories first, and a four-year-old hasn't grown deep roots yet. She grew up remembering a country no one else had ever heard of. She drew maps of it. She sang songs in a language no scholar could place. Her parents, good corrected citizens, had her examined." Maren's mouth twisted around the word. "The guild took an interest. The guild often does. They saw a girl marked by the gate, and they saw use in her, and they took her in, gave her a tidy name, and set her over the ledgers. Because there is one thing a gate-marked keeper can do that no ordinary clerk can, girl."

"Feel it," I whispered. "Know its hunger."

"Be its lock," Maren said. "A seal needs to be living. A gate that must never open fully needs a keeper whose own nature holds it shut. They didn't give you a job, child. They gave you a shape. You've spent your life being the cork in their bottle, and calling it employment."

The fire crackled. Corren hadn't moved from the doorframe, but his arms had come uncrossed, his hands hanging loose, and he was watching me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"And my parents?" I asked. "My sister?"

Maren spread her root-knuckled hands. "Corrected. Living, most likely, within thirty miles of where they've always lived, with names that fit our tongue and memories of a childhood in our country that never happened. They don't miss you. Grief requires a before, and their before was issued by the crown." She paused. "Your sister may dream strangely. The marked run in bloodlines. But she won't know why."

I should have cried. I understood that, distantly, the way you understand weather happening to other people. Instead I felt the strangest sensation: as though a page were being torn out of a book I'd read my whole life and finding, underneath, not blank paper but *older writing* — a text that had always been there, pressed flat beneath mine.

Marguerite Blackwood. Foundling. Guild ledger-keeper. Nine years of meticulous columns, of bottled storms and borrowed years, of loving my work, of feeling the gate watch me back.

And beneath it: a name I couldn't hold. A country I'd never seen. A sister who dreamed strangely. A mother who had carried a four-year-old through fog while existence closed over their heads like water.

"Say it again," I said. "My name. My real one."

Maren said it. It went into my ears and slid out again. I caught the shape of it — two syllables? three? — and lost it before it reached my tongue.

"That's the cruelest part of the toll," Sera said quietly, from the doorframe. "You can be told. You can't be given."

* * *

I walked out of the shelter without deciding to. My legs worked; Sera had promised they would, and she'd been right about everything else.

The fog had thinned to lace. Through it, I could see the borderlands proper — hills that sloped in directions geometry disapproved of, trees that cast shadows slightly out of step with their branches. Erasure-thinned land. The seams of the Correction showing through, like a badly darned sock.

Corren found me there. Of course he did. He moved quietly for a big man, but I was beginning to learn his particular silence — the wolf-silence, deliberate, respectful of whatever he was approaching.

"You knew," I said. Not turning around. "When you brought the sigil-stone to the counting house. The wolf mark. You recognized mine."

"I recognized *something*," he said carefully. "A trader learns to spot marks. Yours matched the stones from the far side. I figured you were crossed-over stock, same as half the gifted in the capital. I didn't know the rest." A pause. "I wouldn't have sold it to you if I'd known the rest."

"You'd have sold it to someone."

"Yeah." No apology in it. I found, unexpectedly, that I preferred that. "That's the trade."

I looked down at the sigil-stone in my hand, warm as a heartbeat. "They filed me," I said. "Nine years. I balanced their accounts. I loved the work, Corren. I loved the order of it. And the entire time, I was — I was the thing being accounted. A line item. *One keeper, gate-marked, maintenance of seal.*" My voice cracked on the last word, and I hated it, and let it crack anyway. "I don't even get to grieve properly. You can't mourn a self you never got to keep. There's no body. There's no grave. There's just a hole shaped like a girl who had a real name, and I'm standing next to the hole, filling in for her."

Corren was quiet for a long moment. Fog drifted between us.

"When I was fourteen," he said, "the pack found me in a river town with no memory of how I'd got there. First change had burned it out of me — some gifts eat the mind before they settle. I spent two years angry about the boy I'd been before. Wanted him back. Went looking." He shrugged, the motion heavy. "You know what I found? Nothing. No records. No family. Just the mark on my arm and everyone else's certainty about who I ought to be. And eventually Maren told me something I've never forgotten."

"What?"

"She said: *you can't live in the house you lost. But you can build on its foundation.*" He nodded at the stone in my hand. "That's not nothing, Marguerite. That's not a filing. Whatever name they struck — the mark survived it. Marks are what the erasure can't reach. That's why the crown hunts the gifted, if you think about it. We're the evidence."

The words settled into me like stones into a streambed, changing the current's course without stopping it.

Evidence. I was evidence. Forty years of the crown's great lie, walking around in a guild-issue name, keeping their gate shut, and I had thanked them for the privilege.

"Maren says the marked run in bloodlines," I said slowly. "She said my sister might dream strangely."

"She said a lot of things."

"She also said the toll takes the deepest memories first." I turned to face him fully, and I felt something kindle in my chest that I recognized, with a jolt, as purpose — the same clean burn I used to feel when a ledger finally balanced, transposed into a new key. "Which means somewhere out there is a woman who dreams of a country that doesn't exist, and sings songs no one can place, and doesn't know why. And if the Correction is accelerating — if the fog is eating villages the way we watched it eat the Forgotten Quarter — then she's standing closer to the seam than anyone realizes."

Corren studied me. "That's a long way to go to find a stranger who shares your scar."

"She's not a stranger." The word came out of me with total certainty, arriving from somewhere deeper than memory. "She's the only person alive who was in the house I lost."

Behind us, from the direction of the elder's shelter, Maren's voice rang out across the camp — sharp, urgent, all the walnut warmth gone from it:

"Wolves! Eyes east! Riders on the fog-line — and they're flying crown colors!"

The sigil-stone burned hot in my palm, and beneath my feet, thin as a thread through the borderland soil, I felt the gate begin to pull.
