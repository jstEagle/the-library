## 10. Vale's Bargain

Seraphine Vale had learned long ago that the truth of a person lived in their hands.

Faces lied. Eyes lied, especially to someone like her, who could peel a lie open like fruit. But hands always told the truth. The way they curled when they thought no one watched. The calluses they carried and the ones they'd lost. She had interviewed two hundred suspected gifted in her eleven years as witchfinder, and she had never once looked at anyone's eyes.

So it was Corren Ash's hands she studied now, across the scarred table of the Waystation at Grayhollow — a crown post so far from the capital that the soldiers stationed there had stopped saluting and started slouching. His hands were flat on the wood, deliberate, the way a man lays down cards he has already counted. Burn-scarred across the right knuckles. A missing half-moon from his left thumb, old, healed wrong. And beneath all of it, hidden by nothing but habit and a rolled-down cuff, the edge of something that made her skin prickle like a struck match.

"You're not going to offer me tea," Corren said. "I'm almost insulted."

"I was going to." Seraphine folded her hands on the table between them. "Then I remembered you'd think it was poisoned."

"Would it be?"

"No. Poison is inefficient." She smiled, because smiling was part of the work. "You've been difficult to find, Master Ash. Or should I say Ash-Corren? The records disagree with themselves about you. It's remarkable. Most men are consistent liars. You appear to be a consistent absence."

"I get that a lot."

"I imagine you do." She let the silence stretch, because silence was also part of the work. Outside the waystation's one window, the fog sat against the glass like something waiting to be let in. They were deep in the borderlands now, deeper than any honest traveler came, and the fog here had a texture she recognized from her worst reports. It moved when it shouldn't. It remembered when it shouldn't.

Three days ago, a courier had reached her at the burned counting house in Vellum Row with news: the Blackwood woman had fled east with the smuggler. Two days ago, a farmer had seen them crossing the mill fields at Hollow Ford, moving toward the border country. One day ago, Seraphine had found the farm family still sitting at their breakfast table, unharmed but weeping, unable to explain why they were crying or why they suddenly couldn't recall their daughter's name — only that they'd had one, surely, and that there was an empty chair.

The Correction was accelerating. She had known it would. She had been told, once, by someone whose face she could no longer summon, that it would accelerate if the gate went untended. She had written this down. She had then been unable to read what she wrote without her eyes watering, which she attributed to fatigue.

Her memories were very tidy. That was the thing no one ever noticed about her: too tidy. Eleven years of service and not one contradiction, not one loose thread, not one dream she couldn't account for. Other people's minds were attics full of broken furniture. Hers was a showroom.

She had learned, recently, to be afraid of showrooms.

* * *

Corren Ash shifted, and his left hand drifted — just slightly — toward his cuff.

"Don't," Seraphine said pleasantly.

He froze.

"There's no need for theater between us. I know what you carry. I've known since Vellum Row." She leaned back. "The wolf-mark. Old sigilry, older than the guild's licensing, older than the crown's registry. Do you know where sigils come from, Master Ash? Truly?"

"I know they get people hanged."

"They get people *taken*. Hanged is what happens after, when the examination goes poorly." She tilted her head. "And the examinations go poorly more often than the proclamations mention. You'd be surprised — or perhaps not — how many of the gifted simply... stop. Mid-examination. Not dead. Nothing so useful as dead. Just stopped, like clocks. We ship them to the capital in wagons with straw in the bottom, and I have never once been permitted to see where the wagons go."

Something flickered through him — quick, banked, like a coal turned over. He was good at hiding it. He wasn't good enough. His hands had gone from flat to curled.

"You don't approve," he said.

"I don't *understand*, which is worse." She heard her own voice flatten and did not correct it. This, too, was rare — Seraphine Vale performed even her sincerity, and here she was, spending real coin. "I am the crown's best instrument. I say that without vanity; instruments don't have vanity. And yet every order I receive about the gifted comes sealed twice, and every answer I send is answered with silence, and every wagon goes into the citadel's undercrofts and does not come out. I have spent eleven years being very good at my work and increasingly unable to say what my work is *for*."

"And that troubles you."

"It should trouble you that it troubles me," she said. "Because I am the most dangerous person you will ever meet, and I am asking questions instead of writing your warrant. That window is not large."

* * *

She laid the papers on the table then, and watched his hands while he read.

The first page was the warrant itself — his name, the wolf-mark described in careful bureaucratic prose, the word TRAITOR rendered in the heavy stamp reserved for crimes against the continuity of the realm. The second page was different. Cleaner stock. A single line of the king's own cipher, which she had broken four years ago out of professional thoroughness and never reported, because reporting it would have meant admitting she'd broken it.

Amnesty, the second page read. Full pardon. Restoration of registry status. In exchange: the Blackwood woman, delivered alive, to the gate chamber beneath the Vellum counting house, before the next dark of the moon.

"The dark of the moon," Corren read aloud. "That's nine days."

"Eight, now."

"And what happens to her at the gate chamber?"

Seraphine held his gaze. It cost her something to hold anyone's gaze — not effort, exactly, but exposure. When she looked into eyes, she felt everything underneath them, all at once, undifferentiated, like drowning in mail. She did it anyway. She watched him decide whether to lie to her, and watched him decide not to bother.

"She becomes keeper," Seraphine said. "Permanently."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've been given." She tapped the cipher page. "This came to me three weeks ago, before the proclamation, before any of it. Before I knew Marguerite Blackwood existed outside a ledger signature. Someone above me decided, three weeks ago, that a guild clerk would need to be bound to a gate. Ask yourself what that means about how much of the last fortnight was improvisation."

His jaw worked. "You're saying the purge was staged."

"I'm saying the purge was *scheduled*." She let that land. "The proclamation names the gifted enemies of the realm. Fine. I have taken gifted children from their mothers' kitchens and called it protection, and I believed it, because believing it was the only way to do the work. But I have also seen the ledgers of the wagons. The numbers don't reconcile. Forty-one taken from the eastern districts last spring. Thirty-eight recorded arriving. Three unaccounted. The year before, six. The year before that, eleven." Her finger traced the column she kept in her memory, because she dared not keep it on paper. "Someone is eating the difference, Master Ash. And whatever eats them lives under a counting house in the capital, and it is hungry, and the crown has decided that feeding it is a matter of state."

The fog pressed harder against the window. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the hour in a voice that cracked on the second syllable, and Seraphine noted distantly that the sentry had called the same hour twice tonight, and that no one else had noticed.

Corren was quiet a long time. His hands had gone still again — the terrible stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want finished.

"If I bring her in," he said slowly, "she's bound to that thing forever."

"If you don't, she'll be brought in anyway. By me, likely. I'm very good." Seraphine spread her hands — honestly, she hoped. "I'm offering you the only mercy available in this arrangement: that she walks in beside someone who chose her company, rather than dragged behind someone who hunted her. Amnesty, Master Ash. Your mark registered, your pack left alone, your name returned to the records that currently can't agree you exist. All of it, for one delivery."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take you both, and the delivery happens regardless, and your pack learns what a registered sigil is worth to a crown that's stopped pretending." She paused. "But you already knew I'd say that. You're not weighing mercy. You're weighing whether I mean it."

"Are you testing me?"

"Everything is a test. That's not cynicism, it's architecture." She rose, gathered the warrant but left the cipher page on the table — deliberately, watching his eyes drop to it, watch it stay within reach of his hand. "Take the night. Take the morning. The border fog is thickening — you'll have noticed the roads forgetting where they go. In eight days it won't matter what either of us chooses, because the Correction will finish what it started, and everything inside this border will be as tidy as my memory."

At the door she stopped, her hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around, because some truths were easier addressed to wood than to wolves.

"For what it's worth — and it is worth very little coming from me — I hope you refuse. I have never once hoped a suspect would run." She heard herself and marveled at it. "Eleven years, and this is the first time I've given a fugitive a head start. I'd examine myself, if I still trusted examiners."

She walked out into the fog, and it closed around her like a coat, and she thought: *I know this cold. I have known this cold before. Why do I know this cold?*

* * *

He didn't tell her.

That was the thing about Corren Ash, Seraphine reflected later, watching from the waystation window as two small figures moved through the gray light below — the tall one and the hooded one, walking close the way people walk when they've decided to trust each other and haven't admitted it yet. Everything else about the man was noise and swagger and scarred knuckles, but his silences were load-bearing.

He had taken the cipher page. Of course he had. She had watched his fingers close on it the moment her back was turned, watched him fold it away beneath the cuff where the wolf-mark lived, watched him carry it back down the hill to the smugglers' den where the Blackwood woman was copying stolen pages by tallow-light.

And he had not shown it to her.

Seraphine knew this because she had done the only sensible thing with a baited hook: she had followed the fish. Shadowing the den had been trivial — the fog loved her, or feared her, or perhaps merely recognized her, though she forbade herself to speculate on that third option, because speculation led to the showroom, and the showroom led to the place in her mind where the doors were painted on.

Through her glass she saw them at the den's narrow window: the woman gesturing over spread pages, the man listening, nodding, his face doing something complicated every time she looked up at him. Twice he reached toward his cuff. Twice he stopped.

He's deciding, she thought. Every hour he doesn't tell her, the decision gets heavier, and heavier decisions want resolving. Either he turns her in and hates himself, or he burns the page and lies to the crown, or — 

Or the third thing. The thing she had privately begun to root for, with a traitor's hope she kept in the same locked room as her too-tidy memories.

Below, the woman laughed at something — a short, startled sound, the laugh of someone who hadn't laughed in weeks — and Corren Ash looked at her the way people look at fires they intend to sit beside for the rest of their lives.

Seraphine lowered the glass.

Eight days, she thought. The cipher gave them eight days, and the fog was already eating the roads to Grayhollow, and the sentries had forgotten each other's names, and somewhere beneath a counting house in the capital something vast turned over in its sleep and dreamed of being fed.

She had made her offer knowing he would refuse. That was the strange part — she had known, the way she knew things off skin, the way she read truth in curled hands. The offer had never been for him.

It had been for the woman. Because Marguerite Blackwood would find that cipher page eventually — folded in a pack pocket, or fallen from a coat lining, discovered at the worst possible hour in the worst possible light — and when she did, she would learn that the man beside her had been offered everything in exchange for her, and had never mentioned it.

Whether he refused it or not wouldn't matter. Secrets of that shape don't survive daylight. Seraphine had spent eleven years learning what suspicion did to people, and it did its work slowest and cruellest between people who loved each other.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, without knowing entirely to whom — the woman, the man, or the girl she had apparently once been, before her memory became a showroom. *One of us should play this game honestly.*

Out in the fog, something howled — long, low, and wrong, a sound with a name buried in it. The sentries didn't stir. The fog thickened. And beneath the border country, thin as paper now, the erased world pressed its palm against the underside of the skin of things, waiting for the door.

Eight days.
