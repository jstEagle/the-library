## 11. Raid on the Fog Camp

### Corren

He woke to silence, and silence in the fog camp was a scream.

The wolves of the borderlands were never quiet — not truly. Even in the dead hours before dawn there was always something: the low murmur of sentries trading watch, the wet click of the fog condensing on canvas, old Sefa's dogs shifting in their sleep. The camp breathed. Corren had grown up inside that breathing, and eleven years away had not taught him how to sleep without it.

Now it was gone.

He was on his feet before his eyes opened, hand closing around the sigil-stone at his throat. Outside the lean-to he shared with Marguerite, the fog hung wrong — too still, too pale, lit from below by something that wasn't moonlight. Lanterns. Dozens of them, down the slope, where the pickets should have been singing the alarm by now.

They hadn't sung because they were already dead.

"Marguerite." He shook her shoulder once, hard, no gentleness in it because gentleness would cost seconds they didn't have. Her eyes opened the way they always did — instantly, completely, as if she'd been lying awake doing arithmetic in the dark. Maybe she had. "Soldiers. Down-slope. We're cut off from the east trail."

"How many?"

"More than us." He was already pulling his boots on, listening. Boots in wet bracken, the muffled jingle of kit carried properly — this wasn't a rabble or a bounty crew. This was a line regiment moving with discipline through fog that should have turned them around twice before they found the outer pickets. Fog that should have eaten their maps and scrambled their compasses and made them march in circles until they wept.

Unless someone had told them exactly where to walk.

The thought landed in his stomach like a swallowed stone. He looked at Marguerite, asleep an hour ago beside him, trusting him, and thought: *Vale knows I met her. Vale knows what I told her without telling her.* Because that was the thing about sitting across a table from a witchfinder and refusing her bargain — you refused with your face, with your posture, with every involuntary twitch of a body she could read like a ledger. He'd said no. But his shoulders might have said *the camp is north of the standing stones, follow the streambed.*

"Corren." Marguerite's voice, low. She'd heard it too — not the soldiers, but the shape of his fear. "What is it?"

"Nothing. Yet." He hated how easily the lie came. "Get your pages. Leave everything else."

Outside, the night tore open.

The first flare went up from the southern rim — white light that turned the fog into a burning sheet — and under it came the sound Corren had hoped never to hear again in this life: the whistle-and-crack of crown arquebuses, followed by the scream of the camp's wards failing. Sefa's protections were old country magic, older than the Correction, woven into the ground itself. They should have held for an hour against anything short of siege engines.

They lasted four heartbeats.

"They knew the wards' anchors," Corren breathed. Nobody knew the anchors. Sefa hadn't trusted anyone with the anchors since —

Since the last time the pack was betrayed, thirty years ago. Since the man who'd sold them out had been flayed and burned and his name struck from every tongue.

And now someone had sold them out again, and the knowledge sat in Corren's chest with a terrible, familiar weight. He didn't let himself look at Marguerite's copied pages in her satchel. He didn't let himself think about who else had known where they'd fled.

*You did,* whispered the voice that lived behind his ribs, the wolf-voice, the honest one. *You sat with the witchfinder and gave her everything but your signature.*

* * *

### Marguerite

The camp dissolved around her the way ink dissolves in water — which was, she realized with a kind of hysterical clarity, exactly the right image, because that was all any of this was. Names in ledgers. Marks on paper. And tonight the crown had come with pens made of fire.

She ran.

Fog closed over her shoulders like cold water. Around her, the pack scattered along routes drilled into them since childhood — she saw a woman with silver-streaked hair sweep two children off their feet and vanish into the white; she heard old Sefa's voice ringing out over the gunfire, not screaming orders but *names*, calling each member of his ragged family by name so they'd know they were seen, so they'd run toward safety instead of freezing. It was the most beautiful and most terrible thing Marguerite had ever heard, because names were her trade and she knew better than anyone in that valley what the crown intended to do with them.

An arquebus ball took a young man off his feet three paces to her left. She didn't stop. Later — if there was a later — she would hate herself for not stopping. In the moment, her legs simply obeyed the oldest law there was.

Then hands closed on her arm, and she bit down on the shriek rising in her throat, and the hands belonged to Sefa.

"The stones," he rasped. Blood at his mouth, though she couldn't see the wound — the fog was full of blood tonight, everyone's and no one's. "Not the east trail, girl, they'll have the east trail. The standing stones. There's a way under." He shoved a bundle into her arms: oilcloth, heavy, tied with cord. "Take it to the door. You'll know when you see it. Your blood will know."

"My blood doesn't know *anything*," she gasped, running beside him now, his grip iron despite his years. "Sefa, I don't know my own name—"

"You do." His eyes found hers in the strobing flare-light, and there was such fierce tenderness in them that it hurt worse than any wound. "That's the whole tragedy of you, Marguerite Blackwood. You've known it all along. It's just been waiting for you the way a door waits."

A shout behind them. Boots. Sefa spun, and pushed her — physically shoved her, with strength no old man should own — into the black seam between two standing stones.

The last thing she saw over her shoulder was him turning to face the soldiers alone, arms spread wide, and the fog rising up around him like a congregation.

Then the dark took her, and the ground opened beneath her feet, and she fell into the earth of a country that had forgotten itself.

* * *

### Corren

He killed two men before he understood he was doing it.

Not with steel — his blades were somewhere back in the lean-to, abandoned in the scramble — but with the other thing, the thing he'd spent eleven years starving. It rose out of him like floodwater through a broken dam, and the world sharpened until he could hear the hearts of the men hunting through the fog, could smell the lamp-oil and gun-grease and fear-sweat of them, and then he was simply *among* them, faster than sight, and the wolf-gift that the guild had branded and bound and that he had hated and hidden all his adult life did what it had been made for.

When it was over he stood in a clearing littered with dropped lanterns, chest heaving, hands shaking — not with horror. That was the worst part. With *hunger*. Eleven years of rationing himself, and it turned out the leash had been holding back something enormous.

He should feel sick. He felt magnificent, and that frightened him more than the soldiers had.

*Marguerite.* The name cut through the animal haze. Where — the lean-to was empty, her satchel gone, and relief hit him so hard he nearly sat down. Then he caught the trail by scent, impossible and unmistakable, leading not east but toward the standing stones, and under it, fainter, another trail that stopped his breath:

Blood. Old-man blood. Leading toward the stones and then nowhere at all, cut off clean, as if the man carrying it had stepped out of the world.

Corren ran.

* * *

### Marguerite

The passage under the stones was older than anything she'd ever touched, and Marguerite had spent her life touching old things.

It wasn't carved; it was *grown*, walls of packed earth threaded with roots that pulsed faintly under her palm like veins. Her stolen pages, her careful treasonous copies, crinkled in the oilcloth against her chest, and beneath them she could feel whatever else Sefa had given her — flat, heavy, wrapped in waxed leather. She didn't open it. Some instinct older than thought told her not to open it in the dark, alone, with the sky above her full of guns.

She counted her steps instead. Counting was prayer, counting was the ledger, counting was the only scripture she'd ever trusted. Forty steps. Sixty. At eighty the passage bent, and at ninety she saw light ahead — not firelight, not lamplight. A gray, sourceless glow, like the fog outside distilled to its essence and set glowing.

The passage opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and Marguerite stopped counting.

Roots formed the walls here too, but they'd been shaped — deliberately, patiently, over centuries — into shelves. And the shelves were full of books. Not guild volumes, nothing with vellum and gilt. These were humble things: farm ledgers, parish registers, birth books, the kind of records every village keeps and no one thinks about. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Their spines were swollen with damp, their covers furred with mold, and every single one of them was *wrong* in a way that made Marguerite's gift rear up in her chest like a startled horse.

The names inside them were being erased. Slowly, page by page, year by year, the ink fading on its own — the same way the ink had moved on her desk in the counting house, that first ordinary afternoon when everything began. The country's forgetting wasn't a single act. It was ongoing. It was *maintenance*.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one. "Oh, you poor things."

She crossed the chamber, knelt, and pulled one book at random from the shelf. A parish register. The earliest entries were in a strong brown hand: births, weddings, deaths, a whole village's life recorded in a script she almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a face across a market crowd. And then, forty years ago, the entries changed. Same village. Same families. But the names were subtly different, the places renamed, the history quietly re-cut — and in the margin of one page, in a hand she recognized with a jolt that went through her like ice water, someone had written a single word in guild cipher:

*Corrected.*

The guild's hand. The guild's cipher. Her masters had come here, or their predecessors had, and annotated the erasure like auditors checking a column of figures.

Marguerite sat down hard on the root-floor and laughed, once, a horrible sound in the quiet chamber. She had spent her life balancing accounts for the Vellum Guild. She had believed — right up until the burning, right up until the flight — that the guild traded in impossibilities that *arrived* on its doorstep. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. She had thought herself a bookkeeper of miracles.

She was a bookkeeper of theft. And this chamber, these dying books, this was the warehouse where the stolen goods were stored.

Her fingers found the oilcloth bundle Sefa had pressed on her. She untied the cord at last, because she needed something true in her hands, and unwrapped waxed leather, and there it was:

A map. Drawn on skin — she didn't let herself think about whose — tanned and inked in the old way, and it showed the borderlands entire, and beyond the border it showed a country. Not empty space, not the vague "unclaimed wilds" printed on every crown map she'd ever filed. A real country, with rivers and towns and a name written across its heart in letters that seemed to shift when she looked directly at them, refusing to be read, refusing even now to be remembered.

Beneath it, a second document, folded small: a letter, decades old, sealed with a sigil she knew intimately because it matched the mark on her gate, the mark on her childhood memory, the mark that had started everything.

She broke the seal with numb fingers.

*"To the keeper of the door, whoever you turn out to be,"* it began. *"We hid the truth where only hunger could find it. The gate does not keep the erased country out. It keeps the memory of it in. When you are ready to give the door what it has always wanted, come to the place where the ledger began. Bring a name. Bring it freely. And forgive us, because we could not bring ours."*

There was no signature. Of course there was no signature. Whoever wrote it had done exactly what Marguerite was beginning to understand she herself would have to do.

Footsteps in the passage. Her whole body clenched — and then the smell reached her, woodsmoke and wet fur and Corren, and she was on her feet and moving before her mind agreed to it, and then his arms were around her and she was holding onto him with both fists like a drowning woman, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

* * *

### Corren

She was alive. She was alive, and she was shaking, and she had blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers, and when he finally loosened his grip enough to look at her face, Corren saw that she was looking at him the way you look at a sum that won't balance.

"What happened up there," she said. Not a question. A summons.

"Sefa—" His voice cracked. He steadied it. "He held them. Bought us the passage. I got there after. There was nothing—" He stopped, because the lie about the rest was sitting in his throat like a bone, and she was watching him with those ledger-keeper's eyes that missed nothing, that had missed nothing since the day she'd caught moving ink on a routine entry.

"The soldiers knew the ward anchors," she said quietly. "Sefa told me no one living knew the anchors."

"No one living knew the anchors," Corren repeated. And then, because some part of him — the part that was tired, the part that had watched an old man die buying seconds with his body — could not carry it one more hour: "Vale offered me amnesty, Marguerite. Three nights ago. My freedom for yours. I said no. I swear on the stone, on the pack, on every god the guild ever invoiced — I said no."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled by earth and roots, the guns had gone quiet. The fog camp was over. The pack was scattered or dead, and the two of them stood in a chapel of dying books with the whole weight of a stolen country around them.

"I believe you," Marguerite said at last. And then, with a terrible gentleness that was worse than anger: "But someone talked, Corren. Someone always talks. So either Vale read it off you anyway — or she has another ear in this war, and we don't know where it listens."

She held up the map. The unreadable name shimmered across it like fish under dark water.

"So we stop trusting anyone," she said. "Anyone but each other. And even that —" her voice caught, and he watched her master it, watched the bookkeeper close the trembling column and draw the line — "even that, we hold carefully. Come on. The letter says the door wants a name freely given. I think it's finally time we asked it what it wants mine for."

Above them, faint through sixty feet of earth, something began to hum — deep and patient and hungry, the sound of the gate waking, as if it had heard her.

As if it had been listening all along.
