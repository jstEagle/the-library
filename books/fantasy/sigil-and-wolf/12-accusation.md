## 12. Accusation

The fog camp was ash and trampled snow by the time we stopped running.

We had walked most of the night — first at a dead sprint through the borderlands where the trees forgot their own shapes, then slower, when the fog thinned and the ground turned to frozen heath that crunched like old parchment underfoot. Corren set the pace. He always set the pace. He moved the way water moves downhill, certain of his route even when the land had no reason to agree with him.

I followed because I had nowhere else to be, and because the elder's last words were still lodged in my chest like a splinter I hadn't yet worked out: *The door remembers what you paid. Go home, keeper.*

Home. The word meant a counting house with ledgers of impossible things and a gate beneath it that hungered. It meant masters who burned people along with paper. It meant Seraphine Vale's gentle voice asking questions that peeled skin from bone.

It did not mean this frozen heath, or the man walking ahead of me with his collar up and his silence wrapped around him like a second coat.

He had been silent since the raid. Not the comfortable silence of the smugglers' den, where we'd hunched over my stolen pages while the fire spat and he'd laughed — actually laughed — when I caught him stealing honey from my ration. This was a different silence. A loaded one. He carried it the way he'd once carried the sigil-stone: carefully, close to the body, as if it might go off.

I had spent eleven years balancing accounts. I knew what concealment looked like on a page. A figure too round. An entry dated twice. A debt recorded in a hand that didn't match the rest of the ledger.

Corren Ash was an entry in the wrong hand.

* * *

We made shelter in the hollow beneath a fallen rowan, its trunk wide enough that three grown men could have hidden behind it standing. Corren built a fire small enough to cup in two hands, feeding it twigs one at a time, and I sat across from him and watched the light move over his face and rehearsed the question until the rehearsal wore the fear off it.

Almost.

"Tell me about the road," I said.

He didn't look up. "What road?"

"The one you took yesterday morning." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "You said you were checking the perimeter. You were gone two hours. The perimeter of this camp is a circle four hundred paces across. You can walk it in twenty minutes."

The twig in his fingers stopped moving. That was all. He was very good — I would grant him that forever — but I had watched merchants lie about shipments for a decade, and I knew the anatomy of a pause. His lasted half a heartbeat too long.

"Longer if you're careful," he said.

"Yes," I agreed. "Longer if you're careful."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a bird made a sound like tearing cloth, and I thought of the maps that ate themselves, roads unravelling into blank vellum, and how a person could do the same thing — present themselves whole and legible right up until the moment someone looked closely and found the country missing.

"Corren." I said his name the way the guild masters said *audit*. "Vale found us. Soldiers came down on the camp within a day of your meeting with her. They knew where the fog was thinnest. They knew which sentries to slip past. They knew—" My voice cracked and I hated it, hated the crack, hated that grief and suspicion sounded so much alike in the throat. "They killed Wren. They killed the elder. And she died putting the truth into my hands while the man who brought her that truth was standing thirty feet away doing nothing."

"I was fighting," he said quietly.

"You were fighting *after*. Where were you *before*?"

Now he looked up. Firelight caught the wolf-mark on his forearm where his sleeve had ridden back — the same sigil burned into a stone he'd tried to sell me a lifetime ago, back when my worst problem was ink that moved on its own. His eyes were steady. That was the worst part. Liars either blink or stare, and he stared, and I couldn't tell anymore whether steadiness meant honesty or practice.

"I met her," he said. "On the road past the boundary stones. She asked me to sit. I sat."

My heart did something complicated and painful. "And?"

"And she talked."

"*Corren.*"

"She offered me amnesty." The words came out flat, each one placed like a coin on a counter. "Full pardon. Clean papers. A new name, if I wanted one — which is funny, isn't it, given everything. All I had to do was bring her the keeper of the Vellum Guild's gate. Bring her you."

The cold went through me then, the real cold, the kind that has nothing to do with weather. I had known. Some part of me had known since the raid, since I'd counted the soldiers' positions against the camp's layout and found the arithmetic too neat. Knowing a thing and hearing it are different animals. Hearing it makes it real the way a signature makes a contract real.

"When?" I asked. My voice sounded far away, like someone else's ledger entry. "When did she offer you this?"

"Two days before the raid."

"Two days." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Two days, Corren. You sat on that for two days. You slept beside our fire. You taught me that card game — what was it called, the one where you lose if you show your hand? You let me sleep. And the whole time you were carrying her offer around in your pocket like a coin you hadn't decided whether to spend."

"I decided." He leaned forward, and the firelight made his shadow loom and shrink against the rowan's fallen bulk. "That's the thing you're not letting me say, Marguerite. I decided. Before I ever came back to camp, I decided. I told her no. I told her no to her face, and I walked away, and I never said a word about it because—"

"Because telling me would have meant trusting me," I finished. "And you don't."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" I stood. My legs had gone stiff with cold and sitting and fury, and standing hurt, and I was glad of the hurt because it gave the anger somewhere to go. "Eleven years, Corren. Eleven years I kept the guild's books, and do you know what I learned? Every fraud starts as a kindness. Every forged entry begins with someone deciding that the truth would hurt someone who didn't deserve to be hurt. So they smooth it over. They protect you from the ugly number. And by the time you find it, the whole ledger is rotten from the inside, and you can't tell which entries were ever real."

"So that's what I am now," he said. "An entry. A number you're auditing."

"Don't you dare make this small." I heard my own voice rise and didn't care. "People are dead. Wren is dead — she was nineteen, Corren, she used to hum while she mended nets, I only knew her for three days and I will hear that humming for the rest of my life. The elder burned herself hollow passing me the truth of the door. And the woman who arranged it sat down across from you and bought the information with a pardon, and you thought the correct response was *silence*?"

"I thought the correct response was keeping you alive!" He was on his feet now too, and the fire between us guttered in the sudden movement, shadows swinging. "You think Vale offered me amnesty because she wanted *me*? I'm a smuggler with a brand. I'm a rounding error. She offered me the world because she wanted the gate-keeper, and every hour I spent weighing that offer was an hour she wasn't spending sending soldiers to drag you out of your bed. I told her no. I chose. But I'll tell you what I didn't do — I didn't hand her a map to your conscience. Because I've seen what you do with guilt, Marguerite Blackwood. You carry it. You balance it. You'd have spent those two days convincing yourself that turning yourself in was the mathematically merciful option, and I wasn't going to give you the chance."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, ripples spreading outward through everything I'd built the argument on.

Because he was right. That was the obscenity of it. I could feel the truth of it settling into me even as I hated him for saying it, because yes — some traitorous chamber of my heart had already run that calculation, there in the burning camp, watching the elder die: *one life weighed against a country; surely the ledger permits it.* And he had seen it. He had seen it without my saying a word, the way he saw safe paths through fog, and he had lied to me to keep me from myself.

Which was exactly what the guild had done for forty years.

"There it is," I said softly. "Do you hear yourself? You lied to protect me from my own judgment. Do you know who else thought they knew best? The masters. They rewrote a whole country because they decided people couldn't be trusted with the truth of themselves. They looked at the world and thought, *this would hurt them, better to smooth the entry.* And here you are, doing it in miniature, and calling it loyalty."

"It's not the same—"

"No. It's smaller. That's all. Same shape, smaller scale." I wrapped my arms around myself. The fire had shrunk to embers, and the cold came back in like a creditor. "You should have told me, Corren. Whatever you feared I'd do — whatever terrible noble arithmetic you thought I'd attempt — I deserved the chance to make it. That's the difference between us and them. We don't get to decide for each other. The moment we do, we're just the Correction wearing kinder faces."

He flinched. Actually flinched, the wolf-marked smuggler who'd stared down witchfinders and border fog and me, and something in my chest twisted at the sight and I refused to soften, refused, because softening was how the rot started.

"Say something," I said.

"What do you want me to say?" His voice had gone rough. "That I'm sorry? I am. That I'd do it differently? I don't know that I would. That's the honest answer, Marguerite, and it's the one you're not going to want. I would do it again. I would sit across from that pale-eyed horror and swallow her bargain and say no and tell you nothing, because you're still breathing, and that's the only column of the ledger I know how to read."

"That's not the only column."

"It's the only one I trust myself with!"

His voice broke on it — broke clean through, the way ice breaks, all at once and irreversibly — and in the ember-light I saw what I hadn't let myself see all night: he wasn't calm. He had never been calm. Underneath the smuggler's certainty was a man holding himself together with both hands, terrified, and the terror had been there since the raid, maybe since the counting house, maybe since long before me, and he had spent it all on making sure I lived.

I sat down slowly. The anger didn't leave — anger doesn't leave, it just changes address, moves from the throat down into the bones where it lives permanently — but exhaustion arrived alongside it, and grief, and a terrible tenderness I resented with my whole heart.

"I believe you refused her," I said at last.

His head came up.

"I believe you told her no. I believe you'd do it again." I held up a hand before hope could finish forming on his face. "But believing you isn't the same as trusting you, and I need you to understand the difference, because I don't think you do. Trust means I can turn my back and know the ledger balances. What I have now is belief with a hole in it. And Corren — we are walking into the capital. Into the guild. Into the teeth of the thing that made us both. I cannot walk in next to a man with a hole in his account. When the moment comes — and it will come, the moment where one of us has to choose and the other can't see it happening — I won't know if you're choosing for me again. I won't know if the choice is even mine."

"So what does that mean?" he asked. Quietly. Like a man asking a physician for a prognosis. "Does it mean you're leaving? Walking to the gate alone?"

I looked at him for a long time. At the fire dying between us. At the fog beyond the rowan, thinning toward dawn, through which the erased country sometimes showed itself — a hill that shouldn't be there, a road with no destination, the ghost-shape of a nation remembering itself in fragments.

"No," I said. "It means we go together, and we watch each other. Both of us. You watch me for the martyr's arithmetic; I'll watch you for the protective lie. We go in with open eyes instead of closed ones." I almost smiled, and it hurt. "Consider it a partnership audit. Ongoing. No more concealed entries — from either side. Agreed?"

Something eased in his shoulders, though not all the way. "Agreed," he said. Then, after a moment: "There is one more thing. One entry I haven't shown you."

The cold returned all at once. "Corren—"

"Not a lie. Not a concealment. Something Vale said, when I refused her." He crouched and fed the fire a fresh branch, and the flames climbed, and his face above them was grim as a border marker. "She didn't threaten me. She thanked me. Said the crown didn't need my answer anyway — that the hunt was never about catching the gifted one at a time." He looked up at me through the smoke. "She said the gate feeds whether we come willingly or not. And that the guild masters aren't hiding from the purge, Marguerite. They're *managing* it. They've been managing it for forty years."

The fire crackled. Far away, eastward, toward the capital, the sky had begun to pale — and beneath the pale, faint as a watermark, I felt it: the pulse of the gate, patient as interest accruing, hungry as ever.

And beneath it, closer, another feeling entirely — the sense that somewhere behind us, on the dark heath, something had been following since the raid. Something that walked politely.

I stood, and pulled my coat tight, and looked north toward the capital that had erased my name before I was born.

"Then we stop being hunted," I said, "and start hunting back."
