## 15. The Witchfinder's Confession

The trap closed at dusk, in a village that was already forgetting itself.

Seraphine Vale stood in the doorway of the last standing house on the last standing street, and watched the fog come down the hill like something poured. Behind her, four crown soldiers waited with iron manacles and the particular boredom of men who had done this eleven times before and would do it eleven more. In front of her, at the end of the lane, Marguerite Blackwood walked out of the mist with her hood down and her hands empty, as though she had simply decided to stop running.

Later — much later — Seraphine would wonder whether that was exactly what had happened.

"Ledger-keeper," she said.

"Witchfinder." Marguerite's voice was steady. Her eyes went to the soldiers, to the manacles, and then back to Seraphine with an expression Seraphine had learned to read across a hundred interrogations: not fear. Arithmetic. *Can I take her? Can I run? Is it worth it?*

"You're alone," Seraphine observed. "Where is your wolf?"

"Gone."

"Liar," said one of the soldiers, almost fondly.

"Probably," Seraphine agreed, and did not take her eyes off the ledger-keeper. "But it doesn't matter tonight. Bind her hands in front. She'll want them free where we're going."

Marguerite's chin lifted. "And where are we going?"

"Somewhere the fog can't follow." Seraphine turned, coat snapping, and let the soldiers do their work. "You've been running toward the capital for six days, Mistress Blackwood. Did you think I wouldn't notice that you were running *toward* me?"

* * *

They traveled by night, which was when the Correction moved fastest now. Seraphine saw it eating the country from the window of the coach: hedgerows going soft at their edges like wet paper, a church spire that was there and then had always been a stand of elms, a shepherd on a hillside who waved at them and then, between one blink and the next, was only a shape of fog wearing a hat. The soldiers didn't look. They had been corrected too, in the small administrative way — given memories of orders that justified all this, a history in which the Purge had always existed, always been necessary, always been *hers*.

Seraphine remembered none of that either. That was the thing no one understood about being a witchfinder. Everyone assumed she remembered everything, that memory was her weapon, that she held the whole country's secrets behind her teeth like coins.

She remembered nothing. She had never remembered anything. Every morning of her life had arrived pre-written.

She looked across the coach at Marguerite Blackwood, whose wrists were chafed raw inside the manacles, who was watching the dissolving countryside with tears she was too proud to let fall, and felt something move in her chest that her issued memories had no name for.

"You knew the shepherd," Seraphine said quietly. "From the hillside."

"He waved like my father used to wave." Marguerite didn't turn from the window. "Do you see it? Do you see what's happening to them?"

"I see everything that happens to them. It's my office to see it."

"And you do nothing."

"I do exactly what I was made to do." Seraphine heard her own voice go flat and strange. "That is not a figure of speech, ledger-keeper. I mean it the way you'd mean *water is wet*."

Marguerite finally looked at her. In the coach lamp's light her eyes were the color of strong tea, ordinary and exhausted, and full of a hatred so clean it was almost restful to be its object. "Then you're a monster."

"Yes," Seraphine said. "Almost certainly. But I am a monster with a confession, and you are going to hear it whether you want to or not, because in three days the guild masters will have you under the counting house and after that there will be no one left alive who knows what I know."

* * *

The safehouse was a tithe-barn outside a market town whose name changed twice while they were in it. Seraphine dismissed the soldiers to the perimeter with written orders — written, because spoken orders drifted in this fog like everything else — and then she sat down across the trestle table from her prisoner, poured two cups of wine from the same jug, drank from one to prove it wasn't poisoned, and pushed the other across the wood.

Marguerite didn't touch it.

"You think I drugged it. You think this is the beginning of some elaborate interrogation." Seraphine leaned back. "It isn't. There won't be an interrogation. I've read your file, Marguerite Blackwood — the real one, the one the guild keeps, not the one the crown issues. Do you know what's in it? Forty years of entries. Storms in bottles. Years bought and sold. A gate beneath the Vellum Counting House that must never open fully, and one keeper in every generation who can feel its hunger." She smiled, and hated how easily the smile came, how it arrived on her face like a clerk arriving at his desk. "Your file is the only honest document in this country. Isn't that funny? The traitors keep the true records."

"What do you want from me?"

"The truth. Just once, from someone who'll believe me." Seraphine set down her cup. Under the table, out of Marguerite's sight, her own hands were shaking; she pressed them flat against her knees. "I'm going to tell you what I am, and you are going to think it's a trick, because I would think it was a trick. Then I'm going to show you proof. And then, ledger-keeper, you and I are going to talk about doors."

"Say it, then."

Seraphine took a breath. It was strange how much courage this took — more than the raids, more than the burnings, more than standing in front of the Guild Masters with their faces like folded paper. Those things required nothing of her but function. This required her to open herself like a ledger to an auditor, knowing what the audit would find.

"My name is Seraphine Vale. I am thirty-one years old. I have been a witchfinder for nine years, and I remember every day of those nine years with perfect clarity, because those memories were *given* to me. Written into me. Before that—" her voice caught, and she was genuinely surprised; she hadn't known there was anything left in her to catch — "before that, there is nothing. No childhood. No mother's face. No first snow. I woke up nineteen years old on the steps of the Office of Inquiry with a head full of installed history and a warrant signed in ink that predates my birth by a decade. They built me the way a smith builds a tool. And the tool they built was cruel, because cruelty was the specification."

Marguerite had gone very still. "Corrected," she said at last. "Like the country."

"Like the country. Like the shepherd on the hill. Like everyone the Correction has ever touched." Seraphine laughed — a short, awful sound. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? When I burn a gifted child's name from the rolls, I feel righteous doing it. I feel *justified*. The certainty arrives fully formed, the way a memory does, and I cannot find the seam. For nine years I thought that certainty was conscience. It's carpentry. Someone hammered it into me, and I have been swinging it at people ever since."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because three days ago I found the seam."

* * *

She rose, went to the barn's far wall, and pulled aside a loose board. From the cavity behind it she took a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth — not a guild ledger, not a crown document, but something older, hand-sewn, its cover scarred with what looked like claw marks. She set it on the table between them.

"This came out of the borderlands. Out of the fog, where your smuggler friends trade. A pack of them found it in the ruins of a village that doesn't exist anymore — a village that existed *before* the Correction." She unwrapped it. The pages were dense with two hands: one faded brown ink, one fresh black. "The brown hand is forty years old. It's a record of the Correction itself. Not the official version — the *costs*. Every family that crossed the erased border. Every toll paid. And here—" she turned to a page near the middle, and watched Marguerite's breath stop — "here is the entry for a crossing party of eleven souls, out of a country whose name I cannot pronounce and you, I suspect, can."

Marguerite's fingers hovered over the page without touching it. Her lips moved, reading. Seraphine watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"That's my family," Marguerite whispered. "That's — the names. I don't know these names. But I know them. They're *familiar*, the way a word is familiar when it's on the tip of your tongue—"

"Read the marginal note. The black ink. That's mine."

Marguerite read aloud, slowly: "*Crossing parties pay the toll of forgetting. But the toll is not taken evenly. Children too young to hold their names lose them entirely. Adults lose the country but keep the grief.*" She stopped. Looked up. "This note isn't forty years old."

"No. I wrote it eight days ago, the night I first read this book. And here is what happened that night, ledger-keeper, and why I am sitting in a barn confessing to a traitor instead of delivering her to the capital." Seraphine sat down again, slowly, as if her legs had become unreliable. "When I read that passage — *children too young to hold their names lose them entirely* — something in me rejected it. Not emotionally. *Physically*. The way a body rejects poison. My installed certainties said: the Correction was necessary, the toll was just, the erased chose erasure. And underneath that, for the first time in nine years, something else said: *no*. One word. Small and feral and mine."

"You felt the seam."

"I felt the seam." Seraphine's hands were shaking openly now; she let them. "And once you've felt it, you can't stop feeling it. I spent eight days pulling at it. Do you know what I found underneath? Nothing. A room with no furniture. Whatever I was before they made me, they took it all — except apparently one refusal, buried so deep even the Correction couldn't reach it. Some part of me has always known. Has always been screaming in a language I couldn't hear until a dead villager's ledger handed me the dictionary."

Marguerite was quiet for a long time. Outside, the fog pressed against the barn's gaps, and somewhere in it a dog barked twice and then, mid-bark, forgot how.

"You want the gate opened," Marguerite said finally. It wasn't a question.

"I want the Correction *undone*. Every stolen name returned. Every installed memory stripped out — including mine, including the ugly ones, including whatever I was before, even if what I was before was worse than this. I want the country to be real again." Seraphine met her eyes. "You want the same thing. But here is where we differ, and here is why you should be afraid of me even sitting across this table with your wrists chained: you want it because it's *right*. I want it because I want to know who I am. Even if the answer is a monster. Even if the woman I was before they built Seraphine Vale was someone I'd despise. I would rather be honestly damned than righteously hollow. That is not a noble hunger, ledger-keeper. It's the same hunger as the gate's. I know that. I feel it in myself every hour — this appetite to tear the world open and see what's underneath. The difference between me and the thing under the counting house is that I'm asking permission."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I deliver you to the capital in chains, the guild binds you to the gate forever, and I go back to burning children's names and feeling righteous about it until the day the Correction eats me too." Seraphine spread her hands. "I am not offering you mercy. I'm offering you an alliance of the damned. You need someone with the crown's keys. I need someone who knows how the lock works. Neither of us trusts the other. Good. Trust is for people with clean hands."

Marguerite looked down at the old ledger, at the names of her family, at the toll they had paid for her. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "Corren warned me about you. He said you read truths off people's skin like braille. He said never let you touch me."

"Your smuggler is smarter than he looks."

"He also said—" Marguerite hesitated, and Seraphine saw her decide to spend the coin anyway — "he said you took something from him once. A name. He said you read it off him and wrote it down, and that wherever you keep what you write down, it *hurts*."

Seraphine went cold. Slowly, deliberately, she reached into her coat and drew out the object she had carried for nine years without once examining it: a slim black book, crown-stamped, its pages filled with names in her own handwriting. Names taken at interviews. Names read off skin. She had always believed it was procedure.

Now, feeling the seam, she opened it to Corren Ash's page and read what her own hand had written there, and understood.

"It's not a record," she said quietly. "It's a *hold*. Every name in this book is a hook in someone. That's how the witchfinders track the gifted — we carry leashes and call them files." She looked up. "Which means I can also let go."

She tore the page out. It resisted — the book gripped it the way a jaw grips — and for a moment the barn filled with a sound like a distant animal in pain, and both of them froze. Then the page came free, and the black ink on it faded to gray, and the gray to nothing, and somewhere out in the fog a man who had been carrying a wound he couldn't name for nine years felt it close and never knew why.

Seraphine slid the blank page across the table to Marguerite Blackwood.

"There's your proof I'm not lying. I just cut a leash the crown gave me, and when they find out, they will unmake me the way they unmade the shepherd on the hill. So understand what I'm saying, ledger-keeper: I am already dead. The only question left is what I die *doing*." She leaned forward. "In three days, the guild performs a rite beneath the counting house to bind you to the gate permanently. I have the duty roster. I have the seals. I can get us both into that chamber — but once we're in, I know nothing about what's down there, and you know everything. So here is my confession complete: I am a constructed monster asking a woman whose family my masters erased to walk into hell with me and open a door that should never open. And I need your answer tonight, because tomorrow I stop being able to pretend, and the day after that, neither of us gets another chance."

Marguerite stared at her for a long moment. Then she picked up the untouched cup of wine, drank it down in one swallow, and set it down with a click that sounded, in the silent barn, like a lock turning.

"All right, witchfinder," she said. "But if you're lying to me, I promise you — I balance accounts. And yours is about to come due."

Far away, beneath a city that was forgetting itself faster every hour, something vast and hungry felt two enemies join hands above it, and began, very softly, to laugh.
