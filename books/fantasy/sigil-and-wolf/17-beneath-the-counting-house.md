## 17. Beneath the Counting House

### Marguerite

The stairs beneath the counting house had never existed.

Marguerite had worked in the Vellum Guild for eleven years, and she had walked past the iron door behind the ink cellar a thousand times without once seeing it. Now it stood open before her, exhaling cold air that smelled of wet stone and old paper and something sweeter underneath — something like the moment before a storm breaks. Seraphine Vale descended ahead of her, lantern held high, witchfinder's grey coat brushing walls that were not quite stone and not quite vellum but something between, as if the whole passage had been written rather than built.

"You've been down here before," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question. She could tell by the way Vale moved — no hesitation at the turns, no surprise when the stair doubled back on itself in defiance of every architectural law the building above obeyed.

"Once." Vale's voice was flat as ledger paper. "Forty years ago. I was carried."

The words dropped through Marguerite like a stone through still water. *Carried.* But there was no time to pull the thread, because the stair ended, and the gate chamber opened around them, and every thought she had ever had about the guild died quietly in her chest.

It was vast. Vaster than the counting house could possibly contain, which meant it didn't — contain, or possibly exist inside the same geometry. The ceiling was lost in darkness that was not darkness but *unwritten*, a blackness with texture to it, like the margin of a page. And at the center, rising from a floor of pale stone scored with ten thousand sigils, stood the gate.

She had felt it all her life through eleven feet of earth and foundation: the pulse, the hunger, the patient appetite she'd mistaken for her own imagination. Here, unmediated, it was almost beautiful. A doorway of black glass taller than three men, its frame carved from a single piece of white wood that seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it. The surface did not show reflections. It showed *absences* — shapes that might have been trees, hills, a coastline, seen through water so deep the sun had never touched it.

Behind the gate, arranged in a crescent, stood six figures in guild masters' robes. Not seven. Marguerite counted twice. Master Ondrell was missing, and Master Hesk, and she understood with a lurch that they were the two who had ordered the ledgers burned, the two who had been closest to the fire when Seraphine's soldiers came — and that their absence here meant they had been spent already, fed to something, fuel for tonight.

"Keeper Blackwood." The eldest master, Corvane, stepped forward. His voice was warm. That was the worst part; it was genuinely warm, the voice of a man greeting a daughter home. "You came. We hoped you would understand, eventually, what you are for."

"I know what I'm for," Marguerite said. Her hands were shaking. She let them. "I read the pages I copied. The Correction wasn't a war. It was an *entry*. You wrote a country out of existence and wrote another one over it, and the gate is the pen."

Corvane smiled, and behind him the other masters began to move, taking positions at points of the crescent, drawing chalk-white lines on the floor with rods of bone.

"And the keeper," he said gently, "is the hand that holds it steady."

Seraphine Vale set down her lantern. When Marguerite glanced at her, expecting — hoping for — some sign, some flicker of the woman who had confessed to programmed cruelty and crown-issue memories, she found Vale's face smooth and unreadable, her eyes fixed on the gate with an expression Marguerite finally recognized.

Not loyalty. Not duty.

*Longing.*

"Bind her," Corvane said.

* * *

### Corren

The wolf had never been this loud.

Corren crouched in the delivery tunnel beneath the counting house — a smuggler's route older than the guild, one his pack had used since before the Correction, back when the tunnels had belonged to people who remembered why they'd dug them — and felt his own blood trying to turn him inside out. His brand burned. Not the surface burn of Vale's touch or the guild's detection charms, but something deeper, the mark itself straining toward whatever waited below like a hound against a leash.

He'd left the fog camp at a dead run four days ago with a single page folded against his heart. He'd crossed half the country in that time, moving only at night, sleeping in ditches, eating nothing he could remember afterward. The page was soft as cloth now from handling. He took it out anyway, by feel, in the dark, and read it again even though he knew every line.

*To open the door truly, a name must be given freely to the ledger, and struck freely. No rite compels it. No binding holds it. This is the flaw in the Correction, and the mercy.*

The elder's last gift, pressed into his hands while the camp burned. He had run with it the way a man runs carrying someone else's child.

Above him, muffled through forty feet of earth, he heard singing begin. Low, layered voices, male and female, in no language he knew — and yet his brand translated it anyway, a cold crawling comprehension: they were reciting names. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Names being *fed* somewhere.

He moved.

The tunnel ended at a grate rusted through in three places; he went through it like water. Beyond lay a service passage, then a door that should have been locked and was instead simply *asleep*, its lock dreaming, and his gift — the feral, wordless thing the guild would have called corruption and his pack called the wolf — slid under the door's attention the way a wolf slides under a fence. Then a stairwell going down, down, impossibly far, the air growing colder and sweeter, storm-sweet, gate-sweet.

He heard Marguerite before he saw her.

She was screaming — not in pain, exactly. In refusal. And the sound of it hit the wolf in him like a hand hitting a table, and Corren stopped being quiet, stopped being careful, and ran.

* * *

### Marguerite

They had put her in the circle.

It was elegant; she hated how elegant. Seven sigils — six masters standing, one empty point where she knelt — drawn in a substance that glowed faintly gold, each line humming with the same pulse she'd felt through the counting house floor for eleven years. The gate had been speaking to her all that time, she realized. Calling its keeper home. Every night she'd lain awake feeling hungry without knowing why, the gate had been hungry *for her specifically*.

"The rite requires consent," Corvane was saying, circling her, patient as a man explaining interest rates. "That has always been the gate's law. But consent, Keeper, is a matter of understanding. Once you understand what hangs in the balance — the stability of the realm, the peace of forty years, millions of lives resting on the Correction holding — you will give yourself freely. Most keepers do. The alternative is that we take the country apart and write it again, and the writing of countries is *expensive*."

"How many?" Marguerite asked. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "How many keepers before me?"

"Four," Corvane said. "You are the fifth. Each served faithfully until the gate's appetite exceeded them. Each was... retired." He said it the way she herself entered losses in a ledger. Cleanly. Without malice.

"And after me?"

"There is no after you. You are the last of the crossing-blood. The Correction thinned the supply." For the first time something moved behind his warmth — something that might have been grief, if grief could be owned by an institution. "Which is why tonight matters. Tonight we bind you permanently, and the gate sleeps for a century, and you sleep with it. It is not cruel, Marguerite. It is arithmetic."

"It's theft," she said. "It's been theft since the beginning. You erased a country and you've been feeding it scraps of gifted people for forty years to keep it quiet, and you call it arithmetic—"

The singing swelled. The masters raised their bone rods. Gold light climbed the circle's lines toward her knees, and Marguerite felt it begin to take hold — not pain, worse, *order*, her thoughts smoothing out, her fear dissolving, a terrible serenity pouring in like warm water, and she understood that this was how the other four had consented. Not persuaded. *Laundered.* Their fear washed out, their will softened, until giving themselves seemed like their own idea.

No. *No.* She bit her tongue hard enough to taste copper and clung to the pain, clung to Corren's face, to the weight of a sigil-stone in a memory of a counting-house table, to—

The gate *opened its eye.*

Not physically. Nothing about the black glass changed. But Marguerite felt it look at her, the way she had felt it watch her for years, except now there was nothing between them, and what looked out was not hunger exactly. It was *homesickness*. An entire erased country pressing against the inside of a door, wanting its name back.

And beneath the singing, from somewhere above and behind, came a sound like the world tearing canvas.

* * *

### Corren

He came through the wall.

Not through a door — the doors below had begun sealing themselves, the guild's wards waking as the rite peaked — but through a section of passage wall that his gift told him was thinner than the rest, thinner than it believed itself, a wall with a doubt in it. The wolf hit that doubt like a battering ram and the wall came apart in a bloom of dust and screaming vellum-grain, and Corren rolled through into the gate chamber with blood in his mouth and the page crushed in his fist.

Six robed heads turned. Marguerite, kneeling in a circle of gold light, turned too, and the look on her face nearly undid him — hope and fury in equal measure, because of course she thought he'd sold her, of course she did, he'd never had the chance to tell her about Vale's bargain, and there was no time now, there would never be time.

"CORREN ASH," boomed Corvane, and the name itself became a force, guild-craft, a spoken writ of arrest. The air tried to close around Corren like a fist.

The wolf refused.

It was the first time in his life he had simply *let it off the leash entirely* — no shaping, no suppression, none of the careful domestication that had kept him alive among normal men. He let the brand blaze and the gift pour up through it, and the fist of air shredded, and the nearest master went sprawling as a shockwave of raw uncaged wildness rolled across the chamber. Chalk lines guttered. The singing faltered.

"Impossible," one of the masters gasped. "The purge took—"

"The purge took your *records*," Corren snarled, and his voice came out half wolf, resonant and wrong and wonderful. "You never took us. You just wrote down that you had."

He was moving as he spoke, low and fast along the crescent's edge, and the masters split — two turning to hold the rite, four turning to hold him, bone rods spitting white fire. He ducked the first bolt and took the second across the shoulder, and it burned like a brand being rewritten, and he kept running, because Marguerite was on her feet now inside the circle, fighting the golden light with her whole body, and she was losing.

"MARGUERITE!" he roared. "THE PAGE! THE ELDER'S PAGE! A NAME GIVEN FREELY AND STRUCK FREELY — THAT'S THE DOOR — YOU DON'T HAVE TO BE BOUND, YOU HAVE TO BE *ASKED*—"

Corvane's head snapped toward him with an expression of pure, institutional horror.

"Kill the smuggler."

* * *

### Marguerite

*A name given freely and struck freely.*

The words landed in her like a key turning. She had copied those pages; she had read them by candlelight in a smugglers' den while maps ate themselves; she had known. But knowing and understanding were different animals, and understanding arrived now, in the circle, in the light, with the gate's homesick eye fixed on her: the door out was never geographic. It had never been about leaving the country. It was about *ending the lie*, and ending the lie required a name surrendered willingly to the ledger — and she was a ledger-keeper. She had been training for this her entire life without knowing it.

But not yet. Not with Corven fighting alone. Not with Vale—

Vale.

Marguerite found her across the chamber. The witchfinder had not moved since the binding began. She stood beside her dead lantern with her hands at her sides, watching the rite, watching the gate, and her lips were moving — silently, over and over, a single phrase, and Marguerite read it off her mouth the way Vale read truth off skin:

*Open it. Open it. Open it.*

Two masters broke from the fight and returned to the crescent, restoring the seven-pointed pressure, and the golden light surged up Marguerite's spine. Her knees buckled. The serenity came flooding again, sweet and annihilating, whispering that resistance was unnecessary, that peace was right *there*, that she only had to stop—

She reached for the anger instead. She fed on it deliberately, greedily, the way Corren fed the wolf: her stolen childhood beyond the border, her family's paid toll of forgetting, the elder dying in the smoke, four keepers *retired*, a country erased and renamed and billed for its own erasure. She let the anger be louder than the peace.

And she spoke to the gate. Not aloud. Down the eleven-year thread of hunger that connected them.

*I know what you want,* she told it. *I'm going to give it to you. But not their way. Mine.*

The gate's attention shifted — infinitesimally, enormously — like a vast animal deciding which hand to trust.

The chamber doors burst open.

For one insane moment Marguerite thought it was more soldiers, more guild enforcers, the whole crown arriving at once. But the figures flooding down the stair wore no uniforms and no robes — lean, fog-marked, wolf-branded, armed with iron and desperation. Survivors of the raid on the camp. The pack. Led by a scarred woman whose name Marguerite had never learned, who took one look at the chamber and howled a word that was not a word, and every brand in the room — Corren's, the pack's, and deep and unwilling, Seraphine Vale's — answered it.

The rite's circle cracked.

Gold light sprayed from a fracture in the seventh sigil like blood from a severed artery, and Corvane screamed — not in pain, in *accounting panic*, a man watching his ledgers catch fire — and turned to Marguerite with his warmth finally burned away, and what was underneath was forty years of fear.

"Hold the rite!" he shrieked. "Hold it, hold it, if the gate opens fully everything comes through, everything, do you understand, the *dead*, the *names*, the country itself will pour through that door and unmake—"

"Yes," Marguerite said.

She got one knee under her. The fractured circle sputtered around her, masters shouting, wolves closing, Corren bleeding and grinning and fighting his way toward her through white fire, and above it all the gate stood silent and black and waiting, its surface rippling now, absences moving behind the glass like fish rising toward a thrown crust of bread.

Marguerite rose to her feet inside the broken rite, tore the copied pages from inside her coat, and looked at the blank space at the bottom of the elder's final page — the space left, she understood at last, for exactly one line.

Across the chamber, Corren fought free of two masters, hurled himself the last ten yards, and skidded to a halt at the circle's edge, chest heaving, blood sheeting down his arm.

"Whatever you're about to do," he panted, "do it fast."

Beyond the gate, something on the other side pressed its palm — or what had once been a palm — flat against the black glass from within.

And the glass began, very softly, to sing.
