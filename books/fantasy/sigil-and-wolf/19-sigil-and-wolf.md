## 19. Sigil and Wolf

**Corren**

The gate's maw was not a place. That was the first thing I understood, falling into it.

It was a *remembering* — a vast interior weather of everything the Correction had swallowed, and I fell through it like a man dropped through cloud. Names went past me like birds startled from a reed bed. A woman's voice saying *supper's on*. A dog barking at a cart wheel. The smell of bread in a village that had not existed for forty years, except that it had always existed, pressed flat beneath the capital like a flower in a closed book.

I should have been torn apart. Anything human should have been.

But the wolf in me was not human, and the gate did not know what to do with it. It reached for my name — *Corren Ash, of nowhere, son of no one* — and found instead a thing that had never been written down. Wolves don't keep ledgers. My gift had been a curse my whole life: too wild to hide, too feral to be respectable, the reason every door in every decent town had closed to me before I'd learned to knock.

For the first time, it was the only reason I was still alive.

I fell through forty years of stolen country, and somewhere below me — below was a lie, but I clung to it, below, deeper, where she had gone — Marguerite Blackwood hung in the dark like a lantern.

I swam toward her. There was no other word for it. The gate resisted, pressing memories against me like hands trying to turn me around: my mother's face, which I had not thought of in fifteen years; the fog camp at dawn; Halloway's voice reciting the rite; Seraphine Vale's offer, *amnesty, Corren, just bring her in*, and my own voice saying *no* with such certainty that even now, drowning in everything, I could feel how true it had been.

The gate showed me my refusals as if they were debts. Maybe they were.

"MARGUERITE!"

My voice came out wrong here — flattened, spread thin across the dark. But she turned. Of course she turned. She had spent her whole life listening for the sound of things going wrong in the dark, and I had been the loudest wrong thing she'd ever let close.

She was standing on nothing. Her hair had come loose from its braid and moved around her face as though she stood underwater, and her eyes were wide and wet and absolutely clear. Behind her, higher up, impossibly high, the guild masters were small figures at the gate's rim, their arms raised, their mouths moving through the binding rite — and the rite was failing. I could see it failing. Threads of light that should have knotted themselves around Marguerite kept unraveling, sliding off her like water off oiled leather.

Because you cannot bind someone who is already giving herself away.

"Corren." She said my name like a hand catching mine. "You shouldn't be here. It eats names."

"I know." I came to a stop in front of her, or near enough — there was no floor, but we stood anyway, because we decided to. "Good thing I never had one worth eating."

That got half a laugh out of her, cracked down the middle. Then her face folded, and she grabbed the front of my shirt with both hands, and for one moment the whole collapsing universe narrowed to the grip of her fists and the fury in her eyes.

"You *left*," she said. "You were taken and I believed — Corren, I believed you sold me. I walked into this alone because I thought—"

"I know. Vale told me what she'd let you think. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it cost you—" I gestured at everything, at the maw, at the rite, at the country bleeding back into being around us. "I ran the whole way here. Three days. I broke out of the guildhouse and I didn't stop, and I brought you this."

I held up the ledger page. It glowed faintly in the gate-dark, ink rising off it like heat shimmer. Halloway's own hand, forty years old, the founding entry:

*Let the door be sealed by consent alone. No lock holds against a willing key. The Correction stands so long as no name is freely given to balance the taking.*

Marguerite read it twice. I watched her read it, watched her lips move on the last line, watched her arrive where I had arrived somewhere on the road, running, with wolves at my heels and my blood singing.

"It was never a place," she said quietly. "The door out. Everyone's been looking for a door out — the pack, the maps, me. And it was never north, never past the border, never anywhere you could walk to." She looked up at me. "It's a transaction. The Correction was a theft. Forty years ago they took a whole country, and theft unbalances a ledger forever. The gate has been hungry ever since because the books don't balance. It's been trying to eat its way back to zero."

"And the way out—"

"—is to pay the debt. Freely. A name, given to the ledger, willingly, to balance what was taken." Her voice didn't shake. That frightened me more than screaming would have. "One name freely given seals the taking. Closes the gate. Restores what was erased."

"One name," I said. "Whose?"

She looked at me the way she used to look at entries that wouldn't balance — patient, thorough, already knowing the answer and hating the arithmetic.

"Mine," said Marguerite Blackwood, keeper of the ledgers, and smiled at me like it was simple.

* * *

**Marguerite**

He said no. Of course he said no. He said it loudly, and then he said it again while grabbing for the page, and I held it above my head like a child keeping a sweet from a sibling, which would have been absurd if the absurdity weren't wrapped around something unbearable.

"Give it to me, Marguerite."

"No."

"There has to be another way—"

"There isn't. I've had time to look. More time than I wanted." Around us the gate churned, and I made myself watch it, made myself see what I was seeing: faces in the dark. Thousands of them. The erased ones, pressing close, not menacing — *pleading*. A farmer mouthing a word over and over. A girl holding up a slate with a name chalked on it, showing it to anyone who would look. They weren't ghosts. They were *entries waiting to be restored*, and they had waited forty years, and every year the guild masters had fed the gate a few gifted souls to keep it quiet instead of paying what was owed.

"They took a country," I said to him. "And they've been paying the interest in people. In us. Every gifted child taken for 'examination' — that's what the crown hunts us for, Corren. Not fear. *Bookkeeping.* The gate must be fed or the Correction sours, and feeding it is cheaper than settling it." My voice came out steady. Good. One of us should sound like an accountant. "A freely given name balances the account. One name, given once, and it ends. No more hunts. No more examinations. The country comes back — all of it, everyone they erased, everyone whose names were struck. And the price is that the name given is struck too. Struck from every record, every memory, every mind that ever held it."

"Not mine," he said. "Not yours. We find another way. There's always—"

"Corren." I took his face in my hands. His jaw was rough with three days of road, and there was a cut above his eyebrow that needed cleaning, and I wanted, violently, stupidly, to have time for all of it. "You told me once that your gift made you nothing to polite people. Unwritten. You survived the gate's throat because you were never in any ledger to begin with." I felt him understand, felt it land in him like a stone in still water, and I kept talking before he could argue, because if I stopped talking I would start weeping and I had work to do. "Mine is the opposite. I have been *written* since the day I was born. Keeper. Accountant. The guild's own signature on its own crime. If anyone's name can pay this debt, it's mine. It was always going to be mine. I think maybe it was always why I could feel the gate hungry — it's been calling in its own collateral."

Above us, the rite collapsed.

I heard it happen — Halloway's voice cracking mid-phrase, the great knotting light shredding apart — and then I heard something worse: laughter. Thin, dry, disbelieving. Master Halloway, looking down into the maw at the two of us, understanding at last what the page in my hand meant.

"No," he said. "No — the seal was to hold *forever*, the terms were fixed, you cannot simply *pay*—"

"The terms were always fixed, Master," I called up, and my voice carried strangely, carried everywhere, because inside the gate every voice is the ledger's voice. "You just never read them. You wrote *freely given* into your own founding entry and then spent forty years making sure no one would ever give anything freely. You built a lock that only kindness could open, and then you made kindness illegal."

The guild masters looked at each other. And I saw the moment the gate understood them.

It rose around them like water finding a crack. All those years of appetite, all those borrowed years and bottled storms and eaten names, all of it turning at once toward the men who had taught it hunger — and the gate did not rage. That was the terrible thing. It was gentle. It gathered them the way a ledger gathers entries: this one owed this much, and this one, and this one. Halloway screamed about contracts, about his rights, about the *terms*, and the gate agreed with him completely, and gave him exactly the terms he had written. They had fed it unwilling things for forty years. Now it accepted them, wholly, as payment on account.

They did not fall so much as become less. Their outlines softened into ink. Their voices thinned into marginalia. The last thing I saw of Master Halloway was his mouth still moving, correcting something, right up until there was no mouth and nothing left to correct.

The chamber above went quiet. Somewhere far away, I thought I heard Seraphine Vale — alive, free, walking away up the stairs with her programmed cruelty finally switched off, choosing the open air. Good. Let her have it. Let her learn to be someone new.

Then it was just us. Two people standing in the throat of a god-shaped accounting error, holding a single page between them.

* * *

**Corren**

She turned to the blank space at the bottom of the page, and I caught her wrist.

"Wait." My voice broke on it. I didn't care. "If this works — if it takes your name out of every head, every book, every memory — then I lose you. Not to death. To *nothing*. I'll stand in a room with you tomorrow and not know you." My hands were shaking. I let them. "There has to be something that survives. Some entry the ledger can't reach."

She went very still. I watched her mind move — fast, precise, the finest accountant the Vellum Guild ever had — and I watched her find it, because she was who she was.

"A sigil," she whispered. "Everyone carries sigils, Corren. Marks, tokens, brands. Things that mean a person without *being* their name. The ledger strikes names. It has no jurisdiction over meaning." Her eyes came up to mine, bright and fierce and wet. "Your wolf-mark. The one on the sigil-stone you tried to sell me, the one burned into my oldest memory. Give it to me. Not the stone — the *meaning*. Hold my sigil in your memory and refuse to let it go, and when everything else is struck, that will remain. Not my name. Just... me. The shape of me. Carried by someone who chooses to keep it."

"That's not enough," I said. "That's a ghost. That's a stranger wearing your smile who I'll grieve without knowing why—"

"It's everything," she said. "It's more than the erased ones got. It's more than anyone gets. Corren — love isn't a name. Love is what remembers when the name is gone. That's the counter-spell. That's the whole of it. Everything the guild built ran on taking. This runs on *giving*. So give."

I pressed my forehead to hers. The gate roared softly around us, patient now, an old animal that smelled the end of its long hunger. Below us — below, deeper, wherever the deepest of it lay — I could feel the erased country straining toward restoration like green shoots under snow.

"How do I hold it?" I asked. "Tell me exactly. You're the keeper. I'm just the wolf."

"You already know how," she said. "You've been doing it since the counting house. Think of the mark. The wolf under the moon-slit. Say it in your head until it's not words anymore, just... weight. And then don't ever put it down."

So I did. I closed my eyes and I took the sigil — her sigil, the mark that had been mine and hers and ours since a smuggler laid a stolen stone on a counting-house desk and a careful woman flinched at it like a memory — and I set it down inside myself, in the deepest room, behind the door that doesn't open for kings or witchfinders or gates. I felt it settle. I felt it *take root*.

When I opened my eyes she was writing.

Her finger moved through the air and ink followed it, black and silver, the ledger's own substance obeying its keeper one last time. She wrote her name into the blank space — all of it, every letter, unhurried, beautiful, the penmanship of a woman balancing the final account of her life — and then she drew the line through it.

One stroke. Clean. Final.

The gate *inhaled*.

Every erased thing rushed home at once. I felt it pass through me like a flood through a valley — names returning to mouths, roads returning to maps, a whole country pouring back into itself with a sound like ten thousand bells and one long exhale. The faces in the dark lit up and let go. The girl with the slate laughed. The farmer said his word — *home*, it was *home* — and was suddenly, gloriously, a man again, standing in a kitchen that existed again, in a village that existed again, in a nation that remembered its own name.

And Marguerite—

Marguerite grew bright. Brighter than the gate, brighter than the restoration, a figure of white fire standing in the closing dark, and she looked at me across the last few feet of everything, and she was smiling, and she was already fading, and she said —

she said, "Don't put it down."

— and the light took her, gently, the way sleep takes a tired child, and the gate sealed with a sound like a book closing, and the world went white, and then quiet, and then ordinary.

I was on my knees on cold stone in the gate chamber beneath the counting house. Dust settled. The great door was smooth and dead and shut, just a wall again, just stone. Above me, dawn was coming through the grate, and somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour, and nobody in the whole restored country knew what had been paid in this room in the night.

I reached into the deepest room behind the door that doesn't open.

The sigil was there. The wolf under the moon-slit. Heavy. Rooted. Mine to carry.

I didn't know whose it was.

But I knew I would never put it down.
