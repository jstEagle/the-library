## 20. The Price of a Name

The gate was still open.

It hung in the vaulted dark beneath the counting house like a wound that had finally been allowed to speak — a door-shaped absence filled not with stone or night but with everything the Correction had swallowed. Names poured through it. I could hear them the way other people heard rain: a vast, patient rainfall of syllables, forty years of stolen selves falling back into the world.

The guild masters had done this. Three of them remained standing around the rite-circle, robes scorched to their bodies, their faces lit from below by the gate's pale hunger. The fourth was ash on the floor; the gate did not distinguish between those who fed it and those it merely wanted. Seraphine Vale lay crumpled at the circle's edge where she had turned her own witchfinder's iron against the men who had programmed her, and she was breathing, barely, and looking at me with eyes that belonged to no one yet.

And Corren — Corren was holding my hand so hard his knuckles had gone white, as if the open gate might take me the way it took names, one letter at a time.

"It's too much," he said. His voice came out scraped raw. The wolf-sigil on his forearm glowed through the torn sleeve, answering the gate like a struck bell answers another bell. "Marguerite. Look at it. You can't put that back in a bottle."

"I'm not putting it back," I said. "I'm giving it a name."

The ledger was in my satchel, where it had been since the night I first committed treason over its pages. It weighed nothing now. That was the trick of impossible things — at the moment you needed them most, they became weightless, because they had already become part of you.

The masters saw what I meant to do before I finished walking toward them. Master Ellery-Vane, the eldest, whose hands had signed off on bottled storms and borrowed years and the quiet erasure of a country, raised his head. There was no fear in his face. There was something worse: relief.

"You understand," he whispered. "You've always understood. That's why we chose you."

"No," I said. "You didn't choose me. My mother paid for me. She crossed the border with a baby and a name, and your Correction took both tolls, and you built a lock out of what was left." I opened the ledger across my knee. "I'm not your keeper. I never was. I'm just the last person who read the accounts."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about ledgers: they are prayers written in arithmetic. Every entry is a small act of faith that the world can be made to balance. I had spent eleven years believing that, ink-stained and proud of my neat hand, and even now — standing at the mouth of an open wound in reality — some stubborn clerk in my chest wanted to do it *properly*. Date the entry. Note the transaction. Record the price.

So I did.

The pages fell open to themselves. Of course they did; the book knew what was coming the way the gate knew hunger. The columns that had held bottled storms and borrowed years now ran wild with restoration — rivers re-entering the record, villages writing themselves home, the dead demanding their dates back. And beneath all of it, in the margin where corrections were traditionally noted, one line waited like an empty chair:

*Received in trade: one name, freely given.*

My pen shook. Not from fear. From the sheer clerical enormity of what I was about to write. Somewhere behind me Corren made a sound — not a word, just a sound, the sound a wolf makes when it watches something it loves walk toward a trap it cannot spring.

"Wait." He caught my wrist. His grip burned; the sigil under his skin was awake now, fully awake, the suppressed gift of his whole life roaring up through him. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. You taught me that — you said the books only look closed until someone reads them properly—"

"That's exactly why it works," I said gently. "Corren. Look at me."

He looked at me. Grey eyes, wolf-marked, the first person who'd ever recognized what I was before I knew myself. Behind him the gate roared softly with forty years of returning voices, and I understood suddenly, completely, what my mother must have felt at the border — standing between the country that would forget her and the child she carried into forgetting, choosing which loss she could survive.

"The gate doesn't want power," I said. "It never wanted power. The guild fed it power for forty years and it stayed hungry, because that's not what doors are *for*. A door wants to be closed by someone on the right side of it. Freely. With a name given, not taken." I turned my hand over and laced my fingers through his. "That's why they could never seal it themselves. Everything the guild gave it was stolen, and stolen things don't close doors."

"And yours?"

"Mine is mine." I smiled, and found to my surprise that it wasn't a brave smile. It was a real one. "It's the only thing I have that was never theirs. They erased my country, my mother's name, my birthday, my first word — but Marguerite Blackwood, keeper of ledgers, woman who likes her tea too strong and her columns straight — that name they let me keep. Forty years of interest." I looked at the page. "Time to spend it."

"Don't." His voice broke clean in half. "Marguerite—"

"Listen to me." I pulled the sigil-stone from my neck — the wolf-marked stone he'd tried to sell me a lifetime ago, in a counting house full of orderly dust — and pressed it into his palm, folding his fingers shut around it. The stone was warm. It beat against his hand like a second heart. "This is how you keep me. Not memory — memories blur, memories get corrected. This. The mark itself. If you carry the sigil, then somewhere in the world there is one person who knows my name, and the ledger can't argue with a true account."

"That's not—" He was crying now, furiously, the way men cry when they've been taught crying is weakness and have lost the argument with themselves. "That's not fair. You don't get to make me the only—"

"I know." I kissed his knuckles, once, over the stone. "But you'll carry it anyway. Because you're the kind of man who carries things for people. It's your worst quality and the reason I love you."

His breath caught. Neither of us had said it before. Of course we saved it for this — for the exact moment when saying it was also leaving it, a deposit made into the only bank that wouldn't fail.

"I love you too," he said. "Come back and hear me say it when you're nobody. I'll say it to a stranger. I'll fall in love with whoever walks out of this fog, I swear it, if she looks anything like—"

"She won't," I said. "She'll be better. She won't be afraid of her own handwriting."

* * *

I wrote my name.

*Marguerite Blackwood,* in my careful clerk's hand, dated the old way, the way the guild had dated entries before the Correction — with the true calendar, the one that had kept counting beneath the rewritten one all these years, patient as roots under frost. *Received in trade: one name, freely given. Purpose: the sealing of the door. Terms: complete.*

Then I drew the line through it.

The strike-through is the oldest entry in any ledger. Older than numbers. One stroke means: *this is finished, this is settled, look elsewhere.* My pen moved through my own name and the world moved with it.

The gate screamed — not in rage but in recognition, the sound of a lock finding its key after forty years of picking. Light didn't pour out of it; light poured *into* it, all the borrowed brightness of the Correction folding home like a letter returned to sender. The returning names rose to a crescendo — I heard my mother's name, I heard it clearly, a woman's voice saying *Vessaline* in a language I had never learned but somehow already mourned — and then the country took a breath.

Not the capital. Not the crown. The *country*. The land beneath our feet remembered itself the way a sleeper remembers a dream upon waking: wholly, instantly, with tears. Through the shrinking gate I saw it happen — borders drawing themselves back like scars fading, roads unblurring, the fog along the borderlands standing up and becoming hills again, becoming farms, becoming a nation with a name that had been waiting in every erased map like a word on the tip of the world's tongue.

And the guild masters — the three who remained — stood inside their own rite-circle as the gate drank the last of what it was owed. Master Ellery-Vane looked down at his hands and found them filling with the memories of everyone his signatures had unmade, and I watched him understand, in his final moment, that he had spent forty years manufacturing a debt he could never have balanced. The gate took them the way a river takes footprints: without malice, without pause. They went into the closing door and the door closed on them, and the sound it made was the sound of a very large book being shut by someone who has finally finished reading.

Then: silence. Dust settling in a vaulted room. An ordinary cellar, smelling of stone and cold iron and, faintly, of rain on far-off hills.

Corren was on his knees, the sigil-stone clenched to his chest, staring at the place where the gate had been.

At me.

I watched it happen. I watched his eyes find me and slide off me like rain off glass — once, twice, three times, his gaze snagging on the space where I stood and refusing to hold. His lips moved. He was trying to say my name and there was nothing there to say. The ledger had collected. Even from him. Almost.

Almost — because his fist was still closed around the stone, and the stone was still warm, and when his hand trembled open I saw the wolf-mark on his skin glowing faintly, steadily, like an ember that had decided not to die.

He looked at the mark. He looked at me. And though his face held no recognition — though to him I was a stranger standing in a ruined cellar, a woman with ink-stained fingers and no name at all — something in him, deeper than memory, older than correction, made him rise slowly to his feet and hold the stone out toward me.

"I think," he said, frowning at himself, at the words coming out of him unbidden, "I think this is yours."

* * *

Seraphine Vale woke at the edge of the rite-circle while Corren was still staring at his own hand.

She sat up with the terrible grace of a puppet whose strings had been cut cleanly, and I understood what she was feeling before she said a word, because I had felt it too, watching the gate close: the sudden unbearable lightness of a mind that belonged to itself for the first time. Her programmed certainties were gone. Her crown-issued memories were gone. Whatever the witchfinder had been before the throne built her, she was meeting that woman now for the very first time, in the ruins of the thing that made her.

Her eyes found the space where the gate had been. Then me. Then Corren. Her expression moved through grief, fury, wonder, and landed — deliberately, I could see her steering it — on blankness.

"What happened here?" she asked. Flat. Almost bored.

Corren started to answer. I touched his arm.

"There was an accident," I said. "A fire. The guild is gone."

Vale studied me for a long moment. She had spent her whole life reading truths off skin like braille; whatever she read off mine now, she chose — *chose*, the way you choose to close a book you know will break you — to set aside.

"A fire," she repeated. She got to her feet, brushed ash from her coat, and straightened the witchfinder's badge on her lapel with two precise motions before tearing it off and dropping it on the floor. "Then there's nothing to investigate. There never is." At the stair she paused, her back to us, her silhouette thin against the grey light rising from above. "Whoever you are," she said, without turning, "I hope you were worth whatever it cost. Someone paid for this room. I intend to never find out who."

She climbed the stairs into the morning and did not look back, and the door at the top closed behind her like the final line of an entry marked *settled.*

* * *

We came out of the counting house into a city that was remembering itself.

It was noon. Bells were ringing from towers that hadn't rung in forty years — bells whose bronze bore inscriptions newly legible, dedications in a script scholars would spend decades learning to read again. People stood in the streets holding their heads, laughing, weeping, calling out names they'd just recovered like coins fallen behind a wall. An old woman clutched a shopfront sign and sobbed at the letters. Children ran past wearing names that had arrived overnight, trying them on like new coats. Somewhere a cart horse stood placidly in a road that had not existed yesterday, eating grass that had grown there all along.

Nobody knew me. That was the shape of the bargain, and I felt it settle over me not like a wound but like a cloak — the particular freedom of the unnamed. A guard looked straight through me. A baker handed Corren bread when he stumbled, and never asked his business. I was a stranger everywhere, invisible as fog, free as fog, and the grief of it was real and enormous and it was *mine*, the first possession I'd ever owned outright.

Corren walked beside me because the sigil-stone told him to. He didn't know why. He kept glancing at me sideways with the baffled, unwilling attention of a man drawn to a stranger against every instinct, and each time he caught himself doing it, he scowled at the stone in his fist.

"We should get off the street," he said finally. "Crowds. After whatever happened down there, someone will come asking questions."

"You don't remember me," I said. It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway, because apparently he couldn't help himself.

"No." His jaw tightened. "Should I? Do you know me?" He looked at the stone again, turning it over. The wolf-mark caught the sun. "I woke up in a cellar with this thing and a hole in my head where a name should go. There's a name missing. I can feel it, like — like a tooth. And every time I look at you, the tooth aches." He stopped in the middle of the remembering street, bells ringing overhead, and stared at me with something between suspicion and surrender. "Who are you?"

I smiled. Somewhere in the restored country, in a village that had its river back, an old woman named Vessaline was probably telling her neighbors about the daughter she'd lost to the fog forty years ago — a daughter who had a name, a real name, if anyone ever thought to ask the ledgers of a certain ruined guild. Maybe someday. Maybe someone would dig through the ashes and find the last page and read it properly, the way I'd taught one smuggler to read things properly.

But that was later. That was another chapter.

Right now the country was waking, the bells were ringing, and a wolf-marked man was looking at me like I was an account he couldn't balance.

"No one," I said, and held out my hand. "I'm no one. Yet."

He hesitated — then shook it, his grip warm and strong and aching with a tooth-shaped absence, and together we walked into the fog that was lifting.
