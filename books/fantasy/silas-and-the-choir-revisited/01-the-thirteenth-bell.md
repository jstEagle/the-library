## 1. The Thirteenth Bell

The bells of Vellamere rang twelve times at midnight. Everyone knew this the way everyone knew that rivers ran downhill and that Master Corvane's bread was best bought stale and toasted fresh. Twelve bells for twelve hours, twelve wards for twelve gates, twelve gods whose names had been politely retired from active worship but still appeared on coins and in wedding vows out of habit more than faith.

So when the bells rang a thirteenth time, Isandro Nystrom did what any sensible keeper of impossible things would do.

He wrote it down.

* * *

The Ledgerhouse stood on Candlewick Lane, which was not where anyone looked for anything important. That was rather the point. To the casual eye it was a counting-house of modest ambition: gray stone, green shutters, a brass plaque reading *NYSTROM & ASSOCIATES, LEDGERS KEPT, ACCOUNTS BALANCED*. The *Associates* were three people and a kitchen boy, and the accounts balanced because Isandro spent four hours every night making certain they did.

He worked in the long room behind the front office, at a desk older than the city walls, beneath a window that looked out on nothing in particular — a courtyard wall, a drainpipe, a slice of sky. The room smelled of ink and tallow and, faintly, of Maren's pipe, though she'd been ordered years ago to smoke only in the appraisal room and had agreed with the particular sincerity of a one-eyed woman who intended to keep doing exactly as she pleased.

Isandro dipped his pen. The night's entries waited in their neat stack, each slip of paper a small confession from the day's trade.

A merchant had paid six silver to have his rival's shipping manifest *misplaced* — recorded, filed, and rendered so boringly accurate that no customs officer would ever read past the third line. A young couple had purchased a certified record of a debt forgiven, which is to say a piece of paper stating plainly that no debt existed, which is to say magic of the dullest and most reliable kind. Bramble had taken a toll from a smuggler in the form of two casks of honey-wine, which Isandro recorded under *BEVERAGES, PROCURED, ETHICALLY AMBIGUOUS*, because he was a professional.

Routine. All of it routine. Isandro found deep comfort in routine; it was the mortar between the stones of a life he had built very carefully over forty years, stone by quiet stone, so that no crack would ever show and no light would ever get in.

He was halfway through the second stack when he found the entry that stopped his pen mid-stroke.

It sat between the manifest job and the debt-forgiveness, written in the ledger itself — not on a slip, not in Wren's careful apprentice hand or Maren's slashing script or Oskar's blocky, laborious letters. It was simply *there*, in a hand none of them used, fine and slanted and old-fashioned, the way people had written before the printing presses came and made handwriting lazy.

*Prayer received: one (1). Petitioner: Edda Marsh, widow, Orchard Row. Requested: rain. Granted.*

Below it, in the same hand: *Payment deferred.*

Isandro read it three times. Then he did what he always did when the world misbehaved: he checked the arithmetic.

The guild's books balanced by law older than the guild itself. Every miracle traded through the Ledgerhouse required a miracle given up — a blessing banked against a blessing drawn, a wonder owed against a wonder paid. In three hundred years the house had never once let an account run open. That was the whole secret of the place, the reason it existed at all: not to trade in impossible things, but to make sure nobody else did either. The Ledgerhouse kept the market closed by keeping the books perfect.

And here was an open account. Rain granted, payment deferred, no clerk's mark, no appraiser's seal, no counterweight entered anywhere in the columns.

Isandro set down his pen, took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on. The entry remained. Of course it remained. Spectacles fixed eyesight, not reality, though there had been a week in his youth when he'd have paid handsomely for the reverse.

"Right," he said quietly, to the empty room, because saying things aloud helped him think and because after forty years alone with the books he had stopped being embarrassed about it. "Either someone has broken into my ledger, learned our cipher, forged Maren's paper stock, and left a joke in period-accurate calligraphy — or something has started writing its own entries."

Neither option was good. One was merely criminal. The other was the thing the guild had spent three centuries ensuring could never happen.

He turned back a page. Then another. He went through the last month of entries with the patience of a man defusing something, and found four more like it. A prayer for a safe birth, granted. A prayer for a son home from the fishing fleets, granted. A prayer for a fever to break — granted, though the petitioner's name was smudged, as if the ink itself couldn't quite hold onto it. Each one marked *granted*. Not one of them marked *paid*.

Five open miracles. Five debts accumulating interest somewhere Isandro couldn't see.

Outside, the city murmured in its sleep. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing, then thought better of it.

Isandro finished balancing the books — because he was who he was, and the world could be ending and he would still balance the books first — and then he sat for a long while with his hands folded, listening to the building settle around him. Above him, faintly, came the thump-thump-thump of Oskar turning over in his cot in the attic, dreaming whatever kitchen boys dreamed. From the rooms behind, Maren's snore rose and fell like weather. Wren slept in the clerks' alcove, no doubt with a pen still inked in her fist; he'd have to check in the morning, before she ruined another pillowcase.

His family, all of them, asleep and unaware. He felt the familiar tightening in his chest that he refused, on principle, to name.

"Nothing is fraying," he told the ceiling. "Nothing is fraying, and you are an old man who needs sleep."

The ceiling declined to comment. Ceilings, at least, kept proper silence.

* * *

He was wrong about the fraying, of course. He knew he was wrong even as he said it. You did not survive forty years in the trade of impossible things by ignoring the itch at the back of the neck, and the itch tonight was less an itch than a hand.

Because here was the other thing, the thing he hadn't written down yet, the thing he'd been circling all evening like a moth around a lamp it suspected might be fire:

At midnight, the bells of Vellamere had rung thirteen times.

He had counted. He always counted. And he had stood at his window with the cold air on his face and listened to the thirteenth bell — a low, bronze note that no bell in the city should have been able to make, because it came from everywhere and nowhere, from the direction of the palace and the harbor and the catacombs beneath the temple district all at once — and he had watched the sound move across the rooftops like something *swimming*.

And he had seen, for just a moment, before the clouds closed again, that the sky above the capital held one moon too many.

It had been thin as a paring of bone, white as unmarked paper, hanging in a quarter of the sky where no moon had any business hanging. Most people hadn't noticed. People rarely noticed the impossible until it knocked on their door and asked to be let in. But Isandro had spent his whole life noticing, and so he had stood there in the dark with his heart going like a drummer boy's and done the only sensible thing.

He had gone back inside, locked the door, and balanced the books.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Isandro was at his desk in the front office when Wren came down the stairs two at a time, seventeen years old and built entirely of elbows and enthusiasm, her hair escaping its braid in every direction like a hedge deciding to be free.

"You're up early," she said, skidding to a stop. "You're *always* up early. Why am I surprised? Good morning, Master Nystrom. There's a woman outside."

"It's barely dawn."

"She says it's urgent. She's got a basket." Wren lowered her voice with the delicacy of a falling bookshelf. "I think she wants to sell something she shouldn't have."

"What makes you say that?"

"Because she keeps looking over her shoulder, and because she asked me if we were 'the kind of house that asks questions,' and I said we were the kind of house that *balances* them, and she said, 'That'll do.'" Wren shrugged. "Also she's crying a bit. But quietly. Like she's practiced."

Isandro closed the ledger in front of him — the clean one, the public one — and felt the private one sitting heavy in the drawer beneath his elbow, five unpaid miracles fat as ticks.

"Show her in," he said. "And put the kettle on. People cry better with tea."

"That's not how crying works."

"In my experience it is exactly how crying works."

She grinned and vanished toward the kitchen, and a moment later Oskar appeared in the doorway as if summoned by the word *kettle*, already carrying cups. The boy was fourteen and had never spoken a word in his life, and communicated in a fluent language of raised eyebrows and precise gestures that the household had all become conversant in without ever agreeing to learn it. He set down four cups, looked at Isandro, tapped his own chest twice, and pointed at the office door.

"Yes," Isandro said. "You listen at the door. Subtly."

Oskar gave him a look of mild offense, tapped his chest twice more, and pointed at the door again, then at his ear, then made a gesture that Isandro chose to interpret as *I will be extremely subtle* and feared actually meant *I will stand directly against the door where I can hear everything*.

"Subtly," Isandro repeated, and Oskar nodded with the solemnity of a knight accepting a quest.

The widow came in like winter. She was perhaps fifty, dressed in good black gone gray at the seams, and she carried her basket the way people carry things they cannot put down. Her face had the particular geography of someone who had cried herself into a new shape and was now living in it.

"Master Nystrom," she said. "They said — the fishwife on the corner, she said if a thing needed doing that couldn't be done, your house was the door that opened anyway."

"The fishwife flatters us." Isandro gestured to the chair across the desk. "Please. Sit. Tell me what can't be done."

She sat. She looked at her hands. When she spoke, her voice was steady in the way of a rope holding weight — taut, and aware of its limits.

"My orchard is dying. Forty trees my husband planted the year we married, and the blight's taken half, and the rest are going brown at the edges like paper near a flame. I've prayed." She laughed, short and bitter. "I haven't prayed since I was a girl, you understand. The gods retired, everyone says so. But I went out under the oldest tree and I knelt in the mud and I prayed anyway, because there was nothing left to lose except the kneeling."

Isandro's hands went very still on the desk.

"And?" he said. Carefully. So carefully.

"And it rained." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and frightened and shining all at once. "Only over my orchard, Master Nystrom. Only over my forty trees. I watched it fall in a circle around my land like rain poured from a jug, and the neighbors' fields stayed dry, and my trees — my trees are *green*, sir. Green overnight. And I don't know what I've done. I don't know what answered. And the fishwife said maybe your house would know, and maybe your house would help, and I don't have money, but I have—" she lifted the basket, and inside it lay a folded cloth, and beneath the cloth something moved, soft and slow, like a sleeping animal, like a shadow shifting in its sleep— "I have this. It came off. When the rain fell, it came right off me, and I can't—" her voice cracked, steadied, cracked again. "I can't seem to pick it up. It won't stay picked up."

In the drawer beneath his elbow, Isandro's private ledger seemed suddenly very loud, though ledgers make no sound at all. *Prayer received: one (1). Petitioner: Edda Marsh, widow, Orchard Row.* He had read her name in a dead language's handwriting eight hours before she walked through his door.

Through the office door, very subtly, came the sound of Oskar pressing himself flat against the wood.

Isandro rose, crossed the room, and gently lifted the cloth.

Beneath it lay a shadow. Hers — he knew it the way you know your own signature — curled up small in the bottom of the basket like a cat that had been put out in the cold. It stirred when the light touched it and pressed itself into the corner, away from the sun, and it was the loneliest thing he had ever seen.

"Well," said Isandro Nystrom, last oath-bound swordspeaker, keeper of a house that existed so that this exact thing would never happen, feeling the invisible walls of his careful life begin, at long last, to show their seams. "Let's talk about what answered your prayer. Because I believe I may have read its handwriting."

The widow stared at him. Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell that belonged to no tower began — softly, patiently, like something counting — to ring.
