## 2. A Trade in Rain

The Ledgerhouse kept no windows on its ground floor, because a house that traded in impossible things could not afford to be seen doing it. But Isandro Nystrom had spent forty years learning the building's moods through other senses: the way the floorboards ticked when the humidity shifted and the ledgers swelled in their shelves like sleeping animals; the particular quality of silence that meant Maren was appraising something dangerous one floor up; and now, this morning, a smell.

Rain. In the entry hall. Where no rain had fallen.

Isandro stood at the top of the stairs with a cup of tea going cold in their hand and listened to the sound of water drumming gently on flagstones that had been dry since yesterday's sweeping. It was not an alarming sound, exactly. It was a *familiar* sound, which was worse. Isandro had heard rain indoors exactly twice before in their long career, and both times had ended with paperwork so complicated that Bramble still complained about it at dinner, eleven years later.

"Oskar," Isandro called down the stairwell, "if you've left the scrying basin full again—"

Oskar did not answer, because Oskar never answered, being mute, but also because Oskar was almost certainly still asleep, being fifteen. The kitchen boy slept like the dead and communicated primarily through drawings, gestures of tremendous eloquence, and the strategic deployment of his enormous brown eyes. He was, in Isandro's private assessment, the only member of the household with any sense whatsoever, which he expressed exclusively by staying out of everyone else's business.

The rain-sound stopped as Isandro descended. That was wrong too. Rain did not stop because someone was coming. Rain did not have manners.

In the entry hall, the flagstones were dark and wet in a neat circle perhaps four paces across, already drying at the edges, and standing at the center of it — dripping slightly, wringing out her shawl with shaking hands — was a woman Isandro recognized from the guild rolls. Widow Sefeya Corren, of the orchard holdings east of the city walls. Fifty-eight years old. Two entries in the ledger, both small trades, both honorable: a year of her mother's recipes for a frost-charm, and her grandmother's lullabies for a luck-blessing on a birthing. She had come to the Ledgerhouse twice before and paid fairly and left quietly, which made her, in Isandro's professional opinion, one of the finest clients a house could ask for.

She should not have been able to get inside. The door did not open for anyone without an appointment, and appointments were made by letter, sealed, and answered by clerk. Isandro had answered no letters this week. Isandro had certainly not granted an audience at dawn, alone, unchaperoned, in the entry hall, which was against eleven separate protocols that Isandro themselves had written.

"Widow Corren," Isandro said carefully. "You're dripping on my floor."

"I'm sorry." Her voice came out cracked. "I couldn't — they told me the door would open if I *asked* hard enough."

"Who told you that?"

Her eyes went somewhere over Isandro's shoulder, into the middle distance, into whatever she had been listening to these past weeks. "I prayed," she said simply. "I prayed every night for a month. And then last night, something answered. Something said—" She stopped, swallowed. "It said the old doors still worked, if you knew how to knock."

*The old doors.* Isandro felt the words settle into their chest like stones dropped into a well. There were old doors under the Ledgerhouse, sealed before Isandro's time, sealed before Maren's time, sealed by founders whose names had been struck from the rolls with a care so thorough it amounted to reverence. No one living knew how to knock on them.

No one living. Which raised the question of what had been talking to Sefeya Corren.

"Tell me what happened," Isandro said, and set down the tea, and took out the day-ledger, because ritual was armor and Isandro wore theirs daily. "Slowly. From the beginning."

* * *

The beginning, as Sefeya told it, was an orchard dying of thirst.

Three summers of thin rain had shrunk the Corren apples to knots of bitter wood. She had tried everything a sensible woman could try: deep wells, hired water-mages, prayers to the river-god whose temple taxes she'd paid for thirty years. The water-mages took her coin and shrugged. The river-god's priests took her offerings and sent back a form letter explaining that drought was part of the divine plan and suggesting, helpfully, that she consider diversifying into root vegetables.

"So I got angry," Sefeya said. She had stopped wringing her shawl and was gripping it instead, knuckles white. "Angry enough to do what my grandmother always said never to do. Angry enough to pray to the *old* ones. The ones before the temples. The ones with no priests to take a cut." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I figured they couldn't be worse than the form letters."

"What did you ask for?"

"Rain. Just rain. Over my orchard, nowhere else, nothing fancy." She looked down at her hands. "And then something spoke. Not in my ears. In my *bones*. It asked what I'd give. And I said — I didn't think, Master Nystrom, I swear I didn't think — I said, anything, take anything, just let the trees live."

Isandro's pen had stopped moving. They noticed this and forced it onward, recording, because recording was the discipline that held the world's edges together. "And it named its price."

"It said: your shadow. The one you cast at noon. Nothing else. You keep everything else — your years, your name, your children, your soul if you believe you have one. Just the noon-shadow." Her voice wavered. "I thought it was a bargain. I thought, what's a shadow? A shadow is nothing. A shadow is the thing that follows you around on sunny days and embarrasses you at dances."

"And you agreed."

"I agreed. And it rained." She gestured at the wet circle on the floor, at herself, at the whole impossible morning. "Not here. *There.* Over the orchard, just like I asked. My neighbor Vess swears it fell in a perfect ring around my property line — she watched it from her hill, says the edges were sharp as a tablecloth. Three inches of rain in an hour, warm as bathwater, and every tree standing in it lifted its branches like arms." Her face did something complicated, joy and horror fighting for the same ground. "My orchard is saved, Master Nystrom. My orchard is saved and I am standing in your hall at dawn because I walked here in the dark and there was no sun, and I kept looking behind me for my shadow anyway, out of habit, and it wasn't there, and I realized I will *never stop looking*, and I wanted — I needed — someone to write it down. To say it happened. To say I existed before I gave the nothing away."

Isandro looked at her feet. In the lamplight of the entry hall, lit from three sides by the wall-sconces that never went out, Widow Sefeya Corren cast no shadow at all. The light fell on her and simply continued to the floor, uninterrupted, as though she were made of glass, as though she were a rumor of a woman.

Professional unease curdled, slowly, into something more personal.

* * *

Here is what Isandro wrote in the day-ledger, in the careful hand that forty years of practice had worn smooth as river stone:

*Received of Sefeya Corren, widow, of the east orchards: one shadow, noon-cast, surrendered willingly. Paid by party unknown: rain, three inches, bounded precisely to the Corren holding, delivered within one hour of agreement. Witnessed: none. Brokered: none. Recorded post hoc.*

That last word was the wound of the entry. *Post hoc.* After the fact. The Ledgerhouse existed to stand between the world and the impossible things — to be the middleman, the weigher, the guarantor, the one who ensured that when a desperate person traded something precious, they were not cheated, and when something hungry bought something precious, it did not get seconds. Every trade in the guild's four-hundred-year history had passed through guild hands. That was the entire point. That was the covenant written into the founding charter in ink that was supposedly mixed with the blood of whatever the founders had imprisoned beneath the house.

This trade had passed through no hands but a god's.

Isandro closed the ledger and found Sefeya watching them with the expression of a woman who has already sold everything sellable and is now pricing the furniture of her own face.

"How much do I owe the house?" she asked. "For the recording. I know there's a fee. I'll pay it. I don't have much, but—"

"The fee is waived." Isandro heard themself say it and winced internally; protocol required fees; fees were how the house stayed invisible, how it paid its candles and its kitchen-boy and its appraiser's one good eye. But some transactions deserved to be recorded free, the way some wounds deserved clean bandages. "Recording a truth is not the same as brokering a trade. You didn't trade through us, Widow Corren. You couldn't have. What answered you went around us entirely."

"Is that bad?" she asked. "It sounded bad when you said it."

"It sounds," Isandro said, choosing each word the way a man chooses footing on a rotten bridge, "like a customer who has decided the shop is unnecessary."

Sefeya considered this. Then, with the blunt practicality that Isandro suspected had kept her orchard alive through three droughts and would likely keep her alive through considerably worse: "Well. Was it? Necessary, I mean. Because I'll tell you what I got from your shop, Master Nystrom. A frost-charm and a blessing on a birth, both fair, both honest, I'll say that to anyone who asks. But when my trees were dying, your shop sent me a form letter about root vegetables. Whatever I knocked on last night *opened the door*."

There was no answer to that which was both true and safe, so Isandro gave the truest unsafe one. "No. Perhaps it wasn't necessary. Perhaps it never was. But I would very much like to know what you knocked on, because things that open old doors are supposed to be locked behind them, and I would like to know how loose the lock has gotten."

"You think I brought something in with me?"

"I think," Isandro said, "that you're dripping on my floor, and the rain stopped the moment I entered the room, and rain does not have manners."

Sefeya looked down at the drying circle of stone. For the first time since arriving, she seemed to truly see it — the neatness of it, the courtesy of it, the way the water had gathered itself into a welcome rather than a spill. A shiver went through her, head to heel, the shiver of a woman realizing the bathwater was warm because something had warmed it on purpose.

"I'd like to go home now," she said quietly. "To my orchard. Where it's loud and muddy and ordinary."

"Take the east road," Isandro said. "And Widow Corren — when the sun reaches noon today, look at the ground where your shadow used to be. If anything answers you when you look — anything at all, a warmth, a whisper, a feeling of being *watched kindly* — I want to know. Come back. Any hour. The door will open for you now, I suspect, whether I like it or not."

She left. Isandro watched her go from the doorway — a woman walking into a grey morning with no shadow trailing her, moving strangely light, strangely quick, like a kite with its string cut. Saved, and severed. Both. The gods of the old doors had always understood that the two words could live in the same sentence.

* * *

Isandro climbed back to the counting room, sat down at the great desk, and pulled the master ledger toward them with hands that were almost steady.

Almost. Because there was a thing they had not said to Sefeya Corren, a thing they had realized while writing the entry, a thing that sat in their chest now like a swallowed coal:

The price had been precise. Too precise. *Your shadow, the one you cast at noon.* Not "a shadow." Not "your shadow." The noon-cast one specifically — the longest, darkest, most defined shadow a body makes, the one that in the oldest stories was called the *true-shadow*, the anchor-shade, the part of a soul that touched the earth. And the payment had been equally precise: rain bounded to the property line, warm, gentle, exactly sufficient. No flood. No rot. No monkey's-paw cruelty, no ironic twist, none of the sloppy malice that lesser spirits always brought to a bargain.

Whoever — whatever — had taken Sefeya Corren's true-shadow knew exactly what it was taking and exactly what it was worth, and had paid fair market value to the copper.

Isandro knew of exactly one entity in the history of the guild's records that bargained that cleanly. It was mentioned once, obliquely, in a founding-era fragment Maren kept locked in her appraisal vault and showed to no one, a fragment describing the thing the first Ledgerhouse had been built to contain — not to fight, not to destroy, because the founders had apparently known better than to try, but to *out-trade*. To corner the market on miracles until the thing behind the old doors had nothing left to buy with.

The fragment had a single surviving line that Isandro had never forgotten, though they had spent thirty years pretending they had:

*It pays honestly, and that is the horror of it.*

Isandro dipped the pen. Below the day's entry, in the margin where clerks were permitted to note irregularities, they wrote:

*Second instance this season of direct transaction. The middle is being cut out. Note for the council: a shop that is bypassed is a shop that is closing. Recommend—*

They stopped. The pen hovered. Recommend what? That the council wake up? The council had been asleep for a century, fat and comfortable behind its neutrality, certain that invisibility was safety. That the old doors were old. That the gods were dead or distant or at minimum *polite*.

Below the unfinished sentence, without quite deciding to, Isandro added one more line, smaller, in the private shorthand only they and Maren could read:

*If it is paying honestly again, ask what it is saving up to buy.*

Then they blotted the page, closed the ledger, and went to find Maren — and nearly walked straight into Wren, the junior clerk, who was coming up the stairs two at a time with an armful of fresh candles and the breathless energy of someone who had not yet learned that most of the news in this house was bad.

"Oh! Master Nystrom, you're up early — Oskar saved you the crusty end of the loaf, he drew you a picture of it and everything, it's really quite flattering, he's given you a heroic jaw—" Wren paused, squinted. "Are you all right? You've gone the color of the accounting wax."

"I'm fine," Isandro said, which was the first lie they had ever written into their own face, and it fit terribly. "Wren. When you finish the candle inventory, fetch Maren for me. Tell her—" How to phrase it? Tell her the old doors are answering knocks? Tell her a widow sold her noon-shadow and got exact change? Tell her the thing under the house is shopping?

"Tell her," Isandro said at last, "it's raining indoors. She'll understand."

Wren blinked, nodded with the unquestioning trust of the young, and clattered off down the corridor, leaving Isandro standing in the counting-room doorway with the smell of rain still faint in the halls below — patient, courteous, and waiting, like a buyer who knows the seller will come around eventually.
