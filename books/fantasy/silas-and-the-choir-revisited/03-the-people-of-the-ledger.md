## 3. The People of the Ledger

Wren had been a junior clerk at the Ledgerhouse for eleven months, and in that time she had learned three things with absolute certainty.

First: never touch the ledgers on the third shelf of the eastern vault without gloves, because the ink there was still wet, and had been for sixty years.

Second: Master Isandro's office was to be approached only when the lamp behind the frosted glass was lit, and even then one should knock twice, wait, knock once more, and pretend not to hear whatever muffled conversation might be happening inside, because Master Isandro talked to his books the way other men talked to their mothers.

Third: breakfast in the Ledgerhouse kitchen was the best hour of the day, and it belonged entirely to Oskar.

She came down the back stairs that morning with her sleeves still buttoned wrong—she'd dressed in the dark, as always, because the Ledgerhouse did not believe in wasting candles—and found the kitchen already full of the smell of toasted barley bread and something sweeter underneath. Honey. Real honey, which cost more per spoonful than Wren made in a week, which meant Maren had won at cards again.

"Sit," Oskar said—or rather, didn't say, but communicated so forcefully with a pointed wooden spoon and an arched eyebrow that Wren sat. The boy was fourteen, small for it, with flour on his nose and a permanent smudge of ash on one cheek that no amount of washing ever fully removed. He couldn't speak. He'd come to the Ledgerhouse four years ago, half-frozen, carried in Bramble's coat, and nobody knew where from; he'd never told them, because he couldn't, and he'd never written it either, because whatever had taken his voice seemed to have taken his letters along with it.

It did not, however, take his opinions. Oskar's opinions arrived through the air like weather.

He pointed the spoon at the table, where a plate already waited: bread, butter, honey, and a slice of salted pear arranged with the fussy precision of a man who took food personally.

"You're spoiling me," Wren said, sitting.

Oskar shrugged elaborately, as if to say *the alternative is watching you eat plain gruel and suffering for it*, and turned back to the stove.

The kitchen was the warm heart of the Ledgerhouse, mostly because Oskar had claimed it the way rivers claim valleys—gradually, inevitably, and with total disregard for any prior arrangement. Officially he was the kitchen-boy. Unofficially he was the conscience, the alarm system, the morale division, and the only person in the building whom Master Isandro obeyed without argument. If Oskar pointed at your plate, you ate. If Oskar folded his arms and looked at you, you went to bed. Once, when a client had raised his voice at Isandro over an appraisal dispute, Oskar had simply appeared in the doorway holding a ladle, and the client had apologized.

Nobody asked how. Some things about Oskar were simply accepted, like the wet ink on the third shelf.

* * *

Maren arrived at half past the bell, which meant she'd been awake since before dawn, because Maren was always awake before dawn and considered this a moral superiority rather than a habit. She came into the kitchen rubbing her good eye, her patch pushed up onto her forehead, her gray hair escaping its braid in every direction like it was trying to leave her.

"Honey," she said, sniffing the air. "Who died?"

"Bramble lost," Wren said.

"Bramble always loses." Maren poured herself tea without asking, which was her right by seniority, though nobody was sure what she was senior *to*. "That man has the gambling instincts of a wet hen. I keep telling him, Bramble, you have to count the cards, and he says counting is cheating, and I say the entire point of our profession is counting, and he says that's different."

"It is different," said Bramble from the doorway.

Bramble entered kitchens the way cats enter rooms: as if he had been there all along and everyone else had just noticed. He was a broad, soft-bellied man of sixty-odd years, with hands like spades and a face that had been punched often enough to develop a sense of humor about it. Forty years a thief-toll collector on the Graywater bridges, he'd retired to the Ledgerhouse under circumstances nobody discussed, and now served as its doorkeeper, groundskeeper, and self-appointed guardian of everything within thirty feet of the front gate.

"I don't cheat," he said now, lowering himself into a chair that groaned in protest. "I play honestly. There's a difference. Honesty is a virtue. Cheating is a skill. I have the first and lack the second, which makes me a better man than Maren and poorer than both of you."

"You're not poorer than me," Wren said. "I'm a junior clerk. My entire fortune is a comb and half a comb."

"That comb will appreciate," Bramble said solemnly. "Hair goods are due for a rise. I heard it from a toll-man."

"You heard it from a man who tried to pay a bridge toll in buttons."

"A *generous* number of buttons."

Oskar set down a plate in front of Bramble—double portion, because Oskar had decided years ago that Bramble was too thin, a judgment based on nothing, since Bramble had the silhouette of a well-fed barrel—and then stood behind him with his arms crossed until Bramble started eating.

"You see what I live with," Bramble told Wren, mouth full. "Tyranny. Absolute tyranny. The boy rules this house with an iron ladle."

Oskar rapped the ladle against the pot in agreement.

Wren laughed, and the sound of it surprised her a little. Eleven months ago she'd arrived at the Ledgerhouse with a forged letter of recommendation, two coins, and the specific talent of being able to remember numbers the way other people remembered faces. She'd expected a cold institution full of cold professionals. What she'd found instead was a building that smelled like bread, where the appraiser cheated at cards, the doorman told lies about buttons, the master talked to his ledgers, and a mute boy fed everyone as if feeding were a form of speech.

She had never had a family before. She hadn't known she was collecting one.

* * *

The morning's work began, as it always did, with the walk-through.

Master Isandro descended from his office precisely at the second bell, ledger under one arm, spectacles on his nose, expression suggesting that the world had once again failed to meet specifications. He was not old—not truly old—but he moved like a man carrying weight invisible to everyone else, and his eyes had the particular stillness of someone who spent most of his life listening to things that weren't being said aloud.

"Maren. Report."

"The Corvessy estate appraisal is finished. The 'priceless ancestral sword' is a decorative reproduction, roughly forty years old, worth less than the scabbard." Maren ticked items off on her fingers. "The widow Ammerline's collection of 'sacred relics' contains one genuine item—the bone whistle—which I've valued at eleven marks and classified under *Objects That Should Not Whistle But Do*. And the client who came Tuesday wanting to sell a vial of last year's summer—"

"Classified?"

"*Seasonal, perishable, non-transferable.*"

Isandro nodded once, which from him was applause. "Bramble."

"No one came over the wall. No one picked the gate. A cat got in through the coal chute and I have decided to allow it, as it catches mice and asks no questions." Bramble paused. "Unlike some clients."

"Wren."

Wren straightened. "Reconciliation of the western accounts is done. There's a discrepancy of three coppers in the candle inventory, which I've traced to the kitchen."

Every head turned toward Oskar, who pointed at the ceiling with great dignity, as if to indicate that candles were a shared resource and the concept of private ownership was beneath him.

"Noted," Isandro said, and Wren could have sworn the corner of his mouth moved. "The rest?"

And here Wren hesitated, because here was the thing she'd been turning over since yesterday evening, the thing she'd almost convinced herself was nothing.

"There are entries," she said carefully, "in the miracle-ledger. The old one, the sealed section. Entries dated this week."

The kitchen went quiet in a way that kitchens shouldn't go quiet. Even the kettle seemed to hold its breath.

"That's not possible," Maren said. "That ledger hasn't been opened in—" She stopped. Looked at Isandro. "Has it?"

"No," Isandro said. His voice was level, but Wren had learned to read the Ledgerhouse the way she read numbers, and everything in the room had just gone slightly, terribly out of balance. "Show me."

They went up together—all five of them, because nobody suggested anyone stay behind, and nobody suggested it because they all knew each other too well to pretend they wouldn't follow anyway. Through the counting room, past the vault door with its three locks, into the archive where the air tasted of dust and older decisions.

The miracle-ledger lay on its stand in the sealed alcove, chained shut, unopened for longer than Wren had been alive.

The chain was intact. The lock was intact. The book was open.

And across the page, in ink that gleamed wet and black as if written moments ago, ran entries in a hand none of them recognized—a hand that was somehow all hands, looping and angular at once:

*Prayer received. Answered. Payment pending.*

*Prayer received. Answered. Payment pending.*

*Prayer received. Answered. Payment—*

The entry stopped mid-word.

Below it, fresh as a wound, was a new line, and this one was addressed upward, outward, at whoever stood reading—as if the ledger itself had lifted its head:

*Four witnesses. Noted.*

Oskar made a small sound. It was the first noise Wren had ever heard him make—a thin, startled breath, like a struck string—and his hand found hers and gripped it hard.

Bramble stepped forward, planting himself between the children and the book with the reflexive geometry of forty years at a bridge toll. Maren's single eye had gone narrow and very still, the way it did when she was valuing something dangerous.

And Isandro stood looking down at the page, and Wren watched the color leave his face by degrees, and thought: *he knows what this is. He knows exactly what this is.*

"Out," Isandro said quietly. "All of you. Now."

"Master—" Wren started.

"*Now.*"

They went. But at the archive door Wren looked back, just once, and saw Master Isandro standing alone in the lamplight with the open book, and saw him reach out—not to close it, but to turn the page, gently, the way you'd turn the face of someone sleeping—and she understood that whatever the Ledgerhouse had been built to keep invisible, invisibility had just walked out the front gate, and the family that lived inside these walls was going to need each other far more than any of them yet knew.
