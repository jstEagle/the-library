## 4. What the King Prayed For

The summons arrived the way all the worst things in my life have arrived: folded inside something ordinary.

In this case it was an invoice for candle tallow — forty pounds of it, billed to the Ledgerhouse by a chandler on Cordwainer's Row who had never once in eleven years of business actually sold us tallow. I knew what the invoice meant before I broke the wax seal hidden beneath the totals column. Forty pounds. The number of steps from our back door to the old tithe-gate of the palace, if one took the route that did not exist on any map.

Wren was at my elbow before I'd finished reading it, because Wren is always at my elbow; I suspect the boy sleeps standing up like a horse and simply relocates to wherever I am the moment my eyes open.

"Bad entry?" he asked.

"It's a tallow invoice."

"We don't buy tallow."

"No," I said, "we don't." I fed the paper to the stove and watched the wax seal bubble. "I'll be out this evening. If Maren asks, I'm auditing the granary tallies in Lowmarket."

"Maren always asks."

"Then tell her I'm auditing the granary tallies in Lowmarket *thoroughly*."

He grinned at me, that gap-toothed grin that had survived two years of copying figures in a house where smiling at the wrong ledger could get you dismissed, or worse, promoted. Then his grin faded the way it does when he's actually thinking, which he pretends never to do.

"Is it the bells?" he said quietly. "The thirteenth ring? Everyone's pretending it didn't happen, but Oskar's been drawing moons all week. Pages of them. Thirteen little circles on every page, and the last one's got teeth."

I looked at him for a long moment. There are days I am proud of the people in my house, and there are days that pride sits in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"Tell Oskar," I said, "that moons don't have teeth."

"And if he keeps drawing them anyway?"

"Then tell him to draw them smaller."

* * *

The route that did not exist on any map took me down through the tithe-gate at dusk, past two guards who looked through me with the practiced blankness of men paid very well to be blind, and up a servants' stair that smelled of beeswax and old stone. The palace at evening is a strange organism. By day it performs majesty. By night it performs nothing at all, and you can hear its joints creaking.

Queen Sera received me in a small room off the west gallery that had been designed, I think, for storing tapestries. There were no tapestries now. There was a table, two chairs, a single lamp turned low, and the queen herself, out of her ceremonial armor of silk and jewels, wearing instead the plain grey wool of a woman who had decided, sometime today, exactly how much of her composure she could afford to spend.

She did not rise. She gestured to the second chair, and when I sat she studied me the way Maren studies an uncut gem — looking for the flaw, or perhaps hoping not to find it.

"You're shorter than I expected," she said.

"Most people expect swordspeakers to be taller," I agreed. "It's the name. It implies a great deal of shouting."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the memory of one, filed away for later use. "Master Nystrom. Or should I say Ledgermaster?"

"Isandro will do. Titles make me itch."

"Good. I'd hate to negotiate with someone comfortable." She slid a folded paper across the table. "Do you know what this is?"

I unfolded it. A prayer card — the cheap kind sold outside temples for a copper, printed with a saint's face so faded it might have been anyone, or no one. On the back, in a hand I recognized immediately, because I had spent three years of my youth forging documents in a script nearly identical to it, was written a single line:

*Return him to me. I will pay anything.*

"This is the king's handwriting," I said.

"Yes."

"And it was found—"

"In the chapel offering box. Along with four hundred others just like it. He has been writing them for seven months, since Prince Corin died of the grey fever. Every night. Same words. *Return him to me. I will pay anything.*" Her voice stayed level, courtly, perfectly modulated, and that was how I knew how much it was costing her. I have spent my life reading ledgers, and a voice held that flat is a column of numbers that won't balance. "Four hundred prayers, Master Isandro. And last month, something began answering them."

I set the card down very carefully, as though it might go off.

"The guild has records," I said. "Of answered prayers. We've been seeing them come through the books — payments arriving without brokers, trades closing without clerks. I assumed it was a new cult, some hedge-priest running a fraud."

"Do we look defrauded?" She rose then, and crossed to the door, and stood with her hand on the latch for a moment, gathering herself. When she turned back, the pragmatist had stepped aside and let the wife through, just for a heartbeat, and it was the wife who spoke. "Come and see him. If you still believe it's a fraud afterward, I will personally apologize and give you a tour of the treasury, which I am told is every money-grubber's dream."

"I prefer the term *record-keeper*."

"Come and see him anyway."

* * *

We went up through the private wing, past sleeping pages and a corridor of ancestral portraits whose painted eyes I have never trusted, and stopped before doors of black oak banded in silver. Two honor guards flanked them. The queen dismissed them with a flick of two fingers, and they went with the relief of men escaping a room they'd been afraid of, which told me more than anything else she'd said.

"He holds audiences," she murmured, hand on the latch. "He holds them beautifully. Better than ever, in truth — Aldric was always a bit stiff in public, and now he's *magnificent*. Warm. Generous. He remembers everyone's name, even names he never bothered with before." She paused. "That's how I knew. Kings do not become better people overnight. It isn't natural. Nothing about this is natural."

"What did the priests say?"

"The priests say the gods have returned to the capital and it is a blessing." Her mouth twisted. "The priests are being given new vestments and a renovated temple, so naturally they say it's a blessing. I sent for you because you people deal in impossible things and, presumably, know when one is eating your husband."

With that, she opened the doors.

The king's study was warm — too warm, the fire banked high though the night was mild — and lit in gold. King Aldric stood at the window with his back to us, watching the sky over the eastern wall, and I had a moment, just a moment, of ordinary human sympathy: a broad-shouldered man gone grey early, shoulders stooped with a grief I could name because I carried its twin. And then he turned around.

His face was Aldric's face. His hands, folded courteously at his waist, were Aldric's hands, ink-stained at the right fingers the way a writing man's are. His voice, when he greeted his wife with evident warmth, was Aldric's voice, a touch deeper than I remembered from public addresses, but voices deepen.

It was his eyes that were wrong.

Not red, not black, none of the theater you'd expect. They were simply *settled*. Human eyes move — flick, dart, drift, betray every thought their owner hasn't spoken. His did not. They rested on me with the patient weight of something that had been looking at me for a very long time and had merely waited for me to arrive.

"Ah," the king said pleasantly. "The swordspeaker."

"My love," the queen said smoothly, "this is Master Isandro, the accountant I mentioned. He's come about the harvest tariffs."

"Of course he has." Aldric smiled, and the smile reached every part of his face except the eyes, which continued their patient resting. "Tariffs. The great unglamorous bones of a kingdom. Sit, Master Isandro. Sera, darling, would you pour? You always underpour for guests and I've never had the heart to correct you."

She poured. I sat. For several minutes we conducted an actual conversation about grain tariffs, and it was — I record this with professional shame — a *good* conversation. The king asked after transport costs on the northern roads. He suggested, shrewdly, that the millers' guild was colluding on storage fees and that a royal audit would crack them within a season. He was funny, in a dry way. Twice he made the queen laugh, and both times something in her face cracked a little as she laughed, because she knew, and the knowing sat there under the mirth like a body under floorboards.

And all the while, the entries wrote themselves in my head. That is the curse of my trade: I cannot stop recording. *One audience, duration nine minutes. Subject: tariffs. Subtext: everything.* And beneath it, in the margin where I keep the true figures: *the thing wearing him knows I know, and finds it amusing.*

Because that was the final horror, the one I carried home with me like a coal in my coat. It wasn't merely that the eyes didn't move. It was that the thing behind them was *enjoying itself*. Enjoying the tariffs. Enjoying the wine. Enjoying the queen's careful, breaking laughter. Enjoying, most of all, sitting across from a swordspeaker and saying, at the end, as I rose to take my leave:

"One more matter, Master Isandro. A personal one." He laced his fingers. "You've been wondering why my wife really brought you here. Wondering is such a small word for what you do. You *reckon*. I can hear it — the little abacus clicking away behind your face. So let me save you the arithmetic." The firelight moved in his unmoving eyes. "Aldric prayed for his son. Seven months, four hundred prayers, *anything*, he said. And I am a god of my word. I intend to return Corin to her. Whole. Breathing. Laughing at his mother's terrible pouring habits. All I require is time enough to finish the work."

"The work," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"The crown sits close to the head, swordspeaker. Closer than any of you understand. Give me until the moon finishes its turn, and this kingdom will have its miracle." He spread his hands, magnanimous, radiant, monstrous. "Surely a house that trades in impossible things cannot object to one delivered free of charge."

Behind him, Queen Sera had gone white as altar linen, and her hand, resting on the table, had closed around the prayer card until her knuckles shone.

I bowed. What else does one do? I bowed, and thanked His Majesty for his insights regarding the millers' guild, and walked out of that golden room on legs that felt borrowed.

At the threshold I made the mistake of glancing back. The king was watching me, and as the doors swung shut between us, his mouth kept smiling while his lips, very slightly, out of rhythm with the smile, shaped two silent words:

*Don't interfere.*

Or possibly — and this is the reading I have chosen, the one I will carry into whatever comes next, because a man must choose what he believes or the abacus behind his face never stops clicking —

*Help me.*

* * *

I did not go straight home. I walked the long way, through Lowmarket where the lamplighters were working and the fishwives were hosing down their stalls, ordinary sounds, ordinary smells, a city that did not yet know it was on a countdown. I bought a meat pie from a vendor who cheated me by a copper and I let him, because it was the last honest transaction I would witness that day.

By the time I reached the Ledgerhouse, the lamps in the counting-room were lit and Maren was waiting, one eye and all, her good eye fixed on me across the ledger she hadn't pretended to be reading.

"Well?" she said.

"Aldric prayed for his dead son," I told her, hanging up my coat. "Something answered. It's wearing him like a glove fits a hand, and it says it needs until the moon completes its turn to finish the job."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then she turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table toward me, and tapped one entry with a blunt, scarred finger.

"Read the signature on tonight's miracles," she said. "All of them. Every hollowed worshipper, every impossible payment. Read who's been co-signing the god's trades." Her voice dropped, and I heard in it something I had never once heard from Maren in twenty years: fear. "Isandro — I know that name. We all do. It's the reason this house exists."

I looked down at the page.

And there it was, written in a hand no living clerk had used, in ink that seemed to drink the lamplight rather than reflect it:

*Vhalor.*
