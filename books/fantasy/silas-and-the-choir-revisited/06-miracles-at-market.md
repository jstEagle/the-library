## 6. Miracles at Market

Wren had been a junior clerk at the Ledgerhouse for exactly two years, three months, and eleven days, and in all that time she had learned precisely three things that mattered.

First: never appraise anything before breakfast. Second: when Maren said "look closer," she meant *with the eye*, not the empty socket, and no, it was not funny anymore. Third — and this was the one Isandro had drilled into her on her very first day, standing in the entry hall with ink still wet behind her ears — *the guild records miracles; it does not make them.*

"Because," Isandro had said, tapping the great ledger with one long finger, "the moment we start making them, we stop being a house of record and become a house of debt. And nobody survives being owed something by us."

Wren had nodded very seriously and understood approximately nothing.

She understood more now.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes did, with a queue.

The Ledgerhouse kept public hours twice a week, and on those mornings the little square outside filled with people wanting ordinary impossible things recorded: a marriage contract with a dowry paid in memories, a will leaving a name to an unborn child, Bramble's eternal dispute with the fishmonger over whether "smell damage to adjacent premises" was a compensable grievance. (It was not. It had been ruled not, four times. Bramble filed again anyway, on principle.)

But this morning the queue was wrong. Too long. Too quiet. And the people in it were not carrying contracts.

They were carrying prayers.

"I want it written down," said a baker's apprentice, sliding a scrap of paper across Wren's desk. His hands were shaking. On the paper, in careful block letters: *Please let my mother's cough break before winter.*

"We don't — that's not —" Wren looked helplessly toward Maren's corner, where the old appraiser sat hunched over a jeweler's loupe like a vulture pretending to be a scholar. "We record trades. We don't take requests."

"You took the widow's rain," the boy said.

And there it was. The whole city knew about the widow. Of course they did. A woman whose orchard had been dying for six years had walked into the Ledgerhouse desperate enough to trade her shadow for rain, and it had *rained* — three days of soft, silver, orchard-saving rain while the woman stood in her doorway with no shadow at all, watching the water fall through herself onto the ground.

Wren had copied that ledger entry herself. She'd thought it was strange. She'd thought it was sad. She had not yet understood it was an *advertisement*.

"That was a private transaction," Wren said, which was clerk-speak for *please go away*, and the boy went away, but he left the prayer-paper on the desk, and by noon there were forty more under it, weighted down with buttons and river stones and one live beetle that Oskar eventually rescued and released out the window with great ceremony.

Maren came over at closing, gathered the papers up in one knotted hand, and read them all without expression.

"Well," she said. "That's going to be a problem."

"Maren, we can't answer prayers. We're clerks."

"No," Maren agreed. "We can't." She tapped the papers together, squared their edges with a precision that Wren envied and could never copy. "But somebody's answering them. And whoever it is has decided our front step is a good place to collect applications." Her one eye lifted to the window, where the evening sky hung clear and cloudless over the rooftops. "I don't care for the theology of that, Wren. I care for it even less than I care for Bramble's fishmonger filings, and you know what a statement that is."

* * *

By the end of the week, the city had stopped bringing prayers to the Ledgerhouse, because it turned out they didn't need to be delivered anywhere at all.

They just needed to be *meant*.

Wren saw the first miracle on a Tuesday, at the spice market, where she'd been sent to verify the weight of a shipment of saffron that the seller swore was honest and which was, per Wren's count, short by four drams. (She recorded the discrepancy. The seller called her a vulture. She wrote that down too, in the margin, where margins were for truth too small to deserve a full line.)

She heard the blind man before she saw him. Everyone did. He stood near the fountain with his cap turned up and his eyes gone white as boiled eggs, singing the same two verses he'd sung at that fountain for as long as anyone could remember, and the whole market had long ago arranged itself around him the way a body arranges itself around an old ache — ignoring him perfectly, completely, with love.

Then he stopped mid-verse.

Wren looked up from her counting. The man was standing very straight, his face tilted toward the sun, and his white eyes were — filling. Color seeping into them like ink dropped in water, brown flooding in from the edges, and he made a sound that Wren would remember for the rest of her life, a sound like a door opening in a wall that everyone had believed was solid stone.

"I see," he said. "I see, I see, I—" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "The fountain. It's *blue*. Nobody told me it was blue."

The market erupted. People surged forward, shouting, laughing, some of them crying, a fishwife throwing her arms around him, someone running for wine. Wren stood frozen with her saffron tally in her hand and felt the strangest sensation — joy first, pure and uncomplicated, *he can see, how wonderful* — and then, underneath the joy, like a stone under moss, something else.

Because she was a clerk. And clerks look at what everyone else is looking at, and then they look at what everyone else isn't.

Everyone was looking at the blind man's face.

Nobody was looking at his hands.

His hands were hanging at his sides, and they were *empty*. Not empty of things — empty of *him*. They hung like gloves on hooks. And when he raised one hand to shield his eyes from the sun — his first sighted gesture, clumsy and beautiful — the fingers moved a half-second late, like a puppet remembering its strings.

Wren told herself it was shock. Anyone would be shaky. Anyone seeing color for the first time in thirty years.

She told herself that all the way back to the Ledgerhouse, and then she opened the day-ledger and wrote, in her smallest, neatest hand:

*Miracle observed, spice market, midday. Blind man restored to sight. Witnessed by approx. 200. Cost: unrecorded.*

She stared at those last two words for a long time. Then she crossed out *unrecorded* and wrote *unknown*, because *unrecorded* implied that a cost existed and simply hadn't been written down, and *unknown* admitted honestly that she didn't know if there was one at all.

Clerk's honesty. It was the only kind she had.

* * *

The second miracle was coins.

A dyer named Pell — a cheerful, round, perpetually worried man who owed money to roughly half the guild district — knelt down in his own workshop on Thursday morning and prayed, out loud, with his face in his dye-stained hands, for enough coin to cover his debts. And when he stood up, his strongbox was full. Not full of gold — full of *coins*, small copper bits, thousands upon thousands of them, packed in like gravel, each one stamped with a moon Wren had never seen on any minted currency.

Thirteen points on the moon. She counted twice.

Pell paid off every creditor by nightfall, laughing, weeping, buying rounds for the whole street. Wren watched him do it from the doorway of the Salt Cellar, where Bramble had dragged her on the grounds that "a clerk who only sees ledgers sees nothing," and Bramble bought the drinks and Wren watched Pell press copper after copper into delighted hands.

"There," Bramble said, satisfied, raising her cup. "Best thing I've seen since the fire at the toll-house. Serves the moneylenders right."

"Bramble." Wren hesitated. "Where do you think the coins came from?"

"The god of small mercies, apparently." Bramble snorted. "Or a very confused dragon. Either way, not our problem. Drink your cider."

But Wren was watching Pell's face. He laughed at every joke now, instantly, a beat too fast, like a man following a conversation by counting the pauses. And when the tavern sang — and the Salt Cellar always sang — Pell's mouth moved along with the words, on time, correctly, but his eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance with an expression Wren had seen once before and couldn't place until much later that night, lying awake:

It was the expression of the widow. Standing in her doorway. Watching rain fall through the place where her shadow used to be.

*Present,* Wren thought. *And not present. Both at once.*

* * *

On Friday, Oskar wouldn't eat his supper.

This was so unprecedented that the entire kitchen staff gathered around him in alarm. Oskar was the Ledgerhouse's mute kitchen-boy, fourteen years old maybe, with a cowlick and a habit of communicating primarily through bread — he expressed approval by baking, disapproval by burning, and complex emotional states through increasingly interpretive use of pastry. He had once baked Wren a pie shaped like her own disappointed face after she'd misfiled the Harrowgate contract. It had been delicious and devastating.

Now he sat pushing beans around his bowl, and when Maren signed at him — *what's wrong, little star?* — he shook his head, then stopped, then signed back slowly, with hands that didn't quite know the words:

*The moon is wrong. There are too many of it.*

"He means there's a thirteenth moon," Wren translated, though Oskar hadn't drawn anything yet. "He told me this morning. He keeps drawing it." She pulled the paper from her pocket — Oskar had pressed it on her at breakfast with urgent, floury hands. It showed the familiar moon over the capital, and beside it, smaller, a second crescent that shouldn't exist, ringed around and around with scribbled circles like a child insisting *look, look, look*.

Maren studied the drawing for a long moment. Something moved behind her one eye — something old and cold settling in for a long stay.

"Eat your beans, Oskar," she said gently. "And Wren — come find me tomorrow morning. Before the market opens. There's something I need to show you in the founding ledgers."

"What is it?"

"Proof that I should have retired thirty years ago," Maren said, and limped off to her corner, and did not come out again that night.

* * *

Saturday morning, Wren witnessed the collapse.

It happened at the fish market, which meant Bramble was there within minutes, on the grounds that any gathering near fish constituted a continuation of her legal proceedings. Wren had gone to buy supper for the house — Oskar had requested, via elaborate mime, "something that was swimming recently" — and she was haggling over trout when the crowd three stalls down went silent in that particular way crowds go silent, all at once, like a held breath.

A woman stood in the open ground between the stalls. Older, gray-haired, dressed in a laundress's smock. She was looking at her own hands.

"They gave it back," she was saying, to no one, to everyone. Her voice was bright and wrong, like a bell rung with a crack in it. "I prayed last night, I prayed *please let me feel it again*, and they gave it back, do you hear me? I can feel it. I can feel everything—"

"Woman's mad," muttered the fishmonger, and Bramble shushed him sharply, because Bramble knew madness when she saw it, professionally, and this wasn't quite it.

The woman turned in a slow circle, showing the crowd her hands, her face radiant. "Forty years I've been numb since the fever. Forty years! And now—" She laughed. "Now I can feel the sun. I can feel my heart. I can feel—"

She stopped.

Wren saw it happen. Saw the radiance go out of the woman's face not gradually but *all at once*, like a lamp pinched between fingers. The woman's arms dropped. Her knees didn't buckle — they simply stopped holding her, and she folded down onto the cobbles like laundry slipping off a line.

The crowd screamed and surged. Wren shoved through, clerk's satchel and all, and reached her among the first, dropping to her knees on the wet stones.

"Madam? Madam, can you hear me?"

The woman's eyes were open. That was the worst part. They were open and aimed at Wren and there was nothing behind them — not death, death has a weight to it, a finality — but *absence*. Like looking through windows into a house where the furniture had been carried out. The woman breathed. Her pulse, when Wren found it, beat steady and slow.

But when Wren lifted the woman's hand and let it go, it fell, and the woman did not notice her own hand falling.

"She's breathing," someone said. "Fetch a physicker!"

"It's the miracles," said another voice, older, from the back of the crowd. "My brother-in-law saw again on Tuesday. My neighbor's debts vanished Thursday. They're all like this now. Smiling and smiling and *nobody home*."

The crowd's wonder curdled, visibly, in real time. Wren felt it curdle in her own chest. Because she had watched the blind man laugh at the color blue, and she had wanted to weep with happiness, and now she understood — with the flat, sick certainty of a figure that won't balance — that somewhere in that man was a room exactly like this one. Emptied. Carried out. And whatever had answered his prayer had taken the furniture as payment.

The physickers came and carried the laundress away on a shutter. The crowd dispersed in murmuring clumps. Bramble stood beside Wren, uncharacteristically silent, her scarred hands flexing and unflexing.

"Well," Bramble said finally, gruffly. "That's torn it."

"Bramble, what's happening?"

Bramble looked down at her — Bramble was a head taller and built like a door — and for a moment the old thief-toll collector's face held something Wren had almost never seen on it: fear, worn openly, like a wound shown to a friend.

"When I collected tolls on the smugglers' road," Bramble said quietly, "there was a rule. Never rob a man who gives you his purse smiling. Because a man who smiles while he's being robbed has already decided the purse costs less than what you'll take next." She nodded toward the direction they'd carried the laundress. "Somebody's collecting. And they're charging more than coin."

* * *

Wren carried the trout home. She forgot about it, actually, and Oskar found it later in her satchel and gave her a look of profound professional disappointment before turning it into a chowder that tasted like forgiveness.

But she couldn't settle. After supper she sat in the common room with the day-ledger open, and she wrote, in her smallest hand:

*Miracle observed, fish market. Laundress restored to feeling; collapsed within the hour. Presently alive but absent. Witnessed by approx. 60. Cost: [she paused here, pen hovering] everything, apparently.*

She looked at the entry for a long time. Then she flipped backward through the ledger, past the widow's rain, past the entries appearing that no clerk had written, and counted.

Blind man. Dyer. Laundress. The baker's mother's cough, broken overnight, according to the boy who came back to the Ledgerhouse weeping with gratitude and asking, puzzled, why his mother smiled at him now "like she was remembering how." The child saved from the well. The drunk made sober. Eleven miracles in nine days. Eleven answered prayers.

Eleven hollowed people, if the pattern held.

Wren set down her pen. Across the common room, Oskar sat by the fire drawing — moons, always moons, page after page, thirteen-pointed circles crowding the paper like a rash. Maren's corner was dark; she'd been in the founding ledgers since dawn. Isandro was somewhere above, doing whatever Isandro did at night, which was balance things that shouldn't balance and worry about them beautifully.

*Eleven miracles,* Wren thought. *And the thirteenth moon rising.*

She wasn't a mathematician, but she was a clerk, and clerks know that when two numbers keep turning up in the same book, they're usually the same number wearing different clothes.

She got up, crossed the room, and sat down across from Oskar. The boy glanced up, flour-dusted and solemn.

"Show me the drawings again," Wren said. "All of them. And this time, Oskar — show me the ones you haven't shown anybody."

Oskar looked at her for a long moment. Then he closed his current page, and beneath the stack of finished drawings — beneath, where he kept things he was afraid of — his fingers found a folded sheet, creased and re-creased until the paper had gone soft as cloth.

He unfolded it slowly, the way you open a door you're not sure you want to be open.

And what was drawn there made Wren forget, entirely, how to breathe.
