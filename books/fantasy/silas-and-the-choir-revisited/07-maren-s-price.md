## 7. Maren's Price

Maren Oduya had one eye and, by her own careful accounting, exactly enough sense to make up for it.

The eye was a good one. It had appraised forty years of impossible things — bottled griefs, secondhand luck, a saint's left kneecap that had come through the trade door in 311 and turned out to be a very convincing turnip — and in all that time it had never once been fooled. The other eye she'd left in Vessany with a man who'd said her prices were criminal. He'd been right, as it happened, but there were standards.

So when the miracle-ledger entries began appearing in Isandro's neat clerk-hand — entries no clerk had written, prayers answered in the margins of the world like ink bleeding through cheap paper — Maren's good eye did what it always did.

It looked for the signature.

Every trade has one. Not a name, necessarily. A *hand*. The way a counterfeiter's coins are always a hair too heavy, the way a liar's gifts arrive too promptly. Forty years of appraisal had taught Maren that everything passing through the Ledgerhouse carried the mark of its maker, whether the maker knew it or not.

She sat in the counting room after midnight with the copied pages spread before her, a candle burning down to its collar, and she read the hand of God.

* * *

It was in the flourishes. That was the first thing she noticed, and it made her laugh — a short, ugly sound in the empty room, because laughing at it was better than doing anything else about it.

The rain over the widow's orchard had been signed in the shape of the rain itself. Look at the ledger's record of the transaction: the curve of each falling drop, rendered in the clerk's transcription, looped back on itself at the bottom. Every loop closed into an open mouth. Every open mouth pointed down.

Vhalor wrote the way Vhalor did everything. From above, and hungry.

Maren pressed her palm flat against the page, as if she could hold the paper still while the world moved under it. Her hands, which could judge the weight of a soul by the grain of its shadow, were shaking.

"Old friend," she murmured to the page, "you should not be able to write."

Because that was the thing. That was the founding sin, the one stitched into the Ledgerhouse's bones and then carefully, lovingly forgotten by every generation since. The guild had not been founded to broker miracles. It had been founded to *starve* one.

And Maren knew because she'd read the sealed archive, which was more than Isandro knew she'd done, and more than the council would ever admit aloud, and if she was going to be honest — and honesty, these days, kept arriving at her door like a debt collector — she'd known since the third entry. Since the blind man who saw. She'd recognized the signature the way you recognize a face across a crowded market: instantly, horribly, from behind.

* * *

She found Isandro where she knew he'd be, because Isandro Nystrom was the most predictable person Maren had ever loved, and she included in that category a clockwork orrery she'd once appraised that did nothing but predict sunsets.

The counting room. Third candle from the window, always, because the light came slantwise across the ledgers and Isandro claimed — claimed! — it reduced errors. As though Isandro Nystrom had made an error in this life. As though the man were capable of it.

He looked up when she came in, and something in his face did the thing it always did around her: softened by a degree so small you'd need her eye to measure it.

"You're up late," he said.

"I'm always up late. You just usually pretend not to notice." She pulled a stool to the table and sat, uninvited, because invitations between the two of them had gone out of fashion sometime in their second decade of friendship and neither had missed them. "I've been reading your miracle pages."

Isandro went very still. Maren watched him go still — watched the stillness move through him like cold water through cloth — and thought: *he knows I know.* Good. It saved time, and Maren was old, and old women do not waste evenings on preamble.

"They're not mine," Isandro said quietly. "You know they're not mine."

"I know whose they are." She laid the copied entry on the table between them, turned it so the loops faced him. "Look at the rain. Really look."

He looked. She watched his eyes track the curves, watched him find the mouths, watched the color leave his face in the particular order it always left — jaw first, then cheeks, then the steady gray calm settling over everything like frost on a window.

"That's..." He stopped. Started again. "That's a style. A school of transcription. Old—"

"Older than the guild." Maren folded her hands. They had stopped shaking, finally; there was a kind of peace in having run out of reasons to pretend. "Isandro. Sit down. No — stay standing, actually, you'll want to be standing for this. I stood for it too, when I learned. Felt right. Like being called to account."

"Maren."

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said, "and you're going to hate me a little for keeping it, and then you're going to hate yourself a lot for not finding it first, because you're you. And then we're going to have tea, because it's nearly morning and we're both too old for this."

* * *

"The Ledgerhouse was not built to trade in impossible things," Maren said. "That's the lie we tell apprentices, and it's a good lie, because it makes us feel useful instead of guilty. We were built to keep one specific impossible thing from eating."

She got up — her knees announced it, they always announced it now — and went to the shelf where the oldest ledgers lived, the ones bound in gray leather that everyone assumed was plain cowhide and everyone was wrong about. She took down the first book. The founding book. The one with no title, because its title had been eaten along with everything else.

"Do you know why our founder was called the First Clerk?" she asked, back turned. "Not the First Trader. Not the First Appraiser. Clerk. Because her whole job, at the start, was record-keeping. Inventory." She set the book on the table with a thump that raised dust smelling of pepper and old fear. "Inventory of a prison."

Isandro's voice came out flat. "There is no prison in the archives."

"No. There isn't. That was rather the point." Maren opened the book. The pages inside weren't paper; they were vellum scraped thin enough to read through, and the writing on them was dense, obsessive, the hand of someone writing against a deadline measured in souls. "Three hundred and forty years ago, there was a god loose in this city. Not a small god. Not a river-spirit or a hearth-whisper. Vhalor. The Turned Moon. He ate worship the way we eat bread, and when the faithful ran out he didn't stop eating — he just stopped caring what he ate. Prayers, promises, names, faces. Whole congregations walked into his temples hollow and walked out as his hands."

"And the guild..."

"Thirteen families. Thirteen oaths. They couldn't kill him — gods don't die, Isandro, they only get *bored* — so they did the next best thing. They starved him. They built the Ledgerhouse on top of his prison-stone, and they made themselves the only brokers in the world for impossible trades, so that every prayer, every wish, every desperate bargain in the capital would pass through their hands first. And their hands did nothing. Recorded everything. Gave nothing back." She tapped the vellum. "Invisibility wasn't cowardice. It was the lock. A god can't feed on a house that never opens its mouth."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"The oath of silence," Isandro said slowly. "The swordspeakers' oath. It's connected."

"Everything is connected; that's the trouble with reality." Maren sighed. "The swordspeakers were the thirteen families' teeth. Words that bind — you know better than anyone what those cost. They spoke the prison shut. And then the families made them swear never to speak again, because a binding word spoken near the stone might unbind it, and the families had gotten comfortable, and comfortable people will sacrifice anything to stay comfortable. Including their own children." She looked up at him, and let him see it — all of it, the whole weight, no appraiser's neutrality now. "Including you, Isandro. You weren't collateral damage of an old rule. You were the rule working exactly as designed."

* * *

He sat down after all. Maren pretended not to notice, out of mercy.

For a long while the only sound was the candle and, far off, Bramble snoring in the toll-room with the particular rhythm of a man dreaming about tariffs.

"The signature," Isandro said at last. His voice was careful, the way a man walks on ice. "If the prison held, how is he signing anything?"

"Because the prison is a diet, not a wall." Maren turned the vellum toward him, to a passage she'd marked years ago and told no one, not even herself, really — some knowledge you carry like a stone in your shoe until the day you finally sit down and take your boot off. "The founders couldn't seal him away from the world entirely. A god cut off completely goes mad, and a mad god breaks any cage. So they left him one vein. One thin thread of worship, rationed, metered, recorded. Enough to keep him sleeping. And the Ledgerhouse kept the accounts." Her mouth twisted. "We were his wardens and his cooks. Every generation of clerks, without knowing it, has been measuring out his meals."

"And now?"

"Now somebody stopped cooking and started serving feasts." She pushed the miracle-pages toward him. "These aren't leaks, Isandro. Leaks trickle. This is a banquet. Prayers answered directly, payment taken directly, the guild bypassed entirely — the lock isn't straining. The lock is *open*. And whoever opened it either doesn't know what they've done—" she thought of the palace, of rumors, of a king said to pray too much lately, of a queen's messenger arriving at odd hours with odd requests, "—or knows exactly."

Isandro stared at the rain that was also mouths. "Why are you telling me this now? Why not the council? Why not thirty years ago?"

"Thirty years ago you were a boy with a burned-out throat and a debt you thought was yours alone, and telling you would have broken you, and I am many things but I have never been cruel." She reached across the table and put her hand over his — his ink-stained, steady, trembling-only-at-the-fingertips hand. "And now? Now the council voted last week to classify the miracle entries as 'seasonal fluctuation.' Seasonal. Fluctuation. I have appraised lying ledgers my whole life, Isandro, and ours has just become one, and I find—" her voice caught, and she was furious at it, and continued anyway, "—I find I am too old to keep a sin warm for people who've decided it's furniture."

"So you're telling me."

"I'm telling you because you're the only other person in this building who reads signatures." She withdrew her hand and stood, knees complaining, and looked down at him with her one good eye and all the love she'd never once said aloud in forty years, because saying it would have required opening her mouth, and Maren Oduya understood better than most what doors do once they're open. "And because if I'm going to help burn this house down and build it back honest, I want to do it standing next to the one person here who already paid the price of our silence. You've been paying since you were nine years old. It's past time somebody told you what the bill was actually for."

* * *

They had the tea. She'd promised, and Maren kept her accounts balanced.

They drank it in silence at first, shoulder to shoulder at the counting table, two old keepers of secrets watching the candle drown in its own wax. Outside, the sky was going gray at the edges, and somewhere beyond the gray, low on the horizon where no moon had any business being, the thirteenth moon hung fat and patient, one turn closer to full.

"There's one more thing," Maren said, when the cups were empty. "The reason I came tonight instead of yesterday, or last week, or any of the hundred nights I almost did."

Isandro looked at her.

"The founding book has a final page. The founders' last entry, written the night the prison was sealed. I've read it maybe six times in my life, and every time I hoped I'd misread it." She turned the vellum to the end, to lines worn nearly smooth by other readers' dread. "It's a warning. About the unbinding. About what happens to the one who speaks it. But that's not the part that matters." Her finger stopped beneath a single line, and she read it aloud so she wouldn't have to watch him read it alone:

"*When the Turned Moon waxes full, the crown shall be his cup, and the king shall be poured out.*"

The words sat in the room like a dropped knife.

Isandro rose. Slowly. All the color gone again, but underneath it now something else — something Maren recognized from mirrors, from her own face on the nights she'd decided to stop pretending. Purpose, arriving late but arriving.

"Isandro," she said. "What do you know about the king?"

He was already reaching for his coat.

"The queen sent for me three days ago," he said. "I haven't told you why. I'm sorry, Maren. I was keeping a secret."

Maren laughed — really laughed this time, tired and fond and a little bit proud — and reached for her own coat, because apparently at her age one did not get to simply confess sins and go to bed.

"Don't apologize," she said. "Just this once, we keep the same ledger."
