## 9. Guild of Impossible Things

Bramble had stolen things from kings, temples, one archduke, and a dragon's hoard that turned out to be a very committed illusionist. In sixty years of taking what wasn't offered, she had developed a professional appreciation for the moment a mark decided to lie to her—the small settling of the shoulders, the too-careful stillness of the hands.

She was watching eleven of the most powerful people in the city do it all at once.

The ruling circle of the Ledgerhouse met in the Round Room, which was not round, on chairs that had been expensive three hundred years ago and were now merely stubborn. Eleven seats for eleven masters, plus a twelfth chair at the head that no one sat in—the Founder's Chair, kept empty out of tradition, or guilt, depending on which master you asked. Above it hung the guild's oldest ledger, chained shut, its cover stamped with the words every apprentice learned before they learned their letters:

*We record what cannot be owned. We own what cannot be recorded. We are seen by no one.*

Bramble had never been a master. She'd been hired forty years ago as a "security consultant," which was the polite way of saying she knew every lock in the capital and how each of them felt about being opened. She sat now in her usual spot—against the wall, near the door, where retired thief-toll collectors and other useful furniture belonged—and watched the council decide, with great care and enormous self-regard, to do nothing.

"The situation," Master Corvane was saying, in the voice he used for announcing weather and verdicts, "is contained."

Contained. Bramble turned the word over like a coin she suspected was shaved. The thirteenth moon hung over the capital like a fingernail paring of silver, and half the city was praying to gods whose names hadn't been spoken aloud in living memory, and the king had eyes like a well someone had dropped a lantern down. Contained.

"Contained," repeated Master Ilseth, who managed the miracle-ledgers and had the complexion of a man who hadn't slept since the bells rang thirteen times. "The anomalous entries have been quarantined in the red folios. No clerk has transcribed them. No client has been informed. As far as the outside world knows, we remain—" she searched for the word and found it with visible distaste, "—boring."

"Boring is our product," said Corvane. "Boring is why we've survived nine hundred years. Every house that traded in impossible things is ash. Every house that *recorded* them quietly, took no side, made no claim—" he spread his hands, "—is us. Neutrality is not cowardice. It is architecture."

Bramble bit the inside of her cheek to keep her face professional. She'd heard this speech before, in variations, at every crisis of her long tenure: the plague year, the fire of the Saltmarket, the night the river briefly flowed uphill. The council always arrived at the same place. The ledgers came first. The ledgers were the guild. The guild was the ledgers. People were, at best, the paper the ledgers were written on.

There was a knock at the door—not a request so much as an announcement—and Isandro Nystrom came in without waiting, which only Isandro did, because only Isandro had spent forty years making himself so indispensable that even masters forgot he wasn't one of them.

Bramble watched the room watch Isandro. It was like watching cats pretend not to track a bird.

"You received my summons," said Corvane.

"I read it," said Isandro. "I didn't agree to it."

"The summons doesn't require agreement. It requires attendance." Corvane gestured at the empty space before the council table, and Isandro stood there, hands folded, wearing the particular stillness Bramble had last seen on a man deciding whether to jump off a bridge. She'd talked that one down. She wasn't sure she could talk this one down, and she wasn't entirely sure she should try.

"The matter before the council," Corvane continued, "concerns you directly, so you will hear it directly. The palace has become... irregular. The queen requests consultations. The king receives visitors at strange hours. And certain entries in our ledgers suggest that the crown has become entangled with—" here the whole table flinched, delicately, in unison, like a bedsheet shaken once, "—a prior holding."

"A god," said Master Ilseth flatly, because euphemism apparently had limits even among accountants of the impossible.

"A *prior holding*," Corvane insisted, "whose containment was the founding purpose of this institution, and whose re-emergence is therefore, I want to be absolutely clear, *not our problem*."

Silence. Somewhere below them, the kitchen boy Oskar was banging pots, and the sound came up through the floor like a small stubborn heartbeat.

"Our charter," Corvane went on, warming to it, "obliges us to record, conceal, and endure. It does not oblige us to intervene. Kings have been consumed before. Cities have knelt before. The ledgers survived. The ledgers will survive this. What the ledgers will *not* survive is a senior swordspeaker—one swordspeaker, the last swordspeaker—deciding unilaterally that his personal loyalties outweigh nine hundred years of discipline." He let that sit, then delivered the actual order as gently as a man setting down something breakable. "You will cease your visits to the palace. You will surrender any correspondence with the queen. You will make no further inquiry into the thirteenth moon. You are, effective tonight, reassigned to the deep archive, cataloguing, alone, until this passes."

"And if it doesn't pass?" Isandro asked quietly.

"Then it will pass through us, as everything does, and we will still be standing when it's gone."

Bramble looked at Isandro's hands. They weren't folded anymore. They were very still, the way water is still right before it goes over the edge.

"You're asking me to let a man die," Isandro said. "A man who trusted this house. A man who—" the voice caught, just slightly, on something private, "—who prayed for his son, and got *that* instead, and the only people in the world who know what's happening to him are sitting in this room deciding whether it's worth the paperwork."

"We are deciding," said Corvane, "whether nine hundred years of the voiceless dead who built this place are worth less than one king. That is the arithmetic. You may not like it. Arithmetic does not care."

"No," Isandro agreed. "It doesn't. But I do."

And then Isandro bowed—actually bowed, formal, correct, the bow of a subordinate accepting instruction—and left the room, and the door closed softly behind them, and Bramble thought: *oh, you beautiful liar.* Because she had spent forty years reading marks, and that bow had been the most fluent lie she'd ever witnessed, and the only person in the room besides herself who'd noticed was old Master Yewen, who was nearly deaf and noticed nothing, which meant nobody had noticed at all.

"Good," said Corvane, to the closing door. "That's settled."

It was not settled. Bramble would have bet her remaining eye tooth on it.

* * *

The copying began two nights later, and Bramble only found out because she was supposed to find out.

She'd been keeping an eye on the sealed archive stair—casually, the way you keep an eye on a suspicious nephew—since the council session. On the second night, past midnight, she heard the faint scratch of a nib through the archive door, and she stood in the dark corridor for a long moment, weighing things.

Forty years ago, when the guild had hired her away from a life of honest crime, they'd made her swear an oath too: *the house above all.* She'd sworn it easily, because houses had always been temporary in her experience, and oaths were cheap when you expected to break them. But then the house had turned out to contain Maren, who saved her seat at supper; and Wren, who laughed at her jokes even the bad ones, especially the bad ones; and Oskar, who drew her pictures of cats whenever she looked sad, which was more often than she admitted; and Isandro, who had once sat up all night with her after the funeral of a man whose name Bramble still couldn't say, saying nothing, doing nothing, just being there the way stone is there.

The house above all. Well. The house *was* those people. The house was not eleven dry sticks voting to watch a kingdom kneel.

So Bramble knocked on the archive door, softly, and heard the scratching stop, and heard Isandro say, without opening it: "If you're here to report me, Bramble, I'd rather you come in first. It's cold in the corridor."

"I'm here," she said, letting herself in, "to complain about your ink. You're using the good iron-gall on forbidden pages. Waste. Use the cheap stuff; if the council burns them either way, the quality won't matter."

Isandro looked up from the desk, lamplit, surrounded by open folios, and for a moment the composure cracked and what showed underneath was just tired—a tiredness with forty years of silence packed inside it. "How long have you known?"

"Since the bow. Nobody bows that well unless they're lying." She pulled a stool to the desk, sat, and peered at the page. Isandro's hand moved across it in the tight, beautiful script the guild trained into everyone, copying out entries from the red folios: dates, transactions, signatures. Miracle after miracle. Payment after payment. And at the bottom of each entry, in a hand that made Bramble's skin crawl though she couldn't have said why—a signature like a coiled thing.

"That's the same mark every time," she said.

"Yes."

"It's not a person's name."

"No."

"Is it the thing in the king?"

Isandro set down the pen. Looked at her. And Bramble saw the decision happen—the decision to trust, which for Isandro was clearly harder than any word of power. "Maren recognized it," they said. "It's older than the guild. It's the reason for the guild. Vhalor."

The name landed in the little room like a dropped anvil. Bramble found she was gripping her own knee. "The god the ledgers were built to bury."

"The god the ledgers were built to *forget*," Isandro corrected. "Which is different, and worse. You can guard against an enemy you remember."

Bramble looked at the copied pages, growing into a neat stack. Forbidden pages, copied by lamplight, against direct orders. Treason, technically. Or fidelity, depending on who ended up writing the history. "You know what happens if Corvane finds this," she said. "He'll expel you. He'll burn the copies. He might burn more than the copies."

"He might."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway."

Bramble nodded slowly, and then, because she was who she was, she reached across the desk and turned Isandro's inkwell ninety degrees, closer to the hand, the way you'd steady a ladder under a climber. "Then work faster and waste less light," she said. "And when you need a second copy made—or a first copy *lost*, or a lock opened, or a corridor empty at a specific hour—you come find me. I'm retired. Retirement is boring."

Isandro stared at her. Something moved through their face—gratitude, grief, the particular ache of being loyal to people your loyalty is about to cost. "Bramble. If the council learns you helped me—"

"The council," said Bramble, "learned forty years ago that I know where every body in this building is buried, metaphorically, and two of them literally. Let them learn it again." She stood, joints cracking like green wood, and headed for the door. At it she paused, hand on the frame, and didn't turn around, because some things were easier said to a wall.

"I swore the oath same as you," she said. "House above all. But I've been thinking about what the house actually is. It's not the ledgers, Isandro. Ledgers are just paper that remembers. The house is the people in it. And one of the people is currently wearing a crown that's eating him alive, and eleven others are arguing about folios." She tapped the doorframe twice, an old thief's knock, *all clear*. "Copy fast."

* * *

They copied for six nights.

On the fourth night Wren found out—walked in with a plate of Oskar's honey-bread, took one look at the red folios, and sat down without a word to grind ink, because Wren was young but not stupid, and because loyalty in that house had always traveled faster than orders. On the fifth night Maren came, one-eyed and grim, bringing appraisals of the signatures that made even Isandro go quiet. On the sixth night Oskar appeared in the doorway with an armful of candles and a drawing held out in both hands like an offering.

Bramble looked at the drawing and stopped laughing mid-something.

It showed the moon. Thirteen circles of it, chalked in a row, twelve filled in with white dust and the thirteenth empty. Below the moon, a stick-figure city, kneeling. Below the city, a crowned figure with hollow eyes, and around the figure, drawn in Oskar's careful, childish hand, dozens of smaller figures with their chests open like doors, and nothing inside them at all.

Nobody spoke. The candle flames leaned, though there was no draft.

Oskar pointed at the empty thirteenth circle, then at the window, where the real moon hung—thin, silver, patient—and then pressed the drawing flat against Isandro's copied pages, aligning the moons, one over another, until the chalked cycle and the written record matched exactly.

Twelve turns done. One remaining.

"Well," said Bramble at last, into the silence, because somebody had to, and gentle humor was the only weapon she'd ever carried that didn't rust. "At least the boy's honest. That's more than I can say for anyone else in this building."

But her hands, as she gathered the finished copies and hid them in the false bottom of her old toll-chest, were not entirely steady. Because she had counted the pages. She knew exactly how many truths were hidden in this house, and how few nights remained to get them out—and somewhere below them, faint through the floor, she could hear the council convening an emergency session, hours early, voices already sharp as shears.

Someone had told them something. Someone had reported something.

And Bramble, who had betrayed every institution she'd ever joined except this one, felt the floor tilt beneath the only home she had left.
