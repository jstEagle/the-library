## 10. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

Oskar had three ways of saying a thing.

The first was his hands, which were quick and clever and could say *more bread* or *the fire's low* or *Maren is pretending not to cry again* faster than most people could say it out loud. The second was his face, which said everything else—the things too big for hands, like *I'm glad you're here* and *don't go yet*. The third way was paper. Charcoal on the backs of spoiled ledger pages, because Wren said the guild threw away enough mistakes to wallpaper the capital, and Oskar had taken her at her word.

He drew every day. Little things, mostly. The cat that lived in the coal cellar. Bramble asleep in his chair with his mouth open, which got him a cuff on the ear and a grin from Bramble both at once. The kitchen garden in spring. Once, memorably, Maren with her one eye crossed in furious concentration while she appraised a jar of what turned out to be bottled thunder—Oskar had hidden that drawing under his mattress for a week until Maren found it, held it up to the light, and declared it "hideously accurate" before pinning it above her desk, where it still hung.

So when the dreams started, Oskar did what he always did with things too big for his hands and too heavy for his face.

He drew them.

---

It began three nights after the bells rang thirteen times.

He dreamed of the moon. That alone wasn't strange; everyone in the city had been dreaming of the moon lately, or so the market gossip said, though the gossip disagreed on what the dreaming felt like. Some said it was like being watched. Some said it was like being *wanted*, which was worse.

For Oskar it was like being called by name—which was its own strange thing, because nobody knew his true name. He'd come up the Ledgerhouse steps six years ago, small and silent and streaked with soot, after the fever took his voice along with his mother, and Master Isandro had looked at him for a long moment and then simply stepped aside and pointed toward the kitchen. No name asked. None needed. Wren had started calling him Oskar within the hour—"you look like an Oskar," she'd said, with the absolute confidence of a nine-year-old who was never wrong about anything except everything—and the name had stuck like flour to wet hands.

But the moon knew something else. In the dream it hung low over the capital, fat and wrong-colored, the color of a coin left too long in a dead man's pocket, and it *knew* him. It counted him. He could feel the counting going past him like cold water: one, two, three—

He woke up with charcoal already in his fist. He hadn't remembered fetching it.

The first drawing was simple: the moon over the palace spires, and beneath it, thirteen small circles in a row, twelve of them filled in with careful shading and the thirteenth left open, waiting. He stared at it for a long time in the dark of the loft above the kitchen. Then he drew it again, bigger, because the first one hadn't gotten the feeling right. The feeling was the important part. Anyone could draw a moon.

Getting the feeling right took four more tries and used up every clean scrap of paper in his box.

---

In the morning he showed Wren, because Wren was the one who read his pictures best. Not because she was cleverer than Maren—Maren was cleverer than everyone, and would tell you so—but because Wren didn't look at a drawing and ask *what is it?* She looked at it and asked *what does it want?*

"New one," she said, sliding down onto the kitchen bench across from him with a heel of bread in one hand and ink stains up both forearms, as usual. She was seventeen now and carried herself like a junior clerk of tremendous importance, which, to be fair, she was. "Let's see. Moon. Palace. Thirteen little—oh." Her chewing slowed. "Is this the bell thing? The thirteenth bell?"

Oskar nodded and tapped the twelve shaded circles, then the empty one.

"Twelve done. One to go." Wren frowned at it. "That's grim, Os. You've drawn us a countdown." She said it lightly, the way she said most things, but her eyes stayed on the page a beat too long. "Where'd you get thirteen? Everyone's been saying twelve turns till the moon's full-whatever. The astrologers have been charging a copper a prediction about it."

Oskar tapped the thirteenth circle again. Then he did the thing he did when words-in-pictures weren't enough: he took her hand, pressed it flat against the paper, over the empty circle, and held it there.

Wren went quiet.

"That's cold," she said finally. "That's—Os, why is your drawing *cold*?" But she didn't pull her hand away, and Oskar saw her look down at the empty circle the way people looked down a well. "You dreamed this."

He nodded.

"You've been dreaming it more than once."

He held up fingers. Four. Maybe five. The nights had gone soft at the edges since the bells.

Wren pulled her hand back slowly and rubbed it on her skirt, and for once she didn't have a joke ready, which frightened Oskar more than the dream had. Wren without a joke was like the Ledgerhouse without ledgers. "Right," she said at last, too brightly. "Well. I'm taking this to Maren. Maren knows things. Maren knows *everything*, it's very annoying." She stood, sat back down, stood again. "Eat something. You look gray."

He ate something. He looked gray anyway.

* * *

That afternoon, Maren held the drawing up to the window light, turned it over, held it up again, and looked at Oskar over the top of it with her single sharp eye.

"You drew this in your sleep?"

*Some of it,* he signed. *The rest after.*

"After waking. With charcoal already in your hand." Maren set the page down on her desk, next to the pinned caricature of herself, and Oskar watched her decide not to touch it again. Maren touched everything—that was her whole trade, touching things and knowing their worth—but she left the drawing lying flat like something that might bite. "And the circles count the moon's turns. Twelve behind us. One ahead."

*It's counting,* Oskar signed. His hands were steady. His stomach wasn't. *Not me. Everything. It counts the whole city.*

"Mm." Maren took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, put them back on, and did all of it again, which was what she did instead of pacing. "Oskar. When you came to us, Isandro had me appraise you. Formally. You were seven years old and you'd walked out of a plague village that killed everyone else in it, and Isandro wanted to know if you were carrying anything." She paused. "I told him no. I told him you were clean. I told him that because I liked the idea of you being clean, and liking a thing is the fastest way to misappraise it."

Oskar's hands went still in his lap.

"I think I made the worst call of my career," Maren said, "and I've never been sorrier of anything, and I need you to know that whatever happens next, none of it will be your fault. Do you understand me? Fault is a ledger entry, and yours stays blank."

He didn't understand her. He nodded anyway, because she needed him to, and Maren almost never needed anything from anybody.

*What happens next?* he signed.

Maren looked at the drawing. At the empty thirteenth circle.

"I don't know," she said, which Oskar had heard her say perhaps twice in six years, and both times someone had died shortly after. "But I think we're about to find out whether the rest of your pictures are warnings or promises. And I'd very much like it if they stopped coming true before we find out."

* * *

They kept coming true.

Small ones first, which was somehow worse. Oskar dreamed of rain falling on one street and no other—a narrow silver ribbon of it, slanting down between the rooftops—and two days later the widow Vessa's orchard bloomed green in a drought while every garden around it browned, and the whole city talked of nothing else, and Oskar sat in the kitchen with his hands pressed flat against the table so nobody would see them shake.

He dreamed of coins. A poor man's purse growing heavy, coins multiplying in the dark like mice, and three days later the moneylender Hobb went raving through the Fishmarket because a debtor had paid him in full and then some, and the coins wouldn't stop coming, spilling out of the strongbox, filling the man's boots—

He dreamed of eyes. An old blind beggar by the Tanner's Gate, and in the dream the man's milky eyes cleared like pond water going still, and Oskar woke up crying without knowing why, and a day later he heard Bramble telling the story in the front room: blind from birth, seeing now, thanking the old gods at the top of his voice, and wasn't it a marvel, and wasn't it fine—

"And what did his face look like?" Maren asked, from her corner. Quietly. Too quietly.

Bramble blinked. "His face? Same as anyone's, I suppose. Happy."

"Happy," Maren repeated, in the voice she used for items whose provenance she doubted. "Not emptied. Not worn."

"Well, I didn't—" Bramble shifted in his chair. "Now you mention it. He laughed the whole time he was talking. Laughed and talked and thanked, all in the same breath, like a man reciting. Like a man *made* to sound glad." He frowned into his ale. "I thought it was joy."

Oskar slipped out of the room and went upstairs and drew the beggar's face from his dream, the cleared eyes and the laughing mouth, and then, underneath, he drew what the face had looked like a moment later in the dream, when the laughter ran out of it like water out of a cracked cup. He drew the hollow where the man used to be.

He gave both drawings to Wren without explanation. She looked at them for a long time.

"Okay," she said finally, and her voice had gone thin. "Okay. So the miracles are real. And the price is—" She stopped. Started again. "The gods are answering prayers, Os. Actual prayers. And they're eating the pray-ers."

He couldn't argue. He'd drawn it.

* * *

The last vision came on a night when the moon hung so low over the capital that Oskar swore he could see marks on its face—not craters, but letters, or the suggestion of letters, a signature written across the sky in a hand too large to read.

He dreamed the whole turn.

He stood—he floated, dreamed-floated—above the city on the night the thirteenth circle closed. The streets were full. That was the first horror: the streets were *full*, packed shoulder to shoulder, thousands upon thousands, and every one of them was kneeling. Kneeling in the Fishmarket and the Processional and the mud of the Tanner's Row, kneeling on the palace steps, kneeling so densely that the cobbles had vanished under bodies, and not one of them was struggling. They knelt the way wheat kneels under wind. They knelt the way Oskar himself had once knelt, small and silent, in a plague village, waiting for something enormous to finish deciding about him.

Above the palace, the moon filled the sky.

And on the balcony below it stood the king in his crown, arms spread wide, and the king was *empty*. Oskar knew it the way he knew heat from cold, the way his hands knew signs: there was nothing behind the royal face anymore. Something else wore the emptiness like a glove, and it was looking down at the kneeling city with love—real love, vast and patient and utterly without mercy, the love a farmer has for a field—and it was pleased.

*Thirteen,* said the moon, in a voice that was also the bells, which were also the counting-water from the first dream, pouring through everything. *Thirteen. Complete. Mine.*

Oskar tried to scream and, as always, had no voice to scream with. So he did the only thing he had ever been able to do.

He reached for charcoal that wasn't there, in a dream that wasn't paper, and he *drew*—scratched the vision into the air itself, into the dream's own skin, lines of black fire hanging over the kneeling city—and the drawing held. The dream froze around it like a fly in amber. And somewhere far away, in a loft above a kitchen, in a body that was sweating and shaking and clutching a stub of charcoal hard enough to crack it, Oskar's hands finished the picture.

He woke at dawn with black dust under his fingernails and blood on his lip where he'd bitten through it, and five pages spread around him on the floor where he'd apparently drawn in his sleep, one after another, all night long:

The moon, huge and lettered.
The streets, full of knees.
The king on the balcony, crowned and hollow.
The city seen from above, every soul in it bent toward the palace like iron filings toward a lodestone.
And the last page, drawn in strokes so frantic they'd torn the paper: a close view of the crown itself, and inside the circlet, where a brow should be, a single word written in a script Oskar didn't know but understood completely, the way you understand fire understands wood.

He gathered the pages with shaking hands. He didn't take them to Wren's desk this time, or Maren's.

He carried them down through the sleeping house, past the kitchen, past the coal cellar where the cat yawned at him, all the way to the sealed door at the end of the east corridor, behind which Master Isandro had shut himself with candles and forbidden books and had not emerged in two days.

Oskar knocked. Once. Twice. Then, because urgency had outrun politeness, he hammered with the flat of his fist until the door finally opened a hand's width and Isandro's face appeared in the gap—gray-lit, sleepless, ink to the elbows.

The swordspeaker looked down at the small mute boy standing in his doorway at dawn, holding out five pages like an offering.

"What is it, lad?"

Oskar pushed the drawings into his hands and waited, heart hammering, while Isandro looked.

He watched the master's face do what faces did when they met the truth: first confusion, then recognition, then the long fall past both. Isandro went through the pages twice, three times, his lips moving slightly over the word inside the crown, and when he raised his eyes again they were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he'd been dreading his whole life and finding the sum exactly as bad as feared.

"Twelve turns spent," Isandro said quietly. "One remaining." He crouched to Oskar's level, and his voice gentled the way it always did, the way that had made the kitchen loft feel like home from the first night. "You saw this. All of it. And it's coming true, isn't it? Piece by piece."

Oskar nodded. Then, because there was one more thing—one thing he hadn't managed to draw, one thing that lived only in the dream's terrible knowing—he caught Isandro's sleeve and pressed the man's palm flat against his own chest, over his heart, the same way he'd shown Wren the cold circle.

Isandro went very still.

"It's counting *you*," he said softly. "It knows what you are. What you can—" He stopped himself. Stood. Looked at the drawings again, and then out the narrow window, where the wrong-colored moon was setting pale into the morning fog.

"Get Wren," Isandro said. "Wake her, wake Maren, wake Bramble—all of them, now, before the house stirs. And Oskar—" He hesitated, and for a moment he looked less like the last oath-bound swordspeaker and more like a tired man holding five pictures of the end of the world. "Stay where I can see you. Whatever it's counting you for—I intend to argue with it."

Oskar ran for the stairs, bare feet loud on the boards, and behind him he heard Isandro begin pulling books off shelves in earnest, fast and hard, like a man who has just learned how little time is left and intends to spend every second of it.

Outside the window, over the waking roofs of the capital, the thirteenth moon slid down toward the horizon—patient, lettered, nearly done.
