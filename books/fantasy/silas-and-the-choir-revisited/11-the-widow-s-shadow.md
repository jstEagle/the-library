## 11. The Widow's Shadow

Wren had been a junior clerk at the Ledgerhouse for three years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about following people.

One: never follow anyone who walks like they know where they're going. People with destinations notice tails. Two: always carry a reason for being wherever you are. A delivery, a question, a wrong turn you can apologize for. And three — the most important one, learned from Bramble over stolen kitchen bread — if you're going to follow someone through a city at dusk, wear shoes that don't squeak.

Wren's shoes squeaked.

She'd borrowed them from Oskar, which in retrospect was an act of profound stupidity, because Oskar was eleven and had feet like a fawn and opinions about footwear that apparently did not extend to *whether or not they announce your presence to the entire street*. She'd tried stuffing the toes with wool. It hadn't helped. Every third step produced a small, cheerful *squeak* that sounded, in the hush of Vell Street, like a mouse being murdered.

Ahead of her, the widow Halven walked without any such difficulties, because the widow Halven no longer cast a shadow to trip over.

That was the thing Wren couldn't stop looking at. The sun was low and orange behind the rooftops, the kind of light that stretched every pedestrian into long ink-stains across the cobbles — bakers, lamplighters, a drunk singing to a door he clearly believed was his wife. Everyone dragged their darkness behind them like loyal dogs.

Except her.

The widow Halven moved through the amber light and left nothing on the ground at all. Where her shadow should have pooled there was just clean stone, bright as noon, a little island of wrongness that made Wren's teeth ache when she looked at it too long. She'd seen it first from the Ledgerhouse window two days ago — Maren had seen it too, and gone very quiet in the way Maren went quiet, which was to say completely, and then said, "Wren. Fetch me the miracle-ledger," in a voice that made Wren fetch it at a dead run.

*Trade: one shadow, entire, for rain upon the orchards of Halven's Reach.*

Recorded in Isandro's neat clerk-hand. Witnessed by no one. Answered anyway.

And now here was the woman herself, walking through the capital at dusk with rain-soaked good fortune in her pockets and nothing underneath her, heading — Wren was almost certain — toward the river district, where the old catacomb entrances were.

"Right," Wren whispered to herself, adjusting the satchel on her shoulder. Inside it: a stub of pencil, a folded sheaf of blank ledger paper, half a meat pie wrapped in cloth, and a lantern with oil enough for maybe two hours. "You are a professional. You are a clerk of impossible things. You are going to observe, record, and absolutely not die."

Her shoe squeaked. Somewhere above, a pigeon laughed at her. She was fairly sure it was laughing.

* * *

The entrance she'd expected the widow to use was bricked over — everyone knew the river district catacombs had been sealed after the flood year, when three toll-collectors went in chasing smugglers and only their boots came back out. But the widow Halven didn't go to the sealed arch at all. She turned down a dead-end alley between a chandler's shop and a wall of stacked barrels, walked straight up to what appeared to be solid brick, and stood there.

Wren pressed herself against the chandler's wall, heart hammering, and watched.

The widow raised one hand — slowly, ceremonially, the way people do things when they've stopped entirely deciding things for themselves — and laid her palm flat against the bricks.

The bricks opened.

Not dramatically. No grinding stone, no burst of eldritch fire. They simply swung inward like a door that had always been a door, and the alley filled with a smell Wren would remember for the rest of her life: cold air, old incense, wet stone, and underneath it something sweeter and far worse, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

The widow stepped through. The bricks closed behind her. The alley was just an alley again, except for the small patch of ground where no shadow fell even though the last light came slanting down from the west.

Wren stood in the darkening alley for a full minute, doing arithmetic.

Option one: go home, tell Isandro, let the adults handle it. This was the sensible option. This was the option Bramble would endorse loudly and at length, probably while demonstrating on a chalkboard all the ways Wren could be killed underground.

Option two: go in alone, after dark, into flooded catacombs, following a shadowless woman through a magic brick door, armed with a pencil.

"You are a professional," Wren told her shoes, which squeaked supportively. "Observation and recording. In and out. If anything goes wrong, you scream, and Bramble hears everything within four districts when he wants to."

This was true. Bramble claimed it was a professional skill from his toll-collecting days. Maren claimed it was because he snored standing up. Either way, it was comforting, so Wren took it, found the seam in the bricks with her fingers — there was a faint warmth where the widow had touched, like a handprint left in sun-heated stone — and pushed.

The bricks opened. The dark breathed out at her, sweet and cold.

Wren went in, and pulled the door shut behind her, and immediately regretted every decision that had brought her to this moment, and kept walking anyway, because that, she was learning, was what the people of the Ledgerhouse did.

* * *

The catacombs were older than the city above them, or at least older than the parts of the city anyone admitted to. Wren followed the sound of footsteps ahead — soft, unhurried, bare-sole-soft — down stairs worn to shallow spoons, along corridors where her lantern showed her niches full of bones arranged with a tidiness that suggested someone still dusted them.

Here and there, older marks on the walls. She held the lantern close to one and felt her stomach drop.

It was a ledger entry. Carved into stone, in a script she recognized from the guild's oldest sealed volumes — the founding hand, the one Isandro sometimes stared at like it owed him money.

*Received: one prayer, offered in desperation. Paid: one answer, rendered whole. Balance carried below.*

Below. Below what? Below whom?

The footsteps ahead turned a corner. Wren counted to fifty, which was as much patience as she owned, and followed.

The corridor opened without warning into a vast chamber, and Wren did the single smartest thing available to her: she pinched out her lantern and dropped flat behind a fallen pillar, heart slamming, before her eyes had even finished adjusting.

Because the chamber was full of people.

Dozens of them. Maybe a hundred. They stood in silent rows facing a great altar stone at the far end — a slab of black rock that predated everything, its surface worn smooth as a mirror. Lantern-light came from somewhere she couldn't see, sourceless and gray, the color of moonlight through fog.

And every single person in those rows cast no shadow.

They were ordinary people. That was the worst part. Wren could see, as her eyes adjusted, faces she half-knew from the market — the baker with the burn-scarred forearm whose bread had saved Wren from starvation her first winter in the capital; a lamplighter; a fishwife; a young man in the livery of a minor noble house; an old soldier with medals on a coat too fine for him. The widow Halven had taken her place among them, slotting into a row like a book returned to its shelf.

None of them spoke. None of them shifted. They stood in perfect, patient silence, and beneath the sweet rot-smell Wren caught something else now — a hum, very low, felt in the sternum more than heard, like the chamber itself was purring.

Then the feeding began.

It started at the front row. The baker — good Master Corvin, who gave day-old loaves to beggars and pretended not to — stepped forward to the altar stone and knelt, and pressed both hands flat against the black surface.

And Wren watched him pour himself out.

There was no better word for it. Something rose out of the man — not blood, not breath, but *himness*, a shimmering thread of color and warmth and memory that lifted from his chest like smoke made of everything he'd ever loved. Wren saw shapes in it: a woman's laugh, a child's hand, the smell of morning bread, a hundred small golden moments. The thread flowed down into the stone and vanished, and the stone drank it, and the hum deepened with satisfaction.

What remained of Master Corvin stood up. He looked... fine. Tidy. Calm. His face was smooth and pleasant and utterly vacant, like a house with all the furniture removed and fresh paint on the walls. He turned and walked away into a side passage, unhurried, and Wren understood with a cold rolling horror that he wasn't leaving. He was going to stand somewhere and wait to be needed again.

One by one, the congregation came forward. The lamplighter. The fishwife. The noble's footman. Each one knelt, each one poured out the warm gold of themselves into the hungry stone, each one rose up hollowed and serene. The widow Halven was near the end of the line. When her turn came she knelt with something almost like relief, and what poured out of her was thinner than the others, paler, mostly grief — a dead husband, a dying orchard, rain that had come too late to matter — and the stone took even that, greedily, and left her a pleasant empty shell with no shadow and no sorrow and no anything.

Wren realized she was crying only when a tear hit the back of her hand.

*They paid,* she thought. *They prayed for something — rain, sight, coin, a son home from war — and something answered, and this is the second payment. Not the shadow. The shadow was just the receipt.* Her mind flashed to the blind man from chapter six of the city's misfortunes, the one who'd suddenly seen again and then sat in his doorway for three days, smiling at nothing. To the debtor whose coins had multiplied while his eyes went glassy and kind.

*Every miracle leaves a hollowed-out worshipper behind.* Maren had said it like a diagnosis. Now Wren had watched the surgery.

And below — the carved words came back to her — *balance carried below.* Because the stone wasn't the end of it. As the offerings sank into the black surface, Wren could feel, through the floor, through her ribs, through her squeaking terrible borrowed shoes, something vast turning over in the deep dark underneath the altar. Something patient. Something that had been fed a sip at a time for weeks and was growing, and growing, and growing.

The thirteenth moon rose over the capital. Isandro had told them that much, grim-faced, the night the bells rang wrong. Wren hadn't understood what the moon had to do with anything.

Now, lying in the dark with a god's dinner bell humming through the stone under her cheekbone, she began to.

* * *

She might have escaped cleanly. She wants, later, to believe she might have.

But as the last congregant filed away into the side passages and the chamber fell silent, Wren shifted her weight to ease a cramping leg, and her satchel — her stupid, faithful, clerk's satchel — slid off her shoulder and struck the pillar base with a soft leather *thump*.

In the vast silence, it sounded like a cathedral bell.

The hum stopped.

Every head in the side passages — every smooth, pleasant, vacant face — turned toward her hiding place at once, in perfect unison, like a field of flowers finding the sun.

Wren didn't think. Thinking was for people with time. She grabbed her lantern, scrambled up, and ran — back down the corridor, past the carved ledger entries, past the bone-niches, shoes shrieking like a flock of murdered mice, lungs burning, the gray sourceless light behind her flickering as though something enormous had turned its attention toward the tunnels and found them interesting.

Behind her, footsteps. Dozens of them. Unhurried. Perfectly synchronized. Nobody ran, because nobody needed to run; the tunnels bent toward them, she was sure of it, the way water bends downhill, and all the calm empty people had to do was walk.

A stairway — she remembered a stairway, left fork, down and then up — she took it on pure screaming instinct, hands and feet, lantern banging against her hip. Cold air. The sweet rot smell thickening, then thinning. Behind and below, the hum resumed, deeper than before, and she felt it say — not in words, never in words, but with the certainty of a stone dropping into still water:

*I see you, little clerk. I will see you again.*

Then: night air, wet grass, the river district's broken skyline, and Wren sprawled gasping on her back outside a collapsed chapel, staring up at the sky where — high above the capital, faint as a fingernail paring — the thirteenth moon hung in the wrong part of the heavens, a hair closer to full than it had been yesterday.

She lay there until her heart agreed to rejoin the rest of her body. Then she sat up, took out her pencil and her crumpled ledger paper with shaking hands, and wrote, by moonlight, the first honest report of her career:

*A hundred hollowed, feeding themselves to the stone. It is not collecting worship. It is eating them in courses. It knows my face. It is nearly ready.*

She looked at the words. They looked back, terrible and true.

Then she got up, tucked the paper inside her shirt, and ran for the Ledgerhouse — because Isandro needed to hear this tonight, tonight, before the moon turned once more, and because whatever counter-word or rite or desperate miracle could pull a god out of a king, they were going to need to find it fast.

Somewhere behind her, deep under the river district, something patient finished swallowing, and smiled without a mouth, and waited for the next course.
