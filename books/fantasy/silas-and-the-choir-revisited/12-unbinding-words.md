## 12. Unbinding Words

The sealed archive lived beneath the Ledgerhouse the way a heart lives beneath ribs: hidden, essential, and not something you wanted to think about too often.

Isandro had held a key to it for thirty years and had used that key perhaps a dozen times. The lock was old brass, older than the guild itself, and it turned with a sound like a polite cough — *ahem* — as though the door were clearing its throat before saying something it would rather not.

Inside, the air tasted of dust and tallow and time. Shelves of ledgers rose into darkness on every side, their spines labeled in the cramped hand of clerks long dead: *Trades in Years. Trades in Names. Trades in Forgiveness.* The archive was where the Ledgerhouse kept everything it had ever been paid to forget.

Isandro lit three lamps, then a fourth, because some rooms deserved more light than they asked for.

"Right," Isandro said aloud, to no one, because the habit of talking to oneself was one of the few luxuries of being the last swordspeaker. "Let's find out how much this is going to cost."

The word was *unbinding*. Queen Sera hadn't used it — she didn't know it — but Isandro had felt its shape pressing against the inside of his teeth ever since he'd stood in that chapel and heard Aldric beg. *Don't let it finish the turn.* There was only one class of words that could pry a god out of a crowned head, and there was only one person left alive who could speak them.

Which meant there was only one person left alive who could look up what speaking them would do.

The catalogue of binding-rites sat on its lectern near the door, chained by a link so corroded it was mostly ceremonial now. Isandro ran a finger down the index with the particular dread of a man reading a menu at a restaurant where the prices are listed in years.

*Unbinding, Rite of. See: Vhalor Cycle, 3rd.*

Of course. Everything came back to Vhalor eventually, like water finding the lowest ground.

The third shelf of the Vhalor Cycle was behind a sliding panel that required knowing which of seven carved moons to press. Isandro pressed the sixth — the thirteenth moon had never been carved; the founders had refused to give it a shape — and the panel sighed open onto a slim volume bound in grey leather gone soft as skin.

He carried it to the reading table, set all four lamps around it like witnesses, and opened it.

* * *

The hand that had written the rite belonged to a woman named Ellery Vance, First Speaker of the Ledgerhouse, and her script was beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when someone knows they are writing them for strangers centuries away.

*Rite of Unbinding,* the page began. *To be spoken once, wholly, without hesitation or amendment. The word does not ask permission of the world. It tells the world what it has forgotten about itself, and the world, embarrassed, complies.*

Isandro almost smiled. Ellery Vance had apparently shared his weakness for talking to herself on paper.

Then he turned the page, and stopped smiling.

There was a list. Of course there was a list; this was the Ledgerhouse, and the Ledgerhouse kept lists the way other institutions kept secrets — compulsively, exhaustively, and without mercy.

*Speakers who have performed the Rite of Unbinding:*

*Ellery Vance, Year of the Second Frost. Outcome: voice lost entire. She wrote her final ledger entry by pointing at letters on a child's alphabet board. It took four hours. It read: WORTH IT.*

*Tomasin Grell. Outcome: life lost. He completed the final syllable and did not complete the breath after it.*

*Ade Sorrow-Kin. Voice lost, and with it, per witness account, "the part of him that found anything funny."*

*Betha of the Low Hills. Life lost. Her last recorded words, spoken mid-rite, were an apology to her apprentice for the mess.*

On and on. Eleven names. Isandro counted twice, hoping the arithmetic would change. It didn't. Six dead. Five voiceless. Not one speaker whole. The rite worked — that was the terrible thing, the rite always worked, every unbinding on the list had succeeded in tearing loose whatever god or geas it was aimed at — but it collected its fee at the moment of speaking, and it had never once accepted payment in anything less than everything.

Isandro sat back. The lamps guttered in the stillness.

"Well," he said to the empty room. His voice came out smaller than he intended. "That's a robust sample size."

He thought about his own voice. Thirty years of silence about swordspeech, yes — the oath had forbidden the words themselves, not the ordinary human noise of living. He still joked with Wren over breakfast. Still argued appraisals with Maren until she threatened him with her good eye's glare. Still hummed, badly, when he thought no one could hear. All of that was voice too. All of that was on the table.

And the alternative was a king consumed on the turn of a moon, a city kneeling and hollowed, and Oskar marked by something vast and hungry.

Isandro pulled the ledger closer and began, methodically, to copy the rite. If he was going to spend himself, he would at least understand the purchase.

* * *

He was three pages deep into Ellery Vance's marginalia — she had annotated her own rite the way a cook annotates a recipe, *less salt here, the world resents being shouted at* — when the lamp nearest the stairs flickered.

Maren came down the archive steps the way she went everywhere: slowly, deliberately, with the air of a woman who had appraised the staircase and found it acceptable. She carried two cups of tea and the expression of someone who had known exactly where Isandro would be tonight.

"You skipped supper," she said.

"I was working."

"You're always working. Tonight you skipped supper *and* you lied about it badly." She set a cup at his elbow. Chamomile. He'd never told anyone it was his favorite; she'd simply watched him choose it eleven hundred times. That was Maren. She priced nothing she gave away.

She looked at the open ledger. Her one eye moved down the list of names, unhurried, and Isandro watched her reach the same arithmetic he had. Her jaw tightened — a small motion, quickly mastered, but he'd known her twenty years.

"Six dead," she said. "Five mute."

"Five mute, though Ade Sorrow-Kin's apprentice claims losing the funny counts double."

"Don't joke."

"I'm finding it helps."

"It doesn't." She pulled a chair around and sat, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked at the rite itself — the great unspooling counter-word, line after line of language that predated the guild, that predated perhaps language itself. "How long have you known?"

"That I'd have to speak it? Since the chapel." He wrapped his hands around the tea. "That it costs this? Since an hour ago."

"And your plan is to say nothing to anyone and walk up those stairs on the last night and die politely where we can't stop you."

"My plan is to say nothing to anyone and walk up those stairs on the last night and *succeed*, where hopefully nobody needs to stop me. There's a five-in-eleven chance I keep the voice."

"There's a six-in-eleven chance we bury you next to Betha of the Low Hills, who died apologizing for the mess." Maren's voice stayed level, appraiser-flat, but her hand had closed around her teacup hard enough that Isandro half expected it to crack. "I've spent my whole career valuing things, Isandro. Let me tell you what you're worth."

"Maren—"

"You are worth breakfast arguments and bad humming and the way you pretend Wren's handwriting doesn't make you crazy. You are worth the fact that Bramble cheats at cards only against people he likes, and learned that from watching you. You are worth—" her voice caught, just barely, a hairline fracture in stone, "—you are worth more than any word, even that one. Especially that one."

Isandro looked at her. In the lamplight her single eye was bright and furious and terrified, and he understood suddenly that she had come down here not to find him but to find the rite first. That she had been planning, in her slow deliberate way, to beat him to the sacrifice.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard my offer."

"I don't need to. You're going to offer to speak it yourself, and the answer is no."

Her chin lifted. "I've been practicing the shapes of it for two days. My throat is strong. I'm old enough that the arithmetic favors me — what am I losing, thirty years I'd spend squinting at silverware anyway?"

"The rite doesn't take arithmetic. It takes a swordspeaker. That's the whole cruelty of it — look at the list again. Every name on it is a Speaker. It won't accept substitutes. It's not a toll, Maren, it's a birthright being spent." He tapped the page. "You could stand in front of that crown and recite it perfectly and the world would simply decline to be embarrassed."

Something went out of her shoulders. Not defeat — recalibration. Appraisers, when one valuation failed, immediately sought another.

"Then we find another way."

"There isn't one. I've looked. This is the door, and I'm the only key."

"Then we break the door."

"With what?"

"With everything," she said simply. "With the guild, with the queen, with Oskar's visions and Wren's stubbornness and Bramble's entire disreputable network of favors. You keep trying to carry this alone because carrying things alone is the only prayer you were ever taught. Well, I'm telling you the price is wrong. Whatever this costs, it will not be paid by one man in a basement."

Isandro laughed — a short, wet, surprised sound. "When did you become the sentimental one?"

"I've always been the sentimental one. I hide it under professional competence. You'd know that if you ever let anyone appraise *you*." She reached over and closed the ledger gently, her palm flat on the cover, keeping his hands from reopening it. "Promise me one thing. Not that you won't speak it. I know better than to price a promise I can't collect. Promise me that before the last night, you'll tell them. Wren. Bramble. Oskar. Let them know what the word costs, so they can stand beside you knowing, instead of finding out after."

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above, faintly, the house settled on its foundations like an old dog turning in its sleep.

"They'll try to stop me," Isandro said.

"Yes."

"It won't work."

"No. But they'll get to try, and you'll get to be loved while they fail, and that is a trade I'd call fair, and I have an excellent eye."

Isandro looked down at his tea, at the steam rising off it in the cold air, and thought about a child's alphabet board and four hours of pointing, and a woman named Ellery Vance who had spent her last voice on two words and judged them worth it.

"Worth it," he murmured. "That's what she wrote."

"What?"

"Nothing. An old entry." He covered Maren's hand with his own. "I'll tell them. Before the last night. You have my word — and unlike the guild's, mine is still good."

Maren nodded once, satisfied, and rose to go. At the foot of the stairs she paused.

"One more thing," she said. "A rider came at dusk. Palace seal. The queen requests you — tomorrow, an hour past midnight, the east postern, alone." Her eye narrowed. "Queens who summon swordsmen alone at midnight generally want either a dagger or a deniable favor. Wear your good coat."

* * *

Isandro sat alone in the archive a long while after her footsteps faded.

Four lamps. One rite. Eleven names, and room on the list for a twelfth.

He drew the ledger back toward him, opened it to the final page, and beneath Betha's apology and Ellery's alphabet-board epitaph, he wrote in his own careful clerk's hand:

*Isandro Nystrom, pending. Outcome: to be determined. Note to whoever reads this — I intend to argue with the precedent.*

Then he blew out the lamps, climbed the stairs toward the light and the noise and the family he had promised to warn, and began composing, in his head, the hardest sentence he had ever tried to say aloud:

*I may lose my voice. Or worse. And I need you to let me try.*

Somewhere over the capital, unseen behind cloud, the twelfth moon began its slow climb toward fullness — and in the palace above the sleeping city, a queen was already awake, waiting, with a bargain folded up small enough to fit inside a smile.
