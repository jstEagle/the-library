## 13. Sera's Bargain

Queen Sera had learned long ago that the most dangerous conversations in a palace were the ones that never appeared on any schedule.

The dinner with the Ambassador of Vell had been scheduled. The meeting with the wool factors had been scheduled. The quiet hour after both, when she dismissed her ladies, dismissed her guards at the corridor door only, and sent a page with a note written in her own hand to a certain counting-house on Candlemaker's Row — that was the conversation that mattered.

The note had said: *Come by the garden gate. Bring nothing. Tell no one. If you are followed, do not come in; I will know you chose wisely.*

She was not entirely sure the last part was fair. But she had discovered that when one is negotiating with people who traffic in impossible things, it helps to open with an impossible condition. It set the tone.

Now she waited in the queen's private garden — not the show-garden with its clipped hedges and its peacocks that screamed like tax collectors, but the walled kitchen garden behind it, where the herbs grew crooked and honest and nobody important ever came. She sat on a stone bench beneath the quince tree with a shawl around her shoulders and a cup of wine she was not drinking, because she needed her head exactly as clear as it was, which was not very.

The moon overhead was wrong. Everyone in the city could feel it even if they couldn't say why. Thirteen days into its turn and still swelling, still climbing higher than any moon had a right to climb, so that its light fell on the garden like milk poured over everything — beautiful, and faintly spoiled.

Her husband slept under that moon. Or something did, wearing his body the way a hand wears a glove.

Sera had stopped praying about it three nights ago, when she'd caught herself at the chapel rail and realized, with a coldness that went all the way through her, that she did not know *who* was listening anymore. A queen who could not trust her prayers was a queen stripped of half her armor. So she would do what queens did instead of praying.

She would make a deal.

* * *

He came over the wall.

Not through the garden gate, which she had left unlatched for him specifically, but over the wall, dropping into the herb bed with the soft economy of a person who had done this sort of thing before and found it dull. Isandro Nystrom stood, brushed chamomile off one knee, and inclined their head with the precise degree of respect owed to a queen met in secret at midnight: more than a merchant, less than a courtier.

"Your Majesty."

"You were given a gate."

"The gate was watched," Isandro said. "By your man Corven, who is very good, and by two other men pretending to be gardeners, who are not. I assumed the gate was a courtesy rather than an instruction."

It was irritating being read that easily. Sera decided to admire it instead, since admiration cost nothing and irritation showed on the face. "You've been watching my household."

"I watch everyone's household. It's the family business." They glanced up at the swollen moon, then back. "Your Majesty asked me here. I confess I've been curious what a queen wants from the Ledgerhouse that she can't buy through the front door."

"What I want," Sera said, "is for someone to finally tell me the truth. I'm told your house deals in impossible things. Well. Here is mine." She set down the untouched wine. "My husband has not been my husband since the moon began this turn. He smiles at the right moments. He signs what needs signing. Three nights ago he held our daughter and told her a story about a fox, and he told it perfectly, every beat where it belonged — and his eyes never once softened. Do you understand? He performed fatherhood flawlessly, like a man reading music. Aldric has never in his life been flawless at anything. He forgets verses. He laughs in the wrong places. Whatever is sitting at my table wearing his face, it knows the *shape* of him without knowing him at all."

Isandro said nothing. Their silence had a quality she recognized: the silence of someone confirming what they already knew and deciding how much of it to admit.

"You may speak plainly," she said. "I give you my word nothing you say leaves this garden except through me."

"That's a generous offer from a woman I've known for four minutes."

"I've known *of* you for twenty years, Master Nystrom. The Ledgerhouse appears in my accounts eleven times a year, always small sums, always for 'clerical services,' always paid promptly. In twenty years you have never once asked for anything. Do you know how loud that is? Every guild, every temple, every cousin in this kingdom wants something from the crown. Your house wants nothing. Which means either you need nothing—" she tilted her head, "—or you are protecting something so valuable that wanting would draw attention to it. Either way, you are the only institution in my capital whose interests might possibly align with mine tonight. So. Plainly, please. What is wearing my husband?"

Isandro looked at the quince tree for a moment. When they spoke, it was quietly, without ornament, and Sera understood that this plainness was itself a kind of gift.

"A god. An old one. Vhalor. The name won't mean anything to you, and I'd ask you not to go looking for it, because the last kingdom that looked for it isn't here anymore. He answers prayers. That's what he does — that's all he does, but he does it completely. You pray, and you receive, and then he receives *you*. Your husband prayed for something he wanted desperately enough to mean it with his whole heart. Something answered. And now it's finishing the work of moving in. When the moon completes its turn — three days from now — there will be nothing of Aldric left to wake up. Just a crown with a god inside it, and a kingdom full of people who've already learned to kneel."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, the city murmured in its sleep, thousands of people dreaming under the too-bright moon.

Sera found, to her surprise, that her hands were steady. She had trained them to be. Grief could come later, in private, in the proper quantities. "And can it be undone?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"It costs more than I can describe and I intend to pay it anyway, so let's not spend time haggling over the price of my soul. What I came to discuss is what happens *after*."

"After," Sera repeated.

"After. Because here is what I know about kingdoms, Your Majesty, having recorded their secrets for decades: they forgive assassins faster than they forgive witnesses. If I unbind your husband in front of the court — and it will be public, it must be public, or the story will grow teeth in the dark — then tomorrow morning there will be a king who was possessed, a queen who knew and consorted with sorcerers, and a guild that made it all possible. Someone will need to be blamed. It will not be the crown. Crowns are never blamed; that's what crowns are *for*. It will be us. And the Ledgerhouse has survived twenty centuries by being invisible. After that night, we will not be able to be invisible anymore. We'll be the answer to the question everyone is asking."

Sera felt the shape of it before he finished — felt it the way she felt weather coming in her knee, the old fencing injury that had taught her more about politics than any tutor. "You want protection."

"I want a charter. New, sealed, witnessed by the full council. The Ledgerhouse incorporated under crown law as a house of record — a house of *truth*, if you like, though I know how that word sounds in a treaty. Its clerks protected. Its premises inviolate. Its records privileged, the way a confessor's are. In exchange, the guild gives you its cooperation: everything we know about Vhalor, everything we hold that might help, and whatever assistance my particular talents allow."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will still save your husband," Isandro said, "because refusing to save him would make me the kind of person the guild has spent twenty centuries pretending not to be. But afterward we will burn the ledgers and scatter, and the knowledge of how Vhalor was bound — knowledge your grandchildren may someday need — will be gone. You'd win the night and lose the archive."

There it was. No threat, no plea. Just arithmetic, laid out honestly between two people who both preferred honest arithmetic to almost anything else in the world.

Sera almost laughed. "Do you know, Master Nystrom, that this is the most pleasant negotiation I've had in nine years of marriage to politics?"

"My colleagues say I'm pleasant. They say it in the tone people use for *difficult*, but I've chosen to take it as praise."

* * *

They walked the garden while they worked out terms, because Sera thought better on her feet and suspected, correctly, that Isandro did too. Past the quince tree, past the beds of sage and thorned roses, along the wall where the espaliered pears grew flat against the stone like courtiers pressed to a palace wall.

"Clause four," Sera said. "The charter protects the house. It does not protect individuals who commit crimes. If one of your clerks poisons a rival appraiser, I will hang him like anyone else."

"Agreed, provided 'crimes' means actual crimes and not 'offending the temple' or 'knowing things the council wishes unknown.' I've seen what passes for crime when a guild gets inconvenient."

"Clause five. The house submits to royal audit once a year. One auditor, sworn to secrecy, full access."

Isandro stopped walking. For a moment Sera saw genuine conflict cross their face — the first unguarded thing she'd seen there. "An outsider in the archive," they said slowly. "Do you know what we keep in the archive?"

"No. And I suspect that's precisely why I want an auditor."

A long pause. Then, unexpectedly, Isandro smiled — a small, dry, real smile, the first crack in the professional surface. "You're good at this."

"I've been practicing since I was fourteen and my father sold me into a treaty. Clause five stands?"

"Clause five stands." They resumed walking. "Though I warn you, whoever you send will come back changed. People who read true ledgers are never quite the same afterward. We had an apprentice cry for a week over the truth about the herring trade."

"The herring trade?"

"There are things in those barrels, Your Majesty, that no fisherman has ever caught."

"I will pretend I didn't hear that, for both our sakes."

They reached the far corner of the garden, where the wall met the gate back toward the queen's apartments, and stopped. The moonlight lay thick on the flagstones. Sera turned to face him fully, and let the negotiator step aside for just a moment, because there was a question she had been carrying since the third night of the wrong moon, and it would not survive another day in her chest.

"If it comes to it," she said, "if the choice is my husband or the rite going perfectly — if saving him means risking the city — I need to know now whether you'll hesitate. Because I love Aldric. I have loved him since before he was anyone, when he was just a second son with mud on his boots and terrible opinions about poetry. And I am telling you, as one pragmatist to another, that if it comes to that choice, I will choose the city, and I need to know the person holding the knife will make the same choice without waiting for my permission."

Isandro held her gaze. There was no flinch in it, and no comfort either.

"I have hesitated once in my life," they said, "and it cost a village everything. I don't hesitate anymore. But I'll tell you what I will do, Majesty: I will find the third option. The one where nobody pays. I will look for it until the moon finishes turning, and if it doesn't exist, I will grieve it and pay the price that does exist. That's the best bargain I can offer you, and unlike most bargains, it's the whole truth."

Sera studied them for a long moment. Then she extended her hand — not the formal gesture, palm-down, for kissing, but the level one, trader to trader.

"Then we have a bargain," she said. "Witnessed by the moon, since apparently that thing listens to everything else. The charter for the cooperation. My protection for your courage. And Master Nystrom — when it's done, when my husband wakes or doesn't — come to the palace. Through the front door. I'll have the wool factors thrown out early and we'll drink the good wine, the kind I hide from the cellar steward, and you can tell me about the herring."

"I look forward to it," Isandro said, and shook her hand. Their grip was cool and dry and very steady. "One more thing, Majesty, and then I'll trouble your pear trees no further."

"Name it."

"Trust no one in your household with what we've spoken tonight. Not your ladies. Not your guards. Especially not anyone who has prayed lately, or been near someone who has. Vhalor hears through the hollow places, and he is very patient, and he has had three days to learn the shape of your court."

Something cold walked down Sera's spine, because she thought — she had been trying not to think it for two days — of her chamberlain, Devos, who had lost a granddaughter in the spring fever and had taken, six days ago, to attending evening services again after thirty years of cheerful indifference. Who had seemed so much *better* since. Calmer. Smoother. Agreeable in a way that reminded her, now, horribly, of a king telling stories about foxes.

"He prays," she heard herself say. "My chamberlain. He started again last week. After the fever took his granddaughter."

Isandro went very still. "Does he seem himself?"

"He seems *improved*. That's what frightens me."

"Keep him away from the palace chapel. Keep yourself away from it. Pray nowhere, worship nothing, until this is done." Isandro stepped back toward the wall, then paused. "And Majesty — the bargain we struck tonight. Write nothing down. No note, no draft, no memory-jogger. From this moment, the only copy of our agreement lives in your head and mine."

"Spoken like a man who's seen what happens to paper."

"Spoken like a man who *is* paper," Isandro said, "and knows exactly how easily it burns."

Then they were up the wall and gone, quick and silent as a ledger entry closing, and Sera stood alone in the silvered garden with a bargain in her head and a cold new arithmetic in her chest.

Three days. One chamberlain she could no longer count. One husband to dig out of a god.

She picked up the wine cup at last, considered it, and poured it out over the rose bed — a libation to no one, offered to nothing, which was, these nights, the only prayer she trusted.

Inside the palace, in a room lit by a moon that should not have been so bright, Chamberlain Devos was writing a letter. He did not know to whom. His hand simply moved, smooth and calm and improved, and the words it wrote were:

*The queen met with a clerk of the Ledgerhouse tonight. In the garden. By the pear trees.*

Below the altar stone in the catacombs, something old turned the words over, and smiled with a borrowed mouth, and began — patiently, the way it did everything — to plan.
