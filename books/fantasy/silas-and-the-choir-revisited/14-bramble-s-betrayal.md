## 14. Bramble's Betrayal

Bramble had stolen things from kings, temples, tax collectors, and one particularly vindictive duchess who kept a grudge like other people kept cats. In forty years of toll-collecting and toll-dodging, she had never once felt bad about it. Theft, in her professional opinion, was a victimless crime as long as you picked your victims properly.

So it was a novel and deeply unpleasant sensation to sit in the guild council chamber on the third floor of the Ledgerhouse, being asked to steal from her own family, and to feel her stomach turn over like a rowboat in weather.

"Master Bramble." Councillor Hesk spoke the way a man closes a ledger: firmly, with a little snap at the end. He was a narrow person made narrower by his robes, with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that had spent so long appraising entries they had forgotten how to look at faces. "You understand why we've called on you specifically."

"Because I'm old," Bramble said, "and therefore assumed to be sensible."

"Because you were a thief-toll collector for four decades," said Councillor Vess, who sat to Hesk's left and had the disconcerting habit of smiling while she said terrible things. "You know how thieves think. You know how they move. And Isandro Nystrom is moving like a thief these days."

"They're not a thief," Bramble said. "They're the most honest person in this building, which granted is a low bar, we're an entire house built on not saying things out loud—"

"The copies," Hesk said, ignoring her. "Pages from the sealed archive. The rite of unbinding. The founding records concerning Vhalor. Someone has been transcribing restricted material and removing it from this house. We believe it is Isandro. We require confirmation."

Bramble looked at the table. It was a very fine table, blackwood, older than the city walls, polished by two centuries of nervous hands. She had once lifted a candlestick off a cardinal's altar with less guilt than she now felt simply sitting here.

"And if I confirm it?" she asked. "What then?"

"Then the council acts to protect the ledgers," Vess said, still smiling. "The pages will be seized. The offender will be expelled. Neutrality preserved. The house endures."

"The house endures," Bramble repeated. She turned the phrase over the way she used to turn over coins to check for shaving. "That's a funny way of putting it. Most people would say *the people endure*. But then, most people aren't a council."

Hesk's jaw tightened. "Master Bramble. You have been with this house twenty-two years. You have a room on the fourth floor with a window facing the harbor. You have a pension accrued against your service. You have—" he paused, delicately, "—a history that would not survive scrutiny in certain quarters, should certain parties be made aware of it."

And there it was. Bramble had been waiting for it since she walked in, the way you wait for the third bell of a trap. They'd dug up her past. Not even all of it — just enough of it, held up to the light like a jeweler showing flaws.

"You're threatening me," she said.

"We are reminding you," Vess said gently, "of where your interests lie."

"My interests lie in a bowl of plum porridge every morning at half seven," Bramble said, "eaten across from a one-eyed woman who cheats at cards and a boy who draws pictures of the moon because words won't come out of him right. My interests are sitting downstairs right now worrying about me, because Wren worries about everyone, it's her whole personality. So let's not pretend my interests are a pension."

The councillors exchanged a glance. Bramble had seen that exact glance before, across negotiation tables, between magistrates deciding whether a smuggler was worth hanging or merely fining. It was the glance of men and women doing arithmetic with someone else's life.

"Take the night," Hesk said. "Consider. Report to us tomorrow at dawn, either way. If you choose silence, no harm comes to you — but the council will find its own path, and we may be forced to involve the constabulary after all. A thief-toll collector's former colleagues might find the sealed archive a fascinating place to visit."

*There it was again.* Not just her past — her people. Old colleagues, old debts, old names she hadn't spoken aloud in decades. The council couldn't search the archive themselves without scandal, but a few retired toll-men with lanterns and no scruples? That could be arranged quietly.

Bramble stood. Her knees cracked; she was seventy-one and proud of every crack. "I'll consider it," she said, which was true. She considered everything. It was what had kept her alive.

She considered it all the way down the stairs, past the second-floor reading rooms where junior clerks bent over their copying like rows of patient birds, past the first-floor hall where Maren was arguing with a merchant about the authenticity of a saint's fingerbone ("It's a pig's toe, Master Corvel, I've counted the joints"), and into the kitchen, where Oskar was peeling apples with the intense concentration of a boy defusing something explosive.

He looked up when she came in. He always knew when something was wrong. The boy couldn't speak, but he heard everything else — footsteps, silences, the particular creak of a door opened by someone carrying bad news.

"Don't look at me like that," Bramble told him. "I'm fine. Everything's fine."

Oskar set down his knife, wiped his hands on his apron with tremendous dignity, and pointed at her face. Then he drew a circle in the air around his own eye.

"I do *not* look worried."

He nodded solemnly.

"I look *distinguished*. There's a difference."

He considered this, then shrugged in a way that meant *if you say so*, which was the shrug of a boy raised by a kitchen and a household of liars, and went back to his apples. Bramble sat down at the scarred kitchen table and watched him work, and thought about how small his hands still were, and how the council wanted her to trade all of this — the apples, the porridge, the cheating at cards, the whole improbable family that had assembled itself around a silent swordspeaker like iron filings around a magnet — for a pension and a clean conscience she wouldn't be able to live inside anyway.

The trouble was, she thought, watching Oskar line up his apple peels in a neat spiral, that the council wasn't wrong about the danger. That was the filthy genius of it. Isandro *was* stealing pages. Isandro *was* planning something that would burn the house's invisibility to ash. And the thirteenth moon *was* rising over the capital like a coin somebody had slipped into the sky's purse without counting it. Fear made excellent glue. The council was simply gluing the wrong things together.

* * *

She tried to warn them. That was the thing she would hold onto later, in the bad nights, when she needed something to hold onto: she tried.

She caught Isandro in the archive corridor at dusk, that narrow stone throat between the sealed wing and the stairwell where nobody lingered because it was cold and smelled of dust and old decisions. Isandro had a satchel over one shoulder and the guilty walk of a person who had gotten too good at walking guilty and stopped noticing.

"Bramble." Isandro smiled, and it was the tired kind of smile, the kind that costs. "If you're here to tell me dinner's ready, I'm afraid I'm—"

"I know what you're doing," Bramble said. "The copying. The pages. Don't make that face, I've watched forty years of smugglers make that face, yours needs work. You drop your shoulder before you lie."

Isandro's hand went, absurdly, to their shoulder.

"The council knows," Bramble said. "They don't know everything, but they know enough, and tomorrow morning they're going to come down on you like a dropped chandelier. They leaned on me today. My past, my pension, my old colleagues — the whole sad toolkit." She crossed her arms. "I'm telling you because I haven't decided yet what I'm going to say to them at dawn, and I wanted you to hear it from me first, whatever I decide. You deserve that much."

For a moment Isandro just looked at her. In the lamplight their face did something complicated — gratitude and grief and a stubbornness Bramble recognized because she saw it in the mirror some mornings.

"You shouldn't tell them anything," Isandro said quietly. "Whatever you decide — don't spend yourself on me, Bramble. I knew the cost when I started copying. This is mine to pay."

"Oh, shut up," Bramble said affectionately. "You sound like a martyr in a cheap play. Nobody gets to decide alone what this family pays. That's the whole point of a family, you great solemn idiot. We split the bill."

Isandro laughed — a short, startled laugh, like a bird getting up off water — and for one moment the corridor was almost warm.

Then Bramble went home to her room with the harbor view, and lay awake under her good quilt listening to the gulls argue, and did not sleep, and at some hour past midnight she got up and looked out at the thirteenth moon hanging fat and wrong above the palace, and understood with perfect clarity that she was going to betray them.

Not because she was a coward. Well — partly because she was a coward; she was seventy-one and honest with herself about her inventory. But mostly because she had done the arithmetic the council wanted her to do and found it came out differently than they expected. If she stayed silent, the council sent for her old colleagues, and those men would tear through the archive and the house and everyone in it, clumsily, loudly, and Isandro would be expelled anyway — worse, arrested, maybe hanged, and the pages seized regardless, and the family broken anyway, only messier. If she reported — if she gave them Isandro's thefts herself, cleanly, contained — then it was expulsion. Expulsion was survivable. Expulsion was a wound, not a grave.

She hated the arithmetic. She despised it with her whole heart. But she had been a toll collector, and toll collectors learn early that every road costs something, and your job is only ever to pick which price gets paid.

"I'm sorry, love," she said to the moon, to Isandro, to nobody. "I'm sorry. I'm choosing the smaller bleeding."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. Bramble walked into the council chamber with her spine straight and her hands steady, and she told them.

She told them about the copying, the satchel, the corridor meetings, the pages hidden in the false bottom of Isandro's writing desk — she gave them everything except the parts that mattered most: nothing about Sera, nothing about the chapel, nothing about the counter-word in the grave-marker three days' ride south. She trimmed the betrayal like a careful butcher, leaving the council exactly enough meat to feel fed and none of the bones they could actually choke Isandro with.

They searched the desk within the hour. They found the copies — the unbinding rite, the founding records, the pages about Vhalor — and carried them upstairs in a locked box, and Councillor Vess smiled her terrible smile the whole time.

The summons came at midmorning. Bramble insisted on being present, and Hesk, perhaps sensing he owed her, allowed it.

They convened in the great hall instead of the council chamber, which told Bramble everything: the council wanted witnesses. Clerks lined the balconies. Wren stood among them, white-faced, her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield. Maren stood at the back with her one eye fixed on the proceedings and her jaw set like a dock cleat. Oskar wasn't supposed to be there, which meant of course he was, pressed into the shadows by the kitchen door with flour still on his sleeves.

Isandro stood in the center of the floor in their plain clerk's coat, and did not deny anything. That was the worst part, Bramble thought. Some defiant spark might have saved them — councils are superstitious animals, and sometimes conviction frightens them more than guilt does. But Isandro only stood straight and quiet and answered each question with the truth, plainly, as though reading entries aloud, and the truth was so much larger than the council had prepared for that it frightened them visibly.

"You copied the rite of unbinding," Hesk read from the seized pages. "You intended to use it."

"I intend to save the king's life," Isandro said.

"The king is not our client."

"The king is a man wearing a god like a collar," Isandro said, and something in their voice rang, faintly, the old swordspeech resonance that Bramble had only heard once before, years ago, and never mentioned, "and every soul in this city is chained to the same leash. Neutrality is a luxury of people who believe the storm will pass around their house. It won't. I've read the founding records. *You've* read the founding records. This house was built to keep Vhalor buried, and you've spent two hundred years polishing the gravestone and calling it duty."

"The founding records are sealed," Vess said smoothly, "which you well know, since you stole them."

A ripple of uneasy laughter ran along the balconies. Bramble didn't laugh. Neither did Maren.

Hesk pronounced sentence with the relish of a man who had rehearsed. "Isandro Nystrom, for theft from the sealed archive, for breach of oath, and for conduct endangering the neutrality of this house: you are expelled. Your keys are forfeit. Your rooms are forfeit. You are struck from the rolls of the Ledgerhouse, and your name will not appear in any entry henceforth." He paused, and added, with what he probably believed was mercy: "You may collect your personal effects under supervision."

"That's generous," Isandro said. "Considering you're erasing me."

"You were never in the ledgers to begin with," Vess said. "None of us are. That is rather the point of the house."

"Yes," Isandro said softly. "It was. And look what it's become."

They gathered their effects in silence — a spare coat, a whetstone, a wooden bird Oskar had carved them years ago, a handful of books — while clerks watched from doorways with the particular cruelty of the innocent, who cannot help staring. Bramble stood in the corridor outside Isandro's room and waited until the supervision ended and the watchers drifted away, and then she stepped inside without knocking.

Isandro looked up. Their face was composed, which is to say it was a locked door, and Bramble had picked better doors than this with less reason.

"Say it," Isandro said. "Whatever you came to say. Say it and mean it, because I can't afford politeness today."

"It was me," Bramble said. "I told them. Where the pages were, the desk, the copying. All of it."

"I know. I saw you in the hall." Isandro folded the wooden bird into their pack with exaggerated care. "I figured it out last night, after you warned me. You warned me *first*, Bramble. You gave me the whole night to run, and I chose to stay, so whatever you told them at dawn — you told them about a choice I'd already made."

"That doesn't make it not a betrayal."

"No," Isandro agreed. "But it makes it a betrayal with a warning label. There are worse kinds." They looked up then, and their eyes were wet and furious and fond, all at once, which was a combination Bramble had never managed in seventy-one years and doubted she could produce now. "Why? Truly. I want to understand, because I need to understand whether I've lost you or whether you've lost yourself."

Bramble sat down heavily on the edge of the stripped bed, because her knees had opinions and her heart was worse. "Because they'd have sent for my old crew otherwise. Because expulsion is survivable and hanging isn't. Because I did the sums and hated every number." Her voice cracked, damn it, cracked like a plate. "Because I love you, you impossible creature, and loving people is the one theft I never learned to do cleanly. I take things. I've always taken things. Giving something up — giving *you* up, to keep you breathing — that's a skill I don't have. I botched it. I'm sorry."

For a long moment Isandro said nothing. Then they crossed the small room and took Bramble's weathered hands in theirs, and held them the way you hold something you're afraid of breaking.

"When this is over," Isandro said, "when the moon finishes its turn or I stop it trying — the house is going to change or it's going to die, but either way, I'm coming back for you. For all of you. Wren, Maren, Oskar. You especially, you treacherous old villain. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got," Bramble said, "until the sums change."

Isandro squeezed her hands once, hard, and shouldered their pack, and walked out of the room, down the stairs, past the hall where two centuries of invisible trades sat recorded in books nobody would ever be allowed to read, and out the front doors of the only home they had known since childhood, into a street already filling with strangers kneeling toward the palace, their foreheads to the cobbles, their faces empty as unpaid accounts.

Bramble watched from the window until Isandro turned the corner and was gone.

Then she went downstairs, because standing still was unbearable, and found the kitchen in an uproar. Wren was there, pale and furious, arguing with Maren in a fierce whisper, and Oskar stood between them holding up a drawing — a fresh one, charcoal still smudging his fingers.

Bramble looked at it and felt the cold go through her like a blade through oiled leather.

Oskar had drawn the catacombs. He had drawn the altar stone, and the hollowed congregation kneeling around it in their dozens, and below the stone, rendered in a child's furious scrawl, something vast turning over in its sleep — and beside it, small and clear and unmistakable, he had drawn Wren. And himself. Walking down the stairs together.

"Wren," Bramble said slowly. "Where are you going?"

Wren took the drawing from Oskar's hands, looked at it, and set her jaw in a way that Bramble recognized — the same stubborn line Isandro wore, the whole ridiculous family infected with it.

"Nowhere," Wren said. "Yet."

Which, Bramble thought grimly, watching the boy and the girl exchange a look full of terrible courage, was the least convincing lie she had heard in forty years of hearing lies for a living.
