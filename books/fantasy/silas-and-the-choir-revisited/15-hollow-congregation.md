## 15. Hollow Congregation

**WREN**

The plan, such as it was, fit on the back of a receipt.

Wren had written it out that morning at her desk in the counting room, in the small tidy hand Master Isandro praised and Maren called "suspiciously legible for a clerk your age." It read:

1. Go down.
2. Make noise (small).
3. Get Oskar back up.
4. Do not die. (Underlined twice.)

It was not, she admitted, a plan of the caliber that won wars. It was barely a plan that won arguments. But Isandro was gone—gone to the ruined village where they'd been born, gone to dig a counter-word out of their mother's grave like some grim family heirloom—and Bramble was busy turning traitor in the useful direction, and Maren was burning ledgers, and somebody had to do something before the twelfth turn became the thirteenth, and that somebody, apparently, was a junior clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a kitchen boy who couldn't say a word but had never once needed to.

"You don't have to come," Wren said, for the fourth time.

They stood at the mouth of the catacombs beneath the Widow's district, where the old chapel had sunk into the hill like a tooth into gum. The door was iron, older than the guild, older probably than the city's manners. Cold came off it the way cold comes off a well.

Oskar looked at her. Then he held up his slate, on which he had written, in his careful round letters: *You'd get lost without me.*

"I would not."

*You got lost in the Ledgerhouse. Twice. In one week.*

"That was the second week of my employment and the corridors are deliberately confusing, it's a security feature—"

Oskar was already smiling, that wide quiet smile of his, and tucking the slate into his belt next to his chalk. He had brought, Wren noticed, three pieces of chalk, a stub of candle, half a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth, and a wooden top he'd owned since before anyone at the house could remember. She did not ask about the top. Some things you didn't ask Oskar about; they were load-bearing.

"Fine," she said. "But we stick to the plan."

He tapped the plan in her pocket, then made a gesture—the one he used for "underlined twice"—and grinned.

The iron door opened easier than it should have. That was the first wrong thing. Doors that old fought you. This one swung inward with a courtesy that felt like an invitation, and Wren thought of every ledger entry she'd ever copied that ended in a number too neat to be true, and she thought: *this is what a trap looks like when it's been waiting a long time.*

She went in anyway. Oskar's shoulder bumped hers as he followed, which was his way of saying *I'm here*, and she found, to her surprise, that it helped.

* * *

**OSKAR**

Down was different from up.

Oskar knew this the way he knew most things—not in words, because words were a country he'd been locked out of since before memory, but in pictures. Up, the city was all sharp lines and shouting shapes, everyone talking over each other, mouths opening and closing like fish in a barrel. Down here the shapes went soft. The dark was thick as wool. And underneath everything, under the stone and the old bones and the dripping, there was a sound.

Not a sound, exactly. A *pull*. Like the feeling of looking over a ledge.

He didn't tell Wren. Wren worried the way Maren polished her glass eye—constantly, thoroughly, and with more force than strictly necessary. If Wren knew the pull was there, she'd make him go back up, and then nobody would be watching her back, and Wren walking into danger unwatched was a picture Oskar liked even less than the pull.

So he walked behind her, one hand near her elbow the way she'd taught him years ago—"so I always know where you are, Os, even in a crowd"—and he counted things, because counting kept the pictures orderly. Forty-one steps. A niche with a clay lamp long dead. Fifty-three steps. A skull with a crack like a laughing mouth. Sixty-two steps, and the pull got stronger, and the pictures started coming whether he wanted them or not.

In the pictures there was a moon, low and fat and wrong-colored, like a coin someone had bitten. Under it knelt rows and rows of people, and their heads were bowed so far forward they touched the ground, and from each bowed head rose something thin and silver, like thread off a spindle, and all the threads ran down, down into a hole in the world, and the hole drank.

Oskar stopped counting.

Wren turned. "Os? You've gone gray as Bramble's porridge."

He shook his head. He took out the slate and wrote: *Close.*

"How close?"

He wrote: *We're already inside its mouth.* Then, because Wren's face did a thing he didn't like, he added: *Joke.*

"It wasn't funny."

*Little bit funny,* he wrote, and after a moment, reluctantly, she snorted, and the pictures loosened their grip, and they went on.

* * *

**WREN**

The catacombs opened, without warning, into a space that should not have existed.

Wren had copied the city's property rolls. She knew, in the boring way clerks know things, exactly how much ground the Widow's district occupied and how much of it was cellar. This chamber violated the arithmetic. Pillars marched away into darkness on both sides, and the ceiling was lost somewhere above the reach of Oskar's little candle-flame, and the floor sloped gently down toward an altar stone that sat in the middle distance like a black table set for a very large, very patient guest.

And around the altar, kneeling, were people.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. They knelt in ranks so precise they might have been laid out with a ruler, and none of them moved except to breathe, and their breathing was all in time—one long inhale, one long exhale, the whole congregation rising and falling like a single sleeping animal.

"There," Wren whispered, gripping Oskar's sleeve. "Third row. The widow."

She recognized the woman from the transaction ledger—the shadow-trade, the rain over the orchard. Isandro had recorded it in a hand shakier than Wren had ever seen from them. The widow knelt like all the rest, head bowed, and where her shadow should have pooled in the candlelight there was nothing at all, just bare stone, just absence shaped exactly like a woman's absence.

"She came here," Wren breathed. "After. The trade took her shadow and she came here looking for it, and it—" Her throat closed. "It kept her."

Oskar tugged her sleeve and pointed.

Above the altar, where a god's statue would stand in any honest chapel, there was only empty air. But the air was *busy*. It shimmered the way heat shimmers over a summer road, and through the shimmer Wren could see—she wished she couldn't—threads. Thin silver threads, hundreds of them, running out of the bowed heads of the congregation and up into the shimmering, and the shimmering pulled them in slow, steady swallows, like a man drinking broth.

"The feeding," she whispered. "This is what we saw in the ledger. This is where the prayers go after they're answered. He doesn't just take miracles as payment—he takes the *pray-ers*."

Oskar's face, in the candlelight, had gone very still. Stillness, in Oskar, was not calm. Wren had learned that in year one, when a delivery cart nearly clipped him and he'd stood perfectly motionless while the whole street screamed. Stillness meant the pictures were loud.

"We disrupt it," she said. "That's step two. Make noise. Small noise." She looked at the ranks of kneeling bodies, at the drinking air above the altar, and revised. "Medium noise. What breaks a ritual? In the stories it's always—someone speaks the wrong name, or the bell rings wrong, or someone *laughs*, or—"

Oskar stepped past her.

"Os—"

He walked out onto the floor of the cavern, into the open, holding his candle in one hand and, God help them, the wooden top in the other, and Wren's heart performed a maneuver no heart should attempt. He stopped ten feet from the nearest rank of kneelers, close enough to touch the widow's bowed head, and he looked up at the shimmering air, and he did not kneel.

Then he wound the string around the top, crouched, and threw it.

* * *

**OSKAR**

The top spun.

It spun the way it had spun on the kitchen floor a thousand evenings, humming its low wooden hum, wobbling and righting itself, and Oskar watched it with the whole of his attention because the whole of his attention was the point. The pull wanted kneeling. The pull wanted bowed heads and silver threads and going down. The top did not go down. The top danced.

The hum cut across the congregation's breathing like a knife through rope.

One by one, heads lifted. Not many—a dozen, two dozen—but they lifted, and eyes that had been fixed on nothing fixed instead on the small spinning thing, and Oskar saw in those eyes something flicker that hadn't been there before. Recognition. Memory. A woman remembering a child's toy. A man remembering being someone who laughed.

The threads wavered.

Above the altar, the shimmering air *stopped drinking*. Oskar felt it the way you feel a hand lift from the back of your neck. The whole cavern seemed to inhale and hold it, and in the held silence the top spun on, stubborn and silly and alive, and Oskar thought: *good. Good. Keep looking at it. Look at the toy. Don't look at me.*

Because he could feel it looking anyway. Something vast behind the shimmer had noticed the interruption, and it was turning its attention toward the source, and its attention was not like a person's attention. A person's attention touches you and moves on. This attention *arrived*. It pressed down on him like deep water, like snowfall, like a name being slowly remembered, and in the pressure he heard—not words, never words, but meaning, arriving whole:

*What are you?*

Oskar picked up the top. Held it against his chest. And smiled at the shimmering air, the way you smile at a big dog, showing it you're friendly and also that you have absolutely no intention of running, because running is what prey does.

*Someone who doesn't kneel,* he thought, as hard as he could.

The pressure tightened. Delighted, somehow. Interested the way a collector is interested.

* * *

**WREN**

"Os, *back away*—"

Wren was moving before she finished the sentence, which was against every rule of sense and several rules of the plan, but the air above the altar had begun to change color, deepening from heat-shimmer to something bruised and violet, and the pressure in the room had doubled, tripled, and Oskar stood in the middle of it holding a toy like David facing Goliath with significantly worse equipment.

She grabbed his arm. He let himself be pulled, which frightened her more than resistance would have—he was never passive unless something had hold of him—and together they backed toward the corridor while the kneeling congregation's breathing stuttered out of rhythm for the first time.

Then the light above the altar gathered itself into a shape.

Not a body. An outline of one, drawn in that bruised violet, tall as the pillars, crowned in something that was either flame or the idea of flame. It had no face, but Wren felt it regard them with the unhurried interest of a moneylender reviewing a debtor's file.

And it spoke. Not aloud—the voice arrived inside her skull, courteous as a contract:

***Small thieves. You have stolen four turns from me. I will remember your faces.***

It reached—not physically, but the way a sentence reaches—and its reaching passed over Wren like a cold draft and settled, with terrible precision, on Oskar.

"No!" Wren threw herself between them, arms spread, which was absurd, she was five foot two and the shape was the height of a cathedral, but she spread them anyway. "Take me instead—he's mute, he's nobody, he's just a kitchen boy—"

The amusement that rolled off the shape was almost warm.

***Nobody?*** it said, and sounded genuinely entertained. ***Child, I have eaten kings and found less in them than in this one. He is full of pictures. I like pictures.***

A tendril of violet light, thin as the silver threads, drifted down and touched Oskar's forehead, just above his left eyebrow, lightly, the way you'd mark a page in a book you intended to return to.

Oskar didn't flinch. He looked up at the towering shape, and—Wren saw it happen, saw it clear as copying a line of figures—he raised his slate and wrote on it, big enough for a god to read:

*WE KNOW YOUR NAME.*

The shape recoiled. Actually recoiled, the violet outline rippling like water struck by a stone, and the pressure in the cavern lurched, and in the lurch Wren seized Oskar's hand and *ran*.

* * *

**OSKAR**

Running. Wren's hand in his. Candle dead, top safe in his pocket, bread abandoned somewhere it would confuse archaeologists. Behind them the cavern roared—not angry, Oskar thought, not exactly. Startled. A thing that had been drinking undisturbed for centuries, suddenly splashed.

They took corners blind. Wren counted steps aloud—her counting, her numbers, her brave cracked voice saying "forty, forty-one, the skull, left here—" and Oskar followed the sound of her because the sound of her was the best picture he had.

But the mark stayed.

He could feel it above his eyebrow, cool and faintly buzzing, like a fly asleep on his skin. Not hurting. Worse than hurting: *waiting*. Somewhere behind his eyes, in the country of pictures, a new picture had been installed without his permission—a violet crown, turning slowly, keeping time with a moon.

And threaded through it, patient as interest accruing, a meaning he couldn't shake:

*I will see you again, little counter. Bring your friends.*

They burst out of the iron door into night air and real moonlight, and Wren slammed the door and put her back against it, gasping, laughing, crying, possibly all three. Oskar bent double beside her, hands on knees, breath sawing.

"We did it," Wren wheezed. "Step two. Noise. Medium noise. We disrupted it—we bought time, Isandro needs time, that's—that's the whole job, we bought—"

She stopped. She was looking at his forehead.

Oskar touched the spot above his eyebrow. His fingers came away clean. But when he glanced at his reflection in the door's black iron, there it was: a small violet mark, fine as a signature, shaped exactly like a tiny turning crown.

Wren's face went white. "Os. What did it do to you?"

He took out the slate. His hand, he noted distantly, was steady. Someone had to be.

He wrote:

*It marked me. It knows my name now. And it said—*

His chalk hovered. How did you write *it likes my pictures*? How did you write *it thinks I'm interesting*? How did you write the worst part, the part sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal?

He wrote the worst part.

*Tell Isandro to hurry. It's expecting company.*
