## 16. The Twelfth Turn

The moon had been whole for eleven nights now, and every night it looked a little less like the moon and a little more like an eye.

Isandro noticed it first in the way the light fell. Ordinary moonlight lay on things; this light *regarded* them. It pooled in doorways like a visitor who would not leave. It found the gaps between shutters. On the road north out of the capital, riding a rented mule named Duchess who had strong opinions about hills, Isandro kept their hood up not against the cold but against being seen.

"You feel it too," they murmured to the mule.

Duchess flicked one ear backward, which was either agreement or a demand for oats. With Duchess it was usually both.

They had left before dawn, without ceremony, which for the Ledgerhouse was itself a ceremony. Maren had pressed a parcel of dried plums into their hands at the stable door and said, "You'll eat these or you won't, but I'm contractually obligated to offer." Bramble had pretended to be asleep in his chair by the fire and then, as Isandro passed, said without opening his eyes, "Graveyards are quiet. Quiet's good for thinking. Don't think yourself into a hole you can't climb out of." And Wren—

Wren had hugged them. Quickly, fiercely, the way Wren did everything, and then stepped back and said, "Bring back the word. Not the oath. The *word*. There's a difference."

"There is?"

"I don't know yet," Wren admitted. "But there's a difference. I can feel it in my teeth."

Isandro had thought about that the whole way north. Three days of it, through orchards gone strange with unseasonal bloom, through villages where people knelt in the roads at moonrise with their faces empty and shining. They had learned to ride past the kneeling ones without stopping. Stopping did no good. Whatever had been taken out of them to make room for the answering had not been put back, and Isandro had no idea how to put anything back. Yet.

*Yet.* That word had been doing a lot of work lately.

* * *

The village of Hollowmere did not appear on any map Isandro had ever drawn, which was fitting, because Isandro had spent forty years making sure nothing about themselves appeared anywhere either. You could find it only if you already knew where it was: a fold in the northern hills where the road gave up pretending to go anywhere and became a sheep track, and the sheep track became a memory, and the memory became a valley with a stream running through it and thirty ruined houses standing in the grass like teeth that had been knocked out and never replaced.

Isandro reined Duchess to a stop at the top of the rise and looked down at the place where they had been born.

"Well," they said. "It's smaller than my guilt made it."

That was the thing nobody told you about returning to the scene of your worst day. Guilt built monuments. Guilt built cathedrals. But the actual place was just—a place. A collapsed barn. A well with its roof fallen in. The blackened skeleton of the meetinghouse where it had happened, where Isandro, nine years old and furious and grieving and holding their mother's sword-speech in their mouth like a stolen sweet, had spoken a binding word they did not understand and bound the fire *into everything*, so that it burned not just wood and thatch but the shape of the village itself, the names of it, the future of it. Thirty-one houses. Eleven families. Their mother among them, though she had been dead three weeks by then, and the grave-marker was what Isandro had been trying to reach when the fire took the rest.

Duchess snorted and began picking her way downhill, having decided the matter required no further discussion.

The grass came up past the mule's knees. Wild roses had swallowed the smithy. A fox watched them pass from the ruins of the tanner's with the supreme indifference of a creature that had inherited the earth and found it adequate. Somewhere a lark was singing, because larks are constitutionally incapable of respecting tragedy.

Isandro dismounted at the edge of the churchyard.

It was still there. Of course it was there. Graveyards outlast everything; it is their entire profession. The wall had crumbled to knee-height, and the yew tree at the center had grown enormous and crooked, and beneath it, half-swallowed by ivy, stood perhaps twenty stones, most of them leaning like tired men waiting for a coach that would never come.

Their mother's stone was the last one, set apart, facing east.

Isandro walked toward it slowly. Not because of dread—though there was dread, a cold knot of it under the ribs that had ridden along from the capital—but because the ground remembered them. Every step felt like walking through water that got heavier the closer they came. By the time they reached the stone their legs ached as though they'd climbed a mountain.

The marker was slate, and the rain of forty years had softened the letters, but Isandro didn't need to read them. They knew them by heart, had known them since before they could read, because their mother had dictated her own marker one autumn evening while sharpening a kitchen knife, in the cheerful, terrible way she did everything.

*Here lies Ysolde Nystrom, who spoke once too often.*

And beneath that, in smaller letters, worn nearly smooth:

*What is bound may be loosed. Ask the stone.*

"Ask the stone," Isandro repeated aloud. Their voice came out rough. "Mother, you dramatic woman."

The stone said nothing. Stones are famously reticent. But the moonlight—the wrong moonlight, the regarding light—shifted across the face of the slate, and for a moment the worn letters stood out dark and deep as if freshly cut, and Isandro felt something in the ground beneath their feet turn over, slow as a sleeper changing position.

They knelt.

* * *

Here is what Isandro had never told anyone, not Maren, not Bramble, not the guild council in all its gray-robed wisdom: the catastrophe at Hollowmere had not been an accident of ignorance. It had been an accident of *grief*, which is worse, because grief is a kind of knowledge, and it knows exactly where to cut.

Their mother had been a swordspeaker—the last before Isandro, though she'd have laughed at the title. She called it "talking back to the world." She had bound the millwheel when its axle cracked, spoken the fever out of the cooper's daughter, argued a hailstorm into sleet over the barley fields. Small words. Practical words. Words with dirt under their fingernails. And she had taught Isandro the grammar of it the way other mothers taught bread: casually, constantly, with flour on her hands. *"A word is a debt, little ledger. Say it and you owe. The world always collects."*

Then the sweating sickness came through the valley, and she caught it, and she died in three weeks with her voice worn down to a thread from speaking the fever out of everyone else in the village first. She saved nine people. She did not save herself. She had known precisely what she was doing, and she had done it anyway, and her last coherent act had been to dictate her own grave-marker to the priest, laughing, saying, "Put it where the boy will find it, someday, when he's ready. Not before. He'll know when he's ready."

Isandro had not been ready. Isandro had been nine, and furious—at the sickness, at the priest, at God, at their mother most of all for the unforgivable crime of dying—and three weeks after the funeral they had come to the churchyard at night with a torch to burn the grief out of the world, and when the flame leapt the wall and caught the meetinghouse they had panicked, and the panic had found a word, and the word had been *stay*, and they had spoken it with the full untrained weight of a swordspeaker's gift behind it.

*Stay.*

The fire stayed. Everything stayed. The village was fixed forever in the moment of its burning, and eleven families' futures were nailed shut inside it, and Isandro's voice tore open on the word like cloth, and they had crawled away from Hollowmere at dawn, mute, nine years old, carrying the only thing they'd managed to save: their mother's ledger-book, with its first page written in her hand.

*A house must keep accounts honestly, or it keeps none at all.*

The guild had found them eventually. Had given the mute child ink instead of speech, and ledgers instead of words, and a rule instead of a mother: *never speak, never trade, never be seen.* And Isandro had obeyed for forty years, and called the obedience healing, and it had even been true, mostly, the way a splint is true—it holds the bone still until the bone forgets it was ever broken.

But bones don't forget. They just get very polite about it.

Now Isandro knelt in the wet grass before their mother's stone, and put both hands flat against the slate, and said, "I'm ready. I think I've been ready for a long time. I was just—" Their throat closed. They tried again. "I was just busy being ashamed. Ashamed is very busy work, Mother. It leaves no time for anything else."

The moonlight lay along the stone. The ivy stirred without wind.

Beneath their palms, faint as a pulse, the slate was *warm*.

* * *

The counter-word was not written on the stone. Isandro had understood that much even at nine, in the single lucid hour after the tearing, when they had crawled back to the churchyard and pressed their bleeding mouth to the slate and heard—not with their ears, their ears were ruined, but somewhere deeper—the second half of their mother's last lesson.

She had hidden it the way she hid everything important: in plain sight, in the shape of a joke. *Ysolde Nystrom, who spoke once too often.* Everyone in the village had chuckled at that. What a wit. What a woman.

But a swordspeaker reads differently. A swordspeaker hears the weight behind the words, the debt underneath. *Spoke once too often* was not an epitaph. It was a *count*. One time too many—one word beyond the tally—and therefore, by the merciless arithmetic that governed all speechcraft, one time short of some other number. Her marker was a riddle with a number in it, and the number pointed at the word she had held back, the unspoken word, the counter-oath, buried in the stone the way you bury a seed: alive, waiting, needing to be asked for properly.

*Ask the stone.*

So Isandro asked.

Not with words—they had learned long ago that the deepest doors do not open to words, which is why the guild's founders had chosen silence as their key. They asked the way their mother had taught them before they could talk: hands flat on the stone, breath slowed, attention narrowed to a single point, the way you listen for a heartbeat under a floorboard. *I am Isandro Nystrom. I am the one who broke it. I am here to finish the sentence.*

The warmth under their palms spread.

It moved up their arms like wading into a bath, and Isandro's breath caught, because it wasn't heat exactly, it was *voice*—the memory of voice, their mother's voice, stored in the slate the way sound is stored in a struck bell after the ringing stops. And it did not speak. Swordspeech never simply speaks; it *agrees*. Something in the stone, something that had been waiting forty years with the patience of the dead, recognized the shape of the asking and agreed to answer it, and the answer rose through Isandro's hands and chest and throat like water finding its level.

One word. Just one. Four letters in the old script, three sounds in the mouth, small as a coin and heavy as a coffin lid.

Isandro did not say it aloud. Not yet. Saying it here, alone, with no crown to aim it at, would spend it the way a dropped coin spends itself—rolling away into some crack, gone. Instead they let it settle into the place behind their breastbone where the torn voice lived, the scarred hollow that had ached quietly for four decades, and felt it fit there with a horrible rightness, the way a key fits a lock you'd rather stay locked.

"Oh," Isandro whispered, in their ordinary, damaged, human voice. "Oh, Mother. It's a *kind* word."

That was the part that undid them. They had expected a weapon. They had braced for a blade. But the counter-word to *stay*—the word that unbinds, that releases, that lets a thing finally end and move on—was not violence. It was permission. It was the word you say to a ghost. It was, Isandro realized, sitting back on their heels in the wet grass with tears running freely down their face, the exact word their mother had been trying to give them for forty years:

*You're allowed to let go.*

"I'm sorry," they said to the stone. "For the torch. For the word. For hating you for dying when you were saving everyone you could reach. I'm sorry I needed you to be a monster so my guilt would have somewhere to live." They wiped their face with their sleeve, and laughed, wetly, at the absurdity of apologizing to slate. "Maren says I apologize to furniture. Apparently it runs in the family."

The lark was still singing. The fox had moved on to more promising ruins. The world, indifferent and gentle, went on around the churchyard, and Isandro sat with their dead a while longer, and for the first time the sitting did not feel like drowning.

When they finally rose, their knees cracked in protest, and Duchess, tied at the churchyard wall, gave them a look of profound equine judgment, as if to say: *three days of travel and you spend it crying at rocks. Typical two-legs.*

"Yes, yes," Isandro told her. "Ledger it under expenses."

* * *

They were halfway down the valley track, leading Duchess by the bridle because the descent was treacherous and because walking felt right after kneeling, when the moonlight changed.

It did not dim. It *focused*. The whole sky seemed to lean, the way a crowd leans when a fight starts, and the light that had been regarding Isandro all week now stared, and the cold that came with it was not the cold of night but the cold of attention.

At the bottom of the track, where the sheep path met the old road, a figure stood waiting. It wore the body of a man—or the memory of one—tall, gray-cloaked, with a king's bearing and no king's face, because the face was still deciding whose it was. When it turned, the eyes were the thirteenth moon entire, round and white and patient.

Isandro stopped. Duchess, sensibly, tried to leave, and was restrained by main force.

"You went home," the figure said, and its voice was Aldric's voice, worn smooth and wrong, like a river stone shaped by something much larger than water. "To the little valley. To the stone." The moon-eyes warmed, horribly, with something like tenderness. "I know what she hid there, swordspeaker. I knew your mother. I answered her prayers too, once. She was wiser than you—she never asked twice."

It smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile did not fit.

"One turn remains, Isandro Nystrom. Come to the palace. Bring your little word. I should like to hear it spoken—I have waited so long to hear a Nystrom finish a sentence." The figure spread its hands, gracious as a host. "And I have an offer you will want to refuse. Do me the courtesy of refusing it to my face."

The moonlight released all at once, like a hand opening, and the figure was gone, and the ordinary dark rushed back in, and Duchess leaned hard against Isandro's shoulder, trembling.

Isandro found, to their surprise, that they were not trembling. They were angry. Clean, bright, familiar anger—the same anger that had burned a village at nine years old, but older now, and aimed, and carrying a word behind its teeth like a coin held for a fare.

"He wants an audience," Isandro said to the mule, climbing stiffly into the saddle. "Fine. I've spent forty years learning how to make an entrance nobody sees. Time I learned the opposite."

They turned Duchess south, toward the capital, toward the crown, toward the twelfth turn rising fat and white over the hills like an eye about to blink.

Behind them, in the churchyard under the yew tree, the slate marker cooled slowly back to the temperature of ordinary stone, and if anyone had been near enough to hear—which no one was—they might have caught, in the settling of the ivy and the hush of the grass, something almost like a woman's laugh, brief and proud, the laugh of a mother watching her child walk toward trouble fully armed.
