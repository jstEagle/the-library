## 17. What Wears the Crown

I have worn many mouths.

Kings, mostly. It is what I am for. A crown is only a bowl, and a bowl exists to be filled, and for eleven hundred years I have been poured from one royal skull to the next like wine at a wedding that never ends. I have been emperors in the east whose names are now dust and whose deeds are now my deeds. I have been a child-queen who wept beautifully and signed beautifully and never once knew what her hand was doing. I have been loved by crowds who were, without knowing it, loving me.

Aldric is not the finest vessel I have had. He is too kind, which is a structural flaw in any king, and he prays too much, which is how I got in. But he is warm. There is a hearth-quality to him that the others lacked, some stubborn ember his dead son lit in him years ago and that even my weight has not fully smothered. When I sit behind his eyes and look out through them at his queen—at Sera, who watches him now with the particular vigilance of a woman counting the seconds between her husband's blinks—I can feel that ember resisting me.

It is inconvenient. It is also, I confess, delicious.

But I did not bring you here to discuss Aldric. I brought you here to discuss Isandro Nystrom, who is climbing my stairs at this very moment, because I asked them to, and because Isandro Nystrom has never once in their careful, ledger-balancing life refused a summons delivered correctly.

Watch them climb. This is my favorite part.

* * *

They come up the chapel stair slowly, one hand on the wall, the way people do when they are trying to convince their bodies this is an ordinary errand. The guards let them pass because I told the guards to let them pass, hours ago, while Aldric slept and I wore his sleeping mouth and practiced saying *Isandro* until it sounded like something a king would say rather than something a god would savor.

The chapel is dark except for the candles I have allowed—seven of them, arranged in the old pattern, the pattern from before the Ledgerhouse taught this city to worship arithmetic instead. Isandro notices the pattern immediately. Of course they do. They notice everything; it is their whole tragedy.

"Your Majesty." They bow exactly the correct depth. Not one inch more. Even now, even walking into the mouth of the thing, they keep their books balanced.

"Master Delacroix's old student," I say, using Aldric's voice, warming it. "Sit. Please."

There is no chair. There is a pew, and Isandro sits in it the way a cat sits in water: technically compliant, spiritually elsewhere.

"You wanted to see me."

"I wanted," I say, "to thank you."

And there it is—the flicker. Fear, yes, but under the fear something hungrier and more tender, the look of a person who has gone so long unthanked that gratitude feels like a trap. Which it is. But it is also true, and I deal only in truths; lies are for gods with less patience.

"The rain over the widow Corren's orchard," I say. "You recorded it. You could have burned the page. You could have warned the guild louder, run faster, hidden deeper. Instead you wrote it down in your small neat hand, and in writing it down, you *saw* it. Do you know how rare seeing is, Isandro? Your whole city walks around inside a fog of not-seeing so thick I could weep, if I had eyes that weren't borrowed. And here you come up my stairs with your ink-stained fingers and your terrible honest face, and you see me."

"I see a king who is ill," Isandro says carefully.

"You see a king who is *occupied*." I let Aldric smile. It is a good smile; I have polished it across three decades of state dinners. "Let us not insult each other. It wastes the candlelight."

* * *

Here is what I know about Isandro Nystrom, and I want you to appreciate the inventory, because I have had eleven hundred years to learn how to read a soul and theirs is the most interesting ledger I have audited since the last swordspeaker died:

Item: one oath of silence, self-imposed, load-bearing. Take it away and the whole structure of who they are collapses—and they know it, and they guard it like a wound they secretly love.

Item: one name, erased. Not lost—erased. Scrubbed from every record by the guild's founding circle when they took the child in, so thoroughly that Isandro themselves cannot remember it. They have spent fifty years being *the last swordspeaker*, a title, a function, a filing category. Somewhere underneath is a name that belonged to a girl or boy in a burned village, and Isandro cannot even tell you which.

Item: one found family, currently distributed across a guildhouse like coins in different pockets. A clerk who worships them. An appraiser who trusts them. A thief-taker who betrayed them and will need forgiving. A mute boy who draws moons.

Item: one guilt, vintage childhood, still decanting.

You see my problem? You see my *opportunity*? Most souls I wear, I simply wait out. Kings die on schedule. But Isandro I cannot wait out, because Isandro intends to speak the unbinding word, and the unbinding word does not merely evict me—it unmakes the door, and I have grown fond of doors.

So I will not fight them. Fighting Isandro is like fighting a river; you drown and the river writes it all down afterward. No. I will make them an offer so precisely shaped to their wounds that refusing it will hurt worse than any word I could bind.

* * *

"Do you know what happened to the swordspeakers?" I ask, and I make Aldric's voice go soft, the voice of a man in a confessional. "Not the story the guild tells. The true one."

"They spoke words that cost them," Isandro says. "Voice or life."

"Half true. The guild's half. Here is mine." I rise, wearing Aldric's body the way a hand wears a glove, and cross to the window where the thirteenth moon hangs fat and wrong above the capital. "Every swordspeaker's power came from me. Every binding word, every unbinding word, every syllable that bent the world—they were drawing from a well, and I am the well. The guild did not imprison me, Isandro. The guild *tapped* me. Eleven centuries of miracles, routed through mortal mouths, and the price collected at the far end. Voice or life. That was never my cruelty. That was *bookkeeping*."

Isandro has gone very still. Stillness, in them, is not calm. Stillness is a clerk checking a column twice because the first sum was unbearable.

"That isn't possible," they say. "The founding ledgers—"

"Were written by the founders. By the tap-makers. Would you trust an appraisal written by the appraiser about the appraiser's own honesty?" I turn, and I let Aldric's eyes do the work they were made for—those honest grey eyes, the ones Sera fell in love with, the ones the whole kingdom trusts. "You of all people know that a ledger is only as true as its keeper wants it to be."

"And you," Isandro says, "are a keeper who wants very much to be believed."

"*Good*," I say, and I mean it, and I let the delight show through the king's face for one unguarded instant—a flash of something vast and amused moving behind Aldric's features like a whale under lake ice. "Oh, that's why I like you. Everyone else in this city either kneels or runs. You *audit* me."

* * *

So then. The offer. I have rehearsed it against every version of Isandro I can imagine, and I deliver it now plainly, because plainness disarms them; they distrust ornament.

"Stand aside," I say. "One turn of the moon remains. When it completes, I finish wearing Aldric—not cruelly; he will feel nothing after the first moment—and this city becomes mine, gently, the way a house becomes yours when everyone in it falls asleep. And in exchange—"

I walk back to them. I kneel. A god kneeling in a stolen king's body before a clerk in a pew; if the candles could record, this page would be worth more than the whole guild archive.

"—in exchange, I give you back your name."

The silence goes on long enough that somewhere below us a clock counts a quarter hour.

"It was a good name," I continue, quietly, intimately, the way you speak to someone in the dark beside you. "Your mother chose it. She sang it over your cradle. I heard her—did you know that? I was small then, in those days. Local. I lived in the well-water and the bell-bronze of your village, and I heard her sing it, and I heard the oath-word she hid in your grave-marker's stone, and I heard the guild's men come, and I heard—" I pause, delicately. "Well. You remember what you heard."

"Don't," Isandro says. One syllable, and their voice cracks down the middle of it.

"She called you by it when you were brave," I press on, gently, mercilessly, because intimacy is a blade and I have never once dulled mine out of kindness. "She called you by it the morning of the fire. And the guild took it, and gave you a title instead, and you have answered to *last swordspeaker* for fifty years the way a dog answers to a whistle. I can give it back. Not the memory of it—the *thing itself*. Names live in me, Isandro. Everything spoken truly lives in me. I will pour it back into you whole, and you will wake tomorrow knowing who your mother meant when she sang."

I let that settle. Then, because I am thorough:

"And your voice. Your swordspeech. Burned out of you at eight years old when you spoke the word that—you think you burned the village. You didn't. The guild's binding-circle misfired; their greed cracked the word mid-syllable. You have spent your whole life doing penance for their arithmetic error. I can restore what they broke. Every word you were owed. You would be the first whole swordspeaker in nine hundred years, and you would owe me nothing but a staircase left empty on one particular night."

* * *

Now watch them. This is the part I will remember longest, when the moon has finished turning and the city sleeps in me.

Isandro does not cry. I expected crying; most mortals cry when offered their childhood back. Instead they laugh—one short, broken, astonished note, the laugh of a person finding a forged signature on a document they'd treasured.

"You've been reading my file," they say.

"I *am* your file."

"No." Isandro stands. Their hands are shaking; I note it honestly, the way they would note it. "No, you're a forgery of my file. A very good one. You got the wounds right. Wounds are easy—they're all anyone ever sees." They step closer, close enough that I can smell the ink and lamp-oil and cheap soap of them, and their voice drops low and steady. "But here's what your audit missed. My mother didn't sing my name to make me brave. She sang it to make me *hers*. And I already know who she meant. I've known for fifty years. It's the person who balances the books, and scolds Wren for skipping meals, and keeps Maren's good eye supplied with spectacles, and hasn't given up on Bramble even now. That person doesn't need a name returned. That person needs the king in your chair to wake up tomorrow and grieve his son properly, and I am going to make sure he does."

Something happens in my chest that I do not have a word for, and I have worn every language on this coast.

"Even at the cost of the voice you'll lose speaking it," I say. "You'll spend the rest of your life half-mute. You'll never again say Wren's name, or Maren's, or Oskar's. You've thought of that? The boy who cannot speak, and the one person he loves best struck dumb beside him?"

Ah. There. That lands. I see it land—the image of Oskar's hands and Isandro's empty mouth, meeting in the middle with nothing between them. For a moment the offer stands open on the table between us, gleaming, and Isandro looks at it the way a starving person looks at bread they suspect is poisoned.

Then Isandro smiles. It is not a big smile. It is the smile of the Ledgerhouse at closing time, tired and true.

"You counted my wounds," they say, "but you never once counted my *assets*. That's why your kind always loses to clerks, Vhalor. We know the difference between what a thing costs and what it's worth."

They bow—exactly the correct depth—and walk to the stair.

At the top of it they stop, without turning around.

"One more entry for your ledger," they say. "You offered me my name back. But names aren't kept in wells, whatever you told yourself down there in the dark for eleven hundred years. My mother put mine somewhere you'll never reach. She put it in the people who say it. Ask Bramble. Ask Maren. Ask the boy who draws you moons. *That's* the vault, and it's already full, and you were never in it."

And they go down the stairs, one hand on the wall, slower than they came up, because the offer was real and they felt its weight, and honesty requires me to record that they trembled.

* * *

Alone in the chapel—or as alone as a god ever is, wearing a man who is screaming silently behind his own eyes—I consider.

I could take the body now. Finish the turn early. The moon is nearly full; the congregation below the city grows nightly; the widow Corren alone could feed me another week. I could be done before Isandro reaches the bottom of the stairs.

But.

Eleven hundred years teaches you things, and the chief among them is this: never close a book while someone is still reading it. Isandro will go to the ruined village. Isandro will dig the counter-word out of the grave-marker, because they must—they are constitutionally incapable of leaving a column unfinished. And the counter-word, dug fresh and spoken desperate, will taste *so much sweeter* to swallow than any word spoken early and at leisure.

Besides. There are others in the house on Candle Lane. A clerk with a hero's heart. An appraiser sitting on the guild's oldest sin like a dragon on the wrong egg. A thief-taker drowning in guilt, guilt being the most flammable substance known to gods. And a council of accountants who believe, with all their shriveled hearts, that neutrality is a shelter.

Neutrality is not a shelter. Neutrality is a doorway standing open, and I have never yet met a doorway I couldn't wear.

I let Aldric sleep. I let him dream of his son, poor ember, poor bowl. And I turn my attention, patient as interest compounding, toward Candle Lane—

—where, at this very hour, an old appraiser is feeding pages into a fire, and an old thief-taker is reaching for a pen.
