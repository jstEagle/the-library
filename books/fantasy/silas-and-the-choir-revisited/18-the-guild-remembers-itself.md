## 18. The Guild Remembers Itself

Maren had appraised ten thousand impossible things in her forty years at the Ledgerhouse, and she had learned one rule above all the others: the moment an object stopped being frightening was the moment it started being dangerous.

The neutral ledgers were not frightening anymore. They sat in the council chamber on their iron stand, fat and calm as sleeping hogs, and every member of the ruling circle looked at them the way a farmer looks at a full granary before winter. Safe. Full. Untouched by the madness outside.

Outside, of course, being where the thirteenth moon hung like a coin someone had flipped and forgotten to catch.

"Read it again," said Corvane, First of the Circle, in the voice he used for pronouncements. He was a narrow man made narrower by thirty years of sitting in judgment over entries rather than people. "The resolution. Slowly."

Clerk-Herald Tamsin cleared her throat. "Be it resolved: the Ledgerhouse shall maintain strict neutrality in all matters pertaining to the current lunar irregularity. No entry shall be altered. No record shall be surrendered. No clerk shall act upon any knowledge contained herein. The ledgers are the guild. The guild is the ledgers."

"There," Corvane said, satisfied. "Clean as a balanced column."

Maren stood at the back of the chamber, her one eye fixed on the resolution scroll, and thought: *You beautiful idiots. You've just voted to watch a god eat a city, and you've done it in perfect calligraphy.*

She had spent three nights with Isandro's copied pages spread across her appraisal table, and she had done what she did best. She had read the signature.

Not the name — names meant nothing to gods, they wore names like coats. But the *hand*. The pressure of the pen. The way the ink pooled slightly at the end of each answered prayer, like something leaning close to drink. She had seen that hand once before, sixty years ago, in the founding ledgers, in the pages the guild pretended were mythological. Vhalor. The Devourer of Choirs. The reason the Ledgerhouse existed at all.

The guild had not been founded to trade in impossible things. It had been founded to *imprison* one — to keep it bound beneath an altar stone in the catacombs, starved of worship, fed nothing but silence. And for six hundred years the guild had kept that silence so well that it had forgotten what the silence was *for*.

Now the prayers were landing answers. Now the hollowed were shuffling through the streets with smiles carved too wide. Now the moon was turning, turn by turn, toward completion.

And the council's response was a resolution about neutrality.

"Maren." Corvane's voice cut across her thoughts. "You have something to add? You've been looming. Looming is Wren's job."

A few of the younger circle members smiled. Maren did not. She walked to the front of the chamber, her boots loud on the old stone, and laid Isandro's copied pages on the table beside the neutral ledgers.

"I've appraised these," she said. "As senior appraiser, under the third charter, I'm entitled to enter my findings into the record."

"Findings," Corvane repeated warily.

"The signature on the miracle-entries is Vhalor's." She let the name sit there. Let it do its work. Half the circle flinched; old Pellam actually dropped his quill. "The same hand that appears in the founding ledgers. The same hand the first twelve swordspeakers died binding. It is not 'a lunar irregularity,' Corvane. It is the thing we were built to bury, waking up, and eating our clients one prayer at a time."

Silence. The good kind, briefly — the kind where minds were working.

Then Corvane folded his hands. "And your recommendation?"

"That we open the sealed archive. That we give Isandro everything we have on the binding rites. That we send word to the palace—"

"The palace," said Pellam, recovering his quill and his composure together, "is currently occupied. Have you seen the king lately? Have you seen what kneels in the square every dusk?" He shuddered theatrically. "We are *record-keepers*, Maren. We are not soldiers. We are not priests. If we insert ourselves into a war between a god and a crown, we will be crushed between them, and six hundred years of records burn with us."

"And if we don't," Maren said, "there won't be anyone left to read them."

"The ledgers will survive," Corvane said. "They always have. Stone, iron, warded vaults. Gods come and go, Maren. Kings come and go. Cities—" he shrugged, a small, terrible shrug, "—cities rebuild. The record endures. That is the whole of our purpose. That has *always* been the whole of our purpose."

Maren looked around the chamber. Twelve faces. Some afraid, some genuinely convinced, two or three — she noted them, appraiser's habit — already calculating which way the wind would blow when this was over. Not one face showed what she was looking for.

Not one face showed shame.

"You know what I appraised last week?" she said quietly. "A widow's shadow. Sold for rain. Do you know who brokered that trade? Nobody. Nobody brokered it. The god took payment directly, off our books, out from under our seals. We weren't bypassed because we failed to protect our monopoly, Corvane. We were bypassed because we're *irrelevant*. Because while the city burns, we voted to keep the books tidy." She tapped the copied pages. "Neutrality isn't keeping us safe. Neutrality is the shape of the hole we've dug, and we're standing in it, congratulating ourselves on the masonry."

"The resolution stands," Corvane said, with finality. "The circle thanks you for your findings. They will be entered into the record."

"They will be entered into the record," Maren agreed. "Everything will be entered into the record. That's the problem."

She turned and walked out, and behind her she heard Corvane say, "Someone fetch the lamp-oil inventory, we're behind on the quarterly count," and she thought, with a strange lightness: *Yes. Count it. Count every drop.*

Because Maren had not walked into that chamber empty-handed. Under her coat, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, she carried the appraisal lamp — the one used to verify forgeries, the one that burned with flame hot enough to scorch ward-marks off vellum without touching the words beneath. And she had spent the previous evening doing something no appraiser in the guild's history had ever done.

She had appraised the neutral ledgers themselves.

Value: irreplaceable. Condition: pristine. Recommendation:

*Burn.*

* * *

Bramble had been a thief-toll collector for twenty-two years, which meant she had spent twenty-two years watching people decide, at the exact moment of decision, who they really were. People thought toll-collecting was about money. It wasn't. It was about the half-second when a merchant with contraband in his second cart looked you in the eye and chose: bribe, lie, or bolt. Bramble had learned to read that half-second the way Maren read signatures.

So when Guildmaster Corvane summoned her to his office at the hour past midnight and asked, in a warm voice, whether she'd noticed anything unusual about Isandro Nystrom's recent movements — she knew, within that half-second, exactly what was happening.

"He's been restless," Bramble said. "Retired men get restless."

"Restless." Corvane leaned back. His office was small and immaculate, which Bramble had always found suspicious; honest lives accumulated clutter. "He hasn't been in the sealed archive? Hasn't borrowed keys? Hasn't spoken with Maren about... findings?"

"Corvane." Bramble settled into the chair across from him without being invited, because at seventy-one she'd earned the right to sit wherever she liked. "I collected tolls on the Sorrow Bridge for two decades. You know what I learned about informants? They always think they're the clever one in the room. They always think the person asking the questions doesn't already know the answers." She smiled, showing the gap where a toll-dodger's elbow had knocked out a tooth in '61. "You already know he's been copying pages. You wouldn't be asking me otherwise. So the real question is: why me?"

Corvane's warmth cooled by a degree. "You were his mentor."

"I was his *neighbor*," Bramble corrected. "There's a difference. Mentors teach. Neighbors borrow salt and return it with interest and notice when the lights burn late." She studied him. "You want me to report him. Formally. On the record. So that when you seize his copies and expel him, it looks like concern instead of fear."

"It looks like *procedure*."

"It looks like procedure," Bramble agreed pleasantly. "That's the beauty of procedure. It can make anything look clean. I once watched a man get hanged on four perfectly procedural documents, and every one of them was accurate, and every one of them was a lie." She leaned forward. "Here's my counter-offer."

"I'm not negotiating with—"

"Everyone negotiates with everyone, dear, that's what conversation *is*." She reached into her coat — slowly, letting him see — and produced a slim ledger. Not one of the great iron-bound volumes. A personal one. Her own.

Corvane went very still. "What is that."

"Toll-book. Mine. Forty years of entries." She set it on his desk and opened it to a ribbon-marked page. "You'll notice I recorded everything. Every toll, every waiver, every exception granted. Including exceptions granted by members of the ruling circle." She turned the page. "Including, on the fourth of Harvestmonth, a waiver signed by you, exempting the Saltwright consortium from the smuggling tariff. Including, eleven years ago, a payment routed through the Ashgate accounts to a name I eventually traced to your late brother's debts. Including—" she turned another page, enjoying herself now, an old woman with a lifetime of careful notes and nowhere left to spend them but here, "—a great many other things that would look very bad entered into the record. Which, as we all agree, is sacred."

Corvane's face had gone the color of old candle wax. "You kept a ledger *on us*."

"I kept a ledger on *everyone*, dear. It's called being thorough. It's also called knowing that someday the institution I loved would ask me to choose between it and a person, and I wanted to be ready." She closed the book and rested her hand flat on it. "Here's the offer. You leave Isandro alone. You reopen the question of neutrality. Or I walk this book down to the Charter Hall and read it aloud until someone stops me, and several someones will help me, because I'm not the only retired collector with a memory and a grudge."

"This is blackmail."

"Oh, absolutely." Bramble beamed. "Best kind. Documented."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. Then, softly, with genuine bewilderment: "Why? He's one man. One oath-breaker. Why burn your standing for him?"

And Bramble, who had been waiting for that question, gave it the answer she'd been carrying since the night Oskar had shown them his drawings — thirteen moons, a kneeling city, small stick figures with their hearts drawn as open doors.

"Because thirty years ago I sat on a bridge and took a toll from a woman carrying her dead child to a healer, and I charged her, Corvane, because the rules said charge her, and she paid, because she had nothing left to fight with. And I told myself the rules were the rules, and the rules kept the bridge standing, and the bridge mattered more than one grieving woman." Her voice didn't shake. She'd had practice. "The rules kept the bridge standing. And then the flood came anyway, and the bridge washed out, and you know what remained? The woman. The grief. The choice I'd made. Institutions don't remember, dear. People remember. People are the only thing that remembers." She stood, joints cracking like green firewood. "Isandro is going to save that king or die trying, and when it's done, someone will need to stand up in front of whatever's left of this guild and say: *we were wrong, and here is the proof, and here is how we do better.* That someone is going to be me. I've been practicing looking wrong in the face for forty years. I'm very good at it."

She picked up her toll-book and walked to the door.

"Bramble." Corvane's voice was hoarse. "If you do this, there's no coming back. The circle will—"

"The circle," Bramble said, "is about to learn what everyone learns eventually. That the people inside it are the only part worth saving." She paused at the threshold. "Oh — and Corvane? The Saltwright waiver. I never told Isandro about it. Consider that my last professional courtesy."

* * *

It was Wren who found Maren in the archive vestibule at dawn, smelling of smoke, holding a scorched iron key, with ash on her sleeves and an expression of profound peace.

"Maren." Wren's voice cracked. "The neutral ledgers. The whole case. There's smoke coming out of the council chamber and Corvane is screaming about treason and — Maren, what did you *do*?"

"What I was trained to do." Maren held up the key. "Appraise honestly."

"You burned six hundred years of records!"

"I burned six hundred years of *hiding*." She took the girl's shoulders — Wren, dear frantic Wren, ink-stained and terrified and braver than the entire ruling circle put together. "Listen. The ledgers recorded trades. Prayers sold, miracles bought, silence kept. Every page was a receipt for something stolen from somebody. Vhalor's whole strength is in the accounting — in the sense that all of this is *owed*, that the debt is legitimate, that the city signed for its own devouring fair and square. So I canceled the debt." She smiled, and there was ash in the creases of her smile. "Six hundred years of entries, voided. Let the god try to collect on a receipt that no longer exists."

Wren stared at her. "The wards. The vault wards. They'll have marked you. The circle will—"

"The circle is busy," said a dry voice from the doorway, "reading my toll-book."

Bramble stood there, hatless, coat-buttoned, holding her ledger like a battle standard. Behind her, in the corridor, a small crowd was gathering — clerks, mostly junior, drawn by the smell of smoke and the sound of Corvane's outrage. And among them, pushing forward, came Oskar, who grabbed Wren's sleeve and tugged urgently, and held up his slate.

On it, in his quick sure strokes: a drawing of the moon, nearly full. Beneath it, a drawing of the palace chapel. Beneath *that*, a single figure standing before an altar, arms raised, mouth open — speaking.

Oskar tapped the figure twice. Then he pointed, solemnly, at each of them in turn. Maren. Bramble. Wren. Himself.

Then he pointed down the corridor, toward the doors, toward the city, toward the palace hill where the last turn of the moon was already beginning.

"Tonight," Wren translated, her voice steadying as understanding arrived. "He says it happens tonight. And he says—" she swallowed, "—he says we're all going. All of us. Together."

Maren looked at Bramble. Bramble looked at Maren, and the old thief-toll collector grinned her gap-toothed grin and said, "Well. I did tell Corvane someone would have to stand up and say we were wrong." She tucked the toll-book under her arm. "Seems tonight's the night for saying things out loud."

Somewhere above them, faint through the vaulted stone, the bells began to ring.

Maren counted.

Twelve.

She picked up her lamp, still warm, still burning, and led the way out.
