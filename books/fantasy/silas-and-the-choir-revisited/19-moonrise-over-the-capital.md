## 19. Moonrise Over the Capital

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as arrive.

Sera stood on the palace battlements and watched it come up over the eastern wall of the capital like a coin being pushed through a slot in the sky—too large, too pale, the color of old bone. It cast shadows that pointed the wrong way. Below her, in the great square before the palace gates, the people were kneeling.

Not all of them. That was the horror of it. Some still walked, still shouted, still carried torches and children and the ordinary business of a city that refused to end. But hundreds upon hundreds knelt in the streets, faces turned up to that bone-colored light, mouths open in silent prayer or perhaps not-silent prayer, and when Sera looked closely she could see that their eyes had gone the same color as the moon.

"Your Majesty." Captain Ives, armor rattling, face gray. "The gate garrison is forty-two souls. The square holds four thousand."

"Then we will have to be very convincing," Sera said.

She had dressed for this the way she dressed for everything now: plainly, practically, in a queen's colors but no crown. Aldric's crown was otherwise occupied tonight. She wore his signet ring instead, on her right hand, where she could feel its weight when she moved. It helped. Not much. But some.

"Majesty," Ives said again, and hesitated.

"Say it."

"The men are asking what we're holding the gates against. They've heard the stories. Hollowed congregations, miracles that eat you from inside. Some of them want to kneel themselves. It's easier, they say. You don't feel afraid anymore, once you—" He stopped.

"Once you stop feeling anything at all," Sera finished. "Yes. I know what it buys."

She thought of Aldric, three nights ago, surfacing inside his own body for eleven seconds—she had counted them, eleven seconds, she would count them for the rest of her life—and saying *don't let it finish the turn* in a voice like a man shouting up from the bottom of a well. Eleven seconds was not enough time to say anything else worth saying. He had spent them on that.

"We're not holding the gates against the people," she said to Ives, and to the garrison gathering behind him, and to herself. "We're holding them *for* the people. Every soul out there who hasn't knelt yet gets to keep choosing. That's what a gate is. That's what a kingdom is. A door that stays shut until you decide for yourself."

Ives looked at her for a long moment. Then he straightened, and something came back into his face that had been draining out of it all week.

"And if the god comes to the door himself?"

"Then he can knock," Sera said, "and wait, like everyone else."

Below, in the square, the first wave began to move toward the gates—not running, never running, walking with the terrible patience of tide—and Sera drew her sword, which she had not done in six years, and found her hand remembered how.

* * *

Wren had never run so fast in her life, and she had spent two years as a junior clerk in a house that traded in impossible things, which meant she had run from appraisers, auditors, one memorable tax collector made entirely of paperwork, and Bramble before he retired from thief-catching and became, inexplicably, family.

"Bramble!" she gasped, skidding around the fountain on the Processional Way. "Bramble, they're already—"

"I see them." The old man was leaning on his toll-staff at the mouth of the chapel colonnade, and he did not look like a retired anything. He looked like every toll booth Wren had ever feared. "Count of thirty before they reach us. Maybe twenty-five. Kneelers walk slow but there's a lot of kneeling between here and the doors."

Behind him, the chapel doors—oak, iron-banded, older than the guild—stood closed. Behind those doors, somewhere above, Isandro was climbing toward the crown room with the counter-oath written on paper because their voice could no longer be trusted to hold the words steady without it. Wren had watched them copy it out that afternoon, letter by letter, hand shaking, and had said nothing, because some things you witnessed and kept, the way the Ledgerhouse had always taught her. Record everything. Hide nothing. Those two rules had spent centuries hating each other, and tonight they finally shook hands.

"How many do we need to hold off?" Wren asked, pulling the ledger satchel tight across her chest. Inside it were the copied pages—the true ledger, the one that named Vhalor, the one Maren had burned the neutral books to protect. If Isandro fell, someone had to carry the truth out the other side.

Bramble spat, considered, and said, "All of them, ideally."

"That's not a plan, that's a wish."

"Wishes are underrated. I've arrested men whose entire strategy was wishing." He rolled his shoulders with a sound like a ship changing its mind. "But no. We don't hold them off. We hold the *doors*. There's a difference. These poor devils aren't attacking us; they're being *poured* at us. You don't fight a flood, girl. You build a wall and you let it break itself against you."

The first of them came around the colonnade then, and Wren's stomach dropped. She recognized him. The baker on Candle Lane, the one who gave Oskar burnt ends on festival days, who laughed too loud and sang worse. He was kneeling-walking now, moon-eyed, hands raised, and his lips were moving in that silent litany, and there was nothing left behind his face but the shape of where a man used to be.

"Oh," Wren said quietly. "Oh, it's *Herron*."

"Yeah." Bramble's voice went gentle, which from Bramble was more alarming than shouting. "That's the job tonight. That's the whole ugly job."

They planted themselves before the doors. Bramble set his staff across the threshold like a toll bar, and Wren—because she was a clerk of the Ledgerhouse, because Maren had taught her that everything has a price and most prices can be named—took out the copied pages and held them high over her head.

"Herron!" she called. "Herron of Candle Lane! Your debt is called!"

The hollowed man paused. Something flickered. Far down, deep under the moonlight in his eyes, like a fish turning in dark water.

"You prayed for your daughter's fever to break," Wren said, louder, voice cracking. "It broke. And it cost you—you know it cost you—but nobody ever showed you the *bill*. So here it is! Here's the whole account! Everything Vhalor took, everything he's going to take, written down plain, because the Ledgerhouse records truths now and hides *nothing*!"

She didn't know if it would work. She didn't know if words could reach whatever was left in there. But Herron stopped. His hands lowered an inch. His mouth worked around the silent prayer like a man trying to cough up a stone.

And behind him, the tide of the hollowed slowed, confused, a hundred moonlit faces tilting.

"They can hear the ledger," Bramble breathed. "Girl, they can *hear* it."

"Of course they can." Wren's eyes were stinging. "They're still people. People can always hear the truth. It's just rarely read loudly enough."

* * *

Maren had one eye and, as she liked to say, that was one more than the guild council had between the lot of them by the end.

She stood now in the shadow of the palace's service gate with Oskar beside her, and the boy was drawing in the dust with a stick, fast, urgent, and Maren read his pictures the way she read hallmarks on silver: quickly, expertly, with growing dread.

A gate. A queen on the walls. Arrows of motion converging. Then—a figure descending the palace steps that was not a figure, that was a *shape wearing a king*, drawn with the wrong number of joints, crowned in scribbled lines that hurt to look at. Then Oskar himself, small, marked on the chest with a black circle where Vhalor's attention had touched him in the catacombs, standing directly in the shape's path.

"No," Maren said flatly.

Oskar looked up at her. He couldn't speak. He had never been able to speak. But his face said, with the eloquence of the truly stubborn, *yes.*

"Oskar. Lad. Listen to me." She crouched, wincing, one eye on the dust-drawing and one on the street, where the kneeling crowds parted around them like water around a stone—they wouldn't touch the marked boy, she'd noticed; they *revered* him, which was worse. "You saw what happens if that thing reaches the ground and finishes its turn. You told us with pictures. You bought us our chance. Nobody's asking you to stand in front of it."

Oskar picked up his stick and wrote, slowly, in the dust:

**IT KNOWS ME ALREADY.**

Maren's throat tightened. Because that was true, and truth was the whole currency of the night. The god had touched him, marked him, and a mark ran both ways—that was basic appraisal. Which meant the boy wasn't just bait. He was a *flaw* in the god's attention, a splinter, and Isandro would need every splinter they could get.

"Fine," she said roughly. "Fine. But you stay behind me, and if I tell you to run, you run, and if you argue I'll appraise every toy you own and declare it all worthless."

Oskar almost smiled. Then he took her hand—his small fingers closing around her scarred ones—and together they went through the service gate, into the palace, toward the sound of the thirteenth moon pressing down on the world like a thumb on a wound.

* * *

At the chapel doors, the tide broke.

It broke the way Bramble had promised—not with malice but with mass, dozens of hollowed pressing forward, hands reaching, and Bramble's staff sweeping, and Wren shouting entries from the ledger until her voice went raw. *"Herron of Candle Lane, paid one daughter's memory! Tamsin Weaver, paid the color blue! Old Cobb, paid his mother's face—"*

Each name landed like a stone in still water. Each hollowed worshipper stumbled, faltered, some of them weeping without knowing why, the truth cutting through the moonlight like a lamp through fog. None of them fully woke. But they *slowed*. They hesitated. And hesitation, Bramble had taught her long ago over stolen apples and a lecture on the philosophy of tolls, was worth more than any wall.

Then, near midnight, the pressure changed.

The kneeling crowds all across the square went still at once, like puppets whose strings had been gathered in one fist. And from the palace's central spire, a light began—not the moon's bone-pale glow but something deeper, redder, patient, ancient—and a voice came down through the stones of the city, through the soles of Wren's boots, through the fillings of her teeth:

***LET THE DOORS OPEN.***

Bramble braced his staff. "No," he said, conversationally, to a god.

***OLD THIEF-TAKER. YOU TOOK COIN AT EVERY BRIDGE IN THIS CITY FOR FORTY YEARS. WHAT IS YOUR PRICE? NAME IT.***

Bramble grinned, gap-toothed and unafraid. "My price?" He tapped the chapel door behind him. "This stays shut. That's the price. Always was. You're the first customer in forty years who couldn't pay it."

The red light flared. The doors shuddered in their frames—

—and held, because Wren had done the last thing she knew to do. She had opened the ledger satchel and pressed the true pages flat against the oak, and the truth in them burned cold and clean, and Vhalor's power slid off it like rain off oiled cloth. The god had spent an age buying silence. He had no purchase on a house that had learned to speak.

Above them, faint through the stone, they heard footsteps on the tower stair. One set. Deliberate. Climbing toward the crown room with a paper full of unbinding words and a voice that had been waiting fifty years to spend itself.

Wren pressed her palm flat against the door, against the pages, and whispered the only prayer she'd ever trusted:

"Go on, Isandro. We've got the doors. Say the word."

* * *

On the battlements, Queen Sera watched the red light climb the spire and felt the whole city hold its breath between one turn of the moon and the next.

Beside her, Captain Ives asked, very quietly, "What happens now, Majesty?"

Sera did not take her eyes off the tower.

"Now," she said, "we find out what my husband's last eleven seconds were worth."
