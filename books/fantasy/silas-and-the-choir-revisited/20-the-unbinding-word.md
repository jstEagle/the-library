## 20. The Unbinding Word

The chapel doors were supposed to be locked.

They were not locked because Oskar had gotten to them first, and the boy had a way with locks that suggested a future career the Ledgerhouse would never officially acknowledge. He pressed something small into my hand as I passed—half a honey biscuit, slightly crushed, still warm from his pocket.

"For after," Wren whispered, translating his quick fingers without being asked.

"For after," I said, and did not say *if*, because we had all agreed on the policy of not saying *if* tonight. It was one of perhaps four policies we had agreed on, and it was the only one that hadn't immediately collapsed.

Beyond the doors, the chapel of Saint Vessia was full of moonlight. The thirteenth moon hung in the eastern window like a coin someone had nailed to the sky, so close now to completing its turn that its edge seemed to tremble against the frame of heaven. The stained glass threw colors across the floor that no glass should have been able to make—colors I had entries for in the old ledgers, filed under *impossible*.

And before the altar, kneeling in the light with his hands folded and his head bowed at an angle no living man would choose, was King Aldric.

Or rather, was the thing wearing King Aldric, which wore the crown, which wore the king like a glove worn so long it had taken the shape of the hand inside it.

I stopped three paces away. Close enough to speak. Far enough, I hoped, to matter.

"You came," said Aldric's mouth, without Aldric's voice. The voice underneath was older than the city, older than the bells, patient as erosion. "I offered you everything, little ledger-keeper. Your voice restored. Your name un-erased. All you had to do was stand aside and let me finish."

"I know," I said. "It was a very generous offer. Maren says you always lead with generosity. She says it's how you can tell."

Something moved behind the king's eyes—not anger exactly. Interest. A god turning a page.

"Your guild voted for neutrality," Vhalor said through the king's lips. "Your friends are holding doors downstairs against congregations who love me. The queen holds the gates with forty soldiers and a promise she cannot keep. You stand alone in my house, with nothing but a biscuit in your pocket and a word you have never dared say aloud." The mouth curved. "Why?"

"Because I've been keeping books for forty years," I said, "and I finally learned to read them."

* * *

Here is what swordspeaking is, since no one living has explained it properly and I am about to stop being qualified:

Words are load-bearing. Every name, every vow, every prayer is a small architecture holding up some piece of the world, and most people go their whole lives never noticing they're walking through cathedrals made of sentences. A swordspeaker sees the beams. A swordspeaker can *pull them out*—speak a word that unhinges what was hinged, unbinds what was bound—and reality comes down around the speaking like a roof in a storm.

That is why the oath existed. That is why every swordspeaker before me went silent or went into the ground. Not because the guild feared the power.

Because the power *costs*. It takes the speaking out of you as it goes. Voice, or life—the archive was very clear, and the archive was never clear about anything else.

I had spent fifty years being grateful for my silence. It had been armor. It had been punishment. It had been both, and I had never once untangled which.

Until a mute kitchen boy drew me pictures of the end of the world, and a widow gave up her shadow for rain, and a king prayed his son back from the dead and got a god instead—and I understood that silence, too, was a kind of speech. That every year I said nothing, I was saying something. *This is acceptable. This is the price of order. Let it finish.*

No more.

* * *

"I brought the counter-oath," I told the god, and took out the paper I had copied from the sealed archive, though I did not need it. I had read it so many times the words were carved somewhere behind my ribs. "Do you want to hear it? Most people find it anticlimactic. It's very short. Gods usually expect more."

"Speak it, then," Vhalor said, amused, vast, certain. "You will lose your voice by the second syllable. I have watched it happen to better speakers than you. And then I will finish my turn, and this kingdom will kneel, and it will be *grateful*, Isandro Nystrom. Gratitude is the gentlest chain ever forged."

"That's the part you don't understand," I said. My hands were shaking. I let them. There was no point pretending to be calm; the ledger of my body had already recorded terror in triplicate. "You think the cost is the argument against speaking. But I've been a bookkeeper my whole life. I know a debt when I see one. And this—" I gestured at the kneeling city beyond the windows, at the hollowed congregation, at the king trapped behind his own eyes— "this is *my* debt. My guild built your prison and then forgot why. My oath kept your door shut while you picked the lock with other people's prayers. I signed off on all of it, year after year, entry after entry. So the cost isn't the argument."

I looked at him—looked past the crown, past the borrowed face, down to where a tired man was folded up small inside his own body, still praying, still hoping someone would come.

"The cost is just the price," I said. "And I'm good for it."

I unfolded the paper. I did not look at it.

* * *

The counter-oath has no name in the archive. The clerks who catalogued it called it *the unbinding word*, which is like calling a wildfire *the un-candle*. It was spoken once, in the founding days, by seven swordspeaker-priests standing in a circle around a chained god, and it cost six of them their voices and one her life, and the guild wrote it all down in beautiful copperplate and then locked the room and pretended the room did not exist.

We were very good at pretending rooms did not exist. It was, if I am honest, our core competency.

I planted my feet the way my mother taught me when I was nine years old and already dangerous. I thought of her grave-marker in the ruined village, and the original oath hidden in its stone, and how forgiveness had felt less like warmth and more like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying.

I thought of Wren's terrible handwriting. Of Maren's one eye, seeing more than two ever had. Of Bramble grumbling about door-holding duty and then volunteering for the worst door. Of Oskar's drawings—thirteen moons, a kneeling city, and in the last picture, drawn small in the corner where he thought no one would look: five figures standing together, unburned.

He'd seen us survive. The boy who saw endings had put us in the corner of one, alive.

I breathed in.

And I spoke.

* * *

I will not write the word here. Not because it is forbidden—it is, but the guild's forbidding has never once stopped me—but because it does not survive transcription. Ink is too slow for it. The word was made of unmaking, and the moment it left my mouth, the world remembered it had seams.

Reality tore.

Not dramatically—not the sky splitting, not the moon cracking like an egg, though I confess I expected both. It tore the way a letter tears when you open it wrong: along a line that had been there all along, waiting. The moonlight in the chapel peeled back like paper. The colors from the window ran. Somewhere below us, in the catacombs, I heard the feeding-stop—a sound like ten thousand held breaths released at once—and knew that Wren and Oskar's work in the dark had done its part, that the congregation's grip had loosened, that the god's anchor in the city had gone suddenly, wonderfully slack.

Vhalor screamed.

It was not a sound. It was a *correction*—the noise the world makes when something that should not be removed is removed anyway. The god came up out of King Aldric the way water comes out of a sponge under a press: reluctantly, in every direction at once, filling the chapel with a pressure that flattened the candle flames sideways and made the stained-glass saints lean away from their own windows.

I kept speaking.

The second syllable took my voice's edge off—I felt it go, a thinning, like frost melting off a blade. By the third, I could taste blood that wasn't there. By the fourth, the word was speaking itself through me, using whatever it found, and what it found was fifty years of silence, all of it at once, every unsaid thing I had ever swallowed rising up to fuel the unmaking.

All those years of *this is acceptable*. Burned as fuel. Spent gladly.

The god thrashed. The crown on Aldric's head glowed white-hot and began, slowly, to slide. And in the last instant—when the word was nearly done, when my throat was a ruin and my ears rang with the sound of a prison closing from the *inside*—Vhalor turned what remained of its attention on me and spoke directly, into the marrow of me, one last time:

*I remember your name,* it said. *The one they erased. I could give it back to you even now, dying. Would you like to die knowing who you were?*

"No," I said—or shaped, with what was left of my voice, which was not much. "I know who I am. I've got a whole ledger of it. Ask anyone."

And I finished the word.

* * *

The crown hit the marble and rolled.

The sound it made was small. Ordinary. Gold on stone, spinning down, wobbling, falling flat with a click that echoed in the sudden enormous quiet like a bell that had forgotten it was a bell.

King Aldric fell too. I caught him—barely, knees buckling, a man in his fifties catching a man in his fifties, neither of us built for it, both of us doing it anyway. He was lighter than he should have been, the way things are lighter when something heavy has finally been lifted out of them.

His eyes opened. They were his own eyes. Tired, wet, human, wrong-colored in the way eyes get after long illness, and completely, blessedly ordinary.

"Don't let it—" he rasped, still half in the dream of his captivity.

"It's done," I told him. My voice came out like gravel poured through a broken sieve. Two words cost me everything I had left. "Finished. The turn."

He stared at me. Then, with the peculiar gravity of a man who has been absent from his own life for a season and is taking inventory: "You're bleeding from the ears."

"So I am," I said.

"Was that—" He coughed. "Was that necessary?"

"Entirely," said a voice from the doorway, and there was Maren, one-eyed and furious with relief, Bramble leaning on the doorframe wheezing from the stairs, Wren supporting Oskar, whose chalk-stained fingers were already moving: *Did it work did it work did it work.*

"It worked," Wren translated, and then ruined the solemnity entirely by bursting into tears and laughing at the same time, which set off Bramble, who claimed afterward that he had dust in his eye, singular, the left one, do not ask further questions.

Aldric looked at all of us—at the appraiser, the toll-collector, the clerk, the kitchen boy, the voice-wrecked swordspeaker holding him up—and asked the question kings apparently must ask even with gods freshly evicted from their skulls:

"Who *are* you people?"

"The Ledgerhouse," we said, more or less together, and Maren added, "We keep accounts," and Bramble added, "We hold doors," and Oskar held up the crushed half-biscuit with an expression of tremendous ceremony.

I laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt. My voice was gone—truly gone, I could feel the absence of it the way you feel a missing tooth, a warm hollow where something used to live. Swordspeech burned out of me mid-word, spent down to the wick, and I would never pull another beam out of the world's architecture again.

I found, examining the loss honestly, in the ringing quiet of the torn-open chapel, with the thirteenth moon beginning—*beginning*—to slip backward out of its impossible window—

I found I didn't miss it yet. Maybe I would later. But in that moment, silence felt less like armor and more like rest.

* * *

We got the king onto the altar steps, because it was the nearest thing to a chair. Sera arrived minutes later with soldiers and soot on her cheek and the expression of a woman who had held a gate through the worst hour of her reign and intended to be compensated. She took one look at her husband—diminished, blinking, *himself*—and did something no court chronicler would ever record: she sat down on the steps beside him and put her forehead against his shoulder and stayed there for a long moment, saying nothing, which from Sera was a sonnet.

Then she stood, smoothed her dress, and became the queen again.

"Nystrom," she said. "You're expelled, I'm told. From your own guild."

I nodded. Writing in the air with one finger: *so I hear.*

"Sloppy of them," she said. "I chartered institutions before breakfast, you know. I could charter another." Her eyes went around the chapel—to the rolled crown, to the scorch-mark in the air where a god had been pressed out of the world, to the five of us standing in the wreckage like the world's least martial war band. "A house that tells the truth. Imagine it. No precedent whatsoever. The council will be apoplectic."

Bramble grinned. Maren's single eye gleamed. Wren was already, I noticed, pulling out a notebook.

But I was looking at the window. At the moon.

The thirteenth moon was retreating—sliding back down the sky it had climbed, unwinding its turn, going home to wherever impossible moons go when their work is interrupted. And as it went, all across the capital, in ten thousand houses and gutters and catacombs, the hollowed were drawing their first shuddering breaths of refilling. Prayers returned. Debts reversed. The greatest reversal ever entered in any ledger, and no one to record it but us.

Wren handed me the notebook, open to a fresh page, pen offered handle-first, the way you offer a sword to someone you trust.

My voice was gone. My hand was not.

I took the pen, and began to write it all down—every word of it, nothing hidden—and outside, over a city learning how to breathe again, the last sliver of the thirteenth moon slipped below the horizon like a debt finally paid.
