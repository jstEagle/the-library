## 21. After the Thirteenth Turn

Aldric woke to the smell of beeswax and lavender, which meant either that he was dead or that his wife had been sitting vigil again.

He decided, after some consideration, that he was not dead. Dead men did not have mouths that tasted like a coin purse left in the rain. Dead men did not ache in every joint as though they'd been used as a doorstop for a season. And dead men, he was fairly certain, did not have Queen Sera of Vaelmark asleep in a chair beside their bed with an open ledger sliding off her knee and ink smudged along her jaw like war paint applied by a very small, very tired child.

"Sera," he said.

His voice came out as a rasp, thin and cracked, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled since his coronation. But it came out *his*. That was the miracle of it — the only miracle he wanted anymore. The voice was his own, hoarse and ordinary and entirely unaccompanied by anything vast pressing at the back of his teeth.

Her eyes opened instantly. Queens, he had learned across twenty years of marriage, slept the way cats did: entirely, and ready to kill.

"You're awake," she said. It was not a question, and it was not relief, exactly. Relief would come later, when there was time for it. What crossed her face first was something more practical — the look of a woman confirming that a siege had lifted before she began planning the rebuilding.

"I'm awake," Aldric agreed. He tried to sit up and discovered that his body had filed a formal complaint about the arrangement. "How long?"

"Nine days."

"Nine days." He absorbed this. "And the city?"

Sera looked at him for a long moment. He watched her decide how much truth a man could carry on his first morning back in his own skull, and he watched her decide — because she was Sera, and she had never once in her life decided to give him less than the whole weight of a thing — to give him all of it.

"Bowed," she said. "For nine days, half the capital knelt in the streets. They're standing up now, slowly, like people waking from a dream they're ashamed of. Some of them don't remember kneeling. Some of them remember everything." She paused. "The hollowed ones are filling back in. Slowly. The prayers are... returning, Isandro says. Whatever was taken is being given back, a little each day, like rain after a drought."

"And the thirteenth moon?"

"Gone. As though it had never been." Her mouth twisted. "The astronomers are beside themselves. Three of them have resigned. One has taken up drink, which Maren says is the traditional response."

Aldric laughed — a small, rusty sound, but real. Then, because he was Aldric, and because there were questions that could not wait even for a rusty laugh to settle: "My son. When it wore me. Did I—"

"You prayed," Sera said quietly. "That's all. You prayed for Tam's return, and something answered, and you spent nine days as a house it was renting." She took his hand. Her grip was careful, as though he might still be fragile, and he found he didn't mind. "You didn't do anything, Aldric. You *endured* something. There's a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my life making sure you believe me."

He looked at her — at the ink on her jaw, the shadows under her eyes, the ledger still balanced precariously on her knee — and felt something in his chest crack open in the good way, the way a frozen river cracks in spring.

"How much do I owe the Ledgerhouse?" he asked.

"Everything," said Sera. "But they've agreed to accept payment in truth."

* * *

Three days later, Aldric stood in the great hall for the first time since the unbinding, leaning on a cane he resented and wearing a crown he resented more.

It was a smaller ceremony than any of his advisors had recommended. No triumph, no grand address from the balcony, no bells — the bells, everyone agreed, could rest for a while. Just the court, the household guard, and a strange little procession entering through the west doors: a woman with one eye and an appraiser's calm; a broad, grey-whiskered man who walked like someone who had once collected tolls on roads that officially did not exist; a junior clerk who kept tugging her sleeves down over her hands; and a mute kitchen boy carrying a wooden tray of documents as though it were made of glass and eggs.

And at their head, a figure in a plain dark coat, moving with the deliberate care of someone whose body remembered a violence the mind was trying to make peace with.

Isandro Nystrom. The last swordspeaker. Voice roughened to a gravel whisper, but present — Aldric could hear it now, that ruined edge, and understood what it had cost in a way no history book would ever capture.

"Your Majesty," Isandro said, and bowed — shallow, precise, the bow of someone paying exact debts and nothing more.

"Master Nystrom." Aldric's own voice was better than it had been, though it still tired quickly. He had rehearsed this speech with Sera four times. He abandoned it now, on the spot, because rehearsed speeches were how kings ended up wearing gods. "I'm told I owe you my life, my kingdom, and roughly nine days of my own body. I'm told also that your guild prefers to remain invisible, and that asking it to stand in a throne room is rather like asking a cat to attend parliament."

A flicker of something warm crossed the appraiser's face. The old toll-collector — Bramble, Sera had called him — snorted openly.

"But," Aldric continued, "the world has changed, and I find I've developed strong opinions about things that hide in the dark and feed on praying people. So." He nodded to Sera.

She stepped forward with the charter, and read it aloud in the clear voice that had held the palace gates for a night and a day while hollowed worshippers pressed against them: the Ledgerhouse of Impossible Things, chartered anew under crown protection, its purpose rewritten in a single line that Sera had insisted upon and Aldric had signed without hesitation:

*Nothing recorded here shall be hidden.*

"The guild will keep its ledgers," Sera finished, rolling the parchment closed, "but it will keep them *open*. Trades in impossible things will be recorded, witnessed, and answered for. And in exchange—" she looked at Isandro, and something passed between the two pragmatists that Aldric suspected neither of them would ever fully explain to him, "—the crown asks only one thing."

Isandro waited.

"When the next god comes knocking," Sera said, "and one always does — knock on our door first. Not theirs."

Isandro's ruined voice, when it came, was dry as old paper. "Your Majesty, we are accountants. Knocking first is the entire profession."

The hall laughed — carefully at first, then properly, the way a room laughs when it realizes it has been holding its breath for a season. Even Oskar smiled, and set the tray down, and produced from somewhere in his apron a small chalk drawing of the thirteenth moon with a large X through it, which he presented to the king with tremendous solemnity.

Aldric accepted it as seriously as any treaty he had ever signed.

* * *

The refilling, as the city came to call it, was not quick, and it was not painless, and Sera learned more about grief in those weeks than she had in all her years of ruling.

The widow Marta Corren — the shadowless one, the first — got her shadow back on a grey Tuesday, and stood in her orchard for six hours watching it move across the wet ground beneath her feet, weeping, while her neighbors pretended very hard to be busy elsewhere. The blind man who had been given sight woke one morning blind again, and told Wren — who had come to check on him, because the Ledgerhouse now followed up on its entries, which was apparently a radical innovation — that it was fine, that he'd had a month of his daughter's face and he would spend the rest of his life being grateful instead of bitter, and then he cried into his bread until Wren sat down beside him and cried too, because some accounts could not be balanced, only carried together.

The debtor whose coins had multiplied found his purse full of ordinary copper again, and reportedly laughed until he had to sit down in the street.

Some of the hollowed did not refill at all. Eleven of them. Sera kept the list herself, in her own hand, and visited the families, and did not send clerks, because some truths a queen had to speak with her own mouth. She learned the shape of each family's loss. She learned that grief, given honesty, became bearable in a way that grief given platitudes never did. She began, quietly, drafting a new kind of royal office — a Keeper of Returned Things — whose duty would be to record what the city had lost and what it had been given back, so that no one would ever again be told their suffering was invisible.

Aldric, meanwhile, relearned his own body the way a man relearns a house after burglars have moved through it. Some rooms were fine. Some rooms he flinched walking past. He caught himself, twice, standing before the chapel altar with his lips moving, and had to remind himself firmly that prayer was permitted, that prayer was *ordinary*, that the problem had never been the praying but the thing on the other end of the line picking up.

He took to visiting the Ledgerhouse instead. Officially, once a week, to review the new public ledgers. Unofficially, because the kitchen made excellent tea and Oskar had appointed himself the king's unofficial drawing instructor, and because there was something in that cluttered, honest, ink-smelling house that his palace had never managed: every person in it knew exactly what everything cost.

"You know," he remarked to Isandro one afternoon, watching Wren and Bramble argue cheerfully over an entry concerning a man who had traded three years of his bad luck for a decent harvest and was now complaining the harvest was *too* decent, "for most of my reign I believed the kingdom ran on laws."

"It runs on debts," Isandro rasped. "Laws are just debts written where everyone can see them. Your Majesty."

"And your guild hid the debts."

"For two hundred years." Isandro turned his teacup slowly. "We told ourselves invisibility was mercy. That people slept better not knowing what traded in the dark." A pause. "Maren says we were keeping the cellar clean by never opening the door. She says a lot of things. Most of them are right, which is insufferable."

"And now?"

"Now we open the door." Isandro looked up, and Aldric saw in that scarred, tired face something he recognized from his own mirror — the look of a person who had paid an enormous price and was still deciding, daily, whether it had been worth it. "Now we write it all down where anyone can read it. It's louder. It's messier. People are angry about things they never knew to be angry about before." The ghost of a smile. "It's better."

* * *

On the evening of the first snow, Sera found Aldric on the balcony overlooking the city, wrapped in enough blankets that he resembled a state secret.

"You should be inside," she said, joining him.

"So should you."

"Yes, well. One of us has to be sensible and I've clearly lost the argument." She tucked herself against his side, and they watched the lights of the capital come on below — ordinary lights, shop windows and hearth-fires, a city going about the miraculous business of being ordinary.

Below them, in the square, the bells rang the hour. Twelve times. Exactly twelve. Aldric counted anyway. He suspected he would count for the rest of his life, and had made peace with it.

"Marta Corren sent up a basket today," Sera said. "Apples. From the orchard." She smiled against his shoulder. "First real harvest since the drought. She included a note for the Ledgerhouse — a thank-you, formally entered. Wren showed me. They've started a new ledger, just for gratitude. Apparently it's filling faster than any ledger they've ever kept."

"Good," Aldric said. "Let that be the loudest book in the building."

They stood a while longer in the cold, saying nothing, which after everything was its own kind of prayer — the safe kind, addressed to no one, answered by nothing but each other.

Then, far below, at the edge of the lamplight where the merchant quarter met the river, Aldric noticed a light that should not have been there: a single window in the old customs house, long shuttered, glowing a deep and patient gold. As he watched, a figure appeared briefly in the frame — tall, robed, still — looking up toward the palace with what he could only describe as *attention*.

"Aldric?" Sera said. "What is it?"

He did not answer at once. Across the rooftops, faint as memory, he heard — or thought he heard, or feared he heard — a bell begin to ring a thirteenth time.

It stopped after one toll.

But it had begun.

"*Sera,*" he said quietly, "send word to the Ledgerhouse."
