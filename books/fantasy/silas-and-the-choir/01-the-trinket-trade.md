## 1. The Trinket Trade

The Nightmarket in Vell woke the way an old dog wakes: slowly, with complaint, and only because it had no choice.

Ansel Dunmore had been setting up their stall for twelve years, and in all that time they had never once managed to do it quietly. The awning pole stuck at the same stubborn angle every evening, requiring a kick in exactly the wrong place to seat it properly. The folding table had a leg that thought deeply about collapsing whenever anyone leaned on the left side. And the lantern — Ansel's lantern, bought secondhand from a chandler who had clearly hated it — flickered in a rhythm that could only be described as passive-aggressive.

"Evening, Dunmore," called Old Brannic from the pickle cart two stalls down, not looking up from his barrels.

"Evening, Brannic."

"You'll want to mind your table leg tonight. Wind's coming off the harbor."

"It always comes off the harbor."

"Aye, and it always tips your table." Brannic said this with the deep satisfaction of a man whose predictions were consistently correct and consistently useless.

Ansel kicked the awning pole. It seated itself with a clunk. They arranged their wares with the care of a jeweler laying out crown regalia: carved bone buttons that were merely buttons; knotted cord bracelets that were merely cord; little tin birds that did nothing whatsoever except sit there, being tin birds, with an air of having witnessed things.

And then, beneath the tray of tin birds, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden so well that even Ansel sometimes forgot where it was: three luck-knots.

Not charms. Ansel was very careful, in their own head, never to call them charms. Charms were guild-work. Charms were what had gotten four hundred people hanged or worse during the Purge, and what kept the Ministry's Inquisitors employed and well-fed. A luck-knot was, officially, a decorative braid in the harbor style, popular with sailors' wives, worth two coppers.

Unofficially, if you tied one while thinking very hard about a specific person, and whispered the old binding-words under your breath — *hold fast, hold kind* — the knot would keep that person's luck from unraveling for a season or so. Not magic, exactly. More like... persuasion. Magic, Ansel's master Wren used to say, was just the world agreeing to be talked around, and most days the world was tired and willing to take a small favor.

Wren had been dead twelve years. Or missing. Ansel had stopped being able to tell the difference, which was its own kind of grief, the kind that didn't ache so much as hum, low and constant, like the bad lantern.

The Nightmarket filled in around them by degrees. First the lamplighters, then the fishwives back off the boats with their voices like gulls fighting over a carcass, then the pickpockets, who arrived with the punctuality of clergy. Somewhere a fiddle started up, badly. The smell of fried onions and tar smoke settled over everything like a blanket that needed washing.

Ansel sold six buttons, two bracelets, and a tin bird to a child who paid entirely in buttons, which Ansel accepted out of respect for the economy of children.

Then came Maren Ostwick.

Ansel saw her before she saw the stall — you learned to read people in the trinket trade, and Maren was reading like an open ledger. She walked the market rows too quickly, stopped too suddenly, doubled back once as though she'd forgotten something and couldn't remember what. Her hands wouldn't stay still. Her husband fished the deep water past the Teeth, where the currents went strange, and he'd been out four days now when the run usually took two.

She'd been here last week, too. And the week before.

"Mistress Ostwick," Ansel said warmly, before she could pretend she was browsing. "You're back. I hope that means business is good."

"I—" She looked at the tray of buttons. At the tin birds. Anywhere but at Ansel's face. "I was just passing."

"The cord bracelets are two coppers," Ansel offered. "Very fashionable. My supplier swears by them, and my supplier has never lied to me about anything except his debts."

Maren's laugh came out cracked down the middle. She pressed her palm flat on the table, leaned in, and said, low, "Please. You know why I'm here."

Ansel glanced down the row. Brannic was arguing with a customer about vinegar. The pickpocket twins were occupied lifting a purse off a man too drunk to notice, which was practically community service. Nobody was watching.

But somebody was always watching. That was the first rule of surviving in Vell, and Ansel had written it on the inside of their own skull in letters they read every morning: *somebody is always watching.*

"The luck-knots are gone," Ansel said gently. "Sold out. Terribly popular this season."

Maren's face did something terrible and small. "Please. He's my whole—"

"Mistress Ostwick." Ansel kept their voice pleasant, market-bright, and slid their hand along the underside of the table where the oilcloth bundle waited. "I understand you're interested in a gift for your husband. When he returns. Which he will. Sailors are notoriously difficult to drown; it's practically their defining feature."

"He's late," she whispered. "He's never late."

Ansel's chest did the thing it did, the slow squeeze, the old guilt turning over like a stone in a streambed. They should say no. Every instinct honed over twelve years of careful, gray, invisible living said *say no, sell her a bracelet, keep your head down, keep everyone safe.* The Ministry didn't hang charm-sellers for the crime itself anymore. They hung them for patterns. For being known. For being *asked for.*

They put the knot in her hand anyway, folded inside a scrap of cloth, quick as a card trick.

"For your husband," Ansel said. "A decoration. Tie it to the mast-post, or his kit-bag, wherever he keeps his things. It's harbor-style braiding. Very lucky. People swear by them." They took her coins — three coppers, though she tried to press a fourth, and Ansel pushed it back. "That's the price. I don't charge extra for good taste."

Maren's eyes were wet and furious with gratitude. "Bless you," she breathed, and then, terrified of her own words, she said it again in a way that meant nothing: "Bless you, sir, lovely buttons," and hurried away into the crowd like a woman escaping the scene of a crime.

Ansel watched her go and felt the familiar split feeling: warmth in one half of the chest, cold dread in the other. Twelve years of keeping both halves balanced, like carrying water in a cracked jug.

*Hold fast, hold kind,* they thought at her retreating back, uselessly, the way you might throw a kiss after a ship.

* * *

The fence found Ansel at the hour when the market was thickest and the lamplight made everyone equally unrecognizable.

Dov Hetch was a small man built like a question mark, with rings on eight fingers and a smile he wore the way other men wore armor — constantly, and with visible effort underneath. He materialized at the stall's corner holding a tin bird, examining it with the gravity of an appraiser for the Crown.

"This is garbage," Dov announced fondly.

"It's artisanal garbage. There's a difference, and the difference is the markup."

"I'll give you four coppers for the lot of them." He set the bird down and produced, from somewhere in his coat, a small drawstring pouch that clinked. "And I have something you might want to look at. Off the books."

Ansel kept sorting buttons. "Everything I sell is off the books, Dov. That's rather the point of me."

"Ah, but this is off *your* books." Dov leaned in, lowering his voice to the register he probably believed was discreet and which carried, in fact, to at least three neighboring stalls. "Came across a piece last week. Guild-make. Pre-Purge. A focus-stone, set in silver, still humming. The buyer I had lined up got nervous — Ministry inspection at the docks, you know how it is — and now I'm sitting on it, and sitting on guild-make is like sitting on a lit stove. I'd rather it be your problem than mine."

The word *guild* landed in Ansel's stomach like a dropped anchor.

"No," Ansel said.

"You haven't heard the price."

"There is no price. I sell buttons, Dov. Buttons and bracelets and birds. If someone wanted what you're describing, they'd be a criminal, and I am a respected member of the market community with a stall and everything."

"You have a table with a broken leg and an awning you kick."

"A respected member nonetheless."

Dov studied them for a long moment, and behind the smile his eyes did the thing that made even the pickpockets polite to him: they went flat and patient, like a fisherman watching a line. "Twelve years, Dunmore," he said softly. "Twelve years you've been the safest person in Vell, because nobody knows what you are. But I know what you are. And I've never once used it. Do you know why?"

"Because I pay you promptly and compliment your rings?"

"Because I liked your masters," Dov said. "Before. They were decent to a young thief who deserved hanging. So." He set the pouch on the table between them. "No obligation. Just know it exists. Know that pieces like it are moving through the city again — three this month, by my count, and I don't count wrong. Somebody's digging up the past, friend. And when somebody digs up the past in Vell, the Ministry brings shovels of their own."

He patted the table twice, smiled his armored smile, and melted into the crowd.

Ansel looked at the pouch for a long time. Then they swept it into the lockbox under the table without opening it, told themselves firmly that they would absolutely not open it later, and knew, with the weary certainty of long experience, that they would open it later.

Three pieces of guild-make moving through the city in a month. In twelve years, Dov had never found so much as a spoon.

*Somebody's digging up the past.*

Ansel straightened the tin birds, which did not need straightening, and waited for the worst part of the night.

* * *

The worst part of the night arrived at half past eleven, as it always did, wearing a Ministry sash and carrying a ledger.

Clerk Fenwick was not an Inquisitor. This distinction mattered enormously to Fenwick and not at all to anyone else. He was a round, damp, apologetic man whose job was to walk the Nightmarket collecting the "commercial maintenance levy" — protection coin, in honest daylight language — and whose secondary job, unspoken and enormous, was to notice things. What stalls appeared. What stalls vanished. Who flinched.

He arrived at Ansel's stall, sighed the sigh of a man approaching a task he found distasteful, and opened his ledger.

"Evening, Clerk Fenwick," Ansel said. "Lovely night for levies."

"Is it?" Fenwick consulted the sky, which was raining. "I hadn't noticed." He licked his pencil. "The levy stands at eight coppers this quarter. There's been an adjustment."

"There's always an adjustment."

"Inflation," Fenwick said, with the tone of a man reciting scripture he didn't believe in. "Also the harbor works. Also, I believe, a new fountain somewhere. I've never seen the fountain. No one has seen the fountain. But we all pay for it."

Ansel counted out ten coppers and set them on the ledger. Fenwick looked at the two extra, and then up at Ansel, and for a moment — the moment that happened every single week, the moment Ansel dreaded more than any Inquisitor's black coat — something passed across the clerk's damp, ordinary face. Something like recognition. Something like *I know what you are, and I have decided, week after week, to know nothing.*

Fenwick swept the extra coppers into his pocket with great dignity. "For the fountain," he said.

"Of course."

"And — hm." He paused, pencil hovering. His voice dropped, became almost human. "There's been a circular from the Ministry this week. Internal. About unusual goods moving through the markets. Guild-make items, specifically. The Inquisitory is asking us clerks to... note anything irregular." He coughed. "I note nothing irregular here. Your stock is the dullest in Vell, Dunmore. Genuinely. I mean that as a compliment. Tin birds. Honestly."

"Thank you, Clerk Fenwick."

"Don't thank me, it's unseemly." He closed the ledger, hesitated, and added, quieter still: "The Inquisitors themselves have been seen in the lower quarters. Not the usual patrol sort. The courteous ones. The ones who ask questions and write things down and smile." He shuddered delicately. "In my experience, the smiling ones are the ones you want to avoid. The shouty ones at least tell you what they want."

"Noted," Ansel said, and meant it.

Fenwick waddled off toward the pickle cart, already sighing in preparation. Ansel watched him go and turned the warning over like a coin, checking its weight. Smiling Inquisitors in the lower quarters. Three pieces of guild-make surfacing in a month. A fishwife's husband four days late off the deep water.

The market roared on around them — the fiddle had found a second, equally bad player, and together they were committing crimes against a reel — and Ansel stood in the middle of it, behind their little table of harmless things, feeling the loneliness arrive right on schedule. It came every night at this hour, after the selling was done and before the packing started, in the gap where there was nothing to do but be a person. Twelve years of being nobody. Twelve years of being so carefully, so completely nobody that there was hardly anybody left inside to keep the nobody company.

Wren would have laughed at the tin birds. Wren had laughed at everything, right up until the soldiers came, and then—

Ansel didn't finish the thought. They never finished the thought. Finishing the thought was a luxury for people whose masters had bodies to bury.

They began packing up. Buttons in their box. Bracelets in their roll. The tin birds, all nine of them, into the crate, where they sat in their rows, silent witnesses to nothing.

It was nearly midnight when Ansel blew out the bad lantern and lifted the lockbox from under the table — heavier than it should have been, because of Dov's pouch, because of course they'd have to open it, because the past apparently wasn't finished sending invoices — and that was when they heard the noise from the alley behind the stall.

A scuffle. Short. Wet-sounding. Then silence, which was worse.

Then footsteps — not retreating, but approaching, uneven, dragging — and a shape lurched out of the dark between the pickle cart and the wall. A man. Big, once; hollowed out now. There was blood on him, a great deal of it, spreading down his side with terrible patience, and his eyes found Ansel's across the emptying market with the desperate accuracy of a drowning man sighting a rope.

He knew. Somehow, impossibly, he knew to come *here.*

"Dunmore," the man rasped, and Ansel's whole body went cold, because nobody in Vell called them that. Nobody in Vell knew that name. "Ansel Dunmore. She said—" He stumbled, caught himself on the stall's post, and thrust something toward them: a scroll case, sealed with wax stamped in a sigil Ansel hadn't seen in twelve years and would know blind, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. "The Choir remembers. Take it. *Take it—*"

Boots. From the far end of the market, hard-heeled and rhythmic and getting closer. Ministry boots, walking the night rounds, unhurried, certain.

The man's legs gave out. Ansel caught him — all that weight, all that blood — and looked once, wildly, down the row of darkened stalls, at the lantern-light bobbing nearer, at the lockbox in the crook of their arm and the dying stranger in their hands and nowhere at all to put either one.

The cellar hatch was three feet away.

Ansel had perhaps ten seconds to decide what kind of nobody they were going to be.
