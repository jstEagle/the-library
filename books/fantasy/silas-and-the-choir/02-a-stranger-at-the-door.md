## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The trouble with living under a pie shop, Ansel had long ago decided, was not the smell. The smell was fine—better than fine, at three in the morning when old Marta fired her ovens and the whole cellar filled with the warm ghost of cinnamon and lard. No, the trouble was that everyone in Vell assumed a man who lived beneath a pie shop must be either a rat or a criminal, and Ansel had spent twelve years working very hard to be neither, or at least to be convincingly the first.

They were sorting buttons when the knock came.

Not a knock, precisely. A sound like a sack of wet laundry being dropped against their door, followed by a scratching that might have been fingers. Ansel set down the button they'd been cataloguing—a horn one, four holes, nothing charming about it whatsoever—and listened.

Above them, through the floorboards, Marta's radio murmured the late shipping report. Herring runs down. Fog expected off the Spits. All ordinary. All safe.

The scratching came again.

Ansel's heart did the thing it always did when the past came scratching: it tried to climb up behind their teeth and hide. *Nobody knows you're here,* they told themselves, the way they told themselves every night, a little prayer worn smooth as a river stone. *You're just the button-seller from the Nightmarket. You're nobody. You're a rumor of a person.*

They opened the door anyway. This was, they would reflect later, the entire problem with Ansel Dunmore in a single sentence: they opened the door.

The man who fell through it was tall in the way that drowned things are tall—all length and no argument—and he was bleeding from somewhere under his coat, because the floorboards were already drinking it in dark ribbons. He had a courier's satchel strap across his chest, cut clean through, and his eyes were the particular gray of someone who has run a very long way and stopped believing in destinations.

"Door," he said, with great presence of mind. "You have a door."

"I do," Ansel said. "It's mine."

"Good." He nodded slowly, as if this confirmed some important theory. Then his knees went, and Ansel caught him—or rather, caught most of him, the rest arriving against the workbench with a clatter that scattered buttons across the cellar like startled beetles.

"Right," Ansel said to the room in general. "No. Absolutely not. Get out. Get—"

The man's hand shot out and closed around their wrist with the last of whatever kept him upright, and he pressed something into their palm. It was flat and stiff-edged. Wax crumbled against their skin.

"The Choir remembers," he said.

Ansel's whole body went cold, top to bottom, the way a house goes cold when someone opens a door onto winter. Nobody said that word. Nobody *knew* that word, not anymore, not outside of ash and rumor and the careful silence where a guild used to be.

"You've got the wrong cellar," Ansel whispered. "I sell buttons. I sell *buttons*."

He smiled then, which was worse than anything, because there was blood on his teeth and recognition in his eyes. "Ansel," he said. Not *friend*. Not *stranger*. Just the name, bare, unarmored, the name that hadn't crossed anyone's lips in twelve years except in Ansel's own head, where they kept it locked in a box inside a box inside a box.

Then his eyes rolled back, and all two hundred pounds of him came down like a felled tree, and Ansel stood in their own cellar holding a wax-sealed map and the knowledge that somebody, somewhere, had just spent their true name like a coin.

---

The boots started overhead ten minutes later.

Ansel knew boot-sounds the way farmers know weather. These were Ministry boots—not the loose slap of the watch, but the measured, even tread of men who walked in step because walking in step made them feel like an idea instead of men. Four of them, maybe five, crossing Marta's shop above.

Ansel looked at the body. The body bled politely, quietly, pooling with the patience of a thing that had nowhere else to be.

"No," Ansel told it. "*No.*"

The boots stopped directly overhead. Floorboards creaked. Marta's voice floated down, muffled and cheerful in the way she got with officials, the voice she used for tax collectors and inspectors and once, memorably, a bear that had wandered into the Nightmarket during a festival: *"Gentlemen! Pies are half-penny after midnight, no exceptions, not even for the Ministry."*

Someone answered, too low to catch. Marta laughed. The laugh had a wobble in it.

Ansel moved.

Twelve years of careful, cowardly, meticulous survival kicked in like a second heartbeat, and their hands did what their hands had been trained to do before they'd ever been trained to do anything: they worked. The trapdoor under the dye-rack—the one Ansel had sworn never to use, the one whose existence they'd nearly forgotten on purpose—came open with a whisper of oiled hinges. Below it, the old drainage crawl, dry these forty years, running back toward the cistern ways. Cold air breathed up out of it, smelling of stone and old water and other people's secrets.

But the body wouldn't fit through the crawl. Ansel measured it with their eyes, twice, hoping to be wrong both times. They were not wrong.

The boots began to descend the shop stairs.

There is a version of Ansel Dunmore, they thought wildly, dragging a stranger's heels across the floorboards, who lets this happen. Who steps back, raises their hands, says *found him like this, never saw him before, I'm just the button-seller*—and survives, because surviving is the only thing Ansel has ever been any good at. That version of them does not bleed for strangers. That version learned better, years ago, in a courtyard, in the rain, learning exactly what happened to people who stood next to Charmwrights when the Ministry came.

That version of Ansel watched from somewhere behind their eyes as they stuffed the man's coat with rags to slow the bleeding, wrapped him in the drop-cloth from the dye rack, and hauled him into the coal alcove behind the false wall, arranging three sacks of anthracite against the seam like a very stupid, very desperate piece of furniture.

The knock on the cellar door was polite. That was the worst part. Ministry politeness, the courtesy of people who have never once needed to raise their voices.

"Open, please. Ministry inquiry."

Ansel wiped their hands on their apron, noted with distant hysteria that the apron now matched the drop-cloth, and opened the door with the face they kept for customs men: open, honest, faintly dim.

Two Inquisitors. Gray coats, gray gloves, gray eyes that took in the cellar the way a ledger takes in numbers. Behind them, at the top of the stairs, a third figure waited by Marta's ovens, and Marta herself stood wringing her floured hands and saying something about herring runs and fog.

"Inquiry?" Ansel said. "About what? I've paid my stall fees. I've—" They gestured vaguely at the buttons, as if buttons were a life's alibi. "I sell buttons."

"So we understand." The taller Inquisitor had a voice like a filing cabinet closing. He consulted a slate. "A courier came over the Marsh Bridge tonight at the turn of the tide. Cut down two streets from here. His satchel is missing."

"Terrible," Ansel said, and meant it, and hated how much they meant it. "Was he—"

"He ran," the Inquisitor said, "in this direction. Before he fell." Those gray eyes moved across the cellar again, slower this time. Across the dye-rack. Across the coal sacks. Across Ansel. "You heard nothing?"

"I heard Marta's radio. Herring's down. Everyone's very upset."

Something flickered at the corner of the Inquisitor's mouth—not quite a smile, more the place a smile would go if it could afford the rent. "Herring," he agreed. "We'll want to look at your floor."

"My floor."

"Standard procedure."

And here was the moment, Ansel thought later, lying awake in the dark with the map hidden inside a hollowed-out hymnal, where the whole shape of their life turned on a hinge so small they almost missed it. Because the Inquisitor knelt, and touched the floorboards, and the boards where the man had fallen were still dark—but dark with old stain too, decades of dye and tallow and Marta's leaks, and the new blood sat in the grain of the old like a word written over a word, legible only if you already knew the language.

The Inquisitor didn't know the language. He frowned at the boards, rubbed a gloved finger across them, found nothing but grime, and stood.

"Buttons," he said, as though confirming it to himself.

"Mostly horn," Ansel offered helpfully. "Some bone. Nothing enchanted, if that's a concern. Nothing's been enchanted in this city in twelve years, as I'm sure the Ministry knows better than anyone."

The silence that followed lasted exactly one heartbeat too long, and Ansel felt it the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects it.

"Of course," the Inquisitor said. "Lock your door. There are cutthroats about."

They left. The boots climbed, faded, dissolved into the fog-bound street. Upstairs, Marta's footsteps crossed to the trapdoor and her voice came down, pitched low: "Ansel? You all right down there?"

"Peachy," Ansel called back, in a voice that fooled absolutely no one, least of all themselves. "Tell me they bought pies."

"Four. Paid full price, the monsters." A pause. The floor creaked as she shifted her weight, the way she did when she was deciding whether to ask a question. She decided against it. Twelve years of neighbors will teach you each other's silences. "I'll bring you one down. The plum. You look like you need the plum."

---

Ansel sat on the floor beside the coal alcove for a long time after that, eating plum pie they couldn't taste, listening to the stranger breathe.

He was breathing. Shallow, wet, stubborn, but breathing. Ansel had cleaned the wound as best they could without light—a slash under the ribs, deep enough to worry, shallow enough to insult—and packed it with cobweb and salt-wash the way Master Wren had taught them, back when such knowledge was passed hand to hand like bread instead of buried like bodies. Their hands remembered everything. That was the cruelty of it. Twelve years of forgetting, and the hands had forgotten nothing at all.

"Why," Ansel asked him quietly, "did you come to *me*?"

The man's eyelids fluttered. For a moment he surfaced, gray-eyed and far away, and looked at Ansel with something terrible and gentle, like pity wearing a friend's face.

"Wren said—" he began.

The pie tin slipped from Ansel's fingers and rang against the floorboards like a small, absurd bell.

"—Wren said you'd open the door," the man finished, and smiled again, and sank back under.

*Wren.* Dead eleven years. Ash-scattered, mourned, wept over in this very cellar on nights Ansel would deny to their grave. Wren, who had taught them knots and cipher and the difference between a charm and a lie (there isn't one, little bird, that's rather the point). Wren, whose name lived in the same locked box as Ansel's own, and who had apparently reached out of the grave tonight and knocked on their door using a dying man's fist.

Ansel put their head in their hands.

From the alcove came the wet rasp of the stranger's breathing. From the hymnal on the shelf came the mute weight of the map. From overhead came Marta, humming, washing pans, keeping her own counsel. And from somewhere much farther away—from under the city, under the years, under everything—Ansel felt the old silence shift, the way a house shifts when someone long gone walks through it in a dream.

*The Choir remembers.*

"Well," Ansel said aloud, to the buttons, to the coal, to the bleeding man, to the ghost of a master who had never once in life done anything the simple way. "I don't."

But even as they said it, their fingers were already finding the hymnal, and the wax seal was already cracking under a thumbnail trained, long ago, in a schoolroom that officially never existed, to read the guild cipher that officially no longer existed—and the first line of the map rose up to meet them like a face across a crowded market, familiar and impossible and absolutely, unmistakably addressed to *them*.
