## 4. The Clerk's Question

The morning after the courier died in my cellar, I opened my stall as though nothing in the world had changed.

That is the first lesson Wren ever taught me, back when I was small enough to sit on a crate and watch his hands work: *Grief is a luxury, Ansel. Fear is a luxury. The Ministry cannot arrest a man who is boring.* He said it while braiding a luck-knot, his fingers moving with the absent grace of someone who had done it ten thousand times, and I had taken it for wisdom then. Twelve years later I understood it was survival, which is a different thing entirely—harder, colder, and far less kind.

So I set out my trays of trinkets in the grey light, arranged the river-glass beads so they caught what little sun Vell was willing to share, and waited for the Nightmarket to become the Daymarket around me. The fishwives came first, as always, their carts rattling over cobbles still slick from the night tides. Old Marta bought nothing but stood at my stall anyway, complaining about her knees and the price of eels and the way the Ministry clerks had been sniffing along the quay lately, "asking questions like they'd lost something." She said it lightly. Everything Marta said was light. That was how she'd survived too.

I smiled at her and agreed that clerks were a plague upon honest folk, and my hands did not shake, because I would not permit them to.

The courier's body was under the floorboards of my cellar workshop, packed in salt and old sacking, because I could not think what else to do with him. The map was sewn into the lining of my coat, against my ribs, where it felt less like paper and more like a second heartbeat. Every hour or so I caught myself pressing a hand flat to my side, the way you touch a bruise to check if it's still there.

It was still there.

*The Choir remembers,* he'd said, and then he'd stopped saying anything at all, and the boots overhead had faded into the ordinary noise of Vell, and I had been left alone in the dark with a dead man's blood on my hands and a word I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was nine years old.

Boring, Ansel. Be boring.

* * *

Voss came at midday, when the market was at its loudest and no one watches anyone else closely.

I knew before I saw her face. There is a particular quality to silence that spreads outward from certain people—a hush that moves through a crowd like oil through water—and I felt it reach my stall a full minute before she did. The fishwives' chatter thinned. The pickpockets drifted elsewhere. Even the gulls seemed to wheel away.

She was not what I expected, though I'm not sure what I'd expected. Something gaunt and grim, perhaps, all black coats and cold eyes. Instead Inquisitor Halle Voss looked like somebody's favourite aunt: a broad, comfortable woman in a good grey travelling cloak, silver-streaked hair pinned neatly, a basket over one arm as though she'd merely paused on her way home from the market herself. Her face was open, pleasant, faintly amused by the world. Her eyes moved like a ledger being written.

"Master Dunmore," she said, and my name landed on the counter between us like a coin I hadn't offered.

"That's me." I kept my voice easy, market-easy, the voice I used for haggling. "Though most folk just call me the bead man. You'll be wanting a charm for the road? Luck-knots are three coppers, and I'll throw in a blessing free, though I'll warn you it's mostly decorative."

"A blessing." She smiled as if I'd said something genuinely charming. "How very sensible of you."

She set her basket down on the counter without asking, which was its own small message—we are past asking—and began to examine my wares with the unhurried attention of a woman who had all day and wanted me to know it. She picked up a river-glass bead and held it to the light. She turned over a carved whistle, a knotted cord, a tin medallion stamped with a fish that was supposed to be lucky for sailors and was, in truth, only tin.

"You have a good eye for arrangement," she said. "Everything exactly where it should be. Nothing out of place." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "I admire that in a tradesman."

"Years of practice. Clutter frightens off customers."

"Mm." She put the bead down precisely where it had been, aligned to the millimetre with its neighbours, and I understood she was showing me she'd noticed. "I'm looking for someone, Master Dunmore. Perhaps you can help. A courier. Youngish man, brown hair, went by the name of Tam. He carried messages along the quay routes. He seems to have gone... astray."

"Astray," I repeated.

"Missing." Her pleasantness didn't flicker. "His last delivery was somewhere in this quarter. Yesterday evening, late. You keep long hours, I understand. Later than most."

"I close when the fishwives close. They're my best customers and their husbands worry." I shrugged, and let a little boredom into it, the way you'd let steam out of a kettle a bit at a time. "Couriers come through here all hours. Half of Vell runs on boys with satchels. Brown hair describes every third person on this quay, including me, if I let it grow."

"True enough." She laughed softly, and it was a warm laugh, and that frightened me more than anything else she'd done. "You're quick. I like that too."

*Don't like me*, I thought. *Like me slowly, and I'm dead.*

* * *

Here is what I knew about Inquisitors, from twelve years of careful listening and careful silence: they were not soldiers. Soldiers kicked doors. Inquisitors knocked, and smiled, and asked after your mother's health, and six months later you vanished between one Tuesday and the next, and your neighbours swore afterward they'd never noticed anything strange about you at all. The Ministry called them clerks, sometimes, in official documents. *Ministry Clerk, Third Rank.* As if the whole apparatus of fear could be made respectable by bad bookkeeping.

And here is what I noticed, as Voss continued her gentle interrogation—about my suppliers, about whether I'd seen any strangers, about whether business was good, about whether I'd lived in Vell long ("All my life," I said, which was true, and "always at this stall," which was not)—was that her right hand never strayed far from her basket.

Not toward a weapon. Toward the basket itself.

When she finally lifted it to leave, she held it a moment longer than she needed to, angled just so, and inside it, among what appeared to be bread and apples, I saw it.

A luck-knot. Braided in the old style, three strands, the pattern tight and true—the way Wren had taught me, the way almost nobody alive still tied them. My knots. Or near enough that the difference would mean nothing to anyone except the person who'd made it, and the person who'd learned from the same hands.

One of mine. Sold, traded, or lifted—I couldn't know which—but mine, sitting in an Inquisitor's basket like a hook sitting in water.

I didn't look at it. That was the hardest thing I've ever done, harder than hiding a body, harder than lying to her face. I looked at her instead, and kept my expression at the exact temperature of mild, helpful indifference, and prayed to whatever outlawed god might still take calls from the likes of me that my face was as well-arranged as my stall.

"Thank you for your time, Master Dunmore," Voss said, settling the basket on her arm. "You've been very helpful."

"I haven't helped at all."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But you've been *very* helpful about it." She paused, and her eyes—those ledger-eyes—rested on me with something that was almost kindness, which was the worst thing yet. "You know, it occurs to me that a man who sells blessings ought to be careful. This city has a way of punishing the innocent for the crimes of others. It's a flaw in the design. I do what I can, within my remit, to protect people like you." A beat. "People worth protecting."

"Kind of you," I managed.

"It's nothing." She smiled, gathered herself, and turned—then stopped, as if struck by an afterthought, and looked back over her shoulder. "Oh—one more thing. Purely idle curiosity. The knot-work you sell. Where did you learn the pattern? It's quite distinctive. Old-fashioned. Most of these quay traders use simpler work."

I let the pause stretch exactly as long as a man's honest puzzlement should stretch, and not a heartbeat further.

"My grandmother," I said. "She was from upriver. They braid everything upriver—hair, rope, grudges."

"Charming." And she laughed again, that warm laugh, and walked away into the crowd, which parted around her the way water parts around something that swims beneath the surface.

* * *

I did not move for a long time. I sold two whistles and a string of beads to a child with coins clenched in a sweaty fist, and my voice came out level, and I have no memory whatsoever of doing it.

Marta drifted back at some point, drawn by the smell of my unease the way gulls are drawn by gutting. "Who was that, then? She had clerk's eyes."

"Ministry," I said. "Looking for a missing courier."

"Hmph. Missing couriers don't usually stay missing, in my experience. They turn up." She lowered her voice to a gravelly whisper. "In pieces. In the harbour. My cousin saw one once, back in the Purge year—" She caught herself, glanced around, and patted my counter awkwardly. "Well. Long ago. Different times."

"Different times," I agreed, and thought of salt and sacking and floorboards, and of a boy named Tam whose real name I would never know, and of the map burning against my ribs like a coal wrapped in cloth.

Different times. The same times, rather, wearing a different coat.

I closed early that day, pleading a headache, and stood in the emptying market watching the light go copper on the rooftops, and made myself think instead of feel, because feeling was a luxury.

Facts. One: Voss had a guild-style luck-knot, which meant either that someone had reported buying charms from me—reported *what kind* of charms—or that she'd been collecting them, tracing patterns, building a case stitch by stitch the way Wren built a binding. Two: she hadn't arrested me. An Inquisitor with evidence doesn't chat about grandmothers. Three: she'd told me, in her courteous, sideways fashion, that she knew exactly what I was—or at least suspected it—and that my safety depended on remaining useful, or interesting, or both.

She wasn't hunting a courier. Couriers were a means to an end. She was fishing, patiently, in waters she believed held something large, and I had just watched her bait the hook with my own handiwork.

And four—the fact I kept circling like a moth around a lamp—the courier had known my true name. Not Dunmore. The other one. The guild-name, whispered to infants in cellars, buried with the dead. He'd pressed a map sealed in wax into my hands and said *the Choir remembers*, and he'd known, dying, exactly whose hands he wanted it in.

Someone had sent him. Someone who knew the old cipher, the old names, the old knots. Someone inside a world I'd spent twelve years pretending was ash.

Which meant the past wasn't finished with me.

Which meant Voss wasn't the only one fishing.

I locked the stall, checked twice that no shadow lingered in the alley mouth, and went home the long way, through streets I'd chosen years ago for their sightlines and their exits, and all the while the map lay against my heart, and the question sat in my throat like a swallowed stone:

If someone from the old guild was reaching for me across twelve silent years—

What did they want me to find?

And what, I wondered, standing a moment in the dark of my own doorway, listening to the city breathe, would Halle Voss do when she found it first?

Above me, in the window of the house opposite, a curtain shifted. Just slightly. Just once.

I went inside, and barred the door, and did not sleep.
