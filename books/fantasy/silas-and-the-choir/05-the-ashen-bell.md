## 5. The Ashen Bell

The map did not want to be read at night.

Ansel discovered this on the third evening of trying, hunched over the workbench with a candle guttering and the wax seal's ghost still crumbling under their thumbnail. By daylight the cipher-lines behaved—cooperative, almost friendly, the way guild script always had been, curling into little jokes if you knew where to look. But by candlelight the lines seemed to *move*, and not in the charming way. In the way of a thing that was deciding whether it trusted you yet.

"Fine," Ansel told it. "Be difficult. Everyone in this city is."

The map said nothing, which was its most convincing performance as an ordinary object so far.

They had spent two days being ordinary themselves. The stall had opened at dawn both mornings; the luck-knots and hearth-blessings and one genuinely cursed comb (Ansel kept it under the counter for emergencies and morale) had sold without incident. They had smiled at the fishwife's sister, haggled with Doss the fence over a crate of "antique" buttons that were neither antique nor buttons, and paid the protection coin to Clerk Ferrow with the usual wordless little pantomime—coin slid under ledger, ledger closed, both parties pretending the other did not exist. Twelve years of practice. It should have been soothing.

It was not soothing. Because twice now, walking home, Ansel had felt the particular prickle between the shoulder blades that meant someone was matching their pace three streets back, and because every time they turned, there was nothing but Vell doing what Vell did: closing up, banking its fires, folding itself away like a market stall at dusk.

And because of the words. *The Choir remembers.* Spoken by a dying man who had known Ansel's true name—not Dunmore, the name they'd stitched onto their stall license with a forged signature and a prayer, but the name Wren had given them at nine years old, inked into a guild register that officially no longer existed.

Nobody living knew that name. Ansel had checked. For twelve years, they had checked.

So: the undercity. Tonight.

* * *

Vell's undercity was less a place than a sediment. The city had been built, burned, rebuilt, and built again so many times that the older Vells simply sank, layer beneath layer, until the streets people walked were only the newest skin stretched over a body of drowned alleys, bricked-up chapels, cisterns, and stairs that went down to nowhere anyone admitted to. The Ministry called it the Undervault and pretended it was a drainage problem. Everyone else called it the Underways and pretended they'd never been there.

There were perhaps forty ways down from the Nightmarket quarter alone. Ansel knew six, because knowing ways down was the sort of thing a Charmwright learned first and forgot last, the way you never quite forget how to swim even when the water is the problem.

They chose the old tannery stair, because it smelled so comprehensively terrible that no one lingered, and descended with a shuttered lantern held low, counting steps out of habit. Forty-one. Forty-two. The air changed the way it always did at the boundary—cooler, wetter, tasting of stone and old smoke—and the sounds of Vell above flattened into a distant heartbeat.

"Right," Ansel murmured to the dark. "Clue one."

The map's first marking sat at the junction of two cipher-lines, and once you translated it—which took Ansel four attempts and one moment of genuine despair—it read, in the cramped hand of someone who had learned letters the guild way, left-handed and slanted:

*Where the bell that cannot ring keeps the hour that never came.*

Which was, Ansel felt, exactly the sort of thing guild elders wrote when they wanted to feel clever. Master Wren had been the worst for it. He'd once set an entire semester's final exam as a riddle whose answer was "a bucket," on the grounds that *a Charmwright must learn that grand questions often have humble answers,* and nine students had failed before someone thought to check the well.

A bell that could not ring. Ansel stood in the junction chamber—a round room, older than anything above, its walls furred with mineral bloom—and let their memory do the walking. Bells of Vell: the Cathedral's great Brazen Voice, which rang constantly and everyone hated. The harbor fog-bell. The chapel bells melted down in the Purge—

Melted down.

Guild seals had been melted down too. Ansel remembered that night, or the shape of it: Wren's hands shaking as he fed the masters' signet-seals into a crucible, destroying the guild's authority in metal form so the Inquisitors couldn't use it to hunt stragglers. *Dead metal for a dead guild,* he'd said, and then he'd wept, which Ansel at nine had found more frightening than the fires.

What did you cast, when you melted thirteen seals?

You cast something that would be recognized by the people who needed recognizing and ignored by everyone else. You cast a marker. A dead drop.

Ansel turned east, following the map's second line, and walked.

* * *

The bell hung in a chapel that had been a chapel, then a storehouse, then a chapel again by popular demand, then nothing at all. Its roof had gone; stars and chimney-smoke shared the sky overhead on negotiated terms. The walls remained just high enough to give the space the feeling of a held breath.

The bell itself was small—hand-bell sized, absurdly, hanging from a rotted beam by a leather strap gone stiff as bark. It was the color of old ash, pitted, ugly, and utterly silent, and when Ansel raised the lantern close, the light caught in the pitting and came back wrong: not reflections but *impressions*. Faces. Thirteen faces, worked into the bronze so finely you'd take them for corrosion unless you knew to look.

Ansel knew all thirteen.

"Mistress Ovel," they whispered, touching a pitted brow. "Master Corrin. Old Tam." Their finger moved around the bell's crown like a child counting family portraits. "You're all here. All of you. Who—" The question caught. *Who cast you?* Because Wren had destroyed the seals. Ansel had watched him do it. Which meant either the map's author had gathered fragments afterward, or—

Or Wren had lied about something else, too, and Ansel's chest did a small cold turn at the thought and refused to elaborate.

*Where the bell that cannot ring keeps the hour that never came.* Ansel looked at the chapel floor. Under the bell, worn into the flagstones by some long-ago congregation, was a sundial's empty socket—the gnomon long since scavenged. A dial that told no hour. The hour that never came.

Ansel knelt. The socket's base was loose. Of course it was loose; everything in the guild's world was loose if you knew the trick of it, and the trick here was the same one Wren used on his spice cupboard: press, turn against the sun, lift.

Inside was a lead box the size of a hymnal.

Ansel's hands were steady right up until they weren't. They set the box on their knees, breathed the way Wren had taught them—*in for the count of a kettle, out for the count of a bell*—and lifted the lid.

Papers. A dozen papers, folded small, and a strip of dried meat wrapped in cloth, and—Ansel laughed despite everything, a startled bark that echoed off the ruined walls—one of Ansel's own luck-knots, bought from their own stall, price tag still knotted to the cord.

Someone had been watching the shop.

They got one paper unfolded before the voice came out of the dark behind them, low and dry as a struck match:

"I'd stop reading there, if I were you. Some of those are addressed."

Ansel did not scream, which they counted later among their life's modest achievements. They did drop the lantern, which rolled, spun, and threw wheeling shadows across the chapel like a small panicked planet, and in its light Ansel saw the stranger: a woman, mid-thirties maybe, sitting on a fallen roof-beam with her boots crossed at the ankle as though she'd been there since the founding of the city. Dark hair cut short and practical. A smuggler's coat with too many pockets, each pocket visibly holding something with an opinion. A knife resting on her knee in the manner of furniture rather than threat.

"You've been sitting in a ruin in the dark," Ansel said, retrieving the lantern, "waiting for me."

"Two nights." She shrugged. "I figured you'd come eventually. You're methodical. You sold a luck-knot to Marta Ostrey on Tensday and didn't charge her full price, which told me you had a conscience, and you checked your shadow twice on Cordwainer Street, which told me you had a problem." She nodded at the map, visible where it jutted from Ansel's coat. "Same problem, I'm hoping."

"And you are?"

"Sefa." She said it plainly, no title, no cell-name theatrics. "And before you ask the next question—yes, I know what you are. No, I don't care. And yes, the map is mine. Ours. My cell's. It was being carried to us when your courier friend got himself killed carrying it."

Ansel kept very still. "That courier died in my cellar."

"I know. I'm sorry. He was ours too. His name was Pell." Something crossed her face—quick, real, gone. "He was better at running than at hiding, which in Vell is the wrong order to be good at things."

"The map was addressed to you."

"In cipher, yes. Guild cipher." Sefa leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here's my problem, trinket-seller. That cipher's supposed to be extinct. Every hand that wrote it was supposed to be ash or gone to ground twelve years ago. So either my dead friend Pell copied it from somewhere, or somebody inside the old order sent it, and either way—" she tapped her own temple— "I can't read half of it. But you looked at that dial-socket like you were greeting an old aunt. So."

"So you let me find it. Follow it. Open it."

"I let you do the dangerous part first, yes." She smiled without much apology. "If there were traps, I wanted them eating you, not me."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in twelve years."

"I find honesty works better down here. Lies need witnesses, and it's very empty underground." She unfolded herself from the beam and stood, and up close she was shorter than her voice suggested, wiry, with the permanent faint squint of someone who spent her life judging distances and exits. "Look. I'll lay it out plain, because you strike me as a person who's tired of not being spoken to plainly. There's a war coming whether anybody wants it or not. The Ministry's found out the Choir exists—I don't know how, but their Inquisitors have stopped sleeping, which is how you can tell. My cell wants what's in that vault before the Ministry gets it. And the map says the door only opens for guild-trained hands. Which makes you"—she spread her palms—"the most valuable person in Vell, standing in a ruined chapel holding a box of other people's mail."

"I'm a trinket-seller."

"You're a terrible trinket-seller. Your luck-knots actually work. I bought one in the Nightmarket on a whim and my worst enemy slipped on ice the same afternoon. Coincidence, maybe. But I've been in trade a long time, and I know product."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above, Vell's heartbeat went on.

"Here's my plain," Ansel said finally. "I have spent twelve years being nobody, and it has cost me everything except my life, and I was mostly at peace with that arrangement. Then a man bled out on my floor knowing my real name, and an Inquisitor smiled at me across my own counter, and now I'm in a hole under the city talking to a woman who admits she wanted traps to eat me. I don't want a war. I don't want a cause. I want to go home, sleep, and open my stall tomorrow and sell a blessed hairpin to someone who needs it."

Sefa considered this. "And the fact that you haven't left yet?"

Ansel looked down at the lead box. At the luck-knot with its little price tag. At the papers, folded in a hand that made their chest ache with recognition they weren't ready to examine.

"The fact that I haven't left yet," Ansel said quietly, "is none of your business."

"It's entirely my business," Sefa said, but gently, for her. "Because it's mine too."

They regarded each other across the ruined chapel—two liars, each measuring the other's lies and finding, annoyingly, that they fit alongside their own.

"Terms," Ansel said. "I read what's mine to read. I decide what I share. If at any point your cell points a weapon at me, our arrangement ends at the speed of running."

"Harsh terms."

"I'm told I haggle well."

Something warmed in Sefa's face—the first unguarded thing she'd shown. "Doss the fence told me that, actually. Said you once talked him down to a third on a crate of buttons and made him thank you for it." She stuck out a hand, callused, honest-looking. "All right, trinket-seller. Partners. Provisionally. With extreme prejudice."

Ansel shook it. Her grip was strong and her palm was rough and it occurred to Ansel, with a small unwelcome pang, that this was the first handshake they'd exchanged in twelve years that wasn't a transaction.

Then Sefa's eyes flicked past Ansel's shoulder to the chapel entrance, and every soft thing in her face closed like a shop shutter.

"Don't move," she breathed. "Don't look. Grey coats?"

Ansel, who had spent twelve years learning to see without looking, let their gaze drift sideways along the lantern light. Two figures at the broken doorway. Ministry grey. One held a shuttered lamp of official make; the other held something that caught the light with a slow, oily gleam.

Not a lamp.

"Charm-sniffer," Sefa whispered. "They're sweeping for residue. And you—" her eyes cut to Ansel, sharp with accusation and something almost like pity— "you've been touching guild-work all night."

The sniffer began, very softly, to hum.

"Well," said Ansel, whose entire life had trained them for exactly one kind of moment and who had sworn off this kind forever, "I suppose running is back on the table."

Sefa was already moving. "Try to keep up, partner."
