## 6. Twelve Years Invisible

Sefa had a theory about running: you could tell everything about a person from how they did it.

The rich ran loudly, complaining. The guilty ran fast and left everyone behind. But Ansel Dunmore—charm-seller, trinket-monger, the most carefully boring person in Vell—ran like someone who had practiced being forgotten until their feet forgot how to be noticed. Quiet steps. No wasted motion. They took corners the way water takes corners, without argument.

Sefa followed, ducking under a laundry line, vaulting a stack of empty fish crates, and thought: *Oh, you're good.*

Not good enough to fool her, of course. Nobody was good enough to fool her. But good.

They came out through the tanner's yard—the smell alone would have covered any scent the Ministry dogs might follow—and dropped down into the drainage cut behind Cordwainer Street, where the city kept its oldest secrets and its worst smells in equal measure. Sefa pressed her back against the wet brick and listened.

The charm-sniffer's hum faded somewhere above them, moving east with its handler, patient as tide.

Ansel crouched beside her, breathing hard, holding a luck-knot they'd apparently refused to drop even while fleeing for their life. Sefa stared at it.

"You saved *that*," she said, "and not, say, your dignity."

"It's paid for," Ansel said. "Mostly."

"You've been carrying guild-work through the Nightmarket for twelve years."

"I've been carrying *knots* through the Nightmarket for twelve years." Ansel's jaw did something complicated. "Knots are just knots."

"Sure. And I'm just a fishmonger." Sefa let that sit. "You didn't run when the sniffer started humming. You know what I mean? Most people run at the hum. You waited. You counted. You knew exactly how long we had before the handler heard it properly. That's not a knot-seller's instinct, partner. That's training."

Above them, boots crossed a grate. Both of them went still, the old shared language of the hunted, and Sefa felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't let loosen in years.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told Ansel yet, the thing she'd been circling all night like a moth around a lamp she wasn't sure she wanted lit: she hadn't found Ansel by accident either.

* * *

The safehouse was a cooper's attic above a shuttered workshop on Vine Lane, reachable only by a ladder that groaned like a widower. Sefa went up first, out of habit, and stood aside as Ansel climbed into the lamplight—thin, soot-smudged, clutching their ridiculous luck-knot, looking around the room with the expression of a cat determining whether this new territory was worth the indignity of staying.

The room held three bedrolls, a cold stove, a map table, and Tam.

Tam was seventeen, all elbows and conviction, and he was supposed to be asleep. He sat up in his bedroll with his hair sticking out sideways and looked at Ansel the way pilgrims look at relics.

"You're real," Tam said.

"I'm very tired," Ansel said.

"He means—" Sefa set the lamp down. "He means you're the first guild-trained hand either of us has ever met. Alive. Out loud."

"I'm not guild-trained. I told you. My master died when I was fourteen. I make knots."

"You deciphered a dead-drop cipher in the dark, by candlelight, in under an hour," Sefa said. "A cipher my whole cell couldn't crack in two months. Sit down, Ansel. Please. There's tea. It's terrible. It's the best we have."

Ansel sat. That was the first surprise. Sefa had prepared arguments, appeals, a whole little speech she'd been polishing since the bell tower. She didn't need any of it. Ansel Dunmore was so starved for a table to sit at that they sat at the first one offered, and something about that made Sefa want to be careful, the way you're careful with a bird that's flown into your window and is sitting stunned on the sill, blinking.

She poured the terrible tea.

"My brother's name was Dario," she said.

Ansel looked up.

"You don't have to hear this. But you're going to ask me sooner or later why a smuggler who deals in salt and silk knows guild cipher, and I'd rather you heard it from me than pieced it together from the way I look at Ministry clerks."

Tam had gone quiet in his corner. He'd heard this story before. He still went quiet every time, which Sefa privately thought was the truest measure of him—not the bravado, not the pamphlets he copied by hand, but the fact that he never got tired of listening to grief that wasn't his.

"Dario was four years older than me. He had hands like our mother—he could fix anything, weave anything, mend nets so fine the fish swam in and apologized." She smiled at the old joke and felt it land wrong, the way old jokes do when the person they were built for is gone. "When the Purge came, he was nineteen. He wasn't a Charmwright. He'd never seen a spell in his life. But he had a friend who was apprenticed to a lantern-wright, and when the Inquisitors came down Fishgut Row with their lists, Dario stood in the doorway of the workshop and said the boy had gone out the back. Which was true. What was also true was that the Inquisitors already knew it, because the boy's own master had given them the name, and they were watching the back door too."

The stove ticked in the silence.

"They took Dario instead. As an accomplice. As an example. There was a trial that lasted less time than it takes to gut a herring, and then there was a posting on the Customs House wall with forty names on it, and his was spelled wrong." Her voice stayed level. She'd had twelve years of practice keeping it level. "Spelled *wrong*, Ansel. Like he wasn't even worth getting right."

Ansel's hands had gone still around the cup. Not frozen—still, deliberate, the stillness of someone choosing very carefully where to put their grief.

"The Ministry didn't invent the Purge," Sefa went on. "That's the part nobody says. Oh, they wrote the laws, they lit the fires, they built the pretty new Vell where magic is a word you whisper and charms are things that get children orphaned. But lists need names, and names come from inside. Somebody fed them the guild, street by street, master by master. Somebody who knew the doors and the passwords and which apprentices slept where." She leaned forward. "And now there's a map going around written in guild cipher, pointing at a vault called the Choir, and somebody inside the old order sent it—which means somebody inside the old order is *alive*, and *talking*, and I want to know why."

"And you think I know," Ansel said quietly.

"I think you're the thread I've got. I think whoever sent that courier chose you specifically, which means you matter to whatever's buried under this city. And I think—" she paused, and made herself say the honest thing, because lies between partners rot faster than anything, "—I think you've been invisible for twelve years and it's eating you alive. I watched you haggle tonight. You're *good* at small. You're excellent at small. And every time you sold one of those knots you looked like a man signing his own name in water."

"That's dramatic."

"That's Tuesday," Sefa said. "I've been watching you for three weeks."

Ansel blinked. "Three—"

"We found you the same night the courier did, more or less. We've been trying to work up the nerve to approach you ever since. Then a bleeding man saves us the trouble by dying in your cellar." She shrugged. "The Choir provides."

"The Choir is a story."

"The Choir is a song," Tam burst out from his corner, unable to contain himself a moment longer, "sealed under Vell before the Ministry existed, half creation and half unmaking, and if it's real and we can reach it, we can break them, Ansel. All of them. Every law they enforce with fear and every sniffer and every list. One song and nobody ever gets posted on a wall again with their name spelled wrong."

"TAM," Sefa said.

"What? It's true!"

"It's true and it's not a recruitment speech, it's a *hope*, and hopes get people killed when you wave them around like banners." But she didn't actually disagree with him, and she could see Ansel see that she didn't disagree, and she sighed and set down her cup. "Here it is, then. Plainly. No banners."

She told Ansel about the cell. Eleven people, no more—salt-runners and scribes and one extremely reformed pickpocket named Olla who could open anything except her heart, ha ha, Tam's joke, Sefa allowed it once a year. They moved goods and moved people and moved paper, and for six years they had chased rumors of the Choir the way sailors chase the green flash at sunset: half-believing, wholly needing to.

"We don't want to *use* the song," she said. "Whatever the hardliners in other cells whisper—we're not going to loose annihilation on Vell to spite the Ministry. But a living song, sealed by the founders themselves? A song that can wake magic or end it? That's leverage. That's the one thing the Ministry fears enough to burn a district over, which means it's the one thing that could make them *negotiate*. Or fall. Either way, the lists stop. The sniffers stop. Nobody else stands in a doorway for somebody else's crime."

She watched Ansel across the lamp. Watched the war behind their face—the one she recognized, because she'd fought it herself, the night Olla had first said *you could belong to something* and Sefa had answered *I belong to myself, thanks, and myself says go to hell.*

"You gave everything up to survive," Sefa said softly. "I know how that works. I did it too. You bury the name, the trade, the teachers, the whole bright stupid world, and you tell yourself it's peace, living small. And some nights it almost is. And then some nights you're selling a luck-knot to a woman whose husband drowned, and you feel the charm answer you, just a little, just a warmth in your fingers, and you have to pretend you didn't feel it, and you walk home past the Customs House wall where the names used to be, and you think: *twelve years. Twelve years of nothing, so that nothing would happen to me.*"

Ansel's eyes were bright and furious. "You don't know what I—"

"No," Sefa agreed. "I don't. Tell me I'm wrong, then."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones and faded. Ansel looked down at the luck-knot in their lap, turning it over and over, and Sefa saw their thumb find the place where the weaving changed pattern—the guild mark hidden in plain sight, the thing they'd sworn was just a knot—and saw them stop pretending, finally, even to themselves.

"I felt it tonight," Ansel said, barely audible. "At the stall. When the clerk took the protection coin. I wanted to hex his inkwell shut. Just—a flicker. Twelve years and it's still *there*, waiting, like a dog that doesn't understand it's been put out." They laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "You're offering me a purpose."

"I'm offering you a family," Sefa said. "The purpose comes free with it."

"Families get people killed. You just told me so yourself. Your brother—"

"My brother died standing in a doorway for a friend," Sefa said. "I can't imagine a better use of a life, honestly. But I take your point. So here are your terms, and I'll not sweeten them. Come with me tomorrow. There's a place on the map—a bathhouse, off Lantern Court, marked in cipher only a guild apprentice could read. Whoever sent your courier will be there, or was, or left word. You walk in, you meet them, you ask your questions, and if you want to walk away after—if you want your small gray life back, knots and protection coin and all—then you walk away, and my cell never bothers you again. On my name."

"And if I don't want to walk away?"

Sefa smiled, and hated how much hope was in it. "Then welcome home, partner. Terrible tea every morning. Tam's opinions every evening. Eleven people who'll stand in doorways for you."

Ansel looked at the luck-knot a long moment. Then, deliberately, they tucked it away in their coat—kept, not discarded—and Sefa understood that something had been decided, though the words hadn't come yet.

"One condition," Ansel said. "If this goes badly. If anyone else ends up on a wall with their name spelled wrong because of me—I walk, and none of you follow."

"Deal," said Sefa, who fully intended to break that promise, and who saw by the flicker in Ansel's eyes that Ansel knew it, and that they accepted the deal anyway, which was maybe the most honest contract either of them had ever signed.

* * *

They slept in shifts. Sefa took first watch by the window, listening to Vine Lane breathe, and let herself do the dangerous arithmetic she usually forbade: eleven plus one. Twelve. A proper number. A choir's number, Tam would say, insufferably.

Near dawn, a knock came at the trapdoor—three slow, one fast, Olla's knock—and Olla's head appeared through the hatch, gray-faced, breathing like she'd run the whole length of Lantern Court.

"Sefa." She hauled herself up, and Sefa knew before she spoke, the way you know weather. "The bathhouse. Someone's been there ahead of us. Door's been opened from the inside."

Olla caught her breath, and her eyes went to Ansel, sleeping curled around a luck-knot like a child around a doll.

"And there's a light burning down there," Olla said. "Somebody's *home*."
