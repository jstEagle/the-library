## 7. The Guild That Wasn't Dead

The map's second clue was a riddle written in steam.

*Where Vell washed its sins and paid by the hour,* the cipher read, *the water remembers colder.* Beneath it, a small glyph I knew intimately: the sign for *boiler*, which Master Wren had once used to mean *heart*, because he claimed the two were interchangeable in any city worth living in.

I stood in the Rainwrights' Quarter at dusk, collar up, feeling like an idiot. The bathhouse on Tanner's Lane had been shuttered since before the Purge—twelve years of boarded windows and pigeon sovereignty. Its sign, a fat brass fish leaping over a tub, had gone green with age. Nobody went to bathhouses anymore that couldn't afford the Ministry's sanctioned steam-halls uptown, all marble and surveillance.

Which was, I supposed, exactly the point.

*The water remembers colder.* I pressed my palm against the service door at the alley's side, the one delivery boys had used when the bathhouse took in ice from the winter carts. Cold. But beneath the cold, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well—

Warmth. A thread of it, humming through the iron hinge. A warding, old-style, guild-made, keyed not to repel but to *recognize*. I hadn't felt another Charmwright's work since I was seventeen years old, and my whole body reacted like a dog hearing its name after a decade of being called nothing.

"Stupid," I told myself quietly. "This is how people die."

My hand knocked anyway. Three times, then twice—the old guild knock, the one Wren had drilled into me until it lived in my knuckles instead of my head.

Silence. Long enough that I'd turned to leave.

Then bolts. Four of them, drawn back in sequence, and the door opened six inches on a chain, revealing one eye, brown and suspicious, set in a face roughly the shape and temperament of a walnut.

"What do you want," said the eye.

"Ice from the winter carts," I said. "For the boiler."

The eye did not blink for an uncomfortably long time. Then the door shut, the chain rattled, and it opened all the way onto a small round woman holding a candle stub in one fist and, now visible, a very large cleaver in the other.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm sorry?"

"The courier was three weeks ago. We assumed you'd died, or worse, gotten sensible." She stepped back and jerked her chin toward the dark interior. "In or out, boy. The draft is ruining my temper, and my temper has nowhere left to live."

Everything in me said *out*. Everything in me said *run home, bar the door, melt the map, go back to selling luck-knots to frightened fishwives until your heart gives out at seventy*. But the courier had known my true name. The map bore guild cipher. And some starved thing in my chest, twelve years thin, walked forward into the dark before the rest of me could vote.

* * *

Inside, the bathhouse was a ruin wearing a disguise. The great sunken pools were dry, floored over with planks, and on those planks someone had built a life: a stove, shelves of jars, cots with actual blankets, a rope strung with drying herbs. Three lamps burned behind colored glass so their light wouldn't leak through the boards. It smelled of rosemary, lamp oil, and underneath it all, faintly, of chalk—the smell of my childhood, of spellwork practiced until midnight, of every lesson I'd ever survived.

The walnut-faced woman locked four bolts behind us. Two more figures waited by the stove. An old man, tall and folded like a compass, mending a sock with the intense concentration of someone avoiding conversation. And a third figure, seated, hands wrapped around a mug, who stood up when the lamplight reached them—

And the floor of the world tilted.

I knew the stoop of those shoulders. I knew the way he pushed his sleeves up his forearms before doing anything, even standing, even grieving. I had watched that door in my mind for twelve years, waiting for it to open, rehearsing everything I would say, and in every version of that reunion I had been dignified, or furious, or magnificently indifferent.

What actually came out of me was: "You're dead."

Master Wren looked older. His beard had gone from black to the color of wet ash, and there were new lines around his eyes, deep ones, the kind carved by things a person decides not to talk about. But his voice, when it came, was exactly the same—warm, wry, infuriatingly gentle.

"Not for lack of trying," he said. "Hello, Ansel."

"You're *dead*." My voice cracked upward, embarrassingly young. "I went to the ash-house. There was a record. *Wren Colvane, Charmwright, executed by order of the Ministry, ninth day of Frostmonth.* I read it forty times. I mourned you. I—" I heard my own voice climbing and made myself stop, made myself breathe, because Inquisitors could hear grief through walls, probably, and also because the alternative to stopping was screaming, and I had done enough screaming for one lifetime in the weeks after the Purge.

The tall man set down his sock. "Perhaps we should sit," he offered mildly.

"We should absolutely not sit," I said. "Who are you people? Who are *you*?" This to Wren, jabbing a finger, which felt childish and which I did anyway. "Twelve years. Twelve years, and you let me believe you were dead. You let me believe *everyone* was dead. Do you know what that did to me? Do you know what I—" I stopped again. Swallowed. "I sold trinkets. I sold *luck-knots*. I pretended the guild never existed. I unlearned my own name. And you were here. In a *bathhouse*. With *herbs*."

"Rosemary keeps well," said the walnut woman, unhelpfully.

"Ansel." Wren took one step toward me and stopped, wisely, when I flinched. "Sit down. Please. Let me explain, and then hate me properly, with all the facts."

"That's not how hatred works."

"It's how *good* hatred works," he said. "Anyone can hate on incomplete information. Craftsmanship, Ansel. Always craftsmanship."

God help me, twelve years hadn't killed the part of me that wanted to laugh at him. I sat down on a cot because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me, and the walnut woman pressed a mug of something hot into my hands—chamomile, with too much honey, exactly the way Wren used to make it—and I hated that too, and drank all of it.

* * *

Their names were Maren Ostwick, the walnut woman, once the guild's mistress of wards; Corvin Hale, the tall man, who had taught counter-charms and apparently darning; and Wren, who had been, before the Purge, the youngest of the guild's elders, and who had spent the twelve years since being the oldest survivor of everything.

They had hidden, they told me, because hiding worked. Nine others had tried resistance, or flight, or simply honesty, and the Ministry had archives full of what happened next. The bathhouse had belonged to Maren's late sister. The wards were hers, rebuilt layer over layer until the building was less a place than a held breath.

"But the courier," I said. "The dying man in my cellar. He knew my name. He gave me the map. That was you."

Wren's hands went still around his mug. "Yes."

"Why? Why now? Why me?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "No—why *me* is obvious, isn't it? Because I'm the last apprentice. Because only guild-trained hands can read the cipher. Because I'm useful." The word tasted like copper. "You didn't come back for me, Wren. You didn't send anyone, in twelve years—not a letter, not a sign, not one blessed luck-knot to tell me you were alive. But the moment you needed a key turned, suddenly I exist again."

"Ansel—"

"Was I even a person to you? Or just an apprentice? Just a tool you left in storage?"

The silence stretched. Corvin looked at his sock. Maren looked at the wall with the expression of a woman who had heard this argument coming for three weeks and had sharpened the cleaver in anticipation.

"The night of the Purge," Wren said finally, slowly, "I carried three children out of the guildhall through the coal-chutes. You were one of them. I got you to the tannery district, to a woman named Odile who owed me a debt, and I told you to stay put until dawn. Do you remember?"

I remembered. Of course I remembered. I remembered it every time I locked my shop door.

"When I went back for the fourth child—a girl, Tam, seven years old—the Inquisitors had the street. I watched them take her out of the drain pipe where she was hiding. I watched, Ansel, because if I had moved, they would have taken me too, and then no one would have come back for *you*." His voice didn't break. It did something worse—it went flat and careful, like a man walking on river ice. "So I stayed hidden. And then staying hidden became the work itself. Every year, coming back meant risking the three of us plus you, and every year the risk stayed the same and my courage got smaller. That's the truth. Not the noble version. The truth."

"You could have sent a *sign*."

"A sign found is a trail followed." He shook his head. "I told myself that for twelve years. Some nights I even believed it." He looked at me then, straight on, and I saw that the new lines around his eyes had been carved by precisely this conversation, rehearsed with a dead apprentice for over a decade. "I'm sorry, Ansel. Not for surviving—for leaving you alone inside your survival. Those are different crimes, and I committed both, but only one of them shames me."

I stared into my empty mug. The chamomile sweetness still sat on my tongue, and I hated it, and I wanted another cup, and none of these things could coexist politely so I just sat there vibrating.

"And the map," I said at last. "The Choir. The courier bleeding out on my floor saying *the Choir remembers*. What am I to you, Wren? What did you send me?"

Maren and Corvin exchanged a look—the particular look of people who had agreed to let someone else deliver bad news and were now checking whether the agreement still held.

Wren set down his mug. "You remember what the founders built," he said. "Before the Ministry, before the guild even had a name. Tell me what you were taught."

"A vault," I said. "A story. Elders' tales to make apprentices behave. A buried song that could wake magic or end it, sealed away because—because no one could carry it whole." I frowned. "It was a fable. Like the boiler-heart. Like everything."

"It was never a fable." Wren leaned forward, lamplight moving on his ash-grey beard. "The founders sealed a living song under this city, Ansel. Half creation, half annihilation, and neither half tame. The guild's first and greatest work wasn't making charms. It was keeping that thing asleep. Every generation added locks. Every generation swore the oath. And twelve years ago, when the Ministry purged us, they weren't hunting charm-sellers and luck-weavers." His eyes were terrible and steady. "They were hunting the lockkeepers. Someone told them the Choir was real. Someone inside."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above us, a cart rattled down Tanner's Lane, ordinary as bread.

"So the map," I said, and my mouth had gone dry. "The map leads to the Choir."

"The map leads to the Choir," Wren agreed. "Because the seal is failing—I've felt it shaking loose for two years, like a tooth working free. And because there are others looking for it now, Ansel. Others who aren't old men in a bathhouse. The courier wasn't followed to your door by accident." He paused, and I watched him choose the next words with the care of a man laying explosives. "Someone else has seen the map's route. Someone with boots and badges."

I thought of the clerk at my stall, taking protection coin with soft apologetic hands. I thought of the fishwife's frightened face. I thought of a stranger's blood soaking into my cellar floor, and Ministry boots overhead, and how many days it had been since I'd slept.

"How long," I said, "do we have?"

Wren didn't answer right away. And in that pause—in the way Maren's hand drifted toward the cleaver, and Corvin rose without being asked to check the door bolts—I understood that the answer had already arrived, and that it was standing outside, patient as winter, learning our address.
