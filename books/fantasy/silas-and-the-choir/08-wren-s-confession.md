## 8. Wren's Confession

The bathhouse smelled of cold copper and old steam, and Wren Coldharbour—once Master of the Second Chair, once a dead man by official decree—was pouring tea for the apprentice he had abandoned twelve years ago.

The tea was terrible. He had made it himself, which was the problem. His hands had never been good at anything but song.

Ansel sat across the drained soaking pool with the map unrolled between them and did not touch the cup. Twelve years, and the first thing Wren learned about his last apprentice was that they had grown a face like a locked door. Good. That would keep them alive longer than talent ever had.

"You knew the courier," Ansel said.

"I wrote to him." Wren folded his hands so they wouldn't shake. "His name was Tam Vetch. He carried fish on Tuesdays and secrets the other six days. He died well, if it helps."

"It doesn't."

"No," Wren admitted. "It rarely does."

* * *

He had rehearsed this confession for twelve years, in the way that a man rehearses an apology to someone who may already be dead. In the bathhouse years—the long grey years of pretending to be a caretaker named Odo Pell, who swept tiles and fixed boilers and never once hummed—he had composed perhaps four hundred versions. In some of them Ansel wept. In some, Ansel struck him. In one memorable draft, Ansel forgave him immediately, and Wren had discarded that version as cowardice even in imagination.

None of the drafts had prepared him for Ansel simply looking at him, patient and tired, the way one looks at weather.

"Start with the Choir," Ansel said. "You sent me a map. I'd like to know what I'm carrying."

So Wren told the story properly, from the beginning, because the beginning was the only honest place left to stand.

"The guild was not founded to make charms," he said. "That's what we let people believe, and eventually what we let ourselves believe. Luck-knots. Weather-bells. Little kindnesses stitched into thread. But the founders—there were nine of them, in the years before Vell had walls—they found something under the city. Or it found them. They called it Silas."

"Silas." Ansel tested the name. "The map says 'the Choir.' I thought it meant the singers."

"It means both. The vault is shaped like a throat, and what sleeps inside it sings. We never decided whether we found it or dug down to bury it. The distinction stopped mattering early." Wren turned his cup, watching the leaves settle. "Silas is a living song, child. Half of it is creation—you could hold its first movement in your chest and grow wheat through stone floors, heal wounds that should have killed, wake the dead if you were foolish enough to ask politely. And the other half—" He paused. There was no gentle way to say it, and he had promised himself honesty, even now. Especially now. "The other half unmakes. Not violently. It doesn't burn things. It simply persuades them they were never there. A street. A family. A law of nature. You sing the second half over something and the world agrees, afterward, that it had always been empty."

Ansel's face did something very small and very controlled. "And the founders sealed it."

"They tried to split it first. Nine of them, each carrying half the burden, thinking distance would dilute it. Four of them died within a year—not killed, just *ended*, worn through like cloth. So the remaining five built the vault, wove the seal, and set a door that tests whoever approaches. And then they spent three generations teaching the guild one rule above all others: the whole song must never be sung. Not by us. Not by anyone. Because no single voice can bear its whole weight, and no two voices can agree on where to stop."

The boiler ticked in the wall. Somewhere above them, Vell went about its morning, buying bread, telling lies, unaware of how thin the floor beneath it really was.

"And the Ministry?" Ansel asked. "The Purge? Twelve years ago they burned the guild out of existence. Where does that sit in your careful history?"

And here it was. Here was the part Wren had never managed to rehearse without his voice failing.

* * *

"We fed them the names," he said.

The words came out flat, and he was grateful for that. Flatness was all the dignity available.

"There were seven elders left when the Ministry began asking questions—Voss's predecessors, men and women who'd noticed that guild-trained children could do things Ministry-trained ones couldn't, and found that intolerable. We could have run. We could have scattered, gone deep, done what we're doing now but everywhere. Instead the six of us who survived the first arrests sat in a room very much like this one and made a bargain." He laughed, and hated the sound. "A *bargain*. As though horror respects paperwork. We gave the Ministry names—but only names of guild members who already knew too much about the Choir. The ones whose curiosity was dangerous. The ones who'd started asking how to open the vault instead of how to guard it. We told ourselves we were pruning the tree to save the roots."

"And the ones who didn't know anything?" Ansel asked quietly. "The luck-knot makers. The charm-sellers. The apprentices."

Wren looked at his hands. They were shaking after all.

"Some of those names went on the list too," he said. "To make the numbers credible. To make our cooperation look sincere. A betrayal offered in halves is refused; a betrayal offered whole is accepted, and the betrayer keeps his position, and the position is the only thing standing between the Ministry and the vault. That was the arithmetic. Seven elders, one vault, and a list of ninety-one names. We counted the vault as worth more than the people. I have been doing that sum every day for twelve years and it has never once come out even."

Silence. The bad tea cooled between them.

"My master," Ansel said at last. "Maren Ostrey. She taught me knotwork. She used to give me honey-cakes on festival days and pretend she'd baked them herself when everyone knew she bought them from the widow on Cordwainer Lane." Their voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "Was her name on your list?"

"Yes."

"Did you put it there?"

"I wrote the list," Wren said. "All of it. Every name in my own hand, because I insisted—if it was to be done, it should be done by the eldest, so the sin would pool somewhere accountable. The others agreed. It was the last time any of them agreed to anything on my recommendation." He met Ansel's eyes, finally, because flinching now would be theft—stealing the easier version of this moment for himself. "I signed Maren Ostrey to her death to protect a door. And then, when the arrests came faster than we planned, when the Ministry took people off our list and *added* their own, I ran. I let them believe I was dead, and I let you believe it too, and I did not come back for you. I told myself a fourteen-year-old apprentice with minor talent and no knowledge of the vault was safer invisible than attached to me. That was true. It was also convenient. Both things can be true. That's the filthiest thing I've learned in twelve years."

Ansel stood. Walked to the edge of the dry pool. Stood looking into it as though expecting water, or answers, or at least a drain deep enough to crawl into.

"You could have sent word," they said. "One letter. One sign. Anything."

"A letter gets intercepted. A sign gets noticed. And every notice risked leading the Ministry back to the bathhouse, back to the vault, back to you." He heard how it sounded. He had heard how it sounded for twelve years. "But yes. If I'm honest—and I swore I'd be honest today, since it's the only payment I can offer—I was also a coward. Grief makes excellent architecture for hiding in. I built a whole life inside mine."

Elder Sarn appeared in the doorway then, old and round and smelling faintly of the disinfectant she used to keep up her cover as a bathhouse attendant, and Wren saw her take in the temperature of the room and wisely say nothing except, "Boats are ready. Tide turns at dusk."

"Boats?" Ansel said.

"The approach to the Choir runs under the harbor," Wren said. "The founders weren't fools. They put the door where only the desperate or the drowned would go looking." He rose, joints cracking, an old man's body around an old man's guilt. "Ansel. Before you decide anything—before you decide whether you hate me, whether you help us, whether you walk out of here and sell my location to the Ministry for a fresh start—I need you to understand why the map exists at all. Why I spent a courier's life getting it to you."

"Because you need an apprentice's hands," Ansel said. "Sarn said as much. Only guild-trained hands survive the approach."

"That's the reason I gave Sarn. It's even partly true." Wren took a breath. "But the real reason is that the seal is failing. It has been failing for years—the song leaks, small tremors, things going briefly wrong in the lower quarters, luck bending in ways that don't average out. And when it fails completely, Silas wakes on its own, unsung, unguided, with no voice to shape it. Creation and annihilation together, loose under Vell, choosing its own expression. The founders' nightmare wasn't that someone would steal the song. It was that someday no one would be left who remembered how to sing it *quietly*."

"So you want me to open the vault."

"I want you to have the choice," Wren said. "It's the only thing I ever gave you freely. Everything else—your training, your exile, your grief—I chose for you. This time, the choice is yours: the elders want it opened and resealed under new terms. The rebels want it opened and taken. The Ministry wants it opened and emptied. And I—" His voice did fail here, at last, right on schedule. "I want to hand the burden to someone younger, which is either wisdom or the final and worst act of a selfish old man, and I genuinely no longer know which."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Then they rolled up the map with precise, angry care, and tucked it inside their coat, and said:

"I need to think. Don't follow me."

"Ansel—"

"*Don't follow me.*"

They walked out through the steam-room door, and Wren sat back down beside his untouched tea, and Elder Sarn came and stood beside him and said nothing, because there was nothing either of them could say that wasn't already on a list somewhere, in someone's handwriting, waiting to be paid for.

* * *

He gave Ansel an hour. Then guilt overrode obedience, as it always had, and Wren followed the route he knew Ansel would take—up through the tile-yard, toward the street—telling himself he only wanted to watch from a distance, to confirm the child was safe, to commit no interference whatsoever. He had become expert at these fictions. They fit him like old gloves.

He reached the yard in time to see Ansel stop dead in the lane.

A black carriage stood at the mouth of the alley, Ministry grey trim, horses too well-fed for the neighborhood. And leaning against its door with the unhurried ease of a person who has never once needed to hurry stood a woman in a clerk's plain coat, holding something up to the light.

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, through twelve years of careful burial.

It was a luck-knot. Guild-tied. Ansel's knotwork—he'd know those hands anywhere; he'd taught them.

"Inquisitor," Ansel was saying, loud enough to carry across the yard, their voice pitched to perfect market-day blandness. "Can I help you?"

Wren pressed himself behind the tile stacks, heart hammering, and understood with terrible clarity that the hourglass had been running far longer than any of them had guessed.

The woman smiled at Ansel, courteous as a drawn blade, and held up the knot.

"I'm hoping," she said, "you can tell me where this was made."
