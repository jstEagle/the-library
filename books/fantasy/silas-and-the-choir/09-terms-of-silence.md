## 9. Terms of Silence

The bathhouse smelled of cold copper and old steam, the way a room smells when it has spent years remembering warmth and given up pretending.

Ansel stood in the tiled hall beneath the great drained cistern-pool, three elders arranged before them like a tribunal that had rehearsed its severity in a mirror. There was Maren Ostwick, who had once taught Countercharm to apprentices too clever for their own good; there was old Pellam, whose hands shook now but whose eyes hadn't shaken since the Purge; and there was Wren.

Wren, who was supposed to be dead. Wren, who had been dead for twelve years as far as Ansel's grief was concerned, and who had apparently found death inconvenient and moved out without leaving a forwarding address.

"You could have said something," Ansel said. "A letter. A knock on the door. A *sign*. I sold luck-knots to fishwives, Master. I ate eel from a cart. Eel. You let me eat eel."

"We couldn't risk—" Wren began.

"Twelve years." Ansel's voice cracked on the number, which annoyed them more than anything else in this entire wretched evening. "I lit a candle for you every year on Founding Day. Do you know what that costs in Vell? Candle-wax is robbery."

Maren cleared her throat with the delicacy of someone interrupting a funeral to discuss rent. "If the family reunion could wait—"

"It is not a reunion. Reunions have cake."

"—the matter of the Choir," she finished, with the iron patience of a woman who had survived both a purge and, evidently, Ansel's childhood.

The map lay open on a folding table between them, weighted at the corners with teacups because even fugitive guild elders refused to drink from anything else. The cipher glowed faintly under the lamp—guild-work, true guild-work, the first Ansel had seen outside their own memory in over a decade—and beneath it, the route wound down through the underways to a door drawn in careful, reverent strokes.

"The Choir," Pellam said. "You've read the histories. Or you would have, if we'd finished teaching you."

"I read the parts you didn't burn," Ansel said. "Which were mostly recipes."

"Then hear the rest." Pellam leaned forward. "The founders sealed Silas—the song—in that vault because no one could carry it whole. Half of it makes. Half of it unmakes. It is not a weapon, child. It is a *voice*, and everything that hears it must decide what to do with what it's told. The Ministry heard it once, through a scrying-glass, and built an empire on the echo."

"And you want me to open the door."

"We want you to survive the approach," Wren said quietly. "The corridor to the Choir is warded in the old way. Guild-trained hands, guild-trained mind. Anyone else who walks it—" He made a small gesture, opening his fingers like a bird released, or a mind emptied. "Sefa's people tried, two winters ago. We stopped them before they reached the third threshold. What came back wasn't entirely them."

Ansel looked at the map. At the door. At the three old people who had let the world believe them ash while one apprentice grew up alone in a city that hated what they were.

"No," they said.

Silence. The kind with weight to it, settling into the tiles.

"Ansel," Wren started.

"No." Ansel held up a hand. "Let me be clear, because I've had twelve years to practice being clear to people who don't listen. You sent a dying courier to my cellar. You let me think the guild was dust so I'd grieve properly and stay quiet. And now that the Ministry and the rebels are both circling, you want the one person expendable enough to walk your killing corridor and open your impossible door." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm not your key, Master. I'm your *apprentice*. There's a difference. There used to be."

"There is no one else," Maren said simply. "That is not rhetoric. That is arithmetic."

"Then your arithmetic is missing a column," Ansel said, "because I count myself in it, and I decline."

They rolled up the map—*their* map, courier's blood and all—and tucked it inside their coat. Nobody moved to stop them, which was either respect or the certainty that Ansel would come crawling back, and Ansel decided, for the sake of their own dignity, to call it respect.

At the bathhouse door, Wren caught their sleeve. His hand was lighter than Ansel remembered. Everything about him was lighter, worn down to wire and stubbornness.

"The approach will test you," he said. "Not the wards. *You.* If you change your mind—"

"I won't."

"If you do, the bell in the Ashen Quarter rings at dusk. Third pull. Sefa knows the signal."

Ansel pulled free and walked out into the rain, and did not look back, because looking back was how you ended up staying, and staying was how you ended up agreeing, and agreeing was how you ended up dead in a corridor built by people who loved you enough to spend you.

* * *

The rain in Vell came down the way debt collectors did: patiently, everywhere, and with a personal interest in you specifically.

Ansel walked. Not toward home—that was the first rule of being followed, and Ansel had been followed often enough to have rules—but through the shuttered cloth market, past the tanneries, along the canal where the water wore its usual skin of rainbow grease. Their thoughts churned worse than the locks.

*No one else,* Maren had said. But that wasn't true, was it? There was Sefa, who wanted the song as a hammer. There was the Ministry, who wanted it as a crown. There was Voss—

There was a carriage.

It sat at the mouth of Tanner's Row, black lacquer, no crest, two grey horses that stood unnaturally still in the rain as though they'd been instructed that dampness was beneath them. A lantern burned inside. The door stood open.

An invitation. In Vell, invitations from carriages with no crests were called something else.

Ansel considered, briefly, running. Then considered where running would get them—wet, tired, and followed anyway—and walked up the steps instead, chin up, the way you approached any animal that fed on fear.

Inquisitor Halle Voss sat inside like a knife sitting in a drawer: neat, sheathed, and aware of exactly what it was. Grey coat, grey eyes, a cup of tea steaming on the little fold-out desk that Ministry carriages all carried, because bureaucracy never slept and neither, apparently, did its refreshments.

"Master Dunmore," Voss said. "Or should I say Apprentice Dunmore? Sit. You're dripping on my case files, which is fine, they're mostly fiction anyway."

Ansel sat. "I don't know what you're talking about. I sell trinkets."

"You do. Very well, actually—I've had your stall watched for three years. Your luck-knots work. Don't bother denying it; I bought six. My clerk's gambling debts cleared up beautifully." Voss sipped the tea. "I'm not here to arrest you. If I wanted you arrested, you'd already be in a cell learning a new name. I'm here because you visited a dead bathhouse tonight, and because a courier died in your cellar four days ago, and because somewhere under this city is a door that three very old liars need you to open."

The rain drummed on the roof. Ansel kept their face pleasant and empty, the expression of a man thinking about lunch.

"I don't know anything about a door."

"Mm." Voss set down the cup. "Here's what I know, and then you can tell me how wrong I am. Twelve years ago the Charmwrights' Guild was purged—not for the crimes in the proclamations, but because the Ministry's founders learned of the Choir and wanted it buried so deep even memory lost the address. The elders went into hiding. One apprentice was overlooked. Sloppy paperwork; I've read the file. Your name was left off the list by someone who cared whether you lived." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "I've always wondered who."

Something cold slid down Ansel's spine and settled there, making itself comfortable.

"So here are my terms," Voss continued, "and I'll only make them once, because unlike the guild, I respect your time. Lead me to the Choir. Open it if you can—or stand aside while my ward-breakers do it badly and die doing it, their choice, they're volunteers. In exchange: full immunity. New papers. A stipend. The protection coin you pay my clerk every month—you'll never pay it again. You walk away from all of this a free citizen of a city that no longer has a reason to fear you."

"And the song?"

"Goes to the Ministry vaults. Sealed. Studied, perhaps, by people with better safeguards than a bathhouse and three seniors." Voss's voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Or refuse, and I take you in tonight, and I find the Choir anyway—it may take me a year, and the rebels may beat me to it, and when they do, they will sing that thing over the walls of the Ministry and this city burns from the inside out. Your refusal doesn't protect anyone, Master Dunmore. It just decides who holds the knife when it lands."

Ansel looked at the tea. Looked at the rain. Felt the map against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

"You talk about immunity," they said, "like it's a gift. Where I come from, immunity is what they give the plague ship so it stops bothering the harbor."

Voss smiled. It was a real smile, which was the most frightening thing yet—a flash of genuine, tired humor, as though somewhere under the grey coat was a person who had once laughed easily and had filed that away with the rest of the confiscated goods.

"You sound like my mother," Voss said. "She was a charmwright too. Small wonder I know your tells."

And then, before Ansel could assemble a single word around that, Voss tapped the carriage wall twice. The door swung open onto the rain.

"Three days," the Inquisitor said. "Come to the Hall of Records, ask for Clerk Ferrow, and give him the phrase 'the ledger balances.' After that, I stop being courteous, and you'll miss this version of me. Everyone does, eventually."

Ansel stepped down into the wet street. Behind them, unhurried, Voss added:

"Oh—and Apprentice? Tell Wren the Ministry remembers his handwriting. He'll understand."

The door closed. The carriage rolled away into the dark, lantern swaying, and Ansel stood alone in Tanner's Row with the rain coming down and three days suddenly feeling like the width of a hair.

*Tell Wren.*

They turned toward the Ashen Quarter, coat soaked through, heart going like a debtor's knock—and began, for the first time in twelve years, to run.
