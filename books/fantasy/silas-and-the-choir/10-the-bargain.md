## 10. The Bargain

The carriage smelled of beeswax and old leather, which Ansel found obscene. Nothing that hunted people had any right to smell like a grandmother's parlor.

Inquisitor Halle Voss sat across from them with her hands folded in her lap, gray-gloved and patient as a cat outside a mousehole. The carriage swayed through the evening streets of Vell, lamps smearing gold past the windows, and she let the silence stretch just long enough to become uncomfortable before she spoke.

"You lied to me beautifully today," she said. "I want you to know I noticed. The detail about the courier's boots, for instance. Very specific. Liars who invent specifics are my favorite kind."

"I don't know what you—"

"Ansel." She said their name the way one sets down a fragile cup. Not *Master Dunmore*. Not *citizen*. Just the name, bare, like a coin placed on a table. "I have held one of your charms for two days now. A luck-knot, woven with a guild knot-pattern that hasn't been tied in this city since the Purge. I could have you arrested at any hour of any day. Instead I am offering you tea in a very nice carriage." She smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it frightened Ansel more than shouting would have. "So let's not waste each other's time pretending we're strangers."

Ansel's heart was doing something acrobatic behind their ribs. They made themselves breathe. Twelve years of practice: the stall, the smile, the harmless trinkets, the smallness worn like a second skin. *You are nobody. You are a maker of pretty junk. You are tired and slightly dishonest and no threat to anyone.*

But Voss already knew better.

"The courier," Ansel said carefully. "I told you what I know. He was dying. He said strange things. I couldn't make sense of them."

"And the map?"

"What map?"

Voss's smile didn't waver, but something behind her eyes did—a flicker, quick as a fish turning. "There it is," she said softly. "That little tightening around your mouth. You do it when you lie. Did you know? Your master Wren did exactly the same thing."

The name landed like a dropped anvil. Ansel kept their face still through sheer spite. "I haven't heard that name in twelve years."

"No," Voss agreed. "Because the guild rolls list him dead. Along with everyone else whose names were given up." She unfolded her hands, folded them again. "Here is what I propose, and I'll only say it once. Lead me to the Choir. Do that, and you walk away clean. New papers, new district, enough coin to open a proper shop where nobody pays you protection and nobody looks twice. Everything you've spent twelve years pretending to be—you'd actually get to be it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I find it without you, and I find you afterward, and the conversation we have then will be much shorter and considerably less comfortable." She leaned forward slightly. The lamplight caught the gray in her close-cropped hair. "I'm not your enemy, Ansel Dunmore. Whatever you think the Ministry is, whatever stories the Charmwrights told their apprentices—I want the same thing you want. For all of this to be over. For the song to stay buried. I simply intend to be the one holding the shovel."

*Everyone wants to hold the shovel,* Ansel thought. *Nobody asks what's buried or whether it minds.*

They thought of the elders in the shuttered bathhouse. Of Wren, alive, gray and guilt-ridden, saying *you were always the steadiest of my students*. Of Sefa's cell somewhere in the dark, wanting the song as a blade. Of the map against their chest, warm from their own skin.

Three days. That was what they needed. Three days to warn the elders, move them, get ahead of every wolf circling the same door.

"Three days," Ansel said.

Voss raised an eyebrow.

"You want me to lead you anywhere, I need three days. There are—" they reached for plausibility and found it, because grief was plausibility, "—there are things I have to settle first. People I owe. If I vanish tonight, questions get asked by people who ask them loudly. Give me three days and I'll bring you to the door myself."

It was a monstrous gamble and they knew it. Voss studied them for a long moment, and Ansel watched her decide—not whether to believe them, but how much disbelief she could afford.

"Three days," she said at last. "At dusk on the third day, you'll be at the Nightmarket fountain. Alone. If you're not, I will assume the worst about you, and I will act accordingly, and Ansel—" the warmth came back into her voice, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, "—I genuinely hope you don't make me. I've spent my whole career hoping people won't make me. It rarely works out for either of us."

She opened the carriage door herself. Outside, the Nightmarket noise washed in—fried onions, a fiddler, someone arguing cheerfully about eels.

"One more thing," Voss said as Ansel climbed down. "Your luck-knot. It works, incidentally. I wore it for two days and didn't die once. Remarkable record, in my line of work." And she closed the door, and the carriage rolled away into the dark, smelling faintly of beeswax all the way down the street.

* * *

The first day, Ansel did nothing but prepare, and preparing meant lying to everyone they loved, which was a short list and getting shorter.

They closed the stall early, hung the *back tomorrow* sign that they had owned so long the paint had given up on the word *tomorrow*, and went home through streets that suddenly seemed full of watchers. Every clerk in a gray coat. Every pair of boots that echoed too evenly. Paranoia, they told themselves, was just attention with anxiety, and attention had kept them alive for twelve years.

They packed badly. That was the truth of it—they stood in their little rooms above the cellar workshop pulling things off shelves and putting them back, unable to decide what a life looked like when it fit in a bag. In the end they took the map, a change of clothes, their father's whittling knife, and, after a long moment, the chipped clay whistle Master Wren had fired for them when they were nine years old and hopeless at everything except listening.

"You were never hopeless," Wren used to say. "You were just early. All the best listeners are."

Ansel put the whistle in their pocket and hated how much lighter it made them feel.

They slept maybe two hours, in a chair, with the door barred and a tripwire of thread and bells strung across the stairwell—an apprentice trick, old as the guild. Somewhere past midnight they heard a cat set it off and nearly died of fright, which felt about right for the week they were having.

* * *

The second day, they went looking for Sefa, because warning the elders was only half the arithmetic. If the rebels learned the Ministry was moving, the hardliners would rush the vault, and rushing the vault would kill everyone including, possibly, the world. Sefa was the only rebel Ansel had ever met who seemed interested in outcomes other than glorious ones.

Finding her, however, required going back to the Ashen Bell.

The undercity received Ansel the way it always did—with dripping ceilings and the smell of wet stone and old smoke, and that particular hush that wasn't silence at all but the sound of a city holding its breath below its own weight. The melted bell still hung on its rusted bracket where the smugglers' passage met the cistern road, a lumpy gray thing cast from a hundred guild seals, its inscription eaten away. Ansel touched it, as they had before, and felt nothing, which was its own kind of grief.

Sefa was not there. Neither was anyone else, which was worse. The chalk marks on the wall—the smugglers' shifting code—had been scrubbed clean.

"Sefa," Ansel said aloud, to the dripping dark, feeling ridiculous. "Sefa, it's the charm-seller. From the market. We—shared a sausage, once, and mutual suspicion."

Nothing answered except water, counting seconds somewhere in the dark.

They waited an hour. Then another. Then, because waiting in the undercity alone was how one became a story told sadly in taverns, they left a message—chalked in guild cipher on the bell's bracket, a message only two kinds of people could read: guild-trained, or dead guild. *Ministry moves in three days. Vault at risk. Find me at the bathhouse. —A.*

It was a terrible message. It told Sefa everything, gave away the elders' hiding place, trusted a woman they'd known for the length of one sausage. Ansel stared at it for a long time, then added a second line:

*I have no one else to tell.*

Which was the truest sentence they'd written in twelve years, and therefore, obviously, the most dangerous.

* * *

The third day began with hope, which should have been Ansel's first warning.

They left before dawn, taking the long way through the tannery quarter, doubling back twice, watching reflections in shop glass. The city went about its morning around them—bakers, bargemen, a woman beating a rug with the rhythm of someone settling a personal grudge—and Ansel loved it and feared for it in the same breath, because whatever happened next, it would happen underneath this ordinary Tuesday, and the ordinary Tuesdays never got a vote.

The bathhouse stood in a crooked lane behind the old salt exchange, shuttered these twenty years, its tiled dome green with age. To passersby it was a ruin. Ansel knew better: the third drain-tile from the left of the women's entrance opened on a counterweighted stair, and the stair went down and sideways and eventually into a warm, lamp-lit room where three elderly Charmwrights played cards and argued about tea and pretended, badly, that hiding was the same as living.

Ansel came down the stair with words already arranged—*the Ministry knows, we have to move, I bought us three days*—and stopped dead on the last step.

The door to the common room hung open. Not broken. Open, politely, the way a door hangs when someone has left in a hurry or been made to.

Inside, the card table was overturned. One chair lay on its side. A teacup had shattered near the hearth, and the tea had dried to a brown star on the tile. The lamp still burned—someone had trimmed the wick recently, within hours—but the room was empty. The alcove where Elder Maren kept her herb chests gaped open, drawers pulled and emptied. The map cabinet stood ajar.

"Wren?" Ansel's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Maren? Osk?"

Their own footsteps sounded enormous. They checked the sleeping cells, the washroom with its cold copper tubs, the little shrine where the elders kept the guild's few surviving seals. Empty, empty, empty—and then, in the third cell, not quite empty.

A body. Not one of theirs. A young man, rebel-marked by the knotted red cord at his wrist, unconscious rather than dead, breathing shallowly with a bruise blooming along his jaw. Someone had hit him efficiently and left him to be found—or to be missed.

Ansel crouched beside him, and that was when they saw the rest: scorch marks on the corridor wall, low and precise. A ward had been tripped. A guild ward—one of Ansel's own patterns, taught to the elders, laid across the back exit. Which meant whoever took the elders hadn't come through the front at all. They'd come through the tunnels. They'd known the layout.

They'd been told.

The realization arrived whole and cold, the way ice water arrives. *I sent word. I chalked the bell. I wrote 'bathhouse' in cipher on a smugglers' wall.* Cipher that guild-trained people read. Cipher that anyone who'd ever been guild—or hunted the guild—could crack.

Every side moves because of you, said a small clear voice in their head that sounded unpleasantly like Halle Voss. You lit the lamp. Don't be surprised about the moths.

"No," Ansel said aloud, to the empty room, to the voice, to nobody. "No, I—Sefa wouldn't—"

Except Ansel didn't know that. Ansel had known Sefa for one sausage and one dead drop and a handful of hard, honest conversations. Her cell had hardliners; she'd said so herself, rolling her eyes, but eyes roll over real dangers all the time. And the chalk marks at the bell had been scrubbed clean. Scrubbed by friends covering a retreat—or by enemies erasing a trail.

The young man groaned. Ansel shook him, gently, then less gently.

"—don't," he mumbled. "Don't touch the—she said don't touch the old man's hands, they're keyed, we need them keyed—"

Ansel's stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, through the undercity itself. *Keyed.* Guild keys lived in the hands—in callus-memory, in the trained reflexes of fingers that had spent decades learning locks. Wren's hands. They wanted Wren's hands because Wren's hands could open what no map alone could.

"Who took him?" Ansel asked, gripping the man's shoulder. "Was it the red cords? Was it Sefa? *Was she there?*"

The man's eyes fluttered, focused, widened. "You're the charm-seller," he said, and then, with the flat delivery of the concussed: "Oh, she's going to be furious. She told them not to take the old ones. She told them." His face crumpled. "They're taking him to the Toll Gate. Through the cisterns. Tonight, before the tide turns. She stayed behind to argue with Davor and—" he winced, "—Davor doesn't lose arguments. He just stops having them."

Ansel sat back on their heels amid the wreckage of the elders' hidden life, and the guilt settled over them like ash. Three days. They'd traded three days to Voss thinking they were buying safety, and instead they'd rung a dinner bell. The Ministry was coming. The rebels had come already. And Wren—guilty, grieving, impossible Wren—was being carried through the flooded dark by people who intended to use his hands like tools until the vault gave up its song.

*You did this,* said the voice. *With a piece of chalk.*

"Yes," Ansel answered it, out loud, because the room was empty and honesty was free here. "I did."

Then they got up, because sitting in ruins was a luxury for people with more time.

They bound the young rebel's ribs with strips of curtain, left water where he'd find it, and stood a moment in the doorway of the common room, looking at the spilled tea drying on the tiles. Twenty years the elders had hidden here. Cards and arguments and tea. It had looked so much like a life.

Ansel blew out the lamp, which felt like closing a book mid-chapter, and went up the stair into the grey morning.

The tide turned at nightfall. The Toll Gate lay four miles into the underways, through cisterns that drowned the careless. Ansel had no rope, no light worth the name, no allies confirmed, and no plan beyond *run faster than everyone's mistakes*.

It was, they reflected, setting off at a jog toward the nearest drain-mouth, easily the worst bargain they'd ever struck—and they had once traded a luck-knot for a haunted goose.

Behind them, far off, a bell began to ring. Not the Ashen Bell. A Ministry bell, high and thin, calling its gray coats home.

Three days were up.
