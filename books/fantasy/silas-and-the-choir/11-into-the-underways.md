## 11. Into the Underways

The cisterns beneath Vell smelled of old rain and older secrets.

Ansel had been following the rebels for six hours by the light of a charm-lamp they'd made that morning—a small thing, a jar of river-glass filled with their own thinned gift, glowing the color of weak tea. It was the last real magic they intended to spend on this madness, and they were already regretting it. The lamp flickered whenever Ansel's hands shook, which was often, because the water was waist-deep and cold enough to make their knees file formal complaints.

"Left at the third arch," they murmured, consulting the map's memory rather than the map itself. The bloodstained original was sewn into their coat lining now, against their ribs, where it lay like a second heartbeat. The cipher marks Wren had taught them—*two strokes for water, three for silence, a circle broken on the left for danger*—were scratched into the archway stones by hands long dead. Guild hands. Their people had walked here once, before the Purge, before everything.

Ansel tried not to think about Wren alive. It was like trying not to think about a stone in one's boot; every step reminded them.

*He never came back for you,* said the small bitter voice that lived behind Ansel's sternum. *Twelve years. He let you believe he was ash.*

*He had his reasons,* said another voice, older and more tired. *Everyone in this story has reasons. That's what makes it unbearable.*

The tunnel ahead split around a fallen pillar, and Ansel stopped, ears straining. Voices. Echoing off wet stone, distorted, but unmistakably human and unmistakably angry. They doused the lamp to a ember-glimmer and waded closer, pressing themselves into the shadow of the pillar, water lapping at their chest.

Three figures stood on a dry ledge above the flooded channel, torchlit. One was Sefa—the smuggler from the bell, all sharp elbows and sharper eyes, arms crossed in a posture Ansel was beginning to recognize as her version of diplomacy. Facing her were two others: a broad woman with a Ministry-issue saber repurposed into a woodcutter's tool, and a thin man whose fingers never stopped moving, drumming, twitching, counting something only he could see.

"—not a weapon," Sefa was saying, for perhaps the hundredth time, judging by the wear in her voice. "It's a *song*, Dree. You don't point a song at anyone."

"You don't know what it is," said the woman—Dree, presumably. Her voice was flat as slate. "None of us do. But the elders sealed it away instead of using it when the Ministry was young and soft. That tells me it's strong."

"It tells me they were *wise*."

"Wise." Dree spat into the water. Ansel flinched; the spatter landed a hand's width away. "My mother was wise. Wise got her shipped to the salt mines. I'd rather be strong."

Ansel sank a little deeper into the water. Cold climbed their spine, though whether from temperature or dread, they couldn't say. These were the people who had taken Wren. These were the people who wanted the Choir. And standing among them, listening, Ansel felt something worse than fear: recognition. Dree's anger wasn't monstrous. It was *reasonable*. That was the horror of it. Twelve years ago, Ansel might have stood on that ledge too, fists clenched, wanting the world to hurt back.

The thin man spoke for the first time, voice like dry paper. "The hardliner position is simple, Sefa. We open the vault. We take the song. We hold it over the Ministry's head the way they've held fire over ours for a generation. Parity. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," Sefa repeated. "Corvel, you've rehearsed that sentence so long you've forgotten it's a lie. The moment we have parity, someone in this cell will want advantage. Someone always does. Then it's escalation, then it's war, then it's the Purge again except this time we're holding the torches."

"And if we do nothing?"

"We're not doing nothing. We're getting Wren out. We're finding out what the song actually is before anyone decides anything."

Dree laughed, short and ugly. "And who decides? You? The charm-seller you've gone soft on?"

Ansel's heart performed a small undignified maneuver. *Gone soft on.* Well. That was—something to examine later, in private, possibly underwater.

"I decide nothing alone," Sefa said. "But I'll tell you what I told the cell when my brother died. We don't become them. The day we do, they've won even if we burn their tower down."

Silence, except for dripping water and the thin man's restless fingers. Then Dree shouldered her tool-blade. "The cell votes at dawn. Pray your conscience outvotes your sentiment, Sefa." She turned—and paused, nostrils flaring, gaze sweeping the dark water below. "Something's in the cistern."

Ansel did not breathe. The lamp against their chest dimmed obediently—they'd built that courtesy into it years ago, a survival habit—and they pressed back against the cold stone, becoming, as they'd practiced for twelve years, nobody.

But Dree's eyes passed over the water, found nothing but darkness, and moved on. The three figures climbed the far stair, torchlight shrinking, voices fading into argument.

Ansel surfaced from stillness slowly, like a creature deciding the predator had gone.

"Gone soft on," they whispered to the empty tunnel. "Honestly. I've known her four days."

The tunnel, being a tunnel, offered no comment. Ansel wrung out their coat, relit the lamp, and followed the rebel party upward, toward the vote, toward Wren, toward whatever came next. The water gave them up reluctantly, and somewhere in the dark behind them, the Choir's distant hum seemed almost amused.

* * *

Sefa couldn't stop thinking about the cistern.

Not the argument—arguments with Dree were weather, constant and survivable—but the moment after, when she'd glanced down into the black water and seen, or thought she'd seen, a ripple spreading outward from the pillar. A ripple shaped like a person trying very hard to be a rock.

*Charm-seller,* she thought, climbing the maintenance ladder two rungs behind Corvel. *Idiot. Brave idiot. Following us.*

She should be angry. Part of her was—it was her neck on the line if the cell learned she'd led an outsider to their door, however accidentally the map had done the leading. But mostly what she felt was a tired, stubborn warmth she didn't have room for. Ansel Dunmore had looked at her across a dead drop marked by melted guild seals, both of them lying through their teeth about how much they trusted each other, and Sefa had thought: *there. That one. That's what honesty looks like when it's wearing armor.*

Her brother Tam had worn the same armor. Right up until the Ministry took it off him along with everything else.

The safehouse was a collapsed wine cellar in the old customs quarter, reachable only through a crawl-space that smelled of vinegar and regret. Sefa went in last, as always, counting heads on the way through: Corvel, Dree, the twins Pell and Oskar, old Marnie who kept the books, and—

Wren.

They had him tied to a chair in the corner, which Sefa had argued against and lost, and fed, which she'd argued for and won, and watched around the clock, which everyone agreed on because nobody trusted anybody anymore. He looked smaller than he had in the bathhouse. Older. His eyes found Sefa the moment she entered, and there was something in them—not pleading, exactly. More like a man watching a fire start in a house he'd already failed to save once.

"Well?" he asked quietly.

"Dawn," Sefa said. "The vote's at dawn."

"Ah." He closed his eyes. "Then I have until dawn to convince you, and you have until dawn to convince them, and neither of us is any good at convincing anyone. Charming symmetry."

"You could try being less insufferable."

"My dear girl, insufferability is the last possession I own. The Ministry took the rest."

Despite herself—despite everything, the ransacked bathhouse, the stolen elder, the whole rotten cascade of the past week—Sefa almost smiled. She crouched by his chair, keeping her voice under the twins' dice game. "Tell me again. The song. What happens if they use it."

Wren opened his eyes. In the lamplight they were the color of pond ice. "I don't know. That's the truth no one wants. The founders sealed Silas because half of them wanted to wake it and half wanted to drown it, and the argument lasted eleven years and cost three friendships and one marriage. Sealing it was the compromise. No one ever tested the alternative."

"Silas," Sefa said. "You keep calling it that. Like it's a person."

"Like it *is* a person." Wren's voice dropped further. "That's what none of them understand, child. Not Dree, not Corvel, not the Ministry. It isn't a sword in a vault. It's a *voice*. And a voice can be begged, bargained with, or silenced—but pointed at someone?" He shook his head slowly. "You cannot aim grief."

Across the cellar, Dree was sharpening her blade against a whetstone, each stroke deliberate, a metronome for the coming vote. Corvel's fingers drummed the table. Marnie counted coins that didn't need counting. And Sefa sat beside a bound old man who had helped destroy her world and was now the only person telling her the truth, and felt the ground tilting under a cause she'd bled twelve years for.

*We don't become them.* She'd said it at her brother's grave. She'd said it tonight in the cisterns. But causes had a way of eating their own words, and dawn was coming, and Dree's whetstone kept its terrible patient rhythm.

"Sefa." Wren's hand, bound though it was, brushed her sleeve. "When the vote goes wrong—and it will go wrong—there's a charm-seller in these tunnels who owes me nothing and keeps showing up anyway. Find them. Trust them further than is sensible."

"Why?"

"Because I trained them to survive," Wren said, "and I have recently learned that surviving isn't the same as living. They figured that out without me. That makes them worth ten of us."

Sefa stood, unsettled, and busied herself with the watch rotation. Outside, rain began on the streets of Vell, filtering down through a hundred cracks, refilling the cisterns inch by inch.

In the flooded dark below, a lamp the color of weak tea moved steadily upward, its bearer soaked, shivering, and more afraid of the dawn than of the water.

Both of them, without knowing it, were racing the same sunrise.
