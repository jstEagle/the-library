## 12. The Toll Gate

The underways ended where the water did.

Ansel had followed the map's third thread for the better part of a day—down through the flooded cisterns where Sefa's cell had scattered like minnows, along service tunnels that smelled of old iron and older rain, past the drowned chapel where someone had hung lanterns from the ribs of the ceiling so that the whole vault glowed like the inside of a paper lamp. Beautiful, in the way the undercity was always beautiful: by accident, and at great cost.

Now the tunnel narrowed to a single archway, and in it burned a light.

Not torchlight. Charm-light—the soft amber of guild-work, steady as a held breath. Ansel stopped three paces out, water lapping at their boots, and felt something in their chest twist sideways. Twelve years since they'd seen that particular shade of amber. It was the color of Wren's workshop on winter evenings. It was the color of home.

"Come no closer unless you mean to pay," said a voice from the arch.

The toll-keeper was an old woman seated on a stool too small for her, wrapped in a coat made of what looked like forty other coats, each sleeve a different decade. Her face was seamed and pleasant, like a walnut that had decided to be kind about its wrinkles. Around her neck, on cords of varying age, hung dozens of small glass beads—and inside each bead moved something. Not light. Not smoke. Something with *texture*.

Ansel knew what those were. Had known, distantly, that such things existed, the way one knows about diseases one has never caught.

Memory-charms. Forbidden twice over—once by the Ministry, once by the guild itself, which had judged them an abomination before the Ministry got around to agreeing.

"You're a long way from any road," Ansel said carefully.

"And you're a long way from anywhere at all," the old woman replied cheerfully. "Which makes us neighbors, of a fashion. Toll's one charm." She lifted a cord, letting the beads swing and catch the amber light. "Paid in memory. One cherished recollection, freely given. I take it, I keep it, you go through."

"And if I don't pay?"

"Then you swim back the way you came, and we both pretend this was a lovely chat." She smiled. She had very few teeth left and deployed them generously. "I'm called Mother Quill. Everyone asks eventually."

Ansel looked past her. Beyond the arch the tunnel continued into darkness, but the darkness had a quality to it—a hush, a held note—that told them they were close now. Closer than they'd ever been to anything that mattered. The Choir was near. They could feel it the way you feel weather coming, in the ache of old scars.

They thought about turning back. They thought about it honestly, weighing it the way they weighed everything: risk against survival, survival against whatever thin thing survival was *for*.

Then they thought about Wren, somewhere ahead or behind or in the hands of people who wanted to torture a key out of him, and the weighing was over.

"How does it work?" Ansel asked.

Mother Quill's smile gentled. "Ah. You're guild-trained. I can tell—you ask how before you ask whether." She drew one bead from the cord, a small grey-green thing like a drop of frozen sea. "The charm listens. You hold the memory in your mind, all of it, every corner, and you give it willingly. It can't be taken—only surrendered. That's the law of the thing, older than your guild and mine. And then—" she turned the bead so the light moved inside it, "—then it lives here instead. In you there'll be a hole shaped exactly like it. Smooth-walled. Painless. You'll know something is missing the way you know a room is missing furniture. But you won't miss the thing itself, because you won't remember there was a thing."

"That's monstrous," Ansel said.

"It's a toll," Mother Quill agreed comfortably. "Everything past this point costs more than coin. Coin's for roads. This is a threshold." She studied them with eyes gone pale as riverwater. "Choose well, child. Most folk pay me their worst memory—grief, shame, the night they ran. Cheaper, feels like. But the charm knows the difference. It only takes what you cherish. So the liars among them try to hand me something they love only a little, and the charm sits in their palm like a stone, dead, worthless, until they either go back or get honest." She tucked the bead away. "You strike me as honest. Tired-honest. The most expensive kind."

Ansel sat down on the wet stone without meaning to. Their legs had simply concluded the matter without consulting them.

One memory. One cherished recollection, and they could pass.

They began, helplessly, to inventory.

There was the morning Sefa had pressed bread into their hands in the cistern dark and said, *you eat first, I don't trust anyone who eats second*—warm, recent, precious. No. They might need Sefa. They might need to trust her, and you couldn't trust someone whose kindness you couldn't remember.

There was the fishwife from years ago, the luck-knot, the way she'd crossed herself with a hand that shook and said *you're a good soul, doing this work*. No. That memory was load-bearing. It was one of the beams holding up the whole crooked house of why Ansel kept selling trinkets at all.

There was—

And there it was. Waiting. It had been waiting the whole time, patient as the amber light, because some part of Ansel had known from the moment they saw the beads.

Wren. The last evening in the workshop, before everything. Before the Purge, before the running, before twelve years of pretending. Snow against the windows. Wren had been teaching them the patience-charm, the one that kept bread warm and tempers cool, and Ansel—fourteen, arrogant, homesick for a home they'd never had—had botched it six times running and thrown the loaf across the room.

And Wren had laughed. Not the disappointed laugh, not the *we'll try again tomorrow* laugh. He'd laughed until he had to sit down on his stool, wiping his eyes, and then he'd picked up the ruined loaf, torn it in half, handed half to Ansel, and said:

*"You know what, little wren? The bread doesn't care if it's warm. Neither do I. Eat."*

And they had eaten warm-less bread together in the cold workshop, and it had been, absurdly, the best meal of Ansel's life. Because that was the moment they'd understood—they hadn't been an apprentice to Wren. They'd been family. The guild hadn't been a school. It had been a kitchen with too many chairs.

That was the memory. Of course it was. The charm would want exactly that one, and Ansel's whole body already knew it, the way you know which tooth aches.

"There's another way," Ansel said, though they knew there wasn't. "I could give you something else. A skill. Years of training. I have—"

"Skills aren't memories," Mother Quill said, not unkindly. "And training isn't cherished. Well. Sometimes it is." Her eyes sharpened. "What is it you're going to give me, child? Say it aloud. The charm likes to hear it named."

Ansel's throat closed. They made themselves open it.

"The last evening with my master," they said. "Before the Purge. When he laughed. When I learned I belonged to something." Their voice cracked on the last word and they let it. "When I learned I was loved."

The old woman was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere far off, water dripped its slow count.

"I've sat this gate forty years," she said finally. "I've taken weddings and christenings and one man's memory of his dog, which broke my heart clean in two. And I'll tell you what I tell them all, though none of them believe me yet." She leaned forward, coats rustling. "The hole heals wrong. Not painfully—but strangely. You'll be kinder in places you were sharp, and sharp in places you were kind. You'll grieve someone and not know who. For years. That's the price under the price." She held out the bead. "Knowing that—do you still pay?"

And here was the terrible arithmetic of it, laid bare: Ansel thought of Wren in rebel hands. Thought of the Choir humming behind that hush in the dark. Thought of Voss, of the Ministry, of the war everyone was sprinting toward. Thought: *if I keep this memory, sweet and whole and warm, and it costs me the chance to save him—then the memory becomes a lie. A pretty thing I chose over the person it came from.*

Love wasn't the keeping of a thing. Love was what you did with it.

"Yes," Ansel said.

They took the bead. It was warm, warmer than glass had any right to be, and it pulsed faintly against their palm like a sleeping bird. They closed their eyes. They found the evening—snow, the ruined loaf, the laughter, *eat, little wren*—and they held it, all of it, every corner, and they gave it.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It simply poured out, smooth as water off a table, and when Ansel opened their eyes the world was the same and they were different in a way they couldn't locate. There was a smooth-walled place inside them. They knew a room was missing furniture. They could not have said what furniture.

Something wet was on their face. They touched it, puzzled.

"Grief comes first sometimes," Mother Quill said softly. "Before the forgetting finishes. Let it run its course; it's the body saying goodbye on the mind's behalf." She pressed something into Ansel's hand—a heel of bread, wrapped in cloth. "For the road. Don't ask how I knew you'd need feeding. Gatekeepers know things."

Ansel stood. Their legs held. The hole inside was smooth and painless and enormous.

"Why do you do this?" they heard themselves ask. "Forty years at a gate, taking people's treasures?"

Mother Quill looked down at her beads—all those little lights, all those surrendered loves—and her walnut face went unreadable.

"Because somebody has to keep them safe," she said. "Every memory anyone ever paid me is still in here. Whole. Warm. Loved." She patted the cord at her throat. "Nothing cherished should have to die just because its owner needed to survive. Go on, child. Through the arch. And—" she hesitated, and for the first time the cheerfulness dropped away entirely, leaving something old and tired underneath, "—when you get where you're going, and you meet whoever sent you down here—remember that some debts are paid by people who never borrowed anything."

Ansel frowned. "What do you—"

But the old woman was already waving them on, cheerful again, and the archway's hush was pulling at them like a tide, and there was no time. Ansel walked through.

The tunnel beyond sloped downward, and the air changed—cooler, sweeter, carrying a vibration they felt in their teeth before they heard it with their ears. A hum. Deep, patient, impossibly sad.

The Choir.

Ansel walked toward it with a hole in their chest shaped like a snowy evening and a master's laughter, grieving someone they could no longer name, and did not stop walking.

Behind them, faint and fading, they thought they heard Mother Quill speaking—to herself, or to the beads, or perhaps to the memory she'd just taken, keeping it company the way she'd promised all of them she would.

*"Eat, little wren,"* the dark seemed to murmur. *"Eat."*

Ansel wiped their face and kept going, and did not know why the words made them cry.
