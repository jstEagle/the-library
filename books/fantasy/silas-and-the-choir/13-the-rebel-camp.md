## 13. The Rebel Camp

The camp smelled of wet wool and burnt barley porridge, which was how Sefa knew things had gone badly. Her cell cooked well when morale was high. When Dagny took over the cookfire, everyone ate punishment.

She came up the goat track from the cisterns just past dusk, boots squelching, hair plastered to her neck, and stopped at the sentry line where a boy of maybe sixteen raised a crossbow at her with the confidence of someone who had never actually fired one.

"It's me, Pilo."

"I know." He lowered it, then raised it again, then seemed to lose interest in the whole enterprise. "You're late. And you're alone."

"Where's the rest of the retrieval team?"

"Gone ahead. Two days ago." Pilo glanced over his shoulder, and something in his face made her stomach drop before he even said it. "They brought back the old man."

*The old man.* Wren. She had left them a description and a warning—*bring him gentle, he's worth more talking than bleeding*—and she already knew, the way you know weather coming in your knees, that they hadn't listened.

"Where?"

"The chapel ruin. Maro's got him there." Pilo hesitated. "Sefa, I don't think it's going how you'd want."

"Nothing ever does," she said, and walked past him into camp.

* * *

The rebel cell had been her family for nine years, which was long enough to know its smells, its jokes, its grudges, and its rot. The rot was named Maro Vask.

He had been a dockhand before the Purge, then a smuggler, then—after the Ministry hanged his crew and burned his quarter—a believer. Believers were useful. Believers would do what the grieving wouldn't. But Sefa had learned to watch the moment when grief finished hardening into certainty, because after that moment a person stopped asking *what will this cost?* and started asking only *how fast can we get it done?*

Maro stood outside the chapel ruin now, arms crossed, watching two of his people feed a brazier. He was a big man gone lean, with a beard he trimmed like he was punishing it.

"You're back," he said. No warmth in it. There rarely was anymore.

"I'm back. Where's Wren?"

"Inside. Resting."

"Resting," Sefa repeated. "With a brazier going and iron in the fire, I'd guess."

Maro's jaw worked. "He won't give us the final key. The vault has a last seal—some guild trick, a word or an object—and he's the only one who knows it. He's had two days to be reasonable about it."

"He's had two days of your hospitality. That tends to make men unreasonable."

"Sefa." Maro turned to face her fully, and she saw he'd been waiting for this conversation, maybe rehearsing it. "I know you went soft on the charmwright. I know you let him slip the net at the toll gate—I heard the report. So let me save us both some time. This isn't your part anymore. You found the map's trail. Good work. Now stand down and let people who still believe in something finish it."

"I believe in something," Sefa said. "That's the problem. I remember what it is."

"We're going to break the Ministry." His voice dropped low, fervent, the voice he used on new recruits. "Twelve years they've ruled by fear and fire. The song ends that. One song, and no Inquisitor ever lights another pyre. Whatever it takes to open that door—"

"Whatever it takes," Sefa said quietly. "Say the next part, Maro. Say what it takes tonight."

Inside the chapel, faintly, through the broken doorway, came a sound. Not screaming. Worse: a man's voice, ragged but steady, reciting something. Numbers, maybe. Or a nursery rhyme. The voice of a man deciding in advance not to give them anything, and holding to it one syllable at a time.

Maro looked away first.

"We haven't started," he said. "Not properly. But we will. Tomorrow, if he keeps being stubborn."

"And if he doesn't break?"

"Then he wasn't going to, and we'll have other options." He said it lightly, the way men say monstrous things when they've practiced until the words feel small. "Get some food. Sleep. You look half-drowned."

Sefa got some food. She did not sleep.

* * *

Her brother Tam had died in the second year of the Purge, and he had died badly—not quickly, not cleanly, but over three days in a Ministry cellar while clerks wrote down everything he said. Sefa had been fourteen. She had learned afterward, from a guard who'd grown a conscience too late, that Tam had given them nothing. Not a name, not a hiding place, not a single true sentence. Three days of silence so that others could live.

She had joined the cell because of that silence. Because someone should carry it forward. Because the world that made a boy pay three days of himself for strangers' safety deserved to burn.

And somewhere in nine years of smuggling and running and losing people, she had watched the burning become the point instead of the consequence. She had watched Maro and the hardliners speak of the song the way the Ministry spoke of order—as a thing that justified any price paid by anyone else.

Tam had held out for three days so that *people* could live. Not so that a weapon could be freed. If he could have seen the chapel tonight, seen the brazier and the iron and the old man reciting numbers to keep his mind whole—

Sefa sat on a fallen wall behind the cookfire and ate cold porridge and understood, with the calm clarity that comes only at the worst moments, exactly what she was about to do. It didn't feel like a decision. Decisions felt like doors. This felt like finally admitting which room she'd been standing in all along.

* * *

She waited for the deep hours, when even believers sleep. The camp settled by degrees—the sentries' conversations shortening, the cookfire banked, Pilo yawning through his watch with his crossbow pointed generally at the concept of danger. Rain came back around midnight, soft and steady, good cover for noise.

She went to the chapel ruin.

It had been a proper chapel once, before the Ministry repurposed such places as tax offices and then abandoned them when the roof went. Now it was a shell of blackened stone with one wall standing and moonlight coming through the empty window frames like the building itself had been gutted and left to heal wrong.

Wren lay against the far wall, wrists bound in front of him with waxed cord, ankles tied to an iron ring set in the floor—an old ring, older than the chapel probably, the kind of thing smugglers installed everywhere. His face was grey. His lips were cracked. But his eyes, when they found her in the dark, sharpened immediately, and he had the audacity to raise one eyebrow.

"You must be the famous Sefa," he whispered. "Ansel described you. They said you had a dishonest face. They undersold it."

"Hush." She knelt beside him, working at the wrist cords with a knife. "Can you walk?"

"I've been sitting on stone for two days. I can barely *sit*. But yes, I'll walk, or hop, or be dragged with tremendous dignity." His whisper was dry as paper, but she felt him trembling under the bravado. "They asked me questions today. Yesterday too. I told them a story about my grandmother's recipe for eel pie. I think Maro believed eels were involved somehow. He's not a subtle man."

"Why didn't you tell them?" She sawed through the cord. "The key. You must know they'll escalate. Tonight was mercy compared to tomorrow."

Wren was quiet a moment. In the moonlight she saw how old he truly was—not elderly, but worn, the way stone steps wear, hollowed in the middle.

"Because the key isn't a word," he said. "It's a choice. The vault opens for hands that come to give, not take. If I gave them the way in, they'd carry their war through that door, and the song—" He stopped. Started again, softer. "The song deserves better than to end its long sleep by being handed to the angriest people in Vell. Including me. *Especially* me, perhaps."

"That's very noble for a man who used an apprentice as bait."

"Yes." No flinch, no excuse. "Ansel has every right to hate me. I'll spend whatever's left of my life earning otherwise, and I expect to fail. But I won't hand my failures a weapon." He flexed his freed fingers, wincing. "Why are *you* doing this? Your cell. Your cause. Your brother—I read the reports, girl, I was in Vell during the Purge, I know what they took from you."

Sefa sat back on her heels. The rain ticked softly on the standing wall.

"My brother stayed silent for three days so people could live," she said. "Not so a song could be stolen. Somewhere along the way I forgot whose side of that I was on." She cut the ankle ties. "Tomorrow I'd have watched them hurt you and called it necessity. I'm done calling things necessity."

Wren studied her for a long moment. Then, absurdly, he smiled—a real one, tired and crooked.

"Ansel said you were decent under all the lying," he said. "Also that you snore."

"They *would* say that. I have shared one—*one*—night of shelter with that person, and I was exhausted, and—"

"Later," Wren whispered. "Argue later. Which way out?"

* * *

The bad news arrived as they reached the chapel door, in the form of Pilo, materializing out of the rain with his crossbow clutched to his chest like a security blanket.

"Oh no," Pilo said. "Oh no, no. Sefa. What are—you're not—are you *stealing* him?"

"Shh." She put a hand on the boy's shoulder. He was shaking, though whether from cold or conflict she couldn't tell. "Pilo. Listen to me. What Maro plans to do to this man tomorrow—it isn't the cause. It's what the cause becomes when nobody stops it. You're sixteen. You still get to decide what you are. Go back to your post, count to five hundred, and be very surprised by everything that happens next."

Pilo looked at her. Looked at Wren, who gave him a small, grandfatherly nod, as though they were all neighbors discussing fence repairs. Then he looked at the dark, and Sefa watched something shift in his young face—the same shift she'd felt on the wall by the cookfire, the admission of a room long occupied.

"Four hundred," Pilo whispered. "I'm bad at counting." And he turned his back and stared ostentatiously at the rain.

* * *

They went north through the goat tracks, away from the cisterns, because Wren—of course—knew another way. A smuggler's stair cut into the hillside, hidden under gorse, leading down toward the flooded quarries where the underways ran deep and the Ministry's lanterns didn't reach.

Halfway down the stair, Wren had to stop. His legs simply quit; he sat on a step, breathing hard, and waved off her help with shaky dignity.

"Go on without me if it comes to it," he managed.

"Don't be dramatic, it's unbecoming of a guild master."

"I was never promoted. I refused the examination. Too much paperwork." He wheezed what might have been a laugh. "Sefa. The others. Ansel is heading for the Choir—they'll have figured out the route by now, and Maro's people will move the moment they find you gone. Everything converges there. You understand that, yes? Every angry soul in this story is walking toward the same door."

"Then we walk faster than our anger does," Sefa said, hauling him up. "Come on, old man. Ansel's probably already there, being insufferably careful about it."

Behind them, faint across the rain, a horn sounded in the camp—one note, then two. Discovery. Pilo, apparently, had lost count early.

Wren gripped her arm, and together they went down into the dark, two defectors carrying nothing between them but a key that wasn't a word, and the thin, stubborn hope that conscience moved quicker than vengeance.

It had never been tested. Tonight it would be.
