## 14. Three Runners

The water came first as a sound, far off and wrong—a rushing where there should have been dripping, a roar building in the dark like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep. Ansel felt it through the soles of their boots before they heard it, a tremor in the old stone, and knew what it meant even before Sefa shouted it.

"They've opened the sluices! Move, move, *move*!"

They ran. There was nothing else to do. The underways of Vell were a maze of brick throats and drowned chapels, tunnels the city had built on top of and forgotten, and somewhere behind them the Ministry was pouring the river into all of it at once. Ansel did not look back. Looking back cost time, and time was the only coin they had left that anyone still accepted.

Wren ran ahead of them, which was its own small miracle. He was seventy if he was a day, with a hip that clicked audibly on every third step, and yet he moved through the dark like a man who had walked these passages every morning for forty years—which, Ansel supposed bitterly, perhaps he had. Left at the broken cistern arch. Right where the mortar crumbled into a black seam. Wren never hesitated, and Ansel hated him a little for it, for knowing the way so well, for having had twelve years to learn it while Ansel had spent those same twelve years learning how to be invisible in a market crowd.

"Here!" Wren's voice cracked out of the gloom. "The maintenance stair—down and along—"

The stair was a ladder, really, iron rungs set into wet stone, and the passage below was barely shoulder-width. Sefa went first, then Ansel, then Wren, and behind them the water arrived—not a flood, not yet, but a tongue of it, black and fast, licking up the tunnel they'd just abandoned. Ansel heard it slap against the walls and thought, with a strange detached clarity: *that is the sound of my whole life ending.* Everything they'd built in Vell—the stall, the routine, the careful gray years—all of it was upstream of this water, and none of it was coming back.

Their feet found level ground. They ran again.

* * *

They stopped, eventually, because bodies insist on it, in a wide chamber that might once have been a reservoir. The ceiling was lost in darkness above; the floor was cracked tile, green with ancient moss, and someone long dead had carved fish into the walls, hundreds of them, swimming in endless circles around the room. Ansel put their back against the wall and slid down it until they were sitting, chest heaving, hands shaking so badly they had to press them flat against the tiles to make them stop.

Sefa stood at the mouth of the passage they'd come through, head cocked, listening. She'd taken off one boot and was pouring water out of it with the expression of a woman filing a formal complaint with the universe.

"How much of the underway do they intend to drown?" she asked.

"All of it, if Halle Voss thinks we're down here," Ansel said. "And Voss knows we're down here."

"You're certain?"

"I'm certain Voss doesn't leave things half-finished." Ansel's voice came out flatter than they intended. "It's why I lied to her for three days. She doesn't rage. She *completes*."

Wren lowered himself onto a dry patch of tile with a grunt, and for a moment nobody spoke. The only sounds were their breathing and the distant, patient rush of water finding its way through the city's bones.

It was Wren who broke the silence, which surprised Ansel, because in all the years of apprenticeship Wren had possessed the rare gift of silence, could sit through an entire afternoon without a word, teaching by gesture and example. That man would have let the quiet stand. This one—older, guiltier, his beard gone white at the chin—said:

"You kept the knot-work."

Ansel looked at him. "What?"

"Your hands. In the market, before all this. I saw you sell a luck-knot to a fishwife." He said it gently, as though offering something fragile. "You tie them the way I taught you. Left over right, then tuck twice. Nobody else in Vell ties them that way. I used to walk past your stall, some mornings. Just to see."

Something hot rose in Ansel's throat, and they were furious about it, furious that after everything—after the courier's blood drying on their cellar floor, after the ransacked bathhouse, after learning that the man they'd mourned for twelve years had been alive and hiding three districts away while they sold trinkets and paid protection coin and ate dinner alone—after all that, some traitor part of them still wanted to lean toward that voice like a cold hand toward a fire.

"Don't," Ansel said.

"Ansel—"

"*Don't.*" Their voice bounced off the tiled walls and came back at them, and the carved fish seemed to swim faster. "You don't get to say my name like that. You don't get to have watched me. You want to know what I did while you were alive and hiding? I buried you. Do you understand? I dug a grave for you in my head and put flowers on it and I *grieved*, and grief was—I was good at it, eventually. I got good at being the last one. And then a dying man bleeds out on my floor and tells me the Choir remembers, and it turns out the grave was empty the whole time, and you—" their voice cracked, and they hated that too— "you sent a courier to find me. Not a letter. Not a knock on the door. A courier, carrying a map that got him killed, so that the Ministry would follow the blood straight to the last apprentice of the Charmwrights' Guild. You used me as a door, Master Wren. You always said I had steady hands. Turns out that's all I ever was to you. Hands."

The words ran out and left a ringing silence. Ansel realized they were standing now, though they didn't remember getting up, and that Sefa had very quietly moved herself between them and the exit, not to block anyone—to give them privacy, or to guard against the water, hard to say.

Wren sat among the carved fish with his hands open on his knees, an old man taking a blow he'd clearly been expecting.

"You're right," he said. "Mostly."

"*Mostly?*"

"The courier was my doing, yes. And yes, I chose you because guild-trained hands can survive the approach, and no other reason I could offer you that wouldn't be a lie." His eyes were steady, and tired, and terribly clear. "But I'll tell you what I told the elders, when they said the same thing you just said. I watched you for two years before I sent anything. Two years. I watched you give a frightened woman a charm that couldn't work, and charge her a fair price, and tell her it would keep her husband safe, because sometimes the believing does the work and you knew it and you let her have it anyway. I watched you pay protection coin to a clerk who pretended not to know you, and you pretended not to know he knew, and the two of you kept each other's secrets like a pair of old dancers." He shook his head slowly. "I didn't send for a pair of hands, Ansel. I sent for the person who'd survived twelve years of erasure and come out of it still kind. The elders wanted a key. I wanted you. Both things can be true, and I'm sorry they arrived in the same envelope."

Ansel turned away. It was either that or cry, and they were not going to cry in front of Wren, not yet, maybe not ever, possibly not even at the funeral this time since apparently funerals were reversible.

"It doesn't fix it," they said finally, to the wall of swimming fish.

"No," Wren agreed. "It doesn't."

"And I'm not forgiving you."

"I haven't asked you to."

"That's—" Ansel stopped. Closed their eyes. Opened them. "That's exactly what you'd say. You always did that. You'd apologize by *not asking*, so the apology would land before I could decide whether to accept it. You taught me that trick. I use it on customers."

Behind them, Sefa snorted. Ansel turned. She was sitting on the flooded edge of the chamber now, barefoot, boots drying beside her, wringing out a stocking with tremendous dignity.

"For what it's worth," she said, "he does the same thing with me. Apologizes in advance so you can't refuse it. Drives the whole cell mad. Orlin once threw a chair."

"Orlin," Wren said mildly, "threw a chair at a great many things."

"Orlin," Sefa said, and her voice changed, went quieter and harder, "sold us to the Ministry's map-makers for a promise of amnesty, so I'll thank you both to remember that whatever our sins, we're currently the ones being chased." She looked up at Ansel, and in the dim light her face was all sharp angles and exhausted honesty. "Three days ago I would have said trust is a currency and I'm broke. Now I'm running from a flood with a guildmaster and a shopkeeper, and the shopkeeper is the only one of us who's lied to an Inquisitor and lived. So. Fragile alliance, then. We measure each other as we go."

"Fragile alliance," Ansel agreed.

Wren, wisely, said nothing at all.

* * *

They moved again within the hour, because the water was rising and because stopping too long invited despair, and despair in the underways was a slow death dressed as rest. Wren led. Sefa took the rear, walking backward every fifty paces to check the black water gaining on them, and Ansel took the middle, which felt symbolically appropriate.

The tunnels worsened. Twice they waded thigh-deep through channels that should have been dry, and once they crossed a gallery where the ceiling had already begun to weep, fat drops falling in the dark with the regularity of a clock. Ansel counted the drops to keep panic at bay. At four hundred and eleven, the ceiling stopped weeping and started pouring, and they ran again.

It was during that run that the tunnel ahead of them groaned.

Not water-groan. Stone-groan—the deep, structural complaint of masonry deciding whether to continue existing. Dust sifted down. Somewhere overhead, something enormous shifted its weight.

"Crack in the crown," Wren said, and his voice had gone crisp, professional, the voice of a master surveying a job site, and absurdly that steadied Ansel more than anything else had. "Brick barrel vault, old construction. It'll hold. Probably."

"*Probably?*" Sefa said.

"I was going to say 'certainly,' but I find I've developed a taste for honesty in my old age."

"Twelve years too late," Ansel muttered, and Wren laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh—and then, horribly, so did Ansel, and for one suspended moment the three of them were laughing in a flooding tunnel beneath a cracking ceiling, and it was the most alive Ansel had felt in twelve years, and they resented every second of it.

Then the ceiling finished making up its mind.

The collapse started as a trickle of bricks at the far end and unzipped toward them with a sound like the world tearing cloth. Sefa screamed a direction—Ansel's brain refused to record which direction—and then they were running through falling dust and bouncing rubble, Wren stumbling, Ansel catching his arm without thinking, muscle memory older than the betrayal hauling the old man upright, and they burst through into the next gallery as the passage behind them filled itself with rubble from wall to wall, sealing the flood out and the way back in with equal thoroughness.

Silence. Dust. The three of them bent double, coughing, in a new corridor lit by nothing at all until Sefa struck a match.

"Well," she said, when she could breathe. "That's the way back gone."

"There are no ways back," Ansel said. "There's only forward and drown. Forward and drown. Those are the options now."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a shopkeeper. We price things honestly."

Wren was standing very still, looking at the sealed wall of rubble, and when he turned around his face in the matchlight was strange—relieved, almost, and ashamed of the relief.

"My leg gave," he said quietly. "Back there. When the ceiling came down. If you hadn't caught me—" He stopped. Started again. "You caught me. Twelve years, and the first thing you do with your hands is catch me."

"Don't make it a lesson," Ansel said sharply. "Please. Don't you dare make it a lesson."

"I'm not. I'm making it a fact." He held up one hand, palm out, the old gesture of a master yielding the floor to an apprentice, and Ansel's chest hurt with the familiarity of it. "Facts are all I have left to give. Here's another: the Choir's threshold is half a mile past the Toll Gate, and the Toll Gate takes payment in memory. One cherished recollection, taken clean out of your head, in exchange for passage. I paid mine thirty years ago. I no longer remember what I gave it, which is the joke, isn't it—you can't mourn what you can't recall."

Ansel went cold. "A memory."

"A cherished one. The toll-keeper knows the difference. They always do." Wren's eyes found theirs in the matchlight, gentle and terrible. "I tell you now so you can choose what to carry lightly, between here and there. Whatever you pay—it will be gone before you know what it was. So hold tight to something. Choose your treasure, and grip it, and maybe the keeper will take second-best. Or maybe it won't matter. But go in knowing, child. That's all I can do for you now. Make sure you go in knowing."

The match burned down to Sefa's fingers and died. In the dark, the water pressed patiently against the rubble wall, and somewhere ahead, faint as a heartbeat through stone, Ansel thought they could hear something humming—a single sustained note, low and patient, like a throat clearing before a song.

The Choir, remembering them already.

"Forward and drown," Sefa said into the darkness, and struck another match.
