## 15. Voss Closes In

Inquisitor Halle Voss had a theory about tea.

Anyone could brew it well in a fine house with clean water and a servant to mind the kettle. The true test of a city was what its tea tasted like at the bottom of the Ministry of Inquiry, three floors below the street, where the pipes were old and the water tasted faintly of iron and older secrets. Voss drank that tea every morning of their working life and had long ago decided it was an education. Humility, served hot.

They drank it now, standing at the map table in the undercity field office—a converted tollhouse that still smelled of lamp oil and other people's bribes—and considered the three red pins.

The first pin sat on the Nightmarket. The second on the drowned quarter's edge, where a smuggler named Sefa Idrin kept a boat full of lies. The third was a guess, and Voss hated guesses the way some people hated spiders: not because they were dangerous, exactly, but because they moved when you weren't looking.

"Report," said Voss, without looking up.

Sergeant Emmerich cleared his throat. He was a large man with the face of a patient ox and the soul of a slightly less patient ox, and he was the only officer in the Ministry whom Voss trusted further than arm's length, which was to say, to about two arm's lengths.

"The courier's body turned up in the cistern outflow," Emmerich said. "Dead before he hit the water. Throat cut by someone who knew what they were doing."

"Rebels?"

"Or our own people tidying up. Hard to say." He shifted his weight. "And there's this." He set a small object on the map table, next to the pin marking the Nightmarket.

It was a charm. A luck-knot, braided cord, cheap-looking, the kind sold by the hundred in the market stalls for a copper each. But the knotwork was wrong—or rather, right—in a way that made something old and cold turn over in Voss's chest.

"Where did you get this?" Voss asked, and their voice came out quieter than they intended.

"A fishwife. Down by the harbor stairs. She said it kept her husband's nets from tangling." Emmerich shrugged. "Superstition. Except—" he hesitated, "—the nets *don't* tangle. Her neighbors swear to it. Three years running."

Voss picked up the charm. It was warm. Not sun-warm. *Held*-warm, as though someone had been carrying it against their skin, worrying it between finger and thumb, the way a person worries a thing they cannot afford to lose.

Charmwright work. Real work. Twelve years after the Purge, twelve years after the last guild hall burned and the last master was dragged out of his study by men who wore the same grey coat Voss wore now—and here was a knot of braided cord doing honest magic in a fishwife's pocket.

Someone had survived. Someone had stayed.

Voss knew, in the way one knows things one has spent a lifetime refusing to know, exactly who.

* * *

Here is what the Ministry believed about Inquisitor Halle Voss: that they were the Ministry's sharpest instrument, that they had no family and wanted none, that they had risen from nothing through sheer cold competence, and that they felt nothing about the Charmwrights' Guild except professional satisfaction at its extinction.

Three of those things were even true.

Voss did not remember their mother's face. They remembered her hands—ink-stained, quick, always folding something, paper or cloth or light itself, because Voss had been four years old and four-year-olds do not distinguish between a conjurer's flourish and a mother's fingers. They remembered the smell of the guild nursery: chalk dust and beeswax and the particular sweetness of a warming-stone on a winter morning. They remembered being happy, which was the cruelest part. Children taken in the Purge were supposed to have been rescued from *corruption*, and corruption was not supposed to feel like being lifted onto a counter so you could watch your mother braid light into a knot.

Then the grey coats came, and the nursery door broke inward, and a woman with a kind voice and dead eyes knelt down and said, *You're safe now. We're taking you somewhere safe.*

Voss had learned, over thirty years, to stop asking safe from what.

The Ministry raised its foundlings carefully. No names from before. No objects from before. Lessons in doctrine, in cipher-work, in the fifty ways a charm can kill and the one way it can save—which is never, the instructors said, worth the risk. And when young Halle showed an aptitude for sensing resonance—the little shiver behind the eyes that told you magic had passed through a room like a draft under a door—they did not praise it. They refined it. They put the gift in harness and called it vigilance, and gave it a badge, and taught it that everything it had loved was a disease it had been mercifully cured of.

For thirty years, Voss had believed them.

Mostly.

* * *

"You're staring at that knot like it owes you money," Emmerich observed.

"It owes me answers." Voss set it down. "There's a charmwright alive in this city. Guild-trained. Working small, staying invisible. And now a courier is dead carrying guild cipher toward the undercity, and a rebel cell is moving, and I want to know how all of it fits together before anyone else burns anything down."

"That's a lot of wanting for one morning."

"Yes," said Voss. "I find I'm greedy lately."

Emmerich studied them with the ox-like patience that Voss privately valued more than any amount of brilliance. "You've been different since the courier report," he said. "Tighter. Like a wire someone's tuning."

"I'm always tight, Sergeant. It's why I don't rattle."

But it was true, and they both knew it. Something had come loose in Voss these past weeks, some pin worked free of its cloth, and the thing underneath was showing through. It had started with the fishwife's charm. Or perhaps it had started earlier—with the file Voss had pulled three months ago and should never have pulled: the intake records from the Purge, the lists of children processed through the Ministry's foundling houses. Their own name was there, of course. *Halle Voss, age 4, no kin claimed.* But so were others. So many others. And beside two of the names, in a clerk's faded hand, a note: *guild nursery, Southwall.*

Southwall. Where the guild's youngest had been kept. Where the founders' own children had learned their letters among warming-stones and singing doors.

Two names. Two children who might remember what Voss remembered. Who might, if found, if questioned, if broken open gently enough, confirm that the thing in Voss's chest—the warmth, the grief, the terrible homesickness for a house that had been ash for thirty years—was real, and shared, and not a corruption to be cured but simply a loss to be carried.

Voss had not yet gone looking for them. That was the line, and Voss knew it precisely. Reading files was diligence. Hunting for one's own reflection was treason.

But the Choir—

Ah. The Choir.

* * *

The Choir was in the deep files, the ones that required a Director's seal, and Voss had read them eleven years ago during the Meridian affair, and had thought about them roughly every day since.

The founders had sealed a living song beneath the city. Half creation, half annihilation—the documents were maddeningly vague, written by people who had seen it and could only describe it sideways, the way you describe the sun. The guild had guarded it for generations. And then, near the end, the guild had done something the Ministry's histories conveniently omitted: they had fed the Purge its names. Had given up their own members, their own students, their own *children*, to keep the Ministry's eyes fixed on witches and heretics while the vault stayed buried and forgotten.

Voss had been one of the children given up. Voss had spent thirty years being grateful for it.

And now a courier had died carrying a map to the vault, and a rebel cell wanted the song as a weapon, and somewhere in the Nightmarket a charmwright with a survivor's caution was selling luck-knots to fishwives, and every faction in the city was converging on the same buried door.

The rebels would loose the song to break the Ministry. The Ministry hardliners—Director Crane and his hawks—would seize it to make certain no one else ever could, and would call the resulting arsenal *stability*. Either way, Silas—no. Either way, *the song* came up into the light. Either way, the thing that had slept under Vell since before Vell had streets woke up with its throat full of creation and annihilation, and everyone who touched it believed themselves the exception.

Voss had watched believers all their life. They had arrested most of them.

No. There was a third option, and it was the option nobody wanted, which was how Voss knew it was correct: the song should be silenced. Sealed forever, or better, unmade—not weaponized, not liberated, not studied. Quiet. The way a grave is quiet. The way a nursery is quiet, afterward.

And if the lower quarters burned in the process—if the flooded district and the smugglers' warrens and the Nightmarket itself went into the fire to keep the vault out of every grasping hand—then that was arithmetic. Voss had learned arithmetic from the Ministry, and the Ministry's sums always balanced, provided you counted people as units.

Voss had gotten very good at not counting themselves among the units.

Until recently.

* * *

"Crane wants an update," Emmerich said. "He's asking why we haven't moved on the Nightmarket stall yet."

"What did you tell him?"

"That we're building the case."

"And what will you tell him tomorrow?"

Voss looked at the map. At the third pin, the guess, sitting on the edge of the drowned quarter where the old bathhouses stood shuttered and condemned. The courier's route, reconstructed from a dead man's boot-mud and a paid informer's memory, bent toward Southwall. Toward the guild quarter. Toward home, said a voice in Voss's head that they did not permit to have opinions.

"Tell him," Voss said slowly, "that we move tonight."

Emmerich blinked. "Tonight? You said build the case—"

"The case is built. A living charmwright. A murdered courier. Rebel activity in the underways. Crane will want the arrest himself, with trumpets, and if he gets to the charmwright first, he'll squeeze them until they lead us to the vault, and then he'll put the song in a box with a Ministry ribbon on it and call it the triumph of order." Voss straightened. "I intend to reach the vault first. And then I intend to make very sure nothing inside it ever comes out."

"That's not going to make Crane happy."

"Crane's happiness is not among my responsibilities." Voss took their coat from the hook—grey wool, Ministry-cut, worn soft at the collar from thirty years of putting it on—and paused with one arm through the sleeve.

"Sergeant. If I give you an order in the next few days that seems... excessive. Regarding fire, regarding the lower quarters." They met Emmerich's steady ox-gaze. "You'll carry it out?"

Emmerich was quiet a moment. "If it's lawful," he said. "That's the oath. Lawful and necessary. You taught me that yourself, sir. You said an order that fails either test is just cruelty wearing a uniform."

Had they said that? They must have. It sounded like the person the Ministry had built, the good instrument, the cured child. Voss found they could barely recognize the words anymore, as if spoken by a stranger across a burning room.

"Lawful and necessary," Voss agreed. "Of course."

* * *

The Nightmarket at dusk was all smoke and shouting and fish-gut shine, and Voss walked it in plain clothes with their hood up, feeling the crowd part around them without knowing why. Instruments always made crowds uneasy. Dogs too. A dog had once growled at Voss for a full minute outside a bakery, and Voss had stood there and let it, because the dog was only doing what Voss's whole career asked of it: smelling the wrongness and naming it.

The charmwright's stall was easy to find once you stopped looking for magic and started looking for care. Third row, near the lamp-seller's corner: trinkets, ribbons, brass buttons, a tray of braided cords. And behind the tray, a figure with ink-stained hands and a survivor's slouch, haggling with a boy over a whistle with the weary patience of someone who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more.

Ansel Dunmore. Last apprentice of the Charmwrights' Guild. Twelve years invisible.

Voss stood in the shadow of the lamp-seller's awning and watched them, and felt the resonance shiver behind their eyes—that old draft-under-the-door sense—and confirmed what the fishwife's charm had already whispered. The stall hummed, very faintly. Warded. Gently, skillfully, lovingly warded, the way you'd ward a thing you wanted to protect rather than a thing you wanted to hide.

*Mother's hands,* Voss thought, and hated themselves for it.

Because here was the unbearable symmetry, the thing that had been tightening around Voss's ribs for weeks now: Ansel Dunmore was what Voss might have been. Taken at four instead of left behind. Raised by the guild instead of the Ministry. Same aptitude, same instinct, same twelve years of survival—one hidden in plain sight selling knots, one hidden in plain sight wearing a badge. Two children from the same burning house, carried out different doors.

One of them had built a family out of fishwives and fences and stubbornness. One of them had built a career out of arithmetic.

And tomorrow night, Voss was going to burn the lower quarters down around both of them, because it was lawful and necessary, and because the alternative was letting the song wake up, and because Voss had run out of other options approximately thirty years ago and had only just now stopped pretending otherwise.

The boy bought his whistle. Ansel smiled at him—warm, tired, real—and Voss turned away before the smile could do whatever smiles did to instruments.

"Watch the stall," they murmured to the shadow where a watcher ought to be. "Don't touch him. When we move, we move on my word alone."

* * *

That night, in the field office below the street, Voss brewed the bad tea and drank it standing up, and laid the fishwife's luck-knot on the map table beside the three red pins, and allowed themselves, for exactly the length of time it took the tea to cool, to remember warming-stones and beeswax and a woman's ink-stained hands folding light.

Then the tea was cold, and the remembering was over, and Voss rang for the sergeant.

"Signal Crane's office," they said. "Full operation authorization. Flood teams at the cistern mouths, fire details staged at the drowned quarter, and a squad at the old Southwall bathhouse—I want it surrounded within the hour, quietly, before our charmwright's friends can scatter." They pinned the third red pin firmly into the map, converting a guess into a fact by the simple Ministry method of committing to it. "And Sergeant—if anyone asks what we're burning for, tell them the truth. Tell them we're going to make the city quiet."

Emmerich lingered at the door. "And if the charmwright's already underground by then? If they've found the vault first?"

Voss looked at the map, at the pins, at the small braided knot lying among them like a heart someone had cut out and left behind.

"Then," said Voss, "we follow them down. And God help whichever of us opens that door first—because I have read the files, Sergeant, and I am beginning to suspect the song does not want to be a weapon at all."

"Then what does it want?"

Voss drained the cold tea and set down the cup.

"To rest," they said. "And I mean to grant it. Even if I have to bury this whole city on top of it."
