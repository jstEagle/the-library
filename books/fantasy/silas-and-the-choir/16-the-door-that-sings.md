## 16. The Door That Sings

The tunnel ended the way all good things in Vell end: abruptly, and with water.

Ansel stood ankle-deep in a channel that had once been somebody's grand idea of drainage and was now mostly mud, breathing air that tasted of wet stone and old iron. Ahead, the passage opened into a vaulted chamber no map had bothered to record—because, Ansel realized slowly, the map hadn't needed to. The room had been waiting to be found.

And it was singing.

Not loudly. Not even, properly speaking, aloud. It was more like the feeling you get when you stand near a struck bell after the sound has supposedly died—the hum living in your teeth, your breastbone, the soles of your feet. Except this hum had *shape*. It rose and fell like breath. It paused where a pause would be kind, and swelled where a heart would ache, and somewhere in its long slow phrase Ansel heard something that sounded unbearably like his master's voice saying *well done, little wren*, twelve years ago and a lifetime away.

"Tell me you hear that too," Sefa said quietly. She had her knife out, which was her way of being comforted.

"I hear it," Wren said. His voice did something strange on the second word, cracked down the middle like old glaze. "I heard it last when I was nineteen years old."

Ansel looked at him. In the grey light of Ansel's own charm-lamp—the last working thing he'd ever made, he thought absurdly, the last thing my hands were ever truly good for—Wren looked older than grief could account for. He'd aged twenty years in twenty days of running. His hands, once steady enough to draw a charm-line finer than a spider's opinion, now trembled at his sides.

"It's the Choir," Wren said. "Or the door to it. The song is sealed behind that wall, but walls are just... suggestions, to a thing like Silas. It leaks. It's been leaking for three hundred years." He laughed, a small broken sound. "We used to say the founders built a vault around a song. We should have said they built a vault around a wound."

*Silas.* The founders' name for it. Ansel had turned the word over every night since Wren first spoke it by candlelight in the ransacked bathhouse, explaining in a low urgent voice what the guild had really sealed away—not a weapon, not a treasure, but a living song, half creation and half unmaking, too heavy for any one voice to carry whole. A song that wanted, as far as anyone could tell, either to finish itself or to be let rest, and no one alive knew which.

Ansel didn't know yet which he intended to find out. He only knew that every road had narrowed to this one, and that behind them, somewhere up in the dark, the Ministry's pumps were still flooding the underways, and ahead—

Ahead, the wall was humming.

It wasn't much to look at, as apocalypses went. A slab of black stone set into the tunnel's end, maybe ten feet high, uncarved, unmarked, wearing a skin of mineral bloom like frost on a window. But when Ansel raised the lamp, the light bent toward it. Not reflected—*leaned*, the way a tired person leans toward a fire.

"There's no handle," Sefa observed.

"There's no need for one," Wren said. "It doesn't open for hands. It opens for—" He stopped. Swallowed. "For whoever it decides is ready."

"Ready for what?"

Wren didn't answer, and Ansel found he didn't want him to. Some part of him already knew. You couldn't grow up an apprentice of the Charmwrights' Guild without absorbing its oldest lesson along with your letters: *magic takes payment, and it always lets you choose the currency, and it never tells you the exchange rate until afterward.*

"Keys first," Ansel said, because practical things were easier than true ones. "You both said the approach kills anyone guild-untrained. We're here. Let's do the keys."

Three keys there were—or near enough. Wren wore his on a cord at his throat, a sliver of white metal shaped like a tuning fork flattened into a pendant; it had been his since before the Purge, and it sang faintly against the chamber's hum, two notes arguing politely. Sefa's had come to her cell through forty years of smuggling and three dead couriers—a disc of green glass that she'd kept, she admitted, as a lucky piece, never knowing what it was lucky *for*. And Ansel's own key had been pressed into his hands by a dying man in a cellar, wrapped in wax and blood, and had spent the whole journey since growing warmer against his sternum, like a small animal burrowing toward home.

They approached the wall together.

The hum changed.

Ansel felt it happen the way you feel weather turn—the pressure shifting, the note deepening, the song becoming aware of them the way a sleeper becomes aware of light through curtains. The black stone's frost-bloom began to move, swirling, gathering, and shapes formed in it. Not pictures. *Doors.* Three doors, sketched in pale mineral, standing open onto darknesses that were somehow darker than the stone around them.

"Oh," said Sefa, in the flat voice of someone who has decided not to be impressed and failed. "It's one of *those* doors."

"The founders weren't sentimental," Wren said. "But they were thorough."

Each door had a figure before it now, faint as breath on glass. Before the leftmost, a woman with a smuggler's shoulders. Before the rightmost, an old man with a tremor in his hands. Before the center one, a slight figure holding a lamp.

Ansel's mouth went dry. "It's testing us."

"It's testing whether we'll walk through our worst fear to reach it," Wren said. "That was the founders' logic. Anyone who comes to Silas wanting power will lie to themselves about their reasons. But nobody lies to the fear. You can't. It knows your true face because it wears it."

Sefa snorted. "My cell has a saying about tests like this. *If a door asks you questions, it's because the answers cost money.*"

"And how much have you got?" Ansel asked.

She looked at the leftmost door—at the shape of herself standing before it—and her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was about to do something brave and refuse to call it that. "Enough," she said. "Watch my back."

She walked into the dark of the drawn door, and the dark took her, and the wall swallowed the sketch of her like water closing over a stone.

* * *

What happened next, Ansel would spend a long time trying to describe, and never quite manage.

There was no passage. There was no time. There was simply a moment when Sefa was gone and then a moment when Sefa was back, stumbling, gasping, soaked in sweat despite the cold, her knife somehow in her hand again though Ansel had watched her leave it sheathed. The frost-door before her position had faded to nothing.

"What did you see?" Wren asked.

"None of your business," Sefa said. Then, quieter, staring at the wall with something like respect: "My brother. Alive. Asking me why I let the Ministry keep his name." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "I told him I didn't let them. I told him I was going to take it back. And the door—" Her voice caught. "The door said that wasn't the same as telling the truth, but it would do. For now."

"That's it?" Ansel said. "It just—lets you pass if you answer honestly?"

"It doesn't let you pass," Wren said softly. "It lets you *proceed*. There's a difference. Ask me how I know."

He stepped forward before Ansel could stop him.

The rightmost door brightened. The sketch of the old man raised his trembling hands, and the frost swirled, and Wren walked into the dark with his chin up like a man walking into a sea he'd been told was shallow.

He lasted longer than Sefa. Long enough that Ansel counted thirty of his own heartbeats, forty, fifty, each one louder than the hum. Long enough to hope.

Then Wren screamed.

It was not a loud scream. That was the worst of it—it was small, and stifled, the scream of a man who has spent twelve years practicing silence, and it came from everywhere in the chamber at once and nowhere at all. The lamp-light guttered. The hum spiked into something that made Ansel's fillings ache and his eyes sting, a chord of pure wrongness, and then—

Then Wren was back, on his knees in the mud, and the rightmost door was not faded but *sealed*, painted over in frost so thick it might never open again, and the wall was humming a new note that Ansel, with the last trained corner of his mind, identified as refusal.

"No," Wren said. He tried to rise. Failed. Tried again. "No—I've carried it for twelve years, I've *paid*, I gave everything, I fed it names, I gave it my—"

"Wren." Ansel knelt beside him. Up close, his master's face was terrible: not weeping, past weeping, arranged into the careful blankness of a man who has just seen himself clearly and wishes very much to stop. "What did it show you?"

"The night of the Purge." Wren's laugh was a horrible thing, thin as wire. "All of it. Every name I wrote down for the Ministry. Every friend I traded to keep the vault secret. It showed me the list, Ansel. It showed me the list, and then it showed me the list with your name on it, and it asked me—" His voice broke entirely. "It asked me if I would write it again. To protect the song. And I said yes. Out loud. In front of it. I said *yes*."

The chamber hummed its low refusing note.

"You were protecting—" Ansel started, the old argument, the one they'd had in the bathhouse, the one that had curdled into betrayal and never quite resolved.

"Don't." Wren held up a hand. "Don't defend me. I've had twelve years to defend myself and I've gotten very good at it, and the door saw through all of it in under a minute. That's what it's *for*." He looked up, and his eyes were clear in a way Ansel had never seen them—clear the way the sky is clear after the smoke blows out. "It doesn't test courage, little wren. Courage is cheap; I've plenty. It tests whether you can look at what you are and still choose. And I looked, and I chose, and my choice was the same cowardice I've always made, dressed in better words."

"Wren—"

"Take the key."

"I'm not leaving you here."

"You're not leaving me anywhere. I'm staying *here*, which is different, and I'll thank you to respect the distinction." Wren fumbled at the cord at his neck. His hands shook so badly it took three tries, and Ansel watched, frozen, guilt and grief and fury all fighting for the same narrow doorway in his chest. "Voss's people are coming. You know they are. The pumps, the flooding—they're driving us like rabbits toward a hole, and this is the hole. Someone has to stand at the threshold and make them think it costs more than it does."

"With what? You have no gift, no charms, nothing but a knife and a bad knee!"

"I have twelve years of practice at looking harmless," Wren said, and smiled, and it was the old smile, the workshop smile, the one that used to mean *watch this, apprentice, and learn something*. "You'd be amazed what a harmless-looking old man can make soldiers hesitate about. Thirty seconds of hesitation is a locked door, in the right corridor. I taught you that."

"You taught me everything except how to say goodbye to you twice," Ansel said, and hated how young his voice sounded.

Wren went still. Then he reached out and pressed the white-metal key into Ansel's palm, folding the younger man's fingers over it with both of his trembling hands, and for a moment the tremor stilled, as if the key's old song were holding them steady.

"When you go in," he said quietly, "it will offer you everything. Whatever you lost. Whoever you miss. It offered me your mother's kitchen once, in a dream, years ago—warm bread, rain on the window, everyone alive. I woke up crying and I've never told a soul." His grip tightened. "Listen to me, Ansel. Listen with the ears I trained. If it offers you the guild back—all of us, whole, the workshops full and the kilns lit—remember that I am *out here*, and I am not in there, and no song can give me back to you honestly, because the man it would give you is not the man I became. Grief isn't a debt, little wren. It's just love with nowhere to put itself. Don't let a door convince you otherwise."

The hum shifted again—softer now, almost gentle, almost sorry—and the center door brightened. The sketch-figure with the lamp stood waiting, and the darkness behind it had changed quality. It wasn't empty anymore. It was *listening*.

Sefa touched Ansel's shoulder. "Company," she said. "Far off. Boots, and a lot of them, and something bigger that squelches."

From far up the flooded tunnel came the sound she'd named: boots, muffled by water, and beneath them a rhythmic mechanical groan that Ansel's bones recognized before his mind did. Ministry pump-engines. They weren't chasing anymore. They were herding.

"They'll reach the bend in four minutes," Sefa said. "Maybe five if we're lucky and their sergeant is stupid."

"Go," Wren said.

Ansel didn't move. Twelve years of practiced invisibility, of small safe trades and paid protection coin and never, ever letting anyone matter enough to lose—and here was the sum total of that arithmetic: one old liar kneeling in the mud, giving away the last thing he owned, smiling about it.

"I mourned you," Ansel said. "Twelve years. I burned incense at your name-day. I told strangers you died well."

"I know. I'm sorry. It was the kindest lie I ever told and I've regretted it every day since, which I believe makes us even for the map." Wren's smile wobbled. "Go sing, little wren. Whatever you decide in there—sing it *yours*. Not theirs. Not mine. Yours."

Ansel stood. Took the lamp in one hand, the key in the other. Walked to the center door, where the frost had opened like a pupil dilating, and the hum rose to meet him, and inside the hum—faint, unmistakable, unbearable—he heard his master's voice saying *well done.*

He stepped through.

Behind him, in the chamber of the singing door, Wren Halloway got slowly to his feet, drew a knife worth perhaps six coppers, and turned to face the sound of the Ministry coming, humming under his breath the first bar of a song no one had sung in three hundred years—loudly enough, he hoped, for the door to hear, and pass along.
