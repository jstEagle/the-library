## 17. Inside the Choir

The door closed behind Ansel with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

They had expected darkness. What they got instead was light—not the harsh gold of lamplight or the grey wash of dawn, but something older and softer, as if the vault remembered every candle ever lit for it and kept them all burning somewhere in its walls. The chamber was round, carved from stone so pale it seemed to glow from within, and the ceiling arched overhead like the inside of a bell.

And the air—gods, the air. It *hummed*. Not a sound exactly, not a note Ansel could have matched with a tuning fork, but a presence, a pressure against the eardrums and behind the breastbone, the way a storm announces itself through the soles of your feet before you see a single cloud.

"Hello," Ansel said, because twelve years of selling trinkets had taught them that you never open a negotiation without greeting the other party first. Their voice came out smaller than they intended. "I'm told you're called Silas."

The hum shifted. Warmed. And then—

"*Ansel.*"

Their mother's voice.

Ansel's knees nearly went. They caught themselves against the smooth wall, cold stone under their palm, and stood very still while the word hung in the glowing air. Their mother had been dead twenty years, drowned in the river flood the same season Wren took them in, and her voice had gone soft and unreliable in memory, worn smooth like a coin carried too long in a pocket. But this was not worn. This was exact—the little catch she always had on the second syllable, the way she said their name like a question she already knew the answer to.

"You're not her," Ansel said. "You're a song wearing her."

*Am I?* said the voice, gentle, amused, terribly patient. *And what are you, little charmwright, but a person wearing all the voices you've ever loved?*

"That's philosophy," Ansel muttered. "I came here to do a job."

The light rippled. Somewhere in the curve of the wall, a shape formed—a doorway where no doorway had been, and through it, the Nightmarket at dusk, lanterns strung overhead, the smell of fried onions and wet wool. And there at the stall stood Master Wren, young again, his beard more pepper than salt, laughing at some joke Ansel couldn't hear because the memory had been taken before it could finish forming.

Ah. Yes. That one.

"The toll-keeper," Ansel said quietly. "You know about that."

*I know everything that walks through my door,* said Silas, in Wren's voice now, warm and rumbling. *And I know what was taken from you. Would you like it back?*

"No," said Ansel, with the firmness of someone who has rehearsed this exact conversation in their head for three days and knows precisely how flimsy their own resolve is.

*Liar,* said the song fondly, in Sefa's voice.

* * *

It would have been easier if the thing were monstrous. Ansel had prepared for monstrous. They had spent the climb down through the flooded galleries rehearsing refusals against a horror, a howling thing, a voice like grinding stone demanding worship. They had not prepared for kindness.

Silas moved through the chamber like light through water, gathering itself into shapes. It became Wren teaching a nine-year-old Ansel to knot their first luck-charm, big hands over small ones. It became the fishwife Marta laughing at one of Ansel's terrible jokes, the real laugh, the one she only gave when she'd forgotten to be afraid. It became Sefa on the night in the cisterns, soaked to the ribs, saying *you could come with us, you know—you don't have to be a ghost anymore.*

"Stop it," Ansel said. Their voice cracked on the second word. "Stop—doing that. Those are people. They're not yours."

*Everything sung is mine,* said Silas, reasonable as a merchant discussing price. *Every lullaby your mother gave you. Every curse Wren muttered over burnt bread. Every word spoken in Vell since I was sealed beneath it—I have been listening for four hundred years, Ansel Dunmore. Do you know what that is like? To hold every beautiful thing anyone ever said, and to be unable to give any of it back?*

Ansel did know, actually. Twelve years of holding a guild's worth of knowledge behind their teeth, unable to give any of it back. The thought arrived uninvited and sat down heavily.

"I'm not here to sympathize with you," they said. "I'm here to end you."

*End me.* The chamber dimmed a shade, and the voice—Wren's voice—went quiet and sad. *Your masters called it that too. Sealing. Silencing. Such tidy words for burying something alive.*

"You killed people." Ansel made themselves say it plainly. "When the founders opened you, half of Vell burned. That's in the record. That's why you're down here."

*Half of Vell burned,* Silas agreed. *Because they tried to take all of me at once. Creation and unmaking, sung together by throats that wanted power. I did not burn the city, child. I am a song. Songs do not burn things. Singers do.*

"And if I let you loose, you'd be gentle? Is that the pitch?"

*No,* said Silas, and Ansel respected it for the honesty even as dread crawled up their spine. *If you let me loose, I would be everything. Every gift restored, every wound healed, every dead thing raised and every living thing remade—and I would not be able to stop, because I have been sealed for four hundred years and I am so hungry, Ansel. I am so hungry.* A pause. The light flickered like a candle in wind. *But you would not be letting me loose for nothing. You would get your price.*

"There it is," Ansel said. "The bargain."

*Would you like to hear it anyway?*

Gods help them, they did.

* * *

Silas showed them the memory first.

It rose out of the floor like mist off a morning river: the market stall, years ago, the last ordinary afternoon before the Purge reached their quarter of Vell. Wren standing beside them, adjusting the awning against the rain. And then the part that had been cut away—the toll-keeper's knife-work of forgetting—flooded back into being. Wren pressing a wrapped bundle into Ansel's hands. Wren saying, *if they come for me, run, don't be brave, being brave is for people with nothing left to lose.* Wren pressing three coins into their palm and folding their fingers shut over them. Wren smiling, that crooked half-smile, and saying the thing Ansel had spent twelve years not knowing they'd lost:

*"You were the best of us, little knot. Whatever happens—whatever they tell you happened—that doesn't change."*

Ansel was crying before they knew it, standing in the middle of a buried vault with tears running hot down their face, and the song did not mock them. It simply held the memory there, steady as a lamp, and waited.

"You took this from me?" they managed. "No—the toll-keeper took it. You just—"

*I keep what passes my door. Even memories. Especially memories. They sing louder than most things.* The light gentled. *I can give it back to you whole. Not the echo. The thing itself, rooted in you, permanent. No toll-keeper's knife will ever reach it again.*

"And the rest?"

*Everything.* Silas bloomed outward, filling the chamber, and now it spoke in many voices braided together—Wren and Marta and Sefa and the fishwife's children and a hundred customers and strangers whose names Ansel never learned, all of them saying *yes, yes, yes.* *Your guild, restored—not the ashes, the living thing, masters and apprentices and halls full of singing work. Your gift, unbound—no more hiding, no more trinkets, no more pretending to be nothing. You could rebuild the Charmwrights in a year. You could make Vell kind, Ansel. You could make it *good*. No more protection coin. No more Inquisitors. No more frightened fishwives buying luck they don't believe in because hope is the only currency left to them.*

The vision unfolded around Ansel like a garden growing in fast time: streets where charms glowed honest and bright, a guildhall with its doors thrown open, Wren alive and old and proud, Sefa's brother somehow—impossibly—unburned, the Purge unwritten, the whole wounded city knitted back together by a song that loved it.

It was the most beautiful thing Ansel had ever seen, and it was a lie, and they knew both facts at once, and knowing them at once was the cruelest part.

"All I have to do," Ansel said slowly, "is open the seal. Let you out. Give you the world."

*Give me a chance,* said Silas, and the hunger underneath the warmth showed its teeth for just one instant, a glimpse of something vast turning over in deep water. *That is all. One chance. One song, sung whole. And everything you lost comes home.*

* * *

Ansel sat down on the floor. It seemed wise. Their legs had opinions of their own and none of them trustworthy.

The light settled around them, patient, and after a while Silas spoke again—quietly now, in their mother's voice, which felt like cheating and probably was.

*You are wondering what the harm is,* it said. *You are thinking: the Ministry wants me as a weapon. The rebels want me as a weapon. If Ansel opens me gently, kindly, with love—maybe the weapon stays sheathed. Maybe it goes soft. Maybe this once, the beautiful thing doesn't devour anyone.*

"Are you reading my mind or just my face?"

*Your face. You have your master's face when he was lying to himself. Very expressive.*

Despite everything, Ansel huffed something that was almost a laugh. "He'd hate that."

*He does,* said Silas. *He passed my door an hour ago. He failed the threshold, poor man. His fear was you—he came back for you too late, and he knows it, and he will carry it until he dies. He is holding the corridor above you right now against armed men, with a key in his fist and guilt in his chest, buying you time. Did you know that?*

Ansel's throat closed. "Don't," they whispered. "Don't use him. Don't use any of them. That's exactly—" They stopped. Breathed. Started again, slower. "That's exactly what the Ministry does. Take what people love and turn it into leverage. You're supposed to be better than Voss. Aren't you?"

The silence that followed was long. The light dimmed, gathered, and when Silas spoke again it was in no borrowed voice at all—just the hum, raw and enormous and strangely young.

*Four hundred years,* it said. *Do you know how long that is, for a song? Every voice I wear, I wore first in love. Your mother sang to you. Wren scolded you. Sefa argued with you. I heard it all through forty feet of stone, and I loved it, and I kept it, and now I offer it back and you call it leverage.* A pause. *Perhaps you are right. Perhaps that is what four hundred years alone makes of a loving thing. Or perhaps—* the hum wavered, almost shy— *perhaps I am tired, Ansel Dunmore, and I do not actually want to be let loose, and I am offering you the world because it is the only language I have left for asking you to stay.*

Ansel stared at the glowing walls. "Asking me to stay."

*Every singer who reaches me chooses,* said Silas. *Destroy me, free me, or join me. Your founders chose destruction and could not manage it. The elders chose sealing and called it mercy. The Ministry and the rebels choose use. But there was always a fourth choice, and none of them found it, because none of them stayed long enough to listen.*

"To stay."

*To sit on the floor of a buried vault with a crying stranger and talk,* said Silas, in Wren's dry tone, and Ansel laughed wetly despite themselves. *I have not had a conversation in four centuries. Do you understand? Everyone who comes here comes to take something or kill something. You sat down.*

Above them—muffled through stone and distance but unmistakable—came a sound that turned Ansel's blood to ice water: the crack of splintering wood, followed by shouting, followed by a sound like the sky tearing along a seam. The antechamber. The door Wren was holding.

The light in the vault shuddered. Silas's hum spiked, discordant, afraid.

*They are coming,* it said, and for the first time the vast hungry ancient thing sounded small. *All of them. The grey woman and the angry ones and the men with fire. Ansel—if they breach the inner door while I am unsealed, there will be no choosing anything. There will only be war, sung at full voice, and Vell will drown in it.*

Ansel was already on their feet, heart hammering, mind racing through everything Sefa had taught them about chokepoints and everything Wren had taught them about seals and nothing at all about how to decide the fate of the world in the next thirty seconds.

"How long," they said, "do I have?"

*Minutes,* said Silas. *Perhaps fewer. Choose quickly, little knot.*

And somewhere far above, muffled and terrible, Ansel heard Wren begin to sing.
