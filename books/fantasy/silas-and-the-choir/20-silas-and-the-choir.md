## 20. Silas and the Choir

The song knew Ansel's name before they opened their mouth.

That was the first thing to understand about it, and the last thing anyone would believe: it was not a force. It was not a weapon lying in a vault like a sword on a rack, waiting for a hand bold enough or foolish enough to lift it. It was *listening*. It had been listening for three hundred years, down here beneath the roots of Vell, in a chamber shaped like the inside of a bell, and when Ansel Dunmore finally stood before it—boots wet from the flooded antechamber, ears still ringing with the sounds of Sefa holding a corridor alone behind them—it turned its attention upon them the way a dog turns toward the door at the sound of a familiar step.

*Silas,* said the song, in Wren's voice.

Ansel's breath caught. "Don't do that."

*Silas,* it said again, patient, in their mother's voice this time—a voice they had not heard since they were six, a voice they had not known they remembered until it filled the chamber like water filling a cup.

"Stop," Ansel whispered. "Please."

It stopped. That was the second thing to understand. It could be asked.

The chamber was round and vast and utterly plain, which was somehow worse than grandeur would have been. No treasure. No throne of bones. Just pale stone worn smooth as river rock, and in the center of the floor a seam—a hairline crack in the world, glowing faintly gold, breathing. In. Out. Three hundred years of breathing, sealed under a lid that the founders had called a vault and the Ministry had called an arsenal and the rebels had called a key, and which had been, all along, none of those things.

It had been a blanket. A very heavy one, thrown over something that wept.

Ansel knelt at the edge of the seam. Their hands were shaking. Not from fear—or not only from fear—but from the effort of holding themselves together, because the song was offering now, as it had offered through the door, as it had been offering to every guild-trained soul who ever came close enough to hear it clearly.

*I can give them back,* it murmured, in a voice like warm bread, like lamplight, like every kind thing anyone had ever said to them compressed into a single tone. *Your master's kindness. The memory you paid at the toll gate—I kept a copy. I keep copies of everything. I can give you back your gift, your guild, your twelve years. I can make Vell remember what it forgot. I can wake every sleeping thing and mend every broken thing and no one will ever have to lose anything again.*

"That's a lie," Ansel said quietly. "You know it's a lie."

A pause. The seam pulsed.

*Yes,* the song admitted, and its voice cracked, and Ansel felt the crack go through their own chest like a hairline fracture in old porcelain. *But oh, how I wish it weren't.*

* * *

They had learned the word *Silas* from Wren, years ago, in another life—the life before the Purge, when Ansel was small and the workshop above the tannery smelled of beeswax and hot copper. They had asked why the elders never spoke the founders' names, and old Master Wren—young then, whole then, laughing then—had gone quiet in a way that frightened the child Ansel, and finally said:

"The founders didn't have names, little wren. Or rather—they had one name between them. When they sealed the Choir, they gave up everything else. Their names went into the seal. Silas is what's left. It isn't a person. It's—" and he had struggled, young Wren, brilliant Wren, groping for words the way he'd later grope for forgiveness, "—it's what you get when you put every grief in the world into one throat and ask it to sing."

Child-Ansel had nodded solemnly and understood nothing, and gone back to winding luck-knots.

Adult-Ansel, kneeling on cold stone while a war assembled itself somewhere overhead, understood completely.

The song wasn't evil. That was the tragedy no faction had ever wanted to hear. The Ministry wanted it caged because caged things couldn't hurt them. The rebels wanted it loosed because loosed things could hurt their enemies. And the song itself—poor, vast, grieving Silas—wanted only what every sealed thing wants. It wanted out. It wanted to stop hurting. And it did not understand—not truly, not in the way of minds—that its waking would unmake the world, the way a flood does not understand the village.

Every voice it wore, it wore because someone had loved someone once, and the song had caught the echo. It was made of echoes. It was the loneliest thing Ansel had ever met, and they had spent twelve years being the loneliest person in Vell, so they knew the species.

"I'm going to sing with you," Ansel said.

The chamber stilled. Even the seam seemed to hold its breath.

"Not open. Not loose. Not cage you deeper." Ansel settled cross-legged on the stone, the way Wren had taught them to sit for the long exercises, spine straight, hands open on their knees. "I'm going to sing you *down*. To sleep. Real sleep. The kind where nothing hurts." They swallowed. "It'll cost me everything I've got left, which isn't much, so don't laugh."

The song did not laugh. The song, in a voice that was suddenly, terribly small—smaller than a child's, smaller than the child Ansel had been—asked:

*Will it work?*

"No idea," Ansel said honestly. "But my master always said the guild's first law was that magic serves the singer, never the other way around. So I'm the singer. And I say we rest."

And far away, faintly, through stone and water and three hundred years of silence, Ansel heard Sefa's voice raised in the corridor—hoarse, defiant, buying seconds—and thought: *all right. Quickly, then.*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about singing a god to sleep: it is not dramatic.

There was no thunder. There was no light show. There was Ansel Dunmore, sitting cross-legged in the dark, doing the most difficult thing they had ever done, which was the same thing they'd been doing their whole life—staying calm, staying steady, staying *themselves*—except now the stakes were everything, and the instrument was their own small gift, the latent ember they'd hidden for twelve years under trinkets and haggling and lies.

They began the way Wren had taught all his apprentices: with the grounding hum. Low. Almost inaudible. The note you find in your own chest and hold like a handrail in a storm.

The song answered immediately, eagerly, rising to meet them in a hundred beloved voices, and Ansel felt the pull of it like a riptide—*come, come, let me carry you, let me give you back your mother, let me give you back the smell of beeswax, let me—*

"Gently," Ansel murmured, and poured the hum into it.

Not fighting. That was the trick, if there was a trick. You cannot fight a flood. But you can sing to rain. Ansel took their tiny gift—the warmth in the luck-knots, the whisper of finding in the charms, the little domestic magics that had kept them fed and invisible—and gave it away, note by note, into the song. And the song, starved for gentleness, drank.

It began, slowly, to quiet.

Not all at once. Grief does not work that way; Ansel knew that too. It came in waves. The song would settle, almost sleeping, and then surge up again—*wait, wait, I haven't shown you everything, I can bring back your masters, I can rebuild the Guildhall stone by stone, I can undo the Purge, I can unburn the names*—and each time it surged, Ansel's heart broke a little more, and each time they answered the only way they could:

"I know. I know you could. But the world isn't a thing you fix, Silas. It's a thing you live in. Even broken. *Especially* broken."

*Why?* the song cried, in the voice of every founder at once, and the chamber shook with it, dust sifting from the ceiling, the seam flaring gold-white. *WHY SHOULD THEY GET TO LIVE IN IT AND NOT ME?*

And Ansel, tears streaming freely now, hands trembling on their knees, gift guttering low like a candle in its last inch, said the truest thing they had ever said:

"Because someone has to stay awake and remember you kindly."

The song stopped.

The silence after was enormous. Ansel sat in it, emptying fast, feeling their gift drain out of them like heat from cooling iron, and waited.

Then Silas—the song, the grief, the buried choir of every echo ever loved—began, very softly, to sing along.

* * *

They sang together for a long time. How long, Ansel would never know. It might have been minutes. It might have been the better part of a night. The song took Ansel's small melodies and wrapped them in its vast ones, and Ansel took the song's vast grief and held it in their small arms, and between them they made something that had no name but sounded, to Ansel's fading senses, like a lullaby sung over a cradle by someone who means it.

And as they sang, the song gave back—not the bargains it had offered, not the stolen mother or the rebuilt guild, but small things, free things, parting gifts pressed into Ansel's hands like coins slipped to a friend at a door:

It gave them the memory of Wren's kindness, returned whole—the toll-keeper's theft undone, or perhaps simply forgiven; the song kept copies of everything, and some things it refused to hoard. Ansel felt it click back into place: a winter morning, flour on Wren's sleeves, the old man burning the porridge *again* and blaming the stove, and laughing, and teaching eight-year-old Ansel the grounding hum right there in the smoke because the boy had been frightened by thunder and Wren had known that fear is just power looking for a shape.

"Oh," Ansel sobbed, mid-note, and did not stop singing. "Oh, you *trickster*. Thank you."

The song dimmed. The seam's glow softened from gold to amber to the deep, warm red of a banked fire. Around the chamber, the smooth stone began—very gently—to close, like a hand relaxing, like an eye closing, like a bell going silent after the last vibration leaves the metal.

And the last thing Silas said, in a voice that was no longer any borrowed voice at all but its own—tired, and young, and grateful—was:

*Tell them I wasn't angry.*

"I'll tell them," Ansel promised. "Everyone. Anyone who'll listen."

*Tell them it wasn't their fault either.*

And then the Choir slept. Truly slept. Ansel felt it go the way you feel a fever break—the release, the stillness, the terrible ordinary quiet—and felt the last of their own gift go with it, pouring out across the dark like light leaving a lamp, spreading up through stone and soil and cellar and street, out into the sleeping city of Vell.

Magic guttered out across Vell that night like candles pinched one by one. Every charm went dull. Every ward went blind. Every hidden thing the Ministry's instruments tracked simply... stopped being trackable. Somewhere above, Ansel would learn later, Inquisitors stood in their offices watching their scrying bowls fill with ordinary water, and rebel hardliners stared at weapons that were suddenly only weapons, and the whole rotten beautiful machinery of power ground toward silence with a sound like a city exhaling.

Ansel did not witness any of it. Ansel was busy falling over.

* * *

The last thing they saw, before the dark took them, was the door.

Sefa came through it—bloody, limping, ash-streaked, having held a corridor against two armies with a smuggler's stubbornness and a knife and, in the end, nothing magical at all—and found Ansel crumpled on the smooth stone beside a seam that glowed no brighter than a dying coal.

"Ansel." She was across the chamber fast for a limping woman. She dropped to her knees, pressed fingers to their throat, and found—thin, stubborn, absurdly persistent—a pulse. "Of course," she said, half laughing, half crying, to nobody. "Of course you're alive. Twelve years invisible. You'd survive the end of the world out of sheer habit."

Ansel's eyes fluttered. Their lips moved. Sefa leaned close.

"Did it work?" they whispered. "Is it—did I—"

"It worked," Sefa said. "Whatever you did, it's done. Everything's gone quiet. The whole city. I've never heard anything like it." Her voice cracked. "It's the quietest I've ever heard Vell. It's the quietest I've ever heard *anything*."

Ansel smiled—a small, exhausted, thoroughly self-satisfied smile, the smile of a trinket-seller who has finally closed a sale—and managed four more words before the dark folded over them like a blanket, like a seal, like sleep:

"Told you. Harmless trinkets."

And then they were gone into it, and Sefa gathered them up—light, far too light, lighter than a person should be—and carried them out of the sleeping Choir, past the corridor where the fighting had stopped as if cut off mid-word, past soldiers and rebels standing frozen in the sudden silence, all of them listening to a city that had, for the first time in living memory, nothing left to hide.

Behind them, deep beneath the roots of Vell, Silas dreamed.

And somewhere in that dream—though no one would ever prove it, though Ansel would spend the rest of their life half-believing they'd imagined it—a small voice that was not quite magic and not quite memory and not quite gone kept watch, the way a lighthouse keeps watch, the way a friend keeps watch, humming one low, gentle, grounding note.

Waiting, without impatience, for spring.
