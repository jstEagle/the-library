## 21. Aftermath in Grey

The first thing Sefa learned about keeping vigil was that it was mostly sitting.

Nobody warned her about that part. In the smuggler's trade you learned waiting—waiting for tide, for patrol rotations, for a buyer who'd promised to come at midnight and came at dawn instead—but vigil was a different animal. Vigil was watching a person breathe and counting the breaths and hating yourself for counting them, because counting meant you were afraid to stop.

Ansel had been breathing for six days now. Small breaths, shallow as a fishwife's coin purse, but steady. Steady was the word Sefa clung to like a rail on a storm-tossed deck.

She sat in the back room of Wren's bathhouse—or what remained of the bathhouse, which was not much: a scorched wall, a cracked cistern, and a cellar that had somehow survived everything above it, including three Ministry raids, one rebel mutiny, and the night the magic went out of the world like a lamp being pinched between wet fingers.

"You should eat," said Tam, from the doorway. He was the youngest of the cell that hadn't defected with her—the only one, really, who'd stayed when she walked out of the hardliners' camp with an old man's arm over her shoulder and no plan worth the name. He was seventeen and had the permanent expression of someone who'd signed up for revolution and gotten logistics instead.

"I'm eating," Sefa said, holding up the heel of bread she'd been gnawing for the better part of an hour without noticing.

"That's the same bread from yesterday."

"It ages well."

Tam looked at Ansel—at the grey face, the hands laid flat on the blanket like something set down carefully by a stranger—and he didn't say anything else. He just left more bread and went back upstairs to argue with the roofers about slate. Because that was the other thing nobody told you about the world ending: afterward, there were roofs to fix.

* * *

On the second day, Vell had noticed its magic was gone.

Sefa had gone out for water and come back with news instead, because that was her nature; she could no more walk through a city without collecting gossip than a magnet could walk through iron filings without collecting those. She stood in the cellar doorway and delivered it the way she delivered everything, fast and sideways, like tossing contraband over a fence.

"The Ministry's in pieces," she said. "Half the Inquisitors' authority rested on charm-detection—you can't sniff out sorcery when there isn't any, and half their warrants are suddenly just paper with rude words on them. The clerks are burning records. Not out of shame, mind, out of self-preservation. Every conviction signed 'by divination of charmwork' is worthless now, and there are a lot of angry families doing arithmetic."

"And Voss?" Ansel hadn't asked. Couldn't ask. But Sefa had watched the name land on them every time she mentioned the Ministry, the way a dropped stone lands in still water, so she kept mentioning it, small doses, medicine administered on schedule.

"Alive." She turned the cup in her hands. "They pulled her out of the vault on a litter, both legs broken, one of them badly. The surgeons say she'll walk again, but she'll walk like winter—slowly, and complaining."

Something moved behind Ansel's eyes. Not quite relief. Not quite grief. Something in between that didn't have a name yet, and might never.

"She let us go," Ansel said. It wasn't the first time they'd said it either. They kept saying it like they were testing whether the words would spoil.

"She did. Stood her whole guard down at the tunnel mouth, on a broken leg, held up between two soldiers, and told us to take you home." Sefa shrugged, though nothing about it felt shrug-able. "I've been turning it over ever since. Best I can figure: she spent her whole life chasing the Choir because she thought silencing it would make her whole. And then you actually silenced it, and she watched it happen, and whatever she was missing—" Sefa gestured vaguely at her own chest, "—it didn't get filled. So now she knows that, and knowing it is the closest thing to mercy she's ever been given. You can't arrest somebody out of that kind of understanding."

Ansel was quiet a long moment. Then: "She was one of us. A guild child. Taken in the Purge."

"I know."

"She could have—"

"She could have been you," Sefa said gently. "Yes. I've done that arithmetic too. It doesn't come out clean no matter how you arrange it."

That was the thing about the aftermath, Sefa was learning. Everyone wanted a verdict. The city wanted heroes and villains, tidy columns, names under headings. But what she'd seen in the antechamber of the Choir was messier than any ledger: rebels who'd wanted a weapon weeping when it died; an Inquisitor ordering her soldiers to stand down with blood in her mouth; an old man staying behind to hold a door he couldn't pass, singing guild lullabies at armed men until they hesitated long enough.

Wren had survived, barely—dragged out by Ministry soldiers who'd found him disarming rather than threatening, an old man with his hands up and tears on his face, saying *thank you, thank you* to the people sent to bury him. He was two rooms away now, sleeping as much as Ansel slept, which was to say badly and often interrupted. The two of them had not yet managed a conversation longer than four sentences. Sefa had decided this was fine. Some conversations needed to cure, like cheese.

* * *

On the fourth day, Ansel woke properly.

Sefa knew it before she saw it—a change in the quality of the silence, the way you know rain is coming by the smell. She looked up from mending a net she had no use for (she'd taken up pointless handcrafts during the vigil; it was that or talk to herself) and found grey eyes open and fixed on the ceiling with an expression of profound grievance.

"You're awake," she said, brilliantly.

"Am I?" Ansel's voice was gravel and rust. "Could have fooled me. I feel like I was buried and dug up by someone in a hurry."

"Six days."

"Six—" Ansel tried to sit up, discovered that their body had opinions about this, and subsided. "What happened to the city?"

"Still standing. Quieter." Sefa poured water and held the cup while they drank, because their hands shook. "The Nightmarket's reopened. Fishwives are selling actual fish. There's a man on Copperlane who swears he can still do charms and has attracted a crowd of loyal idiots. The Ministry's arresting him tomorrow, probably, but honestly? Let him have his week. Half the city wants to believe."

Ansel closed their eyes. When they opened them again, they were wet, and they didn't wipe them, and Sefa pretended very hard to be interested in her net.

"My gift's gone," Ansel said. Flat. Fact-finding. "I reached for it. There's nothing. Just—me."

"Just you," Sefa agreed.

"That's supposed to be a comfort?"

"It's supposed to be true. Comfort's extra." She set down the net and took their hand instead, because some things nets couldn't do. "Listen to me, Ansel Dunmore. I spent years wanting the song as a weapon. My brother died in the Purge and I wanted the Ministry broken and I didn't much care what broke along with it. And then I watched you give up the last of your masters' legacy, your own gift, nearly your own life—not to win anything. To make a grieving thing quiet. And I thought: that's the bravest thing I've ever seen, and it looks exactly like losing." Her throat tightened. "So no, I don't know if it's a comfort. But you're alive, and the city's standing, and my brother would have called that a good day's work even if it cost everything. He was sentimental that way. It got him killed, being sentimental. Don't you dare be the same."

"That's a terrible eulogy for your brother."

"He'd have laughed." Sefa blinked hard. "He laughed at terrible eulogies. It was his best quality."

* * *

On the fifth day, Ansel tried to stand, made it halfway, and swore with a fluency that Sefa found genuinely impressive for someone raised by monks of the arcane arts.

"Guild apprentices swore constantly," they said, collapsing back onto the cot. "Master Wren said a charmwright who couldn't curse couldn't channel. Something about the mouth and the will being neighbors."

"Wren's awake too, you know. Asking after you every hour like a mother hen with a guilty conscience."

"Don't." The word came out sharp, and Ansel flinched at their own sharpness. "I'm not—I can't see him yet, Sefa. He fed names to the Purge. He let me believe he was dead for twelve years. He used me as a key because I was the only lockpick left." Their jaw worked. "And he taught me everything. Both of those are true at once and I don't know how to hold them."

"You don't have to hold them yet," Sefa said. "Put them down. They'll keep. That's the one advantage truths have over lies—they don't spoil if you leave them sitting."

Ansel laughed—a small, cracked sound, but real. "When did you get wise?"

"About the same time you got humble. Somewhere around the flooding tunnels, I think. Adversity's a great tutor and charges ruinous fees."

* * *

On the sixth day, Voss came.

Sefa heard the carriage before she saw it—the particular unhurried clatter of a vehicle that had stopped pretending to be urgent—and she was down the stairs and into the street with a knife she had absolutely no intention of using, mostly for posture.

Inquisitor Halle Voss sat in the carriage on a cushioned bench, both legs splinted and propped, looking thinner than Sefa remembered and older, the way stone looks older after rain. No guards. That was the message, and Sefa read it clearly: *this is not an arrest, or if it is, it's a polite one.*

"Smuggler," Voss said, in acknowledgment.

"Inquisitor."

"I'm not, anymore. Or not functionally. The Ministry is dissolving the Office of Inquiry by month's end. There's a vote." Voss's mouth did something that was almost humor. "It turns out an institution built on seeing the unseen cannot survive becoming blind. We're all clerks now. It's very demoralizing."

"If you've come to arrest Ansel—"

"I've come to say a thing I've rehearsed eleven times and will now say badly, so let me." Voss leaned her head back against the carriage frame and closed her eyes briefly, and for a moment she looked less like an Inquisitor and more like what she'd been: a child taken in the Purge, raised on doctrine and ash. "I wanted the Choir silenced my whole life. I told myself it was duty. It was grief wearing duty's coat. And when I lay at the threshold with my legs broken, watching your friend pour themselves out to quiet that song—I understood that I had spent thirty years trying to destroy the thing that could have told me why I hurt." She opened her eyes. "Tell them: the warrant is void. All warrants touching the guild remnant are void. Tell them Halle Voss keeps her word precisely once, and this is the once."

"Why?" Sefa asked. It wasn't tactful, but she'd never been tactful; tact was for people with something to hide.

Voss considered the question with the seriousness of a judge. "Because they did the thing I couldn't," she said finally. "And because a city that punishes its saviors teaches its next saviors to hide. I was hidden once. It made me what I am." She picked up the carriage handle, then paused. "Also, the bread here is allegedly excellent, and I intend to convalesce somewhere with decent bakeries. Good day, smuggler."

The carriage rolled off, slow as winter, complaining faintly at the cobbles.

Sefa stood in the street a long moment. Then she went inside and told Ansel, and watched them cry for the first time since waking—not loudly, not dramatically, just steadily, the way a thaw happens.

* * *

On the seventh day, Ansel walked to the window.

It took twenty minutes and both of Sefa's arms and a great deal of swearing (the mouth and the will being neighbors), but they made it, and they stood there leaning on the sill in the grey morning light, looking out over a Vell that had stopped holding its breath.

The city did look different. Sefa had been trying to articulate it for days and finally gave up and just described it plainly: "It's like everyone put down something heavy and can't remember where they set it, so they keep patting their pockets."

Ansel huffed. Below, in the lane, a fishwife argued cheerfully with a roofer about the price of slate; children chased a dog that had stolen a sausage with the boldness of a professional; smoke rose from chimneys in ordinary, unenchanted spirals. Nobody glowed. Nobody whispered in tongues. The world ran on muscle and weather and luck, honest as a mule.

"No gift," Ansel said softly. Testing the shape of it. "No guild. No Choir. No—" they touched their temple, where a memory used to live, paid to a toll-keeper in the dark. "There's a hole where Master Wren's kindness was. I keep finding its edges. Like a tongue finding a missing tooth."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I paid it gladly. I'd pay it again." They were quiet. "But it's strange, grieving someone I can't remember loving. I just know that I did, and that it mattered, and that the shape of the absence is warm."

Sefa didn't have anything clever for that, so she offered the truth instead: "Then we'll build new ones. Warm shapes. I'm told it's a cottage industry."

Ansel looked at her then—really looked, the first proper look since waking—and something passed between them that Sefa suspected neither of them would ever put into words, because words were for contracts and this was not one. It was more like two sailors agreeing, without discussion, on whose watch was whose.

"Stay for breakfast?" Ansel asked.

"I was always going to stay for breakfast. I've been staying for breakfast for seven days. You've been unconscious through six of them. Frankly, you're a terrible host."

"Seven days of breakfasts," Ansel said gravely. "I'll never repay that debt."

"No," Sefa agreed comfortably. "You won't. That's what family's for."

The word landed in the room and neither of them chased it. It just sat there, warming its hands, like it had always lived here and they'd only just noticed.

From the next room came the sound of Wren stirring—an old man's cough, the scrape of a chair—and Ansel went still, listening, their face doing complicated arithmetic.

"Sefa," they said quietly. "Will you come with me? When I talk to him. Whenever that is. I don't—"

"Yes," Sefa said. "Obviously yes."

And downstairs, faintly, the front bell rang—the shop bell, the old market bell Wren had insisted on reinstalling—and a voice called up the stairs, young and breathless and utterly ordinary:

"Hello? Is this the trinket shop? My mum says you used to sell luck-knots, and I've got three coppers, and I've got a *very* unlucky week coming up."

Ansel and Sefa looked at each other.

"Well," Sefa said, grinning. "Sounds like the city's found you."
