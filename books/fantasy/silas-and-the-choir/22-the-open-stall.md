## 22. The Open Stall

Spring came to Vell the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and all at once.

One morning the Nightmarket simply smelled different. Less of smoke and wet stone, more of green things pushing up through the cracks between cobbles where nobody had planted them. Ansel noticed it while hauling the stall's folding frame up from the cellar, and stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary, letting the air in like an old friend who'd been away too long.

The stall itself had changed. That was the point of it, really. Twelve years of selling luck-knots and whisper-charms and little braided cords that did nothing except make people feel watched over—and now the same table, scrubbed clean, laid out with honest goods. Brass buttons. Ribbon by the ell. Carved combs that Sefa swore she'd found fair and square at auction, whatever her smirk said about it. A tray of river pearls, small and imperfect, priced so a fishwife could buy one for her daughter without doing arithmetic all night.

No charms. No knots tied with intent. Nothing that hummed if you held it to your ear and knew how to listen.

Ansel set out the wares with hands that still remembered how to be careful. They'd never quite gotten the tremor back—some mornings the fingers ached like they'd been holding a note too long, a low ache behind the breastbone on damp days, but the healers said that would fade, or wouldn't, and either way Ansel was alive to complain about it. Complaining was a luxury they intended to enjoy thoroughly.

"Buttons," muttered Old Maretta from the next stall over, watching the setup with her arms crossed. "You've gone respectable. I don't know whether to congratulate you or mourn."

"Mourn quietly, please. It's early."

"You were a terrible charm-seller anyway. Too honest. Half your luck-knots were just string."

"All my luck-knots were just string," Ansel said. "That was rather the trade secret."

Maretta snorted and turned back to her own display of secondhand kettles, which was the closest thing to affection the Nightmarket permitted before noon.

* * *

Business was slow in the way business is slow when you're not actually worried about it. A dockhand bought buttons. Two sisters argued amiably over a comb until Ansel, tired of listening, sold them one each at a discount for the privilege of ending the debate. A young man with ink-stained fingers fingered the pearls for a long time and finally bought the smallest one, wrapping it in his handkerchief like something breakable, and Ansel thought: someone is going to have a very good evening, or a very honest conversation, and either way the pearl has done its work.

This was the trade now. Not magic. Just the small commerce of wanting things and getting them, which Ansel had come to believe was its own kind of quiet enchantment, and cheaper to maintain.

They were re-inking a price sign when the shadow fell across the table—broad, unhurried, smelling of bread.

"You're charging too little for these," Sefa said, setting down a round loaf wrapped in cloth as though delivering state secrets. "I could fence these pearls in Saltrow for triple."

"And then we'd have no customers, because our customers are fishwives, and fishwives talk."

Sefa grinned and dropped onto the stool kept clear for her—the one spot on the cobbles without a puddle, a courtesy Ansel maintained with a strategically placed board. She looked well. Better than well; she looked like someone who had decided what she was going to be and was busy being it. Her hair was braided back new-style, some smuggler fashion from downriver, and there was flour on her sleeve because apparently the fearsome rebel Sefa Kessari now baked bread twice a week at the harbor kitchen she'd helped open, and defended her sourdough starter with the same ferocity she'd once defended supply routes through flooded cisterns.

"Gossip first," she said, tearing the loaf in half and handing Ansel the bigger piece without discussion, because that was the arrangement and always had been. "Or bread first?"

"Bread. Then gossip. Then you'll tell me why you're really here, because you only bring bread when you want something."

"I'm wounded."

"You're transparent. You always have been. It's your best quality."

Sefa chewed, swallowed, and leaned in with the particular brightness she saved for news too good to keep. "The Ministry clerk on Harrow Street—the one who used to shake down half the market? Gone. Resigned. Says he's opening a bookshop." She paused for effect. "A *bookshop*, Ansel. The man couldn't read a ledger straight and now he wants to sell poetry."

"The city's full of miracles this spring."

"That's not even the best of it. The Inquisitors' office is half-empty. Nobody's renewing commissions. There's a rumor—" and here Sefa dropped her voice, though the entire Nightmarket already knew, "—that Voss herself signed the order dissolving what was left of the magical enforcement division. Signed it, walked out, and hasn't been seen since. Some say south. Some say into a monastery. Tam says he saw her buying a fishing boat."

"Tam says a lot of things."

"Tam saw her, though. Grey coat and all, haggling over the price of a skiff like any other fool." Sefa shook her head slowly. "Hard to picture. Harder to stop picturing."

Ansel turned the bread in their hands and didn't say anything for a moment. They thought about grey eyes and a courteous voice, about a woman who had been taken as a child and spent her whole life trying to put down a weight she'd been handed before she could walk. About the last look Voss had given them across the vault threshold—wounded, furious, and underneath it all, relieved. Like someone setting down a load at the top of a long stair.

"Good," Ansel said finally. "I hope the boat leaks a little. Just enough to keep her humble."

Sefa laughed, loud enough that Maretta glanced over, and tore off another piece of bread.

* * *

The children came in the afternoon, as they had begun to do.

It had started with one—a girl of maybe eight, all elbows and questions, who'd stood at the edge of the stall for three days running before working up the nerve to ask. Now there were usually two or three, orbiting the table under the pretense of admiring the ribbons, waiting for the moment when they could ask what they'd really come to ask.

Today's delegation was the elbow-girl, whose name was Pip and who corrected everyone on this point, plus two smaller boys who mostly served as her audience.

Pip picked up a river pearl, turned it over, set it down with great deliberation. Then, casually, the way one mentions weather:

"My gran says magic was real."

"Does she," said Ansel, restocking ribbon.

"She says her mother saw a Charmwright light a lamp with a word. Before the Purge." Pip's eyes were sharp and unblinking. "She says it all went away. But she won't say why."

Ansel set down the ribbon spool. This was the part they'd been rehearsing in their head for weeks without admitting it—not an answer, exactly, but a shape of an answer, one that fit inside the truth without breaking it open.

"Some things went away," they said. "Some things went quiet. Those aren't the same thing."

"What's the difference?"

Ansel considered. Out past the market roofs, gulls were arguing over something in the gutter, and the spring wind carried the smell of the river and bread from Sefa's ovens and ten thousand ordinary lives being lived at full volume.

"When something goes away," Ansel said, "it's gone, and you grieve it, and eventually you plant something else where it was. When something goes quiet—" they tapped the table, gently, twice, "—it's resting. It decided it was tired of being asked for things. So it stopped answering."

Pip frowned, unconvinced. "Did you ever see it? Magic?"

"I knew people who could do things," Ansel said carefully. "Small things. Kind things, mostly. And I knew one thing that wasn't a person at all, that was more like... a song that got lost, and wanted to go home."

The smaller boys exchanged a look of pure delight. Pip remained skeptical, which Ansel respected enormously.

"So it *was* real."

"Something like that," Ansel said.

Pip stared at them a long moment, weighing the answer against some private standard, and evidently finding it acceptable, because she bought a pearl with three coins counted out one at a time from a pocket that clearly contained nothing else, and informed Ansel that next week she'd be bringing her friend Dossa, who didn't believe anything, and Ansel should prepare better answers.

"I look forward to it," Ansel said, meaning it.

* * *

The light was going gold when Sefa came back from wherever she'd wandered off to—she never stayed still longer than an hour, a habit twelve years of smuggling had burned in too deep to wash out—and sat back down on her puddle-free stool with the sigh of a woman settling in for the long haul.

"You're staying for supper," Ansel said. Not a question.

"I'm staying for supper."

They closed the stall together, an operation refined over months into something almost choreographic: Ansel folded the frame, Sefa stacked the trays, and the whole thing went into the cellar below with a series of thumps that Maretta pretended not to hear because pretending not to hear was also a Nightmarket courtesy.

Upstairs, the little flat above the shop was warm. Ansel lit the stove—ordinary fire, ordinary matches, purchased from an ordinary chandler—and put water on for tea while Sefa claimed the good chair and put her boots on the bad chair, because the bad chair existed solely to hold boots and everyone knew it.

"Wren would've liked this," Sefa said suddenly, to the ceiling.

Ansel's hands paused on the teapot. Not froze—paused. They'd gotten better at that, over the months. At letting the grief move through instead of damming it up.

"He'd have complained about the tea," Ansel said. "He'd have said the chairs were badly made. He'd have told a story about a bathhouse in his day with real imported tile, and by the end of the story the tile would have gotten more expensive every time he told it."

"And then he'd have fixed the loose shutter without being asked, using a technique he refused to explain."

"Refused to explain *on principle*. He said guild secrets belonged to the guild, and then he'd wink, which ruined the whole effect."

They were both quiet a moment. Wren had been dead eight months now, buried on the hill above the harbor in a grave marked with a plain stone and, per his instructions, absolutely no seal, sigil, or symbol—which Sefa had honored right up until the moment she'd carved a tiny bell into the corner where only rain and close inspection would find it. Ansel visited sometimes. Talked to him about prices and weather and the scandalous cost of ribbon. It felt less like speaking to the dead than like finishing conversations, and Ansel had decided that was allowed.

"Do you ever miss it?" Sefa asked. She asked it softly, and she asked it rarely, and Ansel knew she asked it not out of doubt but because she was keeping watch, the way she kept watch over everything—her starter, her boats, her people.

Ansel poured the tea. Considered.

"On bad days, I miss the gift itself. The feeling of it. Like missing a limb that ached anyway." They handed her a cup. "But I don't miss what it cost to keep it hidden. Twelve years of flinching. Of paying men to pretend not to know me. Of being alone in a room full of customers." They sat down across from her, in the chair that was theirs, worn to their shape. "I spent twelve years making sure nobody knew me, Sefa. And then one bleeding courier fell through my door, and inside a season I had a smuggler eating my bread, a master returned from the grave to apologize, an Inquisitor calling me by name, and a song older than the city singing to me in my own voice." They shrugged. "Turns out the hiding was lonelier than the dying nearly was."

"So it was worth it. The vault. All of it."

"No," Ansel said, and smiled. "But what came after was. That's a different thing. People get that confused all the time."

Sefa laughed, and raised her cup, and outside the window the first lamps of evening were coming on along the street—one, then another, then another, lit by ordinary hands with ordinary matches, and none of them flickering with anything but flame.

* * *

Later, when Sefa had gone home to her ovens and the flat was dark and quiet, Ansel stood at the window a while, looking down at the empty market square.

The cobblestones gleamed in the lamplight. Somewhere a cat was conducting negotiations with something in the shadows. The bell above the shop door—new, brass, utterly silent of anything but its own clapper—swayed faintly in the draft.

Ansel thought about the map, folded small, kept in the drawer with the good scissors. Thought about the Choir, sealed and sleeping under the hills, quieter now than it had ever been—or so they believed, most days, the way one believes in a healed bone. Thought about the memory they'd paid at the toll gate, the master's kindness gone from them like a tooth, the tongue forever finding the gap. They could not remember Wren teaching them their first knot. They could not remember his hand over theirs. But they remembered that there had been such a memory, and that it had been worth more than passage, and somehow the remembering-about-it had grown into its own kind of keepsake. Grief, it turned out, could be transplanted.

Down in the market square, the cat won its negotiation, whatever it was.

Ansel blew out the candle and went to bed, and slept the whole night through, dreamless and deep, which was the truest miracle the spring had to offer—and in the morning opened the stall again, because the stall would need opening, and the pearls needed sorting, and Pip was bringing the skeptic at midday, and there was bread to buy, and a life, ordinary and hard-won and entirely their own, waiting to be lived.

Something like that.
