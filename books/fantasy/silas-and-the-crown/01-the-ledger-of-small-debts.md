## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The dead woman's name was Marenna Cole, and she had been trying to finish a letter to her son for eleven days.

Wendell Hartwell knew this because he was holding her knucklebone in his left hand while his right drew the burin along its grain in slow, patient strokes. The bone sat in a cradle of wax and wool on his workbench, lit by a single lamp turned low so the flame wouldn't gutter in the draft that came under the door of the Guild of Impossible Things. Outside, the city of Greyhollow was settling into its evening cough — cart wheels, a drunk singing three streets over, the bell of the Watchtower tolling the first hour before curfew.

Inside, a woman who had been dead for two years was dictating.

*Tell him I kept the blue coat,* she said, in the voice bones make when they speak: not sound exactly, but pressure, a hum against the pads of Wendell's fingers, a taste like cold iron at the back of his throat. *Tell him it wasn't sentiment. Tell him I meant to mend the elbow and never did, and I want him to know that's all it was. Not a message. Just a coat.*

"You've told me," Wendell said to the bone, quietly, because there was no one else in the shop to hear him talking to himself, and even if there were, they would only see a thin man with ink-stained fingers muttering over his work. "Eleven days you've told me."

*I want it right.* A pause, the particular quality of pause that meant she was gathering herself, the way the living did before difficult sentences. *He'll want to know why I didn't say it while I could. There isn't a why. That's what I keep circling. There isn't a why, and I need him to have the letter anyway.*

Wendell set down the burin and flexed his aching hand. The knucklebone was nearly finished — a small thing, no longer than his thumb, carved with the letters of her message in a script that no living eye could read but any dead tongue could speak. When her son held it in a warm hand on a quiet night, the words would rise through his skin like water through chalk, and he would hear his mother's voice as clearly as if she stood behind him. He would probably weep. They usually wept. And then he would come to the guild, or not, and pay the fee, or not, and Wendell would enter it in the ledger either way, because the ledger did not care about grief. The ledger cared about debts.

That was the second rule.

The first rule he'd learned at nine years old, kneeling in his mother's workshop with a splinter of sheep bone driven half an inch into his palm, when the bone had spoken to him in his grandmother's voice — his grandmother, four years drowned by then — and said his name, and asked how he'd grown, and he had answered. He had answered, a child, delighted and terrified, and the dead woman's voice had wrapped around his answer like a hand closing on a rope, and for three days afterward he had heard nothing else. Not his mother. Not the fire popping. Only the dead, crowding, asking, asking, asking, drawn to the open door of him the way moths are drawn to a lamp, except that he was the one being consumed.

His mother had pried the splinter out and burned it and made him swear, sobbing, swearing with him, that he would never answer them again. Hear them, yes — the hearing didn't ask permission — but never answer. Never give them a door. A carver who answered became a mouthpiece, and mouths wore out.

Hear the dead. Never answer them. Keep the ledgers. Stay invisible.

Four rules, and he had kept them for thirty years, and they had kept him alive, which in Greyhollow, in the ninth year of King Aldric's reign, was not a small accomplishment.

*Is he well?* Marenna Cole asked, from the bone. *You've seen him. The tall one who came in autumn, with the crooked shoulder. Is he eating?*

"I don't answer," Wendell said. It came out rougher than he intended. He softened it, the way he always did, because the dead were not their fault, most of them; they were just people caught mid-sentence. "I'm sorry. You know I can't."

Silence from the bone, which was worse than speech. He picked up the burin again and cut the final stroke of the final word — *coat*, the last word, fittingly, the whole letter ending where her mind had ended, on a mended elbow and a blue coat — and blew the bone dust from the grooves, and the presence in the room changed. Not gone. Settled. The way a house settles after its owner has finally sat down.

*Thank you,* she said, and the hum of it was almost warm. *You're a good man, carver.*

"No," Wendell said, wrapping the knucklebone in linen. "I'm a bookkeeper."

But he put the parcel in the drawer reserved for completed commissions, top left, beneath the false bottom, and he wrote the entry in the day-ledger in his small, cramped, deliberately unmemorable hand:

*M. Cole — knucklebone, message to son (T. Cole), 41 words. Fee: 3 silver, unpaid. Note: son does not know commission exists. Deliver via drop-box, Weavers' Lane.*

Three silver, likely never collected. It went on the same page as the widow who'd paid in bread, the deserter who'd paid in a stolen regimental seal, the child — no older than Silas would be, if Silas existed, which he didn't yet, which was a thought for later or never — who had traded a milk tooth for the chance to hear his father say goodnight one more time. The Ledger of Small Debts. Corvane called it the charity column and pretended to despise it. Wendell knew better. He had seen Corvane's own entries in it, disguised in another hand, fees forgiven, and neither of them ever mentioned it, because some kindnesses die if you look at them directly.

The Watchtower bell tolled the second hour. One hour to curfew.

* * *

Closing the shop was a liturgy, and like all liturgies it was designed to keep something out.

Wendell moved through the front room first, dousing lamps, drawing shutters. The Guild of Impossible Things did not look like much from Weavers' Lane: a narrow storefront wedged between a chandler and a shop that sold, according to its sign, FINE NOTIONS, though in nine years Wendell had never determined what notions it sold or whether anyone had ever gone in. The guild's window displayed a curated boredom — candlesticks, a cracked mirror, a taxidermied owl with one glass eye — because impossible things sold best to people who believed they were buying nothing.

Behind the counter, the real inventory lived in drawers and ledgers. Relics, yes — teeth and fingerbones and wedding rings, each tagged with a name and a debt — but also oaths, bottled and stoppered, that still smoked faintly if you held them to your ear. Memories, decanted into vials of grey glass, labeled in the seller's own handwriting: *the smell of my mother's kitchen, 1 memory, firm price.* Promises extracted from countries that no longer appeared on any map, because Aldric's cartographers had scraped them away with the same efficiency his inquisitors scraped away their survivors. Wendell had once catalogued a jar containing the national anthem of a place called Serevan, or maybe Serreva — the label had faded, and faded labels were a kind of death too — and when he uncorked it to check the contents, the shop had filled for six seconds with the sound of ten thousand voices singing in a language the world was no longer permitted to remember.

He had re-corked it and sat with his head between his knees for a quarter hour. Then he had entered it in the ledger. Rule two.

Now he made his rounds. The relic drawers: locked, keys on the ring against his chest. The oath cabinet: locked. The memory vault in the back, iron-banded, warded with a threshold-carving he renewed monthly: locked, and the carving checked, and the check itself checked, because paranoia was not a flaw in Greyhollow but a professional qualification.

Last, the ledgers themselves. There were seven of them, great bound volumes in calfskin, chained to the reading desk in the scriptorium — chained, because the guild had learned in its first century that ledgers walk. Not metaphorically. Old Master Quillon's accounts of the founding were very clear on the point: a ledger that holds enough true debts develops wants. It will drag itself toward the debtor. It will fall open to the page. Once, in 1189 by the old reckoning, a ledger had thrown itself into a fire rather than record a certain forgiveness, and the guild had rebuilt its binding practices accordingly.

Wendell ran his thumb along the spines. Debts of Blood. Debts of Oath. Debts of Memory. Debts Owed To the Dead — the strangest and thinnest volume, because almost no one alive understood that the dead could be creditors too. And the Ledger of Small Debts, which he carried back to the desk now, entering the day's final transactions by lamplight while the third hour bell gathered itself across the city.

Three entries. A tooth. A ring. A knucklebone.

He was blotting the last line when he felt it — not heard, felt, the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm — a faint wrongness in the desk beneath his hands. The ledgers were restless tonight. All seven of them, faintly, like dogs before an earthquake. The Debts Owed To the Dead had shifted a half-inch out of alignment, and when he straightened it, the chain clinked, and the book seemed to lean into his touch the way a cat leans, wanting something.

"None of that," Wendell told it.

The book did not open. But it did not quite settle, either.

He told himself it was the weather. The storm that had been building since noon had finally arrived, rain beginning to hammer the shutters in fat, greedy fists, wind shouldering the eaves. Old buildings spoke in storms. Old books did too. He had thirty years of ordinary explanations and he reached for them the way a man reaches for a familiar coat.

Still, he worked faster after that.

* * *

By the fourth bell the shop was dark and Wendell was moving through the back corridors with a shuttered lantern, doing the final round: cellar stairs checked, warding-carvings touched, the little warded door that connected the guild to the chandler's cellar verified from both sides. Escape routes. Every guild member maintained them; every guild member since the purges of Aldric's fourth year had understood that the difference between a long life and a short one was often just a door you'd thought to carve.

In the kitchen he allowed himself his single luxury: tea, real tea, smuggled upriver at criminal expense, brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in. He drank it standing at the window over the sink, looking out at the rain-lashed yard, and performed the nightly accounting that was not written in any ledger.

Corvane had been strange at supper. Not unfriendly — Corvane was never unfriendly; friendliness was his armor, worn so long it had grown to the skin — but distracted, turning his cup without drinking, watching Wendell with an expression Wendell couldn't parse and didn't like. *You ought to take a holiday,* Corvane had said. *You're getting narrow, Wendell. Narrow men break.* And Wendell had laughed and said something dry about holidays being an impossible thing the guild didn't stock, and Corvane had laughed too, and the laugh had been a half-second late.

Thirty years of reading the dead had taught Wendell to read the living as well. People paused before lies the way they paused before hard roads.

He rinsed the cup. Above him, through the floorboards, he could hear the shop settling, and beneath the settling, fainter than the rain, fainter than his own pulse, something else. A resonance. His carved bones hung in their rack in the workroom — forty-odd pieces, each attuned, each silent unless the dead came near — and one of them, he realized with a slow cold spreading under his sternum, was humming.

Not speaking. Humming. Like a struck bell that would not finish dying away.

He climbed the stairs. In the workroom the sound resolved: it was the oldest piece in his rack, a rib he had carved twenty years ago from bone whose provenance even the guild's records listed only as *V., pre-Decree* — a fragment, the clerk's note read, of a country that is no longer admissible. He had never heard it make a sound. No ghost remained attached to it; whatever nation it belonged to had been unmade too thoroughly even for haunting.

It was humming now, steady and sweet and terrible, harmonizing — and this was the part that lifted the hair on his arms — harmonizing with the storm outside, or with something inside the storm, something approaching through the rain on foot, small, alone, carrying a voice that no living person should possess.

Wendell stood in the doorway of his workroom with his lantern shaking slightly in his hand, and recited his rules to himself the way other men recited prayers. Hear the dead. Never answer them. Keep the ledgers. Stay invisible.

The rules had kept him alive for thirty years.

They had never once told him what to do about a knock.

It came at five minutes past the fourth bell, past curfew, past all sense — three small knocks at the guild's front door, spaced with the terrible patience of someone who has nowhere else in the world to knock.

Through the walls, beneath the rain, Wendell heard — felt — tasted, cold iron at the back of his throat — a child's voice saying something in a language older than any kingdom, and every carved bone in his rack began, softly, to sing it back in words he could almost understand.
