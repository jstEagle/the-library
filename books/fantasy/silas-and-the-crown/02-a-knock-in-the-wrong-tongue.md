## 2. A Knock in the Wrong Tongue

The storm came up the river valley an hour before curfew, the way storms did in Greyhollow—sideways, mean, smelling of coal smoke and drowned things. Wendell Hartwell heard it coming through the shop's front window glass, a low groan in the leaded panes that was almost a voice. Almost. He had spent thirty years learning the difference between almost-voices and real ones, and he closed his ears to both with the same practiced ease.

He was finishing the knucklebone.

The old woman's ghost had been waiting three days for it, patient as only the dead could be patient, standing—not standing, *being*, in that way ghosts were—in the corner of the workroom while he carved her final letter into the small curved bone of her own hand. Her son lived in Tarrow's Reach now, across the border, and she had died with four unsent words caught in her throat like a fishbone: *I forgive you. Run.* The Guild of Impossible Things charged her estate two silver marks and a lock of hair for the delivery. Wendell kept one copper of it, because even bone-carvers ate.

*Run where?* he had asked the air, once, early on, before he'd learned better.

She hadn't answered. The dead never answered him directly; they only poured themselves into what he made of them, the way water takes the shape of a cup. That was the first rule, learned at his mother's knee before he understood it was a rule at all: *Hear the dead, never answer them.* A carver who spoke back would find the dead speaking first, and often, and then always, until there was no room left in a man's skull for anything living.

Wendell blew bone dust from the finished letter, held the knucklebone up to the lamp, and watched the tiny characters catch the light. They were not letters anyone could read. They were the shape of a forgiveness, rendered in the only ink that never faded—the dead woman's own hand, remade.

"There," he said to the empty room, which was not empty. "It's done. He'll have it by week's end."

The presence in the corner eased, the way a held breath eases. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled. Outside, the storm arrived all at once, rain hammering the slate roof like a fist demanding entry, and Wendell began the small liturgy of closing the shop.

This was the part of the day he loved best, though he'd have denied loving anything if you'd asked him. Shutters barred. Sign turned—the guild's sign, a wooden hand holding a wooden key, impossible things indeed. The relics went into their cases: a compass that pointed at the person who'd stolen it; a jar of last words, corked and labeled in six hands; a wedding ring that grew warm when its other half was within a mile, though its other half had been melted down forty years ago. The ledgers came off the shelf—seven of them, leather gone soft as earlobes with handling—and into the iron cabinet behind the false wall. *Keep the ledgers,* was the second rule. Not read them. Not balance them. Keep them, the way you'd keep a sleeping animal that might wake hungry.

The third rule was simplest and hardest: *Stay invisible.* Greyhollow had eleven thousand souls and Wendell Hartwell was none of them. He paid his taxes through a factor. He bought his bread from a baker who thought him a customs clerk. When neighbors nodded to him on the street he nodded back with a face like a locked door. Thirty years of practice had made invisibility less an act than a texture, something woven into him, and he told himself—as he told himself every night while banking the lamp—that this was safety, and safety was enough, and a man could live a whole long life inside a locked door if the door was comfortable.

The knock came at half past the bell.

No one knocked at the Guild of Impossible Things at half past the bell. Curfew drums had sounded; the watch patrolled in pairs with lanterns and short swords and no sense of humor. Clients came by day, through the alley door, having been told how by someone who knew someone. The front door of the guild had not been knocked upon in nine years, and the last time, Wendell still flinched remembering it, it had been two of Corvane's collectors with a writ and a smile.

So when the knock came—small, doubled, fists too little to make much sound against oak—Wendell stood in the dark workroom with the banked lamp breathing beside him and did not move.

The knock came again. Then a third time, and with the third knock, something happened that made the hair on Wendell's forearms rise beneath his sleeves and stand there like a field of pale wheat.

His bones rang.

Not all of them. The carved ones—the stock in trade, the letters and reliquaries and unfinished commissions racked along the workroom wall—a dozen pieces of worked bone, horse and ox and human, each holding some residue of the dead. They *sang*. A thin, sweet, terrible chord, like wet fingers on glass rims, like the moment before a bell is struck rather than the moment after. Wendell had heard his carvings resonate exactly twice in thirty years. Once when a dying saint had been carried past the shop on a bier. Once when—

Once when he preferred not to remember.

"No," he said quietly, to the room, to the bones, to whatever stood in the rain beating on his door. "No, no. Go away."

The knock came a fourth time. And beneath the drumming rain, beneath the wind worrying the chimney, Wendell heard—faintly, unmistakably—a child's voice, speaking.

He could not understand a word of it.

That was the horror of it, though it took him a long moment to understand why it horrified him. The language itself was the horror. It was not Greyhollow gutter-slang, not low Tarrish, not the merchant cant of the coast or the clipped church-tongue of the capital. It was older than any of those. Hearing it was like hearing the foundation stones of a house speak, or the roots under a road. The sounds were round and green somehow, full of open vowels and soft consonants that rolled like water over stones, and every syllable of it made the bones on his wall sing louder, and Wendell realized, with a cold spreading through his chest like spilled ink, that he *was* understanding it—not the words, but the shape beneath the words, the meaning bleeding through the resonance the way scent bleeds through a sealed jar.

*Please,* said the voice, in the tongue of the bones. *Please. Please. Please.*

One word, repeated. Or maybe many words that all meant the same thing, the way grief is many words that all mean the same thing.

Wendell stood with his hand flat against the workbench and felt his own rules arrayed around him like soldiers: *hear the dead, never answer them; keep the ledgers; stay invisible.* Every rule said the same thing. Do not open the door. Whatever is out there, it is not your business. You survived your childhood; you survived the purge; you survived thirty years by being no one, and no one does not open doors at half past the bell to voices that make bones sing.

The voice stopped. The knocking did not resume. There was only the rain, vast and indifferent, and after a moment—a small, wet, exhausted sound that might have been a child sliding down against the door to sit on the step.

"Oh, curse it," Wendell said, to himself, to his rules, to the dead woman's forgiveness still lying on his bench. "Curse it all."

He unbarred the door.

* * *

The boy was seven, perhaps eight, so soaked he looked sculpted rather than dressed, a small gray figure huddled in the lee of the doorway with his knees drawn up and his arms wrapped around them. Water sheeted off the awning a hand's width from his head. His lips were blue. His eyes—when he lifted them to Wendell's face—were the color of deep river water over moss, and they were not afraid, which frightened Wendell more than fear would have. Children at strange doors at night should be afraid. This one looked at Wendell the way a traveler looks at a milestone: confirming where he was.

"Inside," Wendell said, in plain Greyhollow speech. "Before the watch sees you."

The boy tilted his head. The words meant nothing to him—or the words meant nothing and the tone meant everything, because he unfolded himself, stiff with cold, and came inside when Wendell stepped back.

Up close, in the lamplight, Wendell saw what the boy carried. Around his neck, on a cord of braided sinew gone dark with rain, hung a pendant: a single tooth, large as a man's thumb joint, yellowed ivory with a root like a hooked claw. Some great beast's tooth, or—Wendell's stomach tightened—no beast at all. And as the boy crossed the threshold, the tooth *hummed*. A low, round note, steady as a struck bell that refused to fade, and Wendell felt it in his back teeth, in the small bones of his inner ear, in the hollow behind his sternum where a man keeps the things he doesn't say.

The bones on the workroom wall sang back to it. The whole rack of them, a dozen dead voices' worth of carved bone, harmonizing with a tooth.

Wendell shut the door, dropped the bar, and stood with his back against it, dripping rain off the eaves onto his shoulders, looking at the child who dripped on his floorboards.

"You'll want the fire," he said, knowing it was wasted breath, and built it anyway, feeding splinters to coals banked in the workroom hearth until flame stood up and threw moving light across the racks of bone. The singing softened as the boy warmed—it seemed tied to him somehow, to his shivering, to the pace of his blood—and Wendell found he could think again with the chord diminished to a whisper.

He fetched bread, hard cheese, the dregs of yesterday's tea reheated. The boy ate like something starved and disciplined at once—fast, but neat, wasting nothing, watching Wendell between bites with those river-green eyes. When he finished, he set down the crust he'd been saving, deliberately, and pushed it across the table toward Wendell. An offering. A payment.

"I don't want your bread, lad."

The boy frowned, and spoke.

The language filled the workroom like water filling a cup. Round vowels, green syllables, the sound of a country that did not exist saying its own name. And the bones sang it into Wendell's understanding, translating not word by word but *meaning* by meaning, so that what arrived in his mind was less a sentence than a weather front:

*You are the one who hears. I was told. I walked nine days. I was told there would be one who hears, in the city of the grey hollow, behind the door with the hand and the key.*

Wendell gripped the edge of the table. The room tilted and righted itself.

"Who told you?" His voice came out rough. "Who told you that?"

The boy answered, and the bones translated, and the translation was worse than any name would have been:

*My grandmother. Before they burned the terraces. She said, if the crown comes, take the tooth and go east, and find the door with the hand and the key, and the man behind it will hear what no one else can hear. She said he would be the last of the listeners.*

"The last of the—" Wendell stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Boy. What is your name?"

The child pressed a hand to his chest. *Silas.*

And the tooth hummed, and the bones on the wall rang out one clear high note like a choir finding pitch, and in that note Wendell heard—not words, nothing so kind as words—a fragment of something vast: green terraces cut into a mountainside, tier on tier, gold with grain. A crown woven of reeds, sitting on no head, waiting. Smoke. Many voices screaming in a tongue the screamers' conquerors had forbidden on pain of erasure. A decree read aloud in a square, unwriting a nation syllable by syllable, maps burning, names struck—

It lasted three heartbeats. It left him shaking, sweat cold at his temples, the taste of ash in his mouth though no fire burned in the room but his own small hearth.

Vhalen. The name surfaced in him from some depth he didn't know he had, from ledger entries he'd never read and songs he'd never been taught. Vhalen, which had been erased. Not conquered—conquered countries persisted, sullen and taxed and singing their old songs in kitchens. Erased. Struck from the rolls and the maps and the memory of the world by royal decree, twenty years ago, in the first flowering of King Aldric's reign, so thoroughly that most people now could not have said what had been erased or where it had stood. Wendell himself knew it only as a rumor among relic-traders: that certain objects sometimes surfaced with provenance that ended mid-sentence, deeds signed in a script no scholar could read, and that the Guild of Impossible Things paid well for such items and asked no questions about the blank space on the map they came from.

And here, eating his bread at his bench, sat a child who spoke that blank space fluently, wearing a tooth that hummed like a struck bell, sent by a dead woman's instruction to the last of the listeners.

"I'm not a listener," Wendell said aloud, uselessly, to a boy who couldn't understand him. "I'm a tradesman. I carve. I keep accounts. I don't—" He ran both hands down his face. "Thirty years I've kept my head down. Do you understand me? *Thirty years.*"

The boy watched him with terrible patience. Then he reached up, drew the cord over his head—the humming note bending, deepening as the tooth swung free—and held the pendant out to Wendell in both hands, the way you'd hold out something breakable, or sacred, or both.

The bones screamed.

Every carved piece on the wall, every letter and reliquary and unfinished commission, rang together in a chord so dense and sweet and sorrowful that Wendell staggered, and in the chord he heard the tooth's history pour through him like cold floodwater: a beast of the old terraces, a herd-animal, gentle, its tooth worn smooth by a century of grandmothers' thumbs. An heirloom passed mouth to mouth down a line of keepers. A key. The word arrived in him with the certainty of a nail driven home: *a key.* And with it, fainter, a second word that made his blood run to ice water:

*A door.*

The boy—Silas—still held the tooth out. Still watched him. Rain still hammered the roof, and somewhere across the sleeping city the curfew watch would be walking their rounds with their lanterns and their short swords, and somewhere beyond the city, in a palace built on erased ground, men whose profession was the finishing of erasures would be doing whatever such men did on rainy nights.

Wendell looked at the tooth. At the child. At the wall of singing bones, which had begun, very softly, to fall silent—one piece at a time, like instruments being laid down at the end of a song.

He did not take the tooth. He took the boy instead, by the shoulder, gently, and turned him toward the narrow stair that led up to the loft where Wendell himself slept, and said the only true sentence he could find:

"You can't stay. But tonight you can sleep."

* * *

Later—much later, with the boy curled in the loft above like a comma in the middle of a sentence Wendell could not yet read—Wendell sat alone at his bench with a single candle and the tooth pendant lying on the wood before him. It had gone quiet. Quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

He should burn it. He should wake the boy, put bread in his pocket, point him east, and bar the door behind him, and by morning convince himself it had been a dream. That was what the rules demanded. That was what survival demanded, and survival had been Wendell's religion for thirty years, and he had never once missed a service.

Instead he found himself reaching for the tool rack. Instead he found, without deciding to, a length of rib bone—ox rib, seasoned, unclaimed—and clamped it to the bench, and took up his finest chisel, and set the edge to the surface while the candle guttered and the storm wore itself out against the roof.

He told himself he was only testing something. Only confirming a hypothesis, the way a tradesman confirms the weight of metal before pricing it. He would carve a single line, feel the resonance, and stop.

The chisel bit. Bone dust whispered onto the bench. And from the first stroke, the tooth began to hum, and the hum climbed into the chisel, into Wendell's wrist, into the architecture of his skull, and images rose in him unbidden, vivid as memory, intimate as guilt: green terraces climbing a mountain into cloud. A crown of woven reed gleaming gold in low sun. A crowd in a square, silent, forced silent, mouths opening around forbidden words. Then fire, then running feet, then a woman pressing a tooth into a child's small hands and saying words whose translation arrived in Wendell's mind like a knife laid flat against his throat:

*Take it east. Find the listener. Tell him the door must be opened, and only he can hear the words that open it.*

Wendell's chisel stopped. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

Above him, through the floorboards, he could hear the boy breathing in his sleep—slow, even, trusting—and Wendell Hartwell, invisible man, keeper of ledgers, last of nothing, sat in the ruins of his third rule with bone dust on his fingers and dread and wonder fighting in his chest like two hands strangling each other, and understood that whatever came through that door next, his life as he had built it was already over.

Because the rib under his chisel was beginning, faintly, in lines he had not yet carved, to remember.
