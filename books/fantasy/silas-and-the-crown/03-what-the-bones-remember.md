## 3. What the Bones Remember

The storm had spent itself by morning, and the shop smelled of wet wool and river mud where the boy had slept.

Wendell Hartwell worked at his bench with the shutters cracked to let in the grey light, because bone wanted grey light—wanted it the way bread wanted warmth, the way grief wanted company. Full sun made a lie of grain; lamplight flattered nothing but the carver's vanity. Grey light told the truth.

He had not slept either. He'd sat up most of the night in the chair across from the hearth, watching the child sleep in a nest of sacking and one good blanket, and he had listened to his own rules recite themselves in his skull like a debtor's prayer. *Hear the dead, never answer them. Keep the ledgers. Stay invisible.* Three rules, worn smooth as river stones from handling. And every one of them had begun to itch the moment the boy crossed his threshold speaking a language that should have died with its country.

The pendant lay on a square of black velvet before him.

It was a tooth—not human, not quite animal either, though it had the root-shape of a wolf's canine, elongated, the enamel shot through with veins of gold so fine they might have been hair dipped in sunlight. It hummed. Not audibly; Wendell had held it to his ear half the night and heard nothing any ordinary ear would catch. But his bones heard it. The little carved sparrow in his left coat pocket—the one he used for finding lost things—had been shivering since sundown like a dog in thunder.

*Whose tooth are you?* he'd asked it silently, and gotten back only that bell-tone, patient and enormous, like a cathedral holding one note forever.

There was one way to ask properly.

He took up the rib.

He'd bought it honestly enough—or as honestly as the guild ever bought anything—from a tinker out of the Marches who claimed it came from a barrow flooded when the Sere changed course forty years ago. Wendell had paid three coppers and thought no more of it. Old bones were old bones. The guild's cellar held crates of them, sorted by century and temperament. But last night, when the boy's pendant had first touched the shelf above his workbench, this rib had rattled in its drawer like something trying to get out.

Now he turned it under the grey light. It was long—a man's rib, or near enough—and dense in the hand, heavier than it should be, the way things are heavy when they've been loved. Someone had drilled a small hole at the head of it, long ago, and threaded it with cord gone to dust. A keepsake, then. Worn against someone's heart until the heart stopped.

"You'll tell me what you know," Wendell said quietly, "and I won't answer you. That's the bargain. It's always the bargain."

The dead never minded. That was the terrible thing about them, the thing no living person understood: the dead were so *glad* to be heard that they'd take any terms at all.

He set the rib in the clamp, chose his finest graver—a sliver of moonstone-edged steel no longer than his smallest finger—and made the first cut.

The sound came up through the bench, through his wrists, into the architecture of his inner ear, and it was not a sound at all. It was a place.

---

Green terraces falling away beneath him like staircases built for giants, each level stitched with barley so bright it hurt, water running down channels cut five hundred years before anyone now alive was born, and the water singing—actually singing, a low choral note in the stone itself, because the Vhaleni had cut their aqueducts to whistle in the wind, and whole valleys were instruments—

Wendell's breath went out of him.

He had carved ten thousand bones. Soldiers' fingers so their mothers could hear them say *I'm sorry*. Murder victims' vertebrae so the courts could hang the right man. A queen's pet raven once, so she could hear it say her name again, and he still didn't know if that one had been mercy or cruelty. He knew how the dead spoke: in fragments, in the grammar of whatever moment had killed them or mattered most. A knucklebone gave you a letter half-finished. A skull gave you a face you couldn't stop seeing.

This gave him a *country*.

Terraces. Barley. Water singing in the stones. He gripped the bench and kept carving, because stopping mid-cut was how carvers lost fingers and worse, and the graver moved almost without him now, peeling a curl of bone away like the peel of an apple, and with every curl another fragment rose:

A crown of woven reed, set on no one's head, resting instead on a cushion of grey wool in a stone alcove, and around it people passing in procession touching their foreheads—not to the crown, he understood suddenly, but *past* it, to the hills visible through the alcove's window, because the crown wasn't the king, the crown was the *promise*, the reed woven fresh every spring equinox by children too young to lie, and the old crown burned on the solstice fire, and a new one woven, and this was the whole of Vhalen's politics: a country that crowned itself annually and trusted its children to do it honestly—

"Who," Wendell whispered, and caught himself. Rule one. *Never answer them.* But the dead in the bone didn't pause for questions anyway; they poured.

A market square. Bells on the eaves of every house, tuned to different notes, so that wind over the city played chords. A woman arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger in the old tongue—that language, *that* language, the one the boy had spoken at his door, and hearing it now from a mouth thirty years dead, Wendell felt the floor tilt, because the boy hadn't been speaking gibberish, hadn't been speaking some dying dialect. He'd been speaking *this*, fluent and native, a language with bells in it.

And then—

Screaming.

The fragments changed key all at once, the way weather changes. Green terraces burning in stripes, fired terrace by terrace in order, methodically, top to bottom, so that the smoke wrote lines across the valley. Soldiers in the livery he knew too well—the stag-and-crown of Aldric's house—carrying ledgers of their own, checking names off, and the names weren't people, the names were *words*, they were crossing words off lists, seizing dictionaries, paying schoolteachers by the word to forget, and there was a decree read aloud in seven occupied cities whose name Wendell could not make the bone say, because the decree had unmade the name, had reached backward through records and forward through memory, and the bone itself was confused, the bone kept trying to say *Vhalen* and producing instead a smooth blankness like a tongue pressed to a pulled tooth—

The screaming again. Closer. A woman running uphill with a child wrapped against her chest, and the child was screaming too, and the woman was singing over the screaming, a lullaby in the old tongue, singing it wrong on purpose, changing the notes, hiding the melody inside a wrong melody so that anyone who heard it later would hear only a mother's poor singing and never guess—

The tooth. She'd pulled the tooth herself, from her own jaw, with her fingers, kneeling in the barley ash, and wrapped it in cord, and hung it around the child's neck, and said one sentence that the bone carried whole and undamaged across all the years and all the erasure:

*"When you can't remember me, wear this, and I will be your memory."*

Wendell put down the graver.

His hands were shaking. Not the good shaking, the shaking of deep work—he knew that one like a brother—but the other kind, the kind he hadn't felt since he was nine years old and had learned what his gift cost the people who loved him. He looked at the rib lying in its clamp, one clean groove cut along its length, and the groove was weeping. Bone didn't weep. But oil had risen in the cut, a bead of it, amber-dark, and it trembled and did not fall.

Behind him, small feet on the floorboards.

He didn't turn around right away. He watched the boy's reflection come to him in the dark glass of the window—small, barefoot, the blanket dragged over narrow shoulders like a mantle, and the pendant already hanging at his throat where he must have slept in it, must never take it off, and of course, *of course*, the tooth matched the rib, the same gold-shot density, the same impossible age, and Wendell understood with a horror that arrived not all at once but in pieces, like a building coming down floor by floor:

The rib belonged to her. The woman in the barley ash. The boy's mother, or grandmother, or some mother-line stretching back, and the tooth was hers, and the boy wore her voice around his neck the way Wendell's sparrow rode in his pocket, and somewhere in the guild's ledgers there was an entry, faded, water-damaged, that he had read a hundred times without understanding:

*Item: one nation, contents unknown, debt outstanding.*

---

"I heard you working." The boy's voice, when he used the trade-tongue, was careful and accented, each word set down like a coin counted out. "You talk to it. The bone."

"I talk to all of them." Wendell turned on his stool. In daylight the child looked worse than he had by firelight—hollows under his eyes, a healing split on his lip, wrists thin as the reeds in the vision. "It's my trade. Bones hold what lives leave in them. I carve, and I listen, and sometimes the dead finish their letters."

The boy looked at the rib for a long time. Then at Wendell. There was a question in his face that he seemed to be deciding whether spending words on it was safe.

"You can ask," Wendell said. "Asking's free here. Mostly."

"What did she show you?"

Not *what did the bone show you*. *She.* The boy knew whose rib it was. Had known, maybe, the moment he walked into the shop and the pendant began to hum—had known the way you know your own name.

Wendell weighed it. Every rule he had said: give the boy bread, point him east, close the door behind him, stay invisible. The dead had demanded justice from him before and he had learned to let their demands go unanswered, because answering them meant becoming a man who acted, and men who acted got found in ditches with their shops burned and their ledgers confiscated. He had spent twenty years being a hinge that never swung.

But the boy was looking at him with his mother's country in his eyes, and the rib was weeping its single amber bead, and Wendell heard himself say:

"Terraces. Green ones, going down to a river. Water that sang in the channels. A crown made of reed that children wove every spring." He paused. "Bells on the houses. A market. A woman singing a lullaby wrong on purpose."

The boy's hand closed over the pendant. His knuckles went white.

"And then fire," Wendell went on, quietly, because the boy deserved the whole weight of it, whatever it cost them both. "Soldiers with lists. They weren't killing everyone. They were *unwriting* everyone. Words off pages. Names off stones. I tried to hear the country's name and the bone couldn't say it—it's been taken out, boy, it's been taken out of the *bones*, and I didn't know that was possible. I've worked death's ledger for twenty years and I didn't know a whole nation could be—" He stopped. Swallowed. "What do they call you? Your true name. Not the one the orphanage gave you, if one did."

The silence stretched. Rain dripped from the eaves outside, counting seconds.

"Silas," the boy said at last. And then, in the old tongue, soft, like setting down something breakable, he said a second name—a longer one, a lineage-name, syllables that made the sparrow in Wendell's pocket shudder and made the rib in the clamp ring faintly, one clear bell-note, like a struck glass.

Wendell didn't need the bones to translate it. He'd spent the night with the guild's oldest atlas, the one with the burn scars, the one where a kingdom's worth of pages had been razored out so cleanly you'd never know they'd existed—except that the stitching holes remained, a ghost of binding, a country's shape punched out in absence.

Silas. Heir of Vhalen. Standing barefoot on his shop floor, wearing his dead mother's tooth, in a city where saying that nation's name aloud was worth a cell in the Undercroft and a visit from the Crown's inquisitors.

"Do you understand what you are?" Wendell asked, and hated the question even as it left him, because it sounded like awe and he needed it to sound like warning. "Do you understand what they'll do? Not just kill you. *Unmake* you. There are men in the capital whose whole office is making sure no one remembers you ever drew breath. You're not a fugitive, boy. You're a *debt* the Crown is trying to cancel."

Something moved in Silas's face—not fear. Wendell had expected fear. It was closer to recognition, the grim comfort of a child who has finally been told the truth he'd already deduced from the size of the dogs chasing him.

"I know," Silas said. "That is why I came to the guild." He lifted his chin, and for a moment the hollow-eyed refugee was gone and something older looked out, something with green terraces behind it. "My mother said: find the keepers of impossible things. She said the guild remembers what kings erase. She said—" he faltered, the first crack in him, twelve years old again, "—she said someone there owed Vhalen."

The word landed in Wendell's chest like a coin dropped down a well. He heard it fall a long way before it hit bottom.

*Owed.* The ledgers. The faded entries. He'd told himself last night it was coincidence, a dead language resonating in old bone, nothing more. But debts in the guild's books did not fade. They compounded. They waited. Somewhere downstairs, in the vault where the oldest volumes slept under lock and salt, there was an account with Vhalen's name on it—or where its name should have been—and accounts like that did not sit quiet forever. Accounts like that *woke up*.

"The guild owes many people," Wendell said carefully. "Most of them dead. All of them patient."

"Not all," said Silas.

And that was when Wendell heard it—the sound underneath the rain-drip and the boy's breathing, so faint he might have imagined it, rising from the direction of the ledger room below. A dry, whispering rustle, like pages turning themselves.

Like ink moving on paper that had been blank for thirty years.

Wendell stood, and the boy stood with him, and the pendant at Silas's throat began—audibly this time, unmistakably—to hum.

"Stay here," Wendell said. "Touch nothing. If anyone knocks, you're my sister's son from the coast and you don't speak."

He took the lamp and went down the stairs into the dark, toward the ledgers, telling himself all the way that he was only going to check the locks.

He was already lying to himself, and some part of him—the part that had heard a dead woman sing a lullaby wrong on purpose—knew exactly why.
