## 4. The Guildmaster's Price

Guildmaster Osric Corvane kept his accounts the way other men kept their sins: privately, in a hand no one else could read, and always with an eye toward what could be forgiven.

He sat in the upper room of the Guild of Impossible Things with the shutters drawn against the rain, a single lamp burning green—guild lamps burned green; it was cheaper than candles and it flattered no one—and before him lay three objects arranged in a row like suspects in a lineup.

The first was the guild's quarterly ledger. Dull as ditchwater and twice as necessary. Rents owed in Ashfield that would never be paid now that Ashfield was ash. A debt from a widow in Lowmark who had traded her husband's last words for winter coal and was two payments behind. The usual slow bleed of a business built on things the world preferred to forget.

The second was a letter, unsigned, bearing the wax seal of the Crown's Office of Inquiry—a seal Corvane had not seen in eleven years, and which he had hoped never to see again.

The third was nothing at all. It was the memory of a pendant he had seen for perhaps four seconds on the neck of a mute child standing dripping in Wendell Hartwell's doorway, and those four seconds were worth more than the other two objects combined.

Corvane poured himself a measure of plum brandy and did not drink it. Drinking came after decisions, never before. That was another rule. He had many rules, and every one of them existed because some earlier version of himself had broken it and paid full price.

*Vhalen,* he thought, and the word sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He was not an old man, but he was old enough to remember when Vhalen had still been a word you could say aloud without checking the window first. Old enough to remember the green terraces of the Vale country described by traders who came through with reed-woven hats and honey wine and a laugh you could hear across a market square. And old enough—this was the part he kept locked in the back room of his mind, behind the furniture—to remember the year the maps were redrawn, the year the King's criers went through every city in the Nine Provinces reading a decree that unmade a nation in eleven sentences.

Eleven sentences. Corvane had once tried to count how many words it took. Four hundred and six words to erase four thousand years. He'd counted because counting was what he did. Arithmetic was the only prayer he trusted.

And now a child stood in Wendell's shop wearing a tooth that hummed like a struck bell, and the child spoke—or had spoken, before whatever silence gripped him took hold—in a tongue that had been decreed out of existence, and Corvane had done the only sensible thing a man in his position could do.

He had smiled, praised Wendell's charity, paid for the boy's supper out of guild funds, and gone upstairs to calculate exactly how much trouble they were all in.

* * *

Let it be said of Osric Corvane—he said it to himself often enough—that he did not hate the boy. Hatred was expensive and he had never once seen it turn a profit. Nor did he fear Silas, particularly. Fear was for men who hadn't yet read the whole ledger.

What Corvane felt, watching the child sleep that night in the cot behind the bone-carver's workbench, curled around that pendant like a snail around its shell, was something colder and more honest: recognition. The boy was an asset. The boy was also a debt. And in Corvane's long experience, assets and debts were the same thing viewed from different sides of the counter.

The question—the only question that mattered—was whose ledger the debt appeared in.

If it appeared in Aldric's, then the Crown would come for the boy, and when the Crown came for something, it came through the Office of Inquiry, and when the Office of Inquiry came, it left behind the kind of absence you could measure. Corvane had watched them empty a smuggler's den in Cael Row once, years ago. They hadn't killed anyone. Killing was loud. They had simply taken everything: the ledgers, the relics, the memories of the neighbors, until the building stood vacant and no one on the street could say what it had been or who had owned it. The guild had lost a branch office that day and gained a lesson.

If the debt appeared in the guild's ledger—if Corvane moved first, if he set the terms, if he sold rather than surrendered—then there might be a way through this with the shop intact, the members breathing, and one small boy delivered into custody with all the gentleness the transaction allowed.

That was the arithmetic. He ran it again anyway, because good merchants check their sums twice.

Option one: do nothing. Probability of discovery by the Crown within the month: high. The pendant alone would draw attention like a wound draws flies. Outcome: guild destroyed, everyone questioned, everyone emptied. Total loss.

Option two: run. Take the boy east over the moors to Cael Row, where the rival branch kept its own counsel and its own armed men. Outcome: possible survival, certain poverty, and a lifetime of looking over shoulders. Also—Corvane permitted himself a thin smile—Wendell would never agree to it, and Wendell knew more of the guild's secrets than any man alive except Corvane himself. You could not run with a partner who wouldn't run.

Option three: sell. Quietly. Carefully. At the right price, to the right buyer, framed correctly so that even history—if history ever bothered to look—would see a man protecting his people the only way protection was ever actually purchased.

Corvane looked at option three for a long time. It looked back with the face of a friend.

* * *

The letter had arrived three days before the boy did.

Corvane kept it in the false bottom of the brandy cabinet, and now he drew it out and read it again by green lamplight, though he knew every line by heart. The handwriting was precise without being beautiful, the hand of someone who had learned penmanship as a weapon.

*Osric,* it began. No title. Eleven years since they'd last exchanged letters, and still no title. *I am given to understand your house has fallen on hard times. I am given to understand many houses have. The Office maintains a discretionary fund for the relief of institutions that prove... cooperative. You will recall our previous arrangement. I recall it fondly. Should anything of interest pass through your inventory, you know where to send word.*

It was signed simply *D.*

Davitt. Spymaster to the usurper King Aldric, though in those early days, when Corvane had known him, he had been merely a clever clerk in the capital's customs house with a talent for knowing what was inside crates before they were opened. They had done business twice. The first time, Davitt had bought a bottled memory off the guild's books—a minor thing, a dockworker's recollection of a shipment that officially never existed—and paid fairly, and Corvane had thought: here is a man who understands trade.

The second time had been different. The second time, Davitt had come to him with a list of names. Members of the guild. Carvers, brokers, a ferryman on the Sere. Men and women who had dealt in Vhalen relics before the erasure, when such trade was merely profitable rather than treasonous. And Davitt had said, in that mild customs-clerk voice, *You may consider this a purchase as well, Osric. The price is your continued anonymity. The payment is already in your account. It is called being alive.*

Corvane had paid. Of course he had paid. He had handed over seven names and told himself the oldest lie in the merchant's book: that they would have been found out anyway, that his betrayal changed nothing, that survival was its own justification.

Six of the seven had died. He had never learned how. He had made sure never to learn how, because learning would make it knowledge, and knowledge was inventory, and inventory had to be carried.

Now Davitt's letter lay on the desk like an open trap, patient as all traps are, and upstairs a child slept with a nation's last tooth against his throat.

Corvane took out a fresh sheet of paper and his second-best pen—the best pen was for the official ledgers, and this was not going in any official ledger—and began to write.

*To the Office of Inquiry, attention D.:*

*An item of interest has entered my inventory. Provenance uncertain but probable. Description withheld pending verification. Verification requires access to the sealed registry of the Erasure Decree, which your office holds and mine does not. I propose an exchange: verification for assurance. Assurance that the guild, its premises, its members, and its records remain outside the scope of any subsequent inquiry. I will deliver the item myself, to you personally, nowhere else.*

He stopped. Read it back. It was good. It was very good. It offered just enough to hook Davitt's interest and withheld just enough to keep Corvane necessary rather than convenient. Necessary men lived. Convenient men were used up and discarded.

But beneath the craft, in the space between the lines where the truth of a man leaks out whether he wills it or no, the letter said something plainer. It said: *spare me, spare my house, take the boy instead.* And Corvane, who had spent forty years learning to hear what documents said underneath their words, heard it clearly, and signed his name anyway.

*O.C.*

He sealed it with plain wax, no mark. By morning it would be in the hands of a tanner's apprentice who owed the guild a favor and didn't know what he carried. By week's end it would be in the capital. After that, the machinery would begin to turn, and machinery, once turning, does not stop for sentiment.

* * *

He went down afterward. He told himself it was to check the locks, and in fairness he did check the locks, testing each one with a practiced wrist. But the shop was quiet in the way shops are quiet at night—not empty, but holding its breath—and his feet carried him past the counters, past the reliquary cabinets with their little brass labels, to the door of Wendell's workroom.

It stood ajar. Lamplight leaked through the gap.

Through it, Corvane saw the bone-carver bent over his bench, sleeves rolled, a rib bone pinned in the vise and the chisel moving in strokes so fine they might have been breathing. Wendell carved the way other men prayed—with his whole body and none of his attention, as if the hands knew something the man refused to admit. And he was talking. Not to Corvane, not to himself. To the bone. Low, half-voiced, the way you speak to someone sleeping.

"—and then the terraces go up the hillside in steps, see, each one greener than the last, and the water comes down through them in channels the children swim in, and nobody locks a door in the whole vale because—" Wendell stopped. His chisel stopped. His shoulders did something complicated.

Corvane did not go in. Some conversations were not meant to have witnesses, and besides, he already knew how that sentence ended. *Because there was no one left to steal from us.* Everyone who remembered Vhalen ended their stories the same way. Grief was a language with only one tense.

He watched a moment longer. On the bench beside the vise lay the pendant, the tooth of some beast Corvane couldn't name, strung on a cord of woven reed. Even from the doorway, even across the room, he could feel it. Not hear it—nothing so crude—but feel it, the way you feel a storm coming in an old scar. A pressure. A hum below hearing. The sense of a bell that had been struck somewhere far away and was still, impossibly, ringing.

*Provenance probable,* his letter had said. He almost laughed. Probable. There was no probably about it. He had held Vhalen relics before—everyone in the trade had, in the years before the decree, when they were curiosities instead of crimes—and none of them had ever felt like this. This was not a relic. Relics were leftovers. This was a root. This was the taproot of a tree the King had spent eleven years pretending he had cut down, and it was beating like a heart in a bone-carver's workshop in the worst neighborhood of a city that barely tolerated them.

And wrapped around the root, sleeping ten feet away, was a boy.

Corvane turned away from the door. In the dark of the stairwell he stood with his hand on the rail and let himself feel, for exactly as long as he had budgeted for it—ten breaths, no more—the weight of what he was doing.

Here is what he told himself, and he told it well, because he had long practice: the boy was doomed regardless. Kings do not tolerate living heirs. If not through Corvane, then through some other channel, and the other channels would be crueler. The Office of Inquiry did not buy children; it erased them, quietly, the way it erased everything. At least this way—at least with Corvane brokering, with Davitt's vanity engaged, with terms negotiated—there was a chance, a slim merchant's chance, that the boy lived. That he grew up in some gilded corner of the palace as a ward, a curiosity, a tame heir with his dangerous name filed down smooth. Alive. Alive was not nothing. Alive was, in fact, quite a lot, when you compared it honestly to the alternative.

And the guild survived. Forty-three members. Widows on the pension roll. An archive three centuries deep that would burn otherwise, because the Office did not distinguish between guilty ledgers and innocent ones—it burned the shelf and called it hygiene.

Yes. Weighed in any honest scale, the arithmetic held. One boy against a houseful of lives. One nation that was already dead against a hundred people who were not.

It was good arithmetic. It was airtight. It was the kind of sum a careful man could carry to his grave without stumbling.

So why, Corvane wondered, standing in the dark with his hand on the rail, did it feel less like a calculation and more like a coin he was slipping into his own pocket while the beggar watched?

He had no answer. He had stopped expecting answers from that particular quarter years ago. Answers were for men whose ledgers balanced.

* * *

Two days later, the reply came.

It came faster than any letter should have traveled from the capital, which told Corvane that Davitt had eyes closer than the capital—eyes in this city, possibly on this street, possibly closer than that—and the knowledge settled into him with a chill that had nothing to do with the weather. He broke the plain seal alone in the upper room.

*Osric. Your discretion honors our old friendship. Verification is granted: come to the capital yourself, bring the item, and we will discuss terms. Regarding your assurances—I can protect what I can see. Bring me a complete inventory of your current holdings and I will consider the guild's position secured. Consider this a kindness between old partners. —D.*

Corvane read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting the hook show itself.

*A complete inventory.* Every relic. Every bottled memory. Every oath and debt in the guild's keeping, written down and placed in the spymaster's hand. Davitt wasn't buying a boy. Davitt was buying the guild entire—its vaults, its secrets, its three centuries of impossible things—and offering Corvane the privilege of carrying the keys to his own execution, dressed up as a kindness between friends.

And yet. And yet the first part stood. *Bring the item.* The boy remained the prize. The inventory was leverage, insurance, a leash—cruel, but survivable, if Corvane was clever about what went onto the page. Ledgers could lie. He had spent a lifetime proving it.

He sat back in the green lamplight and looked at the two letters side by side, and felt the trap close around him by degrees, gentle as a ledger line. Sell the boy to save the guild; surrender the guild to save himself; and somewhere at the bottom of the sum, a child asleep against a humming tooth, trusting a bone-carver who trusted a guildmaster who had already written the first line of the bill.

Below him, faintly, through the floorboards, he heard Wendell's chisel begin again. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap. Steady as a heart.

And beneath it—so faint he might have imagined it, so faint he needed desperately to have imagined it—came a sound like a struck bell, ringing on and on, from the direction of the workroom bench.

Corvane put both letters in the stove and watched them curl.

Then he took out a fresh sheet, and began composing the inventory that would save them all, and which would lie.
