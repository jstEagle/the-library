## 6. Silas Speaks Once

*The man with the iron teeth comes at dusk. Silas knows this because the bones in his pocket know it first.*

*They hum. They have hummed since the night of the storm, since the door, since the old carver took him in and fed him soup he could not name and asked him questions he could not answer — not would not, could not, because the answers live behind his teeth like birds that will die if they fly.*

*He has not spoken in — how long? He counts in things, not days. Since the burning. Since the mother-who-was-not-his-mother pressed the tooth into his palm and said words in the old tongue that meant: run, little king, run until the world forgets your face. Since the soldiers came to the terraces where the reeds grew gold and the water sang downhill, and a man in a black coat read from a paper and the paper was louder than the screaming, and then there was no Vhalen, there had never been a Vhalen, and only Silas remembered, because remembering was the last thing they could not burn.*

*He remembers everything. That is the curse they left him. The crown under the floorboards. The lullaby. The smell of the green terraces after rain. He is seven years old and he is the whole memory of a country, and the weight of it sits on his chest every night like a sleeping animal he must not wake.*

*But tonight the animal wakes.*

---

It begins with the rat-catcher.

Wendell — the bone-carver, the man who talks to dead things without moving his lips — has gone out to buy bread. He told Silas twice: *Do not open the door. Do not go near the window. If anyone knocks, be furniture.* Silas liked that word. *Furniture.* In the old tongue there was no word for furniture, only words for things-that-serve and things-that-rest, and Silas had spent three days trying to decide which he was.

So when the knock comes — three sharp raps, then two soft ones, then three again, a rhythm Silas does not know — he becomes furniture. He sits very still on the stool by the cold hearth and lets his eyes go dull the way Wendell's do when the ledgers are talking to him.

But the knocking does not stop.

And then the voice comes through the wood, low and friendly as oil: "Guild business. Open up, lad. I know someone's in there. Saw you through the glass yesterday."

Silas does not move.

"I'm a friend of Corvane's," the voice says, and something in Silas's chest goes cold, because Corvane is the guildmaster's name, Wendell said it once with a tone like a stone dropped down a well. "Corvane sent me. Says there's a boy here needs collecting. Says there's a reward in it for whoever helps. A big one."

The handle turns. The lock holds. The voice chuckles.

"Stubborn, are we? That's all right. I've got time. And I've got friends coming. Soldiers, lad. Real ones, with real papers. You know what papers are? Papers can unmake a country." A pause, and Silas hears him smiling through the words. "They already unmade yours."

*How does he know—*

The rat-catcher laughs again, softer now, almost kind. "Oh, I know. I've known since you came through the market with your hood up and your eyes doing that thing eyes do when a body's counting exits. Corvane described you. Dark hair, small for his age, carries himself like — " the man searches for it — "like a little king in exile. Which is what you are, isn't it? The last bit of Vhalen, walking around in shoes too big for it."

Silas's hand goes to his chest, to the pendant beneath his shirt, the tooth that hums.

"And here's the thing," the rat-catcher says conversationally, settling against the door as if he means to stay the night. "I don't much care about kings. Kings are just rich men with better hats. But the crown pays for children, and I've got debts, and debts don't care about hats either. So you can open this door nice and polite, or you can wait for the soldiers. Your choice, little majesty."

Silas looks around the shop. The bones hang from their strings like a rain that never falls. The ledgers sit closed on their shelf, fat and patient. The hearth is cold. The window shows the street going blue with evening, and somewhere out there Wendell is buying bread, walking home, unaware, humming perhaps, and Silas thinks: *he told me to be furniture, but furniture cannot save itself.*

The man tries the handle again. Then stops.

Then says, very quietly, in a voice that has changed entirely: "Actually, you know what? Let me try the back."

Footsteps crunch around the side of the building. Silas hears them stop. Hears the scrape of a lantern being lit. Hears the man mutter — and the muttering is worse than the talking, because now he is talking to himself, planning, and plans made aloud become true faster than plans kept silent.

*Back door. Back door. There'll be a bolt, old wood, old wood gives. And if the boy runs, well. Streets are dark. Boys are slow. And the reward doesn't say anything about the boy being whole.*

Silas stands up.

His legs shake. His hands shake. But his feet carry him to the front door, because the back door is where the man is going, and between the man and the back door stands the only thing Silas has ever been told never, ever to use.

The tooth pendant is warm against his palm. It has been warm since the storm. It hums the way the bells of Vhalen used to hum before the hour struck — that held-breath moment when the whole country waited to find out what time it was.

*If you speak,* his mother's ghost-said words rise in him, *the world will listen. That is why they fear you. That is why you must never give them reason.*

*But he is going to come through the back door. And Wendell will come home to soldiers. And it will be my fault, because I was furniture when I should have been a wall.*

Silas puts his mouth close to the wood of the door.

He opens the cage behind his teeth.

---

*What comes out is not a word. Not really. It is older than words. It is the sound the terraces made when the wind moved through ten thousand reeds at once; it is the sound the water made singing downhill; it is the sound his mother made when she said his name, which no one has said since, because no one alive knows it except him and her and she is ash in an ash-country. It is one syllable and it contains a nation.*

*It is the first sentence of a language that has exactly one living speaker, and the speaker is seven years old, and the sentence means:*

***forget.***

---

The lantern-light under the door gutters.

Silas feels the word leave him the way an arrow leaves a bow — a shudder through the whole string of him, a sudden terrible lightness. And he feels where it goes. He feels it the way you feel a stone leave your hand and know, before the splash, how deep the water is.

The word goes through the door. Through the walls. Into the man crouched at the back of the shop with his crowbar and his greed.

And it takes.

Silas staggers. Sits down hard on the floorboards. The pendant has gone cold against his chest for the first time since the storm, and the silence in the shop is enormous, total, like the silence inside a bell after it has finished ringing.

Outside, the footsteps stop.

A long pause. Then the rat-catcher's voice, muffled, bewildered, ordinary: "...Where am I?"

Silas crawls to the window on hands and knees and looks out through the gap in the shutter.

The man is standing in the alley holding his lantern up as though he has never seen one before. He turns it slowly, examining it. He looks at his own hands. He looks at the crowbar lying in the mud and nudges it with his boot, frowning, the way a man frowns at a tool he cannot imagine needing.

"Evening, friend!" calls a neighbor from across the way, a woman Silas has seen carrying water. "Lost something?"

"No," the rat-catcher says slowly. "No, I don't... think so." He looks up at the sky, at the darkening blue of it, and his face is empty in a way faces should not be. "I was just — walking. I think. Funny. Can't recall where I set out from."

"Rats about?" the woman asks.

"Rats," the man repeats, tasting the word. Something flickers behind his eyes — a shape with no picture attached, a duty with no reason. "Yes. Rats. Suppose I'd best get on with it."

He picks up the crowbar, puzzled by it, pockets it anyway. He walks away down the street at an aimless amble, glancing back once at the shop with no recognition at all, no hunger, no greed, nothing. Just a man passing a building he has never seen, on an evening he will not remember, toward a life with a hole in it shaped exactly like today.

Silas watches until he turns the corner.

Then he sits down on the floor of the shop, pulls his knees to his chest, and shakes.

---

*This is what I did. This is what my voice does.*

*He came to sell me and I did not make him sorry. I did not make him kind. I reached into him and took the day out of him like pulling a thread from cloth, and now there is a hole in a man where this evening used to be, and the hole will never fill, and he does not even know to mourn it.*

*I unmade him a little. The way they unmade Vhalen. The way the paper was louder than the screaming.*

*I am seven years old and I am a weapon that walks and talks and eats soup and misses its mother, and everything I touch of other people's lives, I take.*

The tears come, hot and silent. Silas has learned to cry silently; loud crying brings soldiers. But tonight the silence is not skill, it is horror. Because he understands now — fully, finally, the way you understand a thing only after you have done it — what his mother's warning meant. What the old tongue costs. Why the last king of Vhalen went hoarse and never spoke above a whisper in all the years Silas knew him. Why the language was kept for oaths and coronations and nothing smaller.

*Every word is a theft. Every sentence spends someone else's past.*

*And someday — someday soon, maybe — someone will make me spend more than a day.*

He hears Wendell's key in the lock and scrubs his face with his sleeve, fast, the way you hide evidence. The door opens on bread-smell and rain-smell and the bone-carver's tired, careful face.

"You're pale," Wendell says, setting the loaf down. His eyes go around the shop the way they always do, reading the room like a ledger. They stop on the window shutter, still swaying slightly. On the wet boot-print in the alley mud visible through the gap. On Silas's shaking hands.

"Someone came," Silas manages. His own voice sounds strange to him, rusty, guilty. "A man. He knew. He was going to sell me, and I—" His throat closes. "I spoke."

Wendell goes very still. All the carved bones hanging from their strings begin, very softly, to sway — though there is no wind — and to hum, each one faintly, in a chord Silas recognizes with a sick lurch: the chord of the old tongue, still echoing in the room like smoke.

"What did you say?" Wendell asks quietly.

"One word." Silas wraps his arms around his knees. "I told him to forget. And he did. He forgot the whole day. He forgot why he came. He walked away and he didn't know his own errand, and Wendell—" the tears come again despite everything, "—he didn't even know enough to be angry. I didn't punish him. I *erased* him. A piece of him. Forever. That's what my voice is. That's what I am. I'm the same as the paper. I'm the same as Aldric."

The bone-carver crouches down in front of him. For a long moment he says nothing, and Silas braces for the sermon, the horror, the flinch — everyone flinches eventually, everyone who learns what he is.

Instead Wendell reaches out and closes Silas's fingers, gently, over the cold tooth pendant.

"Listen to me," he says. "There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters. They erased a country so no one would grieve it. You erased an evening so a child wouldn't be sold." His voice is rough. "It's still a cost. You're right about that. It's a cost you'll carry, and you should, and I won't pretend otherwise. But Silas — the man who came here tonight chose his errand. You didn't choose your voice. Don't let anyone — not the crown, not the dead, not yourself — tell you those are the same crime."

Silas wants to believe him. He wants it the way the drowned want air.

But outside, far off toward the capital road, he sees it: a line of lanterns coming over the hill. Too many lanterns. Too orderly. And riding at their head, a figure in a black coat, and beside the figure, riding familiar as a friend, a heavy man in guild grey whom Silas has met only once —

Corvane.

The guildmaster who smiled at him and called him *lad* and asked careful questions about his health.

"He told them," Silas whispers. "Wendell. He told them where I am."

Wendell rises. Through the window, the lanterns descend the hill road toward the town, unhurried, certain, like a verdict that has already been written and only needs delivering. The bone-carver's face in their distant light goes hard and old and terribly awake.

"Get the pendant," he says. "Get your shoes. We're leaving through the cellar, and Silas — "

He is already pulling ledgers down from the shelf, stuffing them into a sack, his hands steady in the way of a man who has run before.

"— whatever happens out there, whatever they make you feel you owe them — your voice is *yours*. No one spends it but you. Remember that. Whatever else happens, remember that."

*But I don't want to remember,* Silas thinks, clutching the cold tooth as the first fists begin hammering on the guild door. *Remembering is the curse they gave me. Remembering is how a seven-year-old boy becomes the last of a country.*

*And now remembering is the only weapon I have left.*
