## 7. The Road Through Ashfield

They left Cael Row's outer farms before dawn, when the fog lay on the fields like a sheet drawn over something the world preferred not to look at.

Wendell had walked this road a hundred times in his life—cart traffic to the river markets, drovers moving geese, the ordinary commerce of an ordinary province. Now it was empty in the wrong way. Not quiet. *Vacated.* The way a room is vacated after a funeral, when everyone has gone and the chairs are still arranged for mourners.

Silas walked beside him, small hand hooked into two fingers of Wendell's right hand, the tooth pendant tucked inside his shirt where it hummed faintly against his sternum like a wasp dreaming. The boy had not spoken since the rat-catcher. He did not need to. He pointed with his chin, and Wendell learned to read the angle of that small jaw the way he read grain in bone.

East, Silas said without saying. East through Ashfield.

The first village was called Bracken Hollow. Wendell knew because the sign still hung at the crossroads, though the paint had bubbled and blackened and someone had dragged a blade through the name so that only *BR CK N* survived, which was, Wendell thought, the whole of Aldric's method in miniature: leave the frame, remove the meaning.

The houses were not ruins exactly. Ruins took time. These were *unmade*—roofs collapsed inward as if the fire had been poured down chimneys from above, walls standing at angles no honest fire would have left them, doors hanging open on rooms full of ash shaped like the things that had once been there. A cradle. A loom. A table set for six, its dishes fused to black glass.

And the ditches.

Wendell felt them before he saw them, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. The bones in his satchel began to murmur—not his own carvings, but every carved thing he carried resonating like struck tuning forks, answering something in the road ahead.

He stopped at the lip of the drainage ditch beside the crossroads and looked down.

They crowded it the way leaves crowd a gutter in autumn. Dozens. Then, as his eye adjusted to the particular light the dead gave off—a grey-gold shimmer, like dust motes in a dark church—he understood it was not dozens. It was hundreds, packed shoulder to shoulder along miles of ditch, spilled into wells, heaped in doorways, standing in the burnt fields in rows so straight they must have died standing in them.

They had been waiting. That was the horror of it. They had not moved on, because there was nowhere to move on *to*. Vhalen had been erased by decree—the roads renamed, the parishes reassigned, the language forbidden—and the dead of Vhalen had nowhere to go, because the country that would have received them no longer existed on any map. So they stood in ditches outside villages whose names had been scraped off signposts, and they waited for someone who could hear them.

Wendell could hear them.

It came up out of the ground like water rising through chalk. Not words at first—a pressure, a crowding, ten thousand voices trying to pass through a doorway built for one.

*—the reeve said we'd be paid—*

*—my daughter has my eyes, tell her my eyes—*

*—it was harvest, we hadn't brought the harvest in—*

*—cold, why is it cold, the fire was warm—*

*—who will finish the wall, I was halfway up the wall—*

He pressed his palms against his ears, which was useless, because he had never heard the dead with his ears. He heard them with the scar tissue behind his sternum, with the old break in his left wrist, with every bone in his body that had ever been carved or broken or mended. His mother had told him once—you are a door, Wendell, and doors don't get to choose what walks through them.

His mother. He held the thought of her like a coal in cupped hands and walked on.

Silas tugged his fingers. The boy was looking at the ditch too, and his face did something Wendell had not seen before: not fear. Recognition. He knelt at the ditch's edge, careful as a child kneeling at a stream, and looked into the crowded grey-gold press of faces, and one of the dead—a woman with a scythe still ghosted across her shoulders—reached up with both hands, not grasping, just *showing* him her empty palms, and Silas bowed his head to her. A small, formal inclination. The bow of a king to a subject, or a subject to a king; Wendell could not tell which, and suspected the boy couldn't either.

The woman's mouth moved. Wendell caught only the shape of it, the sound arriving a half-beat later through his own bones:

*—he has the crown-face. He has the reed-crown face.*

Around her, the ditch stirred. Faces turned. The pressure of attention swung toward Silas like a weathervane in a gale, and Wendell felt the dead begin to move—not walking, nothing so kind as walking, but *flowing*, pouring up out of the ditches and the wells and the doorways, converging on the boy with the terrible patience of water finding a crack.

"No," Wendell said, and pulled Silas back onto the road. "Not now. Not here."

The dead stopped at the edge of the packed earth, all of them at once, hundreds of faces, and Wendell understood that they were not angry. They were *waiting again.* They had waited years in ditches; they could wait while the crown-face walked east. But their waiting had weight. It settled on Wendell's shoulders like wet wool, and it did not lift for the rest of the day.

* * *

They made eight miles by dusk, keeping off the king's road, following hedgerows and field-paths that Silas seemed to know by instinct—which was impossible, because the boy had never walked this country, and yet his feet turned confidently at each fork, as if some older map were folded into his blood.

Ashfield itself announced itself by smell an hour before they reached it. Burnt thatch, yes, and burnt grain—but under it, sweetish and wrong, the smell of a fire that had been fed things fires should not be fed.

Wendell made them stop and eat cold porridge in the shadow of a fallen boundary stone, and it was there, chewing mechanically, watching Silas eat with the steady efficiency of a child who had learned hunger early, that Wendell noticed the soldier.

He was sitting on the boundary stone. Or rather, the memory of a soldier sat on the memory of a stone, translucent as breath on glass, in the uniform of Vhalen's last levy—woven-reed green faded almost to grey, a pike's head rusting beside him, half his skull caved in and shining with that same grey-gold light. He had been sitting there, Wendell realized, since before they arrived. Watching them eat. Waiting, with the courtesy of the dead, for them to finish.

When Wendell met his eyes, the soldier stood so quickly his pike clattered through the real stones and rang against nothing.

"You see me." The voice came through Wendell's wrist-bones, through the healed fracture in his ribs, thin and cracked and disbelieving. "Saints of the terraces. You *see* me."

"I see you," Wendell said, because it was true, and because lying to the dead was the one cruelty he had never managed.

"Then you can—" The soldier's hands opened and closed. "I've been on this stone for—I don't know how long. The seasons went past. I counted the winters and then I lost the number. My wife will have—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "My wife doesn't know."

Wendell looked at his porridge. At the road east, going purple in the failing light. At Silas, who had stopped eating and was watching the soldier with wide, grave eyes.

Here was the arithmetic Wendell had kept all his life, the ledger no one else could read: hear the dead, never answer them. Answer them once and they never stop. Answer them twice and they follow you. Answer them three times and you become what his mother had become—a door worn off its hinges, a man the dead found in crowds the way sharks find blood, until there was no room left in your skull for the living. He had watched it happen. He had buried the result.

But the soldier was not asking for absolution, or justice, or a carving. He was asking for the smallest thing in the world. He was asking someone to carry a message four miles down a road.

"My name was Tam Breck," the soldier said. "Is. Was. I was in the third levy out of Ashfield. We stood at the ford at Marrow Water and the king's horse came through us like—" He shook his head, and the ruined side of his skull caught the light. "Doesn't matter how. What matters is Denna. Denna Breck, née Callow. She'll have gone to her sister's in Millbrook, east of here, past the second bridge. She'll think I deserted. She'll have spent every winter thinking I deserted, because the levy never sent word, because there was no levy anymore, because there was no *anything* anymore—" His voice frayed. The dead could not weep; what happened instead was worse, a flickering, guttering, as if grief were wind and he were a candle. "Tell her I stood. Tell her I stood at the ford and I did not run, and my last thought wasn't fear, it was that she'd left the shutters open and the rain would get the bedding. Tell her that. She'll understand. She'll know it's me, because only I'd worry about the bedding."

Wendell said nothing. The rule said nothing. The rule had kept him alive for thirty years, had kept him invisible while kings unmade countries, had let him do his small work in the margins—carving knucklebones so ghosts could finish letters, taking no sides, making no promises, staying *safe*—

Safe. He tasted the word and found it had gone rancid somewhere in the last week, sometime between a knock in the wrong tongue and a guildmaster's smile.

The dead soldier waited. Around them, out in the darkening fields, other figures had risen from the ditches and the furrows and stood at a distance, watching, hundreds of them, silent. Not pressing. Just witnessing. As if they all knew what was being weighed.

"Wendell," Silas said.

It was the first word the boy had spoken since the rat-catcher—not the true voice, not the unmaking tongue, just a child's voice, hoarse from disuse, saying Wendell's name in the common speech he'd apparently been taught before whatever silence had taken him. And there was no command in it. There was only a boy looking at a dead man and then at Wendell, with an expression that said: *this is what I am heir to. All of this. Every ditch. Are you the kind of man who carries it, or the kind who walks past?*

Wendell closed his eyes. He thought about ledgers. He thought about debts.

"All right," he said aloud—to the soldier, to the fields, to the whole listening dark. "Tam Breck, third levy of Ashfield. Denna Breck, née Callow, Millbrook, past the second bridge. Message received. I'll carry it if the road allows."

The sound the soldier made was not a cheer. It was smaller and more terrible than that—a single exhalation, the sigh of a man setting down a load carried for years, and around the fields the dead bowed their heads in a slow wave, like wind through wheat, and Wendell felt the wool-weight on his shoulders double and treble and settle in for good.

Because they had all heard. Of course they had all heard. He had answered one, and now he had answered *all of them*, and the message that had been one message was already becoming a thousand—names surfacing out of the dark like fish rising to feed, *tell my mother, tell the miller, tell them we stood, tell them we stood, tell them we stood*—

"No," Wendell said, and his voice cracked on it. "One. I can carry one. I'm sorry. One."

The names did not stop. But the dead, to their eternal credit, grew quiet again, folding their wants away the way poor people fold away wants, and Tam Breck sat back down on his stone to wait for whatever came after waiting, and Wendell took Silas's hand and walked east into the dark at a pace just short of running.

* * *

They walked through the night. Not from bravery—from arithmetic. Inquisitors traveled by day, in force, with warrants and wagons; the night belonged to smugglers and the dead, and Wendell would take either company over the first.

Near midnight they crossed a stream on stepping-stones, and Silas suddenly stopped in mid-channel and went rigid, his grip crushing Wendell's fingers.

Below the bridge, in the water, the dead of Marrow Water stood on the drowned ford where they had fallen, rank upon rank upon rank, the flood of two years ago unable to shift them, the current passing through their bodies like light through glass. They were not looking at Silas. They were facing west, all of them, forever facing west, toward the country that had erased them, holding a line no order had ever released them from.

Silas raised his free hand—the pendant-hand—and held it out over the water, and the humming in the tooth changed pitch, deepened, became almost a chord, and along the drowned ranks a shiver passed, helmet to helmet, like a standard being dipped.

"They know you," Wendell whispered.

Silas lowered his hand. In the dark, Wendell could not see the boy's face, but he felt the answer arrive anyway, through the bones of his own gripped fingers, in no language at all:

*And I know what they'll ask of me. And I'm afraid I'll say yes.*

They walked on. Toward dawn, exhausted, Wendell dozed upright against a barn wall while Silas slept curled against his coat, and dreamed—not of his mother, for once, but of a ledger page writing itself in ink that would not dry, entries multiplying, and at the bottom of the page, in a hand too fine to be his own, a new line appearing:

*Debt of passage: one message, Millbrook. Debt accruing: nine hundred and forty-one messages, unpaid. Interest: compounding.*

He woke with the sun full in his eyes and Silas shaking his arm, pointing back down the road they had come.

Far behind them, at the edge of sight, dust hung above the king's road. Not the dust of carts. The dust of riders, many riders, moving fast, and at their head a banner Wendell had hoped never to see outside a courthouse: the grey and iron of the Crown's inquisition.

And beneath the banner, riding a lean black horse with the easy, terrible patience of a heron wading, a figure in a grey coat whom Wendell had met only once, across his own shop counter, and had prayed never to meet on an open road.

Maren Voss had found the trail.

Wendell gathered Silas up, checked the pendant's hum against the wind—east, still east, always east—and ran for the second bridge at Millbrook, where a widow named Denna Breck was about to learn she had never been abandoned at all, and where, if the riders reached the crossing first, neither of them would live long enough to tell her.
