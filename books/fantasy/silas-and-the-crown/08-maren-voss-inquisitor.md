## 8. Maren Voss, Inquisitor

The clerk's name was Pellum Ostrey, and he had been crying for six hours.

Maren Voss knew this because she had been counting—not out of cruelty, but because the crying told her things. The first hour was fear. The second and third were bargaining with a god who wasn't listening. By the fourth hour, the crying became mechanical, a body doing what bodies did when they had given up on being heard, and that was when the real work could begin.

She sat across the table from him in a room beneath the Guildhall of the capital, where the candles burned in glass jars so the flames made no sound. Silence was important to her work. Most inquisitors believed fear was the instrument. Maren had learned long ago that fear only made men lie louder. It was silence that made them tell the truth—silence, and the terrible suspicion that the person across from you already knew everything and was simply waiting to see if you'd waste her time denying it.

Pellum Ostrey was forty years old, soft-handed, an under-clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things, third ledger tier, which meant he touched debts but never set them. He had been taken at the east gate with a satchel of correspondence he should not have been carrying: three letters in his own hand, addressed to a broker in Cael Row, mentioning a child. A mute child. A pendant of carved tooth. A bone-carver named Hartwell.

"Master Ostrey," Maren said.

He flinched at his own name, as they always did, as though hearing it confirmed that he still existed, that he was still someone who could be found.

"You wrote to Cael Row four times in eleven days. That is more correspondence than your office generates in a season." She turned one of the letters over on the table, its wax seal broken, its contents already copied into her own neat script. "You are not a man of initiative. I've read your service record. You have never once volunteered for anything, including a commendation you were owed. So either someone paid you very well—" she let the pause sit "—or someone frightened you very badly. Which was it?"

"I don't—" His voice cracked. He wiped his nose with the back of his bound wrist. "I don't know what you think I've done."

"That's not an answer. But it is interesting. Because it means you don't know *why* you did it, only that you had to." She leaned back. The candlelight moved across her face, and she watched him watch her—the grey coat, the silver pin of the Office of Erasure, the thin white scar that ran from behind her left ear down into her collar, where no amount of collar could quite hide it. People always looked at the scar eventually. She had stopped minding. It was, in its way, the most honest thing about her.

"The child," she said. "Describe him."

"He's just a boy. Ten, maybe eleven. He doesn't talk—I told Corvane that, I told him the boy can't even testify against us, he's mute, he's nothing—"

"What does he look like?"

"Small. Dark hair. Thin. There's—" Pellum's eyes went somewhere else, some interior room where a memory stood waiting, and Maren saw the exact moment he decided whether to share it. Fear won. "There's something about his hands. When he holds them up, like this—" Pellum raised his bound wrists awkwardly, fingers spread "—like he's asking for something without words. Like he's done it before. Like it's a whole language and everyone forgot it except him."

Maren wrote nothing down. She didn't need to.

"And the pendant?"

"A tooth. Carved all over with marks too small to read. The carver—Hartwell—he went white when he saw it. I've known Wendell Hartwell nine years and I've never seen him go white. He looked like a man reading his own death warrant in a language he'd hoped was extinct."

*Extinct.* Maren turned the word over. It was such a guild word. The guild dealt in extinct things—extinct countries, extinct oaths, extinct names—and pretended this made them harmless antiquarians rather than smugglers of the unmade.

"Tell me about Hartwell," she said. "Not the guild record. The man."

Pellum blinked. Of all the questions, this was the one that undid him a little; she could see it. Interrogators were supposed to want names and dates and routes. Asking after the soul of a bone-carver confused people, and confusion loosened tongues better than pain ever had.

"He keeps to himself. He hears—" Pellum stopped. Started again. "He does work for grieving families. Carves bones from their dead so they can say goodbye properly. Everyone knows it's a trick. Stagecraft. Cold reading. But I've seen the letters those carvings produce. Things no cold reader could know. My sister bought one, after her husband drowned. The carving knew about the scar on his knee from when he was nine. Knew he used to sing to the horses. Knew—" Pellum's voice dropped. "Knew he forgave her. For the argument. The last one. She'd carried it two years."

"And you believe the dead speak through him."

"I believe something speaks through him." Pellum looked up, and for the first time there was something in his face besides fear—a raw, offended kind of wonder, the look of a bookkeeper confronted with an entry that would not balance. "Inquisitor, why does it matter? The boy is mute. The carver is a tradesman. Neither of them has broken any law I know of."

Maren rose, walked to the door, and dismissed the guard outside with a small gesture. Then she came back and sat down again, closer than before, and took off her gloves, finger by finger, laying them on the table like a woman preparing for surgery or prayer.

"I'm going to tell you a story, Master Ostrey," she said. "You will not repeat it. Not because I'll punish you if you do—though I will—but because no one would believe you, and you'd spend the rest of your life being laughed at by people who deserve worse. Do you understand?"

He nodded.

"I was born in a refugee camp outside Marrow Ford. Twenty-two years ago. Before your time in the guild, probably before your time anywhere. My parents came out of the east in the last column before the borders closed. They came from Vhalen."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Pellum actually leaned away from it.

"There is no Vhalen," he said automatically. Every citizen said it automatically. It was printed in schoolbooks, preached from pulpits, carved over courthouse doors: *There is no Vhalen. There never was. Speak not of it.*

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "There isn't. And yet my mother sang me to sleep every night in a language that doesn't exist, and my father prayed in it, and the old women in the camp argued in it about the price of fish in towns that were never on any map. Do you know what the Decree of Unmaking actually does, Master Ostrey? Not the pulpit version. The mechanics."

"They... strike it from the records. From memory."

"They strike it from the *world*. Records, maps, gravestones, songs. And then—and this is the part the priests leave out—it strikes it from the living. Slowly. A country is not erased in a day; it is erased in a generation. First you forget the name of the king. Then the shape of the flag. Then your grandmother's lullaby becomes humming, and then the humming stops, and one morning you cannot remember why your mother wept at the river, and neither can she, and the grief is just weather with no country underneath it." Maren folded her bare hands. "But children hear longer than adults. Children's ears haven't been taught what not to hear. And in the camps, the Vhaleni children—"

She stopped. For a moment—three seconds, perhaps four—her composure shifted, and Pellum Ostrey saw underneath the grey coat and the silver pin, and what he saw frightened him far more than anything the crying hours had prepared him for.

"—the Vhaleni children could hear the dead," she finished quietly. "Because Vhalen's dead had nowhere to go. You cannot bury a nation that has been unmade. Its dead stand in the ditches and the doorways of the world, waiting for a country to be buried in. And the children born in exile, half-rooted in the old tongue, could hear them. Singing. Counting. Saying their names over and over, because a name is the last thing erasure takes."

Pellum's mouth was dry. "What happened to them? The children."

"The Crown found a use for us, at first. Little listeners in the border camps, cataloguing ghosts, confirming that the erasure was taking hold. I was good at it. I was seven years old and I could tell you the name of every soldier who died in the Sere crossing, because they told me themselves, night after night, in a language I understood before I understood I shouldn't." Her thumb moved, briefly, to the scar below her ear. "And then a new spymaster came to the Office of Erasure. A practical man. He asked a question no one had asked before: if the children can hear the dead of Vhalen, how long until one of them hears something *else*? Something the erasure missed. A door. An heir. A way back."

"And so he—"

"Ordered the hearing cut out of every registered listener child in the camps. Forty-one of us. It was framed as mercy, Master Ostrey. Write that down in whatever ledger you keep behind your eyes. They called it mercy. *Better deaf than haunted. Better empty than a door.*" She smiled, and it was the worst smile Pellum had ever seen, precise and bloodless as a signature. "I was the last. I remember the surgeon's hands. I remember that the dead were screaming my name while he worked, and I remember that afterward, for the first time since I was born, the world was quiet. Do you know what I felt?"

Pellum shook his head.

"Gratitude." She picked up her gloves again. "That is what erasure is, in the end. Not violence. Gratitude for the silence afterward. The Crown understands something the guild has never grasped: you cannot fight a haunting. You can only make the haunted thankful it's over."

* * *

They walked afterward—she and Pellum, under guard, through the covered way from the Guildhall cells to the records annex—because Maren thought best on her feet, and because she wanted him to see the archive.

The annex held the Decree's master ledgers: the great black books in which every act of unmaking was entered, cross-referenced, and confirmed. Shelf upon shelf of them, going back twenty-two years, each volume thicker than a bread oven. Clerks moved among the stacks with lamps, verifying entries, striking anomalies. It was the most silent room in the capital, because sound behaved strangely here, as though the paper drank it.

"Twenty-two years ago," Maren said, walking, "the first entry was made. The founding act. Do you know what it says?"

"Nobody's allowed to read the first volume. Not even—"

"Not even the Lord Inquisitor. Correct. Only the King, and whoever holds the Office of Erasure's original seal." She stopped at the iron gate of the restricted stacks and rested one gloved hand on the bars. "But I've stood where you're standing, Master Ostrey, for eleven years, and I've read every entry since the second volume, and I will tell you what the pattern shows, because patterns are the one thing they couldn't cut out of me. Every unmaking has a counterweight. Every debt the Decree writes, something is owed against it. The ledgers call it *the balance of the door*. And in twenty-two years, that balance has never once been marked paid."

Pellum frowned. "Paid by whom?"

"By anyone. That's the horror of it. Somewhere in the east there is a door that was closed with an unpaid debt, and the books have been waiting a generation for someone to settle it. And now—" she turned, and the lamp-light caught the scar, caught the silver pin, caught the flat grey patience of her eyes "—now a mute child walks into a guild shop wearing a dead man's tooth, and the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things begin writing entries that no one enters. Entries in faded ink. Entries for Vhalen."

"How do you know that?"

"Because the guildmaster's letters to the crown weren't the only correspondence out of that shop. Hartwell's ledgers talk to our ledgers, Master Ostrey. Debt recognizes debt. Three nights ago, a verification clerk in this annex found a page in Volume Nine that hadn't been there before. Faded ink. Old hand. An entry reopening itself like a wound." She began walking again, faster now, her boots ringing on the stone. "The child is not mute, by the way. I need you to understand that. Whatever you saw in that shop, whatever Corvane told you—the boy is not mute. He is *sealed*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire reason I am riding east tomorrow."

"Sealed," Pellum repeated. "Sealed how?"

"With a voice that opens doors. With a voice that costs." She pushed open the door to the courtyard, where the evening rain had begun, fine and cold, beading on the grey shoulders of her coat. A groom was walking her horse in circles under the archway, saddled, provisioned, ready. "When I was a listening child, the dead told me things. Names. Warnings. Once—an old woman in the camp, dead thirty years—told me that Vhalen would not die while one heir drew breath beyond the erasure's reach. I reported it, because I was seven and loyal and stupid. Two weeks later, the order came down for the surgeries. I have always wondered, Master Ostrey, whether that old woman's warning is the reason I have this scar. Whether I heard the truth of the boy before the boy existed, and the Crown cut it out of me to make sure I'd never recognize him again."

She mounted, gathered the reins, and looked down at the trembling clerk for a long moment.

"So here is what you will carry back to your guildmaster, when he buys his way out of my custody, as men like Corvane always do. Tell him the inquisitor Voss is coming east, and that she does not want the boy's pendant, or the boy's bloodline, or the boy's value to brokers and kings. Tell him I want the boy's voice silenced before it finds its door—because I have stood inside the silence where a country used to be, and I am telling you, as one professional of erased things to another: what waits behind that door is not a nation. It is a debt. And debts of that size are never collected from the guilty."

"Then who—" Pellum started.

"From whoever is standing nearest when it comes due." She turned the horse toward the east gate, where the rain was falling harder, where the road ran out through the wet dark toward Ashfield and the river and the ferry that asked its terrible tolls. "It was supposed to be me, Master Ostrey. Forty-one children, and I was the last one cut, and I have spent twenty-two years being grateful for the quiet. And now there is a boy who can reopen all of it—the screaming, the ditches full of waiting dead, the grief with a country under it again—and everyone in the capital calls that treason, and I alone understand that it is worse."

"Worse how?" Pellum called after her.

She did not slow the horse. Rain ran off the brim of her hood, and the scar beneath her ear ached the way it always ached before a storm, the old wound remembering what had been taken, the way all wounds remember.

"Because he's going to offer it to us," she said. "Mercy. All of it, given back. And God help me, Master Ostrey—some nights, in the quiet where my hearing used to be—I am afraid I'll want to take it."

She rode east into the rain, and behind her the clerk stood in the courtyard a long while, holding letters he suddenly understood were worth nothing, listening to a silence that, for the first time in his life, felt less like peace and more like a held breath.
