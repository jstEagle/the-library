## 9. The Ferryman's Toll

The river Sere did not appear on any map that Aldric's cartographers had drawn, and this was precisely why it still existed.

Wendell had known about it the way he knew about most impossible things: through the ledgers. There was an entry in a volume three hundred years old, written in a clerk's careful hand — *Crossing of the Sere, toll one memory, no exceptions recorded* — and beneath it, in different ink, someone had added: *The ferryman does not take what you offer. He takes what you would least survive losing.*

He had read that line forty times since Ashfield. Each time, he'd told himself it was a warning meant for other people.

Now he stood on the western bank with Silas beside him and watched the water move wrong. The Sere didn't flow so much as *remember* flowing — slow, silver-grey, carrying leaves upstream and down at once, as if the river couldn't decide which direction its past ran. Mist hung over it in shapes that were almost figures. Almost faces. Wendell kept his eyes on the middle distance, where they could do no harm.

The boy had not spoken since the rat-catcher. Not in words, anyway. But he had learned to say things without them: a tug on Wendell's sleeve meaning *food*, a pointed finger meaning *smoke*, and now a hand pressed flat against Wendell's chest, meaning something Wendell chose not to translate.

*I'm fine,* he wanted to tell him. *I'm always fine. That's the whole trick of me.*

Instead he said, "We wait for the boat. And when it comes, you let me do the talking."

Silas looked up at him with those grey eyes that held too much weather for a child's face, and nodded once. Then, after a moment, he reached into his coat and brought out the tooth pendant — the last relic of Vhalen, the bone that had started everything — and held it out.

"No," Wendell said quietly. "Not that. Never that. It stays with you or it stays nowhere."

The boy turned it over in his small fingers, studying it as though seeing it fresh. Then he tucked it away, and together they watched the mist gather itself into a shape with oars.

* * *

The ferryman was old the way rivers are old — not wrinkled but worn, his face smooth in some places and deeply channelled in others, like stone that had argued with water for centuries. His boat was black wood gone silver at the edges, wide enough for six passengers though no other passengers ever came. He poled out of the mist without sound, and when the hull kissed the bank, it made no ripple.

"Two," the ferryman said. His voice came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, the way voices do in dreams. "East side wants two tolls."

"We've coin," Wendell said. He had guild scrip, crown coin, even a few pieces of pre-Decree currency that collectors would weep over. He fanned them out. "Name your rate."

The ferryman did not look at the money. He looked at Wendell the way a butcher looks at a carcass, assessing cuts. "Coin buys passage on rivers that flow one way. This river flows both. Coin can't pay a debt in two directions." He extended one long hand, palm up. "Memory. One each. Or swim."

"You said—" Wendell stopped himself. The ledger hadn't said anything; the ledger had been written by clerks who'd never stood on this bank. "One each. How do we choose?"

"You don't." The ferryman's mouth moved into something that might have been a smile on a living face. "I reach in. I take what grips."

Cold walked down Wendell's spine. "And if there's nothing I can spare?"

"There is always something everyone can spare. That's the cruelty of it." The ferryman gestured with his chin toward the boat. "In. We talk while I pole. The river likes to be talked to."

They boarded. The bench was cold through their clothes, and the moment the boat left the bank, the western shore began to look less like a place and more like a painting of one — colours flattening, details smoothing over, as if the Sere dissolved not just distance but certainty. Silas pressed close against Wendell's side. Wendell put an arm around him and found the boy's shoulders trembling, and pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice was sometimes the only kindness available.

"How does it work?" Wendell asked. "The taking."

"I put my hand on your head. I follow the thread down. I pull one loose." The ferryman poled steadily, his oar entering the water without splash. "It doesn't hurt. That's what people hate most, afterward. They expect it to hurt, and instead there's just... a room in the house of you, empty, and you can't remember what furniture was in it."

"And the rest of us? What happens to what you take?"

The ferryman considered the question with what seemed like genuine interest. "The river keeps it. Sometimes the river gives it back to the water. Rain falls differently here than anywhere else in the world. Old men swear the rain smells like their weddings." He shrugged, a motion like a hillside settling. "Nothing is wasted. Nothing is forgiven, either. Those are different services."

Silas tugged Wendell's sleeve and pointed at the mist-shapes drifting alongside the boat. Up close, they resolved into half-faces — mouths moving, hands raised, expressions caught between greeting and grief. Memories, Wendell realized. Taken memories, swimming beside the boat like fish that had once been birds.

One of them drifted near Silas and paused. It was small — a child-sized wisp, golden at the edges — and it hovered before the boy with an attentiveness that made Wendell's chest tighten. Silas reached out one finger. The wisp touched it, shivered, and sank beneath the surface with something that was almost a sigh.

"They know him," Wendell said softly. Not a question.

"Everything taken knows everything given," the ferryman said. "Your boy has a country's worth of given things in him. The river's been waiting a long time for a passenger like that." He fixed Wendell with a look that had depth but no bottom. "Good thing you're only paying for yourself."

* * *

The crossing took an hour or a day; the light refused to commit. Somewhere in the middle of the river, where the mist thickened until the world was the width of the boat, the ferryman shipped his oar and said, "West first."

Wendell swallowed. "Do it."

"Any last words? People like last words. Gives the memory something to hold onto while it's pulled."

Wendell thought about it. He thought about rules — hear the dead, never answer them; keep the ledgers; stay invisible — and how every rule he'd built his life on had been scaffolding around one central truth: *no one must ever have a reason to look at Wendell Hartwell.* He thought about a shop in a narrow street, and a woman who had smelled of bread and lamp-oil, and—

No. Don't go there. Don't hand it a map.

"Just do it," he said.

The ferryman's hand settled on his head, cool and dry as paper. And then Wendell felt the thread — felt it distinctly, a filament running down through his skull into the woven mass of everything he was, and the ferryman's fingers closed around it gently, the way one lifts a hair from a sleeping child's face.

*What grips,* the ledger had said. Wendell braced for his mother. For the shop. For the first time he'd heard a dead voice and answered it, the sin that had made him a fugitive from his own life.

The pull came.

It was — the ferryman had been right — painless. That was the horror of it. A soft tug, a sensation like a word on the tip of the tongue finally arriving, and then:

Rain.

Rain on cobbles. Rain on the slate roof of a crooked house at the end of a street whose name he suddenly could not— no, the name was there, the street was there, but the *smell* was gone. That particular smell — hot dust meeting the first fat drops, the green exhale of gutters, the way the whole world seemed to open its mouth after weeks of drought — gone. Lifted clean out of him like a splinter drawn by a skilled hand. He could remember that it had rained. He could remember that he had loved it. He could remember standing in the doorway of the crooked house, age seven or eight, arms out, mouth open to the sky.

But the smell itself — the smell that had been, more than any face or voice, the signature of being young and safe and unafraid — was a hole shaped exactly like itself.

"That's it?" Wendell heard himself ask. His voice sounded far away. "That's all you took?"

The ferryman withdrew his hand and studied him. "You offered a big life, carver. Big lives grip shallow, if they're honest ones. The deep things in you are nailed down." Something flickered across the worn face — respect, or its fossil. "You've already paid more than you know. The river only collects what's owed to *it*."

Wendell sat very still. In his mind, he walked the crooked street in the rain and felt nothing rise to meet him, and understood that he would walk it in his mind ten thousand times and feel nothing every time, and that this was the toll: not the loss, but the endless discovery of the loss.

"It doesn't hurt," he said. "You're right. That's the worst part."

"Yes." The ferryman almost sounded sorry. "East side next. The little king pays too."

"He's not—" Wendell started, then stopped, because arguing the point felt like handing the river a knife. "Be gentle with him. He's carried enough."

"Everyone says that." The ferryman turned to Silas. "Everyone's carried enough. The river doesn't weigh. It takes."

Silas didn't flinch. That was the thing about the boy that undid Wendell again and again — he met horror the way stone meets rain, absorbing it, being shaped by it, refusing to be washed away. He climbed up onto the bench to meet the ferryman's hand, chin lifted, grey eyes steady.

The hand touched his head. The thread went down.

And Wendell watched the boy's face as the pull came, and saw — nothing leave. No flicker, no gap, no hole opening behind his eyes. The ferryman frowned. Pulled again, harder. The mist around the boat stirred, and the little golden wisp rose from the water and hovered between them, and the ferryman's hand jerked back as if burned.

"What is it?" Wendell demanded. "What did you find?"

The ferryman stared at Silas for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its dreamlike diffusion and become, briefly, terribly human. "Nothing grips. Everything in him is load-bearing." He shook his head slowly. "There's no memory in that child that isn't holding up a wall. Take one and the whole country comes down."

"So he crosses free?"

"He crosses because the river won't touch him." The ferryman took up his oar again, and his voice returned to its everywhere-and-nowhere register, but the unease stayed underneath it like a stone under moss. "Free isn't the same as untouched, carver. Remember that. Free is just a debt with better manners."

* * *

The eastern bank arrived the way mornings arrive in winter — gradually, then all at once. Colour flooded back into the world. Grass. Mud. A road, rutted and real, running east toward hills that were only hills, thank every merciful dead thing.

Wendell stepped off the boat and his boots hit solid ground and he nearly wept at the ordinariness of it. Behind him, the mist was already folding the ferry back into the river's keeping.

"The city of Cael Row," the ferryman called, poling backward into the fog. "Three days east, if the roads behave. Ask for the branch guild at the sign of the braided candle. And carver—" the mist swallowed him to the shoulders, and his voice came thin and strange across the water, "—when you get there, count what you're carrying. All of you. Count it twice. Some debts travel faster than ferries."

Then the mist closed, and the Sere was just a grey ribbon behind them, and the road ahead was mud and stones and silence.

They walked. Silas matched his stride without complaint, the way he'd done since Ashfield, and after a mile Wendell realized the boy kept glancing up at him — quick, worried looks, cataloguing him. Checking him for holes.

"I'm fine," Wendell said, and heard how hollow it rang.

Silas stopped walking. Tugged his sleeve. Pointed at Wendell's chest, then at the sky, then made a shape with his hands — cupped, tilted, catching — and mimed breathing in, deep and wondering.

*Rain,* the gesture said. *Something about rain.*

"You can't know that," Wendell said. "You weren't there when it—"

But the boy was nodding, patient, insistent, and tapped the pendant through his coat — *the bones tell me, the bones tell me everything* — and Wendell understood that Silas knew exactly what the ferryman had taken, knew it the way he knew the language of dead countries: by resonance. By the hum of what was missing.

The boy took his hand. Small fingers, ink-stained, calloused in ways no child's should be. He squeezed once, hard, and looked up with an expression that needed no translation in any tongue, living or dead.

*I see what it cost. I will not waste it.*

Wendell cleared his throat and discovered that his eyes had decided, without consulting him, to sting. "Come on," he said gruffly, walking on. "Three days to Cael Row. And when we get there, we eat something that didn't come out of my pack, and we sleep behind a real door, and we don't trust anyone with a ledger, a scale, or a smile. Agreed?"

Silas fell into step beside him. After a moment, he tugged the sleeve again and pointed ahead, to the east, where the hills rolled gold-green under a sky full of ordinary, unremarkable, gloriously forgettable clouds.

And then he stopped.

His whole body went rigid. His hand shot out and gripped Wendell's wrist hard enough to hurt, and he pointed — not at the hills, not at the road, but at the mud of the roadway itself, where the ruts held yesterday's rainwater in long silver lenses.

In the reflection, the sky was wrong. The clouds in the puddles were dark and boiling, and threaded through them moved shapes — tall shapes, robed shapes, walking in formation — and though the actual sky above them was clear and blue and empty, the water showed a column of riders cresting the eastern ridge, moving fast, moving toward Cael Row, moving along the very road they meant to take.

Wendell looked from the puddle to the horizon. Empty. Looked back. The riders were closer.

"The river shows both directions," he breathed. "That's why it flows both ways. It remembers forward too."

Silas's grip tightened. In the water-mirror, at the head of the phantom column, a rider raised her arm, and even reflected and rippled and impossibly distant, Wendell knew the pale gleam of an inquisitor's mask.

Maren Voss. Three days ahead of them, or three days behind, or riding out of the river itself — and either way, between them and Cael Row.

"Off the road," Wendell said. "Now. Off the road and into the trees, and we don't stop until dark."

They ran, and behind them the puddles held their terrible weather, and somewhere in the mist of the Sere, the ferryman counted what they carried, and found it wanting.
