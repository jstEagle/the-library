## 10. A Market of Impossible Things

Cael Row smelled of salt and hot iron and, beneath it all, of things that had no business existing.

Wendell felt it the moment they passed through the harbor gate—the way his carved bones woke in their case against his ribs, humming like a struck tuning fork. In the occupied cities, magic kept its head down. Here, in the last free city on the coast, it hung from every stall like laundry: bottled storms, love letters that wrote themselves, a caged bird singing in a language extinct for three hundred years. A woman sold shadows by weight. A man with silvered eyes offered to trade your worst memory for someone else's best, and half the queue looked tempted.

Silas pressed close to Wendell's side, one small hand fisted in the bone-carver's coat. The boy hadn't spoken—not truly spoken—since Ashfield, since the rat-catcher had walked away wearing a hole where his day used to be. But his eyes moved over the market with something Wendell hadn't seen in them before. Not wonder, exactly. Recognition.

"He knows this," Wendell murmured to no one. "He's seen a market like this."

The tooth pendant at Silas's throat hummed faintly, and the bones translated, as they always did: *green terraces, stalls of woven reed, a crown passed hand to hand.* Vhalen had held markets too, once. The dead remembered festivals even when they could not remember mercy.

"Keep your hood up," Wendell said. "And whatever anyone offers you—"

Silas looked up at him. The look said everything the boy's voice would not: *I know what I'm worth. Do you?*

* * *

The Cael Row branch of the Guild of Impossible Things occupied a building that had clearly given up pretending to be anything else. The shopfront leaned into the street like a drunk telling secrets; its sign was carved from a single whale's vertebra, and the letters rearranged themselves when you weren't looking. Wendell had corresponded with this branch for eleven years and had never once been told the same address twice.

Inside, the air was thick with the smell of beeswax and old paper and something sweeter underneath—preserved time, maybe. Shelves rose three stories into shadow, crammed with jars and vials and lead-lined boxes, each labeled in a dozen hands. A clerk with ink-stained fingers led them past a case of bottled last words and up a spiral stair to a room where a fire burned green.

The woman waiting for them was small and sharp as a paper knife, with white hair cropped close and rings on eight of her ten fingers. Wendell knew her by reputation the way sailors know a storm by its name.

"Mistress Ilvane," he said. "Guildmaster of Cael Row."

"Wendell Hartwell." She smiled without warmth. "The ledger-keeper of the eastern branch. The man who answers no letters twice and sells nothing worth stealing." Her eyes slid to Silas, and Wendell watched her weigh the boy like a merchant weighing coin. "Until now."

"We've come about sanctuary," Wendell said. "Two travelers, tired, pursued. The standard arrangement."

"The standard arrangement." Ilvane laughed—a dry, clicking sound. "Sit down, Hartwell. Let us not insult each other with the standard arrangement. You crossed the Sere. You paid the ferryman. You came through Ashfield, where the ditches are full of men Aldric's army left unburied, and you arrived in my city with—" she gestured elegantly at Silas, "—that. Do you imagine I don't have eyes? Do you imagine my clerks don't read the same ledgers yours do?"

Wendell sat. He kept his face pleasant and empty, the face of a man who had spent thirty years being overlooked. Beside him, Silas stood very still, hood shadowing his face.

"I imagine," Wendell said, "that your clerks read rumors. Rumors are cheaper than facts, and lighter to carry."

"And what is the fact?"

"The fact is a boy who speaks a dead language. Which makes him valuable to collectors, useless to kings, and dangerous to everyone." Wendell spread his hands. "He needs a roof. I need time. The Guild protects its own—that was true when your branch and mine were friends, and I'm told it's still true now, in the way that all old promises are still technically true."

Ilvane studied him for a long moment. Then she reached beneath the table and set something down between them with a soft glass click.

A vial. No bigger than her thumb, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. Inside, something swam—a light, a warmth, a suggestion of music heard through a wall. Wendell felt his chest tighten before he understood why.

"What am I looking at?" he asked, though he knew.

"A wedding," Ilvane said. "Harvest-year, a village called Brindle, back when Brindle existed. The bride sold it herself, after. Said she couldn't carry it through what came next." She turned the vial slowly; inside, the light turned with it, gold going rose going gold. "This is what we do here, Hartwell. This is what the Guild has always done. We keep what people cannot afford to keep themselves. And we sell it to those who can afford the price." Her gaze returned to Silas. "So let us speak plainly about what you carry."

"You mean the pendant."

"I mean the voice." Ilvane leaned forward, rings clicking against the table. "Every courier, every informant, every whisper out of the capital says the same thing: the usurper has burned three provinces looking for a child who can speak Vhalen back into existence. And here he sits, in my city, under my roof, eating my bread." She smiled again, and this time there was nothing dry in it at all. "Sanctuary, Hartwell. Yes. We can offer sanctuary. For a price."

"The Guild doesn't sell children."

"The Guild doesn't *keep* them either. Children are liabilities. They grow. They change the terms." She tapped the table once. "Here are my terms. The boy stays. He eats, sleeps, learns, lives—safe behind walls no inquisitor has ever breached. In exchange, we bottle the voice. One extraction. A single speaking, drawn and sealed, kept in trust. The boy keeps his throat and his silence both. And if King Aldric ever comes to Cael Row with armies—and he will, eventually—we open that vial, we show him what he wants, and he leaves the rest of us alone. It's insurance, Hartwell. For all of us."

Wendell was quiet for a moment. Around them, the green fire crackled, and somewhere below, the market hummed.

"You want to put a nation's last word in a jar," he said finally, "and use it as a shield."

"I want to put a *sound* in a jar," Ilvane corrected. "Nations are made of paper and grief. Sounds are just sounds. The boy loses nothing but one breath."

*One breath.* Wendell thought of Ashfield, of the soldier in the ditch who had begged him to tell his wife about the river, about the willows, about how he'd died facing east. He had answered the dead once and it had nearly killed them both. He thought of the ledgers writing themselves in ink that wouldn't dry, debts accruing against a king's unborn line. He thought of Silas in the ferryboat, watching the smell of rain on a childhood street leave Wendell's face forever, learning exactly what escape cost and who paid it.

The boy had already paid more than any vial could hold.

"One breath," Wendell repeated. He nodded slowly, thoughtfully, letting the lie assemble itself in his mouth like a carving taking shape under a chisel. "You'd need him to speak willingly. An extraction from an unwilling source degrades—I assume your people taught you that."

Something flickered across Ilvane's face. Doubt, quickly filed away. "Of course."

"Then you'll need time. Trust. He hasn't spoken since Ashfield—not a word, not to me, not to himself. Whatever happened on that road broke something, or closed something. You can't rush a well that's gone silent; you'll only draw up mud." Wendell stood, unhurried, a tradesman agreeing to terms he found tiresome but fair. "Give us a week. I'll sit with him. I'll carve for him—you know how it works, the bones loosen what the tongue won't. When he's ready, he speaks once, you seal it, and we're all insured against the end of the world."

Ilvane's eyes narrowed. For three long seconds Wendell felt the whole architecture of his lie balanced on her suspicion like a coin on its edge.

"A week," she said at last. "Rooms in the east wing. My clerk Tam will attend you." She rose, rings flashing. "And Hartwell—if I learn you're playing me false, I'll sell what's in that boy's throat to the highest bidder and sleep soundly after."

"Madam," Wendell said, bowing slightly, "I am a ledger-keeper. Falsehood is the only honest work I've ever done."

* * *

Their rooms were warm and windowed and full of other people's history. The bedframes had been carved from ship's timber; the blankets smelled of lavender and, faintly, of someone else's childhood. Silas sat on the floor cross-legged while Wendell checked the door, the shutters, the seams of the walls—for listening charms, for hair-thin wires of spelled copper. He found two and crushed them between his fingers, feeling the little deaths buzz out of them.

When he turned, Silas was holding up the washbasin's water pitcher, angling it so the lamplight bent through the glass, and looking at Wendell with an expression of furious, eloquent question.

"No," Wendell said. "We're not giving them anything."

Silas set the pitcher down and folded his arms.

"They'd keep their word about the walls," Wendell admitted, sitting across from him. "Ilvane is ruthless, but she's precise. That's the most dangerous kind. But a bottled voice isn't insurance, lad—it's a hostage. The day Aldric's spymaster walks in here with enough gold, that vial goes south, and everything your voice could have done—everything it still might do—it does for him instead. Sealed. Owned. Aimed." He rubbed his eyes. "I've watched the Guild sell oaths to tyrants before. It's always framed as survival. It's always *true*, in the way that matters to the seller."

Silas reached out and touched Wendell's wrist. Then he pointed—at Wendell's coat pocket, where the ledger of small debts lived. Then at his own throat. Then he drew, slowly, in the dust on the floorboards, a shape Wendell recognized with a lurch: the reed-woven crown. Above it, a line. A door, standing open.

"You want to speak," Wendell said quietly. "Not for them. For the door."

Silas nodded once.

"Then we don't give them a drop of it. Not one breath." Wendell looked at the door, at the crushed listening charms, at the boy who had carried a murdered country in his mouth across half a burning map. "We need a week. So we give them a week of nothing. And meanwhile—" he allowed himself, for the first time in days, the ghost of a smile, "—we go shopping."

* * *

The market at night was a different animal. By day it sold wonders; by night it sold regrets. The stalls stayed lit, but the crowds thinned to the desperate and the curious, and the vendors' smiles sharpened. Wendell moved through it with Silas's hand in his, past a stall selling bottled courage at ruinous prices, past a woman auctioning the taste of a fruit that no longer grew anywhere, down an alley where the lanterns burned blue and the wares on the tables were the kind that changed owners whether or not money changed hands.

The memory-brokers worked the blue-lit alleys. Wendell had dealt with their kind before—every branch of the Guild did—but Cael Row's brokers were famous for one particular vice: they bought stolen goods and asked no questions, because questions degraded resale value.

He found what he wanted on the third table. A tray of vials, each glowing its private color, each tagged in a broker's slanted hand. Griefs, mostly. First loves. A mother's kitchen. And near the edge, mislabeled as *harvest festival, minor*, a vial of deep gold shot through with rose—

A wedding. Brindle, if the tag was to be believed, though brokers lied about provenance the way rivers lied about depth. Two lives braided into one sealed breath: the vows, the dancing, the terrible joy of it. Someone had sold their wedding day to eat, probably. Or to forget. Or because a broker had taken it from them, which amounted to the same thing.

Wendell thought of Ilvane's vial on the table upstairs, turning its light like a promise. He thought of the Guild's first law, older than any ledger: *what is taken must be returned, or the taker pays.*

"Buying or browsing?" The broker was a narrow man with teeth like a fence.

"Browsing," Wendell said. "Though my employer collects. Weddings especially. Sentimental fool." He picked up the vial, turned it, frowned professionally. "Brindle? There was no wedding in Brindle that year. The village was already ash. This is a fake—or a forgery of a feeling, which is worse."

The broker's smile flickered. "Provenance papers say otherwise."

"Provenance papers in this city say whatever the ink was paid to say. I've authenticated for three branches of the Guild, friend, and I can tell you that glow is wrong. Real wedding-light pools at the bottom, like honey. This floats. Suspended. Suspended means stabilized, and stabilized means diluted with filler—grief, usually, it's cheap." He set the vial down with a small contemptuous click. "My employer would laugh me out of the counting-house."

"Five crowns," the broker said quickly.

"For a forgery? Three."

They settled at four, and Wendell paid in coin that had been washed of its history years ago, and walked away with a stranger's wedding day in his pocket, his heart hammering with a joy so sharp it was almost shame. Behind him the broker was already re-tagging the tray, and ahead of him Silas looked up with wide eyes, and Wendell realized the boy had watched the entire performance—the frown, the jargon, the contempt—and was seeing him, perhaps for the first time, as something other than a frightened man with a bag of bones.

*Liar,* Silas's expression said, delighted.

"Ledger-keeper," Wendell corrected gravely. "It's a related discipline."

* * *

They did not return straight to the east wing. Wendell took the long way, along the sea-wall, where the wind off the water scrubbed the market's sweetness from the air. Below the wall, the tide worked at the rocks, patient as an inquisitor.

He stopped at a stretch of parapet where no one could see, and he opened the vial.

He didn't unseal it—he wasn't a monster, and he wasn't a thief in the way that mattered. He simply held it up to the moonlight and let the light within answer the light without, and for a moment he imagined he could hear it: fiddles, laughter, rain on canvas awnings, a girl laughing the way people only laugh once in a life if they're lucky. Somewhere in that gold was a person who had woken one morning with a wedding and gone to sleep without it, and who perhaps no longer knew why certain songs made them cry.

"Tomorrow," Wendell said to Silas, "we find out who sold this, or who it was taken from. If they sold it freely, we return it anyway and let them choose again—that's the law, and I've broken enough laws lately that I'd like to keep one. If it was taken—" his jaw tightened, "—then the broker owes a debt, and I keep the ledgers, and I collect."

Silas touched the vial, then his own pendant, then pressed his small fist briefly over Wendell's heart. The bones in Wendell's case hummed, translating something the boy didn't have words for in any tongue: *you give back what others take.*

"Somebody has to," Wendell said. "It's the only arithmetic I've ever trusted."

They stood together at the rail while the tide breathed below, two fugitives in a free city, holding a stolen joy between them like a lamp. For a few minutes—one of the last few, though neither knew it—Wendell let himself feel something dangerously close to safety. The Guild's walls above them. The sea at their backs. A week bought with a beautiful lie.

It was Silas who saw it first. The boy went rigid, his grip closing hard on Wendell's sleeve, and turned toward the harbor road.

A carriage was coming up from the docks—black lacquer, no crest, drawn by gray horses that steamed in the night air as if they'd been driven hard and far. It rolled past the sea-wall without slowing, and in the gap of its curtained window Wendell caught a glimpse of a face lit by a traveling lamp: heavy-jawed, familiar, wearing an expression of patient grievance that Wendell had known for twenty years across a guild counter.

Corvane.

His guildmaster. His friend. The man who had sold them to the capital—here, in Cael Row, days ahead of any reasonable schedule, as if the letter had grown wings.

The carriage turned up the hill, toward the leaning shopfront with the whale-vertebra sign, toward Mistress Ilvane and her green fire and her terms.

Beside him, Silas made a sound—not speech, not quite, but the pendant rang like a struck bell, and every stall in the alley below went suddenly, utterly quiet, as if the whole market had held its breath.

"Well," Wendell said softly, sliding the wedding-day vial deep into his pocket. "That didn't take a week at all."
