## 13. Fire in the Stacks

Maren Voss had burned eleven guildhouses in her career, and she had learned that every one of them died differently.

The Cael Row branch did not go quietly. The stacks caught with a sound like a held breath released—a long, wet exhalation of old paper and older vellum—and then the whole building seemed to lean into its own destruction, as if it had been waiting years for permission. Embers rose in spirals through the broken skylight, and for one absurd moment the fire looked almost beautiful, a garden of orange flowers blooming upward through the dark.

She stood across the canal and watched it, hands folded behind her back, and told herself what she always told herself: *this is mercy.*

The dead were a wound. She knew this better than anyone wearing the crown's gray coat. She had been born hearing them—had spent her first seven years unable to walk past a graveyard without weeping at conversations no one else could hear, had learned to sleep with wax in her ears and still woke screaming when the drowned spoke through the well water. Her grandmother had sung to her then, in a language Maren was later forbidden to name, songs about reeds and rivers and a country that was never called by name because naming it was itself a crime now. *Sleep, little fish, the river keeps you.* That was all she remembered of it. A few notes. A handful of words whose meanings had been cut out of her along with everything else.

They had taken the hearing when she was eight. Not metaphorically. The Order's surgeons had worked on her for two days in a white room in the capital, and when it was done, the world was quiet in a way the world had never been quiet before. She remembered the silence arriving like snowfall. She remembered thanking them.

That was the part she never said aloud, not even in her reports: *she had thanked them.*

So when the order came down from the capital—*the boy lives, the boy speaks the old tongue, bring him or end him*—Maren had read it twice and felt nothing she could name. Only a kind of pressure behind the sternum, like a door leaning on its hinges.

*Bring him or end him.* The capital wanted the voice. Aldric wanted the door. And Maren Voss, who had once heard the dead chattering in the walls of her childhood like sparrows in an eave, understood exactly what the crown intended to do with a child who could open a country: they would use him until he was empty, and then they would erase him too, the way they had erased everything else, and call it peace.

She had decided, somewhere on the road east, that if it came to a choice, she would prefer the second option. Ending was cleaner than using. She had built her whole doctrine on that. Erasure is mercy. A thing unmade cannot be made to suffer.

She repeated it to herself now, watching the guildhouse burn, and waited for the boy to run.

They always ran. That was the shape of these nights. You fired the stacks, you sealed the exits but one, and you stood in the alley beyond the last door with your blade drawn and your lantern hooded, and you let the fear do the work. Children came out of burning buildings like water finding a crack. All you had to do was be the crack.

The side door banged open at the third bell after midnight.

But it wasn't the boy who came out first. It was Wendell Hartwell—the bone-carver, the ledger-keeper, the man whose file she had memorized down to the ink stains on his fingers—and he came out backward, which was wrong, which meant he was covering someone, and Maren felt the night tilt toward its purpose.

"Go," Hartwell said, to the dark behind him. "Not that way. The canal path."

And Silas came out of the firelight.

He was smaller than the descriptions. Eight, perhaps nine, though famine and flight had worn him down to something more like six. He wore a coat three sizes too large with the sleeves rolled in thick cuffs, and his face was soot-streaked except where tears had cut clean channels through the grime. He carried nothing. No pendant—the tooth was gone, sold or hidden or spent—but he didn't need it. Maren had read enough of the interrogations to know the truth the clerks kept circling: the voice lived in the boy, not the charm. The charm had only ever been a key left in a lock.

Hartwell saw her lantern light and went very still. She watched him do the arithmetic—two exits, four inquisitors, a canal at his back—and she watched him arrive at the answer everyone arrived at eventually, which was that there wasn't one.

"Voss," he said. His voice was steady. She gave him credit for that. "You don't have to do this."

"Everyone says that." She stepped out of the alley mouth, and her squad fanned behind her, torches low, blades bare. "It has never once changed anything."

"He's a child."

"He's a door." She kept her tone flat, official, the tone of reports. "You've read the same decrees I have, carver. A nation that was unmade by lawful edict does not get to knock. Whatever speaks in that boy's throat isn't a language anymore. It's a debt coming due, and debts are how wars start."

"And you think burning him settles it?"

"I think," Maren said, "that a thing which cannot exist cannot be conscripted. Yes."

It was even true. That was the trick of her life, the thing that let her sleep: nearly everything she said was true. She had simply arranged the truths into a shape that pointed away from the white room, away from the wax in her ears, away from the eight-year-old who had thanked the surgeons and meant it.

"Silas," Hartwell said quietly. He didn't look away from her. "Behind me. Don't run. Running is what they're waiting for."

The boy didn't move behind him. That was the first strange thing.

The second strange thing was that Silas looked at her—not at the blades, not at the torches, at *her*—with the flat, ancient attention of a child who had seen too much to be frightened in the ordinary way. His eyes were gray-green, the color of canal water over silt, and there was soot on his lashes, and he was breathing hard, and he should have been sobbing. Children sobbed. She had cornered runaways before; they always sobbed, or begged, or went feral.

This one studied her the way she studied ledgers.

Then he opened his mouth, and Maren braced for the word—the unmaking word, the rat-catcher's word, the one that took the day out of a man's head and left him standing in an empty hour—and instead what came out was:

*"Slaap, lytse fisk, de rivier hâldt dy."*

The world stopped.

Not the world. Her world. The narrow corridor of it she had built brick by brick over twenty years, the corridor with its whitewashed walls and its tidy doctrine and its merciful, merciful silence—that corridor cracked down the middle, and through the crack poured a woman's voice, low and warm and roughened by age, singing in a kitchen that smelled of rye bread and river mud. *Sleep, little fish, the river holds you. Sleep, little fish, the current knows your name.* Her grandmother's hands, brown and knuckled, braiding reeds into a ring. Her grandmother's lap. The specific weight of being small and held in a country that existed, that had streets and taxes and winters and lullabies, that had been *real*, that men in gray coats had signed out of the world with ink and seals while a girl slept with wax in her ears—

Maren's blade arm would not move.

She had trained against this. Every inquisitor trained against pity, against pleading, against the theater of children. But no one had trained her for this, because no one had known, because she had never written it in any report, because the hearing they cut out of her had taken the song with it and she had believed—she had *needed* to believe—it was gone.

It was not gone. It had been waiting in her like a splinter waits in flesh, and the boy had spoken, and the flesh had risen around it all at once, hot and unbearable.

Her grandmother's name surfaced from wherever names go when they are forbidden. *Saakje.* It arrived whole, unbidden, with the smell of the kitchen attached, and Maren made a sound she had never made in front of her squad, a small broken thing in the back of her throat, and lowered her blade.

"Maren." One of hers—Brannic, young, loyal, frightened by the fire—stepped forward. "Inquisitor. The order says—"

"The order," Maren said, and her voice came out steady, which cost her more than trembling would have, "says bring him or end him. Look at him, Brannic. Tell me what you'd bring back to the capital. A boy? Or a weapon that answers to no oath we can swear?" She turned her head slowly, letting each of them feel her gaze. "I will handle this. Fall back to the bridge line. That is not a request."

They hesitated. She was their inquisitor; hesitation had a short life expectancy around her. They withdrew, boots loud on the cobbles, torches shrinking up the lane toward the canal bridge, and then it was only the three of them and the burning building and the sparks rising like reversed snow.

Silas was still watching her. There was ash settling in his hair. He hadn't moved to run. Behind him, Hartwell had gone rigid with a hope he clearly didn't trust, and Maren understood that too—she had seen men hope before, and she had seen what hoping got them.

"You knew," she said to the boy. Her voice was rough. "Somehow you knew."

Silas tilted his head. Then, softly, in the trade-tongue, in a voice rusty from disuse, he said: "The bones sing it. In the shop. Before the fire. A lady's voice, under the floor. She was singing it and crying." He paused. "I'm sorry. I didn't know it would hurt."

"It doesn't hurt."

"That's the first lie anyone's told me in the old tongue's shadow," the boy said, with the grave precision of children quoting adults, "and I know lies now. I know a lot of kinds."

Something in Maren's chest, something structural, gave way.

She thought about the report she would write. She had already written most of it in her head during the ride east, in the honest hours before dawn: *Subject located within the Cael Row branch during its destruction. Subject declined surrender. In the collapse of the eastern stair, subject sustained fatal injuries. Body unrecoverable due to fire. Recommend case closed.* Clean. Unverifiable. The capital was three weeks away and drowning in its own paperwork; Aldric's spymaster cared about doors, and a dead door was a closed file.

It was mutiny. She knew the word precisely. Not the dramatic kind—not knives in the dark, not whispered conspiracies. Just a sentence in a report that was false, signed in her own hand, in her own rank, with her own seal. Twenty years of service traded for one child's breathing. She found, examining the trade the way she examined all trades, that she did not hesitate.

"Listen to me." She crouched, bringing her eyes level with his, and saw Hartwell tense, and ignored him. "There is a ferryman on the Sere who owes me. Tell him Voss's toll is paid. There's a woman in Cael Row's lower town, a dyer named Petra Ilves—one green shutter, blue door. She hates the crown more than she loves her own safety, and she'll hide you in her cart among the wool bales. Go north first, then east. Never south. Do you understand me?"

Silas nodded slowly.

"And the word you used on the rat-catcher." She held his gaze. "Don't use it again unless dying is the other choice. What it takes from people doesn't come back. I know what it takes. I've been on both sides of that particular theft." She stood. "Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Silas said. Then, in the old tongue, soft as banked embers: *"Tankewol."*

Thank you. She knew that much still. Thank you.

Maren Voss turned away before her face could finish whatever it was doing, and walked to the mouth of the alley, and stopped with her back to them, looking at the fire.

"Hartwell," she said. "The eastern stair is down. Use the dye-house roof. And carver—" she did not turn around, "—when you write whatever it is you people write in those books of yours, write that the boy died here. Make it true somewhere, so it can be false everywhere else."

She heard Hartwell exhale, long and shaking. "Why?" he asked. Simply. As if the question were enormous.

Because my grandmother sang that song in a country that your books say never existed. Because someone cut the hearing out of me and I thanked them and I have spent twenty years calling that mercy. Because erasure is not mercy, carver, it is just murder with better handwriting, and I am done signing it.

What she said was: "Because the file closes easier with a body in it. Even a false one. Especially a false one."

She walked up the lane toward the bridge line, where her squad waited with their torches and their faith in her, and behind her the Cael Row branch of the Guild of Impossible Things folded inward on itself with a groan like a dying animal, and the sparks went up and up into the dark, and none of them came down.

* * *

She wrote the report at dawn, in the barracks at the edge of the free city, with her squad sleeping off the night's work two rooms away.

*Subject Silas, heir-designate of the unmade territory, located within the Cael Row branch during its sanctioned destruction. Subject refused surrender and attempted escape via the eastern stair, which failed under fire load. Subject sustained fatal injuries in the collapse. Remains unrecoverable. Case recommendation: closed. No further heirs of record. The door, without a speaker, is stone.*

She read it three times. She signed it. She pressed her seal into the wax and watched the gray crest set, and felt nothing except a vast, ringing quiet, like the silence the surgeons had given her, like snowfall.

Mutiny, she thought. Filed and stamped. Somewhere in the capital a clerk would shelve it between a grain dispute and a border incident, and the boy would pass out of the empire's knowledge forever, alive and unwritten. It was, she reflected, the only kind of mercy the age allowed: the mercy of the false entry. The dead were lucky that way. Nothing could be done to them anymore.

She was sealing the envelope when Brannic knocked and entered, saluting. "The carver, Inquisitor. We searched the rubble at first light. No remains matching him either. And the dyer's house on Tanner's Lane—" Brannic hesitated. "Green shutters. Blue door. It's empty. Cart gone."

Maren looked at him for a long moment.

"Then the fire was thorough," she said, "and the trail is ash. Write it up. Both of them dead in the collapse. That's an order, Sergeant, and it's the last order I'll give on this file."

Brannic saluted and withdrew, and Maren sat alone with the morning coming gray through the window, and touched, briefly, with two fingers, the place below her ear where the scars were.

*Sleep, little fish,* she thought, in a language with no country. *The river holds you.*

Far to the east, on a moor road she would never see, a boy with ash still in his hair was walking north beside a bone-carver who owed the dead more than he could pay—and behind them, in the cooling ruin of the Cael Row branch, one shelf of ledgers had survived the fire untouched, its entries rewriting themselves in fresh black ink, and the newest line, appearing in a hand no clerk could identify, read simply:

*Debt acknowledged. Witness: M. Voss.*
