## 14. Halfway to Nowhere

The moor began where the map ended.

Wendell had known that before they reached it—had felt it in the way the ledgers in his pack went quiet, the ink settling like silt in still water—but knowing a thing and walking into it were different countries. The road east of Ashfield simply stopped being a road. One moment there was gravel under his boots and a drainage ditch cut by some long-dead surveyor's ambition; the next moment there was only heather and gorse and the low grey sky pressing down like a lid on a pot.

"Maps are debts," he said, mostly to himself. "Someone drew this once. Someone paid for the drawing."

Silas looked up at him. The boy had not spoken—not truly spoken—since the rat-catcher in the river town, since the word that had emptied a man's whole day out of him like water from a jug. He communicated instead with his hands, with the tilt of his head, with the tooth pendant at his throat, which hummed now in a register Wendell had come to think of as *caution*. It was humming caution now.

"I know," Wendell said. "I feel it too."

He did. That was the trouble. For three days something had been wrong inside him, a wrongness he could not locate, like a stone in a boot worn so long the foot had grown around it. He kept reaching for things and finding them gone—or worse, finding them *smoothed over*, the way water smooths a stone, so that what remained looked natural, complete, as though nothing had ever been missing.

It was Tam who showed him first.

Tam Fenwick. Wool merchant out of Cael Row, fat and laughing, a man who owed the guild eleven years of back payments on a bottled grief and who had once, drunk on plum wine, sworn that Wendell Hartwell was the only honest thief in the trade. They had walked together for two days after the ferry, Tam heading home the long way around the Sere's marshes, telling stories the whole distance. And on the third morning, when Wendell thought of him—

Nothing came.

Not absence. Absence would have been bearable; absence has edges, and edges can be mapped. What came instead was a *shape* where Tam should have been. A friend-shaped hole wearing a name. Wendell knew the name. Knew it the way you know a word in a language you've forgotten: *Tam Fenwick, wool, Cael Row, plum wine.* But when he tried to put a face to it, his mind offered him a blank, courteous and terrible, like a clerk sliding an empty ledger across a counter and saying, *Here is your account, sir. It is fully paid.*

He had stopped walking so suddenly that Silas bumped into his back.

"Wendell?" The boy couldn't say it—he said almost nothing now, hoarding his voice like a miser hoards coin—but he made the sound anyway, half-breath, half-name, and tugged Wendell's sleeve.

"I'm fine." He wasn't. "I just—" He turned and looked back down the way they'd come, at the grey miles of moor, and tried to remember Tam's face one more time. Tried to see the laugh. The gap in his teeth. *Was there a gap in his teeth?* There had been. He was nearly sure there had been.

The ferryman's toll. That was the truth of it, arriving late the way truths do. At the crossing, Wendell had paid the smell of rain on his childhood street—a small thing, he'd told himself, a seasoning, not a meal. But memory is not a purse you take coins from one at a time. Memory is cloth. Pull one thread and the weave loosens all around it. The smell of rain had been stitched through everything: through every storm he'd ever sheltered from, every friend he'd ever stood beside in wet weather, every face he'd ever seen lit by lightning. And the toll-taker had pulled, and pulled, and the cloth was coming apart now, thread by thread, day by day, and there was nothing under the world's rain for him anymore but grey.

"Keep walking," he told Silas, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved. "We keep walking."

* * *

By evening of the second day after Tam, he lost Marta.

Marta was easier to lose, which frightened him more. She had been the guild's apothecary, sharp-tongued, smoke-voiced, the woman who had taught him which bones held their echoes longest. When he reached for her he found the name, the shape, a few facts arranged like furniture in an abandoned house—and then, unbidden, unasked, his mind supplied a *replacement*, a generic kind woman with a generic kind face, and for one horrible moment he almost accepted it. Almost nodded to himself and said, *yes, that's Marta,* the way a starving man will eat anything.

He caught himself with his hand pressed flat against his own temple, as if he could hold the contents in by force.

Silas took his other hand and pulled it gently down.

They made camp in the lee of a tor, and Silas built the fire without being asked—built it well, better than Wendell would have, arranging the gorse roots so they caught and held. Somewhere in the last week the boy had stopped being a child who needed guiding and become something else: a guide who happened to be nine years old. He cooked the hare he'd snared. He checked Wendell's boots for blisters with the grave seriousness of a physician. And when Wendell sat staring into the flames trying to assemble the previous year out of its surviving fragments, Silas sat beside him and talked.

Not with his voice. With everything else.

He drew in the ash: a shop, a counter, a bell above a door. He tapped Wendell's chest, then the ground, then pointed back the way they'd come, and Wendell understood: *the guildhouse. You had a life there. I am reminding you that you had a life.* He mimed the carving of a bone, small precise motions of an invisible knife, and then pointed at Wendell's hands, and Wendell laughed—a cracked, surprised sound—and said, "Yes. Yes, I carve. I carve bones. I hear the dead through them." And Silas nodded solemnly, as if confirming an entry in a ledger, and Wendell thought: *he is keeping accounts of me. The boy is keeping my ledger, because mine is being erased.*

That night he dreamed of his mother.

Or rather, he dreamed of the place where his mother had been. In the dream he stood in a kitchen—it was a kitchen, he knew that much, there was bread and there was steam—and there was a warmth at his back like a hand between his shoulder blades, and a voice saying something he could not hear, and he turned and turned and there was no one there, only the warmth, only the shape of the warmth, already fading even as he reached for it. He woke with tears frozen on his cheeks in the moor wind and could not have said, if you had asked him, whose tears they were or why they'd been wept.

Only that they mattered. Only that something irreplaceable had been spent, and he was the poorer for it, and he didn't know what the coin had bought.

* * *

On the fourth day, the faces started going in company.

They passed a shepherd driving a ragged flock along a contour of the hill, and the man hailed them—moor folk were starved for news—and asked if they'd come through Ashfield, and whether the king's soldiers still burned the granaries there. Wendell opened his mouth to answer and discovered that he could not remember Ashfield burning. He remembered that it *had* burned—the dead in the ditches had told him so, hundreds of them, their voices still lodged in his ears like grit—but the remembering was secondhand now, a report rather than a memory, and when the shepherd searched his face for grief Wendell had none to give, only knowledge, which is a colder thing.

"You look like a man reading someone else's letter," the shepherd said, frowning.

"He's tired," Silas said.

Wendell nearly stumbled. The boy's voice, when he chose to spend it, was small and clear and strange, carrying harmonics underneath it like a bell struck underwater—the old tongue living in it even when the words were common ones. The shepherd heard it too. His frown deepened, and his hand drifted toward the crook at his side, and Silas stepped forward and did something with his open palm, some small gesture, and the shepherd's face smoothed over like water closing after a dropped stone.

"What were we saying?" the shepherd asked pleasantly.

"The weather," Silas said. "It's been dry."

"Aye. Dry." The man squinted at the sky, satisfied, and drove his flock on.

Wendell watched him go with his heart hammering. "You didn't need to do that."

"He was going to remember you." Silas's voice cost him visibly; each word seemed to weigh. "Remembered men get reported."

"And you just—" Wendell gestured at the space where the conversation had been. "You took it? Like the rat-catcher?"

Silas shook his head slowly. Held up his hands. Showed Wendell that they were trembling. Then he pointed at the pendant, which had gone quiet, and then at his own throat, and made a motion like swallowing something large and painful.

*It costs me too,* the gesture said. *Everything costs.*

Wendell crouched so they were eye to eye. Up close the boy looked older than nine, the way soldiers look old, the way orphans look old. "Listen to me," Wendell said. "You don't spend yourself to protect me. Not me. Do you understand? I'm the one who's supposed to be spending for you. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement."

Silas looked at him for a long moment. Then, deliberately, he reached out and touched Wendell's forehead, right between the eyes, where the blankness lived.

*No,* the touch said. *You are spending whether you choose it or not. Someone must hold what falls.*

And Wendell, who had spent thirty years making rules to keep himself safe—hear the dead but never answer them, keep the ledgers, stay invisible, want nothing, owe no one—found that he had no rule for this. No ledger line for a child who guarded the guard. He straightened up, cleared his throat, and said gruffly, "We should make the ridge before dark," because gruffness was the only currency he had left, and Silas smiled the smallest smile in the world and let him have it.

* * *

They found the first marker stone at dusk on the fifth day.

Wendell would have walked past it. That was the horror of it, the thing he would carry—if he could carry anything—out of these moors: that he would have walked straight past the beginning of everything, because the stone did not want to be seen. It sat in a fold of the hillside like a debt hidden in a ledger's back pages, and it was only when Silas stopped dead and the pendant began to sing—that struck-bell hum rising through octaves until it was less a sound than a pressure behind the eyes—that Wendell turned and saw it.

A standing stone, waist-high, older than any kingdom's masonry. Its surface was carved all over with writing that was not writing: spirals and strokes and knots that hurt to follow, characters that kept rearranging themselves when he wasn't looking directly at them, like fish seen through dark water. And beneath the carvings, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather and perhaps by centuries of grieving hands, a single image: a door, standing open, with light coming through it.

"Vhalen," Wendell breathed.

The name came out of him unbidden, and the moment it left his mouth the stone *woke*. He felt it the way he felt the dead in his carved bones—a vibration climbing through his boots, up his shins, settling in his teeth. The air around the stone thickened. The heather bent though there was no wind. And out of the ground, out of the stone, out of the thinning places of the world, came voices.

Not one voice. Many. Layered, overlapping, speaking the old tongue in cadences that rose and fell like a tide, and Wendell's bones translated as they always translated, unwillingly, faithfully:

*Who walks the vanished road?*

*Who carries the heir?*

*What has been paid, and what remains unpaid?*

Silas had both hands pressed flat against the stone. His eyes were closed. Tears ran freely down his face, and his lips moved, shaping answers in the old tongue, and Wendell understood maybe one word in ten—but the one word he understood, the one that came through again and again like a refrain, was *home*.

"They're here," Wendell said quietly. "The dead of Vhalen. They've been waiting at the markers all this time." He looked down the fold of the hill, and now that he knew how to look, he saw them: further stones, fainter, marching away into the dusk in a line that no cartographer had ever drawn, the skeleton of a road that a king had ordered unwritten. A road made of debts. A road made of names.

The stone's voices shifted. The tide of the old tongue gathered itself, and the bones in Wendell's pack began to rattle softly against each other, resonating, translating, and what came through then was not a question but a summons, formal and cold and ancient as the stone itself:

*Let the carrier of the heir approach. Let him swear what was sworn and broken. Let the parliament convene.*

"Parliament," Wendell repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "Dead parliament."

Silas opened his eyes. He looked at Wendell—at what remained of Wendell, at the man-shaped collection of surviving fragments who had once promised to guide him and was now being guided—and he did something Wendell did not expect. He took Wendell's hand. Small fingers, cold from the stone, lacing through his own.

Then he pointed at the fire-ring of flat stones nearby, at the cold ashes of some earlier traveler, and made the universal gesture: *light a fire. We stay tonight.*

"We stay," Wendell agreed. His voice sounded far away to himself, thin across the growing dark. "And in the morning—"

In the morning, the stone said, without words, in the marrow of him, the parliament would convene. And they would want an oath. And Wendell Hartwell, who had broken every rule he ever made except the one about staying invisible, understood with perfect clarity that invisibility was over—that the road had found him, that the dead had found him, that whatever his ancestor had done with Vhalen's door, the debt had come due at last, and it was addressed to his blood, and his blood was standing on the moor holding a child's hand while the light died.

He built the fire. Silas sat close against his side, the pendant glowing faintly now, a reed-gold pulse in the dark like a heartbeat kept outside a body.

Far off down the vanished road, fainter stones began, one by one, to glow.
