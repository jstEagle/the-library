## 15. The Dead Parliament

The marker stones stood in a ring on the moor like teeth left in an old jaw.

Wendell had seen them from a mile off—nine grey monoliths leaning at angles no mason would have chosen, furred with lichen the color of old bronze. In the last light of day they cast shadows that pointed inward, toward the center of the ring, as if the stones themselves were listening for something to be said there.

Silas saw them too. The boy stopped walking and went very still, the way he did when his pendant began to hum, but the tooth hung silent against his chest. It wasn't the stones that had caught him. It was something else—something in the air between them, or under it.

"They're here," Silas said. He said little enough that when he spoke, Wendell listened with his whole body. "All of them."

"Who's here?"

Silas looked up at him with those grave, unchildlike eyes. "Everyone who was ever asked."

Wendell did not ask what that meant. He was learning, slowly, painfully, that some questions answered themselves if you gave them until dark.

They made camp inside the ring because the wind outside it was a knife and the stones broke it into something merely cruel. Wendell built a small fire—smaller than he wanted, smaller than the cold demanded—and set their supper to warming while Silas sat cross-legged on the moor grass, watching the stones the way other children watched doors, waiting for someone to come through.

The dead came at full dark.

Wendell felt them before he saw them: the fine hairs on his arms lifting, the bones in his pack stirring against each other with a sound like dry leaves, like whispering. His carvings had been restless since Ashfield, since the soldier in the ditch whose message he had carried and whose name he could not stop hearing in the space behind his teeth. But this was different. This was not one ghost begging at the edge of his hearing.

This was a congregation.

They rose out of the ground the way heat rises off summer stone—not walking so much as arriving, assembling, figure by figure, until the firelight fell on faces that had no business still having faces. Old men and old women mostly, robed in the fashions of a country that no longer existed: high collars worked with reed-patterns, sashes of a green that had faded to the memory of green. Some of them were missing pieces—a shoulder, half a face, an entire arm dissolved into smoke at the edges—as though erasure were not a single act but a slow flaying, still ongoing, still hungry.

One of them wore a crown of woven reed. It sat crooked on a head that had been struck, once, very hard.

Wendell's hand found Silas's shoulder without his deciding to move it. The boy did not shrug him off. The boy was staring at the crowned figure with an expression Wendell had no word for—recognition, yes, but also grief of a kind too large for a child's face, worn the way a man wears armor two sizes too big.

"Grandmother," Silas whispered. Then, correcting himself with terrible precision: "Great-grandmother. Speaker Halvene."

The crowned woman inclined her head. When she spoke, her voice came not through the air but through the bones in Wendell's pack, through the carved knucklebones and rib-slivers and finger-bones of his trade, each of them vibrating like a struck string so that the words arrived already translated, already rendered into the tongue of the living:

*Heir of Vhalen. You are small. You are late. But you are here.*

"I'm here," Silas said.

*And you have brought a Hartwell.*

Every face in the circle turned to Wendell.

He had spent thirty years perfecting invisibility—the slouched shoulders, the downcast eyes, the art of being furniture in any room. None of it worked now. There was nowhere in that ring of stones to be furniture. The dead looked at him the way creditors look at a debtor who has inherited the debt along with the house.

"I have," Wendell said. His voice sounded thin against all that attention. "And I'd like to know what that means, exactly, before anyone starts assigning me anything."

Speaker Halvene smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who has heard every excuse ever constructed and found them all load-bearing but insufficient.

*It means,* she said, *that your blood opened this wound, and your blood will close it. Sit, bone-carver. We have kept a session waiting three hundred years, and we do not intend to wait longer.*

* * *

They convened around his campfire as though it were a hearth in a hall that had burned down before his grandmother's grandmother was born.

Wendell learned their names the way he learned everything—through the bones, which hummed and shivered and passed the dead's voices into his skull like water through a filter. Speaker Halvene, crown of reeds, last lawful voice of Vhalen. Marshal Ondrey, whose left side was smoke, killed holding a pass that history no longer contained. The twins Sefa and Ilena, archivists, who had died with the country's records in their arms and now carried nothing, their hands empty forever. A dozen more, ministers and witnesses and keepers of oaths, each one a fragment, each one insistent.

They had been a parliament. They were, they informed him with a formality that made his skin crawl, still a parliament. A parliament does not dissolve because its country is erased. A parliament dissolves when its work is done.

"Their work," Wendell said carefully, "being what?"

*Justice,* said Marshal Ondrey, and his smoky half-face managed to look amused. *What else do the murdered want?*

"You weren't all murdered."

*We were all erased,* said one of the twins—Ilena, he thought; the archive-ghosts were hard to tell apart. *There is a difference, and the difference is the whole point.* She leaned forward, and the firelight passed through her cheekbone as though it were glass. *Aldric did not merely kill us. Killing leaves graves. Killing leaves mourners. Killing leaves a story that someone, someday, might tell. Aldric paid the guilds of three nations to unmake us—to strike Vhalen from every map, every ledger, every memory that could be reached by ink. Do you understand what that costs, bone-carver? Do you know what must be paid to make a whole nation forgettable?*

"I've read the ledgers," Wendell said quietly. "I know what forgetting costs."

*Then you know it cannot be paid twice. Which is why we are owed.*

Silas had gone pale in the firelight. Wendell put a hand on the boy's back and felt the small spine rigid as a drawn bow. "Owed by whom?"

*By the line of Aldric, who contracted the debt. By the guilds, who profited from it. And by the house of Hartwell,* said Speaker Halvene, *who signed the oath that made the erasure possible—and then broke it.*

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the moor, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"My ancestor," Wendell said. He had read the entry in the vault at Cael Row—he had read it a dozen times since, in stolen moments, trying to make the name *Hartwell* mean something other than what it meant. "She opened the door. She paid for it. Everything she loved—that's what the ledger says."

*She paid to open it,* Halvene agreed. *But opening was only half the work. An oath was sworn at the founding of that door—an oath of witness. The one who opens the door must stand as Vhalen's witness in the world beyond it. Must carry our names. Must speak them. Must ensure that what was erased is remembered until it can be restored.* The crowned head tilted. *Your ancestor took that oath with her hand on these very stones. And then she walked away, and lived, and forgot us on purpose, because remembering hurt, and she had already paid so much. Her descendants inherited her trade, her talent, her name—and her silence. Three hundred years of Hartwells, hearing the dead and never answering. Tell me, bone-carver. Is that your rule?*

Wendell's mouth was dry. *Hear the dead, never answer them. Keep the ledgers. Stay invisible.* He had thought those rules were wisdom. He had thought they were survival. Now they sat in his chest like a confession he'd been reciting his whole life without knowing it was one.

"That rule kept me alive," he said.

*It kept you comfortable,* said Marshal Ondrey, without malice, which was worse. *Alive is what the mice in the walls are.*

Silence. The wind moved through the stones and made a low note, almost musical, like breath across a bottle. Silas's hand crept over and gripped Wendell's sleeve.

"What do you want from me?" Wendell asked.

*An oath,* said Halvene simply. *The same oath, renewed. That the door, when it opens, opens under a witness bound to speak. That Vhalen's restoration is carried not by a child's whim but by a sworn word that cannot be abandoned when the hurting starts. Swear it, Hartwell. Bind yourself. Then take the boy through, and spend whatever the door demands, and know that you may not walk away from what remains.*

Wendell looked at the fire. He looked at the ghosts—at Ondrey's smoking ruin of a body, at the twins' empty hands, at Halvene's crooked crown—and he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, exactly what was being asked and exactly why it was wrong.

"No," he said.

The word dropped into the ring of stones and rang there.

*No?* Ondrey's voice had an edge now.

"No." Wendell made himself sit straight, made himself meet the dead eye to empty eye. "You're asking me to swear an oath on behalf of Silas. To bind his door, his sacrifice, his future, to my word. And I'll tell you what I told your living counterparts, if any of them ever had the decency to ask: nobody swears for the boy. Not me. Not you. Not a parliament of the dead with three hundred years of grievances and nothing better to do than haunt a campfire."

*He is the heir—*

"He is a child." Wendell's voice cracked on it, and he let it crack. "He is a child who watched his language get taken from him before he could even grieve it properly. He is a child who has already lost more than your entire session ever debated. And you want to wrap him in oaths and obligations and the word *heir* like it's a gift, when it's a chain. I've seen what inheritance does to people. I *am* what inheritance does to people."

Ilena the archivist leaned forward. *Without the oath, the door may open and close and mean nothing. Without a bound witness, Vhalen rises and can be struck down again by the next man with gold and a grudge. The oath is not ceremony, carver. It is structure. It is the difference between a country and a memory of a country.*

"Then let him swear it himself," Wendell said. "If he chooses it. When he chooses it. Having understood every cost—which, I might add, none of you have actually laid out for him. You've laid them out for *me*, because I'm the one paying, and apparently my consent matters even less than his."

*Coward,* said Marshal Ondrey.

The word landed like a slap. Wendell felt it in his teeth.

*You heard us in Ashfield,* the marshal said, rising, his smoke-side streaming. *You heard the dead in the ditches begging for a word, a name, a marker, anything—and you broke your precious rule and answered, because it cost you nothing but a night's sleep. But this? This costs. This demands. So suddenly the great compassionate Hartwell discovers principles. You will swear, carver, or you will be remembered as the second Hartwell failure, and we have very long memories, and nothing else.*

"I'd rather be your coward than his jailer," Wendell said.

He reached over and drew Silas against his side. The boy was trembling—but when Wendell glanced down, he saw that the trembling wasn't fear. Silas was looking at Speaker Halvene with his jaw set, with his chin lifted, with an expression so ferociously adult that it hurt to witness.

"I choose," Silas said.

The parliament went still. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

"Not him. Not the oath. Not yet." The boy's voice was small and absolutely immovable. "I'll decide. Me. When I know everything. And if anyone—" his eyes swept the ring of ghosts, and Wendell marveled, and feared for him, and was prouder than he had ever been of anything— "*anyone* tries to decide for me again, I won't open your door at all. I'll let it stay shut another three hundred years. With all of you on the wrong side of it."

Silence, vast and ringing.

Then Speaker Halvene began, very slowly, to laugh—a dry sound like reeds breaking, like pages turning. *Oh,* she said. *Oh, the blood tells true. Three hundred years, and the line produces one honest thing at last.* She looked at Wendell, and her ancient eyes held something that might, in a kinder century, have been respect. *Very well, heir. Very well, coward. You will both have your way—for now. But the door is close, and the door keeps its own accounts, and it does not care who consents to what.*

She raised her smoke-edged hand, and the parliament folded itself downward, back into the moor, back into the dark, figures thinning like breath off glass—until only Halvene remained, crouched at the fire's edge, close enough that Wendell could see the stars through her.

*A warning, Hartwell, since you value honesty.* Her voice dropped to almost nothing, and the bones in his pack ached with it. *The army behind you carries a woman who used to hear us. And the door ahead takes payment in the only coin you have left. When you stand on the threshold—and you will stand on the threshold—remember that we never asked you to die for us.*

*We only asked you to remember.*

And she was gone, and the ring of stones was just stones, and the fire burned low, and Silas slept against Wendell's shoulder with the tooth pendant humming faintly, like something counting down.

Wendell stayed awake until the sky went grey, turning the marshal's word over in his mind like a coin he couldn't spend.

*Coward.*

Maybe. But cowards, he thought grimly, feeding the fire one last stick, live long enough to pay their debts. And somewhere east, past the first light, the vanished road waited with its toll already counted.

He just didn't yet know how much of him it would take.
