## 17. Voss at the Door

Maren Voss had been running since before dawn, and the moor had not forgiven her for it.

The ground here was soft with old rain and older ash. Every step sank a half-inch into peat that smelled of burnt thatch, of wet cinders, of the particular sweetness that came from villages burned so long ago the grass had grown back over them like skin over a wound. Ashfield, they called this stretch of country now. Before the decree, it had been called something else. Everything had been called something else.

She crested the ridge alone because she had ordered her soldiers to stay behind it.

They would have obeyed her if she'd told them to arrest the fugitives. They would have obeyed her if she'd told them to burn the boy where he stood. That was the trouble with inquisitors: their men learned to stop asking what an order meant, and after enough years even the woman giving the orders stopped asking too.

But she was asking now. She could feel the question sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Below her, in the hollow where the marker stones stood in their broken ring, two figures waited at something that was not quite there. She had seen doors all her life—oak doors, iron doors, the door of the interrogation chamber in the capital with its leather padding nailed on from the inside—and none of them had prepared her for this. It did not look like an opening. It looked like a place where the world had forgotten to finish itself: a seam of green light hanging in the air between two leaning stones, thin as a knife's edge, wide as a held breath. Through it she could see—she pressed her knuckles against her eyes to be sure—terraces. Green terraces climbing a hillside under a sky the color of new leaves. Water running in channels cut by hands. Reed-gold light lying across everything like a blessing no one had asked for.

Vhalen.

Her grandmother's word for *home*, spoken into a kitchen in a refugee quarter until the neighbors complained about the foreign noise, until her father stopped answering in it, until one day Maren realized she had spent her whole childhood listening to a language dying one word at a time in a woman's mouth, and had never once asked to learn it.

*You could have,* said the voice in her head that sounded like her grandmother. *You were seven. You were seven and you told me my words sounded like birds arguing.*

Maren had spent thirty years making sure she never heard that voice again.

The hearing had been the first thing they took. Not hers—her mother's, and then her aunt's, and then, when the inquisitors of that earlier reign came through the refugee quarters with their little brass instruments and their clipboards full of bloodlines, hers. She had been nine. She remembered the room, the smell of vinegar and hot metal, the surgeon's calm hands, the way he'd explained—kindly, the way you explain weather—that children of erased nations heard things, and that hearing things made them dangerous to themselves and others, and that mercy sometimes required a small operation. She remembered waking with cotton packed in both ears and the world gone strangely flat, like a painting of a world instead of a world. She had cried for three days, and she had not known why, because whatever she had been hearing—the dead of Vhalen murmuring in the walls, in the ditches, in the bones of the burned houses—was gone, and with it went the grief, and with the grief went the reason for it, and she was left only with the fact of crying, which everyone assured her was normal for a girl her age.

Erasure is mercy. She had built her whole life on that sentence. She had climbed the ranks of Aldric's inquisition on that sentence, stone by stone, and every promotion had come with a fresh coat of mortar.

And now she stood on a ridge above a green seam in the air, and the sentence would not hold its weight.

* * *

She had let him go once already. In Cael Row, in the burning stacks of the guildhouse, with smoke boiling black against the rafters and Silas backed into a corner with his eyes huge and white-rimmed, she had raised her hand to seize him—and the boy had opened his mouth and sung.

Not sung. Spoken. But it had moved like song, the way water moves over stones, and Maren had understood every syllable without knowing how, the way you understand your own heartbeat. A lullaby. Her grandmother's lullaby, the one about the reed-cutter's daughter who sleeps in the boat while the river carries her home. He had sung it in the old tongue, in the true accent, with the little rising lilt on the last line that her grandmother had used and no one else alive could have used, and Maren's hand had dropped to her side, and her soldiers had found her standing in the smoke with tears cutting channels through the soot on her face, and she had told them the boy was dead. Burned. Nothing left to find.

A lie. Her first. Thirty years of service and her first lie, told in the name of a child who had given her nothing but four lines of a song.

Now she picked her way down the slope toward the marker stones, boots sliding on wet peat, and watched the two figures resolve out of the morning mist. The bone-carver stood closest to the seam, and he looked—she had seen the reports, she knew what the tolls were doing to him—he looked like a house with the windows taken out. Still standing. Still shaped like a man. But hollowed, and the wind went through him. He kept one hand on the boy's shoulder the way you keep a hand on something you are afraid the earth will take early.

And Silas. The heir of Vhalen, the last speaker, the weapon the crown had spent eleven years and uncountable lives trying to prevent. He was small. That was the thing the reports never conveyed, the thing she should have known and had refused to know: he was small, maybe ten years old, with mud to his knees and a tooth pendant at his throat and the kind of stillness children get when they have been frightened for so long that fear has become simply the temperature of the air.

He saw her first. Of course he did. Children see everything; it is adults who learn not to look.

Maren stopped twenty paces out. Close enough to speak. Far enough that her hands, empty and visible at her sides, could be seen to be empty and visible.

"I'm alone," she said. "My men are on the ridge. They'll come when I signal."

Wendell Hartwell turned. His eyes were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic with what remained of himself, and she watched him arrive at the sum: one inquisitor, unarmed, twenty paces, a door that opens once. He put himself slightly in front of the boy anyway. Old instinct. Good man, or what was left of one.

"You're the one from the fire," the boy said. Not in the old tongue. In the trade-speech, accented, careful. He had heard her lie through the smoke, or he had heard her cry, or he simply knew, the way children know, that she was the crack in the wall.

"I am," Maren said.

"Are you going to take us?"

Behind her, faint as a headache beginning, she could hear the army cresting the ridge. Hooves on stone. The jingle of harness. A captain's voice calling the column to halt. She had perhaps a hundred breaths before someone grew impatient, before a lieutenant decided that an inquisitor standing idle in a hollow with two fugitives was either a trap or a treason, and came down to find out which.

"No," Maren said.

Hartwell's hand tightened on the boy's shoulder. "Why should we believe that?"

"You shouldn't." She made herself say it plainly. "I've hunted you across half a kingdom. I burned the guildhouse in Cael Row—or my people did, on my order, and I signed the order. I reported the boy dead when he was standing in front of me, and I'd do it again. You have no reason to believe me and I'm not going to ask you to. I'm going to ask you for something else."

The green light pulsed. Slowly, like breathing. Through the seam, on the far terraces, she thought she saw figures—small, distant, moving with purpose. People. Living people, or something that had never stopped being alive, tending channels of water in a country that had been decreed out of existence. Her chest hurt.

"The door closes behind them," Hartwell said. His voice was rough, worn down to the grain. "Once. Then it's done. Whatever you want from us, you won't get it by waiting."

"I don't want anything from you." And then, because it was true and because she had so little time left in which to be true: "I want something from him. From the country."

Silas tilted his head. Waiting. Children are better at waiting than anyone; they have had less practice at being disappointed by it.

Maren took a breath, and the breath shook, and she let it.

"When I was a child," she said, "I could hear them. The dead of Vhalen. My mother could, my grandmother could—we were blood of the blood, refugees of the second purge, settled in the Tallow Quarter of the capital with everyone else your decree scattered. I heard them in the walls of our rooms. In the ditches outside the quarter. Under the floor of the fish market, where they'd buried the ones from the first winter. All my life, voices, murmuring, grieving, and I didn't know what they were saying because my grandmother was the only one left who spoke the old tongue, and I—" Her voice caught. She pushed through it the way you push through a door that has swollen shut. "I told her it sounded like birds arguing. I was seven. I wanted to be like the other children, the ones whose grandmothers sang trade-songs. So I never learned. And then the inquisitors came with their brass instruments, and they took the hearing out of me, and they told my parents it was mercy, and everyone believed them because believing them was easier than not."

Silas had gone very still. The pendant at his throat hummed, very softly, like a struck bell fading.

"They say erasure is mercy," Maren said. "That's the doctrine. That a nation unmade is a nation spared its own suffering. I believed it. I enforced it. I have signed more decrees of confiscation than I can count, and every signature was me telling myself I was sparing someone." She looked at the green seam, at the terraces, at the impossible living light of it. "And now I'm standing here looking at the thing we erased, and it isn't suffering. It's *gardens*. It's water running in channels somebody loved enough to cut straight. And I need—I need one thing before it closes. One true sentence. Not a spell, not a weapon, not testimony for your parliament of the dead. Just—" Her composure, thirty years mortared, cracked clean through. "Just one true sentence of Vhalen. So that when I go back up that ridge and report that the door closed and the heir is lost to us forever, there will be one true thing inside me that isn't theirs. One thing they didn't give me and can't take back."

The army shifted on the ridge. A horn sounded—two notes, the recall-and-report. Someone wanted their inquisitor.

Hartwell was watching her with an expression she couldn't read, or could read too well: the look of a man recognizing another person's ruin because it fit his own like a key. The boy stepped forward, out from under the carver's hand, and Maren saw Hartwell almost reach for him and make himself stop. That, too, was a kind of answer. That was a man who had learned, at terrible cost, what consent weighed.

Silas looked up at her. His eyes were gray-green, the color of the light coming through the seam, and she understood suddenly that this was not a coincidence, that the country had colored its children out of its own sky.

"What was her name?" the boy asked.

Maren blinked. "What?"

"Your grandmother." He said it patiently, the way you explain rain to someone who has never felt it. "The door doesn't open for sentences. It opens for names. Names are how a country remembers. If you want one true sentence of Vhalen—" he touched the tooth pendant at his throat, and it hummed brighter, and the green light leaned toward him like a plant toward sun— "give me her name, and I'll give you back the truest one I have."

And Maren Voss, inquisitor of the crown of Aldric, breaker of guildhouses, signer of confiscations, stood on the ash of a burned road and found that her mouth had to work to remember a word it had not spoken in thirty years.

"Sefa," she said. The name came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh. "Sefa Voss. She was born in a village called—" and the village name rose with it, unbidden, a word she hadn't known she was carrying, carried so deep it had survived the surgery, survived the doctrine, survived her— "called Ilvane. On the river. She was a reed-cutter's daughter. She used to sing about a boat."

The pendant flared.

Silas closed his eyes. When he spoke, it was not the trade-speech, and it was not quite the old tongue either—it was the old tongue the way it must have sounded in kitchens, in boats, in the mouths of women putting children to sleep: warm, unhurried, rising at the end like a wave deciding to come ashore. Four lines. The reed-cutter's daughter asleep in the boat, the river carrying her home, the reeds bending low to keep the wind off her face, the morning finding her safe on the water.

Maren heard it with the ears the surgeons had left her, and she heard it with the ears they had taken, and both hearings agreed: it was her grandmother's voice. Not similar. *Her*. The exact dry warmth of it, the little laugh folded into the third line, the way Sefa Voss had always slowed on the last line because she was falling asleep too.

The green seam widened. Light poured across the hollow, across the marker stones, across Maren's upturned face, and in the light she saw—not imagined, *saw*—a hillside terrace with water running in its channels, and a woman standing ankle-deep in the shallows cutting reeds, and the woman turned, and it was not her grandmother, it was no one she knew, it was everyone she knew, and she was smiling, and her mouth formed a name.

*Sefa.*

The country remembered her grandmother. Somewhere on the other side of a seam in the world, in a nation that had been decreed out of existence, a reed-cutter looked up from her work and spoke a refugee child's name back across thirty years of silence, and Maren Voss went to her knees in the wet peat and wept the way she had not wept since she was nine years old and waking deaf and not knowing why.

* * *

When she could stand again, the horn had sounded twice more, and the light was narrowing.

The door was closing. She could see it—the seam drawing in at the edges like a healing wound, the terraces dimming, the green going gold going gray. Hartwell stood at the threshold with his hand on the boy's back, and his face was already changing, already going somewhere she recognized with horror: the look of a man preparing to pay. She had seen that look on debtors. She had seen it on the condemned.

"Go," she said hoarsely. "Both of you. Now. Before I change my mind or my lieutenant grows a spine."

Silas turned at the threshold. Behind him the light was a blade's width and brightening, and through it Maren could hear—faintly, impossibly—voices. Many voices. Murmuring. Grieving. The sound from the walls of her childhood, except that here it did not sound like grief. It sounded like a parliament taking its seats.

The boy reached up and pressed something into her hand. Small, warm, humming faintly. The tooth pendant.

"It knows her name now," he said. "So do you. That means you're a witness. Witnesses can't be erased—they can only be silenced, and silencing takes witnesses, and witnesses take witnesses." He smiled, and it was a child's smile and an ancient one at once. "Tell them I died if you have to. But tell yourself the truth every day. That's the tax. That's what witnessing costs."

Then he was turning, and Hartwell was turning, and the bone-carver looked back at her once—a long look, a look that said he understood exactly what she had just chosen and exactly what it would cost her and that he was sorry for it, which was the strangest mercy anyone had offered her in thirty years—and then the light took them.

The seam closed with a sound like a book shutting.

Maren Voss stood alone in the hollow of the marker stones, holding a humming tooth in her fist, and up on the ridge she could hear hoofbeats descending, her lieutenant's voice calling her name, the machinery of the crown grinding down the slope toward her, and she had perhaps forty breaths to compose the report that would save her life and bury the truth.

She wiped her face. She squared her shoulders. She began, silently, to draft the lie: *the door collapsed, the boy lost, the carver dead on the threshold, nothing remains.*

And in her fist, soft as a heartbeat, the tooth hummed her grandmother's name.
