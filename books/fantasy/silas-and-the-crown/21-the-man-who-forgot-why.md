## 21. The Man Who Forgot Why

The winter that came to the free cities was the color of old bone.

Wendell Hartwell knew this the way he knew most things now—without knowing how he knew it. He stood at his workbench in a shop on Candlemaker's Row with sawdust in his beard and a dead man's femur pinned under his clamps, and he watched the snow come down past his window like ash from a fire no one had put out. The city beyond the glass had changed hands twice since autumn. Banners of Aldric's crown hung from the customs house, gray and red, and soldiers walked the streets in pairs, and everyone had learned to speak quietly, to walk without hurrying, to be small.

He had learned it too. He was very good at being small. He could not remember learning it, but then, there was a great deal he could not remember.

His chisel found the grain of the femur and followed it. The dead man had been a cooper, drowned in the Sere when the ferry went over in the ice, and his ghost sat on the stool by the stove the way they all did—patient, damp, waiting for their hands to be given back to them. Wendell could hear him. He had always been able to hear them; that much of himself remained, fixed as a nail driven through a board. The cooper's voice came through the bone like water through cloth: *my daughter's name is Betta. Tell her the third barrel was never sold. She'll know what's in it.*

"Third barrel," Wendell said, because he always said it aloud now, writing nothing down. Writing felt dangerous in a way he couldn't explain. "Never sold."

*You're kind,* said the cooper, surprised, the way they were all surprised. *Most carvers don't answer.*

"I know," said Wendell. "I used to have rules about that."

*What happened to them?*

He turned the chisel over in his fingers and considered the question honestly, which was all he could offer anyone these days: honesty without furniture behind it. "I don't remember," he said. "There's a hole where that should be. There are several holes." He tapped his own temple with the chisel's haft, gently, the way you'd knock on a wall to find the stud. "It's like a house where someone took out the load-bearing walls and left the rooms standing. Everything still holds up. I just don't know why it doesn't fall down."

The cooper's ghost shifted on his stool. Dead men were always uncomfortable around living men who were missing pieces; it reminded them too much of themselves.

*Does it frighten you?* the ghost asked.

Wendell thought about it while the chisel moved, peeling a curl of bone away from the knuckle end, beginning the shape of a hand—the cooper wanted to finish something, they always wanted to finish something, a letter or a latch or a last word to somebody, and Wendell's trade was giving the dead their hands back one carved joint at a time.

"No," he said at last, and found it was true. "That's the strange part. It should. A man my age ought to have a mother in him somewhere, or a reason, or at least a face he'd know in a crowd. I've got my craft. I've got my ledgers—" he glanced at the shelf where the guild books sat, fat and patient, some of them writing themselves in faded ink when they thought he wasn't looking, though he no longer wondered about that either, "—and I've got a feeling like a bruise on the inside of my chest that doesn't hurt exactly. It just says *something happened here.* Like a door that's been painted over. You can see the outline of it if the light comes sideways."

*Maybe you paid for something,* the cooper said. Dead men talked about payment the way sailors talked about weather.

"Everything costs," Wendell agreed. "My master taught me that." And then he stopped, because he had just remembered having a master, and could not remember the master's face, and the two facts sat together in him like strangers forced to share a bench.

He worked until the candle guttered. When the hand was finished—a good hand, a strong cooper's hand with the ghost of a scar across two knuckles—he set it on the sill of the little window that faced the alley, because that was the custom here, whatever else he'd forgotten. The dead took their hands and went wherever the dead went when they finally had what they needed. The cooper stood, flexed his returned fingers, wept without tears the way they did, and said *Betta. Third barrel,* one more time, softer, like a man setting down a heavy load, and was gone.

Wendell swept the shavings. Closed the shutters. Banked the stove. Did all the small liturgies of an ordinary evening, and it was only when he pulled off his coat to hang it by the door that his fingers found the thing in the pocket.

The pendant.

He drew it out into the lamplight. A child's tooth, drilled and threaded on a leather cord gone soft with handling, dark at the edges where thumbs had worried it for years. It was not his. He knew that the way he knew the snow was the color of bone—absolutely, and without any story attached. It was not his, and yet it lived in his coat pocket the way a splinter lives in a thumb, and every time he found it again—and he found it again most evenings, because his hands kept going to that pocket like pilgrims to a shrine—he felt that painted-over door inside himself strain against its frame.

He held it up. The lamp flame bent in the window draft. The tooth caught the light and held it, and somewhere far under the floor of the world, so faint he might have invented it, something hummed. Like a bell struck once and heard through a wall. Like a note that had been ringing so long the air had gotten used to it.

"Why do I have you?" he asked it.

The tooth said nothing. Teeth were not bones, quite; they didn't carry the dead the way bone did. But this one carried something. It carried a weight, the way a folded letter carries a weight even sealed. And when he closed his fist around it, the ache in his chest—the bruise, the painted door—opened one eye.

He saw, for half a heartbeat, green. Terraces cut into a hillside, going up and up into weather. Reeds, gold in a light that had no season. Then it was gone, and he was standing in his own shop with snow ticking against the shutters and his heart going hard enough to hurt.

"Huh," he said aloud, to the empty room. His voice sounded older than he felt.

He put the pendant back in the pocket. He did not throw it away. He had tried that once—or his hands had tried it, he suspected, finding the cord coiled in the ash bucket one morning, rescued and rinsed without any decision he could recall—and he did not try again. Some things a man keeps even when he has forgotten why. Especially then.

* * *

Curfew bells rang at dusk now, three notes instead of two, and after them the free cities belonged to the crown's soldiers and the dead.

Wendell walked home the long way along the canal, because the long way passed the guild hall, and the guild hall was burned—a black ribcage of a building with snow filling its sockets—and because he could not stop looking at it. He didn't remember the fire. He didn't remember Cael Row, or the vault, or the ferry, or any of the road east that his own ledgers insisted he had taken. The ledger of small debts had entries in his handwriting describing journeys he had no memory of making, tolls paid in things he could no longer name. *Paid: the smell of rain on my childhood street,* said one entry, in his own neat hand, and beneath it, in ink that had dried brown and strange: *worth it.*

Worth what? he always wanted to shout at the page. Worth WHAT?

But the books had gone quiet since midwinter. Whatever they had wanted—and they had wanted, he was sure of that, the way you're sure a dog wants something even when it stops barking—they had gotten it. The faded ink had stopped appearing. The debts of the usurper's line sat uncollected on their pages like coins nobody would pick up. And Wendell was left holding a trade, a shop, a scar on his palm he couldn't account for, and a hollow shaped exactly like a purpose.

Two soldiers stood at the mouth of Guildman's Walk, stamping their feet, halberds grounded. They watched him pass with the flat professional boredom of men who had learned that everyone in the free cities was guilty of something and that most of it wasn't worth the paperwork.

"Carver," said the younger one, as Wendell drew level. Not a challenge. Almost a plea. "You're the one that does the bones. My sergeant's brother drowned in the Sere. Before the ice broke. They never—"

"Bring me something of his," Wendell said. "A fingerbone's best. Knuckle will do."

The soldier blinked, as if he'd rehearsed the request and hadn't expected it to work. "They say you can hear them."

"They say right," Wendell said, and walked on, and heard behind him the younger soldier telling the older one, hushed, *see, I told you, I told you he's real,* and felt the old familiar pull of the work take hold of him like current takes a boat.

That was the thing he could not make peace with, walking home through the snow: the work mattered. He could feel it mattering. Every bone he carved gave somebody back a hand, a word, a goodbye, and the city was full of people carrying unfinished sentences like stones, and he was the only mason for a hundred miles who could chip those stones down to something bearable. He could feel all of it—the necessity of it, the goodness of it, the way a man feels a stair under his foot in the dark—and underneath the feeling, where the reason should have been, there was nothing. Just smooth emptiness, painted over, warm to the touch.

*I stay because the work matters,* he told himself each morning, shaving, looking at a face he half-recognized. *Why does the work matter?*

No answer. Only the bruise. Only the door-shaped absence, and the faint ring of a struck bell coming up through the floor of the world.

* * *

Some nights the dreams came.

Not dreams, really. More like weather moving through an empty house. He would stand in a place that was nowhere—green light, reed-gold, a wind that smelled like rain on a street he could no longer smell—and something would be leaving him. That was the only word for it. Something large and necessary departing through a door that was narrowing, narrowing, and he would be pushing something ahead of him through it, something small, something that mattered more than his own name, and he would wake with his arms actually extended, actually pushing, palms flat against the dark of his bedroom, and the ache in his chest would be so loud he could hear it.

He never remembered what he was pushing. He only remembered that he had refused to let go until the last possible moment, and that letting go had been the whole point, and that he would do it again.

*Again,* he thought, sitting up in the cold, rubbing his arms. *Would do it again. For what? For whom?*

On the table by his bed lay the ledger of small debts, open to the last entry in his handwriting. He had read it a hundred times. It said:

*Account closed. Balance paid in full. If anyone finds this book: there was a boy. Do not look for him. Look for the country instead. — W.H.*

*A boy.*

He had no children. He was nearly certain he had no children. But the word sat in him like the tooth in his pocket—not his, and yet lodged, and humming.

He got up, lit the lamp, and took the pendant from the coat by the door. He held it in both hands this time, the way you'd hold a dying bird, and he did the thing he had not let himself do in all these months of hollow competence: he listened with everything he had. Not with his ears—with the craft, with whatever it was that the dead spoke through, the instrument he had been made into before he could remember being made.

The hum came up through the tooth like water rising in a well.

And threaded inside the hum, so faint it was almost imagination, almost memory, almost hope—there was a voice. Small. Young. Saying something over and over in a language older than any kingdom, a language his bones understood even though he did not, and the bones translated it for him the way they translated everything, faithfully, mercilessly:

*Wendell Hartwell. Wendell Hartwell. Wendell Hartwell.*

His own name. Being spoken by a child, somewhere impossibly far away, over and over, like a prayer, like a carving, like a name being written back into the world one syllable at a time.

Wendell sat down slowly on the edge of his bed with the tooth pressed to his sternum, and the painted-over door inside him strained so hard against its frame that plaster dust seemed to fall through his whole body, and he wept without knowing what for—which is the purest grief there is, and the cruelest.

"I'm here," he said to the pendant, to the boy, to whoever was listening. "I don't know who you are. But I'm here."

Far away, through the thinning seam of a door that only opens once, a child king heard him—and picked up his pen.
