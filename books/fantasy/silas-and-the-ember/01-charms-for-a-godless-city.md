## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The night market of Vell smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone, and Marguerite Montrose loved it the way one loves a thing that would destroy you if it knew.

They had claimed the same corner of Tanner's Row for three years now—a fold-out table scarred with candle burns, a strip of red cloth to catch the eye, and a basket of charms that were, mostly, honest. Luck-knots braided from river-grass and horsehair. Copper bells tuned to a pitch that made headaches retreat. Little clay mice with painted whiskers, sold as children's toys, which was the closest thing to the truth anyone in Vell would ever hear from her lips.

"Charms here," they called, voice pitched low and pleasant, neither man's nor woman's in the way of market-sellers who'd learned that ambiguity was armor. "Luck-knots, sweet-bells, mouse-whispers. Good fortune cheap, bad luck cheaper."

*Mouse-whispers.* The joke of it sat in their chest like a swallowed coal. Because that was the true merchandise—the part no coin could buy and no inspector must ever see. When a woman bought a clay mouse for her son's fevered sleep, Marguerite whispered into its cooling clay, and somewhere in the walls the real mice came, and ate the lice from the child's hair, and gnawed open the window-latch so the sick-room could breathe. When a fisherman tied a luck-knot to his net, Marguerite had already spoken to the gulls, and the gulls told the fish where to swim. Small magics. Deniable magics. The kind that looked like coincidence if you squinted, and like nothing at all if you didn't.

Vell had no gods anymore. It had wards instead—great woven barriers of light strung along the border hills, humming day and night, keeping the wilds out and everything else in. And it had ward-inspectors, who patrolled the markets in grey coats with silver pins shaped like closed doors, looking for exactly what Marguerite carried folded up behind their ribs.

*Beast-speakers.* That was the word on the old proscription lists, the ones nailed to the gatehouse and renewed every season until the paper went soft with rain. *Beast-speakers, covenant-kin, oath-touched.* Words for people whose voices worked on more than human ears. Three centuries since the founders broke the old pact with the wilds, three centuries since speaking with beasts became not a gift but a crime—and still the inspectors walked the night market with their silver door-pins, listening.

Marguerite had learned to listen back.

That was the trick no one taught you: the gift ran both ways. They couldn't just speak to beasts; they could hear them, all of them, a constant low tide of chatter beneath the city's noise. The pigeons in the eaves gossiping about crumbs and cats. The cart-horses complaining about the cobbles. The rats under Tanner's Row conducting their small, vicious politics. Most of it was boring. All of it was company. And none of it—not one word in twenty years—had ever been meant for them.

That was the loneliness of it, the part they never let themselves think about before dark: having a thousand conversations available and being welcome in none of them. The beasts spoke *around* Marguerite, never *to* them. A person could die of thirst beside a river if the river wouldn't let down its bucket.

"Two knots," said a voice, and Marguerite blinked back to the table. A baker's apprentice, flour still in his eyebrows, counting coppers with cold-reddened fingers. "One for me, one for my mother. She's got the joint-ache."

"For your mother, take the bell instead." Marguerite pressed the little copper bell into his hand and closed his fingers over it. "Ring it by her bed of an evening. Tell her it's for the pain." *And I'll ask the house-spiders to spin warm where she sleeps,* they did not say. "Three coppers."

He paid, thanked them, moved off into the crowd. Marguerite watched him go and felt the familiar ache—the wanting to be thanked for the real work, the invisible work, the ten small kindnesses threaded through every charm. It was enough. It had to be enough.

Then the wind changed, and Marguerite's whole body went still.

Not the market-wind, greasy with fry-oil and chimney smoke. This came down from the border hills, from the direction of the great wards, and it carried voices. Distant ones. Wild ones. And they were—

*Screaming.*

Marguerite's hands stopped mid-knot. Around them the market roared on, oblivious: a knife-grinder's wheel, a woman haggling over eels, two children chasing each other between the stalls. Nobody else heard it. Nobody else ever heard anything.

But Marguerite heard foxes screaming on the border hills, and deer running without any shape or reason to their flight, and something vast and old groaning in a register below sound, below thought, like a mountain shifting in its sleep. The wards hummed back at it, and for the first time in Marguerite's life, the hum sounded thin. Stretched. Like a rope with its strands snapping one by one.

*No,* Marguerite thought firmly, at themselves. *You're tired. You've been hearing things since—*

A weight landed on their table.

It was a cat—or had been a cat once, before hard seasons had pared it down to sinew and stubbornness. Grey as ash, ears chewed ragged, one eye clouded milk-white. It sat on the red cloth among the unsold charms with the absolute entitlement of royalty, and it looked directly at Marguerite with its one good eye, and it said:

"You're the one who talks."

The market noise fell away. Marguerite's heart performed a slow, enormous revolution in their chest. Twenty years of hiding. Twenty years of clay mice and careful silence and never, *never* answering when the beasts spoke near them. And now a half-starved alley cat had strolled up to their stall and opened its mouth, and out had come words—not the loose impressions beasts usually gave, not pictures and hungers, but actual words, deliberate, aimed.

"I don't know what you mean," Marguerite said, barely moving their lips, smiling at nothing like a seller murmuring to their wares.

The cat's tail flicked. "Don't waste my night on lies. I've crossed the whole city to find you. Do you know how hard it is to find one specific human? They all smell of bread and fear." The good eye glittered. "The wards are failing."

Everything in Marguerite went very quiet, the way the air goes quiet before lightning.

"That's not possible," they breathed. "The wards have stood three hundred years. They stood through the Sundering, through the Grey Winters—"

"They stood," the cat agreed, "when there were enough of us to feed them. You think those pretty lights run on nothing?" It licked one cracked paw, unhurried, though Marguerite could see the tremor in it now, could feel the fear coming off its fur like heat off a stove. "The old covenant fed the wards. Oath-keepers, both sides, binding realm to realm. Your founders broke it. Our elders died of it. What's left is an old knot holding together with rotten string, and tonight—" the cat paused, and its voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse, "—tonight the string started going."

"The screaming," Marguerite whispered. "On the hills. I heard—"

"Then you heard true. The wild things feel it first. The land feels it first." The cat rose, stretched, ribs ridging under matted fur. "There are others who remember. Not many. A dragon, if it still lives. Wolves who keep the old stones. Folk in the city too, maybe, if any blood remembers what it's for." It turned its clouded eye toward the dark end of Tanner's Row, toward the distant glimmer of the border wards visible even from here, a faint auroral smear above the rooftops that most Vellish never bothered to look at. "Find them. Or don't. But know this, talker: when the lights go out, the city will learn in one night what the wilds have known for three hundred years. Hunger has no manners."

"Wait—" Marguerite's hand shot out, then froze, because touching the cat meant touching evidence, meant witnesses. "Wait. Who sent you? Who *taught* you to—"

"No one taught me. That's rather the point." The cat leapt down from the table, landing soft as spilled shadow. "The covenant's breaking wakes things up. Old debts come due. Even in a godless city." It glanced back once, one-eyed, ancient. "Especially in a godless city. Mind the grey coats, talker. One of them has been circling your stall for a quarter hour, and he doesn't buy charms."

He was gone between a fishmonger's crates before Marguerite's breath came back.

And then—because the cat had said it, because saying it made it real—Marguerite felt it. The prickle between the shoulder blades. The particular quality of attention that meant someone was watching not a stall but a person.

At the mouth of Tanner's Row stood a ward-inspector. Grey coat, silver door-pin catching the lamplight. He wasn't reading the proscription list nailed to the wall beside him. He was reading Marguerite. Had been, perhaps, for some time. And as Marguerite watched, he raised one hand—not to signal arrest, not yet—but slowly, deliberately, to touch the pin at his collar, the way a man might touch a weapon.

Around them the market roared on, warm and blind. Above the rooftops, far away, the wards' faint glow stuttered.

Just once. Just for a heartbeat, like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.

Marguerite began, very calmly, to pack up their charms.
