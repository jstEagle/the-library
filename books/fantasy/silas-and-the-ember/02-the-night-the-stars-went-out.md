## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The first sign was the mice.

Marguerite had been packing up her stall in the night market, coiling the luck-knots into their basket, when every rat and mouse within earshot of Copperlane came streaming past her feet like a gray river with a destination. They did not scatter. They did not squabble over crumbs. They ran in one direction—south, toward the river—and they were *singing*, a thin chittering chorus that only she could hear: *away, away, away, the walls are waking wrong.*

She stood with a half-wound knot still in her fingers and felt the hair rise along her arms.

"Oi, Montrose." Old Bram from the pie cart squinted at her. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," she said lightly. "A customer." But her hands had gone cold, and when she glanced down the lane she saw them again—the mice, hundreds of them, pouring between cobblestones, under doors, through gutter-grates, all flowing south like water finding its level.

The cat found her before the bells did.

It was the stray from the market's edge, the one with the torn ear who took her mouse-whispers as toll for leaving her stall alone. It landed on her counter without sound, and its eyes were blown wide and black, and it spoke—not in the loose, lazy way cats spoke, all appetite and opinion, but in a voice scraped raw with urgency.

*They're failing,* it said. *The wards. The old ones under the ground. I can feel them through my paws. They're going out like lamps.*

"That's not possible," Marguerite whispered. "The wards have stood three hundred years."

*Three hundred years is a long time to stand,* the cat said, *and no one has fed them.*

Then the bells began.

Not the market bell, not the curfew bell—a sound she had never heard, a deep groaning peal from the Wardens' Tower that made the cobbles shiver and set dogs howling in every yard from Copperlane to the Gate Quarter. Doors banged open up and down the lane. Lamplighters froze mid-climb. And above it all, above the shouting and the clatter, came a sound Marguerite felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears: a vast, splintering *crack*, like the sky itself developing a fault line.

She looked up.

The stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud. They went out in a wave, from the north horizon inward, whole constellations snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, until the darkness overhead was total and close and *wrong*—a ceiling instead of a sky. Vell had never been a city of dark nights; even here, the wards threw up their faint silver shimmer at the borders, and the glow of wardlight softened everything above. Now there was nothing. The darkness pressed down like a hand.

The screaming started small and spread the way fire spreads, street to street, until the whole night market was a churn of bodies and dropped goods and someone weeping openly in front of Bram's abandoned pie cart.

"The stars!" a woman shrieked. "Where are the stars? Where did they *go*?"

"They've fallen! The wards have fallen, the wilds are coming—"

"Get inside, get inside, bar your doors—"

Marguerite didn't move. She couldn't. She was listening.

Beneath the panic, beneath the bells and the screams, she could hear something else now that the wards' constant low hum had been ripped away—a silence underneath the silence, and moving through it, faint as a pulse, the land beyond the walls. She had spent her whole life hearing beasts: cats and rats, pigeons and carriage horses, the dull contented mutter of pigs in the stockyards. That was the limit of her gift, or so she'd believed. But this was different. This was older. A voice without a mouth, rising out of the dark country south of Vell like heat off stone.

*...broken... broken... the door is broken...*

Her knees nearly gave.

*Marguerite.* The cat pressed against her ankle, trembling. *Run. Not home. Home is where they'll look. Run.*

"Why would they look for me?" she breathed. "I sell knots. I'm nobody."

But even as she said it, she saw them: four figures in gray-and-silver livery pushing against the market crowd, ward-inspectors, lanterns held high, and one of them—she knew his face, the narrow one, Inspector Corvel, who'd bought a luck-knot from her twice and watched her too carefully both times—was pointing. Pointing at her stall. At her.

At the cat sitting at her feet like a witness.

"*There!* Stop her!"

Marguerite ran.

* * *

Copperlane became Tanner's Row became the steep crooked stairs behind the dyeworks, and Marguerite took them two at a time with her basket banging against her hip, because some stubborn animal part of her refused to abandon the luck-knots, three months of work, and later she would laugh bitterly at that—at fleeing for her life with a basket of knotted string because letting go felt like waste.

Behind her, boots on stone. Shouting. "*Beast-speaker!* The word rang off the walls, ugly and ancient, a word from law-books older than anyone living. *By writ of the Founders' Compact—stop, or be taken—*"

They'd suspected. Of course they'd suspected. You didn't survive twenty-three years hiding a gift like hers without leaving threads loose somewhere—the way horses calmed under her hand, the way rats never touched her stores, the way Corvel had once seen her murmur at a pigeon and swear afterward that the pigeon had *murmured back*. She'd told herself for years that Vell was too big, too busy, too indifferent to notice one quiet charm-seller. She had built her whole life inside that lie, mortar and beam, and now it was coming down around her ears exactly the way the wards had come down.

*Left,* hissed the cat, keeping pace along a wall top. *Left, then down through the tannery. They won't follow the smell.*

She followed the smell. It was heroic.

The tannery yards at night were a maze of stretching frames and reeking pits, and Marguerite wove through them with her sleeve over her nose while the inspectors' lanterns swung and scattered light across the vats. Twice she heard them pass within ten feet of her. Once, a horse in the adjoining stable whickered softly—*this way, little sister, this way*—and nudged open a gate with its nose to give her a shortcut through to the river road, and she loved that horse with a ferocity that surprised her.

And then there were no more streets.

The South Gate loomed ahead of her, and it was broken.

Not damaged. Broken—the great ward-stone arch that had crowned it for three centuries split down the middle like a cracked egg, its halves leaning apart, and where the silver shimmer of the border should have hung there was nothing at all, just an open doorway onto a darkness so complete it seemed solid. Beyond the gate, the road simply stopped existing, swallowed by a night that had teeth in it somewhere. Wild country. The true wilds, the ones mothers invoked and mapmakers shaded with hatched lines and left blank.

Lamplight bobbed behind her. Corvel's voice, closer now, ragged with running: "*Seal the gate! No one crosses—by the Compact, no one crosses!*"

Marguerite stood at the threshold of everything she knew, chest heaving, basket clutched like a shield.

If she turned back, they would take her. There would be a cell in the Warden's Tower, questions with hot iron at the end of them, and after that—no one knew what happened to beast-speakers after that, because no one ever came back to tell it. Her mother had known. Her mother had *known*, and had hidden Marguerite's gift so fiercely, so desperately, right up until the night the inspectors came for *her* instead—

No. Not tonight. Tonight was for running.

*Go,* said the cat, from atop the shattered gatepost, and its voice was almost gentle. *The land is calling you. Can't you hear it? Only you can hear it.*

"I can't go into *that*," she whispered.

*You can't stay in this,* the cat said. *Choose your dark.*

Marguerite Montrose, charm-seller, liar, daughter of a woman who died for her secret, stepped through the broken gate of Vell and out of the world.

* * *

The dark took her like water takes a stone.

For a hundred paces she could still see the gate behind her, the torches, the tiny furious figures of the inspectors who would not follow—of course they would not follow; the Compact bound them to the walls as surely as it had ever bound anything else. Then a bend in the dead road hid the city, and there was nothing but blackness and her own breathing and the crunch of ash underfoot.

Ash. She knelt and touched it. Warm. Freshly fallen, though no fire burned anywhere she could see, drifting down out of a starless sky like grey snow. It settled on her shoulders, in her hair, and it smelled of embers and far-off thunder and something sweetly, terribly alive.

That was when the land spoke to her properly.

Not the beasts—there were beasts out here, she could feel them, eyes opening in the dark all around her, fox and owl and things with no common name—but the land itself, the deep slow voice beneath the soil, the one she'd caught only a thread of from the gate. Up close it was overwhelming. It came up through her knees, through her palms where they pressed the cold ground, a voice like roots talking in their sleep.

*Small warm thing,* it said. *Small warm thing with the old ears. We smelled you coming. Three hundred years, and we smelled you coming.*

"I don't—" Her voice cracked. She tried again. "I don't understand what's happening."

*The covenant broke,* the land said, simply, the way one says *the sun rose*. *The anchors failed. The doors closed and then the doors rotted and tonight the last hinge snapped. The stars are not gone, child. They are turned away. They will not look upon us until the pact stands again.*

"And if it doesn't?"

Silence, then, but not empty silence—a listening silence, a hundred thousand unseen creatures holding very still around a girl kneeling in warm ash on a dead road.

*Then the hunger comes south,* the land said. *Into your city. Into your soft bright streets. The wilds have been starving a long time, small warm thing, and the walls that kept us out also kept the feeding grounds from healing. Your ancestors made a bargain and then they broke it, and now the bill has come due.*

Marguerite sat back on her heels. Ash drifted through her open fingers. Above her, the blind sky. Behind her, a city full of screaming people who had never known her name. Ahead of her, a darkness full of voices that had apparently been waiting three centuries for her specifically—for her *ears*, her buried, cursed, precious ears.

All her life she had hidden the best part of herself in a cellar and called it safety. All her life she had sold luck to people who would have burned her if they'd known where her luck really came from. And now the choice had been made for her, violently, in a single night: there was no going back through that gate. There was only forward, into the largest silence of her life, which was somehow also the loudest conversation she had ever heard.

She should have been terrified. She was—her hands wouldn't stop shaking, and the dark had real teeth in it somewhere, she could hear them breathing.

But underneath the terror, quiet and shocking as a struck match, was something else.

Freedom. Terrible, irreversible freedom. No more knots. No more hiding. No more pretending to be small.

"Well," Marguerite said aloud, to the land, to the dark, to whatever was listening. "Which way, then?"

The land answered with a warmth on the wind—a thread of it, faint but unmistakable, smelling of ember and honey, drifting from somewhere high in the hills to the east. And beneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, a voice. Not the land's voice. Older. Tired. Burning.

*Come, speaker,* it said. *Before my fire goes out, come.*

Marguerite gathered her basket of useless, precious luck-knots, turned her back on the broken gate and the city that had never deserved her, and walked into the wilds.
