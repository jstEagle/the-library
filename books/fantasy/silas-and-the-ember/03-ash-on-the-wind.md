## 3. Ash on the Wind

The wilds did not want her.

Marguerite learned this in the first hour, when the brambles along the old shepherd's track leaned toward her ankles like fingers testing a wrist for a pulse. In Vell, the land had been silent—muzzled by three centuries of wards, flattened into scenery. Out here, past the shattered gate with its stones still glowing like banked coals, everything had an opinion. The grass resented her boots. The crows debated her weight and concluded she was carrying bread. The oaks overhead muttered about the last time humans came through, which had apparently not ended well for anyone.

She walked anyway, because walking was the only thing left.

Behind her, Vell was a bruise of orange light against the starless dark—the city burning its lamps against a sky that no longer held stars to answer them. Ahead, the wilderness unrolled black and breathing. She had crossed the threshold of the broken gate expecting to die, and instead she had stepped into a conversation that never stopped.

*Small thing,* said the wind through the pines. *Small warm thing.*

"I'm not small," she told it, hoarse. "I'm just far away."

The pines laughed at that, a dry rattling sound, and let her pass.

Her feet found the old road because roads remember being walked. It ran north and slightly wrong, curving where no cartographer would have curved it, and Marguerite understood after a while that the road itself was avoiding something—or going toward something, with the reluctant determination of a dog dragged on a leash. The land spoke of it in fragments as she moved: *the mountain, the mountain, the one who burns low, the one who has been dying since the sky went quiet.*

Three centuries, the wolves would have said, if there were wolves near enough to ask. Three hundred years since the founders of Vell broke the covenant and sealed their half of the world behind wards, and somewhere out here, whatever had kept the other half alive had been guttering ever since.

By the second night she smelled it: ash. Not woodsmoke—woodsmoke is sweet at the edges, hungry, alive. This was old ash, patient ash, ash that had been falling so long it had become a kind of weather. It drifted down through the branches like grey snow and settled on her shoulders, and it was *warm*. Faintly, impossibly warm, the way a stone stays warm hours after the fire dies.

She followed it uphill.

The trail thickened until each flake was big as a thumbnail and glowed faintly at its heart, embers wrapped in their own funerals. The trees thinned with it, giving up one by one, until she stood in a bowl of bare rock where nothing grew and the ground itself was soft with centuries of falling. Her breath made ghosts in the cold air; the ash made halos around her wrists where it touched her skin and did not burn.

At the bowl's center, a cave mouth opened in the mountainside like a held breath.

Warmth rolled out of it—not the warmth of the ash but a living heat, deep and slow, the way a body is warm. Marguerite stood at the threshold with her heart hammering her ribs and every buried instinct screaming that this was either salvation or the end of her, and quite possibly both.

"Hello," she said, in the way she spoke to beasts. Plain words, open hands, no lies in the posture.

The dark inside answered.

*You smell of Vell,* said a voice like a hearth learning to speak again after years of cold. *And of wards. And of fear. Two of those I forgive.*

"I'm not—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I'm not warded anymore. The wards are gone. All of them. That's why I'm here."

Silence, long enough that she counted her own heartbeats. Then, from the darkness, a sound she knew from the night market—from the corner where the old dog slept that everyone thought was only sleeping: the slow drag of breath, the effortful rearrangement of a body too tired to hold itself together.

*Come in, then, daughter-of-both-and-neither.* A pause. *Mind the tail. It is longer than it used to be, and prouder than it deserves.*

* * *

He was dying. She saw it before she saw him.

The cave opened into a hollow vast as a cathedral, and the walls glittered—not with crystal but with ash, compacted over three hundred years into a smooth grey shell, like the inside of a bell. At its center lay a shape that her eyes refused, at first, to make sense of: a curve of scale the color of cooling iron, a wing folded like a banner furled for a funeral, a head the size of a haycart resting on the stone floor because lifting it had become more than he could spend.

Ember-dragon. She knew it the way she knew birdsong—not from books, from the blood. Her grandmother's stories had been full of them, back when her grandmother still talked and hadn't yet decided that talking invited inspectors. *The dragons kept the doors between the worlds,* she'd said, stirring soup. *When they went, the doors became walls.*

"You're the last," Marguerite said. Not a question.

*I am the remainder,* Silas said. His voice filled the hollow and made the ash-shell hum. *There is a difference. The last of something is finished. A remainder is still owed.*

She came closer, stepping carefully over the long architecture of his ribs. Up close she could see what the dimness had hidden: cracks in his scales, fine as hairline fractures in porcelain, and through each crack a dull red glow, like coals under banked ash. He was burning from the inside out, or rather—he was failing to burn, his fire eating itself for fuel with nothing coming in.

"The covenant," she said. "It broke, and you've been—"

*Dying by inches. Yes. Three hundred years of inches.* One vast eye opened, amber shot through with threads of black, and regarded her with an attention that made her feel flayed and seen in the same instant. *I felt the wards fall, little speaker. Do you know what that was like? After three hundred years of silence? Like deafness ending all at once. Every voice on the mountain at full volume. I wept. Dragons do not weep often; it is undignified and the tears scald.*

Despite everything—the exhaustion, the terror still humming in her legs, the knowledge that she was standing in the ribcage of a creature that could end her with less effort than a yawn—Marguerite laughed. It came out cracked and surprised.

"What do you want from me?"

*Want.* The eye narrowed. *Greedy word. Let us say: need. You speak beast-tongue. All of it—I can hear it in your accent, the way you answered the pines without flinching. Wolf and wren, eel and elk. That tongue died out of human throats when the covenant broke. Your city bred it out, generation by generation, because a people who can hear the land cannot lie to it comfortably.*

"My mother had it," Marguerite said quietly. "Before me."

*And where is your mother?*

The question landed like a hand on an old bruise. "Vell took her. When I was twelve." She looked away, at the glowing cracks in his hide. "That's not why I'm here."

*Everything is why you're here,* Silas said, not unkindly. *Survivors are made entirely of their reasons.* He shifted, and the movement cost him—she heard it, the grinding effort of scale on stone—and his head turned so that one amber eye filled her whole horizon. *Here is my bargain, Marguerite Montrose. Yes, I know your name; the wind gossips worse than magpies. The covenant is broken, the anchor-stones scattered, the wards that remain are dying rags. But the pact can be reforged. It was forged once by voices, and it can be forged again by voices—but it needs a speaker who can address every realm at once. Beast and river and stone and ember. It needs yours.*

"And what do I get?"

*My fire.* He said it simply, and the glow beneath his cracks brightened as if the word fed it. *I have been hoarding three hundred years of flame with nowhere to put it. I am a hearth with no house. Give me your voice to carry, and I will give you the fire to carry it—a bond, speaker-to-ember, the old kind. You will walk into places that would kill any other human and be welcomed as kin. You will call oath-keepers out of hiding: the wolf-packs who remember, the river-folk who bargained, the ones your maps forgot on purpose. And when the time comes—*

He stopped. The silence had a shape to it, and the shape was a door he wasn't opening yet.

"When the time comes, what?"

*When the time comes,* Silas said, *you will understand why the price of survival is not blood. But I will not lie to you at the threshold, so hear this now: the bond does not come free. What you give me, you give for good. Your voice will no longer be only yours. There will be days it speaks languages you never learned, grieves losses that were never yours. You will be a door yourself, and doors do not get to choose which winds blow through.*

Marguerite stood very still. Around her, the cathedral of ash ticked and settled, and beneath her feet she could feel his heartbeat—slow, enormous, stubborn as a coal that refuses the morning.

All her life she had hidden the gift. Folded it into luck-knots and mouse-whispers, sold it in pieces small enough to pass for superstition, kept her mouth shut while inspectors walked the market rows and her mother was marched out of the city gates with her hands bound. She had spent twenty-three years being small on purpose.

And here, in the dark, the largest living thing she had ever met was asking her to be large.

"If I say yes," she said, "you teach me. Everything. No holding back because I'm human."

*Human,* Silas rumbled, and there was something in it that might have been amusement, might have been grief. *Little speaker, I have watched your kind for nine hundred years. There is nothing little about you. You simply keep it folded.*

He extended one foreleg—the motion agonized, deliberate—and laid it on the stone before her, palm-up, like a handshake offered across a chasm. The scales were warm as sun-baked rock. Beneath them, the fire waited.

"Wary hope," Marguerite murmured. "That's what my mother called this feeling. Hope you check twice for knives."

*A wise woman, your mother.* The amber eye gleamed. *Check mine, then. Look as long as you like. And when you're done—*

She laid her hand on his palm.

The bond closed over her like warm water. She felt him pour in—not fire, not yet, but the *shape* of fire, the grammar of it—and felt herself pour back into him, her voice settling into his hollows like a key into a lock three centuries rusted shut. Somewhere far above, the ash stopped falling. For the first time in three hundred years, the mountain breathed in instead of out.

*Well,* said Silas, and his voice had changed—still vast, but steadier, as if a draft had been stopped somewhere deep in him. *First lesson, then. We go north, to the first oath-stone. And we go quickly, because the wolves who guard it have long memories, short tempers—*

He paused. His head lifted, further than it had lifted in decades, nostrils flaring toward the cave mouth and the world beyond.

*—and company. Something is coming up the mountain, little speaker. Something that walks like a hound and smells of neither beast nor man.*

Marguerite's hand tightened on his scales. "Friend or hunter?"

*With what's loose in the world now?* The ember-dragon rose, joint by joint, unfolding out of his long dying like a letter finally opened. *We're about to find out.*
