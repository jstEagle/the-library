## 4. What the Wolves Remember

The hound that walked beside me was the wrong shape for a hound.

I noticed it most in the way he moved—too fluid, too economical, as if walking were a language he'd learned from a book and spoke with an accent no one could quite place. His gait never varied, never tired, and when he paused at the edge of a clearing he did not sniff the air like a dog but *read* it, head tilted, eyes half-lidded, the way I imagined a man might read a letter from an old enemy.

"You're doing it again," I murmured, low enough that only he could hear.

"Doing what." Silas's voice came out of the hound's throat like smoke through a keyhole—rough, patient, faintly amused.

"Standing like something that used to be enormous."

He huffed—a sound that in a true dog would have been a laugh. "Three centuries ago I could have lain across this valley like a bridge. Now I am this." He flexed one ash-grey paw, claws clicking on frost-brittle grass. "Be grateful for the disguise. A dragon on the road draws questions. A mangy hound draws only pity."

"And pity is cheaper."

"Pity is *safer*. You of all people should know the difference."

I didn't answer that. The road climbed away from the lowlands where the wards had died, up through pine country where the air smelled of resin and old snow, and with every mile the land grew louder. That was the thing no one had warned me about—the wilds weren't silent now that the covenant was broken. They were *starving*, and starving things are never quiet. I heard it in fragments all day: the bramble-scratch mutter of hedgerows complaining about the cold, the long low grievance of crows who had forgotten what they were supposed to be watching for. And beneath it all, farther out, a chorus of voices I knew at once and dreaded.

Wolves.

They had been following us since dawn. Not hunting—*escorting*. Keeping pace just beyond sight, their presence a pressure against my gift like a thumb pressed to a bruise. Silas felt them too; twice his ears swiveled back along our trail though his nose told him nothing useful.

"They know we're coming," he said as the light went copper and long.

"I know."

"The stone they guard is the first of the three. Without it, the forge is useless. Without the forge—" He let the sentence die. We both knew how it ended: without the forge, without the reforged covenant, his fire guttered out for good, and the wilds finished their slow collapse into something toothless and mad, and Vell starved behind walls that no longer kept anything out except help.

The pines broke open at dusk onto a bowl of a valley, and there they were.

There must have been thirty of them, ringed around a granite outcrop at the valley's heart, and on the outcrop stood a pillar of black stone shot through with veins of dull gold—the first oath-stone, its surface carved so deep with old script that the shadows themselves seemed to spell. The wolves were grey and white and storm-dark, big as yearling calves, and every one of them was looking at us.

Not at me. At the hound.

A she-wolf detached herself from the ring and came forward at an unhurried walk. She was older than the others—silver at the muzzle, one ear notched, her eyes the pale yellow of candlelight through horn. She stopped ten feet away and sat, which somehow made her more terrifying than if she'd bared her teeth.

"*You bring a dead god on a rope of flesh,*" she said.

Her voice arrived inside my skull the way beast-speech always did—not sound but meaning, wearing whatever shape the listener's mind gave it. Beside me, Silas went very still.

"She speaks for the pack," I said aloud, because my mouth needed something to do. "I'm Marguerite. This is—"

"We know what this is." Her gaze didn't waver from Silas. "*Ember-son. Last of the furnace-blood. We smelled your ash on the wind for two days.*" Then those yellow eyes swung to me, and I felt them go through me like a spear through wet canvas. "*And you. The small loud one. The one who talks.*"

My hand found the hound's ruff without meaning to. Under the coarse disguise-fur, his skin was warm as a banked stove.

"We've come for the oath-stone," I said. "We mean to reforge the covenant. All of it. The whole old pact."

A murmur moved through the ring of wolves—not sound exactly, but a shifting, a collective lean. The she-wolf's ears flattened and rose again.

"*Three hundred years,*" she said, "*since the two-legs broke their word. Three hundred years our pups have been born smaller and our hunts have come up empty and the deer have gone strange and the rivers have forgotten our names. Three hundred years, and now a two-leg walks into our valley asking for the stone back.*" She rose. She was taller than I'd guessed. "*Why should we give it?*"

"Because without it, everything dies," I said. "Including you."

"*Everything dies anyway. Slowly is still dying.*" She circled me once, close enough that her shoulder brushed my hip, deliberate as a signature. "*The old ones left a toll on the stones. Did your dragon tell you?*"

I glanced down. Silas's hound-eyes were fixed on the middle distance, and I understood suddenly that yes, he had known, and had wanted to see whether I would flinch when I learned it myself.

"No," I said.

"*A truth,*" the she-wolf said. "*Spoken aloud. One the speaker has never confessed to any living soul. The stone remembers truths. It drinks them. That is how oaths are fed—that is what an oath *is*, little speaker. A truth you keep paying forever.*" She sat again, tail curled over her paws, and waited.

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere above us, the sky was going the bruised violet it went these days, ever since the stars had drowned—no stars now, only a flat dark ceiling, and everyone in Vell pretending not to look up.

I thought about lying. Of course I did. I thought about the inventory of my life, searching for a sin small enough to spend, a truth cheap enough to barter. I had stolen bread. I had shorted customers on their luck-knots when I was tired. I had once, aged eleven, pushed a boy into the canal for laughing at my patched coat.

But the she-wolf's eyes were candle-yellow and ancient, and I knew—I knew the way I knew a mouse's fear or a cat's contempt—that the stone would taste a lie, and the pack would know, and we would leave this valley with nothing but our own footprints behind us.

And underneath the calculation, quieter, there was the other thing. The thing I had carried up the mountain road and through the broken gate and across three days of wilderness, wrapped in oilcloth like a blade.

Silas leaned his disguised head against my leg. Not pushing. Just weight. Just warmth.

"*Take your time,*" the she-wolf said, not unkindly. "*The stone has waited three hundred years. It can wait until moonrise.*"

I looked at the oath-stone. Up close, in the last light, I could see that the gold veins in it pulsed—faintly, slowly, like a heart beating under blankets. Like something asleep, dreaming of being whole.

"My mother," I said.

The word came out cracked. I swallowed and started again.

"My mother's name was Odile Montrose, and she had the same gift I have. Worse, actually—she could do more than talk. Beasts *obeyed* her. When I was nine she stopped a bull with a word. When I was twelve she talked a sparrowhawk out of taking our neighbor's pigeons, one by one, gently, like a customs officer." My voice was steady. That surprised me. "She taught me to hide it. She taught me that in Vell, the gift was a death sentence, and the only way to survive was to be small and dull and lucky—luck-knots, mouse-whispers, harmless trinkets. Never speak plainly. Never let them *see*."

The wolves had gone utterly still. Thirty pairs of eyes, and not one of them blinking.

"When the inspectors came to our street, I was sixteen. They were doing door-to-door sweeps—someone had reported a beast-speaker in the Tanner's Quarter. My mother had maybe a minute of warning. She could have called every animal within a mile to cover our escape. Dogs, horses, the rats in the walls—she could have turned the whole quarter into a riot, and we'd have been over the rooftops before anyone knew what happened." I heard my own breath go out of me. "Instead she looked at me and put one finger to her lips. *Hide it*, she'd taught me. So I hid. I went down into the cellar and I pulled the hatch shut and I stayed absolutely silent while they took her."

The gold veins in the stone pulsed. Once. Twice. Faster.

"She didn't struggle. That's the part I can't—I've had six years to make it kind, and I can't. She went quietly because she was afraid that if she used the gift, even once, even to save herself, they'd start looking for whoever else had it. They'd find me." My hands had closed into fists without my permission, nails biting palm. "She traded herself for my silence. And I *let* her. I sat in the dark under the floorboards and listened to her boots on the stairs and I never made a sound. Six years, and I have never said that out loud. Not to anyone. Not to the dark. I told myself it was what she wanted. I tell myself that every single night, and every single night it doesn't help."

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath in the pines.

Then the she-wolf rose, crossed the space between us, and pressed her broad forehead against my sternum—the way wolves greet the ones they grieve with. Her fur was cold and her breath was warm.

"*The stone accepts it,*" she said softly. "*Grief is the oldest truth. It is the only coin that never debases.*"

Behind her, the oath-stone flared. The gold veins lit from within, bright as dawn through a shutter, and a sound rolled out of it that was not sound—a chord, a held note, the memory of a voice singing in harmony with itself. For one heartbeat the whole valley smelled of summer.

Then it dimmed again, and the pillar stood dark, but different. Fed. Waiting.

"*Take it,*" the she-wolf said. "*Or rather—take its blessing. The stone does not move. But it will know you now, and the road to the second stone runs through country that has forgotten how to be kind. Walk with its memory in you and the wild things will hesitate before they bite.*"

I touched the pillar with two fingers. Warmth sank into them like a promise.

It was Silas who broke the moment. His hound-body had gone rigid, ears pricked toward the southern rim of the valley, and when he spoke his voice had lost all its patience.

"Marguerite. We have company. Two-legged, armed—and they're not ward-inspectors." A pause, and then, grimly: "One of them is carrying something that smells of maps and ink. And she's shouting your name."
