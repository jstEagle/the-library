## 5. The Cartographer's Daughter

Isolde Vane had inherited three things from her father: his steady hands, his terrible eyesight, and a locked drawer she had been forbidden to open since the age of seven.

She opened it the night the stars went out.

The house on Lanternmaker's Row still smelled of him—ink and cedar oil and the faint char of a lamp left burning too long—and she sat at his drafting table with the drawer's contents spread before her like a confession. Maps. Dozens of them, drawn in her father's precise, unlovely hand, and every one of them wrong. That was the first thing she understood. Isolde Vane had been correcting her father's work since she could hold a stylus, and she knew his errors the way other daughters knew their mothers' lullabies. These were not errors. They were lies, laid down stroke by careful stroke over thirty years of deliberate craftsmanship.

The river on the oldest chart did not bend where he had drawn it bending. It bent three miles east, coiling around a hill marked only with a small symbol she had never seen in any guild atlas: a circle crossed by a line, like an eye that had been closed.

There were eleven of those symbols across the whole of the frontier. Eleven hills, eleven springs, eleven standing stones that official maps recorded as *glacial debris* or *unremarkable outcropping* or nothing at all.

Oath-stones. The word arrived in her mind unbidden, in her father's voice, in the tone he used for things too dangerous to say above a whisper: *If anything happens to me, Isolde, burn the drawer. Promise me you'll burn it.*

She had promised. She had also, even at seven, been her father's daughter, which meant she had already learned that promises made to frightened men were debts best paid slowly.

Now the stars were dark, the wards were ash, and somewhere beyond the broken gate her father's lies had walked off into the wilderness wearing her boots and carrying her satchel of instruments. She had followed the rumors through the panicked city—the beast-speaker, they said, a charm-seller from the night market who talked to wolves—and she had found the trail the way she found everything: by reading the ground.

* * *

She caught up with them two days into the wilds, at the edge of a valley where the grass grew silver and the wind carried a sound like distant singing.

Isolde saw the wolf first—or what she took for a wolf, enormous and gray-muzzled, its coat shot through with threads of dull red like cooling embers. It stood at the crest of the hill watching her approach with an attention no animal should possess. Beside it, smaller, raggeder, stood a figure in a patched traveling cloak with a scarf wound up to the eyes.

"You're following us," the figure said. Not a question. And when Isolde was close enough to see the face beneath the scarf—young, sharp-boned, wary as a sparrow—"You're from Vell. You have ink on your fingers."

"Cartographer," Isolde said. "Isolde Vane." She held up her satchel. "I'm not with the inspectors. I'm not with anyone. I brought my father's maps."

The beast-speaker—Marguerite; the wolves had howled the name across three valleys, which was how Isolde had tracked them—did not move. But the great wolf-thing came down the hill toward her, and Isolde's courage, which she had always suspected was mostly stubbornness wearing courage's clothes, very nearly deserted her entirely.

It stopped a hand's breadth away and breathed her in. Its eyes were the color of a banked fire, and behind the animal shape of them she saw something old looking out. Something that hurt.

"You smell of ink and guilt," it said, in a voice like gravel settling in a hearth. "Both are useful. Come."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen menhir, one of her father's eleven symbols, and Isolde unrolled the maps by firelight while Marguerite watched with the expression of someone being handed a live coal.

"These are covenant sites," Marguerite said. "How does a city cartographer come to have these?"

"My father drew them. Over thirty years. He was a surveyor for the Guild—he mapped borders, trade roads, the usual. But he had another commission. One he never named, never spoke of, never wrote down." Isolde traced the closed-eye symbol with one finger. "He'd disappear for weeks. Come back thinner, quieter, with survey notes he kept in cipher. I broke the cipher when I was nineteen. It took me four years and I wish to God it had taken forty."

"What did the notes say?" the wolf asked. Silas, Marguerite called him. A dragon wearing a hound's body, though Isolde had not yet found the courage to ask how.

"They said the founders of Vell didn't lose the covenant." Isolde heard her own voice go flat, the way it did when she was holding something heavy at arm's length. "Everyone believes the wards failed. An accident. A decay. The stars went out because the old magic simply... wore thin." She looked up. "My father's notes say the founders broke it. Deliberately. Three hundred years ago, the nine founding families of Vell performed a severance ritual at the mountain forge. They cut the city loose from the wilds so that all the magic that used to flow between the realms would pool inside the walls instead. Inside *their* walls. They've been drinking from a stolen well ever since, and they built the ward-inspectors and the beast-speech laws to make sure no one ever asked where the water came from."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, the silver grass sang.

Marguerite had gone very still. "Your father worked for the Guild. For Vell. Why would he map the proof of Vell's worst crime?"

"Because guilt is an inheritance too." Isolde folded her hands to keep them steady. "His grandfather was one of the severance ritualists' descendants—a Vane signed the original charter. My father spent his whole life measuring the size of what our family helped break. He couldn't undo it. He wasn't brave enough to publish it, or maybe he was just sensible enough to know what the inspectors would do. So he hid it. He hid it so well that when the wardens finally came for him last spring, they found nothing." Her throat tightened. "He died in the interrogator's cells anyway. They said his heart gave out. I think he simply finished paying what he thought he owed, and stopped."

Silence, except for the fire and the singing grass.

Then Silas moved—flowing across the campsite with that impossible weightlessness—and lay down beside her, close enough that warmth radiated off his ember-threaded coat like a stove. He said nothing. He didn't need to. Isolde discovered that after four days alone in a dying world, the simple animal fact of another warm body beside hers undid her more thoroughly than any words could have.

"He knew about me," Marguerite said quietly, after a while. "Didn't he. Your father. That's why he mapped the sites so carefully. He was leaving a road for whoever came next."

Isolde nodded, and found she could not speak, and was grateful when neither of them pressed.

* * *

They walked for six days, and Isolde learned the shape of her new companions the way she learned all shapes: by measurement.

Marguerite measured small but dense, like a star. They spoke to everything—hawks, beetles, the sour-tempered bramble that tried to snag their packs—and each conversation cost them something visible, a flicker behind the eyes, a hand pressed briefly flat against their own chest as if keeping something in. On the fourth night Isolde asked why they never simply *stopped*, found some quiet valley, lived among creatures who wanted nothing from them but company.

Marguerite was a long time answering. "Because I spent my whole life hiding the thing that makes me me," they said. "And then the world ended, and I got to be useful instead of hidden. I'm not giving that back. Even if it kills me." A pause. "Especially if it kills me."

Silas measured vast, whatever shape he wore. He slept little and ate less, and Isolde noticed—because noticing was her gift—that the ember-threads in his coat were dimmer each morning. When she mentioned it, he looked at her with those banked-fire eyes and said, "Everything that burns is burning down, cartographer. The question is only what it lights on the way." She did not ask again.

And herself, Isolde measured against her father, and found the fit uncomfortably exact. She caught herself drawing in his style, thinking in his shorthand, waking at dawn because his ghost apparently kept his hours. On the fifth day she sat on a ridge overlooking the second oath-stone—a leaning pillar wrapped in living root—and copied its position onto fresh paper, and realized her hand had added, unprompted, the closed-eye symbol beside it.

Her inheritance, making itself known.

That evening, at the stone, things went wrong.

* * *

The stone was guarded. Of course it was guarded; her father's notes had warned of keepers at each site, though the notes had grown vague near the end, as if even in cipher he had feared writing certain truths. What came out of the treeline as they approached was not wolves, exactly—it was too tall, too jointed, its shadow falling wrong—but it wore a wolf's hunger and a wolf's patience, and there were five of them, and Silas was a hound-shaped candle burning low.

What happened next happened fast. Marguerite stepped forward and *spoke*—and Isolde felt the speech pass through her own chest like a plucked string, words in no human tongue that her bones understood anyway. The lead creature answered. There was a long, grinding negotiation conducted in growls and gestures, and at the end of it the pack-keeper turned its wrong-shadowed head toward Isolde and pointed one long claw at her satchel.

"It wants to know what you carry," Marguerite translated, and their voice had gone careful. "It says the stone remembers everyone who approaches. It says your family name is written in its memory. *Vane.*"

The word dropped into the clearing like a stone into a well.

Isolde's mouth went dry. "Then it knows what my family did."

"It knows," Marguerite said softly. "And it's asking whether you do."

She thought of her father in his locked-drawer study, measuring his guilt in ink. She thought of the founders drinking from their stolen well while the wilds starved. She thought of the interrogator's cells, and the heart that gave out, and the promise to burn the drawer that she had never kept and never would.

"Yes," she said aloud, to the creature, to the stone, to the dead. "I do. My family helped break this world. I'm here to help mend it, and I'll carry the debt however it's counted."

The pack-keeper studied her for a long moment. Then it reached past her—she flinched, and held her ground—and drew one claw across the surface of the oath-stone. Where it touched, the stone's gray surface flushed faintly warm, and a hairline seam opened along its length, revealing a hollow within.

Inside lay a fragment of something that glowed like a coal wrapped in cloth: rough, fist-sized, breathing light.

"The first shard," Silas murmured, and his voice shook. "Three hundred years, and the first shard."

Marguerite lifted it out with both hands, reverent as a midwife. And it was Isolde, watching them, who noticed what the others were too moved to see: that the pack-keeper had not left. It stood at the treeline, head cocked, its wrong shadow trembling—and then it threw back its head and loosed a cry that rolled down the valley and was answered, faintly, from the north. From the mountains.

From the direction of the forge.

And beneath the answering cry, carried on the wind like a whisper meant for her alone, Isolde heard her father's voice one last time, speaking the final line of his ciphered notes, the one she had never been able to decode until now:

*The last stone wakes only for the one who gives up what they love most—beware the Hollow King, who gave up nothing, and take nothing he offers.*

The wind died. The shard in Marguerite's hands pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat quickening.

Far to the north, something ancient turned its attention toward them, and began, patiently, to hunt.
