## 6. River of Small Betrayals

The river found them before they found it.

Marguerite smelled it first—green water and cold stone and something underneath that was not a smell at all but a *presence*, the way a room feels when someone is watching. Silas, padding beside her in his hound-shape, stopped so abruptly she nearly tripped over him. His hackles rose along his spine like a row of struck matches.

"It knows we're here," he said through her ear, low. "It has known since the ford at Vellmark."

The trees opened onto a wide brown ribbon of water, slow-moving, deceptively calm. Willows trailed their fingers in it. And standing on the far bank, hip-deep in the shallows, were people.

Or things shaped like people. They were beautiful the way drowned things are beautiful—skin slick and faintly luminous, hair moving as if underwater even though they stood in air. One of them raised a hand in greeting, and Marguerite saw the fingers webbed to the second knuckle.

"River-folk," Isolde breathed. She had her father's journal clutched to her chest like a shield. "The maps call them the *Sons of the Long Water*. My father wrote— he wrote that they remember every promise ever made on their banks."

"Then they'll remember what Vell promised," Silas said grimly, "and broke."

The nearest river-woman smiled. Her teeth were very white and very many.

"Travelers," she called, and her voice was the sound of water over pebbles. "Come down. The crossing is free." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "*Almost* free."

* * *

Isolde had never been good with water.

She told herself it was practical—a cartographer's daughter learned to respect rivers, to chart their moods, to note where bridges had washed out. But the truth was older and less flattering: her mother had drowned in the Vellmark flood when Isolde was nine, and Isolde had spent twenty years drawing coastlines and tributaries with a steadiness that was really just fear wearing a professional face.

Now she stood knee-deep in the shallows while the river-woman circled her, and the water felt like hands.

"You are the mapmaker's girl," the woman said. Not a question. "We knew your father. He came to us, before you were born, before the last of the old sites went dark. He asked to see the covenant roads. We showed him." Her smile thinned. "He drew them beautifully. And then he took those drawings home to Vell, and Vell used them to find the stones, and Vell pulled them out of the earth like teeth."

"I know," Isolde whispered.

"Do you?" The woman drifted closer. The water around her ankles swirled without current. "You carry his guilt like a stone in your pack. We can see it. It weighs more than the journal."

Isolde's throat closed. "I'm trying to fix what he did."

"Mm. Trying is a long road with no inn at the end." The river-woman reached into the water and lifted out—impossibly—a roll of vellum, dry despite everything, tied with green cord. "But roads can end. Look."

Isolde looked.

The vellum unrolled into a map, and it was *her* map—the one she'd been drafting since they left the wolves' forest, the one that existed only in her head, half-finished, the western reaches still blank. Except here it was complete. Every contour. Every site. And there, in the corner, drawn in her own hand because of course it was her own hand, was a small house outside Vellmark with a garden and a south-facing window for the light, and a label in careful script: *Home. No more rivers. No more mountains. No more dying dragons.*

Her breath caught. "That's not real."

"No," the woman agreed pleasantly. "But it could be. Walk away from this quest, and we will carry you home by water, swift and safe, before the Hollow King's hunters even know you've turned back. You'll have years. Decades. You'll finish your father's atlas and set right his name, and no one will ever ask you to burn for someone else's covenant." She tilted her head, luminous and kind. "Your father chose Vell over the wilds, little mapmaker. He lived a long life and died warm. Was he wrong?"

And that was the knife of it, wasn't it. Because Isolde had hated her father for three hundred miles—for the broken pact, for the stolen stones, for the guilt sewn into her like a seam—and she had loved him the whole time too, helplessly, the way you love the weather.

"He was afraid," Isolde said finally.

"Yes," said the river-woman. "So are you. Fear is honest. Come home, and be honest all your days."

Isolde's hand rose toward the map. Her fingers actually touched the vellum. She could feel the paper's texture, the faint tooth of it under her thumb, and behind it the pull of that south-facing window so strong it was almost physical, almost audible, a hum in her blood saying *enough, enough, you've done enough*—

Across the shallows, she heard Marguerite say, quietly, "No."

* * *

They had offered Marguerite hers first.

The river-man who came to her wore the shape of a young clerk, soft-handed, ink-stained—the kind of person who would never look twice at a luck-knot seller in the night market. He stood in the shallows and held out his cupped palms, and in them lay a vision like heat-shimmer: a small shop above a bakery. A counter with jars of charms. Neighbors who knew her name and thought nothing strange of the way sparrows landed on her windowsill. An ordinary life, an anonymous life, the life she'd been performing in Vell for ten years except performed *well*, performed without fear, performed until it became true.

No inspectors. No buried gift. No dying dragon depending on her voice.

"You could stop hiding," the clerk said gently. "Not by revealing yourself. By never needing to. The city forgets quickly. You'd be Marguerite of Copperlane, charm-seller, harmless, safe. Isn't safety what you've been buying all these years, knot by knot?"

And oh, it was. It was exactly that. Marguerite looked at the vision and felt something in her chest crack open with wanting—not grand wanting, not glory, just the small terrible hunger of a person who has been afraid every single day of her adult life.

Silas pressed warm against her leg. In her mind, his voice: *I cannot make this choice for you. I will not even argue. But I will tell you what I see.*

"What do you see?" she whispered.

*A woman in a bright little shop, selling luck to people whose luck has already run out. The wards stay broken. The stars stay dark. And every sparrow on her windowsill goes silent one winter, because the wilds starved, and the silence came over the walls like water.* A pause. *Comfort bought with everyone else's hunger. You already tried living that way. You told the wolves your truth about it.*

Marguerite closed her eyes. She saw her mother's face, the night the inspectors came—her mother shoving her into the cellar, hissing *don't you dare make a sound*, taking the blame, taking the cart, taking whatever came next so that Marguerite's gift could stay buried one more year. She had let her. That was the toll-truth she'd paid the wolves. She had let her mother be taken so she could keep hiding, keep selling knots, keep being safe.

She had already bought this vision once. With her mother.

"No," Marguerite said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "I'm done paying for safety with other people."

The shimmer in the clerk's hands guttered. For a moment his beauty slipped and she saw what moved beneath the shape—long, muscular, silver-grey, eyes like two coins at the bottom of dark water. Then the human mask returned, and the river-man bowed.

"So spoken," he said. "The river keeps count, beast-speaker. Refusals weigh more than acceptances. Remember that when you reach the mountain."

* * *

Isolde snatched her hand back from the map as if it had burned her.

Because Marguerite had said no—to a vision Isolde hadn't seen, couldn't see, but whose shape she recognized perfectly, because it was the same shape as her own. Safety. An ending. Permission to stop.

And Marguerite, who had less than any of them, who owned nothing but a satchel of trinkets and a voice the world had punished her for using—that one had said *no* like it cost her nothing.

It hadn't cost nothing. Isolde could see that now, watching her friend's face across the water. It had cost everything, and she'd paid it anyway, and somehow that made it possible.

"The map's a lie," Isolde said to the river-woman. Her voice steadied as she went. "Not the geography—the geography's perfect, that's what makes it vile. But the rest. There's no version of my life where I go home and the guilt stays behind. I'd just be drawing blank spaces forever instead of walking into them." She swallowed. "My father mapped the covenant roads and gave them to men who broke the world. The least I can do is walk them."

The river-woman regarded her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she lowered the vellum back beneath the water, and it dissolved into ripples.

"Grief refused is grief kept," she said. "Yours will be heavy. But heavy things anchor." She gestured downstream, where the brown water quickened between rocks. "The crossing is yours. All of yours. Go upriver to the mountain, oath-seekers. And when you stand at the forge—" her luminous eyes flicked to each of them, lingering longest on Silas, on the hound-shape that fooled no one here— "tell the fire the river remembers too. Tell it we were not consulted when they broke the world. Tell it we want our banks back."

* * *

They crossed at dusk, the river-folk forming a silent escort on either side, sleek shapes just below the surface keeping pace. On the far bank, the land climbed toward distant peaks where a smudge of smoke—or cloud, or something worse—hung over the saddle of the highest pass.

Marguerite wrung out her hem and did not look back at the water. Isolde did, once, briefly—at the place where a perfect map had dissolved—and then resolutely forward.

Silas shook the river from his coat and paused, nose lifted to the wind coming down off the mountain.

Marguerite felt him go rigid.

"What is it?"

"Smoke," he said. "Not woodsmoke. Not forge-smoke." His voice dropped to gravel. "It's *mine*. Someone up there is burning ember-ash, and there is only one creature left alive who knows its smell." A beat. "Marguerite—we're being followed. We have been since the river. And whoever it is, they know exactly where we're going."

Above them, on the ridgeline, something watched them climb—and did not bother to hide.
