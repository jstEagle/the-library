## 7. The Hollow King

I had not smelled a human in three hundred years who carried stone inside his blood.

The mountain knew him before we saw him. The whole massif shuddered as we climbed the last switchbacks toward the forge, and beneath my feet I felt the old anchor-veins thrumming wrong—out of rhythm, like a heart trying to beat around a swallowed thing. Marguerite walked ahead of me, her hound-shape at her heel, her breath steaming in the thin cold. Isolde clutched her father's maps against her chest like a shield. None of them could feel what I felt. None of them needed to.

*He is here,* I said, in the speech that only beasts and my beast-speaker could hear. *He has always been here.*

"Here," Marguerite repeated aloud for Isolde's benefit, "means where?"

"The forge." My voice came out as smoke through my false throat. Even wearing this diminished shape—a great grey hound, scarred and limping—I could taste the forge on the wind. Iron. Ember. And underneath it, something rotten-sweet, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. "The place where your ancestors made the covenant. And the place where one of them unmade it."

Isolde stopped walking. She had been quiet since the river, quieter even than usual, and when she spoke now her voice was flat as a frozen lake. "My father's maps show a chamber beneath the summit. He called it the Anchorhold. He never drew what was inside it."

"He couldn't have," I said. "No map survives what lives there."

We climbed.

* * *

The forge had not gone cold. That was the first horror of it.

Three centuries since the covenant broke, and the great hearth at the mountain's heart still burned—but wrongly, thinly, a fire fed on nothing, burning nothing, a flame like a memory of flame. It lit a cavern vast enough to hold a cathedral and a dragon both, its light pooling in the hollows of walls carved with the old oaths. I read them as we descended: *WE GIVE WHAT WE ARE. WE KEEP WHAT WE LOVE.* Words in every tongue—human script, wolf-runes, the river-folk's flowing glyphs, and along the highest arch, scorched deep into living rock, the dragon-script that my own grandsire had set there with his dying breath.

And seated before the hearth, on a throne of slagged iron, sat the Hollow King.

He had been magnificent once. You could see the architecture of it still—the broad shoulders, the jaw of a man accustomed to being obeyed, robes that might have been royal before they became rags. But power does strange things to the body that hoards it. The anchor-stone he had consumed showed through him now, glowing dully beneath the skin of his chest and throat and temples like coals under ash, and everywhere the glow touched, his flesh had gone grey and slightly translucent, as though he were becoming the stone he'd stolen—or the stone was becoming bored of wearing him.

He looked up at us with eyes like banked fires, and he smiled.

"A dragon," he said. His voice filled the cavern without effort; the stolen stone did that, spending itself profligately on small miracles because it no longer knew how to do anything else. "I thought you were all dead. I *counted* on you all being dead."

"You missed one," I said, and let the hound-shape fall away.

Transformation is not painless when you are dying. It took everything I had left to unfold into my true form, wings cracking wide until they brushed the oath-carved walls, fire gathering in my throat out of habit rather than hope. I heard Isolde cry out. I heard Marguerite say nothing at all, which was worse—she had known, of course, she had always known, but knowing and seeing are different countries.

The Hollow King did not flinch. He rose from his throne, and up close I could see how terrible he truly was: a man stretched over an emptiness, a skin draped across a hunger. When he moved, the light beneath his skin guttered like a lamp low on oil.

"Silas," he said, tasting the name. "They sang about you, in the early years. The last ember-dragon, hiding in the ash-fields. I sent hunters, you know. Forty years of hunters. They all came back empty, or didn't come back."

"They came back," I said, "because I let them."

Something flickered across his ruined face—not anger. Recognition. The look of one starving creature meeting another.

"And you brought me gifts." His gaze slid past me to Marguerite, and I felt her stiffen. "A beast-speaker. Do you know how long it has been since I heard someone speak to the wild things? The wolves won't come near me anymore. Neither will anything else. I am," and here his voice cracked, just slightly, just humanly, "very lonely, at the top of the world."

"We know what you are," Isolde said, stepping forward with her father's maps held out like a warrant. "We know what you took. The anchor-stone. Your own founders' covenant—you broke it, you consumed it, you've been squatting on its corpse for three hundred years while the wilds starved and the wards rotted and the stars went out one by one—"

"I *saved* it!" 

The roar shook cinders from the ceiling. For a moment the man vanished entirely and only the hunger remained, vast and ancient and terrified, and then he mastered himself, folding back down into his ruined dignity.

"I saved it," he said again, softly. "You think the founders broke the covenant out of greed? Some did. Not Aldric Vane. Not me. We saw what was coming—the realms drifting apart, the magic thinning year by year, the whole beautiful pact dying of neglect because no one would pay its price. No one would *belong*. So yes, I took the anchor-stone. I took it into myself so it wouldn't scatter. I have kept the last of the old magic alive in my own body for three centuries." He spread his translucent hands. "Tell me, cartographer's daughter—what has your family done lately?"

* * *

It was Marguerite he wanted. I understood that within minutes, and understanding it made me want to burn him where he sat.

He circled her the way I had seen collectors circle rare birds. He did not threaten her—that was the skill of him, the horror of him. He *conversed*. He asked about Vell's night market, about luck-knots and mouse-whispers, about what it cost to hide a gift your whole life. And Marguerite, to my despair, answered him, because she was honest the way a blade is honest, and because some part of her recognized the loneliness he wore like his rags.

"I spent thirty years pretending to be nothing," she said. "So don't lecture me about hiding."

"No," he agreed. "You hid. I ruled. And look at us—both of us alone at the end of it." He gestured at the guttering hearth, at the dimming coal-light in his own flesh. "The stone is dying in me. I know it. I have known it for a century. It needs a new anchor, and not a thief this time—it needs a keeper. Someone the wilds will actually accept." His eyes, those banked fires, fixed on hers. "Someone who speaks beast. Someone the wolves have already accepted. Someone, perhaps, who has already given truths at oath-stones and paid grief as toll."

"You've been watching us," I growled.

"I've been *waiting* for you. There's a difference." He turned back to Marguerite, and his voice dropped to something almost tender, which was the most frightening register it had yet reached. "Here is my offer, Marguerite Montrose. Not sacrifice. *Partnership.* Bind the stone to yourself—and bind it to me. Two anchors instead of one. We split the burden, and then—" his smile widened, showing teeth that had gone faintly transparent at the edges, "—we split the worlds. You take the wilds. Every beast, every river, every growing green thing, answering to your voice alone. I keep the cities, the forges, the works of hand and mind. No more hiding. No more inspectors. No more watching your mother be dragged away while you stay silent in the crowd."

Marguerite went very still.

I knew that stillness. I had worn it myself, in the ash-fields, in the long years of dying. It was the stillness of a wound being spoken to directly.

"You know about my mother," she said quietly.

"I know everything the stone remembers. And the stone remembers Vell." He leaned closer, and the coal-glow in his throat pulsed. "Think of it. The wilds would love you—not fear you, not need you, *love* you. And you would never have to give up anything at all. Not your life. Not your self. Not your name. You would simply become more than you are, and isn't that what you've wanted since you were a child whispering to mice in the walls? Power doesn't ask you to disappear, girl. Only belonging does. I learned that lesson three hundred years ago, and I chose power, and I have regretted it every single day since—" he laughed, a dry rustle, "—no. That's a lie. I tell truths at stones too, when it suits me. I haven't regretted it for one moment. That's the trap of it. You won't regret it either. That's what I'm offering you: a choice you'll never have to mourn."

The silence stretched.

And I watched my beast-speaker stand between the dying fire and the dying king, and I watched her consider it—truly consider it, because she was too honest to pretend otherwise—and I found that I could not hate her for it. Three hundred years ago, I might have taken his offer myself. Fire wants to be *for* something. Hunger understands hunger.

But I had watched her refuse the river's vision of a safe ordinary life. I had watched her pay the wolves a truth that cost her more than blood. I knew what she would say before she said it, and I braced myself anyway, because I also knew what the king would do when she said it.

"You're right about one thing," Marguerite said. "Belonging asks you to disappear. I've thought about that every night since the river." She stepped closer to him, close enough that the coal-light of the stolen stone lit her face from below. "But you're wrong about what disappears. When you belong to something, you don't get smaller. You get *held*. And you—" her voice gentled into something that was almost pity, "—you haven't been held in three hundred years, and you're so hungry you've mistaken the ache for ambition."

The Hollow King's face did something terrible and small.

"So no," she said. "I won't share the stone with you. But I'll tell you something for free, king of nothing: there's a place for you in what we're building. If you want it. If you can stop eating the world long enough to notice it's offering you dinner."

For a long moment, the only sound was the hearth-fire guttering.

Then the Hollow King began, very quietly, to laugh—and the laugh curdled halfway through into something else, and the coal-light beneath his skin flared from ember-orange to a hungry white, and the temperature of the cavern dropped as though winter itself had entered to watch.

"Pity," he said. "From a smuggler of mouse-whispers. From a *cartographer*. From a dragon on his last ember." He straightened to his full height, and the stone blazed through him, and every shadow in the cavern bent toward him like iron filings toward a lodestone. "Then we do this the old way. The way of the founders who broke the world rather than share it."

He snapped his fingers, and the forge-doors behind us boomed shut.

"Hunters," he called pleasantly, to the dark. "They're here."

And out of the black tunnels ringing the cavern came the sound I had dreaded for forty years and hoped never to hear again: the soft, synchronized tread of boots, and the rasp of steel, and above it all, faint and sweet and utterly wrong, a whistled tune that every child in Vell knew—the ward-inspectors' marching song.

Marguerite's hand found my foreleg in the dark.

*Silas,* she said, in the beast-speech, calm as deep water. *How much fire do you have left?*

*Enough,* I lied, and drew breath to prove it.
