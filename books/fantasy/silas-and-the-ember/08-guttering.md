## 8. Guttering

The mountain road narrowed to a shelf of black stone, and Marguerite felt the ambush before she saw it—a wrongness in the air, like the held breath of a hundred beasts.

"Silas." She kept her voice low, though the hound trotting at her heel needed no warning. His ears had already flattened. "They're above us."

*I know.* His voice in her skull was a banked coal, tired and warm. *The King's hounds. Six, maybe seven. They've been tracking us since the river.*

Isolde stopped ahead, her father's maps clutched to her chest like a shield. The wind off the peaks pulled at her braid, and her face had gone the color of old parchment. "The forge is only half a day further," she said. "We can't stop now."

"We're not stopping," Marguerite said. "We're—"

The first arrow took Silas high in the shoulder.

He didn't cry out. What he did was worse: he *unfolded*. The disguise of scarred hound split like shed skin, and then there was no hound at all but a dragon, gaunt and gray as winter ash, his wings spreading across the whole width of the pass—not to fight, but to *cover* them, a dying roof against the rain of arrows.

"RUN," he said aloud, and his voice cracked stone.

Marguerite grabbed Isolde's wrist and ran. Behind them came sounds she would carry to her grave: the wet thud of shafts finding flesh, the roar that wasn't rage but pain, the smell of sulfur and burning pitch as Silas set the cliffside alight with fire he could not spare. The hunters screamed. Something large crashed down among them.

She did not look back. If she looked back, she would stop, and if she stopped, everything they had crossed the wolves' forest and the river's treachery for would die here on this ledge with an old dragon who had waited three hundred years for nothing.

*Marguerite.* Silas's voice reached her even through the thunder of their footsteps, fainter than it had ever been. *The hollow. On your left. Take Isolde and go to ground.*

"There's a cave—" she gasped to Isolde, and they scrambled into it as the sky above the pass turned orange.

---

The hollow was shallow, barely deep enough for the two of them, its mouth curtained by frost-killed brush. From inside, Marguerite watched the battle through a lattice of dead branches, her hands pressed over her mouth to keep her own voice locked behind her teeth.

Silas fought the way a candle fights the dark: brilliantly, briefly, at the cost of himself. Fire poured from him in sheets, and each sheet left him grayer, smaller, his scales dulling from ember-orange to the color of cold iron. She could feel him through the bond they'd forged on the road—their bargain, his fire for her voice—could feel his strength guttering like a lamp out of oil.

There were more hunters than seven. There were dozens, pouring up the switchbacks in dark armor that drank the light, and at their center rode a figure on a pale horse: the Hollow King's huntsmaster, wrapped in a cloak stitched from what looked like shadows given weight.

"No," Marguerite whispered. "No, no, *no*."

The huntsmaster raised one hand, and the arrows stopped. The hunters parted. And the huntsman walked—walked, unhurried, through Silas's fire as though it were morning mist—and pressed something small and black against the dragon's throat.

Silas collapsed.

The sound he made was not a roar. It was almost gentle, a long exhale, like a hearth going out at the end of a hard winter. His wings folded. His head came down onto the frozen stone, and the light in his eyes—the ember-light Marguerite had followed out of a cave and across half the broken world—dimmed to a single red point, and then to nothing she could see at all.

"They've taken him," Isolde breathed beside her. Her hands were shaking so badly the maps rustled. "Marguerite, they've—"

The huntsmaster's head turned.

It should not have been possible, at this distance, in this light. But the hood swung toward their hiding place with the certainty of a compass needle, and beneath it Marguerite glimpsed not a face but a smooth gray blankness, featureless as an unmarked page.

"The cartographer's daughter," the huntsmaster said. Its voice was wind through a keyhole. "And the little beast-speaker. My lord wants the stones."

Marguerite moved without deciding to move. She shoved Isolde deeper into the hollow, snatched the satchel holding their two oath-stones—the wolf-stone warm as a sleeping animal, the river-stone cool and slick as a wet pebble—and slung it across her body.

"You run east," she said. "Down toward the tree line. Don't stop, don't look back."

"What are you *doing*?" Isolde grabbed her arm. "You can't fight them, you can't—"

"I'm not fighting anyone." Marguerite pried the fingers loose, one by one, gently, the way she'd once gentled a sparrow out of a market stall. "I'm giving them something else to chase."

"But the stones—"

"Stay with me, Isolde." She tried to smile and felt it come out crooked. "You're the mapmaker now. Get yourself to the forge. I'll find you."

"That's a lie," Isolde said, and her eyes were bright and furious and terrified. "That's a lie and we both know it."

Marguerite kissed her forehead, quick and fierce, the way her mother used to when the inspectors' boots echoed in the stairwell. Then she stepped out of the hollow into the open, held the satchel high, and shouted in every voice she owned:

"Wolf! River! *Man!* Come and take them, if you can!"

* * *

They chased her. Of course they chased her. She led them west along the ledge, away from Isolde's path, away from the forge, leaping scree and sliding down shale with her lungs full of knives, the satchel banging against her ribs like a second heart. Arrows hissed past her ears. Once a hunter's gauntlet brushed her hair, close enough that she smelled cold iron and old blood.

She ran until the ledge ended.

It ended at a gorge, a hundred feet of screaming drop into a river that had once been a friend of hers—or at least a neighbor, back when the world made sense. Beyond it, the far wall rose sheer and smooth. No way across. No way around.

Marguerite skidded to a halt at the lip of the cliff, stones rattling away into the dark below, and turned to face the hunters fanning out across the ledge behind her. The huntsmaster sat its pale horse at their center, patient as winter.

"Give me the stones," it said.

"And if I don't?"

"Then my lord takes them from your corpse, and the dragon dies tonight, and the cartographer's daughter is found by morning. He has ways of knowing where things are."

The words landed like stones in still water. *Found by morning.* Isolde hadn't run east after all—of course she hadn't; Isolde was brave and stubborn and loved her too much to leave—and so Isolde was somewhere on this mountain, hiding, waiting, believing Marguerite would come back for her.

And Silas—

Marguerite closed her eyes. Through the bond, faint as a pulse under snow, she felt him: alive, barely, caged somewhere in iron that ate fire, his last flame guttering in a darkness he couldn't burn his way out of. Three centuries he had endured. Three centuries of slow dying, and he had spent his final reserves shielding two people he'd known less than a season, because that was what covenant-keepers did. That was what belonging *meant*.

*Choose,* said the cliff edge. *Choose.*

If she went back for Isolde, the forge went cold. The last oath-stone stayed lost, the covenant stayed broken, the stars stayed dark, and every soul in Vell and the wilds beyond starved slowly together in the black. Two stones in her satchel, and no third, and no dragon-fire to wake them.

If she went on to the forge alone—if she climbed, somehow, this gorge, this mountain, with her hands and her stubbornness and nothing else—then Isolde died in a cell, or worse, and Silas died in iron, and Marguerite carried their names like stones in her mouth for whatever remained of her life.

The wind gusted. Below her, the river roared, indifferent.

"You're thinking," the huntsmaster observed. "Good. Think on this: my lord does not want you dead, beast-speaker. He wants you *willing*. He will give you the girl and the dragon both, whole and breathing, if you bring him the stones and kneel. A partnership. You've seen what the world is without the covenant. Help him rule what remains of it."

*Share the worlds,* the Hollow King had offered, through every trap and whisper since the river. *Split them between us. No more starving. No more choosing.*

And oh, it would be easy. It would be so easy to hand over the satchel, to let someone else bear the weight of deciding who lived and who burned. She was tired down to the marrow. She had been tired since the night the stars went out, since before that—since her mother was taken, since the first lie she told about the voices in her head.

But she thought of Silas in his cave, three hundred years faithful to a promise everyone else had abandoned. She thought of the wolf-pack's toll, the truth she'd paid: *I let her be taken.* She had chosen safety over her mother once. She knew exactly what that choice cost, and she knew, with a certainty that felt like a bone breaking, that she would not survive making it twice.

"No," Marguerite said.

The word came out small. She said it again, louder, in the voices of wolf and river and dragon and the frightened girl from Vell's night market all at once:

"*No.*"

And then she stepped backward off the cliff.

* * *

The river caught her the way it catches everything eventually: hard, cold, and without apology. The current took her under, spun her, slammed her against stone, and for a long bright moment she thought—almost hoped—it would simply finish things. But her hands, traitorous, stubborn things, clawed for the surface, and her lungs dragged in air, and the satchel's strap held true across her chest, the two stones knocking against her sternum like a heartbeat.

She washed up on a gravel bar a mile downstream, coughing, bleeding, alive. The hunters would not have seen where she'd gone under. The gorge's shadow swallowed everything.

Marguerite lay on her back and stared up at the starless sky and laughed until the laugh turned into something else entirely, ragged and gasping, grief with nowhere left to go.

Two stones. One lost. A friend in a cage. A dragon guttering out. A forge half a day's climb above her, and no way up the sheer far wall of the gorge except the way the river itself went—through the flooded caverns the old maps called the Weeping Stair, passages Isolde had shown her, marked in her father's careful hand with a single annotation: *the water remembers the way.*

Marguerite rolled onto her side and pressed her palm flat against the gravel, against the cold shoulder of the mountain, and spoke in the river's own tongue, the eel-folk's liquid syllables, the ones they'd taught her as payment for sparing their stone.

"Little sisters," she whispered. "You tested us at the crossing. You know my heart now—what I refused, and what it cost me. So I'm asking. Not commanding. Asking."

The water lapped at the gravel bar. Once. Twice.

Then, downstream, where the gorge wall plunged into blackness, a seam of pale light opened in the rock—an underwater arch, glowing faintly green, wide enough for one exhausted woman with a satchel full of promises.

Marguerite got to her feet. Every bruise announced itself. She ignored all of them.

"Hold on, Isolde," she said to the dark, to the mountain, to the dying ember flickering somewhere in its iron throat. "Hold on, both of you. I'm coming—and I'm bringing the fire back with me."

She waded into the glowing water, and the mountain swallowed her whole.
