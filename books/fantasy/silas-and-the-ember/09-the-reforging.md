## 9. The Reforging

The mountain forge was not a building. Marguerite had imagined chimneys and anvils, iron and smoke, but what they found at the summit was a wound in the world—a caldera of black glass where the rock had been hollowed out three centuries ago, and at its center, floating above a pit of dead coals, the anchor-stone's empty setting: a cradle of silver the size of a wagon, its arms open and grasping at nothing.

The cold up there was absolute. Not wind-cold. *Absence*-cold, the cold of a hearth swept clean, of a house where no one had lit a fire in a hundred years.

Silas could barely walk. He had given everything he had left to shield them from the Hollow King's hunters on the ridge below—everything except the last ember, the one curled behind his heart like a seed in winter soil—and now he moved as a dragon no longer but as something between shapes, scales graying to ash, wings folded tight against a body that had forgotten how to hold them. Isolde was gone. Taken. Two oath-stones rode in the satchel against Marguerite's ribs, warm as sleeping birds, and the third was somewhere in the Hollow King's fist, and every hour she spent climbing this mountain was an hour her friend spent in a cage.

*Go,* Silas had said, when she'd stood at the fork in the pass with tears freezing on her lashes. *Isolde would flay you herself if you let the fire die for her sake. She mapped this road knowing what it cost.*

So Marguerite had gone, hating every step, and now she stood at the caldera's rim with an ash-gray dragon dying beside her and a satchel full of stones that weighed nothing and everything.

"The cradle," Silas rasped. His voice was smoke thinning into nothing. "Set them in it. Then speak."

"Speak what? I don't know the words."

"There are no words." His eye, dim as a banked coal, found hers. "There never were. That is what Vell never understood. The covenant was never ink or incantation. It was *voices*. Every realm, speaking together, promising to belong to one another. Your ancestors fell silent. You will not."

Marguerite climbed down into the caldera, boots skidding on glass-smooth obsidian, and lifted the stones from the satchel one by one. The wolf-stone hummed against her palm, smelling of pine and blood and long patience. The river-stone was cool and slick, whispering in currents. She set them in the silver cradle, and they settled like eggs in a nest, and nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. Stones were only stones. The wolves had told her that at the first oath-stone, when they took her confession as toll: *the stone remembers, but it cannot speak. That has always been your kind's burden, and your gift.*

She looked at Silas. He had dragged himself to the caldera's edge and lay there, half in shadow, his breathing so slow it frightened her more than any hunter had.

"I can't do this alone," she said.

"You were never alone," he said. "You only believed you were. There is a difference, little speaker, and it is the whole of the covenant."

Marguerite closed her eyes.

And she reached—not with her hands, but with the thing inside her she had buried since childhood, the thing she had hidden behind luck-knots and mouse-whispers while ward-inspectors walked the night market, the thing that had cost her her mother and her name and her home. She opened it like a door. Like a gate. Like the wards themselves should have been.

*Hear me,* she said, in the voice of a woman. And then, because one voice was not enough, never enough, she let all the others rise through her:

In the voice of the wolf-pack, deep and rolling: *We who kept the northern stone, we who remembered when your kind forgot—we witness.*

In the voice of the river-folk, liquid and layered, many mouths speaking as one: *We who tested your heart and found it unwilling to take the easy gift—we witness.*

In the voice of the small things, the mice and sparrows and stray cats of Vell who had carried her messages through years of hiding: *We who saw you when no one else did—we witness.*

The caldera began to warm. Faintly, like a memory of warmth, like the ghost of a fire—but warming.

*Silas.* She turned to him. "Now. Give it to the stone. Whatever you have left—"

"No."

The word cracked across the caldera like ice breaking.

Silas hauled himself upright, and even dying, even gray as grave-ash, there was something in his bearing that made the mountain itself seem to lean toward him. Three hundred years old. The last of his kind. Keeper of a flame older than Vell, older than the wards, older than the betrayal that had starved him by inches for three centuries.

"The stone is a vessel," he said. "Vessels break. Your founders proved that. I will not pour the last ember of my line into another thing that can be stolen, hoarded, swallowed by some hungry king." His gaze fixed on her, and in it she saw reflected something she had not seen there before—or perhaps had always refused to see. Awe. "It was never meant to rest in stone. The first anchor was a living creature. Someone who chose. Someone who *belonged* to both realms and belonged to neither alone."

Understanding arrived like a blade between the ribs.

"No," Marguerite whispered. "Silas, you're dying. If you give it to me—"

"I am dying *anyway*, little speaker. I have been dying since before your grandmother drew breath. The only question is whether my last flame warms a grave or lights a hearth." He dragged himself forward, down the slope of black glass, until his great ruined head rested level with her. This close, she could see the ember behind his eyes—the true one, the seed of every fire that had ever burned in the world, guttering like a candle in a draft. "I offer it freely. But free gifts require free hands. I cannot choose for you. No one can. That is the entire covenant, Marguerite Montrose. That is all it ever was."

Her whole life, she had been asked to give things up. Her mother, taken by inspectors while Marguerite stayed silent in the cupboard, small and terrified and complicit. Her name, traded for anonymity in the night market. Her voice, buried under trinkets and lies. Every loss had been taken from her, or forced by fear, and she had carried them like stones in a satchel against her ribs.

This was different. This was the first thing anyone had ever asked her to give *willingly*.

And it did not feel like losing.

It felt like the moment she'd stepped through the broken gate into the wilds and heard the land itself speaking—terror giving way, all at once, to terrible freedom.

"Yes," she said. And then louder, in every voice she carried: "*Yes.*"

Silas exhaled.

Not smoke. Not ash. Flame—the last flame, the first flame, pouring out of him in a ribbon of gold and rose and white, streaming across the caldera like a river returning to its source. It did not burn her. It poured *into* her, into the hollow places grief had carved, filling them the way spring fills a dry riverbed, and Marguerite felt the covenant take root in her chest like a second heartbeat, wild and ancient and *hers*.

Silas folded down onto the black glass, smaller now, smaller, his outline softening, his scales going dark—but his eye, as it closed, was bright. Warm. At peace.

"Sleep well," Marguerite said, kneeling beside him, her voice human again and shaking. "I'll wake you when the stars come back."

* * *

She felt the Hollow King before she saw him.

A wrongness at the caldera's rim, a hunger-shaped absence, and then he was simply there—descending the slope with the third oath-stone clenched against his breastbone like a man clutching a wound. Up close he was worse than legend: a lord stretched thin over his own appetite, skin gone translucent with borrowed power, veins flickering with stolen light. He had consumed the anchor-stone three centuries ago and had been starving ever since, and now he looked at Marguerite—at the fire standing in her chest, at the living covenant glowing under her skin—with an expression she recognized from the night market, from the inspectors' faces, from every hungry thing that had ever wanted what she had.

"Split it," he said. His voice was beautiful and rotten, silk over rot. "You cannot hold all of that alone, girl. Share the flame. Split the worlds—I'll take the wilds, you keep your city, and neither of us need ever be lonely again."

And here was the terrible thing: she understood him. Here was a man who had wanted belonging so badly he had tried to *swallow* it, who had mistaken possession for partnership, power for place. He had been the loneliest creature in either realm for three hundred years, and loneliness had made him a king of nothing.

Marguerite could have fought him. The fire in her chest rose eagerly, offering itself. She could have burned him where he stood.

Instead she opened the door inside her—all the way, wider than she ever had—and spoke to him in every voice at once.

Wolf: *We see you, starving one.*

River: *We know what you gave up, and how long you have regretted it.*

Small voices, mouse and sparrow and cat: *We know what it is to hide. To go unseen. To eat scraps of connection and call it a meal.*

Dragon—the new voice, Silas's voice, living in her now: *Brother. You were meant to stand beside us, not above us. It is not too late to stand.*

The Hollow King staggered as if struck.

"That's a trick," he breathed. "That's—you don't want me. *No one* wants me. They want what I have."

"We're not asking for what you have," Marguerite said gently. "We're asking for *you*. Put the stone down. Come to the fire. Be witnessed."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind moving over dead coals, and the Hollow King stood at the edge of the light with three centuries of hunger written across his face, wanting, wanting, wanting—and unable, for the first time, to believe that wanting was all he deserved.

His hand opened.

The third oath-stone fell, ringing on the black glass, and rolled to rest against the silver cradle. And the Hollow King—the lord who had consumed a covenant to rule two worlds—sat down heavily on the mountainside like an old man at the end of a long road, and wept, and did not resist when the fire in Marguerite's chest reached out and gathered him in.

He did not die. He *unmade*—the borrowed light leaving him in slow ribbons, the stretched and starving shape collapsing inward, until what remained kneeling in the ash was simply a man. Old, and tired, and ordinary, and for the first time in three hundred years, warm.

*Witnessed,* said every voice through Marguerite, including her own. *Welcome home.*

* * *

She set the third stone in the cradle with the other two, and laid her hands upon them, and let the covenant finish what it had begun.

Light went up out of the caldera like a reversed meteor—a pillar of gold visible, she would learn later, from the walls of Vell itself, from the drowned river-country, from the den of every wolf and the bed of every eel. The dead wards did not rise as walls. They rose as doors, thresholds of soft light appearing at every gate and border and boundary between the realms, open and welcoming, guarded by nothing but the promise that had reforged them.

And above it all, in the hole the darkness had torn in the sky, the stars came back.

One by one, then dozens, then thousands—reigniting like embers breathed upon, until the whole heavens blazed, and Marguerite stood in the caldera laughing and crying at once, a woman with a hearth burning in her chest and a dragon sleeping at her feet and two worlds suddenly, impossibly, *hers to walk between*.

Far below, at the mountain's foot, torches were gathering. Riders from Vell, ward-inspectors among them, come to find the source of the light—and coming, too, she realized, was a familiar figure in a cartographer's coat, escaping her captors in the chaos, climbing toward the glow with a rolled map clutched in one hand.

Marguerite wiped her face, shouldered the empty satchel, and started down the slope to meet them.

Time, she thought, to introduce the city to its first licensed beast-speaker.
