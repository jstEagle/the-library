## 10. Stars, Rekindled

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

Marguerite would remember that afterward—the absurd smallness of it. Not on some holy anniversary, not at midnight with trumpets, but on an ordinary gray evening while the city of Vell stood in its streets holding its breath. She had come down from the mountain with ash still in her hair and Silas's last warmth banked somewhere behind her ribs like a coal in a lantern, and she had expected—she didn't know what she'd expected. Fire from the sky. A voice out of the clouds.

Instead the darkness simply *thinned*, the way fog thins before a wind no one can see. One star appeared above the eastern gate, tentative as a struck match. Then three. Then a hundred, then all of them at once, pouring back into the sky like water returning to a dry riverbed, and the whole of Vell made a sound she had never heard a city make—not cheering, not weeping, but something between: a long collective exhale, three hundred years held and finally released.

She stood in the crowd near the gate with her hood down for the first time in her life, and nobody looked at her twice. They were all looking up.

*They don't know,* said a voice at her ankle—a lean street cat with one torn ear, one of the dozens who had carried her messages through the dark months. *Wait until they find out what you did.*

*Let them wait,* Marguerite answered, and felt the words move through her throat easily, openly, without the old flinch. Speaking aloud in the open, in daylight, in Vell—it felt like walking on a leg that had been broken for years and had only just remembered how.

The wards rose that same night. She felt them go up the way you feel a change in weather: a pressure behind the eyes, a hum in the teeth. But they were wrong—or rather, they were right in a way the old wards had never been. She walked to the border wall at dawn to see for herself, and found not a wall anymore.

Where the great barrier had stood—black stone, starless, humming with exclusion—there was now a line of standing stones, each one taller than a man, each carved with the oath-marks Isolde had copied from her father's forbidden maps. And between them, where the old warding had been solid, there were *doors*. Open archways of faint golden light, wide enough for a wolf, a cart, a child running through with an armful of wildflowers. Through them Marguerite could see the wilds: green and hungry and alive, the forest leaning toward the city like a dog leaning toward an open hand.

Not walls. Doors.

*It will take them time,* Silas had told her, in the last hour, when his voice had gone thin as smoke. *Three hundred years of fear doesn't burn off in a night. Some will slam those doors shut. Some will never walk through. That's theirs to choose. The covenant asks nothing, little speaker. It only offers.*

She touched the nearest stone. It was warm under her palm—the way his scales had been warm—and somewhere deep beneath the mountain, she felt something stir in answer. Not waking. Dreaming. But dreaming *warm*.

* * *

Isolde Cartwright spread the new map across her father's old drafting table and, after a long moment's argument with herself, weighted its corners with his brass instruments instead of stones.

Her father's maps had always shown two worlds: Vell inside its border, drawn in fine confident ink, and beyond it a blank space labeled in his careful hand, *UNCHARTED — WARD-BOUND*. He had mapped the covenant sites in secret, in code, in shame, and he had died with that shame folded into his breast pocket like a letter he could never send.

Isolde drew differently now.

On the great sheet before her—one page, one world—Vell sat at the center-south like a hearthstone, and around it the wilds unfolded in full detail: the wolf-packs' territory marked with their own names, given by the wolves themselves; the river-folk's waterways rendered in shifting blue-green ink that seemed almost to swim; the mountain with its forge, and beneath the mountain, in letters of gold leaf, a single word: *SILAS*, sleeping. Where the border had been she drew the door-stones, and beside each one she wrote not *WARD* but *WELCOME*, and meant it.

There were things she could not draw. She tried, for weeks, to render the place where the Hollow King had crumbled—that courtyard of ash and broken hunger where a man who had eaten a god's anchor had finally been offered belonging and discovered he could not hold it. In the end she left the space blank except for a small notation in the margin: *Here stood a king who mistook power for a seat at the table.*

Her father's guilt she kept too, but differently now—not as a weight but as a tool, the way you keep a whetstone. Every morning she sharpened her conscience on it. *He knew,* she would tell herself, inking another door. *He knew and he hid it. I know, and I am telling everyone.*

The Guild of Cartographers had expelled her by autumn. She framed the expulsion notice and hung it above the drafting table, next to her father's portrait, and both of them seemed, in certain lights, to be smiling.

* * *

Winter came, and with it the first market day of the new order.

Marguerite set up her stall in Vell's night market in the same spot she'd occupied for eleven years—third row from the fountain, under the crooked awning—but everything else had changed. Her luck-knots hung in bright rows, yes, and her mouse-whisper charms sold as briskly as ever. But beside them, displayed openly on a board stamped with the seal of the newly founded Office of Concordance, hung a sign:

**MARGUERITE MONTROSE — LICENSED BEAST-SPEAKER**
*Disputes settled. Lost animals found. Oaths witnessed. All tongues considered.*

The license had taken three months of arguing, petitioning, and one memorable afternoon in which she'd brought a very large and extremely opinionated mule into the magistrate's chamber to serve as character witness. The mule had won the case.

Now the queue at her stall included farmers with haunted henhouses, merchants whose carthorses refused bridges, and—more often than anyone had predicted—ordinary people who simply wanted to know what their dogs were trying to tell them. She charged what they could pay, which was sometimes coin and sometimes bread and once, memorably, a violin. She gave the violin to a street musician who played outside her stall on cold nights, and if the music drew more cats than customers, well. Cats paid in gossip, and gossip in Vell was worth more than gold.

A girl of perhaps nine pushed through the crowd one evening, clutching a wren in cupped hands so gently it broke Marguerite's heart a little.

"It sings at night," the girl whispered. "Mama says it's nothing. But it sounds *sad*."

Marguerite listened. The wren's song was indeed sad—an old grief, a territory lost, a mate gone to a hawk two winters past. She translated it as best she could, and the girl's eyes went wide and wet and fierce all at once, and she carried the wren home promising to sing back to it every night so it wouldn't be lonely.

After they'd gone, Marguerite stood a moment in the lamplight, hands quiet on the counter, and let herself feel it: the thing she had buried for thirty years, dug up and planted in the open, growing. Somewhere across the market a cat called her name in the tone cats used for *you are late for dinner*, and she laughed out loud, in public, at a talking cat, and not one person thought her mad.

That was the miracle. Not the stars. This.

* * *

Beneath the mountain, Silas dreamed.

He dreamed the way mountains dream: slowly, largely, in centuries. He dreamed of the first covenant, when the worlds had been young and the borders had been drawn by mutual consent rather than mutual fear. He dreamed of ember-dragons in their hundreds filling the sky like migrating sparks. He dreamed of a woman with ash in her hair standing at a forge, speaking every voice at once, offering him the one gift no one had offered in three hundred years—not worship, not fear, but *company*.

And because he was an ember-dragon, and because dreams are the oldest magic there is, his dreaming leaked upward through the stone. The mountain grew warm at its heart. Hot springs appeared where none had been, and the wolves denned near them in winter, and the river-folk swore the water tasted of woodsmoke and safety. Pilgrims came, at first in ones and twos, then in honest numbers—not to pray, exactly. To sit. There was a ledge above the hot springs where the stone was smooth and faintly golden, and travelers pressed their palms to it and reported, variously, the smell of cedar smoke, a feeling of being forgiven, and once, from a skeptical tax collector, the unmistakable sensation of a very large creature chuckling.

Marguerite visited twice a year, spring and autumn, riding out through the eastern door with a satchel of market pastries she absolutely intended to leave as offerings and absolutely ate half of herself. She would sit on the warm ledge and talk—about the market, about Isolde's maps, about the girl with the wren, about the slow hard work of teaching a frightened city to leave its doors open.

*I'm here,* she would say at the end, palm flat against the stone.

*I know,* the mountain would answer, in a voice like banked coals. *Go home, little speaker. You're wanted in both worlds.*

Both worlds. She turned the phrase over the whole ride back, watching the sun set behind Vell's towers on one side and the wilds rise dark and generous on the other, and understood at last that this was what the covenant had asked of her all along—not sacrifice, not even service, but *residence*. Citizenship in two countries at once. The loneliness of her buried gift had never been the price of the gift; it had been the price of hiding it.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the Reforging, the four of them met—though "four" required some generosity, since one was a dragon asleep under a mountain and attended only in the sense that his warmth was present in the room.

Isolde hosted it in her workshop, which by then smelled permanently of ink and woodsmoke. She unveiled the finished master map, ten feet of vellum with both realms on one page, and Marguerite saw that in the lower corner, small and unassuming, Isolde had drawn two figures: a woman with a satchel, and a hound-shaped shadow with far too many teeth, walking side by side along a road that ran unbroken from the city gates into the deep forest.

"You've made me look dignified," Marguerite said.

"I've made you look *accurate*. Dignity is your problem."

The wolf-speaker arrived at dusk—a gaunt grey-eyed woman named Ferren who had guarded the first oath-stone since before Marguerite was born, and who still spoke of truths spoken aloud as currency. She brought a gift: a tooth, old and yellowed, from the wolf who had taken Marguerite's confession three hundred miles south. *Debts of grief are honored forever among packs,* Ferren said. *You paid honestly. They want you to have this.*

And from the river came a messenger of the eel-folk, sleek and silver-eyed and wearing a coat entirely inappropriate for the weather, carrying a sealed reed tube. Inside was a single line, written in ink that shifted color as you watched: *The river remembers every betrayal and forgives most of them. Come swim anytime. — R.*

They ate. They drank. They argued about map projections until midnight. And at some point in the small hours, when the candles had burned low, Marguerite found herself at the window looking east toward the mountain, one hand resting over the place behind her ribs where a dying dragon's final flame lived on—not as burden, not as duty, but as warmth. As company.

The stars were thick overhead, every one of them burning, and below them the doors of the ward-stones glowed softly along the border, open to the night.

*Something's coming,* said a cat on the windowsill—the torn-eared one, older now, grayer at the muzzle. It was watching the eastern sky with its pupils blown wide. *Down out of the mountains, past the springs. Something with wings. Something that smells like—*

It stopped. Its ears went flat.

*—like fire that isn't Silas's.*

Marguerite went very still. Behind her, Isolde set down her pen. Out past the edge of the lamplight, far away and getting closer, something circled once above the mountain where a dragon slept—and the mountain's warmth flared, sudden and startled, like a sleeper stirring at a familiar footstep.

Silas had told her once, in the cave, in the worst hour of both their lives: *I am not the last ember-dragon. I am only the last one who stayed.*

Marguerite reached for her boots.
