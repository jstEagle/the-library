## 5. Auditors

They came on the third morning after the blizzard, when the snow had stopped falling and started simply *staying*, lying over the city like a shroud laid by someone in a hurry.

Mara knew they had arrived before any bell rang. The Ledgerhouse itself announced them—a tremor through the floorboards, the same shiver that ran through the building when a large trade was being weighed in the counting hall above. But this was no trade. This was the sound of the guild's immune system waking up.

He was in the ledger-cellar with Silas, teaching him the word for *spoon* by pressing a tarnished one into the boy's small palm. Silas repeated it—*ssoonn*, rolling it around his mouth like a river stone—and laughed, and Mara felt the laugh land somewhere beneath his ribs where he kept nothing, where he had spent twenty years keeping nothing.

Then the floor shivered, and the lamps guttered all at once, and Mara's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the frost creeping down from the north of the city.

"Silas." He kept his voice level, the way he kept everything level. "The alcove behind the debt-shelves. Now."

The boy looked at him—those pale eyes, the color of ice over deep water—and for a moment Mara saw that he understood more than his broken common tongue should have allowed. Children always understood more. Then Silas slipped into the dark between the shelves, small as a held breath, and Mara pulled the false panel closed and turned to face whatever came down the stairs.

What came down the stairs wore grey.

Three auditors, their coats the color of wet ash, their faces the color of nothing at all. Guild auditors did not wear expressions; expressions were entries, and entries could be read. The one in front carried a slate instead of a ledger, which meant he was not here to record trades. He was here to record *discrepancies*.

"Mara Quintana," the auditor said. Not a greeting. An inventory.

"Keeper of the night ledgers," Mara said, and bowed the precise degree owed to a man whose rank was ambiguous enough that bowing too little or too much were both crimes.

"We are conducting a census." The auditor's voice was dry as old vellum. "An unregistered presence has entered the Ledgerhouse. The gate registers all crossings. All crossings must be accounted for." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes—the closest an auditor came to smiling. "You keep the night ledgers, Quintana. You will help us find what does not exist."

Behind the debt-shelves, Mara heard Silas breathing. Small. Controlled. A child who had learned, somewhere in a country that no longer existed, how to be quiet in the presence of men in grey.

"The gate registers crossings," Mara said carefully. "I balance what the gate records. If there were an unregistered presence, there would be an unregistered entry, and I would have flagged it."

"And yet." The auditor lifted his slate. "The gate recorded a crossing three nights past. During the blizzard. One soul, inbound. And then—" he turned the slate as if showing a card trick, though there was nothing on it to see— "*nothing*. No registration. No declaration. No toll paid. The gate opened, Quintana. Something walked through. And your ledgers"—he tapped them with one grey finger—"show a blank line where a name should be."

Mara's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of treachery against him. Because it was true. He had checked, that first night, hands shaking, and found the entry: a single line in the gate-log, ink still wet, reading only *one (1), origin: —* and then nothing. As if Vessany's language had reached out of the boy's mouth and erased its own address.

"I flagged it," Mara lied. "I reported it to Senior Keeper Corane. She took the matter up herself."

The silence that followed lasted exactly long enough for Mara to understand what he had just done: he had gambled twenty years of invisibility on a single card, and he would not know until it turned whether Ivet Corane had given him that card or whether he had stolen it from her deck.

The auditor studied him. Somewhere above them, the Ledgerhouse creaked, settling its weight, keeping its own counsel.

"Corane," the auditor said at last, and wrote something on the slate. "Yes. Corane is handling it." He turned to go, then stopped, and his voice dropped into a register that was almost human, which was worse. "Quintana. A word of professional courtesy. There are parties within the guild who believe the blank line is not a corruption. They believe it is an *address*. A country that was struck from every record forty years ago does not stop existing because we stopped writing about it. It merely stops paying taxes." He smiled without moving his mouth. "Some of those parties would pay a great deal for a key that opens a market no one else can tax. Dead markets hold their prices forever. Do you understand?"

"No," Mara said, which was true, and also the safest possible answer.

"You will," the auditor said. "Everyone understands eventually. That is what the guild is *for*."

Their footsteps climbed the stairs and faded, and the cellar exhaled, and Mara stood very still in the lamplight, listening to his own heartbeat count out the seconds like a clerk tallying coins.

Then the panel creaked, and Silas emerged, blinking, clutching the tarnished spoon he had refused to put down.

"*Ssoonn*," the boy said, holding it up, as if the world had not nearly ended. As if the world were still a place where spoons were the important things.

Mara knelt. His knees cracked. Twenty years of kneeling at ledgers had not prepared them for kneeling at children. "Yes," he said. "Spoon. You keep it. It's yours."

Silas smiled, and said something in Vessany's tongue, soft and quick, and pressed the spoon against Mara's chest over his heart, solemn as a knight conferring a title. And Mara—who had spent two decades ensuring that nothing in the Ledgerhouse belonged to him, so that nothing in the Ledgerhouse could ever be taken from him—felt the terrible arithmetic begin: *this is a debt. Everything you keep becomes a debt.*

* * *

Ivet Corane sent for him at dusk.

Her office sat at the top of the west stair, the only room in the Ledgerhouse with a window, and she was the only keeper permitted to look out of it. She had been Mara's mentor once, twenty years ago, when he was new and raw and desperate to disappear; she had taught him the first rule—*be accurate, be punctual, be unremarkable*—and she had never once, in all the years since, asked him a personal question. That was her kindness, and he had loved her for it the way you love a wall that holds back a flood.

She poured tea without asking if he wanted any. Two cups. She had known he would come.

"You named me," she said, handing him his cup. "To an auditor. Do you know what that costs?"

"It bought time."

"It bought *me*," Ivet said. Her face was a ledger too—every line an entry, every entry balanced. "Auditors do not forget who appears in their notes. I am now in their notes, Quintana. Beside you. Beside a blank line." She sipped her tea. "So. Tell me why I should not have let them find the boy."

Mara set down his cup because his hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. "You already know about him."

"I know everything about him. I've known since the gate logged the crossing." She rose and went to the window, where the city lay under its wrong white silence. "Do you remember what I taught you? Be accurate, be punctual, be unremarkable. Do you remember what I didn't teach you?"

"That kindness is a liability."

"That kindness is a *transaction*," Ivet corrected. "Everything in this house is. Every mercy has a price column. I covered for you tonight—I told the auditors the blank line was a gate malfunction, that I was investigating personally, that no further census was required. They believed me, because my signature carries forty years of accuracy." She turned. "But now you owe me, Mara. And I collect."

The tea had gone cold in his hands. "What do you want?"

"Not what *I* want." For the first time, something flickered across her face—not quite regret, but its accountant. "There is a faction. Old families, old money, names that predate the guild's charter. Forty years ago they helped strike Vessany from the maps—not out of cruelty, but out of *arithmetic*. A country that trades in impossible things cannot be allowed to exist outside the ledger. So they closed the accounts. Erased the country. Kept the gate." She came back to the table and stood over him. "And now the gate has opened itself, once, for one child, and those families believe the winter coming down from the north is not a disaster. They believe it is an *invoice*. Vessany reaching for its assets. And they want the asset delivered, Mara. Alive, if convenient. The boy is a key. Keys open markets. Dead markets hold their prices forever—you heard the auditor say it. They'll have told him to tell you."

"They want to reopen the gate," Mara said slowly, "and strip a dying country for whatever's left to trade."

"I want you to give him to them," Ivet said, plainly, the way she had once taught him to post figures. "In exchange, you keep your position. Your life. Your invisibility—all twenty years of it, intact. They will make the boy comfortable. They are not monsters; they are *clerks*. They will feed him and clothe him and use him gently until the gate opens, and then—" she spread her hands, "—Vessany returns to the world, the winter ends, everyone profits. Even the boy. He goes home, Mara. Isn't that what you want for him anyway?"

And that was the knife of it, turned with such care: that she was right, or half right, or right enough that the lie could pass through the eye of the truth. Mara looked at his cold tea and thought of Silas pressing the spoon against his heart. *Yours. Keep it.* Thought of the boy's laughter rolling a word around like a river stone. Thought of a grandmother in a dead country sending her grandson through a gate to find the doorkeeper, believing the doorkeeper would be *good*.

"He isn't a key," Mara said. "He's a child."

"He's both. Most valuable things are." Ivet sat down across from him, and her voice softened by a single degree, which from Ivet was a flood of tenderness. "You think I don't understand? I helped erase Vessany, Mara. I was young and accurate and punctual, and I signed my name to the erasure because signing was safer than refusing. I have carried that entry for forty years. It does not balance. Nothing I do will make it balance." She met his eyes. "So take the bargain. Give them the boy. Let me spend the last of my credibility buying you a clean life. Consider it the only apology I'm capable of making."

For a long moment Mara said nothing. The window behind her showed the frost advancing down the far hills, patient as interest accruing.

"You're offering me my life," he said finally, "in exchange for the first thing that's ever made it worth having."

"I'm offering you survival," Ivet said. "It's the guild's oldest currency. Everyone accepts it eventually. That is what the guild is *for*."

Mara stood. His legs felt borrowed, his voice felt borrowed, but the words came out in his own hand at last, clear and unerasable:

"No."

Ivet closed her eyes, briefly, like a woman balancing a column that would not come even. When she opened them, all the mentor was gone, and only the keeper remained.

"Then listen carefully," she said quietly, "because I will only be able to warn you once. The faction meets tomorrow night. And Mara—they already know the boy is in the cellar. They've known since the blizzard. My covering for you tonight wasn't charity." She slid a folded paper across the table toward him, sealed with plain wax. "It was a countdown. You have until dawn before they come down those stairs themselves. Read that, decide fast, and trust no door in this house—including mine."

Mara took the paper. His fingers were steady now, entirely steady, the way a man's hands go steady when he has finally stepped off a ledge.

"What is it?" he asked.

Ivet looked out her window, at the winter coming down like a debt called due.

"It's the way into the old depths," she said. "Where the original gate stands. Where the guild's whole history is buried." She poured herself more tea with a hand that, for the first time in forty years, was not quite accurate. "And where, if my arithmetic serves, the winter is already breaking through."
