## 6. The Price of Keeping Him

Mara taught him the word for *bread* first, because it was the only thing in the cellar that Mara could offer without guilt.

"Br-ed," Silas repeated, frowning at the heel of the loaf as though the fault lay with it. His mouth kept trying to close around the vowel the way his own language did—all vowels closed, all words held like water behind a dam. "Brehd."

"Close enough. Bread." Mara tore off a piece and handed it over. "You say it when you want more. That's how this works. You ask, someone answers."

Silas considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he said, in careful, accented common: "Bread. More bread. Please."

The *please* was Mara's doing—he'd insisted on it from the first lesson, half out of habit, half out of some old fear that manners were armor, and an unarmored child in the Ledgerhouse would not survive the week. But hearing it now, in that small deliberate voice, something in Mara's chest shifted like a ledger page turning.

*Good,* he almost said. Instead he said, "Again tomorrow," and looked away, because praise was a currency too, and he had spent twenty years learning not to spend anything on himself.

The cellar was not so bad, all things considered. It sat below the counting floors, past the vaults where the guild kept its impossible inventory—a jar of last year's thunder, a key that fit no lock but opened every door that had ever been locked in anger, forty years of Vessany's trade records shelved and dusted by clerks who no longer knew what country they catalogued. The cellar itself held the overflow: ledgers too old to matter, too honest to burn. Rats had given up on them decades ago; even rats could tell when paper remembered too much.

Mara had made a nest of it. A cot. A brazier he fed carefully, keeping the smoke below the level where the flues might betray them. A candle stub for lessons, its light pooled between them on the floor like something they owned together.

"Bread," Silas said again, grinning now, gap-toothed, nine years old and pleased with himself in a way that made Mara's ribs ache. "More bread, please."

"You're going to be insufferable in two languages."

"I don't know *insufferable.*"

"No," Mara said. "Give it time."

* * *

That night—because the boy could not sleep until the songs were sung, and Mara could not refuse him, which was becoming a problem he refused to examine—Silas taught him the first one.

It was a counting song, the boy explained, hands moving to show it: one finger, two fingers, folding down as the numbers went. Shepherds sang it in the high passes of Vessany to count their flocks home before dark. The melody was simple and strange, climbing where common songs fell, and the words—

"The words are hard," Silas admitted. He said them slowly, syllable by syllable, and made Mara repeat each one until his tongue stopped tripping. *Avenn. Duvenn. Tressa. Quorren.* One. Two. Three. Four. The sounds came out of Mara's mouth feeling older than any word he knew, older than the Ledgerhouse, older than the guild's founding charter. They tasted faintly of snow.

"What does the last verse say?" Mara asked. "The one you always hum?"

Silas went quiet. His fingers kept counting, five, six, seven, but slower now, and then they folded closed altogether.

"It says the flock comes home even if the shepherd doesn't," he said. "My grandmother says—" He caught himself, corrected in the new grammar Mara had drilled into him. "My grandmother *said*. Past tense. You taught me that."

Mara had taught him that. He wished, suddenly and violently, that he hadn't.

"She's waiting," Silas added, quieter, in Vessany's tongue, and did not translate, and Mara let him keep it untranslated, the way you let someone keep a coat that no longer fits because taking it away would leave them colder.

They sang it through twice more. By the third time, Mara's voice had found the strange climbing intervals, and Silas beamed at him across the candlelight with such unguarded delight that Mara had to look down at the ledger open on his knee and pretend to check an entry.

There was no entry. There was just the boy, and the song, and the small warm cell of light they'd built inside a building designed to weigh everything and value nothing.

*This is what it costs,* Mara thought, watching Silas yawn and curl toward the brazier. *Not coin. This.*

He didn't yet know how right he was.

* * *

The frost reached the city's edge on a Tuesday, though no one called it frost.

Mara saw it from the loading dock at dawn, where he'd gone to fetch coal under the pretense of inventory. The harbor canal—the one that never froze, fed by hot springs the guild had once tried and failed to trade—had gone white at its margins. Not ice exactly. Ice was water's business. This was something else: a rime that climbed the stone quays in feathered patterns, delicate as engraving, and where it touched, the stone went grey. Not cold-grey. *Absent*-grey. Stone that had forgotten it was stone.

Beyond the canal, in the tannery district, the same pattern crept along the rooftops. And beyond that, far off, a haze on the horizon that was not cloud and not fog but distance itself going wrong—the skyline of the far hills looking closer than it should, pulled toward the city like a page curling at its corner.

Toward the Ledgerhouse.

Mara stood very still with the coal sack over his shoulder and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do for weeks.

The winter had been following Silas since Vessany. That much the ledgers confirmed, in entries written in his own hand that he didn't remember making. But the winter had been slow. Distant. A rumor of cold at the world's rim. And now it was here, at the canal, in six weeks, having crossed a distance it should have taken years to cross.

Unless something had been calling it faster.

Unless the thing it followed had stopped running. Had found a cellar and a brazier and a man who sang counting songs with him, and had—for the first time since his country died—stopped moving.

"Oh," Mara said aloud, to the dock, to the white-rimmed canal, to no one. "Oh, you stupid, selfish—"

He didn't finish it. He carried the coal downstairs instead, and smiled at Silas, and taught him the word for *Tuesday*, and hated himself with a thoroughness that surprised even him.

* * *

Silas knew before Mara told him. Of course he did. The cold was his inheritance; he read it the way Mara read ledgers.

"It's closer," he said one evening, unprompted, while Mara pretended to audit a column of figures. The boy sat cross-legged on the cot, braiding a frayed rope into a bracelet—something his grandmother had taught him, he'd explained, for luck, though he wouldn't say luck against what. "I can feel it. Like standing near a big door when someone's about to knock."

Mara set down the pen. "Silas."

"You're going to say I have to go." The boy didn't look up from the rope. His common tongue had gotten good enough, these past weeks, to carry weight in it. "You get the face. The ledger face. When you're adding up something bad."

"The frost is coming because of me," Mara said. "Because I kept you still. Winter follows you, and you stopped running, and now it knows exactly where you are. Every day you're here, it gets a day closer to everything else. The city. The people in it."

Silas set down the bracelet. In the brazier-light his face was very serious, and very young, and for a moment Mara saw the shape of the man he might become in a country that existed—in a garden, maybe, with a grandmother waiting past tense turned back to present.

"My grandmother sent me to find the doorkeeper," Silas said. "She said the doorkeeper would know what the toll was. She said—" he paused, hunting for the words, and finished in Vessany's tongue, then translated himself, laboriously, like carrying water in cupped hands: "She said the doorkeeper would be afraid. And that being afraid is how I would know him. Because only someone afraid understands what a door is *for*."

Mara's throat was tight. "And what's a door for, then? According to your grandmother?"

"For choosing," Silas said simply. "Doors are how you choose which side you belong to."

The brazier ticked. Somewhere above them, three floors up, the night clerks scratched their pens across entries about thunder-jars and angry keys, trading in impossibilities, blind to the one sleeping in their cellar.

"And if I choose wrong?" Mara asked.

Silas picked the bracelet back up and worked the braid, and for a while there was only the sound of rope and fire.

"Then the winter chooses for you," he said. "That's what happened to Vessany. Everyone waited to choose until the choosing was gone."

* * *

Mara did not sleep. He sat at the little table with a single ledger open before him—the oldest one, the one whose entries appeared in his handwriting across years he had not lived—and he read by candlelight while the boy breathed slow and even on the cot.

Near dawn, he found the entry he had been both seeking and dreading. It was dated forty years past, before his time, before the erasure—but the hand was his hand, unmistakably, the same cramped leftward slant he'd been scolded for as an apprentice.

*Debt outstanding: one keeper, owed passage. Toll: memory and place. Payable upon opening.*

Below it, fresher ink, the letters still sharp:

*Interest accrues daily. The account holder is asking for his child back.*

Mara closed the book. On the cot, Silas stirred and murmured something in Vessany's tongue—*avenn, duvenn*, one, two, counting flocks home in his sleep—and Mara sat in the dark and understood, finally, completely, that the warmth in this cellar had a price tag, and the winter had come to collect.

Upstairs, faint through the floorboards, came a sound that did not belong to the night shift: boots. Many boots. And a voice he knew, dry and courteous as a summons, asking the duty clerk whether Master Quintana was in the building.

Ivet Corane had come to call.
