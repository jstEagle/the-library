## 1. The Taste of Other People's Years

The dying woman's summer tasted like peaches.

That was the first thing Ivo noticed, the way they always noticed first—the flavor of it, because memory had a taste if you knew how to open your mouth to it. Peaches and river water and the faint copper tang of a boy's blood where he'd split his lip laughing. Beneath all that, warm as bread: someone else's mother calling them home from the water.

Ivo sat in the corner of the sickroom with their eyes closed and their hands loose in their lap, fingers spread, palms up, the way a beggar holds out a bowl. The extraction was delicate work. You didn't pull a memory like a tooth; you coaxed it, the way you'd lift a ribbon off a sleeping child's wrist. Too fast and it tore, and torn memories bled into everything around them, leaving the dying woman with nothing but a smear of grief where her best summer used to live.

She was asleep. That was mercy. The physician had given her poppy syrup an hour ago, and now she breathed in slow tides, her hands folded on the quilt her granddaughter had stitched, her face turned toward the window where the fog pressed against the glass like something curious and hungry.

*The Hollow keeps what it keeps,* Ivo's grandmother used to say. *No magic climbs our hills. No god answers at our doors. No road leaves the valley but the ones the Wardens watch.* Three rules, older than anyone living, repeated so often they'd worn smooth as river stones. Children learned them before they learned their letters. *No magic. No gods. No leaving.*

And no stealing other people's lives either, technically. But the Wardens' law had never bothered much about Ivo's particular trade. Memory-work wasn't magic—it was craft, patience, a knack that ran in certain families like red hair or bad knees. The Wardens called it a nuisance. The grieving rich of Vell's Crossing called it salvation, and paid accordingly.

The buyer tonight was a merchant named Corvane, whose daughter had drowned in the millrace three springs ago. He wanted the dead girl's happiest summer—not his own memories of her, which he kept locked in a lacquered box in his study and could not bear to open, but *hers*. What joy had felt like from the inside of her skin. He'd heard Ivo could get such things, and he'd paid triple rate without haggling, which told Ivo two things: he was desperate, and he would not ask questions later.

Good. The clients who asked questions always wanted more, eventually. And the clients who wanted more were the ones who ended up standing outside Ivo's door in the fog at strange hours, weeping, offering sums that made Ivo's stomach turn.

Ivo let the summer settle onto their tongue and began to draw it out.

It came slowly, in layers, the way honey leaves a spoon. A river running shallow over pale stones. A boy—her cousin? her brother?—shrieking as she dunked him. Peach trees heavy along the bank, fruit so ripe it split its own skin. The smell of cut grass and hot dust. Someone singing badly, on purpose, to make the children laugh. The whole thing glowed in Ivo's mind like a coal wrapped in cloth, and Ivo held it carefully, so carefully, feeling its edges, learning its weight.

This was the part of the work nobody paid for, the part Ivo never spoke aloud: for as long as the memory rested in them, it was theirs. They lived inside it. The sun on the river was *their* sun. The laughter was *their* laughter. For a few minutes in a stranger's sickroom, Ivo Villalobos was a girl who had been loved loudly and completely by a family that smelled of peaches and river water.

Then the work resumed, and the memory left, and Ivo was themselves again—twenty-six years old, thin as a fence rail, alone in every way that counted, with a head full of other people's best days and not one good day of their own.

They drew the last thread free. The summer lifted away whole, intact, gleaming—and behind it, in the old woman's sleeping mind, Ivo felt the shape of the hole it left. Not empty. Dying people's minds were soft as wet paper; the edges would fold inward and heal smooth within a day. She would wake tomorrow and feel a gentle absence, a summer she couldn't quite name, and she would think: *how strange, I dreamed of peaches,* and then she would think of other things, because there weren't many other things left to think.

Ivo sealed the memory into a vial of smoked glass—a stoppered ampoule no bigger than a thumb, the kind apothecaries used for laudanum. Inside the dark glass, the summer dimmed to a faint warmth against Ivo's palm, like a stone kept near a fire. It would keep for years, if Corvane wanted to hoard it. Most buyers did. They told themselves they'd take it someday, drink the memory down and live inside their daughter's joy forever. Almost none of them ever did. They just liked knowing it was there, the way misers liked knowing the coins were buried under the floor.

Ivo stood, tucked the vial into the lining of their coat, and looked once around the room. Candle stubs burned low. The granddaughter dozed in a chair by the door, her mouth open, exhausted from weeks of watching. On the nightstand, a clay cup of water, a rosary of river-stones that hadn't been prayed on in a century—nobody prayed anymore, not seriously, not expecting anything—and a photograph gone gray with age.

Ivo paused at the photograph. In it, the old woman stood young and straight-backed beside a man with a laugh already starting in his eyes, both of them squinting into a sun the camera had caught and held. Behind them, peach trees.

*I hope it was worth it,* Ivo thought, though they knew better than anyone that it always was, that people would trade almost anything for one perfect day kept safe from rot. That was the whole business. Grief was a country everyone emigrated to eventually, and Ivo sold passage back.

They let themselves out through the kitchen, past the snoring housekeeper, and down the servants' stair into the fog.

* * *

Vell's Crossing at midnight was a city of muffled sounds and half-seen shapes. The fog never fully lifted here—not in summer, not ever—and after dark it thickened until the lamplighters' posts glowed like drowned moons and footsteps arrived before their owners did. Ivo moved through it the way fish move through water, by habit and blind certainty, counting turns by the cobbles beneath their boots: broken stone at the chandler's corner, the dip where the gutter flooded, the church steps—empty church, dead church, a century of silence in its rafters—that marked the turn onto Rope Street.

The Hollow had eleven towns and one valley and no way out. Everyone knew this the way everyone knew rain fell downward. The passes north were watched by Warden towers; the river south sank into marshes that swallowed travelers whole; the eastern cliffs went up and up into cloud and came down the same way. Pilgrims had come once, the story went, before the silence—come to see the gods who walked the valley in flesh and gold. Then the gods stopped answering, the pilgrims stopped coming, the kings erased the roads, and the Hollow forgot, gently and thoroughly, that it had ever been anywhere at all.

Ivo had spent their whole life inside that forgetting, and had made a living off the pieces of it.

Their rooms above the cooper's shop on Rope Street were cold and smelled of oak shavings and lamp oil. Ivo lit the lamp, set the vial on the table, and allowed themselves the ritual: five minutes with the merchandise. Just five. Long enough to remember why the work was worth doing.

They unstoppered the vial and let the summer breathe against their mind, not taking it in, just letting it warm them, the way you'd warm your hands at someone else's hearth. Peaches. River water. The boy's split lip. The mother singing badly on purpose.

*One day,* Ivo thought, the same thought they thought every night, *I'll make one like this of my own.*

It was a stupid thought. Ivo was a thief of other people's years; they had no husband, no river, no peaches, no one who would sing badly on purpose just to make them laugh. Their own childhood was a thin gray thing, mostly fog, mostly hunger, mostly a grandmother's voice reciting the three rules until the words wore grooves in Ivo's skull. There was nothing in it worth bottling. Nobody would pay two coppers for the happiest day of Ivo Villalobos's life.

But the thought stayed anyway. It always stayed. It was the one thing Ivo had never managed to steal for themselves, or sell, or stop wanting.

A knock at the street door, three quick raps.

Ivo stoppered the vial, snuffed the lamp, and waited in the dark, listening. Three raps again, softer. Then a voice, low and rough:

"Ivo. It's Maren. Open up—I've got work, and it's strange work, and it pays double."

Maren the fence, who never came at midnight, who never said *strange*, who never offered double unless she was frightened of the job herself. Ivo stood in the cold dark room with a stolen summer warming the table beside them and felt, for the first time in years, the small animal at the base of their spine lift its head and sniff the air.

Below the fog, below the city, below the silence of a hundred years, something had begun to move.

"Coming," Ivo said, and reached for their coat.
