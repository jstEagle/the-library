## 2. A Prayer Answered in Ash

The client's name was Odile Ferran, and she smelled of tallow and fear.

She came to my rooms above the chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane an hour past dusk, hood up, hands wrapped in a wool scarf even though the autumn had been mild as bathwater. People who came to me at night with their hoods up always smelled of fear. It was the honest ones who came in daylight, weeping, asking me to take the worst of what they carried. The night ones wanted something taken from someone else.

"Her name is Maren," Odile said, setting a purse on my table. It clinked wrong—too heavy for the sum we'd agreed. "My sister. She's dying. Fever took her lungs, and the physicker says three days, maybe four." Her jaw worked. "I want her summer. The one at Larkspit Cove. She talks about it when the fever climbs. She laughs in her sleep. I want—I want to keep it. After."

I counted the purse without touching it. Too heavy by half.

"The rate is fixed," I said.

"Then take the rest and buy yourself something warm." She pulled back her hood, and I saw that she'd been crying on the walk over, or before it, or both. "Please. I don't have anyone else to ask. The priests say grief is holy. The priests can choke on it."

In the Hollow, everyone has a relationship with the gods the way everyone has a relationship with the weather: distant, resentful, mostly practical. A century of silence will do that. My grandmother used to say the gods left us like a husband who walks out for tobacco and never comes back, and after enough winters you stop setting a place for them at the table. The Wardens said it differently—said there had never been gods at all, only stories, and that the Hollow's peace depended on everyone agreeing to the story. No magic. No miracles. No leaving. Three rules carved over every Wardenhouse door in letters deep enough to rest your fingers in.

But grief makes believers of us all, briefly. Grief is a prayer with nowhere to go.

"Maren Ferran," I said. "Larkspit Cove. The summer she turned nineteen." I'd already done the asking-around; I always did. "I'll come tomorrow at noon. She should be awake. Memories go strange when the fever's high—they come out tasting of smoke."

Odile nodded too many times, thanked me too many times, and left the extra coin anyway because she knew I wouldn't chase her down the stairs to return it.

I sat a while after she'd gone, turning the purse over in my hands, listening to the chandler's family below put their children to bed. That was the part nobody warned you about, this trade. Not the stealing. The listening. Every house on Ropewalk Lane leaked its small music through the floorboards, and I had spent thirty-one years in this valley learning other people's lives by heart because I couldn't seem to make one of my own.

The memories I kept—the ones I didn't sell—filled three locked chests under my bed. A fisherman's first time standing on ice so clear he could see the trout hanging beneath him like thoughts. A bride's whole wedding compressed to the smell of rosemary and the sound of her mother crying in the second row. A boy's memory of his father lifting him onto his shoulders to see the harvest fires, the heat of it still living in the skin of the recollection. I told myself I kept them for study. For craft. For the day I might need to sell something rare.

I told myself a lot of things.

* * *

Maren Ferran's room stank of fever-sweat and camphor, and she was smaller than I expected, folded into her bed like a letter someone had given up trying to send. Her eyes were bright and wet and entirely present, which was worse than delirium. Dying people always looked at me like they knew what I was. Maybe they did. Maybe dying teaches you to recognize a thief of small eternities.

"You're the memory man," she rasped.

"I'm a collector of certain goods."

"My sister sent you." Not a question. She laughed, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that I poured her water and held the cup. "Tell her—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell her I know. Tell her it's all right. A person ought to get to keep something of a person."

"I'll tell her."

"Do it now, then." She lay back against the pillows. "While I'm still in here to be robbed."

I won't pretend I didn't hesitate. There are rules in this trade beyond the Wardens' three, the ones no one carves anywhere. You don't take from the unwilling. You don't take more than the buyer paid for. And you never, ever take a dying person's last good thing unless they hand it to you themselves.

Maren handed it to me herself. She closed her eyes and thought about Larkspit Cove with the deliberate ferocity of a woman doing her last piece of work, and I set two fingers to her temple and slipped into her like a key into a lock I'd cut myself.

The extraction itself is easy to describe and impossible to explain. You find the memory by its weight—happy ones are dense, dense as river stones—and you lift it whole, roots and all, and coil it into the little glass vial you carry for the purpose. Nineteen years old, salt wind, a striped awning over a fish stall, her sister Odile young and round-faced and laughing so hard at nothing that she snorted, and Maren loving her so much in that moment that the love was the memory, really, the cove just the room it happened in—

The vial burned my hand.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left now. The glass did not burn me. The *memory* burned me—a line of heat straight through the leather of my glove, through the skin beneath, searing a mark across my palm like a brand pressed quick and withdrawn. I dropped the vial. Caught it. Dropped it again. And inside the glass, coiled where a stolen summer should have been sleeping quiet, something *moved*.

Not stirred. Moved. The way a sleeper moves when they've decided to wake.

I stood in Maren Ferran's sickroom holding a vial that glowed faintly gold at its heart, my palm blistering, my whole body gone cold as well-water, and I understood three things in the space of one heartbeat.

First: magic does not work in the Hollow. This is not belief or policy; it is physics, older than the Wardens, older than the kings, as reliable as falling. Nothing burns that shouldn't burn. Nothing glows. Nothing wakes.

Second: something in this woman's happiest memory was not hers. It had been *in* her, folded into that day at the cove the way a seed folds into an apple, and I had just picked the apple without seeing the seed, and now the seed was in a glass vial in my hand and it was *warm*.

Third: Maren was looking at me. Awake. Clear-eyed. And she was smiling.

"It's early," she whispered. "Isn't it? It's early this year."

"What's early?" My voice came out sanded flat.

"The snow." She turned her face toward the window, toward the gray sky over the rooftops. "He always sends the snow first. So you'll look up. He said—" Her brow creased, and for a moment the fever swam back into her eyes. "He said someone would carry it. Someone with pockets full of other people's days. He said the thief would be the bellows, blowing on the ember until—"

"Maren."

"No, no, listen, he's *very* patient, that's the whole trouble with him, he waited a hundred years and he didn't mind at all—" She gripped my wrist with shocking strength, her fingers over the burn, and the pain flared white. "You have to keep it safe. Not hidden. *Safe.* There's a difference. Hidden is what they did to us. Safe is what you do for someone you—"

She coughed then, a long terrible tearing cough, and when it passed she was smaller again, dimmer, the brightness guttered back to fever-light. "Go on," she murmured, waving vaguely at the door. "Take it to my sister. Mind the cold coming."

I went. I am not ashamed to say I nearly ran.

* * *

I did not go to Odile Ferran. I told myself I would, tomorrow, once I'd thought—but the truth is I walked out of Ropewalk Lane with the vial buttoned inside my coat, against my chest, like a coal I was carrying home through a storm, and every few streets I pressed my hand flat over it to feel whether it still burned.

It did. Fainter each hour, but steady. Patient. *Very patient, that's the whole trouble with him.*

Whom. Whom. I corrected the pronoun in my head all evening and it refused to be corrected.

By full dark I was in my rooms with all three chests open on the floor, every stolen memory I owned laid out in its vial on the boards, dozens of them, catching the lamplight like a hoard of frozen honey. I told myself I was checking for damage. Contamination. Craft reasons.

What I was doing was looking for him.

Because here is the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I've told no one: over the years, certain memories I'd lifted had come out *strange*. A fisherman's ice-clear morning with a voice in it, low and amused, saying *look how they hang, like thoughts*—and I'd assumed the fisherman had been drunk. A bride's wedding where the rosemary smell arrived a heartbeat before the memory did, as if announced. A boy on his father's shoulders at the harvest fires, and in the corner of the recollection, at the edge of the firelight, a shape like a very tall man watching the flames with an expression I could only call *homesick*.

I'd sold some of those. Kept the best ones. Told myself the same story I always told.

Now I knelt on my own floorboards with a burning vial against my sternum and let myself think the thought entire: the gods did not leave the Hollow. Something in the Hollow *took* them—or took their names, their worship, whatever food they lived on—and starved them down to embers, and embers do not die, embers wait, and a hundred years of waiting ends somewhere, with someone, and the oldest stories agree on one detail above all others.

They agree that when the gods come back, they come back through whatever door is nearest.

And I was a man made entirely of doors. Thirty years of other people's most treasured moments, stacked and shelved and locked in chests, every one of them a warm room with the latch off.

Outside, the light changed.

I don't know how else to say it. Lamplight doesn't change color, but everything else does—the quality of the dark outside the window thickened, silvered, went soft in a way the dark never goes in the Hollow in autumn. I crossed to the window and stood there while the first flakes came down, fat and slow and unhurried, settling on the sill, on the roofs of Ropewalk Lane, on the Wardenhouse three streets over where the carved rules caught the snow like scars filling with white.

Out of season. Weeks out of season. The chandler's children below were shrieking with delight, and somewhere a bell began to ring—not alarm, not yet, just the Wardenhouse bell marking the hour, though I swear it rang one stroke too many.

I looked down at my palm. The burn had cooled into a mark: a thin line, curved like a hook, or a shepherd's crook, or the first stroke of a letter in an alphabet no one in the Hollow had written in a hundred years.

Snow fell on the glass. On the city. On the kiln district, where the poor were banking their fires against a cold that had no business arriving yet. On the sealed archives beneath the Wardenhouse, though I didn't know that yet, and on a Door I couldn't have imagined.

"He always sends the snow first," Maren had said. "So you'll look up."

I looked up. Between the clouds and me, in the falling white, I felt it—not heard, not saw, *felt*, the way you feel a large animal standing behind you in a dark field:

*There you are,* something said, gentle as an ember settling. *Bellows.*

The vial against my chest pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat, and began, very softly, to glow through my shirt.
