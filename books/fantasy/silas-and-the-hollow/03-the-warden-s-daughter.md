## 3. The Warden's Daughter

Sefa Veyran had been raised on the law the way other children were raised on lullabies. Her father's voice, low and certain, reciting the Three Provisions over supper: *No magic. No gods. No leaving.* He had taught her that the Wardens were not jailers but gardeners, pruning back anything that might grow wild in the Hollow's still air. He had taught her that order was a kindness. That silence was safety.

She was twenty-six years old and she had never once doubted him.

She was doubting him now, standing in the ash-fall of the kiln district with her father's seal burning a hole in her coat pocket like a coal she could not put down.

* * *

It had begun innocently, as betrayals always do. A ledger misfiled. Sefa kept her father's correspondence in order—she had done it since she was fifteen, when grief for her mother had settled into the household like a second winter and organizing his papers was the only way she knew to be near him without speaking. That evening, looking for a warrant template, she had pulled the wrong drawer in the archive beneath the Warden's Hall, and found a folder bound in grey cord that did not belong among the civil records at all.

Inside: eleven names. Eleven addresses in the kiln district. And beside each name, in her father's own hand, a single word.

*Touched.*

No charges. No hearings. No dates of trial, because there would be no trials. She had sat on the archive floor for a long time with the folder open on her knees, telling herself there was an explanation. There was always an explanation. Her father was rigorous; her father was fair; her father had once spent three days untangling a false accusation against a tanner's boy because, he said, *a law that punishes the innocent is no law at all.*

But the word *touched* was not a legal term. It appeared nowhere in the Provision codes. It belonged to the old stories—the ones mothers told badly, about the century before, when the gods still answered and the kings had walled the valley shut to starve them of worshipers. Stories. That was all they were supposed to be.

The eleventh address was Marla Quist's. The dyer. The woman who had once re-dyed Sefa's mourning sash after her mother died because the shop-bought black came out blue at the seams, and who had refused payment, saying, *Grief shouldn't look cheap, girl.*

Sefa went to the kiln district that night because she told herself she only wanted to warn them. She took the folder. She told herself that too was simple: evidence, in case she needed to confront her father with proof. She took her father's riding cloak so no one would mark her face under the hood. Every choice felt reasonable in the moment. That was the horror of it, she would think later. Treason assembled itself out of ordinary decisions, one sensible step at a time, until you looked down and found you were already standing somewhere you could not come back from.

* * *

The kiln district smelled wrong before she saw anything wrong.

Sefa knew the smell of the kilns—every child in Cindermark did. Hot clay and woodsmoke and the mineral tang of glaze. But underneath it tonight was something sweeter and fouler, something her body recognized before her mind allowed it to. Burning hair. Burning fat. The particular stink of a person burning, which she had only ever encountered in the description of a fire inquisitor's manual she'd catalogued years ago and thought nothing of.

The street was empty. Doors shut. Shutters drawn. In the Hollow, people had learned a hundred years ago what to do when power walked past: become furniture.

The old tile-works stood at the end of the lane, its great beehive kilns cold for a decade, condemned when the new gasworks opened across the river. Tonight every chimney breathed. Orange light pulsed from the loading arches like the district had grown a row of open mouths.

There were wagons. Warden gray, unmarked. Men in half-masks moving with the unhurried efficiency of dockworkers, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been—cruelty at least implied the work meant something to them. This was logistics. This was a Tuesday.

And by the third kiln, tall even among the masked men, his gray cloak catching the firelight, stood her father.

High Warden Aldous Veyran did not watch the arches. That was the detail that would follow Sefa into every sleep for the rest of her life: he wasn't watching. He was reading from a list, checking names against a clerk's tally, nodding. A woman was being walked up the iron ramp toward the loading arch—Sefa knew her walk, knew the set of her shoulders, knew the stubborn tilt of her chin—and her father did not look up from his paper.

Marla Quist. Whose hands had been stained indigo to the wrist for forty years. Who had once said, laughing, that the old gods were just the colors nobody mixed anymore.

They put her in the arch. They sealed it with a clay plug, the way you'd bank a kiln for the night, and the light inside changed, and the screaming started, and Sefa's father turned the page of his list.

Sefa did not remember deciding to move. She remembered the gravel under her boots, remembered a masked man turning, remembered the folder leaving her hand—not thrown, not dropped, simply released, as if her body understood before she did that she could not carry it through what came next. It skidded across the yard. Her father looked up.

For one full second their eyes met across the burning yard, and Sefa searched his face for the man who had untangled the tanner's boy's false accusation, and found instead someone patient. Someone doing arithmetic. Someone who saw his daughter standing in the light of his work and began, visibly, to calculate.

"Close the north arch," he said quietly, to no one in particular. "My daughter is here."

She ran.

* * *

She ran the way she had run drills in the Warden cadet yards at fourteen, when her father had insisted she train alongside the boys—*the law must be able to catch the lawless, Sefa, or it is only opinion*—down the tile-works' drainage cut, through the culvert that smelled of a decade of standing water, out into the tannery rows where the stench covered everything. Behind her, boots. Ahead of her, the river fog coming off the water in slow gray banks, thickening as the night deepened, the valley's ever-present breath.

A hand caught her cloak at the mouth of Tanner's Row. She twisted the way the drill-master had beaten into her—turn into the grip, not away—and the man grunted, off-balance, and she drove her elbow into the hinge of his jaw and was free, cloak torn, hood gone, running again with her braid whipping loose behind her.

The bolt came from the crossbow of a man she couldn't see. She heard the twang, heard the hiss of it, and then the world went white along her left side.

Not a direct hit. Later, piecing it together, she would understand: a glancing strike, or a deflection off the culvert lip, or simply a marksman firing blind into fog. It didn't matter. What mattered was the burn—a stripe of agony from her shoulder blade down across her ribs, the cloak smoldering, the smell of scorched wool and her own seared skin, and she made a sound she was not proud of and kept running, because behind her the kilns were still roaring and ahead of her was only fog, and between those two things there was no middle ground anymore.

She went into the river. Not the bridge—the bridge had eyes. She went down the tannery slipway and into water cold enough to stop her heart if she let it, and she let the current take her under the pilings while the burn screamed and the fog closed over the place where she had been.

* * *

Dawn found her in the drying loft above a rope-walk on the far bank, a space some tanner's family used for hemp and had, apparently, also used for hiding things, because when Sefa dragged herself up the ladder she disturbed a nest of blankets, a tin of hardtack, and a boy.

He was small. Seven, maybe eight. Filthy, hollow-eyed, with the stillness of a child who had learned that stillness was survival. He did not cry out at the sight of her—which itself was wrong; children cried out. He only watched her with an attention too old for his face, and when she collapsed against the wall clutching her burned side, he crept closer, and did something impossible.

He pressed his small grimy hand to her forehead, and Sefa heard—*heard*, inside her skull, the way you hear your own thoughts—a voice that was not hers and not the boy's. It sounded like wind through a door left open in an empty house. It sounded like a name being spoken very slowly by something that had forgotten how.

The pain in her side went quiet. Not healed—she could feel that plainly—but *quieted*, held, the way you'd hold a hot iron with a wet cloth.

The boy snatched his hand back as if scalded, eyes wide, terrified of himself. His mouth moved. No sound came out. He was mute; she could see that now, see the old scar of a childhood illness at his throat. And yet the voice had been enormous, layered, many-throated, nothing a child could make.

"What are you?" she whispered.

He shook his head. He pointed—at her, at her burned side, then at his own chest, then upward, vaguely, at the rafters, at the sky beyond them, at whatever lived above the fog. Then he made a shape with both hands, careful and deliberate, like a man handling something holy: a rectangle. A frame.

A door.

Sefa sat in the dust and the hemp fibers and laughed until it turned into something else, pressing her sleeve against her mouth so the sound wouldn't carry. Her father was burning people alive in a condemned tile-works. The law she had given her life to had split apart in front of her like wet clay in a kiln, and on one side of the crack was everything she had been, and on the other side was a mute orphan in a rope-loft who had just touched her with a god's hand.

Her shoulder throbbed. She would carry that scar the rest of her days, a long white stripe across the ribs, and she would think of it honestly: not a wound from the enemy. A wound from her father's justice. The two had finally, fully, come apart.

Below, in the fog-bound streets, the Wardens were waking the city. She could hear the proclamation criers already, their bells ringing the assembly pattern, their voices flat with rehearsed authority: *By order of the High Warden, any citizen harboring the god-touched commits treason against the Provision. Report all manifestations. Report all strangers. Report all wonders.*

Wonders. Her father had outlawed wonder.

The boy tugged her sleeve. He pointed down, through the floorboards, toward the city—then made a walking motion with two fingers, urgent, insistent. Someone was coming. Or somewhere they needed to go. He couldn't say which, and neither, yet, could Sefa.

But she had her father's seal in her pocket, a list of eleven names in her head, and a burn she intended to show the whole Hollow if it came to that.

"Right," she said to the boy, to the fog, to the god or whatever it was. "Then we move."

Somewhere across the river, in the crooked lanes of Cindermark, a memory-thief was taking a job they should never have taken—and the boy's fingers were already walking toward it.
