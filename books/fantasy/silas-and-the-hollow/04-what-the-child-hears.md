## 4. What the Child Hears

### IVO

The job came to me the way most jobs did: through a note folded into a loaf of black bread at the Widow Crake's stall, paid for with a coin that had been warm from someone else's hand.

*The foundling house on Candlemaker's Row. The boy in the third bed from the window. His memories, all of them, delivered whole. Payment enough to winter twice over.*

I read it twice and felt the old familiar itch behind my teeth—the one that wasn't hunger exactly, though I'd learned to mistake it for hunger years ago. Twice a winter's pay for one child's head. Children's memories were easy lifting. Soft as plums. They hadn't yet built the walls adults build around their worst rooms, hadn't learned to hide the things they couldn't bear to look at. You could walk into a child's mind like walking into an unlocked house, take what you wanted, and leave the door swinging.

I told myself the buyer was probably a relative. An aunt who wanted to know what the boy had seen before whatever silence took his voice. Foundlings were nobody's; somebody paying for a mute orphan's memories was either monstrous or grieving, and in the Hollow those two things wore each other's coats so often you stopped checking which was which.

That was the litany I said over every job, the little prayer of a godless country: *someone has to do this work. Better me than a butcher. The memories go to people who need them. Nothing is wasted.*

It was a good litany. I'd polished it smooth as a river stone from saying it so many times.

Candlemaker's Row smelled of tallow and wet ash, the way half the city did now that the kiln district burned day and night. The foundling house was a long low building with a slate roof furred green, and inside it smelled of boiled barley and carbolic and the particular sourness of too many children in too little space. A matron with a face like a closed ledger led me down the dormitory corridor without meeting my eyes. She'd been paid. She didn't want to know what for. In the Hollow, not-knowing was practically a civic virtue.

Third bed from the window.

He was small—six, maybe seven, all elbows and collarbones under a nightshirt gone grey with washing—and he was sitting up in the dark, watching me come. He didn't look afraid. That was the first wrong thing. Children always looked afraid when a stranger leaned over their bed at night. This one looked *interested*, the way you'd look at weather coming in.

His mouth was closed. His lips were thin and unmoving. And still—

*Hello,* said something inside my skull, gentle as a hand on the back of my neck. *You're the one who carries him. The one who carries our brother.*

I staggered back into the bedframe. My hands went cold. Every trick I knew—the breathing, the grounding, the counting of exits—I reached for and found nothing there, because there was nothing to defend against. The voice wasn't coming *at* me. It was already *in* me, the way my own thoughts were in me, only it wasn't mine and it had never been mine and it knew things about me that no living soul knew.

*You've been keeping him safe,* the voice said, warm, enormous, patient as geology. *All these years. Our name. You kept our name without knowing it was a name.*

"I don't know what you're talking about," I whispered, and the matron two doors down didn't even glance in, because to her I was just another visitor murmuring over a sick child.

The boy tilted his head. His eyes were dark and bottomless and terribly awake, and when he looked at me I understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that the voice was coming from *him*. That it poured off him constantly, into everyone who passed—that the other children in this room slept badly and dreamed of standing stones, that the matron hummed hymns she'd never been taught, that the whole street around this house had been marinating in divinity for months without knowing why their prayers had started to feel less like shouting into fog.

The boy couldn't speak. He didn't need to. He was a bell that never stopped ringing, and everyone in the Hollow had begun, without knowing it, to hear the sound.

*Ivo,* the voice said, using my name, tasting it. *Thief. Keeper. Vessel. We have been silent a hundred years because they took our names, and you—you have been carrying one of us in your pocket like a coin.*

My hands were shaking. I thought of the client's memory last month, the one that had burned me, the glimpse of something vast turning over beneath the valley like a sleeper rolling toward wakefulness. I thought of the snow falling out of season. I thought of every memory I'd ever stolen, hoarded in the vault-rooms of my own mind, and wondered which one held a god's name, and whether opening it would be like opening a furnace door.

Then the window shattered.

Not the glass—a body through it, boots-first, rolling across the floorboards and up onto her feet in one motion, cloak snapping, a Warden's shortblade already bare in her fist. She stood between me and the door with snow melting on her shoulders and a burn scar crawling up the side of her jaw like ivy made of firelight.

"Sefa Villamare," I breathed. "The High Warden's daughter. Come to arrest me personally? Your father should be flattered."

"Shut up." Her eyes weren't on me. They were fixed on the boy, and her blade-hand was trembling—not with fear. With recognition. "What is he doing? What is he—" She pressed her free hand hard against her ear as if she could stopper it. "I heard him from the street. Everyone on the street can hear him. They're singing. Why are they *singing*, thief?"

"That," I said slowly, "is an excellent question."

And then we both heard the boots below. Many boots. Lantern-light climbing the stairwell wall.

"They followed me," Sefa said, and there was something raw in it, something that sounded like a girl watching her own name get crossed off a list. "They let me lead them here."

### SEFA

I had planned everything except this.

Three weeks I'd spent being hunted by men I'd eaten supper beside, three weeks sleeping in drain-culverts and chapel attics with my father's sealed ledger strapped against my ribs like a second heart, and in all that time I had told myself the story that kept me moving: *find the thief, use the thief, get to the Door.* The thief was a tool. The ledger said the Door needed hands that could walk through minds; the thief was the only such hands in the Hollow. Simple. Clean. Professional.

Then I'd climbed through a foundling-house window and walked into a wall of voices, and every clean plan I owned had gone up like paper.

The boy sat in his bed with his small hands folded in his lap, and out of him poured something I had no honest word for. Singing, I'd called it, but it wasn't song. It was older than song. It was the feeling of my mother's kitchen, the smell of bread, the exact weight of my father's hand on my head when I was four and the world was still a place where fathers protected children—it was every good thing I'd ever lost, given back to me all at once by a stranger's voice that was not a stranger's voice at all. I stood there with a knife in my hand and tears running down my face and I could not remember deciding to cry.

*Daughter of the law,* the voice said, kind, unbearably kind. *Daughter of the lock. We do not blame you for what your blood built. Blame is a small country. We have been away too long to live there.*

"Stop it," I whispered. "Stop—"

Boots on the stairs. Six pairs, by the sound. Sergeant Halloway's cadence among them—heavy left foot, the man had favored it since the border riots. Men I'd trained beside. Men who would burn me in the kiln district with the same efficiency they'd burn anyone else touched by this, and they would call it justice while they did it, because that was the family trade: dressing cruelty in law's clothing until you couldn't tell the seams apart.

The thief moved fast. Faster than I'd credited. One moment he was pressed against the bedframe looking like a man underwater; the next he had the boy up and wrapped in a blanket, and he was shoving open the shutters onto the roof of the coal shed.

"Window or stairs," he said. "Choose fast, Warden's daughter. I'm choosing the window."

"You don't give orders to—"

A lantern flared at the end of the corridor. Halloway's voice, flat and terrible: "God-touched inside. Burn order stands."

Burn order. For a seven-year-old. My father's seal on the warrant, I'd have bet my life on it, because I'd seen his handwriting on eleven others.

I went through the window.

The roof was slick with the season's first real snow, and we went down it in a controlled fall, the thief ahead of me with the boy bundled against his chest, dropping into the alley where the shadows pooled deep between the foundling house and the tannery. Behind us the dormitory windows bloomed orange. They weren't even going to search it properly. They were going to burn the whole row and write *structure fire* in the report, and somewhere in a warm office my father would initial it without reading.

We ran. Three of us now, which was two more than I'd budgeted for, through the tannery yards and down into the culvert country beneath Tanner's Bridge, where the city's forgotten poor had always lived like water seeping into foundations. The thief knew the way. Of course he did. Thieves know the underside of any city better than its lawful face.

Finally, in the dry chamber beneath the bridge footings, lit by one smuggled candle, he set the boy down and turned on me with his back against the wall and his breath smoking in the cold.

"Now," he said. "You're going to tell me what's really in that ledger you sleep with, Warden's daughter, and why your father wants a mute child burned. Because I've done a lot of ugly work in this valley, but I have never once taken a job that ended in a bonfire, and I'd like to know how close I just came."

I looked at him—this hollow-eyed memory-thief with a stranger's griefs stacked behind his face like cordwood—and I looked at the boy, who was watching us both with those bottomless eyes, and I heard again the voice that had called me *daughter of the lock* and forgiven me for it.

"The Hollow isn't a country," I said. "It's a prison. My father's family helped build it. And the Door out of it is real, and it's under the archive, and neither of us gets there alone."

The thief stared at me. Above us, muffled by earth and stone, the foundling district went on burning, and the boy reached out and took my hand in his small cold fingers, and in my mind, gentle as falling snow, the gods began to sing.

* * *

The candle had burned to a stub before either of us spoke again.

The boy—Tam, the matron's ledger said, though the matron was likely ash now along with her ledger—had fallen asleep against the thief's shoulder with the terrible ease of children, as if the world hadn't just tried to kill him. The thief sat very still so as not to wake him, and I watched him watch the boy, and I saw something in his face I recognized from mirrors: the look of a person whose story about themselves had just died and whose replacement hadn't arrived yet.

"He talked to me," the thief said finally. "Before you came through the window. Not the boy. The... other thing in him. It said I've been carrying one of them. A name. In my head. In the memories I've stolen." His laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand what that means? I've spent ten years taking other people's lives for coin, and apparently one of them was a *god*, and I've been walking around with it like a purse I forgot I owned."

"Yes," I said.

"That's it? Yes?"

"My father's archives say the old kings starved the gods out. Took their names, locked the valley, let a century of silence do the rest." I pulled the ledger from inside my coat and set it on the stone between us, and watched his eyes track it the way starving men track bread. "If a god's name is in your head, thief, then you're not a tool anymore. You're evidence. You're the most dangerous person in the Hollow, and my father will peel this city apart to find you."

Above us, faint through the bridge footings, a bell began to ring—the Wardens' assembly toll, once for fire, twice for heresy.

Twice.

The thief looked up at the sound, then down at the sleeping boy, then at me, and I watched him make the decision the way I'd watched my father make a hundred decisions: quickly, cleanly, and with the cost filed away for later.

"All right," Ivo said. "Tell me about this Door."
