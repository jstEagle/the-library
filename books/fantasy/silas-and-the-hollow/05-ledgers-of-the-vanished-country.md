## 5. Ledgers of the Vanished Country

The seal on her father's archive was wax the color of dried blood, pressed with the Warden's sigil: a closed eye above three bars. Sefa had seen that seal broken exactly once in her life, the day she turned twelve, when her father let her watch him lock away a petition from the kiln district and told her, *Some doors are locked for love, little wren. Remember that.*

She remembered it now, standing in his study with the seal cracked under her thumb and her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

*Some doors are locked for love.* She had believed him. That was the worst of it. Not the burning—though she would carry the smell of the kiln district in her nose until she died—but the believing. Twenty-three years of believing.

Outside, the early winter ground on. Snow fell past the window in fat, wrong-seasoned flakes, and the city below had gone quiet in the way of a held breath. Three days since she'd fled the archive stairs with her father's ledgers burning a hole in her satchel and her arm wrapped in linen that was already soaking through. Three days since the boy with no voice had looked at her with eyes full of someone else's thunder.

Three days since Ivo Villalobos had hidden her in a cellar that smelled of tallow and rats and said, *Sleep. Nobody looks for a corpse.*

She wasn't sleeping. She was reading.

* * *

Her father's archive occupied the lowest floor of the Warden's Hall, behind iron and wax and a lock changed every season. What Sefa had stolen was not the whole of it—she'd had minutes, and blood loss, and a guard making his rounds—but she had taken the oldest thing there: a ledger bound in grey hide, its spine stamped with a crown she did not recognize, because no king had ruled the Hollow in living memory.

She sat by candlelight in the safehouse cellar with the ledger open across her knees, and she read until the words stopped being words and became a wound.

*In the Year of the Quiet Bell, by command of Aldric the Third, King of Vail and the Marches and the Valley Between, let it be entered:*

*That the gods of the valley are many, and the people love them, and love cannot be taxed or garrisoned.*

*That the war goes ill, and the priests preach peace, and peace is treason.*

*Therefore: let the valley be closed. Let the passes be dropped into the deep earth. Let the maps be burned and redrawn without us. Let no road out remain, save one, and that one sealed beneath stone and oath.*

*Let the names of the gods be gathered up and buried, for a god unfed is a god forgotten, and a god forgotten is a god that cannot raise its hand against the crown.*

Sefa read it twice. Then a third time, moving her lips, as if the shape of the syllables might change what they meant.

They didn't. The Hollow was not a country. It was a cell. Her whole life—the fog walls, the silence where prayers should have gone, the Wardens' catechism recited in every schoolroom, *no magic, no gods, no leaving*—was not tradition. It was sentence. A prison built a hundred years ago by kings who feared being loved more than they feared being hated, and her family had been made gaolers, and the gaolers had made themselves lords, and the lords had taught their children to call the bars *home*.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above, snow kept falling out of season, and she thought: *the gods aren't waking. They're starving back to life. There's a difference, and the difference is us.*

* * *

There were other documents. She went through them the way you dig through ash for something that survived the fire—a surveyor's report, its edges charred, describing "the Door" as a structural feature beneath the old archive hill: *a leaf of black metal, unrusting, warm to the touch; the masons refuse to work near it; sealed by royal writ.* A list of names, hundreds long, written in a clerk's careful hand: *Names Gathered and Interred, per Command.* Each name crossed through with a single line—not struck out, she realized, but *buried*, the way you mark a grave.

And near the bottom of the satchel, folded small, a page in her father's own handwriting. Recent. The ink still sharp.

*The girl is asking questions. The kiln matter was necessary. If she has found the grey ledger, then the time is shorter than I hoped. Let the Door be checked. Let the offerings continue. Better one district burn than the valley open.*

*Better one district burn than the valley open.*

Sefa set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something venomous, and discovered she was shaking. Not with grief. Grief had come and gone in the first night, wept out against Ivo's indifferent shoulder while the thief pretended to sleep. This was something hotter and cleaner. This was fury—the kindled, architectural kind, the kind that wanted a blueprint.

He had known. He had known the valley was a prison and called it protection. He had fed people to a fire to keep a lock turned, and he had written it down in his tidy hand because he had never once imagined his daughter would read it. He had locked the archive *for love*. For love. As if love were a thing you did to people with iron and wax and lies.

"You knew," she said aloud to the empty cellar, to the man who wasn't there, and her voice came out low and steady and strange to her. "You've always known."

Above her, through the floorboards, footsteps crossed the safehouse—Tam, pacing his endless restless circuit, the gods murmuring through him like wind through wires. She had learned to sleep beside it. She had almost learned to sleep beside it.

The footsteps stopped. Then quickened toward the stairs.

* * *

Ivo came down first, which surprised her; she'd expected Tam. The thief moved like a rumor, all quiet edges, and stopped at the bottom of the steps with their head cocked, reading her face the way they presumably read locks.

"You've been down here two days," Ivo said. "You only come up to eat. You've got your father's look right now. The one he wears when he's signing something."

"I know what the Hollow is." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "It's a prison. It was built to be one. The old kings walled off the valley and starved the gods on purpose—burned the maps, buried their names, left one Door and sealed it. A century of fog and silence, and everyone calling it the natural order of things." She pushed the grey ledger across the table. "Read it. Read what we are."

Ivo didn't touch it at first. They stood looking down at the crowned spine, and Sefa watched something move behind their eyes—something hungry and frightened at once, the look of a person shown the answer to a question they hadn't dared ask.

"Starved," Ivo repeated. "You mean the memories. The ones I take." Their voice had gone flat and careful. "People's happiest years. Their dead children. Their summers. I always thought—" They stopped. Started again. "I thought I was robbing a poor country. I didn't think the poverty was *manufactured*."

"The gods fed on worship. On prayer. On being *remembered*." Sefa tapped the ledger's buried names. "So the kings took remembering away. And when that wasn't enough, they took the names themselves. Everything the valley forgot, it forgot on purpose. Except—" she hesitated, and the next words came out slower, "—except some of it didn't stay buried. It leaked. Into minds. Into keepsakes. Into whatever a memory-thief scoops up when they're working."

Ivo had gone very still. Stillness, Sefa was learning, was what Ivo did instead of screaming.

"That's why it burned me," they said quietly. "The client's memory. Gods don't exist here. Nothing holy should be able to scald. Unless what I took wasn't a memory of a god." Their throat worked. "Unless it was a piece of one."

"Or a name," Sefa said. "A buried name, looking for somewhere to live."

From the top of the stairs, a sound: Tam, come down without either of them hearing, standing in the doorway with his hands curled at his sides. He couldn't speak—he hadn't spoken since the day they found him—but when he opened his mouth now, the voice that came out was not entirely his, and not entirely one voice either. It was several, braided, like a choir heard through a wall.

"*The door under the hill,*" the boy said. "*Warm metal. Buried oath. We remember the shape of our own cage, warden's daughter. We have had a hundred years to memorize it.*"

Sefa's skin prickled. "Can you tell us where—"

"*You already hold the map.*" Tam's borrowed voices softened, and for a moment he just looked like a tired child. "*Your father checks it every season. Watch him. Follow him. The Door wakes when it is fed, and it is hungry, and so is the man who keeps it fat and locked.*"

Then the boy blinked, shivered, and looked around the cellar as if he'd been asleep standing up. "Was I—" he began, and stopped, confused, and Ivo steered him gently back upstairs with a hand between his shoulders.

Sefa sat alone with the ledger and the candle and the page in her father's hand.

*Better one district burn than the valley open.*

She thought about her father checking the Door every season—descending beneath the archive hill with his lantern and his keys, feeding whatever needed feeding to keep the lock turned. She thought about the offerings. She thought about the word *necessary*, and how many times in her life she had heard him use it, and how she had nodded every time.

Purpose settled over her then, not gently—it came down like the snow, like a verdict, like a roof. She was the daughter of the High Warden. She knew his routes, his seals, his seasons. She knew which guards loved him and which merely feared him, and she knew, now, exactly what he was guarding and why it had to stop being guarded.

They would need the Door. Which meant they would need her father.

Which meant, sooner or later, someone was going to have to make him open it—and Sefa blew out the candle, folded the grey ledger into her coat, and climbed the stairs into the falling snow to tell the others that she knew exactly where they were going, and exactly who was standing in the way.
