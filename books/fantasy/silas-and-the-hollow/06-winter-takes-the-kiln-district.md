## 6. Winter Takes the Kiln District

The snow came on the ninth night of Frostmonth, and it did not stop.

Ivo had lived in the Hollow long enough to know what winter was supposed to look like here: a polite dusting that melted by noon, a gray slush season the merchants complained about and the poor endured. This was not that. This was a white wall falling out of a black sky, piling knee-deep by morning, swallowing the gutters, the carts, the steps of the kiln district's leaning houses. By the third day the city's bells rang muffled, as if the sound itself were freezing.

"Wrong," Tam said. He stood at the window of Ivo's rented room above the tannery, his breath fogging the glass, his eyes reflecting the snowfall like two small lamps. "It's wrong. It's listening."

"Don't say things like that out loud." Ivo pulled their coat tighter. The boy had been saying things like that for six days now—since Sefa had smuggled them both out of the archive district with her father's seal burning a hole in her pocket—and every word made Ivo's stolen memories itch behind their eyes.

"It isn't weather," Tam said simply. "Weather doesn't wait for permission."

Sefa sat at the table sharpening a knife she didn't know how to use yet, her burn-scarred hand steady on the whetstone. She looked up. "Then whose permission is it waiting for?"

Tam shrugged, which was somehow worse than an answer.

* * *

The Wardens found their explanation quickly, because frightened cities always do.

By the fifth day of snow, notices went up on every door in Ashfall, nailed straight through the wood by men in gray-and-gold who did not knock. THE LONG WINTER IS A PUNISHMENT, the notices read, in the High Warden's own cramped hand. THE GOD-TOUCHED HAVE OFFENDED THE ORDER OF THE VALLEY. REPORT THEM. PROTECT YOUR CHILDREN. PROTECT YOURSELVES.

"They're using my father's handwriting," Sefa said, staring at one nailed to the tannery gate. Her voice was flat in a way Ivo had learned to fear. "He wrote this. He sat at his desk and he wrote *protect your children*, and then men took hammers to doors."

"He believes it," Ivo said. "That's the worst part. Men who lie sleep fine. Men who believe—" They stopped. Down the street, a crowd had gathered around a cobbler's shop, and the crowd was not a friendly one. A woman was being dragged out by her arms, an old woman, still wearing her work apron, flour or chalk dust on her sleeves. She wasn't screaming. She was praying, quietly, the old words nobody had used in a century, and the crowd backed away from her mouth as if the words might be contagious.

"She touched a shrine," someone said. "My cousin saw her. She wiped the ash off the shrine on Candle Lane."

The Wardens put her in a wagon with three others. The wagon's wheels left black lines through the fresh snow, and Ivo noticed—for the first time but not the last—that no snow seemed to settle inside the wagon bed. It fell around it. It fell everywhere else.

Tam watched from between Ivo's legs, small fingers gripping their coat hem. He didn't say anything. But every person in that wagon, as it rolled past, turned their head—not toward the crowd, toward *him*—with expressions Ivo couldn't read and didn't want to.

* * *

The arrests became a harvest.

They came first for the shrine-tenders, then for anyone who'd dreamed loudly, then for anyone denounced by a neighbor over a debt or a grudge—the old currency of cruelty, suddenly legal again. The kilns of the kiln district, cold for years, began to smoke day and night, and the smell that came off them was not the smell of lime or pottery. Ivo had stolen enough memories from enough dying people to know what burning flesh smelled like even secondhand, and they caught themselves standing in alleys, breathing through their mouth, cataloguing the smell against memories they wished they could forget instead of keep.

"We leave," Sefa said on the twelfth night. She'd come back from the lower city with bread, actual bread, bought at triple price from a baker too scared to ask questions. "Tonight. Over the pass before it closes."

"There is no over the pass," Ivo said. "There's never been an over the pass. You've read the archives. The valley doesn't have edges you can walk off. It has the Door, and the Door is under your father's house, and your father has forty armed men between us and it."

"Then we go back for the Door."

"Not yet." Ivo spread their hands. "Not with half the city watching every face that moves after dark. We need somewhere to be *nothing*. Somewhere the Wardens don't look because there's nothing worth looking at."

Sefa set down the bread. "And where in all the gods-forsaken—"

"The Undercroft," Tam said.

They both turned. The boy hadn't spoken in hours; he spent most of his silence listening to something none of them could hear. Now he pointed down, through the floor, through the frozen mud beneath the floor.

"The forgotten ones live under everything," he said. "They're warm. They kept the fires."

* * *

Ivo had known the Undercroft existed the way everyone in Ashfall knew—vaguely, dismissively, as a story told to children about where beggars went. What Ivo had not known, because theft only reaches as deep as memory allows, was its size. Beneath the kiln district's collapsed foundry, beneath the old cisterns, whole streets ran in the dark: vaulted brick corridors from some earlier century, lit by oil drums burning scrap, hung with laundry that would never dry, crowded with people the census had stopped counting generations ago.

The forgotten poor. The ones the valley's tidy order had always required and never acknowledged.

A woman named Brannoc met them at the foundry's throat—a huge, gray-haired woman with a smith's forearms and a ledger-keeper's eyes, who looked at Sefa's fine coat and Ivo's thief's hands and Tam's silent, lamp-bright gaze and asked exactly one question:

"You running from the wagons?"

"Yes," Sefa said.

Brannoc nodded once. "Then you're ours till spring. Rule's simple. Everybody pulls. Even the little one." She looked at Tam. "Especially him, maybe. Half my people can't sleep since the snow started. Say they hear singing. If he knows anything about that, he works it off same as anyone."

Tam smiled for the first time in days.

* * *

They learned the Undercroft the way water learns a channel. Ivo, who had spent a lifetime making themselves useful in exchange for coin, discovered how strange usefulness felt when no one paid. They mended locks. They taught Brannoc's runners which cellar doors opened silently. They sat with an old man named Pell who was dying slowly of something in his lungs, and when he asked—they always asked eventually, the desperate ones, the ones with nothing left to lose—Ivo took the pain's edge out of his last months the only way they knew how, lifting the sharpest hour of each bad night and leaving him the softer parts.

"You're not charging him," Sefa observed, the fourth night, watching Ivo climb back down from Pell's alcove.

"No."

"That's new."

"Don't get used to it."

But Sefa was smiling, tired and crooked, and Ivo found they didn't mind being seen. That was the trouble with the Undercroft. There was nowhere to hide your face down here, and after a while you stopped wanting to. Sefa organized the food stores with a warden's precision and taught the older children to read off salvaged ledgers. Tam moved through the corridors like a lit candle through a dark house, and wherever he passed, people slept. Brannoc started calling him *the lantern* and posted him, gently, outside the fever tents.

On the sixth night, Ivo woke to find Tam sitting cross-legged at the foot of their pallet, watching them with those reflecting eyes.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Holding on too tight." The boy reached out and touched Ivo's wrist, right over the old scar where a client's stolen memory had burned them weeks ago. The scar was warm. It had been warm for days. "It's getting heavy. What you're carrying. It wants to be put down."

"I'm not putting down anything," Ivo said. "What I carry is mine. Bought and paid for."

Tam tilted his head, listening to something far away. "He says that's what a magpie says. About shiny things." A pause. "He says he remembers you. From before you remember yourself."

Before Ivo could ask—he was already gone, padding back down the corridor toward the fever tents, and the scar on Ivo's wrist pulsed once, twice, like a second heartbeat learning the rhythm.

* * *

The betrayal, when it came, wore a familiar face.

Odd Vessel—born Oskar Vesely, called Odd by everyone including himself—had fenced Ivo's goods for eleven years. He was a soft, damp man who ran a chandler's shop near the tallow market and paid fair prices and never asked questions, and Ivo had trusted him precisely as much as that arrangement required: completely, within its walls; not one inch beyond.

So when Ivo slipped out on the fourteenth night to sell a few recovered pieces—silver reliquary cases, godless and worthless to anyone but collectors, but collectors existed even in a dying country—they knew something was wrong before they reached the tallow market. The streetlamps burned too evenly. The snow held no footprints but their own, which meant someone had swept it, which meant—

The chandler's shop was lit. Odd sat behind his counter, hands folded, flanked by two Wardens in gray-and-gold, and his face was wet and miserable and relieved all at once.

"I'm sorry, Ivo," he said, and meant it, which somehow made it worse. "They have my daughter. The god-touched lists. Her name was on a list because she sang at her grandmother's grave, Ivo, she *sang*, and they said clemency for information, and you were the only name I—" His voice broke. "You were always going to be worth more than me someday. I just hoped I'd be dead first."

The Wardens moved. Ivo almost made the window—eleven years of exits don't vanish—but a third man was already outside in the snow, and a blow from a weighted glove took them across the temple, and the last thing Ivo saw before the dark was Odd weeping into his folded hands like a man at his own funeral.

* * *

They interrogated Ivo in a wagon, which was a mistake, because wagons move.

Two Wardens, questions about the girl Villalobos, the child, the tunnels under the foundry—and Ivo gave them eleven years of professional misdirection, bleeding lies so smoothly they half believed them: Sefa fled south alone, the boy was dead of fever, the Undercroft was a story. And all the while, bound and swaying, Ivo felt the scar on their wrist growing warmer, and warmer, until it wasn't pain anymore but attention, like something vast turning over in sleep beneath the snow, beneath the valley, beneath everything, and noticing *them*.

The wagon stopped. Boots in snow. The rear doors opened onto torchlight and the smoking silhouette of the kilns—

—and every torch on the street guttered out at once.

Every lamp. Every brazier. Every window-flame down the whole length of Kiln Row, snuffed in a single breath, and in the sudden dark Ivo heard Tam's voice, impossibly close, coming from everywhere at once, the way it had come from everywhere the night the snow began:

"*Let my thief go.*"

The Wardens' grip went slack. When Ivo stumbled free into the darkness, no one followed. When they reached the foundry's throat, half-blind, blood in their eyes, Sefa and Brannoc hauled them down into the warm dark of the Undercroft, and Tam knelt over them with his small hands pressed flat against their temples, and his eyes were full of falling snow.

"They know where we are now," Sefa said quietly, above them. "Odd will hold out a day, maybe two. Then they bring lanterns and iron and they come down here, and there are four hundred people in these tunnels, Ivo. Four hundred."

Ivo, bleeding into the dark, thought of the Door beneath the High Warden's house. Thought of the weight behind their eyes, heavier every day, humming like a struck bell.

"Then we don't wait for spring," they said. "We go for the Door. Tomorrow."
