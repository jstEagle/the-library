## 7. The God Who Remembers Being Stolen

Tam knew the voices were real because they never talked to him.

That was the first thing he'd learned, back when he still tried to tell people. The voices in his head didn't want anything from him. They didn't say *pray* or *obey* or *you are chosen*. They mostly argued about weather. One of them hummed a song that had no words and made his teeth ache in a pleasant way. Another one wept sometimes, quietly, the way you'd cry in another room so nobody would hear.

The priests at the foundling house had called it demons. The matron had called it sickness. The man with the ledger and the soft voice who came to buy children's memories had called it nothing at all — he'd just looked at Tam for a long time, the way you look at a door you thought was painted on.

So when the Wardens came to the undercity with their iron masks and their lists, Tam did what he always did: he went quiet inside and listened to what the voices said about running.

*East,* said the humming one. *No. East is where they'll expect.*

*Under,* said the weeping one. *It's always under. It was under before there was an over.*

And the third voice — the new one, the one that had arrived three nights ago riding in on Ivo's shoulder like smoke off a banked fire — said nothing at all. It just *looked*, if a voice could look, and what it looked at was Ivo.

Tam sat cross-legged on an overturned crate in the flooded chapel the forgotten poor used for sleeping, watching Ivo sleep. Sefa was on watch by the broken window, her burn-scarred hand resting on her father's confiscated Warden's blade like it might apologize to her. Snow came down through the hole in the roof, out of season, out of reason, settling on the pews where forty people breathed together under blankets.

Tam looked at Ivo too. And the silent voice finally spoke.

*That one,* it said. *Ask that one what they're carrying.*

"I'm not asking anybody anything," Tam whispered. "I don't talk."

*You talk to us.*

"That's different." He picked at the crate's rotting edge. "You live in my head. They'd call me touched. They'd put me on the list."

*We know about lists,* the voice said, and something in it was so old and so tired that Tam's chest hurt. *We invented grief, little door. Lists are younger than we are.*

He hated when they called him that. *Door.* Like he was a thing to be walked through instead of a boy who liked boiled eggs and couldn't stand the smell of tallow. But he'd learned that arguing with gods was like arguing with winter. You could do it. It wouldn't care.

Below him, Ivo shifted in sleep and murmured something — a name, maybe, or half of one — and the silent voice went very still, the way a deer goes still.

*There,* it said. *Do you hear it?*

"Hear what?"

*My name. In their head. They've been carrying my name for years and they don't even know the shape of it.*

Tam frowned. "You said your name got stolen. All of them did. That's why the valley's quiet."

*Stolen, hidden, eaten, buried.* The voice turned over the words like a man turning over stones looking for something alive underneath. *The kings of this country built a bowl and tipped every prayer out of it, and then they wrote the names into things instead of mouths. Into rings. Into songs. Into memories worth selling.* A pause, long as a held breath. *Into a thief's hoard.*

Tam's stomach went cold. He looked at Ivo again — the sharp face, the hands scarred pale from years of other people's burning summers, the way they slept curled around their satchel like a dog around a bone.

"You mean," he whispered, "Ivo's been carrying you?"

*I have been carried,* the voice corrected gently. *There's a difference. Ask them which one they'd rather be.*

* * *

Ivo woke wrong.

Tam saw it happen — saw them come up out of sleep like a diver surfacing too fast, hand already at the knife under their blanket, eyes finding the exits before they found the room. Then the eyes found Tam sitting two feet away, staring, and narrowed.

"Most people," Ivo rasped, "wait until a person's awake to stare at them."

"The voice wants to talk to you."

Ivo went very still. Across the chapel, Sefa's head came up from her watch like a hound catching scent. She crossed the room without a sound, which still unnerved Tam; she moved like her father's daughter even now, all Warden training wearing a fugitive's coat.

"Which voice," Sefa said quietly. Not a question. An accusation shaped like one.

"The quiet one," Tam said. "The one that came with Ivo." He wrapped his arms around his knees. "It says it knows its own name. And it says—" He stopped. This part felt like handing someone a knife handle-first and hoping they didn't turn it around. "It says Ivo's been carrying it. In the stolen stuff. For years."

The silence that followed had weight. Snow ticked against the broken glass. Somewhere below, in the crypt levels, a baby cried and was hushed.

Ivo laughed. It was a bad laugh, thin and cracked down the middle. "Of course it does. Of course. I steal a dying woman's summer, a drowned man's wedding, a soldier's last morning — and somewhere in the pile there's a *god*, folded up small, waiting for me to get hungry enough to lift it." They dragged both hands down their face. "Do you know how many memories I've sold? How many I've *eaten*, just to feel like I'd lived something? If one of them was—"

"If one of them was mine," the voice said through Tam's mouth.

Tam hadn't decided to let it. It was more like stepping aside in a narrow hallway. His lips moved; the words came out in his own voice but with a cadence underneath it, deep and slow, like bells heard through water. He felt Sefa flinch. He felt Ivo go white.

"I remember being taken," the god said. "That is the cruelty of this valley. Your kings could not kill us — worship is not a thing that dies, it only starves — so they took our names and scattered them where no mouth would speak them. Into stone. Into silence. Into the hoard of a memory-thief who buys and sells the lives of the dying." The bell-sound softened. "I have spent ninety years inside a hundred heads, and none of them knew they were keeping me. But you, thief. You *catalogued* me. You put me on a shelf and dusted me. You are the closest thing to a temple I have had since the Door closed."

"Don't," Ivo said. Their voice came out strangled. "Don't call me that. I'm not a temple. I'm not a *keeper*. I take things that aren't mine and I sell them and I told myself it was mercy because the dying don't need them anymore, but it was never—" They stopped. Swallowed. "What name? What's the name?"

The god was quiet a moment. When it answered, it answered through Tam's own thoughts rather than his mouth, gentle as snowfall: *Not yet. Names are doors here, and there are Wardens above us listening through iron. When we reach the Door beneath the archive, I will give it to the only hands that can carry it. Until then, thief, hold me carefully. You have been doing it for years without dropping me. That is more devotion than most priests ever managed.*

Then it stepped back out of Tam like someone leaving a room, and Tam was alone in his own body again, shaking, tasting cold metal.

Above them, faint through the chapel roof, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. Then a third time, closer.

Sefa was already moving. "Patrol horns. That's the sweep pattern — they're closing a ring, not searching a line." Her face had gone the color of the snow falling past the window. "They know the district. They know *this building*. My father taught me this pattern when I was nine."

"Then we go out the undercroft," Ivo said, and their voice was steady now, professional, the loneliness locked away behind competence — but Tam could hear the crack running through it, could see it in the way their hand pressed flat against the satchel, against the shelf of borrowed lives where a god had been sleeping all along. Thief and relic. Keeper and kept.

They woke the others. Forty souls stirred in the dark, and the forgotten poor began the old practiced shuffle toward the crypt stairs while Sefa counted heads and Ivo checked the satchel's straps twice, three times, like a rosary.

Tam fell in beside them, small feet quick on wet stone. The humming voice sang its wordless song. The weeping one wept. And far above, boots were coming down through the snow toward the flooded chapel, dozens of pairs, iron-shod, in step.

In the dark of the stairwell, Ivo glanced down at him. "Kid. When it talks through you. Does it ever say what happens to the person carrying it? At the Door?"

Tam thought about lying. He was good at silence, not lies.

"It says names are heavy," he said. "And that you've been strong enough for ninety years without knowing it."

Ivo looked at him a long moment. Then they pulled the strap tight one final time and said, "Let's hope knowing doesn't change the math," and led them all down into the dark, with the ring of horns tightening overhead like a fist closing around the whole buried city.
