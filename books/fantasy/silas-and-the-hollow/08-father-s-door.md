## 8. Father's Door

The archive had a heartbeat.

Sefa felt it through the soles of her boots as she descended the stair behind the sealed shelf of *Proclamations, Redundant*—a slow, deep pulse rising out of the stone like something enormous sleeping under the mountain and dreaming in time with the valley above. Tam had heard it first, three nights ago, standing in the snow outside the Warden's Hall with his head tilted like a bird's. *It's not the gods,* he'd signed, small hands quick in the lantern light. *It's the thing that keeps them out.*

She had thought she was prepared for what lay beneath her father's archive. She had read the ledgers—*Ledgers of the Vanished Country*, she'd named them privately, because that was what they were: the accounting of a nation erased, borders struck from maps, harbors drowned on paper, a whole people rewritten into a valley with no exits. She had memorized the names of the old kings who built it. She knew the architecture of the lie down to its foundations.

She was not prepared for the door.

It stood at the bottom of the stair where the archive's stone gave way to something older—black basalt veined with silver, carved in relief with figures whose faces had been deliberately chiseled away a century ago. All except one. Near the center of the door, untouched by the iconoclasts' hammers, a single face remained: a woman with her hands open, palms up, as if offering something, or asking.

And before the door, holding a lantern, stood her father.

High Warden Aldric Vane did not look surprised to see her. That was the first wound. He looked tired, and old in a way he had never allowed himself to look in public, and he looked at her burn-scarred hand—the one she'd wrapped in linen against the cold—and said, quietly:

"I wondered how long it would take you."

"You knew I'd come." Her voice came out flat. "You left the shelf unlocked."

"I left nothing unlocked. You picked the lock your mother taught you to pick, on the cabinet where we kept the festival wine." A ghost of a smile crossed his face and died there. "I simply stopped replacing the wax seal after the third time you came down here. I wanted to know if you'd keep digging."

"So this is a test."

"This is a door, Sefa." He turned to face it fully, and the lantern light made the silver veins in the basalt glow faintly, like embers under ash. "Everything else was just paper."

Behind her, at the top of the stair, she heard Ivo shift their weight, and Tam's small breath, quick and shallow. She'd told them to wait. She'd told them this part was hers alone. But she was glad—Ivo especially—with their thief's stillness and their thief's eyes cataloguing every exit—that they hadn't obeyed.

"The kiln district," she said. "Eleven people. I counted the names off the arrest rolls. Marta Osten. The Ferrow brothers. A girl of nine years old, Father. Nine." Her voice cracked on the number and she hated it. "You burned them alive and called it quarantine."

"I fed them," her father said.

The words didn't make sense. She stared at him. Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the city groaned under the weight of early winter—the wrong winter, the god-winter, snow falling in autumn while the Wardens' criers blamed the god-touched for every frozen well and empty granary.

"Come and see," Aldric Vane said, and pressed his scarred palm to the center of the door.

* * *

The basalt parted along seams she couldn't see, folding inward like a flower closing in reverse, and beyond it was not a chamber but a throat—a shaft of pale light descending into darkness, and rising up through that light, visible only when Sefa leaned over the rail of black stone, an ocean.

Not water. Prayers.

They rose like slow golden smoke from the valley above—she could almost trace individual threads, the desperate flicker of a farmer praying over dead fields, the steady pulse of a mother's prayer over a sick child, the great dim mass of the congregation prayers rolling out of the Wardens' own chapels every seventh day. And around each thread, as it sank past the threshold of the shaft, something *fed*. Shapes moved in the deep below—vast, coiling, patient shapes that gathered the light of the prayers the way spiders gather flies, and grew, and waited.

"What are they?" Sefa whispered.

"Wardens," her father said. "The first ones. The ones the kings bound into the foundation when they built the prison." He held the lantern out over the rail, and far below, one of the vast shapes turned toward the light with the unhurried attention of a fish rising to a lure. "Every prayer spoken in the Hollow goes to them. Every hymn, every candle lit at a shrine, every frightened whisper to a god who never answers. They eat it. And what they eat, they give to us—to the Wardens' line. Our authority. Our certainty. The way a crowd falls silent when a High Warden enters a room. It was never respect, Sefa. It's been *tithing* for a hundred years. We are the drain at the bottom of the bowl."

"And the gods?"

"Starving." He said it without flinching, and that was somehow worse than if he'd flinched. "That was the design. You can't kill a god, the old kings learned that much. But you can build a country with walls too thick for worship to escape, lock the gods outside those walls, and let a century of unanswered prayers teach the people inside that the gods are dead. Silence the gods by starving them, starve them by silencing them. Elegant. Horrible. It worked." He looked at her. "It worked for a hundred years, right up until this autumn."

"The answered prayers."

"The leak in the dam." His jaw tightened. "Something woke. Something found a crack—a thief with stolen goods in his head, a child with a hole where his voice should be. And now the Wardens below are hungry and frightened, and frightened things demand more, and the only way to feed them enough to hold the Door is—" He stopped.

"Burnings," Sefa said. "You're feeding them people."

"A god-touched person burns brighter than any chapel." He said it like a man reading a supply ledger. "Their fear, their pain, their dying faith—it's concentrated. Eleven lives have kept the Door sealed through the worst month of the century. Eleven lives against every soul in the Hollow. Do the arithmetic, Sefa. You were always better at arithmetic than I ever was."

"Don't." Her hand went to her knife before she knew she'd moved. "Don't dress it in arithmetic. Don't you dare stand there in your father's voice and call murder bookkeeping."

"It IS bookkeeping!" For the first time his composure broke, and beneath it she saw—not the marble High Warden, but the man who had taught her to pick locks, who had carried her on his shoulders through the harvest fair, who had gone gray overnight the year her mother died. "You think I don't know what they cost? You think I sleep? I have fed eleven people into that pit with my own signature on the orders, and I would feed eleven hundred, because the alternative—" he pointed down into the golden dark, where the vast shapes circled, restless now, sensing the argument above like sharks scent blood in water— "the alternative is that door opening and everything the kings feared coming true. The gods come back, and they are NOT kind, Sefa. Read the fragments we salvaged. They took worship like taxes and punished shortage like tax collectors. The kings didn't imprison monsters out of cruelty. They imprisoned gods because gods are hunger wearing a beautiful face."

"Then why," Sefa said, very quietly, "is the child upstairs hearing them sing?"

Her father went still.

"He hears them," she pressed. "Tam. The boy you've got half the garrison hunting. He doesn't hear hunger. He hears *names*. He hears them remembering themselves. Whatever's down there—" she pointed at the coiling shapes— "that's not what the gods are. That's what your kings MADE of them, walled out in the dark, eating scraps through a grate. And you've spent your whole life being the grate."

For a long moment the only sound was the slow pulse of the archive's heart, and the faint, endless whisper of prayers sinking into the deep.

"You sound like her," her father said at last. "Your mother. She found this place too, near the end. She stood where you're standing and she said almost exactly that." He closed his eyes. "I chose the Wardens. Someone had to choose, and I was stronger than she was, or crueler, and either way the valley needed a jailer more than it needed my conscience. I have never regretted it. I regret it every day. Both things are true, and neither one helps."

"Father." Her voice shook. "There's a way to open it. Isn't there. There's a reason you're telling me all this instead of calling the guard."

He opened his eyes, and what she saw in them frightened her more than the pit below: hope, worn thin as old coin.

"The Door doesn't open with keys or blood. The kings designed it to be unopenable—except by payment. It was built to run on the same currency as everything else in this valley." He looked past her, up the stair, toward where Ivo and Tam waited in the dark. "Memory. A life's hoard of it, poured into the lock, every year and every name and every borrowed thing, given freely and completely. The kings made sure no Warden could ever pay that price—we're trained from childhood to hold nothing dear, to keep our minds shallow and clean. But a memory-thief..." His gaze came back to her, and it was an apology and a surrender both. "A memory-thief has spent a lifetime filling a vault. Your friend downstairs may be the only person born in this valley in a hundred years who could afford to open that door."

"And what happens to them," Sefa said, "when the vault empties?"

"You know what happens. You've seen the hollowed ones in the poorhouse rows." He didn't soften it; he owed her that honesty, at least. "They walk out with nothing. No name. No grief. No love. An empty room where a person used to be."

Sefa looked down into the golden dark, at the vast patient hungers her family had fed for a century, and thought of Ivo's face in the lantern light—wry, guarded, hungry always for a life that wasn't borrowed—and thought of Tam's hands saying *they sing, they sing, they remember us*—and thought of her father, standing at the door he'd guarded all his life, finally showing his daughter the truth because he could no longer carry it alone.

Above them, faint through the stone, came the sound she had been dreading all night: boots. Many boots. The garrison, moving through the archive above, and a voice she knew—Warden-Captain Hesse, her father's own hound—calling down the stairwell:

"High Warden! We have them. All three. Come and see."

Her father closed his eyes. "So," he murmured. "Hesse found the sealed shelf after all. Or someone paid him to." He turned to her, and drew his sword—not toward her, but toward the stair, placing himself between the approaching boots and the Door, between a hundred years of arithmetic and the three fugitives in the dark above.

"Sefa," he said. "Whatever happens next—get them to the lock."
