## 9. Everything I Ever Took

The Door did not look like a door.

It looked like a wound in the world—a seam of pale light running floor to ceiling through the black stone beneath the archive, thin as a knife's edge, humming with a frequency I felt in my teeth. A hundred years of dust had settled against it and never crossed. Even the fog of the Hollow stopped a hand's breadth away, as if the valley itself were afraid to touch it.

"It's real," Sefa whispered behind me. She had her father's ring of keys still looped at her belt, and her burn-scarred hand pressed flat against the stone wall like she needed it to hold her up. "All those years of ledgers and lies, and it was under our feet the whole time."

"Everything in this country is under someone's feet," I said. "That's how they built it."

Tam stood closest. The boy hadn't spoken since we came down the stair—didn't need to. His silence was full. The gods' voices poured off him now like heat off a kiln, and I could feel them pressing against the inside of my skull, pressing against the fragment *I* carried, two halves of something old straining toward each other across the width of my stolen mind.

And below us, boots on stone. Many boots.

"They're coming," Sefa said. "The archive stairs won't hold them long."

"Nothing has to hold them long," I said. "Just long enough."

I knelt before the seam of light and opened myself the way I'd never once dared to do in twenty years of theft: all the way.

* * *

You have to understand what a memory-hoard is, from the inside. It isn't a bag you open. It isn't coins in a purse. Every life I ever took is still *lived*, in there, still warm, still going on in some sealed room of me where a fisherman's daughter learns to swim and a dying woman tastes summer plums and a nameless god watches the first snow fall out of season. Twenty years of other people's years, stacked like cordwood in the dark of me, because I could not bear to spend them and could not bear to give them back.

I had told myself I stole for coin. That was the lie I wore like a good coat. The truth was that I was starving, and every memory I took was a meal that never fed me, and I kept eating anyway because the alternative was to sit alone in my own one small life and feel how hollow it was.

The Hollow took the gods' names. I had spent my whole career taking everything else.

Now the lock wanted it back. All of it.

I put my hands against the light.

* * *

The first memory left like blood leaving a wound.

The grieving buyer's wife, laughing in a plum orchard, juice running down her chin—and gone, and where she had been there was only smooth empty dark, and I gasped because I had carried her eleven years and had forgotten what it felt like to have a room in me go quiet.

Then the rest came, and they did not wait politely in line.

The fisherman's daughter swimming. The mason laying his thousandth stone. The soldier deserting in the rain. The midwife's hands. The thief who taught me the trade—I'd taken his craft along with his coin, though he never knew. The child's first snowfall. The old man's last. They tore out of me in a flood, decades of borrowed living pouring through my palms into the seam, and the light drank them, and the Door began—

—to *open*.

Not wide. A finger's width. But the sound it made was the sound of a held breath released after a hundred years, and through the gap came air that smelled of rain on far-off hills, of salt, of growing things, of a world so much larger than this valley that my knees nearly went out from under me just breathing it.

"Keep going," Tam said. His voice cracked on it—he was crying without noise, tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face. "They're almost through. I can hear them lining up. *Don't stop.*"

"I'm not—" My voice came out shredded. "I'm not stopping."

But gods, it hurt. Not like fire. Like being *unmade politely*, one room at a time. Each memory that left took a piece of the scaffolding of me with it. I had built an Ivo out of stolen timber, and now the house was coming down beam by beam, and underneath it there was—what? A frightened kid from the fog-flats with one small gray life? Nothing? I didn't know. I was finding out in real time, at the worst possible hour, with Wardens hammering down the stairs.

Because they were. Torchlight bounced up the stairwell now, and voices—Sefa's father's voice among them, iron-flat, calling orders. Crossbows would follow. In the Hollow, magic couldn't reach us, but steel worked fine and always had.

"Sefa." I couldn't turn around; my hands were welded to the light. "Take Tam and go. Now. While it's wide enough."

"No."

"*Sefa.*"

"You're giving up everything you are," she said, and her voice shook but her feet didn't move. "Every life you ever took. You don't get to do that alone in the dark like you're nothing, like you're just a—a purse to be emptied. So no. I'm staying until the last one leaves you. That's not negotiable, thief."

Something in my chest that wasn't any stolen memory at all cracked clean through.

* * *

The Wardens reached the bottom of the stairs.

There were twelve of them in the torchlight, white-cloaked, crossbow bolts already nocked, and at their head, tall and terrible in his formal regalia, High Warden Aldric Vane—Sefa's father, his face lit orange from below like a man standing at the mouth of his own private hell.

"Away from the Door," he said. "All of you. Daughter, step aside. Whatever lies you've been fed, whatever the god-touched child has put in your head—step *aside*."

"You burned people," Sefa said. She turned to face him, and put herself between the crossbows and us, small and scarred and absolutely unmoving. "In the kiln district. I watched. I have your ledgers, Father. I know what this valley *is*. You didn't keep us safe. You built a cage and called it safety and starved God for a century."

"The alternative was the wars," Vane said. "The burning of the lowlands. You weren't alive for what worship does—"

"I've read your own archives. I know exactly what worship does. It makes *harvests*, Father. It makes children who sing. Step aside yourself."

"Last warning."

"Make it a good one," Sefa said, "because I'm not going to remember it fondly."

He gave the order. And Sefa Vane, disgraced daughter of the High Warden, drew her father's own ceremonial blade—the one she'd stolen off his desk along with the keys—and met twelve crossbows in a cavern at the bottom of the world, and bought us time with every second she stood.

I poured.

* * *

The last memories were the deepest, and they fought me.

The god's name came last of all—not because it was buried, but because it had grown roots. Somewhere in twenty years of hoarding, the fragment had woven itself through everything I owned, threaded through the orchard and the snowfall and the drowning soldier's last sight of the rain, until pulling it free meant pulling *everything*, until there was no line between the god in my keeping and the keeper himself.

*I know,* said a voice that was not Tam's and not mine, vast and tired and gentle, somewhere inside the leaving. *I know what it costs. I will carry the remembering of you, little thief. When we are through, somewhere in the wide world, someone will say your name kindly and neither of us will know why.*

"Don't," I said out loud, sobbing, hands burning white. "Don't waste a miracle on me. Just *go*."

The name tore loose.

The Door blew open.

* * *

Light like the first morning of the world. Sound like ten thousand bells underwater. And through the seam—through the wound that was a door that was a century of starvation finally ending—they came.

I cannot tell you what they looked like. Language was built by people who lived inside the cage; it doesn't reach that far. There was one like a stag made of riverwater. One like a woman whose shadow fell in every direction at once. One that was mostly laughter and the smell of bread. They poured past me like a flood past a stone, and where they passed, the fog of the Hollow recoiled, and the black stone of the cavern bloomed with moss a hundred years of darkness had never seen.

Tam ran into it laughing—laughing, the boy who had never made a sound—arms spread, and the stag-of-riverwater bent its impossible head and touched its nose to his forehead, and the voices that had haunted him all his life settled into a chorus singing *one* song, and he walked through the Door into the wide world like a child finally coming home.

Behind me: shouting. A bolt's crack. Sefa's cry—not a scream of pain, I knew the difference, a scream of effort—and then her father's voice breaking apart mid-command, saying her name like a question, like a prayer, like a surrender.

I turned, empty-handed, empty-headed, holding on to consciousness by my fingernails, and saw Sefa standing over three dropped crossbows with her blade still raised, and the High Warden on his knees before her with his white cloak smoking where the gods' passage had brushed it, staring up at his daughter as if seeing her for the very first time.

"Go," Sefa shouted at me over the roar of divinity. "*Go through!*"

"I can't," I said.

And it was true. I reached for the reason and found the rooms where reasons used to live, all of them swept bare. But the truth of it stood in me anyway, plain as hunger, plain as the one thing I had left:

Someone had to stay. The valley was waking up—gods returning, fog lifting, a hundred years of lies cracking open like river ice. Someone had to be here when the people stumbled out of their houses into a world that had changed overnight. Someone who knew every stolen year in this place, even if they no longer remembered owning them. Someone to keep what was lost.

The last of the light was narrowing. The gods were through. Tam's small hand waved from the far side, from green hills under an enormous sky, and then the seam began to close like an eye.

Sefa backed toward it, blade lowered, eyes on mine—asking.

"Go," I told her. "Get him safe. Then come back, if you want. If it's still here. If I'm still someone worth coming back to."

She hesitated one heartbeat—one, no more—then stepped backward through the light, and it flashed white, and the Door was gone, and the stone was only stone, warm as a sleeping animal.

The cavern was dark and quiet and smelled of moss and rain.

I sat down on the floor of the emptied country, in the place where a door used to be, and tried to remember my name.

I couldn't.

But somewhere above me, faint through a hundred feet of rock, the fog was lifting off the Hollow for the first time in a century—and far away, in a world I would never see again, someone was about to say my name kindly, and neither of us would ever know why.

* * *

Footsteps on the stairs. Slow ones. Unarmored.

A lantern came around the stairwell's edge, held in a scarred hand, and Sefa stood in its glow with ash in her hair and her father's cloak over her shoulders, looking down at me sitting in the dark like a man at the bottom of a well.

"Well," she said, and her voice broke, and she laughed anyway, and came down the last steps to sit beside me in the ruins of everything. "I suppose we start from nothing, then."

I found, to my surprise, that I was smiling.

"I suppose," I said, "we do."
