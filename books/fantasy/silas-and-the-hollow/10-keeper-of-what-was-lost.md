## 10. Keeper of What Was Lost

The first thing Ivo learned about being empty was that the valley had a smell.

They had never noticed it before, when their head was a crowded house of other people's summers and funerals and first kisses. Now there was room, and the room filled with small things: woodsmoke and wet stone, the green rot of the thawing kiln district, bread Sefa burned every third morning because she refused to admit she was bad at it. The Hollow smelled like itself. Ivo had spent thirty years borrowing other lives and had never once stopped to smell the one they were standing in.

They kept a ledger now. Not of stolen things — those days were done — but of what remained. It was a thin book. *One knife. One coat, patched at both elbows. One house above the old chandlery, roof half-collapsed but honest about it. One scar on the left palm where the god's name went out of me and into the Door.* The ledger was mostly for Sefa's benefit. She liked to read it aloud on bad nights, in her father's clipped Warden cadence, as though inventory were a kind of prayer.

Ivo did not remember why that made them laugh. They only knew that it did.

* * *

Spring came to the valley like a rumor finally confirmed.

For a hundred years the seasons had been a wheel turning in mud — the same gray winter, the same brief gray summer, the fog holding everything in its cupped hands. Now the fog was thinning. Ivo stood on the chandlery roof some mornings and watched it pull back from the hills like a sheet drawn off furniture in an abandoned house, revealing ridgelines no one alive had seen. People came out of doorways just to look. They didn't talk much while they looked. There was nothing in the Hollow's long vocabulary for *horizon*.

Below, the kiln district was becoming something else. The great brick kilns where Sefa's father had burned the god-touched still stood, black-mouthed and terrible, but around them the forgotten poor — the ones who had taken Ivo and Sefa and Tam underground through that awful winter — were building. A cobbler's stall where a barricade had been. A school in the shell of the tithe-house, taught by a one-eyed woman who had once smuggled prayer-candles and now smuggled letters of the alphabet instead. Children ran the alleys Ivo had once haunted as a thief, and they ran them loudly, unafraid, and every time Ivo heard them they felt a feeling they could not name and did not need to. Some things you didn't have to remember to keep.

"You're brooding," Sefa said from the ladder.

"I'm supervising."

"You're sitting on a roof doing nothing, which is brooding with better posture." She hauled herself up beside them, breath steaming, a hammer hanging from her belt like a badge of office. She'd traded her father's white-and-silver for carpenter's leathers, and she wore them the way she'd worn everything: like a challenge. "The tithe-house roof won't patch itself. And Marta says if we don't finish the schoolhouse by planting festival, she's teaching the children in our kitchen, and I *will not* have eleven children and flour in my hair again."

"Our kitchen," Ivo repeated.

Sefa went pink to the ears. She was still, after all these months, adjusting the pronouns of her life — *our*, *stay*, *home* — words that had been confiscated from her the day she watched her father light the kilns. "You know what I mean."

"I don't," Ivo said, and grinned. "Say it again."

She hit them with the hammer's handle, gently, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking. Below them the valley went on rebuilding itself, plank by plank, and the fog drew back another mile, and somewhere far away — farther than maps allowed — a boy was writing them a letter.

* * *

Tam's letters came four times a year, carried by traders bold enough to walk the pass beyond the Door, and each one arrived smelling faintly of a world Ivo would never see. Cedar, maybe. Or rain on a different kind of stone. The traders said that past the mountains the gods walked openly now — that there were temples with the doors flung wide, that a river goddess had been seen standing hip-deep in the shallows of a city called Vael, laughing, letting children braid ribbons into her hair.

Ivo couldn't picture it. That was the strange mercy of the emptiness: they had poured everything into the Door — every stolen summer, every borrowed grief, every name hoarded over thirty years of theft, including the one name that mattered, the god's own name, which had lived inside them without their knowing like a seed in a pocket. They remembered none of it. They remembered the giving up, the way you remember the moment before sleep. Everything after was other people's stories told back to them, and they had learned to hold those stories lightly, the way you hold water.

But Tam wrote it all down so they wouldn't have to carry it.

*I saw the sea,* said the letter pinned to the chandlery beam, the ink gone soft at the folds from handling. *It is bigger than anyone says. It does not end. I stood in it up to my knees and the god who remembers being stolen stood beside me and cried, and he said salt water is the oldest prayer there is. I am learning to speak out loud now. My voice sounds strange. Sefa will laugh when she reads this but my voice sounds like someone else's and I think maybe that is what growing up is.*

He always signed it the same way: *Your brother, Tam.* Not asked. Declared. As though family were a fact of geology rather than blood, and he had simply surveyed the ground and found it so.

Sefa read each letter aloud twice — once fast, hungry, and once slow, savoring — and then Ivo copied them into the ledger, in their careful thief's hand, because paper rotted and ledgers lasted. It was the closest thing to keeping a memory that Ivo permitted themselves. Other people's words, written down honestly, given freely. Nothing taken. Everything kept.

* * *

On the anniversary of the Door — the night the Wardens stormed the cavern and the gods poured through the opening like light through a cracked shutter — the valley held a vigil in the kiln district.

They didn't call it that. The Hollow was still learning to say holy words without flinching; a century of silence leaves scar tissue even on a language. They called it *the Lighting*, and it was simple: everyone brought a candle to the mouth of the great kiln where Sefa's father had burned the god-touched, and they lit them, and they stood together until the candles burned down. No speeches. No prayers, exactly. Just fire used for remembering instead of erasing.

Sefa lit hers first, as she did every year, and held it toward the kiln's black throat, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say to a father who had chosen power over his daughter except *I survived you*, and she said that with her whole body, every year, standing straight as a blade.

Ivo lit theirs last. Their candle was always the plainest — no ribbon, no carved beeswax flower, just tallow and string — and every year the same thought moved through them like weather: *somewhere in the lock of that Door is everything I ever took, and it is burning bright, and it is finally mine because I gave it away.*

This year, though, something else happened.

A woman approached them through the crowd — old, wrapped in gray wool, face seamed like a dry riverbed. Ivo knew her distantly. One of the underground. She had fed them stew in the dark months and never asked questions.

"You're the keeper," she said.

"I'm a roofer," Ivo said.

"The Door-keeper." She said it patiently, the way you correct a child who doesn't know their own name. "The traders brought word up the pass. Word travels, Ivo Villalobos. Out there, they tell it different than we do. Out there they say a thief opened the way with everything they'd ever stolen, and the gods walked out into the world, and one stayed behind to watch the Door." Her eyes were wet in the candlelight. "We decided you should hear how it sounds from the outside."

Ivo stood very still. Around them the candles guttered, hundreds of small flames reflected in the wet cobblestones, and the fog had pulled so far back this year that stars were visible over the kilns — actual stars, which half the children present had never seen.

"What do they call me?" Ivo asked. "Out there?"

"Nothing yet," the woman said. "Names take time. But they're waiting for one." She touched Ivo's scarred palm, briefly, like a benediction or a dare. "And in here—" she tapped her own temple, "—in here, the gods left something behind when they went through. A door swings both ways, keeper. Ask yourself sometime why the fog is lifting. Ask yourself why the valley smells like spring for the first time in a hundred years. Something is coming back through. Slowly. Like a tide. And somebody has to be standing here when it arrives."

She shuffled away into the crowd, leaving Ivo holding their plain candle and a question that fit perfectly, like a key, into all that beautiful emptiness.

Sefa appeared at their shoulder. "What did she want?"

Ivo watched the flames. Watched the stars. Felt, in the hollow place where a life used to be, something faint and warm begin to gather — not a memory, not yet, but the *shape* of one, the way you feel rain before it falls.

"A job," Ivo said. "The best kind. The kind where nobody has to steal anything."

Above the kilns, the stars thickened, and far beneath the archive, deep under stone, something old turned over in its sleep — and began, softly, to hum a name.
