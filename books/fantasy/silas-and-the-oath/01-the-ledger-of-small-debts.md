## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The ink was the first thing to go wrong.

Quentin noticed it at the bottom of a page she'd been copying for an hour—a ledger entry, routine as breathing: *Marta Ellowen, two measures of barley, owed against the harvest.* The words had been dry for minutes. Now they glistened. She touched one with her fingertip and it smeared, wet as if freshly written, though her pen had been nowhere near it.

She sat back in the scriptorium's last lit corner and looked at the candle. It burned steady. The room was cold but not damp. Nothing explained the ink.

Around her, the temple of Silas slept the way it always did: forty scribes' desks, thirty-nine empty, the smell of tallow and old vellum, the soft tick of the great clock over the door that had kept valley time for longer than anyone could remember. Quentin had been here since she was seven years old, when her mother signed her over against a debt of eleven silver marks. She was twenty-six now, and by her own careful arithmetic she still owed nine of them. The interest was gentle but eternal. That was how debts worked in Silas's Rest. They never ended. They were never meant to.

She blotted the smear, told herself it was nothing—cheap ink, a change in the air—and went back to work.

That was when the stars went out.

She didn't see it happen. She felt it, the way you feel someone enter a room behind you. A pressure on the back of the neck. She looked up through the high window, past the frost-ferns on the glass, into a sky that should have been scattered with the winter constellations the valley children learned by name—the Ploughwife, the Nine Sisters, old Silas's own Lantern hanging low over the western ridge.

Black. All of it. Not clouded—clouds had edges, clouds moved. This was a clean and total absence, like a page torn out of the sky.

Quentin stood so fast her stool went over backward. The crash of it echoed down the dormitory corridor and no one stirred, which was wrong too. Forty people slept in this building. Someone always woke. Someone always grumbled.

"Hello?" Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

The clock over the door stopped ticking.

In nineteen years she had never heard silence from that clock. It was the sound under all other sounds, the heartbeat of the temple, and now it simply wasn't, and the silence it left behind was enormous, alive, listening.

* * *

She did what scribes do. She went looking for the record.

The archive lay below the scriptorium, down a stair worn into a smooth trough by generations of feet, past shelves of ledgers going back two hundred years. Every debt in the valley lived down here, every measure of barley and hour of labor and silver mark, copied and recopied in hands long dead. Quentin had spent half her life among them. She knew their smells the way other people knew their mothers'.

The oldest ledger sat chained to its lectern at the far end—the Ledger of Small Debts, the novices called it, because its entries were humble things. A coat mended. A prayer said. A child's name entered against a family's account. She unhooked the chain with the key every senior scribe carried and opened it to the first page.

The handwriting was beautiful. That was the first thing that snagged in her. Debts were recorded plainly, functionally; nobody wasted flourishes on barley. But this hand curved and sang across the page, and the entries beneath the beauty were strange:

*Let the river keep its course. Entered against the account of the valley entire.*

*Let the wards hold at the border stones. Entered against the account of the valley entire.*

*Let the stars remain where we set them. Entered against the account of the valley entire.*

Quentin read that line three times. *Where we set them.*

Her mouth had gone dry. She turned pages faster now, and the pattern emerged like a figure rising out of fog. Entry after entry, centuries of them—not debts incurred but decrees made, each one phrased as an obligation because an obligation required a debtor, and the debtor was always the same: *the valley entire.* The valley owed its rivers their courses. The valley owed the sky its stars. The valley owed the wards their holding, night after night, year after year, and the ledger kept the account current.

These weren't records of debt.

They were bindings. The whole valley, stitched to the world by ink and obligation, held in place like a specimen pinned to a board.

And the wards had failed tonight. And the stars were gone.

She was reaching for the current page—there would be answers there, there had to be—when the candle guttered though no draft moved, and a voice spoke from the dark of the stacks behind her.

"You shouldn't be reading that."

Master Corvane stood between the shelves, and Quentin's heart did something complicated, because Corvane was the head of the scriptorium, the man who had taught her her letters, who had praised her neatness for twenty years. He was dressed. At midnight. And he was holding a lantern he hadn't lit.

"The wards are down," she said. "The stars are gone. Sir, the ledger says—"

"I know what the ledger says." His voice was calm in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have. "Which is why you will close it, return to your desk, and copy nothing further tonight. Tomorrow everything will be restored. There is a procedure."

"A procedure," Quentin repeated. "For the sky?"

"For the valley." He stepped closer, and the light from her single candle caught his face, and she saw something she had never seen in nineteen years of knowing him: fear, carefully filed away, like a debt deferred. "Quentin. You are a fine scribe. The finest I've trained. Which is precisely why you must not read what you are not contracted to read. Some accounts are above your station."

"Whose station are they above?" she asked. "Yours?"

He didn't answer, and the silence answered for him.

She turned back to the ledger, to the final written page, the most recent entry, and Corvane lunged—but she was faster, twenty years of quick hands, and she read it before his fingers closed on the vellum:

*Let the binding be renewed in the hand of the anchor. Entered against the account of:*

And then her own name.

*Quentin Blackwood.*

Not Marta Ellowen. Not the valley entire. Hers, in that beautiful singing hand, dated the day she was born—the day, she realized with a lurch like falling, that her mother had brought her to the temple. Eleven silver marks. A child entered against an account. She had thought all her life that she was paying her mother's debt.

She had been the collateral. She had been the *anchor*, whatever that meant, chosen before she could walk, raised to invisibility and diligence and obedience by men who knew exactly what she was for.

Corvane's hand closed over hers on the page. His grip was trembling.

"It doesn't have to be terrible," he said quietly. "The renewal is a formality. You'd live your life as you've lived it. You'd simply... matter more than you knew."

"Let go of me."

"Quentin—"

"*Let go.*"

He let go. And in the space between them, in the dead silence where the clock's heart used to be, Quentin understood that she had perhaps an hour before the procedure arrived, whatever it was, and that the only thing in this valley worth stealing was chained to a lectern in front of her.

She broke the chain at the clasp—it was old iron, older than the temple's pride—and wrapped the Ledger of Small Debts in her scribe's cloak.

"What are you doing?" Corvane whispered.

"What I'm contracted to do," she said, and for the first time in her life the word tasted like hers. "Keeping the accounts."

She ran, past him, up the stairs, out into a starless night where the border stones, she knew, were already failing—and where, if the oldest maps in the archive were honest, there had once been a road leading somewhere the ledgers swore did not exist.

Somewhere beyond the valley, someone had erased a country.

Quentin meant to find out what remained of it.
