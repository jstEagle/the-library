## 4. A Country With No Name Yet

The first star came back on a Tuesday.

I know because I was in the temple courtyard arguing with Vess about whether the well water had started tasting of copper or if she was just being difficult, and old Marta from the bakery looked up mid-sentence and said, "There," the way you'd point out a bird that hadn't been seen in years. One star, low over the eastern ridge, small and stubborn and real.

Nobody cheered. That surprised me most. We all just stood there in the cold with our breath hanging in front of us, looking up like we'd forgotten how, and after a while Vess said, "Huh," and went back to complaining about the water. But her hand found my shoulder on the way past and stayed there a moment longer than it needed to.

That was how everything happened afterward. Gently. As if the valley itself had learned something about not grabbing.

* * *

Magic came back the way frost comes—slowly, in corners first, so you could pretend not to notice until the whole morning was silvered with it.

The miller's daughter made light without a lamp while telling us, embarrassed, that she'd only been trying to remember where she'd left her shoes. The orchard trees fruited twice that autumn, and no one could explain it, and no one tried very hard. A hedge-witch who'd spent forty years pretending to be merely eccentric began leaving charms on doorsteps again, and this time nobody reported her to the temple, because there was no temple to report her to—not as it had been. The building still stood. We used it for dances now, and for arguments about drainage, which is what most governments are anyway.

I won't pretend there wasn't fear. There were nights when someone's new gift went wrong—a boy who froze his own hands, a woman whose grief came out of her as weather—and people looked at each other across those fires and asked the question none of us had an answer for: *what are we now?*

Vess answered it best, though she'd have denied answering anything. She said: "We're whatever we decide before anyone else decides for us." Then she stole a roll off my plate, because even prophets get hungry.

* * *

The ledgers burned on the first anniversary of the night the stars went dark.

Not ceremonially—I want to be honest about that. It wasn't a ritual. It was a bonfire, and there was cider, and half the valley turned up mostly for the warmth. But when the master ledger finally caught—the old vellum reluctant, the ink flaring green as the bindings let go one last time—everyone went quiet despite themselves. You don't watch your chains melt without feeling something, even if what you feel is mostly vertigo.

I watched my own name go. Quentin Blackwood, debt-bound, entered in a dead man's hand thirty years before I could write my own name. The letters curled black and lifted into the smoke, and I felt nothing leave me, because nothing had ever been inside me except the lie of it.

What surprised me was what I did next. I took the blank ledger—the one I'd bound myself, good paper, sewn spine—and I sat down by the fire where everyone could see, and I wrote the first line:

*Quentin Blackwood, here by choice.*

Then I passed the book to Vess, who rolled her eyes, took the pen, and wrote *Vess Carrow, here under protest,* which everyone knew was a lie, including Vess.

Marta wrote next. Then the miller. Then the boy with frozen hands, spelling his name wrong and crossing it out and writing it again, and I told him the crossed-out version was better and he should keep both. By midnight the fire had burned down to embers and the ledger had forty-one names in it, and someone had drawn a dog at the bottom, and the dog stayed.

* * *

We argued for three days about what to call ourselves.

Not the valley—we kept Silas's Rest, because names are history and we weren't erasing anything ever again. But the country. The country beyond the border that had been erased, the space where a whole nation used to be, the emptiness we'd walked into together through a door that no longer existed. Maps called it nothing. Officially, everywhere else, it was still nothing.

"Call it Freedom," someone suggested, and got booed.

"The Unbound Marches," said another, and Vess said that sounded like a disease.

I kept quiet through most of it, because I'd learned something in my years of copying other people's words: naming is a debt too, if you do it alone. Whatever we called ourselves would bind us, gently or otherwise. It should come from all of us or not at all.

In the end it was the hedge-witch, old Sef, who settled it. She stood up at the winter meeting in the former temple, looked around at the crowd, and said: "It hasn't earned a name yet. Neither have we. Let's be a country that's still becoming. Write down *A Country With No Name Yet*, and when we've grown into something worth calling, we'll call it."

So that's what the second page says. Underneath it, in smaller letters, someone added: *provisionally excellent.*

* * *

Here is what I know now, and I know it the way you know a scar—by touch, in the dark, without looking.

Belonging isn't given. It isn't owed, either—that was the great lie of the ledgers, that we were bound to each other by what we lacked. Debt says: *you are less than what you receive.* But a name freely written says: *I am here, and I choose this, and choosing costs me nothing but staying.*

The stars come back one at a time. There are eleven now. Children born since the unbinding think that's normal, eleven stars, and argue about which one is best. The magic keeps arriving like guests who heard the party late—awkward, wonderful, occasionally setting the curtains on fire. Vess has stopped pretending she's passing through; her maps hang on the wall of the house we share, and lately she's been drawing roads that don't lead anywhere yet, just in case.

And every evening, whoever comes to the temple—which is to say, the town hall, which is to say, the biggest room with the best acoustics—writes their name in the book. Not because they must. Because it feels good to say it. Some nights there are two names. Some nights there are twenty. Last week a traveler from beyond the western passes signed it, read the whole thing standing there by candlelight, and then asked, very quietly, if there was room for one more.

There is always room for one more. That's the first law. We wrote it down.

---

*This record is kept by many hands now. Quentin Blackwood began it, but a beginning is only a loan against what comes after, and we pay our debts differently here—we pay them forward. If you are reading this, the valley held. The stars returned. The names below were given freely, and freely meant.*

*If you are reading this and your name is not yet written: the pen is on the table. The fire is lit. Come in from the dark.*

*We were nobody's. Now we are each other's.*

*A Country With No Name Yet — page one hundred and six, and counting.*
