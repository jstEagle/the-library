## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The vault under the Salt Guildhouse had no door that a thief could pick and no darkness that a lamp could argue with. It had a tide.

Twice a day the sea came up through channels cut into the living rock, filled the antechamber to the height of a man's chest, and withdrew again, and in the hour between high water and low the vault's inner shelves stood dry and open to whoever held the guild's key. Everyone else got the sea. Ines Reyes had watched a master smuggler from Karst try to beat the schedule once, years ago, with a stolen key and a diver's lungs. The ledger still recorded what they recovered of him: one boot, one knife, and eleven seconds of borrowed courage, returned to inventory slightly damp.

She unlocked the counting room at half past six, as she did every morning, hung her coat on the peg shaped like a leaping fish, and lit the lamps over the long slate table where the day's entries would be reconciled. Then she stood for a moment with her hand flat on the table's cold edge and listened to the silence inside her own chest.

Still nothing.

Nine months now. Two hundred and seventy-one days since the oath had last spoken — she kept the count the way other people kept scars — and every morning she checked anyway, the way you touch a tooth that aches, hoping the ache has moved on overnight. *Speak*, she thought, not quite a prayer. Nothing answered. There was only the ordinary quiet of a woman standing alone in a room, and beneath it, faint but constant, the sound of the sea working at the rock like a creditor at a locked door.

"Ledger-keeper," said the doorwarden through the speaking-tube, "the morning carts are in."

"Send them down when the tide turns," Ines said. "I'll be in the deep shelves."

* * *

The Salt Guild traded in impossible things, and the impossible things required impossible bookkeeping.

There were three ledgers for every item in the vault. The white ledger recorded what the thing was. The grey ledger recorded what it cost. The black ledger recorded what it was *worth*, which was never the same as either, and which Ines was not supposed to read without a master present. She read them all. That was the advantage of being the last swordspeaker in the guild's employ: everyone assumed the oath made her incorruptible, and no one checked the work of a woman who could, in theory, cut a lie out of the air before it finished leaving your mouth.

If only the oath were still listening.

She started the day's audit in Shelf Nine, the weather. Bottled storms sat in their racks like wasps' nests made of glass, each one tagged with wax seals and a slip of vellum in her own careful hand. She lifted the first jar into the light and turned it slowly. Inside, a small grey tempest turned with it, rain falling upward, lightning no bigger than a fingernail flickering against the glass.

"Bottled squall, Vhal coast vintage," she recited, writing as she spoke. "Harvested spring before last off the drowned road. Purchased by the harbor-master of Greywater against the storm season. Condition: restless."

Restless was the word the old ledger-keepers used, and Ines had come to believe they chose it deliberately. The things in the vault were not objects. They were *held*. A bottled storm was a storm being prevented, the way a debt unpaid was a promise being broken, and everything held strained, eventually, toward being let go.

The next shelf down was worse. Shelf Ten held time.

Borrowed years came in flasks of blue salt-glass, each one faintly warm, each one humming at the very bottom of hearing — or perhaps that hum was only Ines's imagination, only the ghost of an oath that used to sing in her blood whenever she came near the vault's deeper magic. She had been able to hear the years once. Now she had to trust the labels.

"Fourteen flasks, borrowed years, mixed provenance." Her pen scratched. "Three due for return this quarter per contract 8811. One—" she paused, checked the grey ledger, felt the familiar sourness rise in her throat— "one purchased outright from a debtor's estate. Seller deceased. No return clause."

Purchased outright. As if a year of someone's life were a chair.

The guild's founding covenant said otherwise, of course. The covenant — the actual document, inked by Silas himself two hundred years ago, kept in a case of black iron behind the masters' dais — said that nothing sold by the Salt Guild could be truly owned, only stewarded, because everything impossible belonged in the end to the world it came from. Borrowed years went home to their borrowers. Bottled storms went back to the sky. Names sold at auction reverted to their bearers at death, free of charge, as a courtesy between the living and the dead.

That was the theory. Ines had spent six years auditing the gap between the theory and the shelves, and the gap grew wider every season. Somewhere below Shelf Twelve there was a rack of names whose owners had died decades ago and whose reversion slips had never been filed. Somewhere in the cold annex there were forty years of borrowed childhood belonging to a girl in the Mirefen who would be an old woman before the guild found a reason to give them back.

Ines wrote the numbers down. That was her job. Write the numbers down, reconcile the columns, flag the discrepancies, and trust that somewhere above her the masters read the flags and cared.

She had stopped believing they cared around the same week the oath went silent. She had never told anyone these two facts were connected in her mind. She wasn't sure they were connected anywhere except her mind. But some nights, doing sums by lamplight, she felt the two silences lean against each other inside her like conspirators.

* * *

Midmorning brought the trades.

The counting room filled and emptied in its rhythm: a weather-broker from upcoast buying three fair-weather days for his daughter's wedding; a widow selling back four hours of her husband's last evening because she could no longer afford to keep them — the guild charged storage on memories, too, a fee Ines had argued against at three consecutive convocations and lost three times; a nervous young man from the inland colleges wanting to know if the guild bought unused courage, and at what rate.

"Depends how unused," Ines told him, and he laughed uncertainly, and she wrote him a referral to the appraisal desk and watched him go with something that was almost envy. He still believed his unspent bravery was worth money. He had not yet learned the guild's oldest lesson, the one printed nowhere and taught everywhere: that everything you sell here, you sell cheaper than it will cost you later.

At noon the doorwarden's voice came through the tube. "Ledger-keeper. Master Corvane requests the quarterly reconciliation on his desk by Friday. He says—" a pause, the sound of paper turning— "he says he wants the black columns included this time."

Ines looked at the speaking-tube for a moment.

"The black columns are sealed to masters' eyes," she said. "That's covenant law, section nine."

"He knows. He says to tell you the seal is lifted. By order of the full convocation." Another pause. "He says, and I'm reading this exactly: 'Tell Reyes it's time she saw the whole account.'"

The tube clicked off. Ines sat with her pen hovering over the white ledger, a drop of ink gathering at its nib like a slow black star.

Corvane wanted her reading the black ledgers. Corvane, who for six years had kept those ledgers behind doors even the other masters didn't open, who smiled at her across the counting floor with the warmth of a man watching a well-behaved dog. Either the convocation had finally grown a conscience, or Master Corvane needed the guild's true accounts seen by exactly one person — a person bound by an oath of truth, a person whose signature on a reconciliation would make the numbers *real* in the way only a swordspeaker's word could make things real.

A person, said a small cold voice in her, who hasn't actually been able to swear anything for nine months.

She dipped the pen and wrote, in the margin of the day's page, a single private notation in the shorthand only she used: *Something owed is coming due.*

Then she went down to the deep shelves, because the tide had turned, and the work did not wait, and competent routine was the only armor she had left.

* * *

The afternoon's inventory took her past the case she always avoided.

It stood alone on Shelf Fifteen, apart from the weather and the years and the names: a single object under a bell of clear salt-glass, and it was nothing much to look at. A stone the size of a fist, sea-worn, veined with green, dull as any beach pebble. The label read, in handwriting two centuries dead:

*COVENANT FRAGMENT. ORIGIN: THE FIRST WARD. STATUS: INERT. DO NOT SELL.*

Every ledger-keeper before her had copied that status forward, quarter after quarter, century after century. *Inert.* Ines had copied it too, for six years. But she had also, in her first month, made the mistake of resting her bare hand on the glass while the vault tide ran high, and the stone had warmed under her palm like a sleeping animal noticing a familiar step, and her oath — her silent, inherited, blood-bound oath, the words her grandmother had spoken into her at seven years old with salt and fire — had stirred once, deeply, like a whale turning over in dark water, and then gone still again.

Two weeks later it had gone silent altogether.

She had never told anyone about the warming. She had spent nine months telling herself the two events were coincidence, that oaths went quiet sometimes, that grief and overwork dulled the inner ear. But she had stopped touching the case, all the same. You don't keep knocking on a door after something behind it asks you, in its sleep, to stop.

Now she stood in front of it with her tally-book against her hip and made herself look, the way she made herself check the silence every morning.

The stone lay in its cradle of dried kelp. Dull. Green-veined. Ordinary.

Except that today, in the lamplight, the veins looked brighter than she remembered. Fainter than a glow — more like the memory of a glow, like coals an hour after the fire. And the vault tide, which should have been halfway through its withdrawal by now, was running late. The water in the antechamber channel was still high. Still rising, if she was honest about the sound of it, and Ines Reyes had spent her whole life learning to be honest about the sound of water.

"Status: inert," she said aloud, to the empty aisle, and heard how it sounded. Like a lie entered into a true book.

Her pen hovered. Then she wrote the status forward, as every keeper before her had done, and added beneath it, in her own hand, the smallest flag the guild's format allowed: *Re-inspect at next tide.*

Somewhere above her, muffled through fathoms of rock, a bell began to ring. Not the tide-bell. Not the trade-bell. The third bell, the one that existed for emergencies so rare that most guild employees went their whole careers without hearing it, and Ines stood very still in the aisle between the bottled storms and the borrowed years while its sound came down the stairwells like cold air.

The doorwarden's voice came through the shelf-speaker, and for the first time in six years it was not bored.

"Ledger-keeper — all staff to the great hall. All staff, immediately." A crackle, and then, quieter, meant for no one: "The stars are gone. Every one of them. The whole sky's gone dark."

Out on Shelf Fifteen, behind two hundred years of salt-glass, the first covenant fragment began, very softly, to shine.
