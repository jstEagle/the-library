## 2. A Night Without Stars

The trouble with impossible things, Ines Reyes had learned in her first year at the Salt Guild, was that they kept books worse than anything else in the world.

Borrowed years came back short. Bottled storms sweated their weather through the glass and fogged the neighboring shelves until the whole vault smelled of rain that belonged to someone else. Names—sold names, pawned names, the ones people sold when they had nothing else left to sell—drifted. You'd shelve a name on Tuesday and by Friday it would be three rows over, whispering itself to the dark like a child calling for its mother.

So she counted them. Every night, or near enough. That was the work nobody else wanted: the audit of things that did not want to be counted.

She stood now in the third sub-vault with her ledger open across her forearm and her stylus moving in quick, clean strokes. *One storm, bottled, minor, provenance the Amber Coast, seal intact.* *Fourteen years, borrowed against a miller's second son, interest accruing.* *One name, unclaimed, shelf nine, humming.*

The lamp she carried burned steady. Outside, the night was doing what nights did—being dark, being ordinary—and Ines found a strange comfort in that. The world above the guildhouse could rot and riot all it liked. Down here, among the impossible things, there was only arithmetic. Arithmetic never lied. Arithmetic never went silent.

Her stylus paused over the page.

*Arithmetic never goes silent,* she thought again, and hated herself for it, because it was not arithmetic that had gone silent. It was the oath.

Nine months now. Nine months since the words her grandmother had spoken into her blood at fourteen—the old swordspeaker's covenant, the salt-and-iron litany every Reyes carried in their marrow—had simply stopped answering. She still said it. Every dawn, alone in her room, hand flat against the floorboards, she spoke the words into the stone the way you'd speak into a well you suspected had gone dry. And every dawn the stone gave back nothing. No warmth. No hum beneath her ribs. Just the ordinary sound of her own voice in an ordinary room, belonging to an ordinary woman who happened to be the last of a line that had once anchored the border between two worlds.

The Guild believed the oath slept in her, dormant, waiting. Master Corvane had told her so himself, kindly, the way you tell a soldier the wound will heal. *Blood is patient, Ines. It keeps its own time.* She had almost believed him. She had very much wanted to believe him, because the alternative—that the oath hadn't gone dormant but gone out, snuffed like a lamp in wind—was a thing she could not write in any ledger and survive writing it.

She finished the count. Sealed the ledger with wax and her thumbprint. Climbed the long spiral stair from the vaults with the lamp swinging shadows around her, and stepped out onto the gallery walk of the guildhouse tower to check the sky, because checking the sky was the other half of her job. The wards along the coast answered to the stars. Everyone knew that the way everyone knew water was wet; nobody thought about it unless something went wrong.

The sky was clear. Cold. Salt-smelling, as always this close to the water.

And empty.

Ines stopped walking.

Not cloudy-empty. Not moonless-empty. She knew those empties the way she knew her own handwriting. This was different. This was the emptiness of a page where writing had been and then was not. She scanned from the Towermark in the north to the Salt Crown low over the southern water, the constellations she had navigated by, audited by, measured borrowed years against—gone. Not dimmed. Not veiled. *Gone*, as if a hand had come down through the dark and wiped the sky clean.

"Stars," she said aloud, stupidly, as if naming them might call them back. Her breath hung white in the cold.

Below her, the harbor lay black. Not lamplit black—she could see the lanterns along the quay, the watch-fires, the fishing boats riding at anchor with their little lights. But beyond the breakwater, past the line of the old ward-stones that ran out into the sea like a row of broken teeth, the water was dark in a way water should not be. It wasn't reflecting anything. There was nothing left to reflect.

Then the horizon turned green.

It began as a smudge, a stain of color low in the east where the drowned kingdom lay under forty fathoms, and Ines's first thought—her absurd, professional first thought—was that some trader had broken a bottle of aurora on the docks and the light was escaping. Then the green climbed. It climbed the way fire climbs oil, fast and hungry, running west along the coast in a burning seam, and Ines understood with a lurch that she was watching the wards go down. All of them. The great chain of anchored covenants that had stood between the human shore and Vhal's drowned realm for four hundred years, lighting up as they died, each one flaring its last strength into the air like a scream.

She gripped the gallery rail. The iron bit cold into her palms.

*Anchor,* she thought. *The covenant is the anchor. The anchor is—*

The thought broke apart, because the tower hummed.

Not the tower. The stone. The deep foundations of the guildhouse itself, the bedrock the whole Salt Guild stood on, and beneath the hum—a note she felt in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the silent place behind her sternum where her oath used to live—something *shifted*. Like a lock turning. Like a debt called due.

"No," she said quietly. "No, no—"

The green light reached the harbor line. Out past the breakwater, the old ward-stones began to sing—a high, thin, glassy keening that rose over the water and set the gulls screaming off the rooftops—and then, one after another, in a rippling line from east to west, they shattered. Not crumbled. *Shattered*, bursting into shards that caught the green light and threw it back, so that for one terrible beautiful moment the whole coast glittered like a necklace breaking, and the sound rolled over the city like the sky cracking open.

Ines had heard glass break before. She had never heard it mean the end of the world.

The keening faded. The green light guttered, sank, and died along the waterline, and what remained was darkness—true darkness, oceanic, pressing in from the sea like something leaning its weight against a door that no longer existed. The harbor lights looked small and foolish. Somewhere below, a church bell began to ring, and then another, and then all of them, the whole city waking into panic.

Ines became aware that she was shaking. She made her hands stop. Hands were for ledgers. Hands were for work. Whatever had just happened, whatever was happening, there would be records to keep and counts to take, and if she held on to that—if she held on to the counting—she would not have to feel how small the rail felt under her fingers, how vast the dark felt beyond it.

She turned for the stair and nearly walked into Master Corvane.

He filled the doorway to the gallery, silver-haired, immaculate even at midnight, his gray eyes catching the last dying reflection of the green. Behind him clustered three of the senior factors, pale as candle-wax, and behind them the stairwell echoed with running feet and shouted questions. Corvane ignored all of it. He was looking past Ines at the shattered wards, at the starless sea, and his face wore an expression she had never seen on him before and would spend years trying to forget.

He looked *satisfied*.

"Master Reyes." His voice was calm. Calmer than the bells. Calmer than the screaming gulls. "How much of the coastal ledger is current?"

She blinked. "Current? Master, the wards—"

"I know about the wards." He stepped past her to the rail and surveyed the dark water the way a merchant surveys a warehouse he has just acquired. "I am asking about your ledgers."

"The coastal series is audited through tonight. Everything's sealed and shelved except—" She stopped. He hadn't asked. But he was waiting, with the particular patience of a man who already knows the answer, and Ines felt the hairs rise on her arms. "Except the founding covenant archive. Sub-vault one. It hasn't been opened in sixty years."

"Hasn't it." Something moved behind his eyes, quick and cold as a fish turning. "Then here are your orders, and I'll have them only once. As of this moment, the archives are sealed. All of them. Every vault, every ledger, every record from the founding forward. Wax, chain, and key. Nothing leaves the building—not a scrap, not a whisper of a scrap. The factors will tell the city we are assessing damages. We are not assessing damages. We are assessing *exposure*."

"Exposure," Ines repeated. The word tasted strange. "Master, people are going to drown tonight. The villages on the coast, the tide-towns—the wards were the only thing standing between them and—"

"And the Guild will do what the Guild has always done, which is trade in protection." Corvane turned from the rail, and now his composure was fully reassembled, smooth as poured wax, and somehow that was worse than the satisfaction. "There will be fear in the morning, Master Reyes, and fear is the oldest currency we deal in. Our position must be exact. Which means our records must be exact. Which means—" he smiled, thin and courteous, "—no one reads them but me. Including you."

"Including me." She heard her own voice go flat. Sixteen years in the Guild. Keeper of the ledgers. Oath-bound—or formerly oath-bound—of the founding line. "Master, I keep those books. I've kept them since I was nineteen."

"You did." He descended one step toward her, and lowered his voice, and the kindness in it was the most frightening thing yet. "You are tired, Ines. Everyone can see it. The audits, the night-counting, carrying your grandmother's burdens alone. Go to your rooms. Sleep. In the morning this will look different, I promise you. In the morning, the Guild will need its best sword more than ever—but tonight, the books belong to me."

Behind him, the senior factors were already moving, keys jangling, chains rattling, sealing the arteries of the building one by one. Through the doorway Ines could see down into the courtyard, where clerks spilled out of doors in their nightclothes, faces tipped up toward the empty sky, mouths open, pointing at nothing. A boy was crying. An old factor knelt on the stones with his hands pressed together, praying to stars that weren't there anymore.

The ordinary world had ended an hour ago, and Master Corvane was worried about filing.

"Yes, Master," Ines heard herself say. "Of course, Master."

He studied her a moment longer—long enough that she understood he didn't believe her either, and didn't care, because what could she do?—and then swept away down the stairs, trailing factors like a wake.

Ines stood alone on the gallery. Below her the city rang and panicked. Beyond the breakwater the sea lay black and unguarded, and somewhere under that blackness, forty fathoms down, the drowned kingdom waited with whatever had swallowed the sky.

She should sleep. She should obey. She should be a good keeper of ledgers and let the masters manage the crisis, because that was the order of things, because it had always been the order of things.

But her hand had gone, without her deciding it, to the place behind her sternum. To the silence where the oath lived. And for the first time in nine months, the silence felt less like a wound than like a warning—as if the part of her bound to the old covenant was trying, wordlessly, the only way it could, to tell her something.

*The wards answered to the stars. The wards answered to the covenant. The covenant answers to my blood.*

*And the masters sealed the founding archive within the hour.*

Ines looked once more at the starless sea. Then she turned, lifted her lamp, and started down the stairs—not toward her rooms, but toward sub-vault one, and the locked door at the bottom of everything.

Whatever her family had signed, she was going to read it tonight.

* * *

The corridors of the Salt Guild at night had always felt like the inside of a shell—curved, hushed, holding the memory of the sea. Tonight they felt like the inside of a trap. Chains clinked behind her as the factors worked their way through the building. Twice she flattened herself into alcoves and let them pass, lamp shuttered, heart loud, telling herself the same lie with each step: *I'm the keeper of these ledgers. I'm allowed to be here.*

The lie thinned with every corridor. By the time she reached the vault stair, it had worn through entirely, and what was underneath it was simpler and colder: she was sneaking through her own house because her own house had become afraid of her.

Sub-vault one sat deeper than the others, below the waterline, behind a door of black saltglass etched with the founding sigil—a circle crossed by a sword, crossed again by a wave. Ines had walked past it a thousand times. She had never once seen it open.

The chain was new. The wax was fresh. Corvane's personal seal gleamed in her lamplight, unbroken.

She stood before it a long moment, listening. Above her, faint through the stone, the guildhouse groaned and muttered with its sealing. Below her—she pressed her palm flat against the door, the way she pressed it to her floorboards at dawn, the way she had done every morning for nine months—nothing. Silence. Dry well. Dead lamp.

But the door was cold in a way that had nothing to do with depth, and when she leaned close, she caught it: a smell rising through the seams of the saltglass, thin and ancient. Old water. Deep water. The smell of the sea at the bottom of the world.

And beneath the smell, so faint she might have invented it, a sound. Not the oath. Not a voice. A knocking. Slow, patient, rhythmic, like something on the far side of a wall keeping time.

Like something waiting to be let in.

Ines Reyes, last of the swordspeakers, keeper of the ledgers of impossible things, stood in the dark with her hand on the seal of her ancestors and understood that she was about to commit treason against the only home she had ever known.

She was reaching for her stylus to cut the wax when, from the darkness of the drain-channel that ran along the vault floor—a channel built, she had always assumed, for seepage—came a sound that stopped her heart.

Wet footsteps. Dragging. Coming closer.

And then a voice, cracked and salt-scoured, half-drowned, speaking from the dark at her feet in the old tongue of Vhal:

"*Keeper.*"
