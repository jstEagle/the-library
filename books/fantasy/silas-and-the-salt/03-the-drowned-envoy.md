## 3. The Drowned Envoy

The cellar smelled of brine and old paper, and Ines Reyes had spent enough of her life in it to know which smell was lying.

Salt was honest. Salt got into everything and stayed, patient as arithmetic. Paper was the liar—it held the scent of ink and candlewax and the particular dust of secrets, and beneath all of it tonight, something else. Something green. Something that did not belong two floors below the tide line of a guildhouse that had stood dry for three hundred years.

She came down the spiral stair with a lamp in one hand and the cellar ledger under her arm, because habit was armor and she had learned to wear it everywhere. The wards on the door should have stopped her. They didn't even flicker.

That was the second wrong thing. The first was the sky.

* * *

She had been in the tower when it happened—everyone had—and no one had slept since. A night without stars was not an event; it was an amputation. She'd watched Master Corvane stand at the eastern window with his hands folded behind his back, watching the horizon burn that low sickly green, and he had not looked surprised. That detail had lodged in her like a splinter: *he wasn't surprised.* He'd ordered the records sealed with the calm of a man closing up shop before a storm he'd read about in advance.

Now the cellar. Now the green smell.

The lamp found him before her eyes did—a shape slumped against the far wall where the guild's oldest casks were racked, half in the drainage channel that ran along the floor. He was huge, or had been; the sea had taken most of what made him imposing and left the bones. His skin was gray as wet slate, weeping water from everywhere water could weep, and his robes—if they had ever been robes—hung off him in ribbons of kelp-stiffened cloth. His lips were split. His chest moved, barely.

And around his neck, on a cord of braided hair, hung a stone the size of a fist that glowed faintly, stubbornly, like a coal refusing the dark.

Ines stopped three steps from the bottom.

Every instinct she owned said *fetch Corvane.* Every instinct she owned also said *run.* Both instincts agreed on one thing: do not touch him. Guild procedure for drowned things was clear—quarantine, record, report upward, let the masters decide. She had written half those procedures herself during the audit years.

Instead she set down the lamp, knelt, and pressed two fingers to his throat.

His pulse was there, but wrong—slow as a bell tolling underwater, each beat arriving late, as if his heart had to send for permission first. At her touch his eyes opened. They were the color of deep water over sand, and they found her face with terrible precision, the way a drowning man finds light.

"You," he rasped. Water came out with the word. "You have her jaw."

Ines went very still. "I don't know you."

"No." He coughed, and more seawater came, and with it something else—a sound, faint, like a choir heard through a hull. "But I knew your grandmother. Merrin Reyes. And you wear her mouth like a debt unpaid."

The name went through her colder than the cellar draft. Merrin Reyes appeared in exactly one place in the guild's founding documents, and the entry was four words long: *First Signer's Line. Sealed.*

"Who are you?" she said. "How did you get past the wards?"

"The wards are dead." He laughed, or tried to; it came out as bubbles. "I swam through their corpse. Do you understand what I'm telling you, little keeper? The border between your world and mine is gone. Vhal is coming up the coast road, and nothing is holding the door."

"The wards have held for three centuries."

"They held because someone paid for them." His hand rose—shaking, barnacled, absurdly gentle—and touched the glowing stone at his throat. "This is a piece of the anchor. The covenant-stone that bound the ward to a bloodline. It broke last night. When the last star went out. Do you see the order of events now? The stars did not go dark and then the ward failed. The ward failed, and the stars—" He stopped. Swallowed. "No. That part comes later. First you must hear the rest."

"Rest of what?"

"Why I crawled out of my own drowned city and let the sea chew me for six days." His grip closed on her wrist—not strong, but absolute, the way iron is not strong so much as final. "Because the covenant has one heir left. One bloodline the anchor will still answer to. Yours."

* * *

She got him upstairs by degrees, which took the better part of an hour and cost her a torn sleeve, a wrenched shoulder, and whatever remained of her belief that the night could still be survived by paperwork. She installed him in the disused counting room off the east gallery—the one with the drain in the floor, chosen without letting herself think about why—and built a fire he seemed to find more offensive than comforting. He sat close to it anyway, dripping, wrapped in a horse blanket, looking like a monument the sea had returned as damaged goods.

He said his name was Brannoc. He said it the way men say titles they've been stripped of: *tide-priest of the Ninth Circle, Vhal,* and then, before she could ask, *the last one who matters.* He drank four cups of salted water she brought him and refused fresh, and when she raised an eyebrow he said, "You would refuse air," and she decided not to argue theology with a man whose lungs worked differently than hers.

It was only when he was marginally less gray that he set the stone on the table between them, and Ines felt it.

Not saw. Not heard. *Felt*—a pull behind her sternum, faint and rhythmic, matching the lagging beat of his heart. Or hers. She couldn't tell anymore which.

"What is it?" she asked, though the ledger-keeper in her already knew the shape of the answer and hated it.

"A fragment of the Anchor Stone. There were seven once. This is the fourth I've found." He turned it in the firelight. It was ordinary-looking, sea-worn granite shot through with veins of something that caught light the way fish scales do. "When the covenant was signed—when your guild was founded, Ines, do you understand? Your guild *is* the covenant, its charter, its vaults, its whole rotten beautiful structure—the ward needed an anchor in the living world. A bloodline sworn to hold the border shut. Seven stones, one oath, one family."

"And that family is—"

"Yours. The Reyes line. The First Signer's descendants." He watched her with those deep-water eyes. "Your grandmother held the oath until she died. Then it passed down. To you."

"My oath is swordspeaker's work," Ines said. "Ledgers and blades. I've carried it since I was sixteen. It's never—" She stopped.

"It's never what?"

*It's never spoken back,* she almost said, and did not, because she had told no one that. For months now—longer—the oath had been silent in her blood. She had told herself it was fatigue, age, the quieting that comes to all bound things. She had kept auditing. She had kept smiling at the masters' tables. She had recorded trades in borrowed years with steady hands while something inside her sat mute as a snapped string.

Brannoc saw the silence and read it the way tide-readers read water.

"It's gone quiet, hasn't it," he said softly. "Your oath. Months ago. Around the time the ledgers started showing entries nobody would explain to you."

Her head came up. "What do you know about our ledgers?"

"I know the ward has been failing for thirty years, and I know someone has been paying to keep the failures out of the books." He leaned forward, dripping onto the table, and the stone between them pulsed once, slow, like a sleeping thing turning over. "I was a priest of the Ninth Circle, Ines. My circle kept the *other* side of the accounts. Every year the tide came a little further into Vhal. Every year we sent envoys asking why the anchor-bloodline did not renew the covenant. And every year your guild sent back the same answer: *the matter is in hand.*"

"In hand," Ines repeated. The words tasted like the cellar's green smell.

"For thirty years. While the stones scattered. While the rites were forgotten. While my city drowned street by street and we were told it was natural subsidence." His voice did not rise. That was the worst of it—it stayed level, liturgical, a man reciting a grief he had memorized. "Last night the pretense ended. The stone broke. The stars went out. And I swam north carrying the only piece of the truth anyone on either side thought worth saving."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, the guildhouse settled and groaned, and Ines thought: *it sounds like a ship.* It had always sounded like a ship. She had just never asked herself what kind.

"If what you're saying is true," she said slowly, "then the masters know. They've always known. Corvane sealed the records within the hour—"

"Corvane knows exactly what happened and exactly why," Brannoc said. "Which means you have a choice, little keeper, and I'll be plain about it because the tide won't wait for tact. You can take this stone and your silent oath and your questions to the masters, and they will tell you a story smooth as glass, and you will believe it, because believing is cheaper than the alternative. Or you can come with me and find the other stones before they do, and learn what your family actually signed."

"And if I refuse both?"

"Then Vhal finishes drowning, the border stays open, and everything the sea has been holding back for three hundred years walks up your beaches on a night with no stars to watch it." He spread his ruined hands. "I am a disgraced priest, Ines. My own circle cast me out for asking these questions too loudly. I have no standing, no temple, no god who answers. All I have is a rock and a name, and the name is yours."

The stone pulsed again. And this time—involuntary, unasked—the thing behind Ines's sternum pulsed *back.*

Brannoc's breath caught audibly. He stared at her chest as if he could see through wool and skin to the place where a silent oath had just, for the first time in months, twitched in its sleep.

"There," he whispered. "Do you feel it? It knows you. It's been waiting for you the way the sea waits for the moon."

Ines looked down at the fragment of broken covenant, glowing its stubborn coal-glow, tugging at something older than her training, older than her name. Her inheritance. Her burden. The thing her grandmother had sealed away in four words and left, like all the worst debts, to be discovered by the next generation.

She thought of the audit. Of the entries that didn't reconcile. Of Corvane's folded hands and unsurprised face while the horizon burned.

"I need to check the archives," she heard herself say. "Before I decide anything. The founding covenant. The original signatures. If my bloodline anchored that ward, there will be a record."

"There will be a record," Brannoc agreed, "of whatever they wanted you to find."

"Then I'll look for what they didn't."

He smiled then, and it transformed his ruined face into something almost young. "That," he said, "is the first true thing a Reyes has said to Vhal in thirty years."

Outside the counting room's high window, the sky stayed black from horizon to horizon—not clouded, not stormy, simply *empty*, the way a vault is empty after a theft. And beneath the guildhouse, in the flooded dark of the old cellar, the drainage channel ran quietly toward the sea, carrying with it the green smell of a kingdom rising to collect.

Ines picked up the stone. It was warm. It fit her palm the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

The archives opened at dawn. She intended to be the first one through the door.
