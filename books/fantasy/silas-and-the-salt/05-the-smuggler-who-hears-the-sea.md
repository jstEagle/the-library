## 5. The Smuggler Who Hears the Sea

Wren knew they were trouble before they reached the dock, because trouble always walked the same way—too fast, too straight, like people who'd forgotten how to look bored. These two came down the Salt Row stairs at a dead clip: a tall woman with a ledger-satchel strapped across her chest like armor, and a big man moving like the sea had personally wronged him and he intended to file a complaint.

She went back to mending her net. Mending nets was what you did when you wanted to be left alone, and Wren had gotten very good at being left alone, which was another way of saying she'd gotten very good at surviving.

They stopped at her boat anyway.

"That's not a smuggling vessel," the woman said. "That's a rowboat with ambitions."

"It's a *fast* rowboat with ambitions," Wren said, not looking up. "And you're standing on my dock, which costs a penny an hour for strangers."

The big man made a sound that might have been a laugh if it had been allowed to finish. The woman dug in her coat and produced a copper, set it on a piling with the precision of someone who counted things for a living.

"We need passage south," she said. "Tonight. Past the guild patrols."

"Past the guild patrols." Wren let the net fall into her lap and looked up properly. The woman had salt-gray eyes and the kind of face that kept its secrets in a locked drawer behind other locked drawers. "Guild patrols are why passage south costs more than passage anywhere else. Guild patrols are why most of the boats that try don't come back."

"How much?"

"Depends how much you're worth catching."

Something flickered across the woman's face—not fear exactly. Recognition. Like Wren had said something true about her and she hadn't decided yet whether to be angry about it.

"My name is Ines Reyes," the woman said. "This is Brannoc. We're not running from the law. We're running from the guild."

Wren spat over the side of the boat, which was the traditional response to the guild and also good luck, probably. "Same thing, most days. What's in the satchel?"

"Ledgers."

"Stolen?"

"Borrowed," said Ines Reyes, and there was something in her voice when she said it—a hairline crack under all that competence—that made Wren's ears prick up. She'd spent six years reading adults the way other kids read primers. This one was lying about something, but not about the important thing. The important thing—the fear, the running, the desperate need to be somewhere else by dawn—that part was true as tide.

"The sea talks to me," Wren said, because it was best to get the strange part out early, before money changed hands. "Dead sea, mostly. It tells me where the patrols are, where the sandbars moved, which wrecks have anything worth salvaging. You want me because of that or you want me because I'm cheap?"

"You're not cheap," Ines said. "You're twelve."

"I'm twelve and I'm the only captain taking passengers tonight. Every other hull on this dock is either guild-chartered or gutless." She stood, finally, and stuck out a hand crusted with rope-burn scars. "Wren. Sole proprietor, operator, and crew of the *Kestrel*. Passage to wherever you're going is eight silver, half now, half when you stop screaming."

Brannoc did laugh then, short and startled, like it had escaped custody. Ines just looked at her for a long moment, doing sums behind those gray eyes, and then counted four silver pieces onto the piling.

"Where are we going?" Wren asked, pocketing them.

Ines glanced at Brannoc. Brannoc glanced at the water, and his jaw did something complicated.

"South along the coast first," he said. "Then we'll know."

*We'll know.* Wonderful. Wren loved a passenger with a destination of *we'll know*. "Get in the boat before I change my mind."

---

They cast off two hours past midnight, riding the ebb, and Wren learned three things about her passengers before the harbor lights fell behind them.

One: the big man, Brannoc, was afraid of the open water. Not seasick—afraid. He sat in the stern with his hands flat on the gunwale like he was holding the ocean down by force, and every time the *Kestrel* lifted on a swell his breath went thin. A man built like a dock crane, scared of swells no bigger than a dog. That wasn't fear of drowning. That was fear of something the water had *done* to him.

Two: Ines Reyes didn't relax. Ever. She sat amidships with the satchel hugged to her chest and her eyes moving in a constant slow sweep—horizon, sky, water, horizon—and Wren recognized the pattern because she'd invented it. That was the watch-keeping of somebody who'd grown up waiting for the next bad thing to arrive on schedule.

Three: neither of them had eaten. Wren divided out hardtack and dried fish without being asked, because hungry passengers made stupid decisions and stupid decisions got captains drowned.

"Eat," she said. "You can be tragic after breakfast."

Ines took the food but didn't eat it right away. She turned the hardtack over in her fingers like it might be a document requiring audit. "How long have you been doing this? Alone?"

"Six years."

"Six years." Something moved through her voice, quiet and terrible. "Who taught you the boat?"

"The boat taught me. Mostly by trying to kill me." Wren chewed her fish. "My mother ran cargo before the wards went soft and the coast started walking inland. She went out one night in November and the sea kept her. I heard it happen." She said it flat, the way you say things you've said so many times inside your own head they've worn smooth. "That's when the talking started. Or maybe it started before and I just noticed after. Dead sea doesn't explain itself."

Ines was quiet a moment. "What does it say?"

"Depends on the night. Tonight it's grumpy. Big tides coming, it says. Bigger than tides." Wren nodded toward the east, where the horizon should have been showing the first bruise of dawn and instead showed nothing at all—no stars, no moon-glow, just a black so complete it looked painted on. "It's been grumpy since the stars went out. Says something was taken. Says somebody owes." She shrugged. "Sea's been owed things for centuries. Usually it just complains."

"And now?"

"Now it's started naming names." Wren looked at Ines Reyes directly, and watched the woman go very still in the way of prey animals and accountants. "Funny thing. Last night, while you were climbing down my stairs, it said a name. *Reyes*, it said. *The Reyes debt comes due.*"

The silence that followed had weight. Even Brannoc turned to look.

"You could've mentioned that before I paid you," Ines said finally.

"You paid me to get you past patrols, not past prophecy. Different rates." But Wren held her gaze, and softened by maybe one degree, which for her was a landslide. "I'm telling you because you should know I know. I don't carry passengers who lie to me about what's chasing them. So. What's chasing you?"

Ines looked down at the satchel. When she spoke, her voice was the voice of someone reading from a ledger she hated. "The guild's founding covenant anchored the border wards between our realm and Vhal. My family's oath was the key to that anchor. The wards broke the night the stars vanished. The masters sealed the archives rather than explain why. And what's chasing us—" she exhaled— "is Master Corvane's enforcers, led by a woman named Sefa, who trained me, and who will not hesitate."

"Huh," said Wren.

"*Huh?*"

"I expected worse. That's almost honest." She pointed the fish bone at Brannoc. "Him too? He talk, or does he just loom?"

"He talks," Brannoc rumbled. "When there's something worth saying."

"Rare quality. Annoying in practice." Wren went forward to check the line, mostly so she didn't have to keep looking at their faces. Two orphans, she thought. That's what they were, both of them, though Ines Reyes probably had a fancy word for it. *Disinherited. Unmoored. Cut loose from institutional care.* Same animal, different cage.

The dead sea murmured against the hull, low and continuous, like a crowd beyond a wall. Wren listened with the half of her mind that was always listening, and the murmur shifted, sharpened.

Patrol. Northeast, closing. Lantern-rigged cutter, six oars, guild pennant.

"Down," she said. "Oars shipped. Everyone breathe quietly."

---

They lay in the bottom of the *Kestrel* under a scrap of oilcloth while the patrol cutter slid past maybe forty yards off the port bow. Wren kept her eyes shut and listened with her whole body: the creak of oar-leathers, the mutter of men who'd rather be warm, the particular rhythm of a helmsman not really paying attention. The dead sea helpfully narrated it all, whispering up through the planks—*six men, cold, bored, one of them thinking about his wife, none of them looking south.*

The cutter passed. The dark swallowed it.

Wren pushed back the oilcloth. Her passengers emerged like creatures from burrows, blinking.

"They didn't see us," Brannoc said.

"They weren't seeing much of anything. Guild pays for presence, not attention." Wren took up the sculls again. "That's the whole secret of smuggling, if you're taking notes. Nobody catches what nobody's looking for. Now hush, both of you. Dawn's coming and dawn's the dangerous hour—patrols change shift, and shift-change crews are awake enough to be dangerous and stupid enough to be thorough."

The sky to the east stayed black. That was the wrongness none of them could stop noticing: dawn was coming, the gulls were waking, the air had that pale iron smell of morning—but the light never arrived. Just a gray thickening, like the world was a window fogged from the outside.

Ines stood carefully amidships, holding the mast-step for balance, and stared at that starless, sunless east. "I used to watch the sunrise from the guildhouse tower," she said, mostly to herself. "Every morning. I thought it would always be there."

"Everything's always there until it isn't," Wren said. "Ask my mother. Ask the stars. Ask the village of Saltmark, which is underwater twice a day now instead of once." She sculled, steady, easy. "You get used to it. That's the horrible part. You get used to anything."

"Do you?" Brannoc asked, and his voice had a raw edge to it, like the question was really about him.

"No," Wren admitted. "But you get *good* at pretending, and eventually the pretending holds weight. Like a patched hull. Doesn't make the patch the boat. Makes it float."

Neither of them answered that. But she felt them both turn it over, the way passengers do, and she felt something else too—something she hadn't felt in six years of solo runs. The boat felt bigger with three. The night felt less like a mouth.

She hated that. She'd built her whole life on needing nobody.

---

The shift-change came an hour later, and it nearly killed them.

Wren heard it first as a wrongness in the water-murmur—a tightening, a hush, like the sea drawing breath. Then the whispers resolved: *two boats, working together, sweeping a line. They know. Someone talked. Someone at the docks sold your heading for ten silver and a bottle.*

"Somebody sold us," she snapped, shipping the sculls. "Dockmaster's boy, probably. There's a pair of cutters sweeping a net-line across the channel ahead, and if we keep this heading we sail straight between them like a coin into a purse."

Ines was already pulling charts from the satchel—of course she had charts, she probably had charts of places that didn't exist yet. "Options?"

"North channel's too shallow this season. Open sea and we're a beetle on a plate. That leaves the wreck-field." Wren grimaced. "Old graveyard off Gullspit Point. Forty hulls on the bottom, masts like a forest. Patrols won't follow in there because patrols like their bottoms unbarnacled. Problem is, the dead sea is *loud* in the wreck-field. All of them talking at once. Most sailors can't think in there. Some go mad."

"What can you do in there?" Brannoc asked.

"Steer." She met his eyes. "Question is whether you two can hold still and trust a twelve-year-old while dead men shout at me from every direction."

Ines Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Wren watched her do the arithmetic—bad option, worse option, child—and watched her arrive at the answer any sensible person would dread.

"Take us through," Ines said.

"That's the spirit. Sit down, hold the gunwales, and whatever you hear, don't answer it."

The wreck-field announced itself as a smell—old wood, old iron, old grief—and then as a sound only Wren could properly hear: a roar of voices rising through the keel, dozens of them, hundreds, the accumulated last words of forty years of drownings, all talking over each other, all wanting something. *Tell her. Tell him. Look down. Look down. The cargo's still lashed in the hold, it's still there, tell them—*

Wren planted her feet and steered through it, jaw tight, picking the channels between sunken spars by ear and by the sea's grudging guidance. Behind her, Ines breathed in controlled counts. Brannoc whispered something rhythmic, prayer-shaped, in a language that made the dead sea go briefly, respectfully quiet.

And then, cutting through everything, one voice louder than the rest—close, urgent, *young*:

*Turn. Turn NOW. Hard to port. Net in the water. They strung a net—*

Wren threw her whole weight on the sculls. The *Kestrel* slewed. Something heavy dragged along the hull—a line, a hooked line, singing taut—and then scraped free, taking a sliver of gunwale with it.

Behind them, in the gray false-dawn, a lantern flared. A voice shouted, thin with distance: *"Contact! Wreck-field, port quarter!"*

"They saw us," Ines said.

"They *grazed* us. Different thing." Wren's heart was going like a drum-corps but her hands were steady; her hands were always steady; it was the one inheritance her mother had actually left her. "Now we find out how much these guild boats hate their bottoms."

The answer, as it turned out, was: quite a lot. The pursuit lanterns slowed at the wreck-field's edge, argued among themselves in fragments of shouted orders, and did not follow. The dead sea chuckled around the hull, low and mean and satisfied.

Wren steered out the far side into open gray water, and only then let herself shake, small and private, where nobody could see.

Except somebody saw. Of course somebody saw. Ines Reyes crawled forward and sat across from her in the bilge-water and the fish smell, and didn't say *you did well* or *thank you* or any of the useless things adults said. She just said:

"Your mother would be proud of you."

"You don't know that. You never met her."

"She ran cargo alone with a daughter and a boat, past guild patrols, in a world that was already breaking." Ines's voice was quiet and certain. "Yes. I know that."

Wren looked away, blinked hard, and pretended it was spray.

"Eight more silver gets you to Saltmark," she said gruffly. "After that we renegotiate."

"We don't have eight more silver."

"Then you'd better hope I'm feeling charitable." She took up the sculls. "Or that the sea keeps giving you free advice, because it's definitely charging me for yours."

Ahead, the false dawn refused to break, and the dead sea murmured its one repeating phrase against the hull, patient as tide:

*Reyes. The Reyes debt comes due. South. Go south.*

Wren rowed south.
