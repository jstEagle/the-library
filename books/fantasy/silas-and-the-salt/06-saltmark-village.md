## 6. Saltmark Village

Brannoc smelled the village before he saw it. That was the first wrongness. Saltmark sat three miles inland, or had, in the years when he had rowed its tide-offerings out to the deep priests and carried back their blessings in wax-sealed jars. Three miles inland, and the air should have smelled of peat smoke and pig dung and drying nets gone stiff with brine. Instead it smelled of open water at slack tide — that green, patient smell of a sea deciding where it wanted to be.

"The sea walks up here now," Wren said from the bow of the borrowed skiff, her voice flat in the way she reserved for things she considered obvious. "Every night when the moon's high enough to pull it. Comes up the river road like a guest nobody invited. Goes back down by morning." She was poling them through what had been, until recently, a barley field. Stubble broke the surface in pale tufts. A fence post leaned out of the water at an angle that suggested it had given up arguing.

"How do you know that?" Ines asked. She sat amidships with the covenant-fragment wrapped in oilcloth across her knees, holding it the way a woman holds a sleeping animal she doesn't entirely trust.

"Because I can hear it," Wren said. "I told you. It talks all night. Doesn't shut up." She made a face. "Mostly it complains."

"About what?"

"You," Wren said, and shipped the pole, letting the current carry them the last stretch toward the cluster of rooftops rising gray-green out of the floodwater. "And your guild. Mostly your guild."

Brannoc said nothing. He had been saying nothing for two days, which was a discipline. The fragment of the broken stone rode against his chest inside his soaked priest's coat, and every time his heart knocked against it, it seemed to knock back, faintly, like something asking to be let in. He had served the Salt Guild for nineteen years. He had carried their jars and sung their rites and told drowning villages that the water was a blessing they should be grateful for. He had believed it, mostly. Belief was the cheapest thing a tide-priest ever sold.

The village resolved out of the mist: rooftops, a church tower with its lower courses submerged, boats tied to second-story windows. People moved along a walkway of planks strung between the upper floors, and every one of them stopped moving when the skiff came into view.

"They see the guild colors on the sail," Brannoc murmured.

"I know," Ines said. She reached up and began untying the sail. "Take it down. All of it."

He did. His hands knew the work; his hands had done a thousand small obedient works for the guild, and they went on doing them now without consulting him. He folded the sail away and stood bare in his salt-stiff coat, and felt the eyes of Saltmark settle on him like gulls on a fish cart.

They grounded the skiff against the walkway below the church tower, and a man came down the ladder to meet them — broad, weathered, one eye clouded white, carrying a gutting knife with the casualness of a man who had decided how he would use it if he had to. Behind him, along the plank-walk, others gathered. Brannoc counted eleven before he made himself stop counting.

"You're guild," the man said. Not a question.

"We're not—" Ines started.

"He is." The clouded eye fixed on Brannoc, and Brannoc understood that the good eye had already read everything worth reading: the coat, the tonsure grown out but still visible, the way he stood like a man who had spent decades kneeling on wet stone. "I know you. You came to the harvest rites. Year after year. You sang over my father's boat when the deep priests blessed it." His jaw worked. "Tam Voss. My father was Tam Voss. Your people took his years, priest. Forty of them, traded for a new hull and a season of fair winds. He died at fifty-one with an old man's heart. I was nine."

"I remember him," Brannoc said quietly. It was true. He remembered everyone. That was the cruelty of the work — the guild taught its priests to keep perfect ledgers of the souls they harvested, because a debt unrecorded was a debt unpaid. Tam Voss had wept with gratitude as he signed. They all wept with gratitude. That was the trick of it, the beautiful machine of it: the guild never took anything anyone didn't beg them to take.

"Don't," the man said. "Don't you say his name. Don't you stand there with your soft voice and your remembering." The knife came up off his hip. "Every night the sea comes up this road. Every night it takes another house, another field, another grave — it took the graveyard, priest, do you understand? It took our dead. And then your masters seal their records and lock their doors and send nothing. No relief. No rite. Nothing. And now you come sailing up our barley like it's a harbor."

"It is a harbor now," Wren said. "That's the problem."

"Girl, I'll—"

"She's right," Brannoc said. He stepped forward, off the skiff, onto the walkway, putting himself between the knife and the others. His knees wanted to bend. Nineteen years of liturgy lived in his body, and every instinct he had said: kneel, lower your eyes, make yourself small and holy and harmless. He kept his feet. "You're right about all of it. The sea is walking because the wards are broken. The wards are broken because the guild let them break. I helped maintain those wards for nineteen years, and in the last six I watched my superiors starve them, and I signed the reports saying they were sound. So yes. Blame me. Blame us. You'd be right to."

The walkway had gone very quiet. Somewhere under the water, faintly, Wren cocked her head — listening — and Brannoc saw her expression change, saw her go still in a way that had nothing to do with the mob.

"But I'm not here for forgiveness," Brannoc went on. "I'm here because there's a way to stop the water. A real way. And it starts under your church."

* * *

They did not put the knife away. The man — his name was Aldous Voss, though he never offered it — walked them up the ladder and along the walkway at knifepoint's distance, through a village that had become a shoreline. Children watched from windows with the particular silence of children who had learned that loud things got taken. An old woman was mending a net on a roof that had once been a hayloft, and she spat into the water as Brannoc passed, a small precise gesture of contempt that he found himself grateful for. Contempt was honest. Pity would have been worse.

Ines walked beside him. "You didn't have to do that," she said low. "Confess in front of all of them."

"Yes, I did."

"That's not what I mean. You handed them a reason."

"A reason they already had," Brannoc said. "I only made it true out loud. There's a difference between a lie and a secret, swordspeaker, and I've spent nineteen years confusing them." He looked out over the drowned fields, the water lying flat and patient under the noon sky. "My order had a rite for this. For villages that turned on us. We called it the rite of witness. You stand in front of the wronged and you name every debt aloud — yours and theirs both — and you don't ask absolution. Absolution is a trade, and trades are how the guild ate the world. You just... witness. You let them carry the truth instead of you."

"And does it work?"

"No," Brannoc said. "It's not supposed to work. It's supposed to be true."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. She had a way of looking at people as though reading a ledger entry written on their faces, and he had the uncomfortable sense of being audited. "The fragment," she said finally. "It's been humming since we passed the church tower. Hasn't it."

It wasn't a question either. Everyone was full of questions today, and none of them needed answers. "Since the field," he admitted. "Louder now."

Under his coat, against his sternum, the shard of the broken covenant-stone pulsed like a second heart. It had been quiet for years — dead glass, he'd thought, a relic of a failure — until the night the stars went out, when it had woken screaming against his skin hard enough to leave a bruise. Now it beat slow and insistent, and it beat loudest here, over the flooded chapel of Saltmark, where the water stood chest-deep in the nave.

Wren stopped walking. "It's talking again," she said. "The sea. Right now. Under the church." Her face had gone pale, and Wren did not go pale; Wren had the unshakeable composure of a child who had survived things that would have broken adults. "There's a lot of them down there. Voices. Old ones. They're all saying the same word."

"What word?" Ines asked.

Wren looked at Brannoc, and her eyes were ancient and twelve years old at once. "*Witness,*" she said.

* * *

Aldous Voss would not let them into the chapel alone, and would not let them in armed, and so they went down into the flooded nave in a loose procession — Brannoc first, then Ines with the fragment, then Wren, then six villagers with boathooks and one with the gutting knife — wading through water the color of old bottle glass. The pews floated in their rows, knocked gently against each other, a congregation of empty wood. Light came down through the high windows in long green blades. At the far end, the altar stood above the waterline still, and around its base, half-submerged, something glowed.

Not glowed. *Answered.* As Brannoc waded closer, the fragment against his chest began to warm, and the glow beneath the altar warmed with it, a deep amber light like lamplight through honey, pulsing in time — he realized with a lurch — with his own heartbeat.

"There," Ines breathed. "That's it. That's the first stone."

"It's been there always," Aldous said behind them, and his voice had changed, lost its blade-edge, gone uncertain. "My grandfather said the chapel was built over it. Said the founders sank it in the crypt and built God's house on top to keep it humble. We thought it was a story."

"Most of what keeps the world together is a story," Brannoc said. "The guild just learned to charge admission." He reached the altar steps. The water here was waist-deep and cold, cold in a way that felt deliberate, and the amber light lay across the surface like spilled gold. Beneath the altar, visible through the clear water, a stone the size of a loaf of bread sat in the silt, carved over every face with script older than any kingdom — and around it, faint as heat-shimmer, shapes moved. Figures. Standing in the water where no figures stood. Watching.

"The dead," Wren whispered. "Those are the ones who were talking. They're not angry." She frowned, listening to something none of them could hear. "They're waiting. They keep saying — they want someone to say it out loud. What was done. All of it. Before they'll let anyone touch it."

Brannoc looked at the watching shapes, and at the villagers behind him, and at the water that walked inland every night because of what his masters had done and what he had countersigned. Nineteen years of kneeling had taught him every posture except the one that mattered.

He turned to face the villagers. He waded back a step, so that he stood between them and the stone, in the deepest part of the nave, and he began the rite of witness — the true one, the old one, the one the guild had edited down to a collection plate and a blessing. He named the guild. He named Corvane. He named the starving of the wards, year by year, report by report, and his own signature on each. He named Tam Voss and forty years, and the widow who had remarried a man she did not love because the harvest failed, and the child who had gone hungry in a good year because the years had been sold out from under it. He named the drowned graveyard. He named the sea itself, and apologized to it, which no priest of his order had ever done, because the guild taught that the sea owed *them.*

His voice cracked twice. He did not stop. The water around him grew very still, stiller than still, and the amber light rose until it lit the whole nave, and the shapes beneath the surface drew closer, and closer, and stood in a ring around him, and listened.

When he finished, the silence lasted three heartbeats. Then the light beneath the altar flared white-gold, and the stone rose out of the silt of its own accord, streaming, turning slowly, and came to rest on the altar above the waterline, clean and humming, waiting.

No one moved. Then Aldous Voss lowered the gutting knife, all the way down, until it hung at his side, and said, in a voice rough as rope: "Take it. Take it and fix it. And priest—" his clouded eye found Brannoc, and there was something in the good one that was not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but was at least a door left unlatched, "—come back after. Tell me if it worked. That's all. Just come back."

"I will," Brannoc said. "On my witness."

Ines lifted the stone from the altar, and it was lighter than it should have been, and the moment her fingers closed on it the fragment against Brannoc's chest rang once, clear as a struck bell — and then rang again, differently, urgently, in a pattern Brannoc had not heard since his ordination and had prayed never to hear again.

Wren heard it too. She spun toward the high windows, toward the sea beyond the drowned fields, and her face went white.

"What is it?" Ines demanded. "What's wrong?"

"It's not the stone," Wren said. "The sea's saying something else now. Something's coming up the river road." She swallowed. "Fast. And it's singing guild work-songs while it comes."
