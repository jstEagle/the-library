## 7. The First Stone

The chapel had drowned standing.

That was the first thing Ines understood about Saltmark's church: it had not collapsed when the sea came for it, the way buildings did. It had knelt. The steeple leaned at an angle that suggested prayer rather than ruin, and the bell tower's arch still held its shape beneath six feet of green-black water, like a mouth open mid-hymn. Kelp hung from the broken windows in long gray banners. Fish moved through the nave where pews had been, quick silver flickers that might have been light or might have been souls, depending on which of Brannoc's stories you believed.

Ines believed all of them now. She'd run out of disbelief somewhere around the night the stars went out.

"You're sure this is it," she said. Not a question. She was buying time and they both knew it.

Brannoc stood waist-deep at the chapel door, his tide-priest's robes—what remained of them—clinging to him like shed skin. He held the covenant fragment up. Even underwater-dim, even cracked down its center, the stone drank the morning light and gave nothing back. "The fragment pulls," he said. "Feel." He took her wrist and pressed the shard against her palm, and there it was: a faint tug, seaward and down, like a fish on a line too deep to see. "It wants its body. And its body is under your chapel."

"Our chapel," Wren said from the rowboat. She sat on the gunwale with her boots off, feet trailing in the water, which no sane person did in a sea that walked inland nightly. "I found the village, I found the priest, I found you two arguing about it for half the night. That makes it ours."

"It was never mine," Ines said. "That's rather the point."

She said it lightly. It didn't land lightly. Brannoc looked away, out at the water, and Wren pulled her feet up into the boat, suddenly busy with her rigging.

They'd been in Saltmark three days. Three days of shutters closing as they passed, of children pulled indoors, of old Marta—the only one who'd speak to them—leaving bread outside their room and saying nothing. *Guild witch*, someone had scratched into the inn's door. Ines had stared at it a long time. She couldn't argue. Her guild had sold these people bottled weather at famine prices while the wards rotted, and she had kept the ledgers neat.

Now the sea came up the high street every night like a debt collector, and left again at dawn, and everyone in Saltmark knew whose fault it was.

"Low tide is in an hour," Brannoc said. "The rite wants slack water. After that—" he gestured at the drowned doorway, at whatever waited below, "—we go down together or not at all."

"I'll watch the boat," Wren said.

"You can't swim."

"I can float. There's a difference and it's called talent." But her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was scared and hiding it behind arithmetic. "You come back up. Both of you. That's the deal. The dead sea doesn't get to keep you. I've got an arrangement."

*An arrangement.* Ines had learned not to ask what Wren's arrangements were. The girl heard things in the water that no one else could, and whatever bargain she'd struck with the drowned, it worked, and it was hers.

"All right," Ines said. "One hour."

* * *

Slack tide came gray and windless, the whole bay holding its breath.

Ines stripped to her shift, strapped the knife to her thigh, and stood at the chapel door with the covenant fragment in her fist. Brannoc murmured beside her—not a spell, exactly; he'd been clear about that. Tide-priests didn't command the water. They *witnessed* it. They named what was true so the water had something to hold onto.

"The rite of descent," he said. "Old words. You don't have to mean them. You just have to say them and let the sea hear."

"What do they mean?"

"'We come down as guests. We rise as witnesses. What we take, we carry. What we leave, we leave in peace.'"

"That's not a spell. That's manners."

"The oldest magic there is." He almost smiled. Then the smile went, because he was looking at her hand, at the fragment, and whatever he saw in her face made him say, quietly: "Ines. When the stone answers—and it will answer, your blood is the key, that part of the masters' archives was true—when it answers, it will want payment. Oaths always do. Yours has been silent for a year. A silent oath is a debt accruing interest. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand you think I should be afraid."

"I understand *I'm* afraid, and I've done this before." His eyes were the color of the deep water, and very tired. "Say the words anyway. Say them anyway."

She said them anyway.

The water closed over her head like a ledger shutting.

Down was a country of green murmur. Light came through in slanted columns, full of drifting silt that turned slowly, patiently, like snow that had nowhere to be. The nave stretched ahead of her, pews furred with weed, an altar crusted white with salt so thick it looked like frost. Brannoc swam beside her, kicking in slow strokes, his robes billowing. Above, distorted by the surface, Wren's boat rocked—a dark comma against the pale sky.

The tug in her fist grew stronger with every stroke. Seaward and down. Down toward the altar.

The stone was *under* the altar. Of course it was. Under everything holy, there was always a foundation, and foundations were where guilds buried their sins.

Her lungs were beginning to speak to her by the time she reached it—a low insistent voice behind the breastbone, *soon, soon*—but she made herself look first. Made herself witness, in Brannoc's word. The altar salt was carved, she realized. Marks in the crust, old ones, deliberate: names. Hundreds of names, chiseled into the salt by hands working underwater, working in the dark, working after the drowning. Villagers who had come down here to write their dead into the stone because there was no dry ground left to bury them in.

*Silas*, said one mark near the base, worn nearly smooth. Just that. No surname. No dates. The first signer, the man who drowned paying the guild's first debt, the story every guild child learned as a cautionary tale about overreaching. Ines had assumed he was legend. He had a grave-mark in a drowned chapel, and it was worn smooth by touch—by generations of fingers finding it in the dark.

She pressed her own palm flat against it, once, in respect. Then she dove under the altar's lip.

The silt there was deep and soft as ash. She pushed her arm into it to the shoulder, groping through cold mud that clung like hands, following the pull of the fragment—and touched stone.

Not drowned stone. Not salt-crusted, weed-hung, patient stone. This was *alive*. Warm, almost, in the way a banked fire is warm, and it hummed against her palm with a frequency she felt in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the scar tissue over her heart where the oath lived.

The oath.

For eleven months it had been silence. She'd told herself it was grief, then fatigue, then the natural quieting of a gift that had never been loud to begin with. Swordspeakers heard their oaths the way some people hear music in their heads—a constant companion, a voice that knew their name. Hers had gone quiet sometime last winter, and she had hidden the silence the way she hid everything: neatly, in a ledger no one audited.

Now, with her hand on the covenant-stone, the silence broke.

*Ines.*

Not sound. Deeper than sound. It came up through the stone, through the water, through the marrow of her, and it was her own grandmother's cadence, and her mother's, and underneath both of them something older and colder and vast as the horizon—the original voice, Silas's voice maybe, or the sea's, or the covenant itself, which might have been the same thing.

*You kept my words. You stopped speaking them. Why?*

And Ines, underwater, out of air, out of excuses, answered honestly, in the way oaths demand honesty:

*I was afraid of what you'd ask me to pay.*

The stone turned in the silt. Rose. It came up slowly, shedding mud like a moth leaving its cocoon, and it was beautiful the way storms are beautiful—a rough sphere the size of a heart, veined with light the color of the vanished stars, and it rose until it hung in the water before her, waiting.

*Then pay attention,* said the oath. *Because here is what asking costs.*

She was nearly blacking out. She knew it distantly, the way you know a thing in a dream. Brannoc was pulling at her arm, trying to drag her up to air, but she shook him off—or meant to shake him off; maybe her arm just failed—and she put both hands on the stone and spoke her oath aloud for the first time in a year, bubbles streaming past her face, her voice a strange ringing thing in the water:

*"By salt and star and signed intent, I hold the border. I hold the border. I hold the border."*

The stone *answered*.

Light detonated through the chapel. Every name in the altar-salt flared white at once, hundreds of the dead lighting up like a congregation raising candles, and the water itself seemed to stand still—to *attend*—and the covenant-stone blazed in her hands with a warmth that had nothing to do with temperature. It was recognition. It was a lock finding its key after years of rust. It was, God help her, *welcome*: the oath wrapping around her like arms, like homecoming, like a dog that had waited at a door for a year without being let in.

*I'm here,* it said, in every voice she had ever loved. *I'm here. I'm here. What took you so long?*

And then it began to collect.

* * *

Pain came later, on the surface, in the boat. First came the loss.

She surfaced coughing, Brannoc hauling her over the gunwale, Wren shoving a wool blanket at her with shaking hands and a stream of curses far too sophisticated for a twelve-year-old. The covenant-stone lay in the bottom of the boat between Ines's knees, dimming gently, satisfied. The fragment in Brannoc's pouch had gone dark and inert—it had given itself back, rejoined its body, job done.

"Ines." Brannoc crouched in front of her, gripping her shoulders. "Ines, look at me. What did it take?"

"What do you mean, what did it—"

She stopped.

There was a hole in her head. Not pain—a hole. She reached for yesterday and found the shape of it, the outline, the way you remember a room you've left: *something happened yesterday.* She'd woken in the inn. Marta had left bread. And between waking and the chapel there should have been hours, conversations, preparations—

Gone. Smoothed over. Not hidden—*paid*. Paid the way you pay a coin from a purse, leaving the purse lighter.

"Three days," she whispered. Her own voice sounded foreign. "It took three days."

Brannoc went gray. "Which three?"

"I don't know. That's rather the definition." She laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "Yesterday. The day before. The day we arrived, maybe—I can't tell. There are gaps. There are *gaps*, Brannoc, and I can't even miss what's missing because missing it would require remembering it."

Wren had gone very still, hugging her knees at the bow. "Is it going to take more?" she asked. "When you do the next stone. Is it going to take more?"

"Yes," Ines said.

"How much more?"

"I don't know."

Wren nodded slowly, processing this with the flat pragmatism of a child who had already lost more than most adults. "Okay," she said. "Okay. So we write things down. Everything important, every day, starting now. Names, promises, plans, anything you'd hate to lose. You keep a ledger of yourself." She said it simply, the way she said everything, and Ines felt something crack in her chest that had nothing to do with oaths.

*A ledger of yourself.* That was what she had been, wasn't it, her whole life. Entries and balances. Debts and payments. The guild had taught her that a person was just an account that hadn't been settled yet.

"Here." Wren dug a stub of pencil and a scrap of oilcloth from her coat and pushed them across the boards. "Start now. Write: *Wren found the boat. Wren is extremely brave. Wren gets a share of any treasure.* Sign it."

Ines wrote it. Her handwriting looked the same. That was somehow the worst part—that the hand was unchanged, that three days of her could vanish and the letters still came out level and neat, a guild clerk's script, legible to anyone.

Underneath, she added, in smaller letters: *My oath spoke today. It sounded glad to hear from me. I don't know what to do with that.*

Brannoc watched her write, and when she finished he said, quietly: "The old texts say the anchor's price grows with each binding. First stone, days. Second stone—" he didn't finish it.

"Weeks," Ines supplied. "Months. My whole childhood, if it's feeling ambitious." She wrapped the blanket tighter. The sun was fully up now, burning off the bay mist, and the drowned chapel behind them looked almost peaceful, its leaning steeple gilded with light. Somewhere under the altar, hundreds of names glowed faintly and went dark again, one by one, like lamps being carried home. "I used to think the worst thing the guild ever did was lie in its ledgers. Now I think the worst thing is that the ledgers are honest. Everything costs. Everything is priced. Even memory. Even *this*—" she touched the stone, warm as a sleeping animal, "—even the thing that saves the world sends an invoice."

"Then we renegotiate," Wren said. "That's what you do with bad terms. You said so. Chapter four of your boring book."

Ines blinked at her. "You read my handbook?"

"I read *everything* you own. You sleep too deeply for a smuggler's passenger." Wren shrugged, unrepentant. "Point stands. Four stones, four bearers eventually—you said the covenant wants to be shared. Shared means shared costs. Maybe the stone takes less from each of us if all of us hold it. Nobody's tested it. Nobody's been stupid enough to try."

It was a child's logic and it was possibly brilliant and Ines was too exhausted to know which. She looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at the stone. The stone, if stones could look smug, looked smug.

"Row," Brannoc said finally, taking up an oar. "Back to the inn. Food, sleep, and tomorrow we start writing everything down. All three of us. If we're going to be a covenant, we may as well practice keeping records."

Wren scrambled to the tiller, delighted, already narrating the day's events aloud for the record—"Wren steers magnificently; the priest is slow; the swordswoman looks terrible"—and Ines leaned back against the gunwale and let the boat rock her, holding the stone against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

It was only as they neared the village dock that she noticed the gap had edges.

Three days gone—but not empty. At the rim of each absence, something remained, the way a page torn from a book leaves fibers in the spine. And at the edge of the largest gap, the day before yesterday, she could just make out the ghost of a sensation: someone knocking at the inn door, late, urgent. Someone with a guild seal. Someone saying her name.

Someone she had, apparently, already met—and paid the stone to forget.

She sat up so fast the boat lurched.

"Ines?" Wren said.

"Turn us around," Ines said, staring at the shore, at the narrow high street, at the inn with its scratched door where a figure in travel-gray was even now dismounting a lathered horse—a figure she didn't recognize, and recognized completely, the way you recognize a voice from a letter you've only read. Tall. Gray-cloaked. Guild-issue boots. Moving with a swordspeaker's economy, straight toward the dock, straight toward them.

"Ines," Brannoc said sharply, "who is that?"

"I don't know," Ines said, and reached for her sword. "But I think I paid three days not to find out."
