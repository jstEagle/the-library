## 8. Guild Hunters

Wren heard the river before she saw it, which was wrong, because rivers were loud and her whispers were quiet, and they didn't usually agree on anything.

The dead sea talked to her in a hundred small voices — barnacles and drowned gulls and old men's boots rotting on harbor bottoms. Most of it was nonsense. Grief, mostly, and gossip. But that morning, poling their leaky skiff up the Saltmark river with the fog coming down thick as wool, the whispers went suddenly, completely silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The way a room goes when someone dangerous walks in.

She stopped poling.

"What?" Ines asked. She had the second oar and was using it badly, all shoulders and no patience, but she'd been getting better since Saltmark. Brannoc sat in the bow with his broken stone fragment wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a baby, praying under his breath in a language that made the water flinch.

"Shut up a minute," Wren said.

Ines shut up. That was one thing Wren liked about her — for an oath-lady with a famous bloodline and a face like a closed ledger, she took orders from a twelve-year-old without making it a whole argument. Brannoc needed more convincing. Everything with Brannoc was a sermon.

Wren put her palm flat on the river.

The water was cold and brown and full of silt, and underneath the silt it was full of other things. Old fish. A sunken barge from before the wards fell, its cargo of salted herring gone to bones and ghosts. And downstream, maybe half a mile, where the river bent around a hill and narrowed between two stony banks —

Boats. Three of them, riding low. Men who weren't moving, weren't talking, weren't even breathing loud. Waiting boats. Waiting men.

And something else. Something the dead sea wouldn't name but kept flinching away from, the way you flinch from a hot stove. A cold spot in the water shaped like a person.

"Backwater," Wren whispered. "Now. There's — "

The first crossbow bolt came out of the fog and buried itself in the gunwale six inches from Brannoc's knee.

* * *

Later — much later, when her hands had stopped shaking and she'd had time to think it through properly — Wren would figure out that they'd been lucky twice over. Lucky that the ambushers wanted them alive enough to warn first, loosing that single shot as a message instead of a kill. Luckier still that the dead sea loved her enough to go quiet, because silence, she understood now, was its way of screaming.

But at the time there was no later. There was only now: the bolt quivering in the wood, Brannoc shouting something holy, Ines hauling the oar around so hard the skiff nearly rolled, and the fog ahead of them peeling open like a wound to show three longboats sliding out of the gray.

Guild boats. Wren knew the shape of them from the harborside — black hulls, no rails, the Salt Guild's wave-and-weight sigil painted on the bows in silver that never tarnished. She'd spent her whole life stealing from boats like those. Small thefts, careful thefts. Never once had she imagined being *in* the water while they came for her.

"Down!" Ines yelled, and threw herself across Wren's back as a second volley hissed overhead. One bolt punched through the sail and another clipped the mast with a sound like a snapped bone. The skiff staggered. Water slopped over the sides.

"They're not trying to sink us," Brannoc said. He'd gone very calm, the way he did, tucking the stone fragment inside his shirt. "They want us breathing."

"I don't care what they want!" Wren got her pole down into the mud and shoved with everything her skinny arms had. The skiff lurched sideways, off the channel line, toward the near bank where the reeds grew tall as masts. "I care what I want, and I want to be somewhere else!"

The river helped her. That was the thing nobody understood about the dead sea — it wasn't just dead things in it. It was everything the water had ever touched, every current and every stone, and right now the current owed her nothing but gave her everything anyway, shoving at the skiff's hull, dragging at the guild longboats' keels, doing the small treacherous work of a river that had decided whose side it was on.

The whispers came back all at once, a flood of them: *left left left, the deep hole by the willow, the snag, the snag, mind the snag—*

"There's a snag," Wren gasped. "Port side, underwater, big one. We can't — "

"We can." Ines was already steering for it. "If they don't know it's there, it's ours."

"That's insane."

"Yes," Ines said, and for the first time since Wren had met her, the oath-lady smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile at all. It was the smile of somebody who'd found out her whole life was a lie and had decided to make the lie pay for it.

The snag came up out of the murk like a drowning giant's finger — a great black root-bole, ancient willow or older, furred with weed. Ines put the skiff's nose within a hand's breadth of it and turned hard at the last second, and the current took them, whipped them around the bole in a sickening arc that soaked them all to the skin, and behind them Wren heard the lead longboat hit the snag with a crack like the world's largest door slamming.

Screaming. Splintering. Men in the water.

Brannoc started to turn. "They'll drown — "

"They won't drown." Ines was already poling for the reeds. "Guild enforcers wear cork vests under their coats. Corvane doesn't spend men he can't afford to lose. He spends *us*."

Wren looked back anyway. Through the fog she saw dark shapes bobbing, cursing, hauling themselves toward the shallows. Alive. Angry. And among them, standing hip-deep and utterly still while her own crew floundered around her, one figure who wasn't cursing at all.

A woman. Tall, gray-streaked hair cut short as a soldier's, watching the skiff go with an expression Wren couldn't read across the water and didn't much want to.

The whispers knew her, though. The whispers said her name the way they said the names of shipwrecks.

*Sefa,* they said. *Sefa Sefa Sefa.*

* * *

They ran.

There was no dignity in it. They abandoned the skiff in the reeds and went up the bank on foot, Brannoc carrying the stone, Ines carrying Wren when the mud got too deep for short legs, all three of them crashing through wet brush with the fog swallowing their tracks behind them. Wren's heart banged like a hatch cover in a storm. Her lungs burned. Every noise behind her — a branch snapping, a bird going up — was pursuit, was bolts, was hands closing on her collar.

Twice they heard the whistles. Two notes, high then low, passed from voice to voice through the fog, marking the net closing. Guild signals. Wren had heard dockside kids imitate them for laughs. They weren't funny anymore.

"Left," she panted, when they hit a game trail. "The trail bends right toward the ford. They'll have the ford."

"How do you know?" Ines demanded.

"Because if I were them, that's where I'd be, and because the river told me." She didn't explain further. Explaining took breath, and breath was for running.

They cut left. The trail thinned, climbed, turned to bare rock slick with mist. Below them and behind, the whistles changed pattern — frustrated, searching. Wren allowed herself one lungful of hope.

Then the fog ahead of them moved wrong, and Sefa stepped out of it.

* * *

Wren had seen plenty of scary people. Harbor masters. Debt collectors. One customs officer with a hooked chain for a hand whom she still had nightmares about. None of them prepared her for Sefa, because Sefa wasn't scary the way those people were scary. Sefa stood relaxed, sword sheathed, hands empty, and looked at Ines Reyes with an expression that was almost gentle, and somehow that was the worst thing Wren had ever seen.

"Ines." Her voice was low and even, a voice built for giving orders that people thanked you for. "You're making this difficult."

"Hello, Sefa." Ines put herself between the woman and the rest of them. Wren noticed her hand had found her knife — the swordspeaker's blade she carried but hadn't drawn since Wren had known her, the one that was supposed to sing and didn't. "Come to drag me back?"

"Come to bring you home before you do something we both regret." Sefa's eyes flicked to Brannoc, to the lump of oilcloth at his chest, and something hardened in her face. "Corvane knows about the stones. You can't outrun the guild, Ines. You especially can't outrun it with a drowned priest and a *child* slowing you down."

"Hey," Wren said.

"You've been following us since Saltmark," Ines said. "Why am I still breathing, Sefa? Your crossbowmen don't miss."

Something crossed Sefa's face — quick, quickly buried, but Wren caught it because catching things was what kept smugglers alive. It looked like grief. It looked like guilt.

"The masters want you alive," Sefa said. "That's the only reason."

"It's not," Wren said.

Both women looked at her.

"The river says you've been lying," Wren said. She didn't fully know why she said it. Maybe because terror had curdled into something meaner and braver. Maybe because the whispers were pushing at her, insistent, urgent, the way they pushed when a wreck was about to give up its dead. "It says your boats doubled back last night. It says you sent half your men downstream past the ford. If you wanted to catch us, you'd have caught us. You're letting us run. Why?"

"Wren," Ines said quietly. "Don't."

"No, let her talk." Sefa studied Wren the way you study a chart for a hidden reef. "What else does the river say, child?"

Wren's mouth was dry. But the whispers were steady, and she trusted them more than she trusted her own heartbeat, which at the moment wasn't saying much. "It says you argued with a man on the boat two nights ago. It says you threw something in the water after. It says—" she frowned, listening harder, the dead sea's voice threading through her like cold silk, "—it says you sleep with your sword on the far side of the tent, where you'd have to get past it to leave. Like you're keeping yourself in."

For a long moment nobody moved. Fog drifted between them. Somewhere below, a whistle sounded and faded, searching the wrong bend of the river.

"You always were too clever for your size," Sefa said at last, and there was no kindness in it now, but there was no cruelty either. Just tiredness, deep as ballast. Then she did the last thing any of them expected.

She stepped aside.

"Three days," she said. "The ford is watched until dusk tomorrow; go north along the ridge and cross at the old mill. After that, my orders change and I stop being able to be slow." She reached into her coat and tossed a canvas sack onto the rocks between them. It landed with the heavy clink of iron rations and the soft slither of a coil of rope. "Your supplies are in the longboat I sank. Consider these a replacement. Call it a debt."

"We don't take guild debts," Ines said.

"This isn't the guild's." Sefa's jaw tightened. "It's mine. I trained you, Ines. Ten years. I know what you are better than you do, and I know what Corvane means to do with you, and I will tell you this once because you're too stubborn to hear it twice: whatever he offered you, whatever story he tells you about anchors and duty and the price of realms — *run faster.*"

"Sefa—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice. For half a heartbeat, Wren saw the woman underneath the mentor — someone old and cornered and grieving something that hadn't happened yet. Then it was gone, shuttered away, and Sefa was just a guild officer again, standing in the fog with her sword sheathed and her orders waiting. "Go north. Don't look back. And keep the child away from the water at night."

"Why?" Wren asked.

Sefa's eyes found hers, and for a moment the whispers rose up so loud Wren could barely stand — the dead sea roaring a warning through her, through the ground, through the fog itself.

"Because things are waking up that hear better than you do," Sefa said. "Now go."

* * *

They went.

They climbed north through the dripping trees until the river was a gray rumor below them and the whistles had faded entirely, and then they walked, because running was done and the fear had to go somewhere. Wren carried the supply sack even though it weighed nearly as much as she did. Nobody offered to take it. She was glad. It felt good to carry something instead of being something carried.

They made camp at dusk in the hollow beneath a fallen oak, no fire, cold rations. Brannoc prayed. Ines sat sharpening her silent knife with slow, methodical strokes, staring at nothing.

Wren sat apart, hugging her knees, and tried to stop shaking, and failed, and got angry at herself for failing, and shook some more.

It had been close. Closer than close. She kept seeing the bolt hit the wood beside Brannoc's knee. Kept hearing the longboat crack. Kept seeing Sefa step out of the fog, and understanding, in her bones, how easily the day could have ended with all three of them face-down in the reeds, and nobody would ever have found them, and the ledger would just have a neat little entry: *three lives, forfeit, account closed.*

That was what the guild did. That was what debts were. Somebody always paid, and it was never the person who wrote the entry.

"Here." Ines settled down next to her, close enough that their shoulders touched. She held out half of her ration biscuit, which was exactly as stale as Wren's own, and therefore a completely pointless gesture, and therefore the nicest thing anyone had done for Wren in about four years.

"I'm not hungry," Wren lied, and ate it.

They sat in the dark a while. Down the slope, the river murmured to itself, and the whispers rode on top of the murmur, soft and constant, telling her things she mostly didn't want to know.

"Ines," she said finally. "That woman. Sefa. She loves you."

Ines's knife stopped moving. "You don't know that."

"The river knows it. It knows everything people drop in it. She drops a lot." Wren picked at the biscuit crumbs on her knee. "She's scared of it. Loving somebody the guild wants to use up. So she does her job and she hates herself and she throws things in the river at night." She shrugged. "I know the type. Half the harbor's like that."

Ines was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "Is that what you think love is? Something you throw in the water?"

"No." Wren thought about it. "I think it's something you *don't* sell. Even when the price is good. Even when you're starving." She glanced sideways at the oath-lady, at the famous face, at the knife that wouldn't sing. "My mum sold me to a tide-gang for two barrels of fresh water. I used to hate her for it. Now I just think — she priced me wrong. That's all anybody does, really. Prices things wrong."

"And what's the right price?"

"There isn't one." Wren said it simply, because it was simple. "Some things aren't for sale. That's the whole trick. That's the thing the guild never learned."

Ines looked at her for a long moment — really looked, the way nobody had looked at Wren since before the tide-gang, like she was a person and not a line item. Then she nodded slowly, and put her arm around Wren's shoulders, awkward as a plank, and left it there.

Wren should have pulled away. Smugglers didn't lean. Leaning was how you got caught, how you got sold, how you got hurt. She'd built her whole life out of not leaning on anything.

She leaned.

* * *

She woke once in the black hours, stiff and warm and disoriented, and lay listening. Brannoc's slow breathing. Ines's quieter one. The river, far below, talking in its sleep.

And under all of it, faint but unmistakable, the whispers again — not warning this time, not screaming. Singing, almost. Pulling. Coming from the north, from the ridge, from somewhere deep and drowned and old.

They were singing about a bell.

*A bell in the water,* the whispers said. *A tower under the fen. A stone that asks questions, a stone that weighs hearts. Come and see. Come and see. Bring the girl who hears. Bring the lady who owes.*

Wren lay in the dark with her heart going fast and quiet, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: whatever waited for them in the Mirefen would ask more of Ines than any crossbow ever could.

Second — and this was the strange one, the one she turned over like a coin found in the sand — she wasn't afraid of it. Not for herself. Somewhere between the ambush and the ration biscuit and the arm around her shoulders, the fear had changed shape, hardened into something with edges and weight. These two — the broken priest and the oathless lady — were hers now. Hers to steer, hers to warn, hers to keep alive however she could.

Her people. She'd chosen them, the way you choose a crew, the way you choose a course: once, and for keeps.

Wren closed her eyes and let the whispers sing her back to sleep, and dreamed of bells.
