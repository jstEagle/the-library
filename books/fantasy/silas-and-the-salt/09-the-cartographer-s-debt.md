## 9. The Cartographer's Debt

The map shop smelled of lamp oil and old paper and, beneath both, faintly of the sea—the way everything smelled of the sea now, three days after the stars went out, as if the drowned kingdom had leaned close to listen.

Ines found the door by the address Wren had scratched into a strip of birchbark: *Ilsa Vey, Cartographies & Corrections, third stair past the fishmonger's, knock twice and once.* The street was called Rope-Walk in the guild's ledgers, though no rope had been walked here in a generation. Saltwater stood ankle-deep at the lowest end of it. A woman was bailing her doorway with a bucket, unhurried, methodical, like someone who had decided the flood was a neighbor she'd have to learn to live with.

Ines knocked twice and once.

"Ledger number?" said a voice through the wood. Dry. Unimpressed.

"Ines Reyes. Guild of Salt."

A long pause. Then bolts—three of them, then a fourth she hadn't known existed—and the door swung open on a small woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of a storm over shallow water.

"The Reyes girl." Ilsa Vey looked her up and down. "You have your grandmother's chin. And her talent for showing up on doorsteps when it's inconvenient." She stepped aside. "Come in before the sea does."

* * *

The shop's interior was a canyon of rolled charts. Maps hung from every surface: coastlines, soundings, trade routes drawn in faded vermilion, and—Ines noticed with a lurch—charts of places that should not have existed. An island labeled in careful script: *Here the guild sold Tuesday.* A river delta annotated: *Bought back, 40 years, interest compounded.*

"You keep maps of the trades," Ines said.

"I keep maps of what the trades *did*." Ilsa cleared a stool of chart tubes and gestured for her to sit. "Every impossible thing your guild ever moved left a scar on the world. Someone has to draw the scars. That someone is me, and my payment schedule is forty years past due."

Brannoc stood near the door, dripping less than he had in Saltmark but never entirely dry, his broken fragment of covenant-stone wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. Wren had wedged herself onto a shelf between an atlas of the Mirefen and a chart of the drowned road, knees up, watching everything with the flat attention of a gull watching a fisherman's hands.

"You sent word you'd found something," Ines said. "About the stones."

"I sent word I'd found something about *your family*, which is not the same thing, and you should know the difference before you thank me." Ilsa pulled a tube from a rack, cracked the wax seal—an old seal, brittle as a moth wing—and unrolled the chart across her worktable, weighting the corners with brass weights shaped like anchors.

It was a map of the coast as it had been two hundred years ago. Before the wards. Before Vhal drowned. Ines recognized the cartographer's mark in the corner because she'd grown up staring at it: a compass rose with a salt crystal at its heart. Her grandmother's mark. Older even than that—the first signer's mark. Silas's mark.

"This is the original survey," Ilsa said. "The one that mapped the border before there was a border. Look at the Mirefen."

Ines leaned over. The great mire south of the coast sprawled across the lower third of the chart, rendered in washes of green and brown, its waterways threaded like veins. And at its heart, where modern maps showed only a nameless sinkhole, the old surveyor had drawn a bell.

Not a church. Not a tower. Just a bell, small and precise, with a line of script beside it in her grandmother's hand:

*Second witness sleeps here. Let no debtor disturb it until the debt comes due.*

"The sunken bell-tower," Brannoc said quietly. He had crossed the room without seeming to move; tide-priests walked like that, Ines was learning, as if floors were optional. "I rang it. Before the fall. It was the last rite I performed for Vhal." His hand pressed flat against the oilcloth bundle at his chest. "I did not know what slept beneath it."

"Nobody knew," Ilsa said. "That was rather the point. Your guild buried its collateral so deep even its own masters forgot—or pretended to forget, which with Corvane amounts to the same thing." She tapped the bell. "The stone under the Mirefen is the second anchor. If you're gathering them—and the dead sea says you are, or so the child claims—"

"I don't claim," Wren said from her shelf. "I hear."

"—then that's where the second one waits." Ilsa straightened, and something in her face shifted, the dryness cracking just enough to show the fear underneath. "And the third?"

Ines said nothing. Brannoc said nothing. The silence answered for them both.

Ilsa Vey closed her eyes briefly, the way people do when they've drawn a card they already knew was coming. "The drowned capital," she said. "Of course. The first vault. Where Silas signed." She opened her eyes and looked at Ines with terrible gentleness. "Child, no living thing returns from Vhal unchanged. I drew the charts of the expeditions. Fourteen parties went down after the drowning, hunting salvage, hunting proof, hunting anything. I mapped their routes going in." Her ink-stained finger traced lines on a different chart, one already hanging on the wall—a route drawn in black that simply stopped. "I never mapped one coming out. Do you understand? Fourteen routes in. Zero routes home. Whatever came back wore the faces of the people who left, mostly, and remembered their names, mostly, and was not them."

"Mostly," Wren repeated, and hugged her knees tighter.

* * *

Ilsa made tea. It was terrible—brackish, brewed with water that had clearly come from the wrong side of the flood line—but she made it anyway, and there was a ceremony to it that Ines recognized from the guildhouse: the ritual of offering, the small economies of hospitality that people cling to when the large things have failed.

They sat among the charts like passengers in a paper boat.

"Why help us?" Ines asked. It wasn't suspicion, exactly. It was bookkeeping. She needed to know which column this belonged in.

Ilsa laughed, short and sharp. "Because I owe you. That's the joke, isn't it? Forty years ago my husband's lungs filled with salt-sickness, and there was a cure in the guild's vaults—bottled, priced, available to anyone who could pay. We couldn't pay. So we did what everyone does." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "We signed. Borrowed years against our children's children. My son died owing your guild eleven years he never got to live. My granddaughter will be born into that debt if anyone's still keeping the books when she arrives."

Ines's throat tightened. She thought of the ledger room, the endless columns, the neat red figures that meant borrowed breath and stolen time. She had audited those columns. She had *corrected* them, found the errors, balanced the books, and gone home each night telling herself it was honest work.

"I can strike the debt," she said. "If I get back to the guildhouse. There are forms—"

"No." Ilsa said it fast, and hard, and then softer: "No. Don't you see? That's how they keep us. Every kindness comes with a form. Every mercy has a counter-signature. You'll forgive my granddaughter's debt and the forgiveness itself will be entered somewhere, filed somewhere, and someday someone will collect on it in a way none of us foresaw. That's the guild's whole genius, girl. It doesn't just own what we owe. It owns the shape of what we're owed." She set down the cup. "Pay me the only way that means anything. Finish it. Break the covenant open and let all of it—all the hoarded years, all the bottled weather, all the names in jars—go back to the world they were taken from. Then nobody owes anybody, and my granddaughter gets born free."

The words landed in Ines's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard them fall a long way before they stopped.

"That's not how oaths work," Brannoc said quietly. "An oath-bound bloodline can't simply—"

"Can't she?" Ilsa raised an eyebrow. "I map the world, priest. I've seen what it does when people insist on the impossible. It has a habit of agreeing."

* * *

They stayed the night. There was no choice in it—the streets flooded fully at dark now, the sea walking inland on a schedule that obeyed nothing so sensible as the moon—and Ilsa spread bedrolls among the chart racks and lit a single lamp and pretended, convincingly, that hosting fugitives from her creditors was an ordinary Tuesday.

Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, curled against an atlas of the southern deeps, one hand still gripping her knife. Brannoc sat by the window through the small hours, murmuring rites over his stone-fragment, his lips moving in a language Ines didn't know and her blood, somehow, half-recognized.

Ines couldn't sleep. She lay looking at the ceiling, where a chart of the night sky hung upside down—drawn, she realized, from memory, by someone who had loved the stars enough to record them before they went. Every constellation correct. Every star in its place. A sky that no longer existed anywhere except on this paper.

She got up quietly and stood before it.

"There's a cost you should know about," she said softly, to Ilsa, who was awake too—old women and smugglers, Ines was learning, slept lightly. "When I raise the stones. When I speak the oath. It takes memories. Three days of them, for the first stone. I don't remember my mother teaching me to write. I know she did. I know it the way you know a fact in a ledger. But the *her* of it is gone."

Ilsa was quiet for a while. The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the door, where the sea breathed against the wood.

"My husband, at the end, forgot me," she said finally. "Salt-sickness eats the mind last. For six months I was a stranger who fed him." She turned her cup again, around and around. "Do you know what I would have paid to keep him? Anything. Years. Names. Stars." She looked up. "So I won't tell you the price is too high, Ines Reyes. I'll only tell you what I learned sitting beside him: memory is the only currency that was ever real. Everything else is paper."

Ines looked at the paper sky. Somewhere above it, behind the black, the swallowed stars waited in a vault at the bottom of the sea, held as collateral on a loan her grandmother's grandmother had signed.

*I'll only tell you what I learned:* she was spending herself. Stone by stone, day by day, she was paying out the only real currency she had, and the ledger of her own life was filling with blank spaces where a person used to be.

And the worst part—the part she couldn't say aloud, not to Ilsa, not to anyone—was that some of the blanks felt like relief. The training yard. The first time she killed. The look on Master Corvane's face the day he told her her worth was her obedience. The magic took those too, or would, eventually, and some traitorous part of her whispered: *good.*

That was the trap inside the price. Magic that pays in memory will always find the memories you least want to keep, and leave you unable to tell whether what remains of you is what survived, or merely what was convenient.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. The floodwater had receded to the middle of Rope-Walk, and the fishmonger was already at his stall, selling eels to people who could no longer quite afford to ask where they'd been swimming.

Ilsa walked them to the door. From a rack by the frame she took a chart tube, sealed with fresh wax, and pressed it into Ines's hands.

"The Mirefen," she said. "Current survey, corrected for the flood. The safe paths are marked in green. The paths that *were* safe are marked in yellow. The ones marked in red—" she smiled without any humor at all, "—are the ones other people have taken. Learn from their cartography."

"Thank you," Ines said. "For the map. For—" she gestured vaguely at everything, the charts, the terrible tea, the story of her son, "—all of it."

"Don't thank me." Ilsa's voice was crisp. "This isn't a gift. It's a payment. Mark it in whatever ledger you're keeping: Ilsa Vey, account settled in full, by delivery of intelligence regarding the second and third witnesses. I want it *written down*, girl. I want a record somewhere in this drowning world that one debt got paid clean, with no interest, no fine print, no form." Her storm-colored eyes were fierce and wet at once. "Let it be one."

Ines held her gaze. "It's written."

It was. Not in any guild book—in the place behind her ribs where she was starting to keep a different kind of accounting, one entry at a time, in a currency she hadn't named yet.

They went out into the gray morning. Behind them, the bolts slid home—one, two, three, four—and the little shop full of scars and skies sealed itself against the flood.

Wren unfolded the chart as they walked, tracing the green threads south toward the Mirefen, and after a while she frowned.

"Ines."

"What?"

"The dead sea's talking again." The girl's voice had gone carefully flat, the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to be. "It keeps saying the same thing. Over and over." She looked up, and her eyes were very wide. "It says *the bell is ringing*. Down in the mire. Nobody's touching it, and it's ringing."

Brannoc stopped walking. His hand went to the oilcloth at his chest, and Ines saw—felt, in the silent place where her oath lived—that the fragment of stone had begun, very faintly, to hum.

Something below the Mirefen knew they were coming.

Or something below the Mirefen knew what was coming *after* them—and was calling the second witness awake to meet it.
