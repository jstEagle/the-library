## 10. Mirefen

The Mirefen did not begin so much as it accumulated. One step the ground was honest peat and sedge; the next, the world went soft and gray-green and endless, a country of standing water and drowned alder trees that looked like they had been waiting a very long time to be asked something.

Ilsa Vey's map called it *the Mirefen (do not linger)*, which was the most useful cartography I had ever read.

"Everything traded away ends up here," Brannoc said as we picked our way along a ridge of firmer ground. He walked with his staff held out like a divining rod, tapping the muck ahead of each step. "The guild doesn't destroy what it buys. It stores. And when the vaults overflow—" he gestured at the whole sodden horizon "—it leaks."

"Leaks," Wren repeated. She moved through the wet like she'd been born in it, barefoot on the tussocks, her boat-pole balanced across her shoulders. "You mean like a cellar floods."

"I mean like a wound weeps."

I kept my eyes on the path and my hand near my sword, though I wasn't sure anymore what good the sword would do. The oath was awake now—had been since the chapel—but it sat strangely in me, like a second heartbeat that beat only when it wanted to. It had taken three days of my memory to raise the first stone. Three days gone: I knew this because Brannoc told me, because Wren told me, because my own journal told me in handwriting I recognized but could not remember writing. There was a hole in me shaped like three days, and around its edges, other things felt loose. My mother's voice. The smell of the guildhouse kitchen in winter. Whether I had ever liked honey.

The stone rode against my sternum in its oilcloth wrap, warm as a sleeping animal.

We made camp that first night on the roof of a collapsed tollhouse, the only dry ground for a mile. Wren cooked eels over a smokeless fire and refused to share her technique. Brannoc prayed facing east, toward Vhal, his lips moving around words I pretended not to hear. And out in the dark, the Mirefen began to speak.

Not speak. *Move.*

* * *

I woke to Wren's hand clamped over my mouth.

"Don't scream," she whispered. "They're shy."

Beyond the fire's embers, at the edge of the light, stood a figure. Or the shape of one—a man-sized absence, darker than the night around it, edged in a faint gray shimmer like breath on cold glass. It had no face. It had no anything. It simply stood there, at the exact boundary where our small circle of warmth ended, and I understood with a lurch that it was waiting for permission.

"What is it?" I breathed against Wren's fingers.

"A shadow." She said it the way you'd say *a neighbor*. "Somebody sold theirs. Guild used to buy them off people who couldn't sleep, or who'd seen too much. Shadowless folk pay double for calm." She lifted her hand from my mouth. "This one's been wandering since before I was born."

The shadow shifted. Not toward us—just a slow ripple, the way water moves when something beneath it turns over. And then, impossibly, I felt it notice me. Felt it the way you feel eyes on your back.

"It likes you," Wren said, and there was something almost pitying in it. "Shadows remember their owners' owners. You smell like ledger-paper."

"We're not buying it back," Brannoc said without opening his eyes. He had been awake the whole time, of course. "Whatever it offers. Whatever it shows you. The guild's prices are never paid once."

The shadow stood until dawn grayed the sky, and then it folded down into the water like ink dispersing, and I lay staring at the paling stars—the few that were left, the ones too dim or too distant to have been worth swallowing—and thought about all the versions of myself that might be standing out there in the dark, waiting to be claimed.

* * *

The second day, we found the letters.

They came drifting across a flooded meadow on the morning breeze—actual paper, dozens of sheets, skimming the water's surface like water-striders. As we watched, one of them rose, unfolded itself with a sound like a sigh, and began to walk. It had no legs. It simply progressed, creases flexing, ink glistening, and when it reached the edge of our path it stopped and turned its blank face up toward us.

Wren snatched it out of the air before Brannoc could warn her.

"*Dearest Mother,*" she read aloud, holding it at arm's length like a fish. "*By the time you receive this I will have taken the tide-road south, and I want you to know it was not your fault, and I do not blame the sea—*" She stopped. Lowered it. "Oh," she said quietly. "It never got sent."

"They write themselves into flesh if they wait long enough," Brannoc said. His voice had gone rough. "Unsent letters, unkept promises, apologies swallowed. The Mirefen is full of everything people meant to say and didn't." He took the letter from Wren with both hands, gently, the way he handled his broken covenant-stone fragment. "There are rites for these. In Vhal we had a whole order dedicated to delivering them. Posthumous postmen." A ghost of a smile. "I was defrocked partly for saying the guild should fund it."

"What happens if you deliver one?" I asked.

"Someone weeps. Someone forgives or doesn't. The letter rests." He set it down on a dry tussock, faced it toward the south, and murmured something in the old tidal tongue. The paper shivered, settled, and slid away across the water with purpose now instead of drift. "And sometimes," he added, not looking at me, "the thing delivered is heavier than anyone expected."

I didn't ask what he meant. I was already afraid I knew.

Because all that day, as we waded deeper into the Mirefen's heart, I felt it following me. Not the shadow—something else. Something smaller. Quicker. It kept pace just beyond the reeds, and whenever I turned to look, there was only a flash of color where the world was gray: a scrap of yellow ribbon. A red shoe. A child's laugh, thin and bright, coming from nowhere.

The oath stirred against my ribs like a dog scenting home.

* * *

It caught up with us at dusk, on a spit of land where four drowned roads met, and it was not a shadow and it was not a letter.

She was eight years old. She wore a guildhouse training smock two sizes too big, sleeves rolled six times, and her hair was braided the way my mother used to braid mine before—before. She was sitting cross-legged on a flat stone in the middle of the flooded crossroads, playing jacks with a set of knucklebones, and when she looked up at me her face was my face, thirty years younger, unlined, and absolutely terrified.

"Oh," I said. The word came out very small.

"Ines?" Brannoc's hand found my shoulder. "What do you see?"

"Myself." My throat worked. "When I was eight."

The girl—she—went back to her knucklebones, deliberately, the way children perform indifference. But her hands were shaking, and the bones scattered wrong, and one rolled off the stone into the black water and sank.

"The guild bought it," Brannoc said softly. It wasn't a question. "Your memory. Your childhood."

"Sefa trained me." I heard my own voice as if from far away. "The program was called *tempering*. They said grief slowed a swordspeaker's blade. Fear, love, longing—all of it, dead weight. So the guild offered to take the excess. For free, the first time. A gift, they called it." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "My mother died that spring. I couldn't stop crying during drills. So Master Corvane himself sat me down and explained that I could keep the pain and fail my oaths, or give him the memory of her and become what I was born to be."

"And you were eight."

"And I was eight."

The girl on the stone had stopped pretending to play. She was watching me now with an expression I recognized from mirrors—the look of someone doing arithmetic about how much of themselves they can afford to lose.

"You came back," she said. Her voice was mine too, high and unbroken. "They said you wouldn't. They said the traded things never get reclaimed, that nobody comes into the Mirefen, that—" Her chin wobbled. Thirty years of practice keeping my face still, and hers couldn't manage five seconds. "That you forgot me on purpose."

"I didn't forget." The words tore going out. "I *paid*. There's a difference. There has to be a difference."

"There isn't," she said. "That's the worst part. That's why I've been following you for two days. Because there isn't any difference, and you have to know that before you decide."

Brannoc's grip tightened on my shoulder. "Ines. Listen to me. Reclaimed memories come back with everything attached. All of it, at once, undiluted—thirty years of deferred grief landing in a single heartbeat. I have seen it break people. And you are carrying two covenant-stones and an oath that feeds on exactly this kind of—"

"I know what it costs."

"Do you? You've been paying in installments your whole life. This would be the entire bill."

Wren crouched at the edge of the floodwater, hugging her knees, watching the girl with an expression I couldn't read. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it got when the dead sea was talking through her and she was trying to make sure the words were hers. "The sea says the Mirefen keeps nothing forever. Says everything here is waiting to be either taken back or let go. Says those are the only two doors." She glanced up at me. "Says there's no third door where you leave her standing in the water another thirty years."

The girl flinched.

"I know," I said. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me this long to even remember there was something to be sorry for."

"That's not an answer," she said. "I need the answer. I've been practicing it, you know. What I'd say. If you ever came." She stood up on the stone, small and straight-backed, wearing my mother's yellow ribbon in my old braid. "If you take me back, I'll hurt you. I'll hurt you worse than the stones did, worse than anything, because I'm not just the memory of her dying—I'm the memory of her being *alive*, and you don't get one without the other, and you haven't felt either in thirty years and your heart will crack like river ice." Her voice steadied. That was me too, that terrible steadiness under pressure. "And if you let me go—if you walk past—I'll finally rest, and you'll stay the person they built out of what was left. Efficient. Oath-bound. Half a woman with half a sword."

The water around the stone had gone perfectly still. Even the Mirefen, it seemed, was listening.

Brannoc said nothing. Wren said nothing. The knucklebones gleamed wetly in the last light.

I thought about my grandmother's signature in the vault ledger, mortgaging the stars. About Corvane's arithmetic: *one anchored life is cheaper than chaos.* About every entry in every guild book I had ever kept, where the whole art of the trade was making the price look smaller than the thing priced.

I had spent my life believing that what I gave up was gone. Clean transactions. Balanced columns. But nothing the guild ever sold was gone. It was all here, standing in the shallows, waiting. Every shadow. Every letter. Every eight-year-old girl.

"No," I said.

Her face fell.

"No, I won't take you back, and I won't leave you." I stepped off the bank, into the cold water, up to my knees, up to my thighs, until I stood close enough to see myself in her frightened eyes. "Both doors are the guild's doors. Take it back, leave it behind—it's all still accounting. It's all still *price*." I knelt in the floodwater so we were eye to eye. "So here's my answer. I'm staying right here, in the middle, for as long as it takes. Not reclaiming. Not releasing. Just—*witnessing*. You waited thirty years for someone to come back. I'm not going to make you wait alone one more minute while I decide what you're worth to me. You're not a line item. You never were."

For a long moment she just stared. Then her face crumpled—not into tears, exactly, but into something older than tears, the expression of a debt being forgiven rather than paid—and she stepped off the stone into my arms, and she weighed nothing at all, and she weighed everything.

The grief, when it came, came like Brannoc promised: all at once, undiluted, thirty years of it. My mother's hands braiding ribbon into my hair. Her cough in the next room getting worse by the week. The morning the house went quiet. The drills afterward, and Corvane's kind voice, and the strange clean silence where she had been. I shook apart in the flooded crossroads of the Mirefen and did not try to hold any of it together, and the oath against my sternum burned and burned and did not take payment, and I think—I will always think—that was the moment it stopped being the guild's oath and started becoming mine.

When it was over, the girl was gone. Not dissolved. *Integrated.* I could feel her in me like a room in a house that had been locked for decades and was suddenly full of light. The yellow ribbon floated on the water beside me. I took it. I tied it around my wrist.

Wren helped me up the bank. She didn't say anything clever, which from Wren was its own kind of mercy. Brannoc pressed a waterskin into my hands and said only, "The third door. Huh. The old rites mention a third door. We thought it was a metaphor."

"Everything true is a metaphor until somebody walks through it," I said, and my voice was wrecked and whole.

We camped on the crossroads stone that night, the only high ground, and I slept without dreams for the first time in months—or with all of them at once; I couldn't tell the difference, and didn't care to.

I woke before dawn to Wren shaking my shoulder, and her face in the gray light was the face she wore when the dead sea was shouting.

"Ines," she whispered. "Boats on the water. Lanterns doused. Six of them, maybe seven." She swallowed. "And the sea says the woman leading them knows your name, and your sword-form, and exactly which memory you just got back—and she's brought something to trade for it."
