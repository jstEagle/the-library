## 11. Sefa's Terms

Sefa had tracked them for nine days, and in all that time she had learned one thing worth writing down: the child was the dangerous one.

Not the swordspeaker. Not the disgraced priest with his salt-crusted hands and his eyes like a man reading his own funeral rites. The girl. Wren. Twelve years old, maybe thirteen, small enough to fold into a fish barrel, and she moved through the world like the world was telling her secrets. Twice now Sefa had closed to within a bowshot and twice the party had changed course without reason—drifting away from her ambush points like leaves on a current they could feel and she couldn't.

The sea was talking to her. Sefa didn't believe in that. She believed in ledgers, in weight and counterweight, in the clean arithmetic of a debt collected. But belief was a luxury, and she had stopped buying luxuries the day Corvane handed her Ines Reyes's file and said, *Bring her back alive. That part matters.*

*That part matters.* He'd smiled when he said it. Sefa had spent eleven years learning to read Master Corvane's smiles the way sailors read weather, and this one had been the kind that comes before a storm takes your whole fleet.

Now she crouched in the reeds above the old ferry crossing at Marrow Water, watching firelight flicker through the willows where the three of them had made camp. Close enough to hear. The wind was wrong for it—coming off the water, carrying their voices to her in fragments—and she caught pieces. Brannoc's low voice, steady as tide-priests' voices were trained to be. The girl's laugh, quick and bright and entirely too loud for someone being hunted. And Ines Reyes, saying something Sefa couldn't make out, in a tone Sefa knew too well: the careful, competent voice of a woman keeping dread folded up small so it wouldn't spill into her work.

Sefa had taught her that voice. Back when Ines was nineteen and fresh from her mother's funeral, all sharp edges and borrowed authority, Sefa had sat her down in the guildhouse records room and told her: *A ledger-keeper who shows fear gets audited by everyone. Fold it small. File it deep.* Ines had learned the lesson so well she'd become a better guildswoman than Sefa ever was.

That was the problem. Everything about Ines Reyes was the problem.

Sefa checked her blades out of habit. Two knives, one short sword, a garrote she'd never once used because knives were faster and kinder. She was forty-one years old, she had served the Salt Guild since she was younger than the smuggler girl, and she had never disobeyed an order. Not one. She had broken kneecaps for the guild, burned a debtor's warehouse with the debtor still inside—not killed, the guild preferred its examples living—had dragged weeping men across three provinces to sign papers they couldn't read. All of it clean. All of it filed. All of it *that part matters*, over and over, until the phrase wore a groove in her soul smooth as a river stone.

And then, four nights ago, in the sealed annex of the guildhouse, she had finally read the file Corvane hadn't given her. The one beneath Ines Reyes's file. The one labeled with a word she'd only ever seen stamped on documents that were then burned: **ANCHOR**.

She had read it standing up, by candlelight, with her hand pressed flat against the page as if she could hold the words down. She had read it twice. Then she had sat down on the floor of the annex among the dust and the dead masters' correspondence and understood, with the terrible clarity of a woman whose arithmetic has always been perfect, what *bring her back alive* meant.

The wards were failing because the anchor was failing. The anchor was failing because the anchor was a bloodline, and bloodlines thin. Corvane's solution sat in the annex in his own handwriting, neat as a recipe: a living ward. A person bound to the covenant-stones permanently, held at the border, sustained by the guild's hoard—a life spent once and forever instead of a realm spent slowly. He had even costed it. There was a column of figures. Years of maintenance. Rations. The price of the binding rite.

He had not included a column for what it would be like. For her. For the person inside the ward. Sefa had looked for it, absurdly, some line item acknowledging that they were discussing putting a living woman into a box forever, and found nothing, because Corvane's arithmetic did not have a column for suffering. It never had. That was why he was Master of Ledgers and she was only a hunter.

Ines laughed at something the girl said, low and brief, and Sefa's chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

She stood up out of the reeds and walked into the firelight, because there was no other way to do this, and because she had decided—if you could call nine sleepless nights of arithmetic a decision—that she would rather be a traitor who chose her moment than a loyal hound who got one.

* * *

They heard her at the last second. Of course they did—the girl's head came up like a deer's, and Brannoc was on his feet with salt already crawling along his forearms, and Ines had a knife out that Sefa recognized as guild issue, third standard, the one they gave clerks because clerks weren't supposed to need better.

"Sit down," Sefa said. "All of you. If I wanted you dead or taken, you'd already be."

"You're alone," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question. Tide-priests counted heartbeats; he'd have known if she had backup within a mile.

"I'm alone." She stayed at the edge of the firelight, hands loose and visible, an old courtesy between professionals. "I have maybe half an hour before my relief picks up my trail marker. I'm spending it badly. Listen fast."

"Sefa." Ines hadn't put the knife down. Her knuckles were white around it, and her face in the firelight was the face of someone meeting a ghost she'd expected. "The last time I saw you, you told me if I ran you'd find me, and you'd bring me back in whatever condition preserved the paperwork."

"I remember. I wrote the condition myself." Sefa crouched slowly, deliberately, bringing her eyes level with Ines's. "I've come to amend it."

Wren had gone very still by the fire, the way she went still when the water spoke, and now she said, quietly, "She's telling the truth. Mostly. There's—" the girl frowned, cocking her head like she was listening to something under the ground, "—there's something she's carrying that hurts her. Like a stone in a boot."

"The girl talks to the sea," Sefa said flatly.

"The girl talks to everything," Brannoc murmured. "Let her speak."

"She's right," Sefa said, and watched surprise move across all three faces, because hunters didn't validate smugglers. "There is a stone in my boot. I've been carrying it since the night the stars went out. Here it is." She reached into her coat—saw Ines tense, saw Brannoc's salt flare—and drew out not a weapon but a folded packet of papers, oilcloth-wrapped, and tossed it onto the ground between them. "Corvane's own hand. Read it."

Ines picked it up the way she picked up everything, like a ledger she was auditing, and unfolded it, and Sefa watched her read. Watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out. Watched her lips move over the words *permanent anchorage* and *living vessel* and *in perpetuity*.

"He means me," Ines said at last. Her voice was very even. That voice. The folded-small voice. "This is what 'alive' means. Alive and *kept*."

"A living ward," Sefa said. "Bound to the stones, fixed at the border, fed on the hoard until the hoard runs out or she does. Whichever. He's run the numbers both ways." She let that sit. "You asked me once why I stayed with the guild, back when you were young enough to ask impertinent questions. I told you: because the ledger balances. Because somebody has to collect what's owed or the whole thing collapses and everyone drowns. I believed that." She looked at the fire. "I've spent four nights doing sums, Ines. Do you know what I found? The equation doesn't balance. It never balanced. We just kept moving numbers around so nobody had to look at the body under them."

"Ines," Brannoc said softly. "What is this woman to you?"

"My mentor," Ines said. She hadn't looked up from the pages. "She trained me. She—" a pause, a hairline crack in the even voice, "—she carried me out of the archives fire when I was twenty-two. Broke her arm doing it. Never reported it, because reporting it would have meant explaining why she was in a sealed section after hours, teaching a junior clerk how to read pre-covenant script."

"You were a bad student," Sefa said. "You read too fast and trusted the text too much."

"You were a worse teacher. You trusted nothing."

"And look how well that turned out." Sefa finally sat, cross-legged, at the fire's edge, because her knees were forty-one years old and the speech she'd rehearsed for nine days was nearly done. "Here are your terms. My relief reaches this crossing in half an hour. I'll walk east and lay a false trail toward Greyharbor—it buys you three days, maybe four if the fog comes up. After that, Corvane sends more than me. He sends the Deepwater team, and they don't do trails and terms. They do nets."

"Why?" The question came from Wren, blunt as a gull stealing bread. "Why turn? Hunters don't turn. The sea says hunters turn only when they're drowning."

Sefa looked at the girl for a long moment. "The sea asks good questions," she said. "Fine. The truth, since I'm spending everything else tonight." She turned back to Ines. "Because I taught you to fold your fear small and file it deep, and I have watched you do it for eleven years, and I am not going to watch them take the folding and put it in a box forever. Because you are the best thing I ever made and the worst thing I ever served, and I can't be the instrument of both anymore." Her voice roughened; she hated it; she kept going. "Because love isn't in Corvane's ledger, girl. It's not a line item. It's the thing that makes you burn the ledger. And I have loved you since you were nineteen and insufferable, and I have expressed it in every wrong currency there is—discipline, silence, orders followed. This is the first time I've ever spent it right. Betraying him. For you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the dark river, a heron cried once and quit.

Ines set down the papers. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious about being wet, and she said, "Three days isn't enough to cross the Mirefen."

"It's enough to reach Ilsa Vey," Sefa said. "The cartographer. She owes the guild a debt older than your career—call it in. She knows the fen better than the fen does." She stood, joints cracking, and settled her coat. "One more thing. The third stone. Whatever Brannoc has told you, whatever the whispers say—Corvane already knows where it is. He's had the Vhal charts since before the stars went out. You aren't racing to find it. You're racing him to it. Assume he's ahead. Plan for him being ahead."

Brannoc went gray. "The capital," he breathed. "He's going to the capital."

"He's going to win," Sefa said. "Unless you get there first and do something no one's done yet." She looked at Ines one last time, and everything she couldn't say—all eleven years of it, all the discipline and silence and orders—she tried to fit into the look, and mostly failed, and said the rest aloud anyway: "Don't come back to the guild, Ines. There's nothing there to save. Save the world instead. It's a smaller job than it looks."

Then she turned east, toward the false trail and the ruin of her own life, and walked into the dark without looking back, because looking back was a luxury, and she had finally learned which luxuries to stop buying.

Behind her, faint on the wind, she heard the girl say: "The sea just told me something. It says the hunter's debt is paid."

And Sefa, deserter, traitor, free for the first time in thirty years, kept walking, and let herself smile at that, and did not stop until dawn.
