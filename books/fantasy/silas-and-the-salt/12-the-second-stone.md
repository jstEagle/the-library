## 12. The Second Stone

The bell-tower stood in the middle of the Mirefen like a drowned man's finger, pointing at a sky that had nothing left to point at.

We reached it on the third day after Ilsa Vey's map, wading through water the color of old tea. The fen had been a town once—Vey had called it Merrow's Rest, back when there was a rest to be had—and now its rooftops broke the surface in black rows, and its streets ran beneath six feet of brackish stillness where things moved that did not make ripples.

Wren poled our borrowed skiff with the ease of someone who'd been born doing it, which she more or less had. Brannoc sat in the bow with his hands folded around his tide-bell, and I sat between them and tried not to think about what the first stone had taken from me.

Three days of memory. Gone like water down a drain. I knew it only by its shape now—the absence of it. There was a hole in me shaped like my mother's voice singing, and another shaped like the winter I turned nine, and when I reached for them I found smooth walls where the rooms used to be.

*You knew the price,* I told myself. *You paid it anyway.*

That was the thing about the oath. It didn't ask twice.

"There." Wren pointed with her chin. "Tower."

It rose out of the fen ahead of us, half-swallowed, its belfry just breaking the surface like the tower itself was treading water. Salt crust scaled its stones white as bone. And beneath the waterline—I felt it before I saw it, the way you feel a struck bell through your teeth—the second covenant-stone hummed.

My silent oath stirred for the first time since the chapel. Not words. Just a leaning, the way a compass needle leans toward north. Toward *home*, if a stone could be home.

"It knows we're here," Brannoc said quietly.

"Everything in this fen knows we're here," Wren muttered. "Half of it's been staring at me since dawn. There's a letter down there that keeps trying to hold my hand. I hate it."

"The unsent ones are the worst," Brannoc said. "They've been waiting so long to be delivered."

I looked at him. He'd been saying things like that all week—small, offhand sentences that landed like stones in still water. He'd served the guild once. He'd carried their rites into Vhal and carried their lies back out, and he'd spent twenty years drowning slowly on dry land because of it. He knew what waiting did to a thing.

"We go down together," I said. "Or not at all."

"No," Brannoc said.

I turned. He met my eyes, and his were calm in a way I'd learned to distrust, because calm men who'd done what he'd done were usually calm the way deep water is calm.

"The stone will test each of us," he said. "The first one tested only you, because your blood signed the covenant. But this one—" he touched the fragment of broken stone he wore on a cord at his throat, the piece he'd carried out of Vhal's collapse, "—this one is older. It predates the Reyes line. It predates the guild. It will ask each bearer what they are willing to be."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I have stood before one before." His thumb moved over the fragment. "In the capital, when I was young and certain. It asked me what I would give up to keep my city safe, and I answered wrong, and I have been answering wrong ever since." He looked at the tower. "If I go down there and fail again, my failure could shake the stone loose. Could wake something. You need me standing watch above, not drowning below with my guilt around my neck like an anchor."

"That's cowardice dressed as strategy," Wren said.

"Yes," Brannoc agreed simply. "But it is also strategy."

For a moment nobody spoke. The fen breathed around us—that slow tidal exhale, water sliding off roots, the groan of settling stone. Somewhere close, the unsent letter rustled against the hull, patient as a dog.

"Watch the surface," I said finally. "Anything comes up that isn't us—ring the bell."

"I will ring it until it cracks," he promised.

* * *

Diving into the Mirefen was like diving into ink that remembered being tea.

The cold hit first, then the dark, then the taste—brine and rot and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in a vase. I had a rope around my waist tied to the skiff, a knife at my belt, and lungs that complained from the first stroke. The tower's doorway gaped below me, tall enough for mourners in their Sunday clothes, and I swam through it into the nave.

The water inside was clearer. That was the first strange thing. Down past the flooded pews, past the rotted altar, light gathered—not sunlight, nothing so honest—but a pale silver glow rising from the base of the bell-tower's shaft, where the bell had fallen through rotted floors and come to rest among the silt.

Except it wasn't a bell anymore. Or it hadn't been for a long time.

The stone sat in the cradle of the fallen bell like an egg in a cupped hand. It was smaller than the first—fist-sized, sea-green, veined with lines that moved when I wasn't looking directly at them. The glow came off it in slow pulses, and each pulse pressed against my chest like a thumb testing fruit for ripeness.

*Testing.* Right. Brannoc had warned me. I planted my feet on the silt and waited.

The pressure came.

It went through me like a hand going through a drawer—opening compartments, reading labels. I felt it touch the hole where my mother's singing used to be, and pause there, curious. I felt it flip through what remained: the guildhouse tower, Corvane's smile, Sefa teaching me to fall without hurting myself, Wren's laugh on the boat, the green burn of the starless horizon. It read me the way I read ledgers. Line by line. Looking for the entry that mattered.

Then it asked.

Not in words. In weight. It set something on me—an offer, or a bill—and let me feel the full sum of it. And I understood, in that pressing silence, exactly what it wanted to know:

*What will you pay?*

I thought about the three days I'd lost. I thought about the holes in me, smooth-walled and permanent. I thought about the way I'd caught myself last week calling Sefa by a childhood nickname and not knowing why I knew it.

And I thought about the sky, empty as a stripped bed, and the wards gone to glass, and Wren poling us through a world where dead letters held your hand because no one had ever sent them home.

*I'll pay,* I answered. *Whatever it costs. Whatever it takes. Bind it to me.*

The stone pulsed once—surprised, almost—and then it came apart.

Not shattered. *Unfolded.* The green stone opened like a hand unclenching, and inside it was light, and inside the light was—

Everything.

Memory poured into me and it was not mine. That was the horror of it, the wonder of it: the second stone did not test you and reward you. It *joined* you. It poured four hundred years of watching into the vessel it had chosen, and I stood on the floor of a drowned church and lived centuries in the space between two heartbeats.

I saw the coast before the guild existed—fisherfolk setting ward-stones by hand, singing them into place, a whole shoreline of small promises. I saw Silas—yes, *that* Silas, the one from the story every guild child learned wrong—kneeling in the surf with the first covenant-stone, signing away something enormous so that something larger could survive. I saw the stones passed hand to hand for generations, keeper to keeper, and I saw the moment the guild's masters decided that passing was inefficient, that hoarding was safer, that a promise kept by many hands was a promise no single family could profit from.

And underneath all of it, threaded through everything, I felt the stars.

Swallowed. Held. Four hundred years of collateral sitting in a vault under the sea, and every covenant-stone was a keyhole, and I had just volunteered to be the hand that fit the lock.

When I came back to myself, I was kneeling in the silt with the stone closed small and warm in both fists, and my nose was bleeding into the water in slow red ribbons, and I could not remember my father's face.

I knew I'd had one. I knew his name—Tomas, Tomas Reyes, ledger-keeper, died when I was eleven. But the face behind the name was a smooth wall now, and beside it, newly, the wall where my seventh summer had been, and the wall where the smell of the guildhouse kitchens had been, and the wall where—

I counted. The first stone had taken three days. This one had taken years.

I knelt there in the dark while my blood thinned into the fen, and I made myself do the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one thing I had left that no stone could repossess. Three days plus some scattered years plus whatever came next. I was spending myself like coin. And the terrible thing—the thing I turned over and examined in the silver light, honestly, the way you examine a coin for clipping—was that I would keep paying. Gladly. Because the alternative was a world under glass, and I had seen what the guild's hoarding made of people, and I would rather be hollow than be Corvane.

*Resolve,* I thought. Or maybe acceptance. They weighed the same, down here.

I kicked for the surface.

* * *

Wren hauled me over the gunwale by the rope, swearing the whole way, a stream of dockside profanity far too fluent for a twelve-year-old, which meant she'd learned it somewhere bad and I chose not to ask where.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding *and* lying. Great combination. Very trustworthy." She shoved a rag at me, then hesitated, and her voice dropped out of bravado into something younger. "Did it work?"

I opened my hands. The stone lay in my palms, sea-green and quiet, its pulse slowed to match my heartbeat—which, I realized distantly, it was now sharing. I could feel it the way I'd once felt my oath: a second pulse under my own, patient, permanent.

"It worked," I said.

Above us, Brannoc rang the tide-bell once—a signal, not an alarm—and began poling toward us across the fen, and I watched him come and felt the new memories shift in me like ballast settling. I knew things now that I hadn't an hour ago. I knew the words to ward-songs in a language that had been dead two centuries. I knew the shape of the third stone's hiding place in Vhal's capital, glimpsed through the eyes of a keeper three hundred years drowned. I knew what the stars sounded like when they sang through stone.

And I knew, with a certainty that settled into my bones colder than the fen water, what Corvane wanted with me. What a living anchor was worth. What he'd pay to get one.

"Ines." Wren was watching me with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing. "What did it take?"

I started to lie. The reflex was old and well-oiled; I'd been concealing things since chapter one of my own life. Then I looked at her—at this child who heard the dead sea, who'd lost whatever family she'd had and built a new one out of driftwood and stubbornness—and I made a different choice.

"Years," I said. "Memories. I don't know which yet. Some of them I won't miss until I reach for them."

She considered that with the seriousness of a customs officer. Then she nodded once.

"My ma forgot things too," she said. "At the end. Fever, not magic. She'd look at me and know she loved me but not why." Wren picked up the pole. "So I'll remember for you. Whatever goes missing—you tell me what it was, and I'll keep it. That's fair trade. That's how it works on the water."

Something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was there.

"That's not how ledgers work," I managed.

"Yeah, well." She pushed off from the tower, and the skiff slid out across the fen, and the drowned town sank away beneath us, its unsent letters folding themselves shut. "Your ledgers are why the stars are gone. Maybe it's time somebody kept books different."

Brannoc reached us and took the bow without a word, but I saw his eyes go to the stone in my hands, and then to my face, and something in them eased—the smallest loosening of a guilt he'd carried for twenty years. One stone recovered. One step closer to Vhal.

I closed my fingers around the sea-green warmth and let its second heartbeat settle against mine, and I did the sums one more time, the way you check a total you already trust: two stones bound, one remaining, and a vault of stolen sky waiting at the bottom of the drowned capital.

Whatever it costs, I told the dark water. Whatever it takes.

The fen, being a fen, said nothing. But far off to the east, past the edge of the Mirefen, the sea whispered against shores it no longer should have touched—and beneath the whisper, faint as a bell rung underwater, something in Vhal answered.

It knew we were coming. And it was counting, too.
