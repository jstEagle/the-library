## 13. What Wren Heard Underwater

Wren had been carrying the secret for eleven days.

She knew it was eleven because she counted things when she couldn't sleep, and she hadn't slept properly since the bell-tower. Eleven days of hauling the truth around like a stone in her coat pocket, except you couldn't put a thing like that down. You couldn't set it on a dock and walk away from it. It stayed in the pocket. It got heavier.

They were camped in the lee of a sea-wall ruin on the last spit of land before the drowned road began. Tomorrow they'd sail into Vhal proper—under it, technically—and Wren had decided, lying awake listening to the water talk to itself, that she could not go down there with it still inside her.

The dead sea didn't whisper anymore. Down here, close to where it had died, it *spoke*. Full sentences. Whole arguments. Sometimes it sang, and the songs were worse than the arguments, because at least an argument ended.

Ines sat by the fire sharpening a knife that never seemed to get duller no matter how much Wren watched her work at it. Brannoc was asleep sitting up, which he did now, like a man who'd learned somewhere that lying down was a luxury he hadn't earned. The second covenant-stone hung against Ines's chest under her shirt, and Wren could feel it from across the fire—not hear it exactly, but feel it, the way you felt weather coming in your teeth.

*Tell her,* said the sea, from thirty feet below them, patient as rot. *Or don't. But carry it into my capital and I will make sure everyone hears.*

The dead sea held grudges. That was the thing nobody understood about it. Living seas forgave; living seas just kept moving. Dead ones remembered every single thing.

"Ines," Wren said.

"Hm." Ines didn't look up from the knife.

"I have to tell you something and you're going to want to hit me, so maybe put the knife down first."

That got her attention. Ines set the knife on the whetstone with the exaggerated care of someone deciding not to be insulted yet. "Wren."

"It's not—I didn't do anything wrong. I just knew something. For a while. And I didn't say."

"How long is *a while*?"

Wren looked at the fire. "Since before Saltmark."

The silence went on long enough that Brannoc's breathing changed—he'd woken, and was pretending not to have, which was its own kind of confession. Everyone in this crew pretended. It was practically their flag.

"Before Saltmark," Ines repeated. Her voice did the flat thing it did when she was doing arithmetic instead of feeling. "You've known since the boat. Since we left the guildhouse."

"Since before that, even." Wren hugged her knees. "It came in pieces. The sea doesn't—it doesn't tell you things all at once. It tells you like it's paying out a line. A little, then more, then more, and by the time you've got enough to understand, you're already holding it and there's nowhere to put it down."

"Start at the beginning," Ines said. "Whatever the beginning is."

Wren took a breath. Underneath everything, faint but unmistakable, the dead sea went quiet. Listening. It wanted to hear this too. It had been waiting years for someone to be told.

* * *

The beginning, as far as Wren could tell it, started when she was nine, the winter after her mother sold her hearing to the Salt Guild.

That was the part people always tripped over, so she'd learned to say it fast: her mother hadn't sold *her*, just the hearing. There'd been a bad year, a debt, a man with a ledger who smelled like vinegar and ink, and her mother had gone into the guildhouse with a girl who could hear things other people couldn't and come out alone with a purse full of coin and a receipt. The guild collected odd senses the way other collectors got paintings. They were useful for appraising impossible things—a bottled storm needed someone who could listen to it, confirm it was still angry, price it accordingly.

Except the guild had made a mistake, or maybe the vinegar man had skimped on the ritual, or maybe her mother had haggled so hard they'd cut corners. Whatever the reason, they'd taken Wren's ordinary hearing—the sound of doors and voices and dogs—and what grew back in the empty space was the other kind. The deep kind. The kind that heard the sea.

Not the living sea. She'd figured that out early. The living sea sounded like nothing much, honestly, like wind through a big empty room. What she heard was underneath it: the drowned kingdom, Vhal, and everything in it that had ever gone under and stayed. Fishermen called her cursed. Smugglers called her lucky. Wren called it a job skill and charged accordingly.

"But here's the thing," she told Ines, and her voice cracked, and she hated that it cracked, and kept going anyway. "Here's the thing I should have said weeks ago. The dead sea talks about the stars. It's been talking about them since the night they went out. Because it *felt* it. All of it. Every star going dark felt like—" she searched for it, hands moving, useless at holding the shape of it— "like being swallowed. Like the whole sky was one mouth and it closed."

Ines had gone very still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"And the sea knows why," Wren said. "Because the sea was there. Because the sea was *part of it*. Ines—the stars weren't destroyed. Nobody blew them out. They were *taken*. Swallowed. Put away somewhere, whole and alive and screaming, the way the guild puts away everything it collects."

"Put away where," Ines said. Not a question. She already knew. Wren watched her know it.

"The vault," Wren said. "The first vault. The one your grandmother helped build, down in Vhal, before the city drowned. The stars are collateral, Ines. They're *collateral*. Somebody took out a loan against the sky."

* * *

Brannoc had stopped pretending to sleep. He sat forward into the firelight, tide-marks crawling on his throat, his face gray. "A loan," he said. "Against the stars. Signed by whom?"

Wren pulled her knees tighter. This was the worst part. This was the part she'd been chewing on for eleven days like gristle she couldn't swallow.

"That's the thing. The sea showed me. Not words—memories. It keeps memories the way the guild keeps ledgers. Everything that ever happened in it, filed away." She made herself look at Ines. "There was a signing. Long ago. Before the wards, before the covenant even, almost. A woman from your line—Reyes, but older spelling, the sea says it funny, *Rey-es*, like two words. She stood on a dock in Vhal harbor with three guild masters and she signed a contract written on vellum soaked in starlight, and what she bought was—"

She stopped. Because saying it would make it real, and some cowardly piece of her wanted another minute where it wasn't.

"Wren," Ines said quietly. "Say it."

"What she bought was time. Borrowed years. Enough borrowed years to survive a famine that should have killed your whole bloodline. A hundred years of life, drawn down against—" Wren spread her hands at the sky, at the black hole where the stars used to be. "Against that. Against the constellations themselves, held as security. And the terms were that if the debt was ever called—if the guild ever demanded payment—the stars would be forfeit. Swallowed. Held in the vault until the account was settled."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, the dead sea murmured agreement, checking her facts, finding them true.

"They let the wards fail on purpose," Ines said slowly. Wren recognized the tone—she was working it through, testing each link. "Corvane. He let the wards decay so the debt would get called, so the stars would default into the vault, so the guild would hold the only light left in the world. Monopoly on miracles."

"And on darkness," Brannoc said hoarsely. "Think what a candle costs when there are no stars. Think what a *blessing* costs."

"But that's not even the worst part," Wren said, and now her voice was shaking and she couldn't stop it, eleven days of shaking finally arriving all at once. "The worst part is the terms. The sea read me the contract. It was there, Ines. It was the harbor. It heard every word. And the repayment clause—the way you settle the account, the way you get the stars back out of the vault—isn't money. It was never money. The original signer wrote it herself, in her own hand, at the bottom of the page. She wrote: *this debt may be discharged by the signer's blood, freely given, or by the return of all that was borrowed.*"

"Her blood," Brannoc whispered. "An anchor. A life."

"One life," Wren said. "One Reyes life, freely given, and the stars go free and the debt closes forever. Or nobody pays, and the stars stay swallowed, and the guild owns the dark until the end of everything." She wiped her face with her sleeve, furious at herself for crying, furious at the sea for making her the one who had to say it. "And Corvane knows. He's always known. That's why he needs you alive, Ines. That's why he sealed the records and hunted us and let the wards break. You're not a fugitive to him. You're an *asset coming due*."

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The tide breathed against the sea-wall. Far off, something in the water sang three notes and stopped.

Ines stared into the fire. Wren watched her do the thing she did—go inward, go quiet, run numbers behind her eyes. Ledger-face, Wren called it. But this time the ledger-face cracked, just at the edges, and what showed through underneath was young and scared and about twenty-two years old, which was easy to forget, because Ines carried herself like a woman whose bones were older than her skin.

"My grandmother," Ines said at last. "My grandmother signed it. The woman who taught me the oath. Who taught me the ledgers. Who told me the guild's first rule was that everything has a price and the second rule was that you always pay fair." A short, terrible laugh. "She mortgaged the sky to keep our family alive through a famine, and then she built the ward system on top of the debt to hide it, and then she bound me to the oath before I was old enough to know what I was binding myself to. She wasn't keeping a tradition alive. She was keeping a *collateral arrangement* alive."

"She might have thought she was saving the world," Brannoc offered gently.

"She was saving *us*. Everyone else was fine print." Ines looked up, and her eyes were wet but her voice was level, and Wren thought: there it is, that's the thing about her, that's why I follow her. "So the choice is one life or the whole sky. That's the arithmetic. That's what Corvane will offer me down there—an honest trade, a fair price, everything aboveboard. One Reyes dies, the stars come home, the realms are saved, and the guild gets to look like the hero that arranged it all."

"You can't," Wren said, too fast, too loud.

"You don't know what I'm going to do."

"Yes I do. You've been getting quieter every day since the second stone. You've been *pricing yourself*. I can hear it—you think in ledger lines now, I hear it when you sleep, you're already writing yourself into the column marked *paid*." Wren was on her feet without remembering standing. "Well, I'm telling you the sea's version, and the sea's version is different. The sea says debts aren't paid by dying. The sea says debts are paid by *returning what was taken*. Your grandmother borrowed years. Fine—then give the years back. Give everything back. Every borrowed thing in every vault, poured out, returned, and the account closes because there's nothing left owed. That's the clause nobody reads. *Or by the return of all that was borrowed.* All of it. Not one life instead of it. Everything."

Brannoc had gone white. "Dissolving the hoard," he said. "Every impossible thing the guild holds. Bottled storms. Sold names. Borrowed years. Given back to the world." He laughed, a broken sound. "They would rather drown both realms."

"Then they can drown," Wren said, "because I am not letting her die for their bookkeeping."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. Then at Brannoc. Then at the black seam of the horizon where tomorrow's road waited, the drowned road, the road down into the glowing dark where the stolen stars hung in a vault like coins in a miser's fist.

"You've known this for eleven days," Ines said.

"Yeah."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

And Wren, who had rehearsed a dozen clever answers on the walk down from the bell-tower and discarded them all, found she only had the true one. "Because telling you felt like handing you the bill," she said. "And I didn't want to be the one who did that. I'm twelve and I steal things and I lie to customs men, and I didn't want the worst thing I ever did to be telling my friend she was born to be spent." Her chin wobbled. She locked it down. "But the sea wouldn't shut up, and it's right. You deserve the truth before you walk into that vault. Even if the truth is a bill. *Especially* if it's a bill."

Ines rose. For one horrible second Wren thought she was going to be angry, and braced for it, and found she'd rather be hit than pitied.

Instead Ines crossed the fire-pit and put her hand on Wren's head, briefly, awkwardly, the way you'd touch something you were afraid of breaking. "Thank you," she said. "For carrying it. You shouldn't have had to carry it alone, and I'm sorry you did, and I'm glad you're the one who told me. Of everyone in the world, I'm glad it was you."

Wren nodded hard and looked at the fire and did not cry, mostly.

Then Ines straightened, and the ledger-face came back—but different now. Harder. Brighter. A face doing arithmetic it intended to win.

"Tomorrow we go down," she said. "Into Vhal. Into the vault. And when Corvane shows me the contract and offers me the trade—one life against the sky—I'm going to show him the clause he's spent two hundred years hoping nobody reads." She touched the second stone through her shirt. "*All that was borrowed.* Every drop of it. We'll see how he likes a creditor who collects."

Below them, far down in the dark, the dead sea began—very softly—to sing.
