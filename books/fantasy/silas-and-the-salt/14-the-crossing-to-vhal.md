## 14. The Crossing to Vhal

The drowned road began where the old causeway ended, a seam of pale stone running down into black water like a staircase built for giants and abandoned halfway. At slack tide the sea went still around it—no waves, no current, just a vast held breath—and the lantern-fish rose.

They came up in slow spirals, hundreds of them, each no bigger than a fist, glowing a soft amber that turned the water into something like honeyed glass. Where they swam, the road showed itself: flagstones furred with weed, broken arches leaning like drunkards, the occasional statue with its face worn to a suggestion. A highway under a ceiling of ocean.

"Slack tide lasts four hours," Wren said, coiling rope with quick, practiced hands. "Maybe five if the dead sea's feeling generous. After that the road closes and anything on it gets chewed."

"You say that so calmly," Ines said.

"I say everything calmly. It's my best quality." Wren spat over the side, an old sailor's habit she'd learned from someone she refused to name. "Boat's ready. Get in or get left."

The boat was a smuggler's skiff, low and narrow, tarred black so it would be invisible from above. That was the plan, such as it was: ride the drowned road at slack tide, beneath the surface of things, while guild warships hunted the open water overhead. Brannoc had called it *traveling in the god's blind spot*. He'd said it quietly, the way he said most things now, as if every word about Vhal cost him something.

Ines climbed in. The two covenant-stones rode against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth, bound to her body with sail-twine. She'd stopped thinking of them as objects days ago. They were heavier than stone should be. They were *hungry* in a way she couldn't describe without sounding mad—the first one had taken three days of her memory, the second had doubled the weight of everything she carried, and both of them hummed against her sternum now like a second heartbeat that wasn't quite in time with her own.

She was forgetting who she was. She could feel the edges of it, soft places where hard facts used to be. She knew she was Ines Reyes. She knew she was the last swordspeaker. But when she reached for the memory of her mother's voice—the specific grain of it, the way she'd said *Ines* with the emphasis on the first syllable—she found a smooth blank wall, polished by use, and behind it nothing.

*You're paying,* Brannoc had told her. *That's what anchors do. The old ones paid in years. You're paying in self.*

*Then I'd better finish before there's no one left to pay.*

She took the tiller. Wren lay flat in the bow with her ear pressed to the boards, listening. Brannoc sat amidships with his ruined hands folded, murmuring the opening words of a rite he probably no longer believed in, the way a man whistles in the dark.

"Road's clear," Wren reported. "Dead sea says go."

Ines let the skiff slide off the causeway's edge and down.

* * *

Underwater was not what she expected. She'd imagined pressure and dark and terror. Instead the lantern-fish gathered around them as they descended, forming a loose corridor of light, and the drowned road unspooled below like a promise. The water here was strangely warm. Salt hung in it visibly, glittering threads drifting past the hull. Once, far off to the side, she saw a whole orchard standing submerged, trees still bearing fruit gone translucent as glass, and a stone well with a bucket still tied to its rope.

Villages. Whole villages, taken by the sea when Vhal fell—or when the wards began to fail, depending which history you believed. People had lived here. People had been told, by priests like Brannoc, that the border would hold forever.

"How deep does the road go?" she asked.

"Deep enough," Wren said, without lifting her ear from the boards. "Shh. Listening."

Ines shushed. The silence under the sea was enormous. It wasn't empty silence; it was *occupied*, like a room where someone has just stopped speaking. And threaded through it, faint as a pulse, she could hear the stones against her chest answering the water—a low harmonic, almost a word, almost a name.

"The dead sea talks all the time," Wren said suddenly, apropos of nothing, eyes closed. "Mostly it's boring. Fish things. Current things. Old arguments between rocks. But down here—" she tapped the hull twice, "—down here it's loud. It keeps saying the same thing over and over. It says *she's coming, she's coming, she's finally coming.*"

"Who?"

Wren opened one eye and looked back at Ines with an expression too old for her face. "You, obviously. Who else would the whole dead sea be waiting for?" She closed the eye again. "No pressure."

They traveled. Hours passed, marked only by the drift of the lantern-fish, which changed their formation as the road bent—sometimes a corridor, sometimes a ring, once, briefly, a shape that looked uncomfortably like a procession of mourners. Ines steered by them and by Wren's flat, calm reports: *left at the fallen tower. Straight past the bell. Don't touch the door.* There were doors down here. Doors standing alone in fields of silt, doors in cliff faces, one enormous door lying flat on the seabed like a fallen giant, and Wren said *don't touch any of them* in a tone that made Ines not want to ask why.

It was almost peaceful. That was the trap of it. The warmth, the light, the hush—it lulled you. Ines caught herself twice steering half-asleep, her mind sliding along grooves of memory that led nowhere, checking her inventory of self like a merchant counting stock. Her grandmother's kitchen: still there. The oath-lesson, age nine, her grandmother's hand closing hers around a practice blade: still there, though the words spoken in it had gone fuzzy. Sefa teaching her the seven parries of the guild style, laughing at her terrible footwork: there, but thinning, like cloth washed too many times. Corvane's face the day she signed her indenture—

Gone. She reached for it and found the wall again, and this time the wall was bigger.

*How many memories is that?* she wondered. *How much of me have I already spent?* She couldn't count what she couldn't remember missing. That was the elegant cruelty of it. The magic didn't take things you'd notice. It took load-bearing walls.

"Ines." Brannoc's voice, quiet. "Your hands."

She looked down. The tiller was slick. No—not slick. Glowing. Faint lines of light ran up her forearms where they'd touched the saltwater spray, tracing the old oath-marks that had been silent for a year and then woken screaming in the flooded chapel. The marks were *drinking*. Every drop of spray that touched her skin vanished into those lines like rain into cracked earth.

"It knows where we're going," Brannoc said. "The oath. It's waking up because we're close to where it was made."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know." He said it simply, without comfort. "Nothing that wakes up hungry is good. But it may be necessary."

Overhead, far above, something passed. They all felt it before they heard it—a pressure change, a shadow across the light. Wren's head came up off the boards like a hauled anchor line.

"Ship," she whispered. "Big one. Iron-bellied. Guild make." Her eyes unfocused, listening to something Ines couldn't hear. "Two more behind it. They're dragging nets. Not fishing nets." She swallowed. "Soul-nets. The kind they used to use for catching runaways who tried to swim the border."

"Depth?" Ines asked.

"They can't see us. The dead sea's folding the water over us like a blanket. But the nets are long." Wren's jaw tightened. "And the slack tide ends in an hour."

* * *

The nets came an hour and ten minutes later, which meant the dead sea had lied, or hoped, or both.

They came down through the light without warning—vast gray meshes, wide as cathedral doors, sinking slow and patient as jellyfish. The lantern-fish scattered from them instantly, and Ines understood: even fish that glowed knew to fear these. Where the netting passed through a stand of glass fruit, the fruit dimmed. Where it trailed through a school of small silver fish, the fish kept swimming but stopped being *there* somehow, their light going out from the inside.

"Soul-net," Brannoc breathed. "Down. Everyone down. Flat on the bottom of the boat."

They flattened. The net came down over them like the sky falling. Ines felt it touch the hull—a whisper of contact, cold beyond cold, and where it touched, the wood *grayed*, aging decades in a heartbeat. The skiff groaned.

"It's tasting us," Wren hissed. "It's tasting the boat, it'll taste us next—"

"Can it be cut?" Ines had her knife out before she finished asking.

"You can't cut a soul-net, it isn't *made* of anything—" Wren started.

But Brannoc had already moved. He surged up from the bilge, planted himself against the mast-step, and did something with his ruined hands—an old gesture, priestly, precise—that Ines had seen him use exactly once before, in Saltmark, to stop a mob. A rite of witness. *I see you. I name you. I remember you.*

The net flinched.

For one moment the whole gray mesh above them shuddered like a struck bell, and Ines heard—everyone heard—a sound come down through the water, vast and distant and furious: the sound of men shouting on a ship's deck, orders, alarm, the creak of winches hauling.

"They felt that," Brannoc gasped. He was white to the lips, swaying. "They'll know someone's under them. They'll drop the rest—"

He was right. The water above churned. More nets, a whole curtain of them, descending.

"We run," Ines said. "Full speed down the road. Now."

She threw her weight on the tiller and the skiff leapt forward—but the drowned road was not made for speed. It was ancient, cracked, choked in places with silt-drifts and collapsed arches, and the lantern-fish were scattering ahead of the nets, their beautiful corridor dissolving into panic. The light failed. The dark came down. Ines steered blind through a blackness so complete it felt physical, hearing Wren shout directions that contradicted each other because the dead sea itself was screaming now, hearing the nets whisper through the water behind them like the hem of a passing funeral gown.

An arch loomed out of the dark—too low, too late. Ines saw it a half-second before impact and hauled the tiller over, and the skiff scraped through with a shriek of wood on stone that she felt in her teeth, losing part of the gunwale, nearly losing Wren, who fetched up against the bow boards with a grunt and immediately started swearing with impressive fluency for a twelve-year-old.

Then the second net caught them.

It didn't catch the boat. It caught *her*—a trailing edge of mesh sweeping across the stern as they burst free of the arch, snagging her shoulder, her arm, the twine that bound the stones to her chest. Cold went through her like a lance of winter. She felt it start to pull—not her body, her body barely moved—but the *her* underneath, the continuity of her, the thread that made yesterday connect to today. Memories ripped loose. She felt them go: her tenth birthday, gone. The smell of the guildhouse library, gone. The name of the village where she'd been born—

*Gone.*

She was sliding. The net was reeling her out of herself, and the last thing she saw clearly was Wren's face, terrified, reaching for her, and Brannoc shouting something in the old tongue of Vhal, and then the skiff rolled, and the water took her.

* * *

Drowning, it turned out, was quiet.

The cold was gone. The net was gone—or she was through it, torn loose, sinking. Below her the drowned road glowed faintly where lantern-fish were regathering, brave amber sparks in the immense dark, and above her the surface was a distant silver ceiling getting further away with every heartbeat. Her lungs burned. Her body wanted air with a simple, total urgency that made every other thought irrelevant.

*Breathe,* said her body. *Breathe or die.*

She couldn't breathe. She was too deep, the surface too far, her limbs too heavy with wet wool clothes and two covenant-stones that dragged at her like sins. She kicked anyway. She clawed upward through water that had become solid, endless, patient. The silver ceiling didn't come closer.

The burning in her chest crested and broke into something worse: the awful calm that comes after panic, when the body starts making its final arrangements. Spots of light. Sounds going distant. And in that calm, absurdly, she thought of the ledger. Of entries. Of debts.

*Everything costs,* her grandmother had taught her. *The only question is whether you choose the price or the price chooses you.*

There was a price for breathing. She understood it suddenly, completely, the way you understand things at the edge of death: the oath-marks on her arms were awake, and awake, and hungry, and they could give her what she needed if she paid. The chapel had taken three days. The bell-tower had taken more. This would take—

She didn't let herself finish the thought. If she looked at what it might cost, she'd refuse, and refusal meant drowning, and drowning meant the stars stayed swallowed and Vhal stayed dead and Wren grew up alone in a world with no sky.

*I'm coming,* she told the water, or the oath, or whatever was listening. *Take what you need.*

She opened her mouth underwater and *breathed*.

The sea poured in and it was air, impossible sweet air, and the oath-marks blazed down her arms like brands of molten gold, and the payment came due in the same instant.

She felt it leave. Not pain—absence. A whole country of herself, evacuated in a single breath. She grabbed at it as it went and caught only edges: a woman's laugh. A harbor at dusk. Someone saying her name with love, over and over, a child's name, *little ledger, little ledger*—and then it was gone, all of it, a coastline slipping under a rising sea, and she floated in the after-silence of it trying to remember who had laughed, whose harbor, whose voice, and finding nothing but the shape of the hole where it had been.

She didn't know what she'd lost. That was the worst part. She would spend the rest of her life not knowing.

*You're becoming what you must be,* said a voice that might have been the oath, might have been the dead sea, might have been her own. *Stop counting the cost. Anchors don't get itemized.*

"No," Ines said, into the water, into the dark, her voice a stream of bubbles and defiance. "I keep the ledgers. Nobody spends me without an entry."

She swam.

* * *

They pulled her aboard the skiff forty feet above, Wren hauling on her collar with a strength that seemed impossible for her size, Brannoc holding the girl's belt so neither of them went over. Ines came up coughing seawater that tasted like starlight and old promises, and lay on the grayed, scarred planks, shaking, alive.

"You *idiot*," Wren said, kneeling over her, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "You absolute—you went under, the net had you, we thought—we thought—" She punched Ines's arm, lightly, twice, the way you punch someone when hugging them would break you open. "Don't do that."

"What did it cost?" Brannoc asked quietly. He always asked. He never flinched from the answer, and she loved him a little for that, in the tired way you love someone who shares your wounds.

"I don't know." Ines stared up at the underside of the sea, the distant silver ceiling beginning to pale toward some deep-water dawn. "That's the answer. I don't know. Something with a woman laughing in it. Something with a harbor." She worked moisture into her dry mouth. "Ask me what I remember and I'll tell you true: I remember the guild. I remember the vaults. I remember every debt in every ledger I ever audited. I remember Sefa's footwork drills and your rites and Wren's swearing. What I don't remember is—" her breath hitched, "—whatever came before all that. Whatever made me."

Wren sat down heavily beside her. For a while nobody spoke. The lantern-fish regathered around the skiff, tentative, forgiving, rebuilding their corridor of amber light, and the drowned road went on ahead of them, down and down, toward the place where no living thing returned unchanged.

"Hey," Wren said finally. "Whatever you lost. I'll remember you the parts you forget. That's how family works. Right? Somebody holds the pieces you drop."

Ines looked at her—at this fierce, sea-hearing child who had decided, somewhere between a river ambush and a night of terror, that these were her people—and felt something in the hollow where the memory had been, something new growing in the vacancy like green shoots in a burn scar.

"That's not how ledgers work," Ines said.

"Yeah, well." Wren shrugged. "Good thing we're not doing ledgers anymore."

Ahead, the road plunged into a trench of deeper dark, and at its edge the lantern-fish stopped—all of them, at once, refusing to go further, hovering in a trembling wall of amber light like a crowd at a threshold. Beyond it, far below, something glowed. Not fish-light. Not moonlight. A cold white radiance, star-colored, leaking up out of the deep like light through a locked door.

Brannoc stood up slowly. His face, when he turned to her, was the face of a man seeing his own grave.

"That's Vhal," he said. "The capital. And that light—" his voice failed, steadied, "—that's our stars, Ines. That's the hoard. We've found it."

From somewhere below, faint through fathoms of water, came a sound none of them expected: bells. Dozens of bells, ringing in the drowned city, though no living hand moved them.

The dead sea, Wren whispered, says they know you're coming.
