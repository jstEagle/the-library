## 15. The Drowned Capital

Brannoc had told himself he would not weep when he saw Vhal again. He was a liar, then, and had always been one; that was the first thing the sea taught him.

They came down the drowned road at slack tide, as Wren had promised, in the hour when the water forgot which way it wanted to move. Lantern-fish rose around the boat in slow constellations of their own making, blue-green and patient, lighting a road that had been a king's highway once—flagstones visible beneath them, cracked and furred with weed, running straight as an argument toward the place where his city stood.

Had stood. Stood still, in the way that drowned things stand: upright, ruined, and unwilling to fall.

"Your hands are shaking," Ines said quietly. She sat across from him with her back to the mast, two covenant-stones wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like sleeping infants. Since the bell-tower she had been quieter. He kept catching her testing her own memory the way a sailor tests a rope—one pull, another, checking for rot. *What year did I take my vows? What did my mother's voice sound like?* He could see the questions moving behind her eyes and finding nothing to hold.

"The water's cold," Brannoc said.

"It isn't," said Wren, at the tiller. She was twelve years old and steered their stolen skiff through a drowned kingdom by listening to its dead, and she had no patience whatsoever for grown people's lies. "The water's warm. It's been warm since the stars went out. It's *you* that's cold."

He looked at her—at the small fierce shape of her against the grey sky—and could not argue. The girl heard things under the water that no living ear should catch. For three days now she had been repeating fragments of what the dead sea told her, and every fragment had been worse than the last.

"They're singing tonight," she added, tilting her head. "The dead ones. They know you're coming."

"Do they?" His voice came out rough. "Then they know more than I do."

"No they don't." Wren's mouth twisted. "They're waiting to see if you'll apologize."

* * *

Vhal appeared out of the murk the way grief appears out of ordinary days—all at once, and everywhere.

It began with spires. Then rooftops, black and slick, breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. Then whole streets descending into green-gold light, because the city glowed. That was the horror of it, the thing Brannoc had failed to describe on the crossing and knew now he never would have managed: Vhal did not lie dark in its drowning. It burned from within, every window and archway and shattered dome lit from below by a light that had no business being there—a light like starlight, hoarded, compressed, leaking up through the seams of the world.

The swallowed stars. All of them. Poured into the guild's first vault at the heart of the city, and there they stayed, paying interest to no one, while above the water the night sky sat empty as a stripped table.

"I grew up in that quarter," Brannoc heard himself say. His hand rose without permission and pointed. "There. The terraced street with the broken arch. My mother sold salt-cured eel from a stall at the corner. On feast days the tide-priests would walk the whole length of it barefoot, and children would run ahead of them—" He stopped. The sentence had nowhere left to go. Every child who had ever run ahead of a tide-priest in that street was dead or changed beyond naming, and the priests themselves were worse than dead; they had been the ones who signed, who witnessed, who blessed the mortgage of heaven.

Ines said nothing. She only moved, deliberately, until her shoulder rested against his arm, and let him look.

Below them, at the edge of the glow, the tide-gates waited.

* * *

The tide-gates of Vhal had been built, in the old days, to admit pilgrims and exclude everything else. They were great bronze mouths set into the harbor wall, carved with the faces of the nine tides, and Brannoc had passed through them ten thousand times in his life without once thinking about the fact that they opened inward.

Everything about Vhal opened inward now. That was the nature of the debt. The city had become a throat.

"We go in at the slack," Wren said, "through the third gate. The sad one." She pointed at a face whose carving showed the tide weeping, channels worn deep by centuries of rain before the drowning. "She says she'll hold her breath for us."

"She talks to you about gates," Ines said.

"She talks to me about everything. You'd be amazed how bored dead cities get." Wren shipped the oars. "But she says hurry. There are boats already inside. Big ones. Guild boats."

Brannoc felt the words land in his stomach like stones. "How many?"

"Three warships. And something smaller that went in first, days ago, and hasn't come out." Wren's face did something complicated—young and old at once. "The dead don't like them. The masters. They say the masters walk through Vhal like it's empty. Like it's *inventory*."

Of course they do, Brannoc thought. That is the whole of the guild, distilled. A room full of miracles, and men who see only stock.

They slipped over the side in the last minutes of slack water, three swimmers and two stones wrapped in waxed canvas, and the weeping gate took them in.

* * *

Inside, Vhal was a cathedral of light and loss.

They swam the flooded boulevard beneath the surface for a quarter mile, following the lantern-fish, rising at last into an air pocket where a collapsed aqueduct had domed itself over a plaza. Brannoc broke the surface first and hauled himself onto a marble step, and the plaza spread out before him, and he made the mistake of recognizing it.

The Plaza of Nine Tides. Where he had been ordained. Where he had sworn, at nineteen, with seawater on his tongue and his whole family watching, to keep the balance between the living shore and the drowned deep. Where, eleven years later, he had stood with the other priests and countersigned the amendment—the quiet little clause, buried in a ledger's back pages, that let the Salt Guild defer the ward-renewals. Just for a season. Just to relieve a cash-flow problem. Just this once.

The plaza was lit gold from below, every crack in the flagstones glowing, and the fountain at its center still played—water rising and falling in a slow rhythm, though nothing fed it now but the sea itself, cycling through the pipes of a dead city like blood through a stopped heart.

On the fountain's rim, someone had arranged objects in a careful row. Boots. A child's wooden horse. A priest's collar, rotted soft. Offerings, Brannoc realized. The dead of Vhal, unable to leave, unable to rest, leaving gifts for whoever might someday come home and say their names.

His legs gave out. He sat down hard on the wet marble among the offerings, and Ines crouched beside him, and Wren stood watch with her head cocked, listening.

"You were here," Ines said. Not a question.

"I was here." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I want you to understand something, Ines Reyes, because you keep looking at me like I'm a guide and not what I am. I didn't lose my priesthood. I *resigned*. After the amendment passed, after I understood what we'd done—I walked out of the chapter house and I never came back, and I told myself that walking away was penance. That shame was a kind of payment." He lowered his hands. The golden light turned his tears strange, almost luminous. "It wasn't. Shame is just theft with extra steps. I stole my guilt from the people it belonged to. The people it *belongs* to are down there, in the dark streets, singing to a child in a boat."

Wren glanced back at him. "They say that's the first true thing you've said since the chapel," she reported. "They say keep going."

* * *

They moved through the city by Wren's navigation and Brannoc's memory, avoiding the open canals where guild lanterns swept, climbing through the bones of buildings—up staircases that ended in air, along rooftops furred with kelp, across a bridge whose balustrade had become a garden of white coral. The vault lay at the center of everything, as it had in life: the Underhall, the first repository, dug into the bedrock before the guild had a name, before it had anything but a covenant and a hunger.

From a high gallery they looked down into the approach to the Underhall, and Brannoc's blood went cold.

The masters had arrived first.

Guild engineers had strung lamps through the flooded halls—air-fed, hissing, casting hard white circles on the stone. Scaffolding climbed the walls of the approach channel. A winch barge sat anchored where the great doors used to be, and men in guild grey worked its ropes with the brisk efficiency of people doing a job, hauling crates up from the depths. And on a platform of planking at the water's edge, guarded by six enforcers, sat a stone.

Even at this distance, even through water and lamplight, Brannoc knew it. The third covenant-stone, pried from whatever shrine had held it these hundred years, sitting in a crate lined with straw like a thing to be sold.

"They've got it," Ines breathed. Her hand found the two stones beneath her coat, and Brannoc watched her knuckles whiten. "Two of three. If they take the third back across the border—"

"They won't take it anywhere," Wren said softly. "Listen." Her eyes had gone distant, unfocused, the way they did when the dead spoke loudest. "There's something else down there. Under the vault. Something the masters woke up when they started pulling stones. It's *old*, older than the loan, older than the guild. The dead call it the First Creditor." She shivered. "And Corvane is here. He's down there right now. Negotiating."

"Negotiating with what?"

"With the thing that holds the stars."

* * *

They found a way down through the old pilgrim stairs—a route Brannoc had taken as a boy, dry-shod, carrying candles; now it was a drowned throat they descended with lungs full of borrowed air from a diving-bell improvised out of a guild supply crate Wren had spotted and stolen with breathtaking speed. Twice they froze in alcoves while patrols passed, close enough to hear the enforcers complaining about the damp, about the pay, about the way the light down here made shadows move wrong.

The shadows did move wrong. Brannoc saw it himself: shapes in the lamplight that paused when the men paused, that leaned when the men leaned, half a heartbeat behind—as if the drowned city were trying on the gestures of the living and hadn't yet got the timing right.

At the last landing, above the vault doors themselves, Wren caught Brannoc's sleeve.

"There's a door," she whispered. "A little one. Behind the third pillar. The dead use it. They say it goes into the vault's upper gallery—the counting room, where the signers used to sit." Her grip tightened. "Brannoc. They say *your name* opens it. They've been keeping it unlocked for you. For eleven years."

He stared at her. "That's not how locks work."

"That's not how *their* locks work." Wren shrugged. "Dead cities make their own rules. Are you going or not?"

He thought of the offerings on the fountain rim. The collar, rotted soft. He thought of the amendment, and his signature on it, small and neat and damning. He thought: *this is what penance actually looks like—not walking away, but walking back.*

"Yes," he said.

* * *

The little door was exactly where the dead said it would be, and it opened at the touch of his palm as though it had been oiled yesterday. Beyond it, a narrow gallery ran above the vault floor, screened by a lattice of bronze that had once let the signers watch their inventory without breathing the same air as it.

Brannoc looked down through the lattice, and the breath went out of him.

The vault was a sphere, a hollowed pocket in the bedrock, and it was full of light. Not lamplight. Starlight—actual starlight, the swallowed constellations of a hundred nights, swirling in slow rivers through the flooded dark like milk stirred into ink. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and it was also, unmistakably, a prison: the light moved in patterns too regular, too confined, currents circling walls that held them, and everywhere the light touched, Brannoc could feel the pressure of it, like standing near a dam.

Below, on a platform of planks at the sphere's center, Master Corvane stood with his hands clasped behind his back, addressing the light itself. Beside him, two clerks held ledgers open against the spray. And floating in the starlit water before him, coalescing and dissolving, vast and unhurried, was a shape that Brannoc's training named and his courage flinched from: the First Creditor, the old power beneath the loan, wearing a body made of the same light it held lien upon.

Corvane's voice carried up through the lattice, calm as a man discussing freight rates.

"—the terms remain as drafted. One anchored life, freely offered, in exchange for release of the stellar collateral. The realm keeps its sky. The guild keeps its charter. Everyone's accounts balance." A pause. "You'll find my signature adequate. I have never defaulted."

The shape in the water turned—or seemed to turn—and Brannoc felt its attention pass over the gallery like a searchlight, and beside him he heard Ines stop breathing.

Because Ines was there. Of course she was there; she had come down the pilgrim stairs at his shoulder, and now she stood at the lattice with both covenant-stones against her chest, looking down at the man who had offered her partnership and then planned to spend her life as masonry.

"He's selling me," she whispered. "He's already priced me."

"And he's shortchanged you," Brannoc whispered back, and the fury in his own voice surprised him—it was the fury of a man standing in the ruins of his home, holding the door key the dead had kept for him. "Look at the terms. *One* life, *freely offered*—but the anchor has to be bound, Ines. Bound means chosen by the stone, not the seller. Corvane isn't negotiating. He's committing fraud against something that eats fraudsters for interest."

Below, the First Creditor gathered itself taller, and its voice, when it came, was the sound of a tide going out over gravel—vast, patient, and absolutely without mercy.

"The collateral was never yours to trade, guildmaster. Bring me the signer's heir, or bring me nothing, and default."

Corvane's composure flickered—just once, just slightly, the first hairline crack in all that arithmetic.

And in the gallery above, Ines Reyes drew a long, slow breath, and Brannoc saw her look at the two stones in her arms, and then at the third in its crate far below, and then at the swirling hoard of stolen sky—and he recognized the expression on her face, because he had seen it once before, years ago, in a mirror, on the day he decided to burn his own life down rather than live inside the lie of it.

"Ines," he murmured. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"Stay close," she said. "Both of you. And when I tell you to run—" her jaw set, "—don't."

She stepped up onto the gallery rail, high above the vault floor, directly in the First Creditor's line of sight, and the whole drowned capital seemed to lean in to listen.

"You asked for the signer's heir," Ines Reyes called down, her voice ringing off the starlit water. "Here I am. And I've read the contract. *All* of it—including the parts my grandmother never showed you."

The First Creditor went very, very still.

"So have I," said Ines. "And I'm calling the loan due."
