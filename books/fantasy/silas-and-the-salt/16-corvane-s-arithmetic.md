## 16. Corvane's Arithmetic

They took her sword at the tide-gates, and her boots at the vault door.

The boots bothered Ines more. Barefoot on the drowned marble of Vhal's great hall, she felt every seam and salt-crusted joint through her soles, as if the city itself were taking inventory of her. Two guild enforcers walked behind her with the patient courtesy of men who had already won. Ahead, lantern-fish drifted past the high broken windows in slow silver constellations, and beyond them—through forty feet of green water—the sky was a flat, starless dark that pressed down like a lid.

Corvane had lit candles.

That was the first thing she understood about him here, in his element: he liked small lights in large dark places. Dozens of them, guttering on iron stands arranged around the vault's central dais, each flame reflected doubled in the water film across the floor. It made the hall look endless. It made him look, when he turned from the far side of the chamber, almost gentle—a gray-bearded man in traveling wool, ink stains on his fingers, an accountant surprised by visitors.

"Ines." He said her name the way he said everything: as though reading it aloud from a well-kept book. "You look tired. Sit, if you like. The chairs are original Vhalan. They've held better weight than ours."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." He gestured, and the enforcers withdrew to the doors. Not gone. Never gone. "Do you know what this room is?"

She looked past him. The vault proper lay open behind the dais—not the Salt Guild's coastal strongrooms she'd audited for eleven years, all iron and ledger-lock, but something older. A circular chamber sunk into the heart of the drowned palace, its walls lined with niches, and in the niches—

Her breath caught despite herself.

Stars.

Not all of them. Not even a tenth of them. But enough. They hung in their niches like eggs in a dovecote, burning without heat, without flicker, each one wrapped in a shroud of woven dark that muted it to a coal-red ember. She could feel them the way you feel a fire in the next room. A pressure. A warmth with nowhere to go. The whole chamber hummed at the edge of hearing, a note held for centuries by throats long dead.

"The original vault," Corvane said. "Older than the Guild. Older than the covenant, if you can believe that. Silas built it before he signed anything." He smiled faintly. "He was a hoarder, our Silas. Everyone remembers him as the martyr. Nobody remembers he was also the first man to look at a miracle and ask what it would fetch."

"You keep them in cages."

"We keep them in *storage*." He corrected her the way he'd corrected junior clerks for decades, mild and immovable. "There is a difference, and the difference is everything I have spent my life defending. Come here. I want to show you an entry."

"I've read your entries my whole life."

"Not this one." He lifted a slim volume from the dais—black leather, silver corners, older than any ledger in the coastal archives. He held it out. "Page one. Read it properly. Aloud, if you can manage it. Some debts only hold their shape when spoken."

She didn't take it. So he set it down on the dais, open, and stepped back, and waited, because he was very good at waiting; it was the guild's oldest weapon and he wielded it like a master.

Ines told herself she wouldn't look. Then she looked.

The hand was her grandmother's. She knew it instantly—the sharp downstrokes, the way Meren Reyes crossed her sevens like a woman daring the numbers to lie. Ines had learned her letters copying that hand. She had thought, until this moment, that she would know anything her grandmother wrote on sight, the way you know your own name shouted across a courtyard.

She had never seen this.

*Borrowed against: the light of heaven, being one hundred and nine stars, principal and interest.*

*Collateral held in trust by the undersigned, for the term of the covenant, renewable upon payment of the annual tithe.*

*Signed under seal, Meren Reyes, Swordspeaker of the Salt Guild, anchor of the border ward, in the name of her blood and her blood's heirs unto the last of them.*

Below the signature, in a different hand—older, wetter-looking, the letters slumped like drowned men—was a second mark. Not a name. A spiral, drawn in what the ledger's margins helpfully identified, in faded red, as *seawater and blood*.

"The Drowned King's own sign," Brannoc had called it, once, in the boat, explaining why the third stone could only be found where the old capital kept its secrets. Ines stared at it now and felt the floor tilt gently under her bare feet.

"She mortgaged the sky," Ines said. Her voice came out flat. Audit-flat. The voice she used for numbers that were too large to be felt. "One hundred and nine stars. As *collateral*."

"As security," Corvane said. "For the ward. You've seen the wards fail, Ines. You watched them go. Do you imagine the first ones were weaker? The border between realms is a wound that never closes. Someone had to hold it shut, and holding it shut costs—" he spread his hands at the humming niches, at the shrouded embers, "—this. Light is the only currency the deep respects. So we paid in light. We borrowed against heaven itself, and for three hundred years the arrangement held, and the tithe was paid, and nobody drowned who didn't have to."

"And then the tithes stopped."

"And then the tithes stopped," he agreed pleasantly. "Because paying them required a swordspeaker willing to bleed into a stone every solstice, and the last three generations of Reyes women found increasingly creative ways to consider that someone else's duty. Your grandmother's grandmother deferred. Your grandmother deferred. And then there was you, and the oath went quiet, and Master Sefa reported—faithfully, I might add—that the anchor-line had frayed to a single thread." He tilted his head. "Did you think the stars went out as some cosmic accident? Debts don't default quietly, Ines. The sea simply collects."

The hum of the vault seemed louder. Or perhaps that was her own blood.

"So this is collection," she said.

"This is *arithmetic*." He picked up the ledger again, cradling it, and for a moment the accountant fell away and she saw what lived underneath: not cruelty. Something worse. Conviction. "Let me show you the sum, since no one else ever has. One anchored life keeps the ward standing. One life, bound to the stones, bleeding on schedule, holding the border for—what did your grandmother's contract say? Unto the last of her blood. That's you. That's the position you were born to, the same as I was born to count. One life, Ines. Against that—" he nodded upward, at the lid of dark sky, "—two realms. Every harbor from here to the Serpent Coast. Every village where children sleep unguarded while things with too many joints walk the tideline. You've seen Saltmark. You know what comes inland when the ward is down. One life against all of that. Show me the column where that isn't the cheapest entry on the page."

"You're describing my death."

"I'm describing a *price*. Death is what happens when you refuse to name prices. Everything costs. The only question worth asking is who pays." He set the ledger down and stepped closer, and his voice softened into the register he'd used on her once, years ago, when she was nineteen and had flagged a trade in bottled griefs as predatory. *You're right,* he'd said then. *And the grieving widow still paid, because the storm didn't care that the price was cruel.* "You've carried the oath your whole life. You know better than anyone that it was never going to release you. The choice was never whether you'd pay. The choice is whether your payment buys something or nothing. Bound to the vault, renewed each year, you hold the border for a century. Refuse, and the sea takes the collateral—all of it, permanently—and the border stays open forever, and everything after that is just a longer list of names in a longer ledger. I am offering you the only version of this story where you matter."

"And Wren?" Ines asked. "Twelve years old. Does she get a column?"

"Wren is a rounding error."

The words landed like a slap, and Corvane watched her take the blow with the same mild patience, because he had wanted exactly that—he needed her angry, angry was legible, angry was a number he could work with.

"No," Ines said slowly. "Wren is the whole point. That's the difference between us. You look at a child and see a cost. I look at a child and see—" She stopped. Breathed. "You want me to do arithmetic. Fine. Here's mine. My grandmother signed away a hundred and nine stars to avoid paying a debt honestly. Three generations of Reyes women deferred rather than bleed on schedule. And every deferral made the interest worse, and every shortcut moved the cost onto someone poorer—onto Saltmark, onto the mire-things, onto a drowned kingdom that never agreed to any of it. Your cheap entry was never cheap, Corvane. It was *deferred*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Something flickered across his face. Not doubt. Recognition—the look of a man hearing his own argument quoted back with the errors marked in red.

"The covenant can't be remade," he said. "It can only be honored or defaulted. Those are the options. Blood signed it; blood settles it."

"Then watch me void it."

"You can't. The oath doesn't answer to you. It answers to the *line*—"

"It stopped answering to me months ago." She took a step toward the dais, barefoot, and the vault's hum shifted, and every shrouded star in every niche brightened by a fraction, like eyes opening behind gauze. Corvane's head turned sharply toward the sound. "Did you wonder why? Did any of you ever wonder? An oath isn't a chain, it's a promise—and a promise made by one bloodline to hold a wound shut alone was always going to fail, because no one person can hold a wound shut alone. It went quiet because it was *waiting*. Waiting for someone to stop honoring it and start ending it."

"That's not how contracts work."

"No," Ines said. "That's how drowning works. And you'd know, if you'd ever been in the water instead of counting it."

The nearest niche cracked.

A hairline fracture, spidering through stone older than the guild, and through it leaked a thread of true light—white, fierce, unshrouded—and the hum rose half a tone, and Corvane took one step back from the dais, the first uncalculated movement she had ever seen him make.

"Ines." His voice had changed. The accountant was gone; underneath was something older and more afraid. "Ines, listen to me. If the hoard breaches, the sea collects *everything*. Not the ward. Everything. The vault holds back more than starlight—it holds the terms. Break it and there are no more ledgers, no more prices, no more *arrangement*. Just the deep, and the deep does not negotiate."

"Good," Ines said.

He stared at her.

"Good," she said again, quieter. "Because I'm done negotiating on behalf of people who never got a seat at the table. My grandmother sold the sky. Your masters let the wards rot to corner the market on salvation. And every one of you called it arithmetic." She looked up at the niches, at the hundred and nine caged fires, and felt the oath stir in her chest—not the silent, sullen thing of these past months but something rising, something with her grandmother's sharp downstrokes and Silas's hoarder hunger and three hundred years of deferred interest all coming due at once. "I'm settling the account. All of it. Principal, interest, and the guild's cut."

"You'll kill us all."

"No. I'm going to forgive us all. There's a difference." She met his eyes. "And the difference is everything."

The second niche cracked. Then the third. Corvane shouted for his enforcers, and the vault doors boomed open behind her, and through the ringing in her ears Ines heard—faint, impossible, coming up through forty fathoms of drowned city—Wren's voice, thin and clear as a whistle over water:

*"Ines! The sea says NOW!"*

She put her hand flat against the dais, against the black ledger with her grandmother's signature, and spoke the words she had been carrying since Saltmark, since the chapel, since a childhood traded away in a training yard:

"Covenant of the Reyes line—I dissolve thee. Let the debt fall open. Let the promise be remade."

The vault began, very softly, to come apart.
