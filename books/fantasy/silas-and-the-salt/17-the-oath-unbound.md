## 17. The Oath Unbound

The vault of Vhal had no door. It had a mouth.

Ines stood at the edge of it, wrists bound in salt-cord, and looked down into the place where the sky had been kept. The chamber descended in terraces of black coral, and every terrace was stacked with the guild's oldest inventory: jars of bottled dawn, casks of borrowed years gone cloudy with age, and above it all—suspended in a lattice of silver chain like fruit on a terrible tree—the swallowed stars.

They were not dead. That was the worst of it. They burned behind their cages with the patient light of things that had been promised back and never returned. Their glow filled the drowned capital the way guilt fills a house: everywhere, touching everything, warming nothing.

"You see the problem," Corvane said. He stood beside her at the terrace's rim, hands folded behind his back, a man admiring his own cellar. "Light wants to be free. Hoarded light is a debt accruing interest. Every year we keep it, the wards weaken further, because the covenant was never meant to hold this much withheld promise." He turned to her, and his smile was the one he used in negotiations, the one that had closed a thousand impossible trades. "Unless we change what the covenant is."

"Your arithmetic again."

"My *solution*." He gestured, and two enforcers brought forward a slab of pale stone—the third covenant-stone, torn from its resting place, still weeping brine. "One anchored life. Yours. Bound to all three stones, fused into the ward itself. You would feel every tide for ten thousand years, yes. But the realms hold. The trade routes reopen. The guild survives. One life against two worlds, Ines. Show me the ledger where that's a bad entry."

Behind him, held between more enforcers, Brannoc knelt in the flooded gallery with seawater running from his braids, and Wren was screaming something Ines couldn't hear through the press of bodies—and Sefa wasn't there at all, which meant either she'd failed them or she'd kept her word about three days, and Ines had spent those days getting caught anyway.

She looked at the stone. She looked at the stars.

Her grandmother's signature was somewhere in Corvane's ledgers, inked a lifetime before Ines was born: *the heavens held as collateral until the first debt is paid.* Her whole bloodline had been collateral since then. The oath in her chest—the one that had gone silent, then woken, then grown heavy as ballast with each stone she bound—wasn't a gift from her ancestors. It was a lien.

"Here's what you've never understood about ledgers," Ines said. "I've kept them my whole life. Debts, obligations, terms of payment. And there's one thing in every contract I've ever recorded that people forget."

"Enlighten me."

"A contract requires two willing parties." She met his eyes. "And mine died with the last person who understood what she was signing."

Corvane's smile thinned. "You'll be bound regardless. The stones don't need your consent. Only your blood."

"I know." Ines let out a breath, long and slow, and felt something in her go very quiet and very clear, the way the sea went clear before a storm made landfall. "That's why I'm not going to be your anchor, Master Corvane. I'm going to do something much worse."

She began, in the old tongue of the Salt Guild, the tongue of contracts and closings, to speak.

* * *

Every swordspeaker learned the forms of binding before they learned to hold a blade. *I swear by salt and season. I swear by the ledger and the light.* Words that locked an oath into the marrow, that made a promise heavier than iron. Ines had spoken them at nine years old, kneeling on cold tile while her grandmother's hand pressed her shoulder like a hand pressing a seal into wax.

But there were other words. Older ones. The ones in the archive's deepest folio, the page Corvane had never let anyone read because they were bad for business.

Words of dissolution.

Her voice carried strangely underwater—or perhaps it didn't carry at all, and simply *was*, filling the vault the way the starlight did. "*I, Ines Reyes, last speaker of the founding line, holder of the anchor-oath, do declare the covenant broken at its root. Not defaulted. Dissolved. Let no blood inherit what no blood agreed to. Let the hoard return to the world that lent it. Let the debt be unmade—not paid, not forgiven, but unmade, as if the signing hand had never pressed pen to page.*"

"No," Corvane said. All the negotiation had drained out of his voice. What remained was naked, startled greed-fear, the sound of a man watching his inventory catch fire. "Seize her—*stop her mouth*—"

The enforcers moved. They were too slow, because the vault had already heard her.

The first star slipped its chain.

It came loose without violence—a link of silver simply opening, the way a hand opens—and the star rose off its terrace like a bubble rising through deep water, trailing light, and where it passed, the bottled dawns on the shelves began to glow and strain against their glass. A cask of borrowed years split along its seam and spilled decades across the coral floor, and the decades *moved*, quicksilver-bright, flowing toward the open water like salmon going home.

"*Hold the vault!*" Corvane roared. "Net the lights! Do you know what that stock is worth—"

A second star slipped free. Then a third. The lattice of chains groaned, a sound like a ship breaking up, and the whole drowned city shuddered around them as something vast and ancient felt its collar come undone.

Brannoc was laughing. Kneeling in the floodwater, bound, half-drowned twice over, the disgraced tide-priest threw back his head and laughed with tears cutting lines through the salt on his face. "You hear it?" he shouted to Ines over the rising roar. "The sea remembers! It's taking back what was traded—it's *taking it all back*—"

Wren had stopped screaming. She stood among the frozen enforcers with her head cocked, listening to whatever the dead sea said to her, and her face was wet and fierce and young. "Ines!" she called. "It says hurry! It says the sky's been holding its breath for a hundred years and it can't hold much longer!"

Ines kept speaking. The dissolution-words poured out of her, and each phrase cost her something she could feel leaving—a memory loosening, a name going soft at the edges—but she found she didn't care, because this was different from the stones. The stones had taken payment. This was *spending*. Deliberate. Chosen. There was a difference between bleeding and giving blood, and she finally knew it in her bones.

Corvane lunged for her himself, no more enforcers, no more intermediaries—just a man and his hoard drowning together. His fingers closed on her throat, and even now, even here, he tried to bargain. "Stop the words and I'll forgive the guild's debts. All of them. Every ledger. You want mercy? This is mercy, Ines—"

"This," Ines said, and her voice didn't shake, "is *dissolution*."

The third star broke free.

The shockwave took Corvane off his feet. The salt-cord on Ines's wrists parted like thread. Light filled the vault—not the hoarded, sullen glow of captivity but living light, moving light, streaming upward through the drowned streets of Vhal in rivers and ribbons, and everywhere it touched, the dead city woke. Windows lit in towers that had been dark for a century. Lantern-fish scattered like sparks. Somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms of water, Ines heard the border wards begin to sing.

The stones answered.

All three of them—the one bound to her ribs, the one at her hip, the third still clutched in an enforcer's forgotten arms—began to hum in harmony, pulling toward each other, pulling toward *her*, and Ines understood with terrible clarity that dissolution was only half the work. You could void a covenant. But a void left unfilled would suck both realms into it like water through a cracked hull.

The covenant had to be remade. Tonight. Here. With the vault coming apart around them.

"Ines!" Wren fought free of the chaos, sprinting across a terrace awash in spilled years, and shoved something into her hands: the fragment of covenant-stone Brannoc had carried out of the sea, the first piece, the one that had started everything. "The dead sea says four hands! It's been saying it the whole time—*four hands, four hands*—"

Four.

Ines looked up and saw Sefa standing at the vault's mouth, dripping, breathing hard, having cut her way through the guild's own rear guard to get here—too late to stop any of this, and exactly on time for what came next.

The ceiling of the vault groaned. Above them, the sea gathered itself to come down.

* * *

"Take my hand," Ines said, "and don't let go, no matter what it asks of you."

Sefa stared at her—at the light pouring off her skin, at the stones singing in the floodwater—and for one heartbeat the old mentor-look crossed her face, the one that had guided Ines through her first blade-forms, her first ledgers, her first betrayals. Then Sefa stepped forward and gripped the fragment-stone beside her, and said, "You always did find the most expensive way to be right."

Brannoc came up on her other side, hauling himself upright with a tide-priest's stubborn grace. "Vhal stands witness," he said, voice breaking. "Let it be witnessed."

Wren took the last place, small hand wrapping around the stone, and grinned up at Ines with seawater in her teeth. "Told you the sea liked you."

Above them, the vault's roof gave way, and the ocean came down like judgment—

—and Ines Reyes, last of her line and first of something new, opened her hands, and let the stars go home.
