## 19. Stars Returning

Wren had never seen a star before. She'd heard about them, of course—old sailors talked about them the way they talked about wives they'd lost, soft and far away and never coming back. The dead sea didn't sing about stars. It sang about pressure and cold and the long dark weight of things that had sunk. So when the sky began to come apart into little burning holes, Wren's first thought was that it was a trick, and her second thought was that she was dying, and her third thought—the one that stuck—was *so that's what all the fuss is about.*

She was lying on the tide-gate steps with Brannoc's cloak over her and Ines's hand still tangled in hers, because none of them had managed to let go since the vault ruptured, and the light came down through the water like coins dropped through green glass.

"Count them," Ines whispered. Her voice was wrecked. Everything about her was wrecked—her hair half gone to salt-burn, her hands scarred where the stones had held on too tight. "I used to count them when I couldn't sleep. I want to know if I remember right."

"You don't," Brannoc said. He was sitting up, which surprised Wren; she'd been fairly sure he was dead for most of the night. His tide-priest's tattoos had gone from black to a pale silver, like frost on a window. "There are more now. There were always more. We just stopped being able to see how many we owed."

The water around them was full of light. Not the hoarded kind—not the greedy, stacked-up glow of the guild's original vault, which had burned like a miser's chest pried open—but loose light, moving light, pouring up out of the drowned city in slow ribbons and climbing toward the surface as if it had somewhere to be and had finally remembered where.

Vhal itself was changing. Wren could hear it better than any of them—the dead sea had quieted to almost nothing, and in its place there was something else, something she didn't have a word for yet. A settling. Like a house at night after everyone stops arguing.

"The bells," she said.

Brannoc went very still. "What about them?"

"They're ringing." She tilted her head. All around them, across the sunken terraces and drowned avenues of the capital, the old temple bells were swinging in the current—and instead of the groaning, drowning sound they'd made before, they rang clear. Bright. Underwater bells shouldn't sound like that. Underwater bells should sound like grief. "They're ringing *right*, Brannoc."

He put his face in his hands. Wren thought he was crying, but she'd learned you couldn't tell with tide-priests; sometimes they were just praying with their whole bodies.

* * *

They surfaced an hour later, or what Wren guessed was an hour later, because time underwater had gone strange and syrupy, and came up into the gray edge of dawn off the Vhalen coast. Their boat was where Sefa had left it, tied to a stanchion that had spent the last century under ten fathoms and now stood in knee-deep water, crusted white with salt but standing. The sea was going out. Not the ordinary going-out of a tide—the *going-out*, the great recession, water sliding back off land it had no business touching, uncovering fields and roads and the foundations of villages that had been fish territory for three generations.

Wren stood in the bow and listened to it go, and it sounded like a crowd leaving a festival, tired and happy and talking among themselves.

"It's not coming back the same," she said. "The sea. It'll come back, but—" She frowned, trying to translate. The whispers weren't words exactly; they were more like weather you caught in your teeth. "It's not angry anymore. It's just going to be the sea."

"That would be new," Ines said.

Ines looked terrible. Wren kept checking on her sideways, the way you check a candle you're not sure is going to make it to morning. During the reforging, when the four of them had held the stones and the hoarded starlight had torn loose and poured skyward through their hands, Wren had felt Ines's grip change—had felt something *leave* her, some load-bearing thing inside, the way a rope goes slack when the anchor cuts free. She hadn't said anything. There hadn't been time. There still wasn't, quite, but the sun was coming up and nobody was trying to kill anybody, so Wren figured the accounting could start.

"Your oath," she said. Blunt, because she was twelve and tired and blunt was all she had left in the tank. "Is it gone?"

Ines was quiet for a while. The dawn came up the color of pewter, then oyster, then a thin embarrassed pink.

"I can't feel it," she said finally. "For months it was silent—I told myself it was dormant, that it was sulking, that it would wake up if I did everything right." She turned her hands over, looked at the palms like they belonged to a stranger. "Now it's not silent. It's just... not there. Like a tooth that finally came out. My tongue keeps going to the gap."

"Does it hurt?" Wren asked.

"No," Ines said. And then, quieter, in a voice that made Wren's chest do something complicated: "That's the strange part. It doesn't hurt at all."

Brannoc hauled himself over the gunwale and sat down hard, spraying brine. Up close his silver tattoos were fading even as Wren watched, the old vows washing out of his skin like ink in rain. He noticed her looking and gave her a lopsided grin.

"Don't grieve for them, little eel. They were someone else's promises. I've got room for new ones now."

"Yeah?" Wren said. "Like what?"

"Breakfast," said Brannoc, "for a start."

* * *

They made landfall at a fishing camp two miles up the coast—a scatter of drying racks and lean-tos where the people who'd fled Saltmark and the other walking-sea villages had gathered to wait out the end of the world. Wren watched the camp from the boat as they came in, and she saw the moment it happened: a woman standing at the tideline, pointing east, screaming something, and then everybody running—not away, but *toward*, toward the water, toward the horizon, and Wren realized they were pointing at the sky.

By the time they beached the boat, the whole camp was on the shingle, faces tipped back, making sounds Wren had only ever heard at funerals and births.

The constellations were wrong. That was the thing nobody had warned anybody about. People remembered the Salt Road, the Fisher's Yoke, the Weeping Sisters—and the stars were all there, but rearranged, shifted, older somehow, patterns that predated every chart in the guild archives. Brannoc stared up at them and started laughing, this cracked, disbelieving laugh, and when Ines raised an eyebrow at him he wiped his eyes and said:

"These are the *first* charts. The ones from before the covenant. The sky's showing us what it looked like when Silas signed." He shook his head. "We mortgaged the stars and the interest compounded for nine generations. Of course they moved. Everything moves when you owe it money."

A crowd had gathered around them by now, and Wren felt the mood of it shift the way she always felt weather shift—through her skin, through her back teeth. These were people whose houses the sea had eaten. Whose children had nearly been taken by the nightly walk of the water inland. Whose priests had told them it was punishment, whose smugglers had told them it was profit, and here came three soaked figures out of the drowned kingdom itself.

An old man with a gutting knife on his belt pushed to the front. Wren's hand found her belt-knife without her telling it to.

"You're guild," he said. Flat. Not a question.

"I *was*," Ines said. She didn't flinch. Wren admired that about her, in the way you admire someone standing on ice you know is thin. "I'm not anymore. The guild's ledgers burned last night—all of them. Every debt in them."

"Liar," someone said from the back. But it was a hopeful liar-sounding voice, the way people say *liar* when they're praying.

"Not a liar," said a fourth voice, and Sefa came down the shingle with her enforcer's coat unbuttoned and her sword peace-knotted at her hip, and the crowd parted around her like she was made of knives, which, fair. "I was the guild's chief hunter until roughly midnight. Ask me anything. Ask me what Corvane ordered done to this woman." She jerked her chin at Ines. "Ask me why I'm not doing it."

The old man with the gutting knife looked at Ines for a long time. Then he looked at the sky. Then he looked at the sea—which was out past the low-tide mark further than anyone living had ever seen it go, glittering and flat and, for the first time in a year, *quiet*.

"What do we do now?" he asked, and it wasn't hostile anymore. It was the question of a man holding a tool he no longer knew the use of.

"Honestly?" Ines said. "I have no idea. I've spent my whole life keeping accounts. I've never once had to figure out what happens after they're forgiven." She looked at the crowd, at their ruined, exhausted, upturned faces, and something in her steadied. "But I think maybe that's the work now. Figuring it out together. Nobody signs alone ever again."

Wren watched the crowd take that in. It went slowly, like water into dry ground. Then somebody started laughing—really laughing, the unhinged relief-laugh of people who'd spent a year waiting to drown—and it spread, and the old man with the gutting knife sat down right there on the wet shingle and laughed until he wheezed, and then people were hugging each other and yelling at each other and a kid Wren's age ran down to the boat and touched the hull like it was a relic.

Wren stayed by the water. This was her spot. Crowds were fine but crowds plus feelings were a lot, and besides, she wanted to listen.

The sea whispered. Not the dead-whispers she'd carried all her life, not the cold pressing voices of drowned things wanting things. Just... sea-noise. Waves doing wave business. It was the quietest her head had been since she was small, and she found she didn't know what to do with the space.

"You okay, little eel?" Brannoc had come to stand beside her, breakfast-less but patient.

"Yeah," Wren said. Then, because lying to him felt pointless: "No. I don't know. The voices are gone. The dead sea—it's not talking anymore. It's done. Whatever it needed me for, it's finished." She kicked at the sand. "I spent my whole life being the girl who hears the dead sea. That was the whole job. Who am I if the sea shuts up?"

Brannoc considered this with the seriousness he reserved for liturgy and breakfast.

"When my vows washed out," he said, "I panicked. Thought I'd been hollowed. But it turns out the vows weren't the shape of me—they were just stuff packed *around* me. Take it out and there's still a me left. Smaller. Better quality." He nudged her shoulder. "You'll find out what's under yours. Might take a while. Might be annoying. You'll survive it."

From behind them, Ines called: "Wren! Come eat something. That's an order from your captain."

"You're not my captain," Wren yelled back, already walking.

* * *

Corvane, they learned three days later, had not drowned, though Wren maintained this was the sea's one tactical error.

Sefa brought the news herself, rowing out to the fishing camp in a borrowed skiff, and delivered it standing in the shallows with the awkward posture of a woman who had resigned her command and hadn't yet figured out what her spine was for instead.

"He made it back to the mainland in one of the vault skiffs. Reached the guildhouse ahead of the collapse." Sefa's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Except there's nothing to collapse *into*. The wards are reforged on shared covenant—the protection trade is worthless. Every client contract voided overnight. And the masters' own creditors..." She paused, savoring it. "You know the guild borrowed against its holdings? Of course you do, you audited it. Well. The holdings are gone. Dissolved back into the world, per the terms of a certain formal dissolution filed in the Vhal vault. Which means the debt remains, and the collateral does not."

Wren, sitting on a drying rack with a heel of bread, felt her grin split her face. "He's broke."

"He's worse than broke." Sefa finally let the smile happen, and it transformed her whole face into someone Wren might have liked years ago, under different circumstances. "The Salt Guild's creditors include the Tide-Court of the outer banks, three merchant princes, and—at least according to the oldest entries in the archive—one entity whose name is written as a sound rather than a word. Corvane sealed himself in the guildhouse with the remaining staff and a ledger he keeps reading like it will rewrite itself." She shrugged. "The doors won't hold forever. Debts like his don't drown. They *wait*."

"And the other masters?" Ines asked quietly.

"Gone to ground. Scattered. Some will try to rebuild. Some will try to hide." Sefa met Ines's eyes. "None of them will try to touch you. Word's out about what you did in the vault. About four hands holding the covenant." She glanced at Wren, at Brannoc, back. "Word travels fast when the sky changes. Everyone saw it. Everyone knows someone was up there paying the bill."

After Sefa's skiff dwindled southward, Ines sat down next to Wren on the drying rack and didn't say anything for a while. The evening stars were coming out—wrong-old constellations, bright as new coin—and below them the sea breathed in and out, in and out, even and calm as a sleeper.

"He'll come back," Ines said at last. "Men like Corvane always come back. Maybe poorer. Maybe stranger. But he'll find a ledger somewhere and start writing in it."

"Then we'll be faster," Wren said.

Ines looked at her. "We?"

Wren rolled her eyes, because obviously. "Yes, *we*. Don't make it weird." She hugged her knees and watched the water. "Besides. Somebody has to keep listening. The sea's quiet, but quiet isn't the same as empty. And somebody has to teach you how to be a person without an oath, because honestly, Captain, you're hopeless at it. You tried to pay a fisherman for a fish today. With *coin*. Like a weirdo."

Ines laughed—that rusty, surprised laugh Wren had heard maybe four times total and was greedily collecting. "And what would you have done?"

"Traded him a story about the drowned capital. Everybody wants those now. I'd have gotten three fish and a boat out of it." Wren bumped her shoulder against Ines's arm. "See? Family isn't a contract. Nobody owes anybody. You just show up. It's the only deal I've ever liked."

Ines was quiet again, but a different quiet—the thinking kind, the kind Wren had learned meant something was being built behind her eyes. When she spoke, it was half to Wren and half to the darkening water.

"My grandmother kept a ledger her whole life. Every entry a debt. Every page a chain." She turned her hands over in her lap, palms up, empty. "I keep thinking about what comes after the last entry. What you write on the page when there's nothing owed on either side." Her fingers curled slightly, like they were already holding a pen. "I think I'd like to find out."

Far out past the bar, where the drowned road sank away toward Vhal, the water glimmered with the last of the returning light—starlight coming home through the deep, unhurried, unowned. Wren listened to it pass. It didn't whisper. It hummed.

And underneath the hum, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the drowned city where Brannoc had gone to rebuild the rites: the bells of Vhal, ringing clear, calling something home.

Wren grinned at the dark.

"Come on, Captain," she said, hopping down from the rack. "We've got a boat to borrow and a priest to embarrass. First lesson in being a person: you don't wait for the world to hand you the next page. You steal it."

Ines stood, and followed, and for the first time in nine generations of Reyes women, nobody's footsteps were weighted with anything at all.
