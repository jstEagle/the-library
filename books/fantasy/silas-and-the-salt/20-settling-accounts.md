## 20. Settling Accounts

The tide-priests of Vhal had a saying, carved into the sea-gates before the water took them: *What the sea forgives, it keeps.* Brannoc had spent forty years believing he understood it. Standing now on the rebuilt quay of Saltmark Village, salt crusted in his beard and the first covenant-stone warm against his chest, he finally did.

Forgiveness was not forgetting. The sea kept everything. It simply stopped charging interest.

He watched the villagers carry their salvaged belongings back into houses that had spent three weeks underwater. The sea had receded from the coast in a single night—pulled back like a sheet drawn off a sleeping body—and left behind mud, ruin, and an eerie quiet where the dead had been shouting for months. Wren said the quiet was the loudest thing she'd ever heard.

Brannoc believed her. He'd heard the dead of Vhal go silent too, all at once, like a congregation finishing a hymn.

"You're brooding," Sefa said, coming to stand beside him at the rail of the ferry that would take them upriver. She had a ledger under one arm—the first of many, hauled out of the guildhouse archives in crates marked with Corvane's own seal, which she'd broken with her boot heel and no ceremony at all. "I can tell because you're doing the thing with your hands."

"I am not doing a thing with my hands."

"You're wringing them like a laundress. Tide-priest habit. You people never could stand still." She set the ledger down on the rail between them. "First one's yours, if you want it. Harbor debts. Saltmark and the villages south. Names, amounts, terms."

Brannoc looked at the book without touching it. "You're giving me the power to forgive debts."

"I'm giving you the power to *read* them. Forgiving comes after." Sefa's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "That's how Ines says it has to work. You can't forgive what you won't look at. She's very insistent. She's written it on the wall of the guildhouse in charcoal, actually. Twice."

*You cannot forgive what you will not look at.* Yes. That sounded like Ines Reyes—the woman who had dissolved a bloodline oath older than kingdoms because she refused to let one more person be priced.

"She should be here for this," Brannoc said.

"Ines is where she needs to be." Sefa picked the ledger back up. "So are you. So am I. That's what sharing an anchor means, priest. Nobody gets to be everywhere. We just have to be somewhere, honestly, together." She paused. "Mostly together. Some of us are staying in a drowned city, which I maintain is insane."

"Vhal is not insane. Vhal is home."

"Vhal is three hundred feet down and lit by starlight, and you want to rebuild rites there." She shook her head, but gently. "At least tell me you'll surface sometimes. For council. For quarrels. For Wren's birthday, which she has decided is the day the stars came back, and which she will fight you about if you miss it."

"I will surface," Brannoc said. "For quarrels especially."

* * *

They spent four days in Saltmark unwinding the harbor ledgers, and Brannoc learned that forgiveness was harder work than drowning.

It wasn't the arithmetic. The arithmetic was simple enough once Ines's method took hold: strike the principal, strike the interest, record what remained—which was, in nearly every case, nothing but a name and a debt acknowledged. What was hard was the looking. Every entry was a wound someone had agreed to, signed in desperation, because the guild's miracle-trade had made hunger feel like a choice.

A fisherman named Aldous Vey—Ilsa's cousin, come down from the mire country to help—found his own father's contract in the second crate: ten years of life borrowed against a boat that sank anyway. The old man had died owing years he'd already lived. Aldous sat with the page open on his knees for a long time before he spoke.

"He thought the guild owned him."

"The guild thought so too," Brannoc said. "Both were wrong."

"And now?"

"Now the debt is void. Not paid. Void. There's a difference, and it matters." Brannoc touched the entry, feeling the faint warmth of the stone through his shirt. "Paid means the account balanced. Void means it never should have existed. Your father owed the sea nothing. He owed his children a boat, maybe, and even that the sea has forgiven."

Aldous laughed—a wet, cracked sound—and wiped his face with both hands. "You talk like a priest again. You know that? The last time I saw you, you talked like a clerk."

"The last time you saw me, I was one."

By the third day, word had spread, and villagers began coming to the quay not to beg but to *witness*. They stood in lines while Brannoc read each debt aloud—name, amount, term—and then struck it through with the tide-mark, the old sign of the sea's release, which his hands remembered even after everything. An old woman brought him bread. A child asked him to mark her toy boat's hull with the same sign, "so the sea knows it's mine and doesn't charge me."

He marked it. He was aware, distantly, that he was weeping while he did it, and that this was apparently permitted now.

On the fourth evening, Wren found him at the tide-line, watching the water sit quietly where it belonged.

"They're ready," she said. "Sefa's got the ferry loaded. And Ines sent something." She held out a small package wrapped in oilcloth. "From the capital. Well—from the vault, before they sealed it proper. She said you'd know what it is."

Inside was a shard of pale glass that glowed faintly, like a lamp behind fog. Swallowed starlight—one small piece of the hoard, released before the rest went back to the sky. A tide-lamp. The kind Vhal's priests had carried before the guild taught everyone that light was a commodity.

"She said," Wren recited, clearly quoting, "'Tell Brannoc the first rite is his to write. Tell him also that if he drowns being noble about it, I will be extremely annoyed, and I am very annoying when annoyed.'"

"That sounds exactly like her."

"She practiced it on the crossing. Eleven times." Wren sat down beside him in the sand, pulling her knees up. For a while neither spoke. Then, quieter: "You're really going back down there. To live."

"For a while. Until the rites hold on their own."

"It's dark down there."

"It's full of stars, little smuggler. That's different."

Wren considered this with the ferocious seriousness she applied to everything. "Okay. But you have to teach me the listening-rite. The real one, not the one you do for tourists. Because the sea's been quiet since the stars came back, and I don't—" Her voice caught, just once, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything. "I don't like the quiet. I need to know if they're resting or if they're gone."

Brannoc put his arm around her shoulders, and she allowed it, which was its own kind of trust. "They are resting," he said. "And I will teach you. Both rites. All of them. You hear better than any priest I ever trained, Wren. That isn't a compliment. It's a diagnosis."

She snorted. "Sefa says the same thing about my lying."

"Sefa says that about everyone."

* * *

The farewell happened at dawn on the quay, because Brannoc insisted farewells should happen in the light, facing the water, where everyone could see everyone's face.

Sefa went first, as she did everything—briskly, gripping his forearm hard enough to leave marks. "Council meets at the equinox. Guildhouse. If you don't surface, I'm sending divers after you, and they'll be guild-trained, so they'll bill you."

"Noted."

"And Brannoc." Her voice dropped, roughened. "What you did in the vault. Holding the stone when it tried to take your name. I saw it. I'll always—" She stopped, jaw working. "Just don't drown being noble. Ines's rule. It's a good rule."

Then Wren, who hugged him around the middle with the desperate strength of someone who had learned early that goodbyes were usually permanent. "Equinox," she said into his coat. "And I'm bringing the toy-boat girl. She wants to see the drowned city. I told her it's dangerous and she said danger is just weather you haven't named yet, which is honestly better than anything you've ever said."

"Where does she get these things?"

"Me. I lie beautifully."

Last, Ines—who had come up from the capital two days prior, still thin, still moving carefully, still learning the shape of a life without an oath inside it. She stood before him a moment, and then pressed the tide-lamp back into his hand, closing his fingers over it.

"No," Brannoc started. "This is—"

"Yours. It was always going to be yours. It's a gift, not a payment. There's a difference." She smiled, tired and certain. "I've written it on the wall."

He looked at the lamp glowing between their hands, and then at the three of them—swordspeaker, enforcer, smuggler; oathless, deserter, orphan; his family, assembled by catastrophe and held together by choice—and found that every rite he knew had failed him for this moment. So he wrote a new one, standing there, speaking it plain:

"What the sea forgives, it keeps. And what we forgive, we keep too—not as debt, but as ballast. Go carefully. Come back often. The water remembers all of you kindly."

Wren sniffed loudly and blamed the wind. Sefa turned away to shout at the ferryman about nothing. Ines squeezed his fingers once and let go.

Brannoc walked down into the water at slack tide, the way his ancestors had, the lamp held high, and the sea closed over him warm and quiet and, for the first time in his long disgrace, clean.

Below him, Vhal waited—its towers hung with drifting light, its streets full of resting dead, its temples cracked but standing. Enough work for ten lifetimes.

He intended to start with the temples.

But as he swam through the tide-gates, past the old market where the guild's first stall had once sold bottled storms to frightened fishermen, Brannoc saw something that stopped him cold: fresh chalk marks on the gate stones. New. Recent. The old sign of the sea's release—but drawn wrong, mirrored, backwards.

Someone else was settling accounts in Vhal. Someone who had learned the rites from watching him.

And beneath the mirrored marks, in careful letters, a single line of text he hadn't seen since the night the stars went dark:

*The fourth stone was never lost. It was spent.*
