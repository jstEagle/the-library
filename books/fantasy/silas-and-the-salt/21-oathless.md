## 21. Oathless

The first morning without the oath, Ines woke reaching for it.

Her hand went to her sternum the way it had every dawn since she was nine years old, when Grandmother Reyes had pressed her palm flat against Ines's chest and spoken the words that bound blood to covenant: *You are the ledger and the blade; you are the debt and the payment.* For twenty years that oath had been there when she woke—a low hum beneath the ribs, a compass needle that always pointed toward obligation. She had hated it on most days. She had leaned on it on all of them.

Now there was nothing. Just the ordinary architecture of her own body, breath and heartbeat and the faint ache of healing bruises from Vhal. She lay in the narrow bed in Sefa's borrowed rooms above the archive and felt for the absence the way you probe a socket where a tooth used to be.

*Who am I,* she thought, *if no one is owed anything by me?*

She didn't have an answer. She got up anyway, because her body still remembered how to be a person before her mind did, and went downstairs to make tea in a kitchen that smelled of salt and old paper.

* * *

The harbor town of Greywater had spent three weeks learning to live with returned stars. Fishermen had stopped flinching at night. The tide had drawn back to lines it hadn't touched in a generation, uncovering oyster beds and a sunken chapel and, in one memorable case, an entire smuggler's cache of bottled weather that Wren had appropriated with the shamelessness of a professional reclaiming stock. The sea was quiet now—not dead-quiet, not the held-breath silence of the dark months, but the ordinary mutter of water against pilings. The dead of Vhal had finally been allowed to sleep, and their sleeping had settled over the whole coast like a blanket pulled up to the chin.

Ines should have felt triumphant. Some mornings she almost did.

But she kept catching herself standing in doorways, waiting for instructions that would never come. Reaching for a purpose that had been carved out of her like a stone from a setting, leaving the metal thin and ringing.

"You're doing it again," Wren said.

Ines blinked. Wren was sitting cross-legged on the kitchen table, which Sefa would have objected to if Sefa were home, eating bread that belonged to someone else's breakfast with the serene entitlement of a girl who had grown up owning nothing and therefore considered everything provisionally hers. Her feet were bare. There was brine in her hair. She looked, as always, like something the sea had coughed up and decided to keep.

"Doing what again?"

"Standing there. Waiting." Wren tore off another piece of bread. "You do it when you think nobody's watching. You get this look like a clerk whose counter closed. Like you're waiting for someone to hand you your next entry."

"I don't—"

"You do. I've watched you for months. You're very watchable." She said it without flirtation or mockery, just fact, the way she said everything. "You had an oath since you were little. That's worse than having a master. A master might die. An oath just sits inside you and tells you what you're for."

Ines set down the teapot more carefully than the gesture required. "And what am I for, then? Since you've been watching."

Wren shrugged. "Dunno yet. That's the good part."

"That is *not* the good part," Ines said, and heard her own voice crack on it, and hated that Wren heard it too.

The girl didn't gloat. She just slid off the table, took Ines's cold untouched tea out of her hands, drank half of it herself, and pushed the rest back. "Drink that. Sit down. We're going to do an inventory."

"An inventory of what?"

"Of you." Wren said it the way other people said *obviously*. "You've been spending yourself for a year. Memories, hours, blood, sleep. Nobody ever counted what was left. So we count."

* * *

They sat at the kitchen table while grey light came up over the harbor, and Wren demanded, item by item, an accounting of Ines Reyes.

"What's the first thing you remember?"

Ines reached—and found the hole where it should have been. The flooded chapel. Speaking the dormant oath aloud, feeling it answer after a year of silence, raising the first covenant-stone from the silt. And then three days gone, wiped clean as a slate, paid into the stone like coin into a merchant's palm. Whatever had lived in those days—some ordinary childhood morning, some small unguarded happiness—was seawater now.

"Gone," she said. "That one's gone. Paid at Saltmark."

"And the breathing one?"

"The crossing to Vhal." She touched her throat. "I know I nearly drowned. I know I chose to spend a memory so I could breathe the water. But I can't remember what memory it took. It's like—" She searched for it. "It's like finding a room in your house empty and not being able to say what furniture was in it. You just know the shape of the missing thing."

Wren wrote nothing down—she couldn't write well and made no pretense otherwise—but she listened with the ferocious attention of someone who had learned early that listening was survival. "What do you still have?"

"My mother's face. Mostly. A little blurred at the edges." Ines turned her cup slowly. "My training. Swordwork, ledgers, guild law—all of it, they never let me trade any of that, it was too useful. Grandmother's voice. The smell of the vault. Corvane's face when the starlight slipped his fingers." She smiled thinly. "That one I'm keeping."

"Your name?"

"Mine's mine. They couldn't take that—it wasn't mine to spend. It belongs to the bloodline." She paused. "Belonged."

"So you're rich," Wren said. "Compared to some."

Ines laughed despite herself. "Compared to some."

"But you keep looking at what's missing instead of what's left. That's a debtor's habit. My mother did it. Counted what the sea took every morning before she'd look at what the boats brought in." Wren hopped off her chair. "New rule. You're not allowed to open the day with losses. Losses come last. Gifts first."

"Wren—"

"Gifts first," Wren repeated, with the immovable authority of a twelve-year-old who had once talked a guild patrol boat into believing she was a customs inspector, and Ines found herself nodding.

* * *

Sefa came by at midday with a crate of recovered archives and the particular grim satisfaction of a woman who had spent thirty years obeying orders and was enjoying disobedience enormously now that it was sanctioned.

"The masters' creditors are circling Corvane," she reported, stacking ledgers on the table. "Half the coast wants its money back and he hasn't got a hoard left to pay with. Brannoc sends word from Vhal—the rites are rebuilding. Slowly. He says the dead sing differently now. He says to tell you—" Sefa hesitated, and Ines saw the old mentor struggle with tenderness the way she once struggled with insubordination. "He says to tell you the city remembers your name kindly. Which, from Vhal, is high praise."

"And the stones?"

"Safely divided. One with me, one with Brannoc, one—" Sefa nodded at Wren, who was hanging upside down off a chair in a manner suggesting she was listening to every word through her heels, "—with our young colleague, who refuses to tell anyone where she's hidden it, which frankly is the only security arrangement I fully trust."

"It's under a rock," Wren said, upside down. "A very specific rock."

Ines smiled. Then, because the smile cost her something, she looked away, and Sefa—perceptive as ever, kind in the ways she could afford to be—pretended not to notice.

"You'll want to see this, though." Sefa drew a slim volume from the crate and set it in front of Ines. "Found it in the sealed stacks. Your grandmother's personal ledger. The real one, not the guild copy."

Ines knew the book before she touched it. She had seen it once as a child, in a room she'd been forbidden to enter, and had carried the image of it for twenty years: grey leather, salt-stained, the Reyes mark pressed into the cover. She opened it with hands that weren't quite steady.

It was exactly what she expected. Page after page of entries in her grandmother's precise hand. *Borrowed against: forty years, fisherman of Saltmark. Interest: compounding. Collateral: his daughter's name.* On and on, the predatory arithmetic of the guild, the machine that had mortgaged the stars themselves. Ines read until her stomach turned, and then—

Then she turned a page near the end and stopped.

The handwriting changed. Looser. Older. Shaky in the way of a hand that had begun to admit its own mortality. And the entries changed too.

*Given freely: a coat, to the widow Marsh, who had none.*

*Given freely: my afternoon, to a child who wanted to know about stars.*

*Given freely: forgiveness, to myself, though I have not earned it.*

There were dozens of them. Hundreds. Years of them, interleaved among the debts like green shoots through rubble. Her grandmother—the architect of the mortgage that had swallowed the sky, the woman whose covenant had chained Ines's blood to a dying ward—had kept a second ledger all along. A ledger of gifts. Things given with no expectation of return, entered not because they were owed but because they mattered.

At the bottom of the final page, in that same shaking hand:

*One day the debt comes due and I will not be here to pay it. To whoever holds this book after me: the first ledger tells you what the world will take if you let it. This one tells you what you can give instead. Choose which book you write in.*

Ines sat for a long time with her thumb resting on the page.

"Ines?" Sefa said quietly.

"She knew." Ines's voice came out rough. "She knew what she'd done. She knew what it would cost. And she—" She laughed, wetly. "She left me instructions. Not for paying the debt. For living after it."

* * *

That evening she walked down to the harbor alone, which was itself a strange new freedom—going somewhere with no errand, no oath pulling her, no one to report to. The lamps along the quay were lit, and above them the constellations hung in their ancient arrangements, fat and bright and slightly smug, as if they hadn't spent a year in a vault.

Wren found her, of course. Wren always found her; Ines suspected the girl navigated by people the way she navigated by tides.

"You're doing it again," Wren said, settling onto the bollard beside her.

"Doing what?"

"Looking up like you owe them something."

Ines considered denying it. She was getting better at honesty, though. It was one of the few things she seemed to be gaining while everything else drained away. "Maybe I do. They were collateral. My family put them up as surety. If I hadn't dissolved the covenant—"

"If you hadn't dissolved the covenant, we'd all be drowned and the stars would still be in a box." Wren swung her legs. "You keep trying to be grateful to the sky. The sky doesn't need it. Trust me, I've asked."

"You've *asked* the sky—"

"It doesn't listen great. Lots of static." She bumped her shoulder against Ines's arm. "Here's what I actually came to say. Sefa's staying at the archive. Brannoc's staying in Vhal. Everyone's got a place now except you, and you keep acting like that means you're the leftover. Like everyone else got assigned and you got returned."

Ines said nothing, which was agreement.

"Well, I've decided," Wren went on, "that you're assigned to me."

Ines turned to look at her. In the lamplight the girl's face was solemn in the way it only got when she meant something completely. "Assigned."

"Assigned. Like cargo. High-value cargo, fragile, handle with care." Wren crossed her arms. "You keep thinking family is a contract. Something you sign, something that can default. I've seen contracts. Contracts are what people use when they don't trust each other yet. Family's what you've got *before* anybody writes anything down. Before the ink. You fed me when I was hungry and you didn't charge me. That's the whole document. That's the entire ledger."

"Wren—"

"I'm not done." She held up a finger. "You're going to say you have nothing to offer. Wrong. You have a boat-share, you make acceptable tea, and you know more about impossible things than anyone alive, which is going to be extremely useful, because somebody has to teach me what's in all these bottles I stole. Also you snore."

"I do not snore."

"The walls in Sefa's house are thin and I have excellent hearing. Anyway." She stuck out her hand, suddenly businesslike, a smuggler closing terms. "Do we have an accord?"

Ines looked at the small salt-crusted hand, and at the girl attached to it—who had heard the dead sea speak and carried its worst news without putting it down, who had passed the second stone's test untouched, who had decided, somewhere between a river ambush and a drowned road, that these two broken adults were hers to keep—and felt something shift in the hollow where the oath used to live. Not filling it. Nothing would fill it. But settling into it, the way water settles into a shape, becoming a new kind of container.

"No contract," Ines said, taking the hand. "No terms. No interest."

"No interest," Wren agreed solemnly. "Terrible business model. We'll be ruined within the year."

* * *

Late that night, in the lamplit kitchen, Ines opened a fresh ledger.

She had bought it that afternoon from the stationer on Rope Lane—an ordinary book, cream pages, unruled. She sat with her pen poised over the first line for a long time, because she understood that whatever she wrote there would be a kind of founding document, and she had seen what founding documents could do.

Her grandmother's first ledger had begun with a debt. Ines's would begin differently.

She wrote:

*Ledger of gifts given. No debts recorded herein. No collateral accepted. No interest shall accrue, compound, or be collected. Entries are final, forgiven, and free.*

Then, beneath it, the first entry:

*Given freely: a home, to Wren of the Sea Roads, who asked for nothing and received everything I have.*

She thought about it, and added:

*Given freely: forgiveness, to Reyes Reyes, who broke the world and tried to mend it, and to myself, who finished the mending and am learning to live in the mended place.*

*Given freely: this page, blank, to whoever needs it next.*

She blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the harbor breathe, feeling the strange lightness of a body that belonged to no covenant, no guild, no ledger but the one she'd just begun.

Upstairs, Wren's voice drifted down through the floorboards, drowsy and matter-of-fact: "Ines. There's someone at the door."

"There's no one at the door. It's midnight."

"There's someone at the door," Wren repeated, and now she was awake, and her voice had changed, gone flat and careful, the smuggler's voice, the voice that meant the sea had told her something. "They've been standing there since the tide turned. Not knocking. Just waiting. And Ines—they don't have any footsteps. Whoever it is, they're not touching the ground."

Ines picked up the new ledger, and then, out of twenty years of habit, picked up her sword as well, and went to see who had come calling on a woman with no debts left to collect.
