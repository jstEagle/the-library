## 1. Ink Under the Fingernails

The ink was always under his fingernails by midday.

Gideon Pemberton had learned to stop noticing it years ago—the way it settled into the quick of him, black crescents beneath every nail, a stain no amount of scrubbing with lye or river sand could fully lift. The other scribes wore it too, but they wore it differently. Brother Aldous, who had taken his vows freely at nineteen and would die in this scriptorium content as a fat cat in a sunbeam, wore ink like a badge. Gideon wore it like a brand.

He dipped his pen and copied another line of the grain ledgers.

*Received into the Temple stores, third month of the Emperor's Grace: two hundred forty measures of barley, quality fair, from the estates of Lord Havelock. Tithed under bond of the Harvest Accord.*

His hand moved without him. That was the trick of it—his hand had been trained so thoroughly that it could work while the rest of him went elsewhere. Elsewhere was where Gideon lived most days. His body sat on a hard bench in the copying hall of the Temple of Vessia, shoulders bent over a desk scarred by fifty years of other men's pens, while the rest of him walked roads that didn't exist anymore, or had never existed at all.

*Two hundred forty measures.* He wrote the numeral, then paused, pen hovering. Lord Havelock's estates were east of the city, past the mill towns. Two hundred forty measures meant six carts, and six carts meant six drivers, and six drivers meant tolls at the Millgate unless someone knew the old drover's path along the canal—which had no gate at all, only a broken fence everyone pretended was still intact because pretending kept the peace.

He wrote the number. He filed the thought away.

That was the other habit, the one he'd never confessed to anyone, not in confession, not to himself in the dark hours before dawn. Gideon collected routes the way other men collected debts—and Vessia knew he had plenty of those too. Every ledger that crossed his desk was a map drawn in numbers. Grain shipments traced the good roads. Wine tithes marked the passes that stayed open through winter. The Temple's own correspondence, which he sealed and carried to the dispatch room every evening, mapped the empire's arteries for anyone patient enough to read them.

Gideon was nothing if not patient.

"Brother Pemberton."

He didn't flinch. Flinching was a luxury for people whose mistakes cost them less than everything. He simply lifted his head from the ledger with the expression of a man who had been about to look up anyway.

Sister Maren stood at the end of his row, her grey habit spotless, her face carrying the particular disappointment of a woman who had hoped to catch him idle and found him working instead. She was the Undermistress of the Scriptorium, which meant she owned his daylight hours the way the Temple's debt-office owned the rest of him.

"You're behind," she said.

"I'm on the Havelock entry."

"The Havelock entry should have been finished an hour ago." She came down the row, her sandals whispering on stone worn smooth by centuries of feet exactly like his. "You've been slow all week."

It was true, though not for any reason she'd have believed. All week he'd been counting. Not the entries, not the measures, not the candles burned per page for the accounting reports. He'd been counting something else entirely.

Four thousand two hundred and eleven days.

That was what remained on his bond. Eleven years, give or take the leap-feasts the Temple generously did *not* count against your term. Gideon had entered the Temple of Vessia at fourteen, when his mother died and her debts came due, and he had signed the indenture because the alternative was the debtor pits, and the debtor pits were where men went to be slowly converted into statistics. Fourteen years served. Eleven remained. He would walk out of these gates—if he lived that long, if the Temple didn't extend his term for some infraction real or invented—at thirty-nine. An old man by the standards of the streets he'd grown up on.

"Forgive me, Sister," he said. "My eyes have been tired in the evenings."

"Eat more liver." She glanced at his page, found it flawless, and hated it a little; he could see the hatred, small and bright as a pinprick. Maren disliked scribes who were better than their station. A bonded man should be grateful, plodding, unremarkable. Excellence suggested ambition, and ambition in a bonded man was like a crack in a dam—harmless until suddenly it wasn't. "Finish the Havelock entry, then the Cordwell tithe. Both before evening bell."

"Yes, Sister."

She moved on. Gideon watched her go, then looked down at his hands, resting either side of the ledger like two pale animals at rest. Ink to the second knuckle. A callus on the middle finger of his right hand, thick as horn, from twenty-five years of holding a pen.

Somewhere south of here, past the harbor and the customs houses, there were ships that paid crews in coin instead of contracts. Somewhere north, past the mountain wall, the clan territories began, where the Emperor's writ ran thin and a man's word was worth more than his seal. Gideon had never seen either place. He had memorized them anyway, road by road, pass by pass, the way a caged bird memorizes wind.

He dipped his pen and went back to work.

* * *

Evening bell released the scribes at sundown, and sundown came early now, the autumn pressing its thumb down on the days. Gideon walked the cloister with the others, thirty grey-robed men moving in the loose procession of the exhausted, and broke from them at the refectory doors as he always did, claiming stomach trouble, claiming a vow, claiming whatever excuse required the least follow-up. Tonight it was a vow to Vessia of the Ledger, patroness of accounts owed and rendered. Nobody questioned a man's private devotions. Private devotions were one of the few properties a bonded man still owned outright.

His cell was in the oldest wing, the one they called the Wet Side because damp climbed the walls in winter and the mortar wept salt like something grieving. It was eight feet by ten, furnished with a cot, a stool, a shelf for his permitted books—two hymnals and a treatise on agricultural law, riveting stuff—and a window barred with iron rods spaced just wide enough, he had measured, for a man of his build to pass through if the man were desperate enough and the drop below forgiving enough. The drop was three stories onto the herb garden. Not forgiving. But survivable, possibly, if a man landed right.

He wasn't going anywhere. That was the thing people got wrong about him, the few people who thought about him at all. They assumed a man who memorized escape routes was a man planning to use them. But Gideon had understood something long ago, back when he was fourteen and signing his life away with a hand that shook: the routes weren't for escaping. They were for remembering that the world had edges. That the cage had walls, which meant it had an outside, which meant the outside existed whether he ever touched it or not.

A man could live eleven more years on that.

He lit his candle—it was permitted until ninth bell—and pulled the loose stone from the wall behind the shelf. Behind it lay his treasury: a stub of pencil no longer than his thumb, saved from a clerk's sweep; a sheaf of paper scraps, each smaller than his palm, hoarded from trimming waste; and the maps.

Not maps, really. The Temple would have called them diagrams, if the Temple had ever found them, and finding them would have earned him an extension of term and a session with the discipline master. What they actually were was memory made visible. On each scrap, in pencil fine as spider silk, Gideon drew what he knew: the canal path with its broken fence and the drover's ford where the water ran knee-deep in late summer. The smugglers' stair above the Salt Gate, cut into the cliff by hands older than the empire, still passable if you trusted the third step—and he'd interviewed enough sailors in the refectory lines to know the third step held. The high trail through Thornfell that the clan folk used, the one that avoided the imperial checkpoint at Grey Stone Bridge entirely, winding up through country so steep the Emperor's soldiers couldn't ride it and wouldn't walk it.

Thornfell. He drew it again tonight, though he'd drawn it a dozen times, adding a detail he'd pried out of a carter last month: a shrine waystation at the tree line, abandoned, its roof fallen in, but the walls sound. A place to shelter. A place to breathe.

Why? He'd asked himself the question a thousand times and never gotten an answer that satisfied. Curiosity, maybe. Or the same instinct that made prisoners count their steps. Or something older and stranger, some inheritance from a mother who had vanished for weeks at a time throughout his childhood and come back smelling of woodsmoke and pine, singing lullabies in a language she claimed was just "old farm talk." She'd taught him to read terrain before she'd taught him letters. *Watch how the water moves,* she'd say, kneeling beside a stream in the parklands, her dark hair coming loose from its braid. *Water remembers every low place. So do roads. So do I.*

Then she'd died on a pilgrimage he hadn't known she was taking, to a destination nobody would name, and the debts had come due, and the water had carried him here.

Gideon blew on the fresh pencil lines, folded the scrap of Thornfell small as a seed, and slid it atop the others. Then he returned the stone to the wall, pressed his palm flat against the cool plaster, and let the familiar arithmetic settle over him like a blanket.

Four thousand two hundred and eleven days. Minus today. Four thousand two hundred and ten.

He could do four thousand two hundred and ten. He'd done five thousand already. You did them one at a time, the way you copied ledgers—one entry, one line, one number at a time, until the sum arrived without you watching it come.

He pinched out the candle and lay down on the cot to wait for sleep, which came to him the way it came to soldiers and sailors: fast, shallow, and ready to run.

* * *

Three bells past midnight, the storm arrived.

Gideon woke to thunder rolling off the mountains like siege stones, and rain hitting the Wet Side windows sideways, and a wind that moaned in the chimney flues of the old wing with a sound like a choir that had lost its faith. He lay in the dark listening, the way he always woke—completely, instantly, cataloguing. Thunder. Rain. Wind from the north, hard, the kind of north wind that came down off Thornfell and reminded the city that the mountains were older than the empire and would outlast it.

And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, footsteps in the cloister.

Not the night brothers. Gideon knew their tread the way he knew the bell schedule; old Brother Fenwick dragged his left foot, and young Brother Callum walked on his toes like a boy afraid of being caught at something. These steps were neither. These steps were uneven, hurried, and wet—a slapping sound, boots full of water, moving fast past the refectory, past the chapel, toward the oldest wing. Toward the Wet Side. Toward him.

Gideon sat up in the dark, heart going quiet and cold the way it did before trouble, which was the opposite of how other people's hearts behaved and had always struck him as the truest thing he knew about himself.

The footsteps stopped outside his door.

For a moment there was nothing but the storm. Then came a sound he knew intimately, professionally, a sound he heard two hundred times a day in the ledgers: the sound of a body giving out. A wet gasp, a collapse, the dull knock of a shoulder or a skull against stone.

He should have stayed in bed. He understood that even then, even as he rose, even as his hand found the latch. The rules of survival he'd written for himself across twenty-five years of bondage were simple and sacred: see nothing, know nothing, owe nothing, want nothing. A man dying in a corridor at fourth bell during a storm was Temple business, guard business, anybody's business but the business of a bonded scribe with four thousand two hundred and ten days left.

He opened the door anyway.

Lightning lit the corridor white, and in that white flash he saw her—a woman crumpled against the wall opposite his cell, soaked through, one arm wrapped around something clutched to her chest the way a drowning sailor hugs driftwood. Her hood had fallen back. Young, maybe younger than him. Dark hair plastered to a face gone grey as wet ash. Blood, he saw in the next flash, a lot of blood, pooling under her and running the channels between the flagstones in thin black rivers.

Her eyes found his. Even in the dark, even half-conscious, they found his with terrible precision, as if she'd been looking for this exact door in this exact corridor her whole way here.

"Pemberton," she said. His name. Not *brother*, not *sir*. Just the name, spoken like a password, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known he carried.

"I'll fetch the infirmary—" he started.

"No." Her hand shot out and caught his wrist, and her grip was astonishing, iron in a dying woman, and she pressed something against his palm—wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, small and heavy and warm from her body. "North road. They're coming. Don't—don't let them—" A cough took her, deep and ruinous, and when it passed she was smiling, which was somehow worse than the blood. "Found you. Mother said. She said you'd—"

The lightning came again and showed him her face fully for the first time, and something turned over in Gideon's chest, some recognition too large and too fast to name, a face like his mother's face, a face like—

Like his own, thirty years younger, if his own had been a woman's and had been loved better.

"Wren," she whispered. "Tell them my name was Wren."

Then the grip on his wrist loosened, and the storm filled the silence where her breathing had been, and somewhere across the sleeping temple the first bell of morning began to ring.

Gideon Pemberton stood in his doorway holding an oilcloth bundle and the wrist of a dead woman, and heard, beneath the thunder, the far-off sound of horses on the north road.
