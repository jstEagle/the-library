## 3. What the Oilcloth Holds

The body was still in my bedroom.

I want to be clear about that, because everything I did afterward has to be understood against the fact of it: a dead man lying on my floorboards with rainwater pooling under him and the Temple bells ringing third bell not forty paces away, and me standing there holding a bundle of oilcloth like it might bite.

I should have gone for the Watch. Or the Temple wardens. Or anyone whose job it was to deal with corpses in storm-lit doorways. That was the sensible sequence of events. That was what a man who valued his neck did.

Instead I locked the door, dragged a rug over the blood, and sat down across from him to wait out the bells.

His name—I would learn later—was Wren. At the time he was just a stranger with grey lips and a hole in his side that had soaked through two layers of wool, and I was just a scribe with ink under his fingernails and no idea how badly the next year of his life was about to go.

The bells rang nine times. Third bell always does. I counted every one of them, because counting is what I do when I'm frightened, and by the ninth toll I had made a decision so stupid that I can only describe it as inevitable.

I waited until the last echo died. Then I unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

Let me explain something about curiosity, since it's about to ruin my life and I'd rather you understand it properly.

People think curiosity is a hunger. It isn't. Hunger you can satisfy. Curiosity is more like debt—it compounds. Every question you don't answer accrues interest, and the interest accrues interest, and one morning you wake up owing more than you can ever pay, and the only way out is to open the thing you were afraid to open.

I had spent eleven years at the Temple of Vessia learning to leave questions alone. Eleven years of copying ledgers and tithing rolls and confession abstracts, of reading other people's sins in shorthand and never once wondering past the point of usefulness. The Temple teaches you that early. Wonder is expensive. Wonder gets bonded men sold to worse masters than the ones they have.

But I had also spent eleven years drawing smuggling routes from memory on scraps of vellum I kept beneath a loose floorboard, and if you think a man who does that has any real talent for leaving questions alone, then you haven't been paying attention.

So. The oilcloth.

It was wrapped tight, soldier-tight, the kind of fold you learn doing work where wet maps kill people. The cloth itself was good—waxed linen, worth more than anything I owned—and it had been wrapped around something flat and rigid with a smaller shape nested beside it. Blood had soaked through at one corner and dried to a brown crust that flaked onto my desk when I set the bundle down.

I lit both candles. Not because I needed the light, but because I wanted witnesses, even useless ones.

Then I cut the cord.

Inside was a map, folded twice, drawn on vellum that had gone soft and yellowed with handling. And beside it, wrapped in its own scrap of leather, a locket.

I'll come back to the locket. I have to, because it's the part that matters, but my hands went to the map first, because maps are my native language and fear makes us reach for what we know.

It was a map of the Thornfell mountains. I knew that within three seconds, and knowing it made my stomach drop through the floor, because everyone in Vessia knows what Thornfell means. It means the clan territories. It means the passes the empire closed after the last uprising. It means the shrine roads that appear on no imperial chart because the empire decided, sixty years ago, that the shrines didn't exist anymore, and a thing that doesn't exist doesn't get mapped.

Except someone had mapped them. Someone had mapped them beautifully.

The cartography was old—the vellum told me that, and the iron-gall ink gone rust-brown told me that—but it had been annotated recently, in a different hand, in a fine black script that crawled along the margins like ivy. The base map showed the range from the Vessia lowlands to the high spine of the mountains, and threaded through it were routes. Not imperial roads. Not even smuggler roads, though some of them matched paths I'd drawn myself in the small hours, which unsettled me more than I can easily say. These were older. They followed watercourses and ridgelines and things that weren't quite either, and they all converged, eventually, on a single point in the high country north of the Heartstone plateau.

At that point someone had drawn a symbol.

Not a temple glyph. I want to be precise here, because I spent four years as an apprentice cataloguing the Temple's scriptorium indexes, and I know our script the way I know my own hands. This wasn't it. It wasn't clan knotwork either—I'd seen enough of that on confiscated banners to know the style, all interlocking loops and lineage-counts. This was something else. A circle bisected by a descending line, with seven short strokes radiating from the junction like rays, or roots, or the spokes of a wheel half-buried in the earth.

And around it, in that crawling marginal hand, someone had written the same phrase four times in four scripts. Two I couldn't read at all. One was Old Imperial, degraded but decipherable, and I worked it out letter by letter with my heart going like a drum:

*What sleeps must be fed.*

The fourth script was the strangest, because it almost resembled temple shorthand—not the formal liturgical hand but the working ciphers we used for confidential ledgers, except warped, archaic, wrong in ways that made the hair on my arms stand up. I couldn't read it. But I recognized its bones. Which meant whoever wrote it had learned their letters from the same root system the Temple's ciphers grew from, and that raised questions I genuinely did not want to ask, because the Temple's ciphers were supposed to be ours. Invented here. Ours alone.

I sat back. The candle flames leaned in the draft from the window and righted themselves.

Outside, the storm was moving off eastward, and the city had that washed-clean quiet it gets at night after rain, broken only by the groan of cart-axles somewhere near the tanners' quarter. A normal night. A night in which a bonded scribe named Gideon Pemberton had exactly one problem—a body on his floor—and exactly one obvious solution involving the canal and the ebb tide.

Instead I picked up the locket.

* * *

It was silver, or had been once; now it was dark with age and wear, the hinge loose, the clasp broken so it hung open like a mouth. On the front, worn nearly smooth, was an engraved crest.

A tower above water. Three stars above the tower.

I know that crest. Of course I know that crest. It's stamped into the first page of every ledger I've signed for eleven years, pressed into the wax of every bond-contract the Temple issues. It's the seal of House Pemberton—the indentured line, the debtor-clan, the family that sold its name into Temple service four generations ago and never bought it back.

My name is Gideon *Pemberton*. I have met perhaps six other people who share it, all of them distant cousins scattered through the Temple's service houses, none of them close, none of them the sort who'd carry a courier's bundle through a storm. The crest could have come off any contract, any bond-paper, any of ten thousand documents. Silver lockets don't just fall off documents.

I turned it over. Inside, behind clouded glass, was a miniature portrait.

A woman.

She was young—younger than me, maybe twenty, maybe less—with dark hair braided back from her face in a style I'd only ever seen in illustrations, because no one in Vessia wore their hair that way. The braids were intricate, woven with what looked like thread or thin cord, pulled tight against the skull, and the painter had caught them with loving precision, each strand distinct. Her eyes were dark. Her mouth was set in the particular firm line of someone sitting for a portrait they hadn't asked to sit for.

And her face—

Here is the thing I keep circling, the thing I've now spent a sleepless night failing to explain to my own satisfaction. Her face was familiar. Not in the way a face is familiar when you've seen it in a market crowd. Familiar the way your own handwriting is familiar. Familiar the way a room you grew up in is familiar when you walk into it after years away and everything is smaller than you remembered but the light falls exactly where it always fell.

She looked like my mother.

Not exactly. My mother had been harder, thinner, worn down by the years before she died—worn down by whatever those years contained, because she was never forthcoming and I was never brave enough to press. But the structure underneath was the same. The same deep-set eyes. The same jaw. The same slight asymmetry at the corner of the mouth, left side higher than right, which is the kind of detail you don't copy. You inherit it.

I held the locket up to the candlelight and my hands were not steady, and I thought: this is a coincidence. Portraits are generic. Faces repeat. There are a hundred thousand dark-eyed women in the world and most of them look like somebody's mother.

Then I noticed the braid-work again, really noticed it, and the coincidence argument collapsed.

Because my mother used to braid my hair when I was small. Before she disappeared on her "pilgrimage"—the family word, the lie we all agreed to say out loud—she would sit me between her knees on winter evenings and weave my hair into patterns while she hummed songs in a language I never learned the name of. The Temple fathers said the songs were gibberish. My mother said nothing at all, which was her way. But the patterns stayed in my hands. I could still make them, if I tried. Interlocking loops, thread-bound, pulled tight against the skull.

Clan braids. I know that now. I didn't know it then, not fully, but I knew the portrait woman wore my mother's hands' work, and no amount of coincidence explains that.

There was a second thing inside the locket. When I pried the backing loose—gently, with a knife-tip, the way you open something you already know you shouldn't—a curl of paper fell into my palm, rolled tight as a seed.

I unrolled it. Four lines, in the same fine black script that crawled across the map's margins. Not Old Imperial this time. Not any cipher I knew. Just four lines of a writing system I couldn't read, and beneath them, in cramped, hurried Common Tongue, as if the writer had added it as an afterthought or a translation for someone else:

*If you are reading this, the bearer failed. Find the keeper's son. Only his blood—*

The rest was torn away.

I read it maybe thirty times. *Find the keeper's son.* The words sat in my chest like swallowed stones. Keeper of what? Son of whom? The map pointed into Thornfell, into closed country, into territory where an imperial scribe would live approximately as long as a snowflake on a stove. And somewhere in that direction, apparently, was a man's son, and someone had died on my doorstep trying to reach—or fleeing from, the distinction mattered and I couldn't determine it—that son.

I looked at the courier's body under its rug. Grey-faced, rain-soaked, a man who had run through a storm with a hole in him because whatever he carried mattered more than stopping. He had pressed this into my hands with the last of his strength. Not the Watch's hands. Not the Temple's. Mine.

He'd known my name. I realized that only then, replaying it—he'd said it, or tried to, before the cough took him. One syllable. *Gid—* That was all. But it was enough. A dying stranger in a city of ninety thousand souls had come to my door specifically, deliberately, carrying a map to forbidden mountains and a locket wearing my mother's face and a note addressed to a keeper's son.

You cannot unknow that. I want to be honest about the moment, because everything afterward follows from it: there was a moment, standing in my cold room with the candles guttering, when I could have stopped. Bundle in the stove. Body in the canal. Back to ledgers at dawn, back to invisibility, back to the long slow countdown of my bond with nothing in it but ink and obedience. The fire was right there. The oilcloth would burn like a wick.

I stood in front of the stove for a long time.

I thought about my mother humming in a language the Temple called gibberish. I thought about the way she'd gone quiet whenever Thornfell was mentioned, which was rarely, because nobody mentioned Thornfell, which was itself strange if you thought about it too long, and I had spent eleven years carefully not thinking about it too long. I thought about the phrase on the map—*what sleeps must be fed*—and about the torn sentence—*only his blood*—and about how both of those phrases sounded like the openings of stories, and how I had spent my whole life reading other people's stories in shorthand and filing them away unfinished.

Fatal curiosity. That's what the old texts call it, the sin that walks men off cliffs. The Temple fathers warn against it in the same breath as pride. I sat through those lessons as a boy and nodded along, and I understood, even then, that I was nodding along to a warning aimed directly at the center of my chest.

I banked the stove instead of lighting it. I folded the map along its original creases, soldier-tight, the way the dead man had folded it. I put the locket and the note inside the oilcloth and wrapped the whole bundle the same way he had, copying his knots without thinking, my fingers remembering folds my mind had only glimpsed.

Then I dealt with the body, because that part couldn't wait for dawn and couldn't wait for courage. The canal took him quietly, the way the canal takes everything in Vessia, and I told myself the necessary lies about it—*he was beyond saving, the Temple would have burned his papers and asked me questions I couldn't answer, this was mercy as much as survival*—and the lies held about as well as oilcloth holds water. Which is to say, for a while. Which is to say, long enough.

By the time I got back, soaked to the knees, shaking, the sky was going grey at the edges and the bells would ring prime in two hours and I had a decision sitting in my room like a second corpse.

I could take the bundle to the Temple. Confess everything. Throw myself on the mercy of an institution that has never once, in recorded memory, shown mercy to a bonded man in possession of contraband. Or I could hide it, say nothing, and spend the rest of my life waiting for the knock at the door.

Or—and this option arrived quietly, without fanfare, the way the worst options do—I could find out what *only his blood* meant. I could follow the map. I could go to Thornfell.

Me. A scribe who hadn't left the temple district in three years. A man whose entire strategy for existence was to be forgettable.

I sat at my desk until full daylight, the bundle under my hand, and I did the thing I do when I'm frightened. I drew. A route, from memory, from the map I'd just memorized—because yes, I had already memorized it, that's the kind of man I am, the damage was done before I finished the first fold. The route ran north out of Vessia, through the tanner flats, up the old drover road, into country I knew only as lines on stolen charts.

Somewhere on that route, someone was waiting for a keeper's son.

I looked at the portrait one last time. The woman with my mother's jaw looked back at me with my mother's patience, as if she'd known all along what I'd choose, and had simply been waiting for me to catch up.

Under the loose floorboard, beneath my collection of impossible maps, I hid the oilcloth bundle.

I burned nothing.

Two days later, the Temple sent word that an Inquisitor would be arriving to conduct an audit of the district scriptoriums, and that all bonded personnel would be subject to individual interview.

His name, the notice said, was Malachi Voss.
