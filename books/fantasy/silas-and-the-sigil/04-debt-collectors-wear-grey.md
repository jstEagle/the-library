## 4. Debt Collectors Wear Grey

The grey riders came on the fourth morning after the storm, and Gideon knew it before anyone told him, because the temple's rhythm changed.

It was a small thing at first. Brother Aldous stopped in the refectory doorway mid-sentence, his cup halfway to his lips, and stood there listening to a sound Gideon couldn't hear over the clatter of bowls. Then two of the under-priests went scurrying down the east corridor with their hoods up, which they only did when someone important had entered the outer court and they wanted very much not to be seen. By the time Gideon carried his morning stack of copied ledgers toward the scriptorium, the whole building had developed a held-breath quality, like a house where someone has just died.

He set the ledgers down and looked out the scriptorium's narrow window into the courtyard.

Six riders in grey. Not the Temple's brown, not the city watch's blue-and-white. Grey — the color of the Inquisition, the color of ash and winter roads and closed doors. Their horses were lathered despite the cold, which meant they'd ridden hard from somewhere, and their saddles were packed for distance, which meant they weren't leaving soon.

Gideon's hands did something strange then. They went still. Completely still, both of them flat on the windowsill, as if some older and wiser part of him had taken control and was refusing to let the rest of him tremble.

*They found the courier,* he thought. *Or they found where the courier died.*

He thought about the oilcloth bundle sewn into the lining of his winter coat, hanging in his cell three floors below. He thought about the locket in the false bottom of his ink chest, wrapped now in a spare stocking because he hadn't known what else to do with it. He thought about the body.

That was the thing he kept circling back to, in the long hours when he should have been sleeping. The body. He had hidden a dead man in the old cistern beneath the tannery row, weighted with stones he'd stolen one at a time from the mason's pile over six nights, and every hour since, some part of him had been waiting for the knock, the shout, the hand on his shoulder. You didn't hide a body in a city like Vessia's capital and expect the ground to keep your secret. The ground here belonged to the Temple. Everything did.

"Copyist Pemberton."

He turned. It was Sister Maren, the scriptorium's mistress, an old woman with ink-stained knuckles and eyes that missed nothing, which was why she'd survived forty years in this building. Her face today was carefully blank in the way of someone delivering news they've been ordered not to editorialize upon.

"You're wanted. The Inquisitor's audit. All bonded scribes are to present themselves in the Chapter Hall, in order of seniority." She paused. "You're near the end of the list. You have perhaps an hour."

"An hour," Gideon repeated.

"To compose yourself," she said, and there — just for a moment — the blankness cracked, and he saw something underneath it that might have been pity, or might have been warning. "Inquisitors ask questions the way surgeons ask questions. The answer isn't the point. The cutting is the point."

She left him alone with the ledgers and the window and the sound of his own pulse.

* * *

The hour passed the way hours pass before executions: too fast and too slow at once. Gideon tried to work and found he'd copied the same line of grain tariffs four times. He tried to pray, in the perfunctory way the Temple permitted its bondsmen, and found the words wouldn't arrange themselves. Finally he simply sat in his cell with his coat across his knees, feeling the hard rectangular weight of the map through the lining, and made himself think.

Not panic. Think.

What did they know? A courier had come down the north road in a storm. If Wren — if the stranger — had been followed, the followers would know roughly where he'd been heading. But Gideon had found him dying in a doorway off Cooper's Lane, three streets from the Temple, with no witnesses Gideon knew of. The old widow across the passage slept like the dead and was half-deaf besides. Gideon had dragged the man inside, and later dragged the man out again, and between those two acts he had touched nothing in the street except snow, which had since buried everything.

So: they might know a courier existed. They might even know he'd reached the city. What they could not yet know — what no one could know unless Gideon told them — was whose door he'd died at.

Unless they suspected. Unless the Inquisitor was here precisely because they suspected, and the audit was theater, and every question was a net laid flat on the ground waiting for one particular foot.

Gideon looked down at his hands. Scribe's hands, soft at the fingertips, stained faintly brown at the cuticles no matter how he scrubbed. Hands that had signed his debt-bond at fourteen because his mother was dead and the Temple paid burial costs and there was no one left to say no. Eleven years served. Four years remaining. He had spent eleven years being exactly invisible enough to survive, and in one storm-wrecked night he had become the kind of man who hid corpses and stole maps and lied by omission to everyone who asked him how he slept.

*Four more years,* he reminded himself. *Keep your head down. Give them nothing. Walk out of this hall and go back to being furniture.*

He put on his coat. The map pressed against his spine like a second heartbeat.

* * *

The Chapter Hall of the Temple of Vessia had been built to make bonded men feel small, and it succeeded. Vaulted ceiling painted with the Ninefold Mercy of Vessia, all gold leaf and serene faces; floor of black marble polished to a mirror shine so that petitioners walked, in effect, on their own reflections. At the far end rose the abbot's chair, empty today, and beside it a plain ironwood desk that had been carried in for the occasion, and behind the desk sat the Inquisitor Malachi Voss.

Gideon had heard of Voss, the way you hear of wolves in the mountains — as a fact about the world rather than a presence in it. The reality was worse than the reputation, mostly because the reputation had prepared him for cruelty, and Voss wasn't cruel. That was the first thing Gideon understood, standing at the back of the queue of scribes while Voss questioned the man ahead of him. Voss was *precise*. He was a lean, grey-templed man of maybe fifty, dressed in unadorned wool the exact color of his riders' cloaks, with a face like a ledger itself — balanced, audited, every feature entered in its proper column. He listened the way Gideon copied: completely, without waste.

And he was kind. That was the horror of it. To the trembling journeyman currently in the chair, Voss spoke in a low, almost apologetic voice, thanking him for his time, commending the neatness of his hand, asking — gently, as one colleague to another — whether the man had seen anything unusual on the night of the great storm. Any strangers. Any disturbance. Anything at all that lived in memory but hadn't yet been written down.

"No, Inquisitor. I copied accounts until third bell and went straight to my bunk."

"Of course. Thank you, brother. Your diligence honors the Temple." A nod, and the next name was called.

One by one they went up. One by one they came back down unchanged, relieved, blinking in the candlelight like men surfacing from deep water. Gideon watched Voss work and felt his stomach slowly turn to stone, because he could see the method in it. Voss wasn't searching for guilt. Guilt was loud and easy to find. Voss was measuring each man against his own baseline — the speed of his answers, the pitch of his voice, the place his eyes went when a question landed. He was building a picture of what *normal* looked like in this room, so that whatever deviated from it would stand out like a bloodstain on white linen.

*Don't be interesting,* Gideon told himself. *Be exactly what you have always been.*

"Pemberton, Gideon. Bonded copyist, eleventh year."

He walked the length of the black floor and sat in the ironwood chair, and it was colder than he expected, or perhaps he was colder than he expected, and the two facts refused to separate.

Voss consulted a slate on his desk. "Eleventh year. Four remaining. Your record is clean — no infractions, no debts beyond the original bond, no petitions filed." He looked up, and his eyes were pale grey, nearly colorless, and utterly without hurry. "That's rare, copyist. Most men accrue *something*. A fine, a favor, a sin they end up paying interest on. You've kept yourself remarkably... unremarkable."

"I try to serve faithfully, Inquisitor."

"I'm sure you do. And faithful service is rewarded." Voss folded his hands. "Now. The Temple is conducting a routine audit of correspondence entering and leaving the city during the recent storms. The north road was closed for three days. Before that, however, a courier is believed to have traveled it — a man carrying documents of interest to this office. Perhaps you saw him. Perhaps you heard of him. A courier on the north road."

The question hung there, simple as a hook, patient as a hook.

Gideon had rehearsed nothing, because rehearsal shows, and yet some part of his mind — the part that mapped smuggling routes for pleasure, the part that had spent eleven years reading other people's ledgers and learning what people did with money they shouldn't have — answered for him, calm and clear.

"The north road?" he said. "I don't leave the scriptorium, Inquisitor. My days are ledgers."

"Of course. But news travels inside walls faster than outside them. Kitchens talk. Courtyards talk." Voss smiled slightly. "Did anything talk to you?"

*Say no. Say it plainly. Say it the way you say everything.*

"No," Gideon said. "Nothing came through the storms except wet and cold. We lost roof tiles on the south range. That was the excitement."

A silence. Small, measured, exact — Voss letting the answer sit in the air to see what would grow out of it.

"Roof tiles," Voss said.

"And a dead gutter on the east face. Brother Aldous fell in the cistern yard. Nothing broken but his dignity."

Something flickered across Voss's face — not suspicion, Gideon thought. Interest. The interest of a man who has asked a hundred men about storms and finally received an answer with texture in it. "You remember details," Voss observed. "Most men remember fear, or boredom. You remember gutters."

"Gutters are my scale of catastrophe, Inquisitor. When you spend your life copying other people's disasters in columns, yours had better stay small."

And there — God forgive him — there it was. The thrill. It moved through Gideon like cold wine, sweet and sickening at once, because he heard his own voice doing something it had never done in eleven bonded years: performing. Shaping the truth into a shape that suited him. Every word he'd spoken was technically true — the tiles, the gutter, poor Aldous — and arranged, deliberately, artfully, to build a wall of mundane detail between the Inquisitor and the dead man in the cistern. He was lying the way a forger lies, with true ink.

Voss studied him for a long moment. Then he did the last thing Gideon expected. He laughed — softly, briefly, a dry sound like paper turning.

"Well answered, copyist. Most men, when I mention a courier, either freeze or babble. You gave me weather reports." He leaned back. "Which tells me either you truly know nothing—" the pale eyes settled on Gideon's, weightless and total, like snowfall, "—or you're the most composed liar in this province. For the record, I meet perhaps one of those a decade."

"I'll take that as a compliment, Inquisitor."

"Take it as an observation. Observations are all I deal in." Voss made a small mark on his slate — Gideon couldn't see what it was, and the not-knowing would live in him for days — and gestured dismissal. "Thank you for your time, copyist. Keep your records clean. Clean records are a kind of freedom, in this life. Few men appreciate that until they lose it."

Gideon rose, bowed the precise degree required, and walked the length of the black floor on his own reflection.

He did not run. Running was interesting. He walked back to the scriptorium at a copyist's pace, sat at his desk, dipped his pen, and copied a line of grain tariffs with a hand that should have been shaking and was not.

Only when the bell rang for midday meal did he understand what had actually happened in that chair. Not interrogation — *investment*. Voss had marked him. Filed him away the way Gideon filed unusual entries in a ledger: not evidence, but anomaly. Anomalies got revisited.

The sick thrill was still in his blood, warm as brandy, and beneath it, quieter, a colder knowledge forming:

The Inquisitor would be back. And next time, he wouldn't be asking about couriers.

He'd be asking about gutters.
