## 5. Sera Quill Sells Passage

The Saltwrack smelled of tar, spilled ale, and the particular desperation of men who had bet their last coin on a bad tide. Sera Quill liked it for exactly that reason. Desperate men were honest in the way that mattered: they told you what they wanted before you asked.

She sat with her back to the wall, boots on the bench opposite, and watched Doss Marlow sweat through his third attempt to count her fee without wincing. He was a fat man with a merchant's rings and a smuggler's nerves, and he was about to learn that those two things did not mix.

"Forty crowns is robbery," he said. "I could get the whole run done for twenty."

"You could," Sera agreed. She turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the wine catch the lamplight. "And then you could explain to your widow why you took the cheap route through Thornfell in the first week of thaw. The passes are still sliding, Doss. Half the mountain's held together by prayer and old grudges. You want someone who knows which grudges are currently armed."

"That's why I came to *you*."

"And that's why it's forty."

He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at his partner, a narrow man named Pell who had been silent all evening in the manner of someone doing arithmetic he didn't like. Pell gave the smallest nod.

"Thirty-five," Doss said.

"Forty, and I carry nothing myself. Your crates stay sealed, your manifests stay blank, and if the Temple stops us, you never heard my name." She leaned forward. "That last part isn't negotiable. It's the whole product."

Doss chewed on it. Outside, the harbor slapped against pilings, and somewhere down the quay a winch groaned under a load too heavy for its ropes—a sound like an animal complaining about its work.

"Forty," he said at last, as though the word cost him teeth. "Half now."

"Half now, half when the cargo breathes free air on the far side of the pass." She stuck out her hand. He shook it, ringed fingers damp. "Pleasure doing business. Try not to look so relieved until we're actually through."

Pell counted out the coins from a canvas purse, and Sera swept them into her coat without counting them back—counting them would have been an insult to both of them—but she felt the weight, and the weight was right.

* * *

When they'd gone she stayed where she was, because leaving immediately after a deal closed was how you got followed, and being followed was how you got robbed, and getting robbed was how you ended up drinking in taverns like this one instead of merely conducting business in them.

She ordered another cup she didn't want and let the room come back into focus around her. The Saltwrack at mid-evening: dockhands off shift, two Temple clerks slumming it badly in the corner booth, a card game going wrong near the hearth, and the usual drift of people who existed in the space between what the city permitted and what it tolerated.

And one man at the end of the bar who had been watching her since before Doss Marlow arrived.

Sera clocked him the way she clocked everything—sideways, through the smoky mirror behind the bottles, without ever letting her eyes land long enough to be caught. Thin. Younger than she'd first guessed, maybe mid-twenties, with ink stains worked so deep into the creases of his fingers that no scrubbing would ever get them out. A scribe's hands. Temple-grey cloak, but worn soft at the shoulders, the hem darkened with street grime. He sat like a man trying to take up less space than he occupied, hunched over a single cup of something cheap, and he had been nursing it for the better part of two hours.

But none of that was what held her attention.

What held her attention was the look on his face. Not lust—she knew lust when it was aimed at her, and this wasn't it, though there was something adjacent to it, some hungry attention that kept circling back. Not fear either, or not only. It was the look of a man carrying something heavy and trying to decide whether the woman across the room might be strong enough to help him hold it—or dangerous enough to take it.

She'd seen that look before. She'd worn it herself, once, coming down the coast road with a hold full of contraband and her brother's name burning a hole in her pocket. It was the look of a man whose life had recently stopped fitting inside the shape it used to be.

*Interesting*, she thought, and made herself look away.

Curiosity was a luxury. That was the first lesson of her trade, learned young and paid for dearly: the curious smuggler died rich in stories and poor in everything else. You moved cargo. You didn't ask what was in it. You noticed exits, prices, and threats, and you let everything else wash past you like water off waxed canvas.

But curiosity was also the only thing that had ever gotten her anywhere worth going.

She turned the cup again. In the mirror, the scribe shifted, and his coat pulled tight across his chest—and there, just visible at the collar where the fabric gapped, a line of fresh stitching. Coarse thread, hurried work, sewing shut something flat and rectangular sewn *into* the lining itself.

Well, now.

* * *

She gave him ten more minutes, then rose, crossed the room, and set her cup down on the bar beside his with the deliberate casualness of a woman claiming territory.

"This seat taken?"

"It's a bar," he said. His voice was quiet, careful, the voice of a man accustomed to being spoken over. "Seats are generally available."

"Generally." She settled onto the stool, angled so she could see the door in the mirror behind the bottles. Old habits. "You've been nursing that cup since before the fat man and I finished our business. Either you're broke, or you're waiting for something, and broke men don't sit in the Saltwrack—they drink at the Broken Oar, where the watered ale costs half as much."

Something flickered across his face. Wariness, mostly, but underneath it a flicker of dry humor, quickly suppressed, like a candle flame cupped against wind.

"Maybe I like the company."

"Nobody likes the company here." She smiled. "What's your name?"

"Why do you want to know?"

"Because names are how conversations start, and conversations are how I find out whether you're a problem or an opportunity. Right now you're sitting exactly between the two, and I don't love ambiguity."

He looked at her then, fully, for the first time. Grey eyes, tired around the edges, with a sharpness underneath the exhaustion that surprised her—she'd expected a beaten thing, and instead found something that had been beaten and had *noticed*, and was keeping accounts.

"Gideon," he said finally.

"Sera Quill." She didn't offer a hand; offering a hand invited reading her palm, and her palm had calluses in places that told stories better left untold. "Temple scribe, by the cloak. Off duty, or escaped?"

His jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "Indentured," he said. "There's a difference."

"There always is." She signaled the barkeep for two more of whatever Gideon was having, and paid, and watched him register the small transactional kindness and immediately distrust it. Good. Distrustful men were honest men. It was the trusting ones who lied beautifully. "So, Gideon-the-indentured. What does a Temple scribe do in the Saltwrack on a wet Thursday? The clerks' quarter's a mile east, and the wine there doesn't taste like bilge."

"I could ask what a smuggler does negotiating passage through Thornfell in the first week of thaw."

It was her turn to go still. She covered it by lifting her fresh cup, but she saw him see the stillness, and cursed herself silently for the tell. *Careless.* Two hours of watching him and she'd let a stranger read her in a sentence.

"You know my trade," she said lightly.

"I know Thornfell closes to licensed traffic after the first slides. Which means anyone booking passage through it now is either a fool or—" he paused, choosing the word with a scribe's precision, "—unlicensed."

"A smuggler."

"I didn't say it."

"You didn't have to." She studied him properly now, dropping the pretense of the mirror, and he bore the scrutiny better than most men did—didn't fidget, didn't bristle, just met her eyes with that ledger-keeper's steadiness. "You've been watching me all night, scribe. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. So here's my question, and I'll only ask it once: are you looking to buy, or looking to sell?"

"Neither."

"Everyone's buying or selling something. Most people just don't know what yet." She drank. "You're carrying something. Sewn into your coat, left side, over the ribs. Heavy enough to change how you sit—you keep your left arm tucked, like the weight pulls. And you've been staring at me for two hours because you heard me book a run through Thornfell, and whatever you're carrying needs to go to Thornfell, or past it, and you don't know how to ask."

The silence between them stretched. Behind them the card game erupted—shouted accusations, a chair scraping—and neither of them flinched, which told her more about him than anything he'd said.

"You're wrong," he said quietly.

"I'm rarely wrong. When I am, it's expensive, so I try to schedule it." She leaned in, close enough that he could smell the salt and leather of her, close enough to watch his pulse move in his throat. "Here's what I think, Gideon. I think three days ago you were nobody—a grey cloak copying somebody else's numbers, invisible, safe. And then something happened. Something that put weight in your coat and a hunted look in your eyes, and now every instinct you've ever trusted is telling you to keep your head down and wait for it to blow over. Am I warm?"

"You're a very strange person," he said.

"I'm a very *observant* person. It's the same thing with better manners." She straightened, letting him have his air back. "I'm not going to rob you. If I wanted what's in your coat, I'd have already taken it—I've been behind you twice tonight and you never noticed, which, no offense, is genuinely embarrassing. I'm not going to report you, either. The Temple and I have an arrangement: they pretend I don't exist, and I return the courtesy."

"Then what do you want?"

"Information," she said. "Freely given, freely returned. You want to move through Thornfell. I'm the best route-runner on this coast, and unlike Doss Marlow, I don't panic in rockfall, and I don't leave clients bleeding in ditches. Whatever you're carrying, wherever it's going—if it goes through the mountains, it goes safer with me than without me. That's not a threat and it's not a trap. It's just arithmetic."

"And the price?"

"Depends what I learn on the road." She smiled, slow and unapologetic. "I told you. Everyone's selling something. My price is finding out what yours is."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched the war happen behind his eyes—the careful man versus the desperate one, the scribe who'd survived years of invisibility versus whatever new thing had cracked him open. She'd seen that war before. She'd even won it before, though usually the winnings hadn't been worth the fight.

"Not tonight," he said at last. He stood, leaving coins on the bar—enough for both cups, she noted, and a tip besides, which marked him as either generous or guilty. "Thank you for the drink, Mistress Quill."

"I haven't sold you anything yet."

"No," he said, pulling his hood up. "But you will."

He walked out into the rain, and Sera watched him go through the fogged window—watched him check the street both ways before stepping out, a habit he clearly hadn't had a week ago, and watched him turn north, toward the Temple district, walking like a man rehearsing innocence.

* * *

She stayed another hour, out of discipline, and spent it thinking.

The client was the problem. Three weeks ago a message had reached her through channels she didn't fully trust, offering a sum that made her eyes water: find a courier traveling south from the clan territories, recover what the courier carried, deliver it to a contact in the capital. No questions. No names. Payment half up front, delivered to a dead-drop that had been stocked on time and in full, which meant whoever was behind it had resources and meant business.

The courier, she'd learned through two weeks of quiet asking, was dead. Found collapsed in a doorway in the clerks' quarter, no wounds anyone would admit to, body gone before the watch arrived. And the thing he'd carried—

Her eyes drifted to the window, to the wet street where a grey-cloaked figure had disappeared.

Well. Apparently it hadn't gone far.

She turned her cup. The arithmetic was ugly. Her anonymous client wanted the package recovered, and here was the package wearing a Temple cloak and stitching his secrets into his own lining, sitting three feet away from her for the price of two cups of bilge wine. She could have taken it tonight. Could follow him home, put a knife to that soft careful throat, cut the stitches herself, and be on a horse by dawn with half her fortune secured.

She'd done worse for less. She wasn't going to pretend otherwise; self-deception was a luxury for people with clean ledgers.

But.

But the client's terms had been specific in one strange way: *recover the item, learn who carries it, report.* Learn who carries it. Why would a buyer care who the carrier was, unless the carrier mattered? Unless the carrier was part of the value?

And there was the other thing, the thing she kept turning over at night like a coin worn smooth: the client had promised her more than money. Buried in the fine terms of that first message, in language she'd read a dozen times, was a second payment offered in kind rather than coin. *Information regarding persons formerly held in Temple custody.* She had a brother in Temple custody. Had been eleven years old when the grey cloaks took Tam from the dock at Corrow's Bend, and had spent nineteen years not knowing whether he was alive, and had signed on to this job with her heart hammering like a girl's because of four words in a contract.

If she robbed the scribe and ran, she'd have the item. But the client wanted the *carrier* understood, and if she failed the terms, the second payment evaporated—and with it, maybe, the only lead to her brother she'd had in two decades.

So she needed Gideon alive. Needed him moving. Needed him, ideally, heading exactly where she needed to go—which, if the map stitched into his coat was what she suspected, was precisely the direction of Thornfell and the high country beyond.

"Fool's luck," she murmured to her cup. "Or somebody else's design."

Either way, the play was the same. Stay close. Let him run. Be there when the running brought him to the mountains, because it would—men carrying weights like his always ran for the hills eventually, and the hills were where she lived.

She left coins on the bar, shrugged into her oilskin, and pushed out into the rain. The harbor stretched black and gleaming under the lamps, masts swaying, the whole city smelling of salt and secrets. Somewhere north, a bell tolled the late hour—one of the Temple's bells, patient and proprietary, counting hours for a city that had forgotten it was ever allowed to count for itself.

Sera turned her collar up and started walking toward the docks, already composing in her head the coded note she'd send to her dead-drop in the morning:

*Package located. Carrier identified. Moving.*

She didn't sign it. She never signed anything.

Behind her, up the hill in the clerks' quarter, a certain scribe was walking home through the rain with death sewn into his coat, unaware that three separate hungers had now fixed on him—hers, her client's, and whatever cold patience the Temple itself brought to bear on lost property.

By morning, one of them would reach him first.

She intended it to be hers.
