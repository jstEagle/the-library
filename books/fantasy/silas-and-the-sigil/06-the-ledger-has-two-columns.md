## 6. The Ledger Has Two Columns

They came for me on the fourth day after the storm.

I knew it before the knock came. I'd spent four days learning what guilt felt like when it lived in a body instead of a ledger—how it sat in the shoulders, how it turned sleep into a negotiation you lost by dawn. Every morning I woke certain that today was the day they found Wren under the flagstones of my cellar, and every morning I rose and copied tithing records in a hand so steady it looked like print, because steadiness was the only armor I owned.

The knock came at midday, which was worse than night. Night knocks were smugglers and debt collectors, things with shapes you could name. A midday knock from the Temple meant procedure, and procedure meant someone had already decided how this would end.

"Gideon Pemberton." Not a question. Inquisitor Malachi Voss stood in my doorway wearing grey the way other men wore knives—not carried, but grown into. He was younger than his reputation suggested, forty perhaps, with a scholar's pale hands and a face built for patience. Two temple guards flanked him, and behind them, the alley had gone quiet in that particular way alleys go quiet when neighbors decide they were never home.

"Inquisitor." I bowed the correct depth. Bonded scribes learned bow-depths the way sailors learned knots. "An honor."

"It's an audit." He stepped past me without invitation, and I let him, because refusing an Inquisitor entry to a bonded man's quarters was a legal act the way drowning was a swimming technique. "You'll forgive the informality. I find informality loosens tongues better than irons do."

"Mine is already loose, sir."

"We'll see." His eyes went over my room the way mine went over a ledger column—top to bottom, left to right, hunting for the figure that didn't add. The cot. The desk. The single window with its view of the Temple's eastern wall. The cold hearth I hadn't lit since the storm, because ash held smells and smells held questions.

He stopped at the desk. My current work lay there: the third quarter's candle-tithes for the Sisters' dormitory, forty lines of perfectly dull arithmetic. He read it for a long moment without touching it.

"Your hand is very good," he said.

"Thank you, sir."

"Too good, some say. Brother Aldous complains you finish allocations in half the allotted time and then sit idle." He turned. "What do you do with the idle hours, Pemberton?"

I thought of the map sewn into the lining of my winter coat, three floors down in the chest where I kept my mother's things, and I thought of the cellar flagstone, and I said: "I sleep, sir. Idle hours are the only luxury of my station."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe, of a move well played—or a note taken for later.

* * *

He made me walk with him to the Temple. That was the form the audit took: not a room with instruments, but a stroll through the cloisters while he asked questions in the tone of a man discussing weather. The guards trailed us at a distance calibrated to be visible without being acknowledged.

"The north road," Voss said, as we passed the scriptorium's high windows. "Tell me what you know of it."

"The north road runs to Thornfell pass, sir. Closed eight months of the year. Toll house at the mile-marker, shrine waystation at the ridge line, both imperial charters."

"I didn't ask for the surveyor's answer."

"You asked what I know."

He stopped walking. We stood beneath an arch carved with Vessia's scales, and the light through it fell across his face in bars, and I understood suddenly why they built arches like that—to make every conversation look like a judgment whether it was one or not.

"A courier died four nights ago," he said. "Somewhere between the north gate and the river district. We don't know precisely where, because no one has reported a body, and no one has reported a missing courier, which interests me more than the death itself. A man who dies unmourned and unreported was either alone in the world"—he resumed walking, and I had to follow—"or was made to be alone. Which do you suppose?"

"The former, sir. The city is full of such men."

"It is. I was one of them once." He said it flatly, a coin paid out to buy something. "Storm that night. Bad night to be on the roads. A wounded man wouldn't have gotten far." He glanced sideways at me. "Would he?"

"No, sir."

"No. He'd have needed shelter. A doorway. A cellar. The kindness of a stranger." We walked ten paces in silence. Somewhere above us a bell marked the half-hour, and I counted the strokes the way I counted everything now, because counting was the wall I'd built between myself and the memory of blood spreading across my threshold in the rain.

"Inquisitor," I said carefully, "am I accused of something?"

"You're *asked* something. Accusation comes later, if it comes at all, and it will come dressed far worse than I'm dressed now." He paused at the cloister's edge, where the gardens began—the Temple's famous herb garden, rows of medicinal plants laid out in the shape of Vessia's sigil, visible only from above. From ground level it was just dirt and green. "I've read your bond record, Pemberton. Do you know what it says?"

"Eleven years served. Fourteen remaining."

"It says you were bonded at nineteen for your mother's debts. Medical debts—her last illness. It says you've never once been flagged for irregularity. No late filings, no discrepancies, no complaints. Fourteen years of perfect columns." He turned to face me fully, and up close his eyes were a grey so light they seemed almost colorless, like water over slate. "Do you know how rare that is? In fourteen years, a man makes mistakes. Rounds numbers in his own favor. Loses a page. I've audited six hundred bonded scribes, and every one of them had a flaw in the pattern somewhere. Except you."

"Perhaps I'm simply careful."

"Perhaps. Or perhaps a man who has spent his whole life being watched learns to leave no marks at all." He let that hang. Then, softly, almost kindly: "Here is what I believe, Pemberton. I believe a courier came off the north road in that storm carrying something worth killing for. I believe he was hurt, badly, and that he reached the river district before he failed. And I believe that whoever found him is now holding whatever he carried—and hasn't slept since."

The bells finished their count. A gardener knelt among the feverfew, pretending with tremendous discipline not to listen.

"And if I were that person," I said, "why would I tell you?"

Voss smiled then—a real one, brief and tired, and somehow that was more frightening than everything before it. "Because," he said, "I can take fourteen years off your bond."

* * *

They gave us a room for the offer. Of course they did; the Temple never made a bargain in the open air, where words could be witnessed by weather. A small chamber off the audit wing, a table, two chairs, a carafe of water and two cups—the ceremony of fairness performed over the machinery of leverage.

"Fourteen years," Voss repeated, seated across from me. He set a document on the table between us, face down, his hand resting on it like a man resting his hand on a dog's head. "Full absolution of the remainder of your debt-bond. Your name struck from the indenture rolls. Free papers, countersigned by the Provost himself. You could walk out of this building tomorrow and no door in the empire could lawfully close to you again."

I looked at the paper. I made myself look at it the way you make yourself look at a wound—because looking away was how you died of these things.

Fourteen years. I had done the arithmetic of my life so many times it had worn grooves in me. Bonded at nineteen. Free at forty-seven, if my health held, if the Temple didn't extend the term for some technical infraction, if the debt didn't accrue interest during years of illness as it had for others I'd known. Forty-seven. My mother hadn't reached forty. Most bonded scribes didn't reach freedom; they reached the end of their usefulness, and the Temple pensioned them into a charity ward with a view of the wall.

And here it was, on one page, in exchange for one sentence.

"What information?" I asked. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"The courier. Where you found him. What he carried. What you did with it." Voss poured water into both cups, though I hadn't agreed to anything, and slid one toward me. "That's all. One account, honestly given, and the columns balance."

"And if I tell you I found nothing? That no one died at my door?"

"Then I'll write that down too." He drank. "But I'll keep asking, Pemberton, and each time I ask, the price goes up. That's not a threat. It's just how audits work. Today it's absolution. Next week it might be absolution *and* your cooperation in a formal inquiry. The month after, a tribunal. The offers don't improve. Only the consequences do."

I wrapped my hands around the cup. The water was cold, and the cold felt honest in a way nothing else in the room did.

"May I ask you something, Inquisitor?"

"You may."

"Why does the Temple care about one dead courier? The city buries forty unnamed men a week. Why send an Inquisitor—an *Inquisitor*—to walk the cloisters asking after him?"

For a moment he said nothing, and I watched him decide, deliberately, how much truth to spend. It was the same calculation I'd been making for four days. Recognizing it in another man's face was like seeing my own reflection in dark water.

"Because of what he carried," Voss said finally. "There are symbols appearing on documents moving through the underworld of this city. Old symbols. Pre-imperial. They're attached to smuggling manifests, to clan correspondence, to things I am not at liberty to describe. Something is waking up in the mountains, Pemberton, and the last time it woke, the war that followed filled two mass graves and put the fear of Vessia into a generation." He leaned forward. "The Temple does not care about a dead courier. The Temple cares about the peace. The peace is the only wall standing between the empire and the clans, and walls, as any bonded man knows, require maintenance."

"And you think a courier's map threatens it."

"I think a courier died to move something, and moved it anyway, which means he believed it mattered more than his life." Voss's voice dropped, and for one strange moment he sounded less like an instrument of the Temple than like a tired man telling the truth. "People die for gossip every day. People do not bleed out in a stranger's doorway clutching oilcloth unless what's inside it frightens them more than dying did."

The room was very quiet. I could hear the Temple breathing around us—the distant shuffle of novices, the creak of the great clock, the sound of an institution digesting.

"I want to help you," I said, and the terrible thing was that part of it was true. Some starving corner of me wanted to set the whole weight down, wanted to say *yes, there was a man, he died in my doorway, I buried him under the floor, there's a map covered in writing no temple script matches and a locket with a woman's face in it, take it, take all of it, let me sleep.* The confession sat in my throat like a stone I could have spat out in three seconds and been free.

Free.

The word did its work. It always did its work. Fourteen years of my life, weighed against one sentence. Any auditor in the empire would call that a fair trade. Any auditor would mark the column *paid in full* and feel good about it.

I thought about Wren's face in the rain. I hadn't known her name then—I still didn't—but I remembered how she'd looked at me when she pressed the bundle into my hands. Not pleading. *Trusting.* She had chosen my doorway out of every doorway in the river district, and she had spent her last strength not on begging for help but on making sure I took it. Whatever she'd been running from, she'd believed I was the destination. Not a shelter. A destination.

You don't hand a stranger your life's errand. You hand it to family, or to fate, or to whoever the map says. And I had spent four days not asking why my doorway, because the answer was a door I wasn't ready to open.

"Pemberton." Voss's voice recalled me. "You've gone somewhere. Come back."

"I'm here, Inquisitor." I set down the cup. "I have no courier to report. No stranger, no oilcloth, no map. On the night of the storm I worked late on the Aldous allocation, went home, and slept poorly, as I always do. If a man died on the north road, I grieve him, but I never saw him."

I lied. I heard myself lie, in a bonded man's measured voice, to an Inquisitor of the Temple of Vessia, and the astonishing thing was how easy it was—like a muscle I'd never used turning out to be strong. Somewhere beneath the terror, a small cold voice noted the fact for future reference, and I hated that voice, and I knew I would need it.

Voss studied me for a long time. Then he turned the document over, drew a pen from his coat, and wrote a single line across it. He turned it back toward me.

*Subject interviewed. No relevant knowledge. File remains open.*

"Do you understand what 'remains open' means?" he asked.

"I believe I do, sir."

"It means I don't believe you. It means I lack the evidence to prove otherwise, and the Temple acts on proof, not suspicion—that's the covenant, and I honor it even when it inconveniences me." He stood, and the interview was over in the way a door closing is over. "But suspicion is patient, Pemberton. Suspicion can wait years. It has your address."

At the threshold he paused, without turning around.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—the men who lie best to me are always the ones who found something they can't put down. Whatever it is you're carrying, scribe, ask yourself who gave it to you, and why they chose *you*. People rarely choose at random. When you understand the choice, you'll understand the weight." He glanced back, grey eyes catching the lamplight. "My offer stands until it doesn't. Pray you come to me before I come to you again."

Then he was gone, and the guards with him, and I sat alone in the little room with the untouched water and the open file and the shaking I had been holding off for four days finally arriving all at once, in my hands first and then everywhere.

* * *

I didn't go straight home. I walked the long way, through the market district, buying things I didn't need—a twist of dried figs, a stub of tallow candle—because a man followed by suspicion should be seen doing ordinary things. The fig-seller joked about the weather. I laughed in the right places. Inside, behind the laughing, I turned Voss's parting words over and over like a stone with something underneath.

*Ask yourself who gave it to you, and why they chose you.*

I got home after dark. I checked the street, checked the window, checked the cellar flagstone that hid what remained of Wren, and then I went to the chest and took out the coat with its sewn seam, and I sat by the unlit hearth holding the locket up to the candlelight.

The woman in the miniature looked back at me. She had looked back at me for four days now, and every day the resemblance grew harder to dismiss—the same narrow jaw, the same deep-set eyes, the same left-tilted mouth that I shaved around every morning in a cracked mirror. I had told myself it was coincidence. Coincidence was a comfortable country, and I had lived there a long time.

But Voss was right about one thing. People rarely choose at random.

A dying courier had crossed a city in a storm, bleeding, hunted, and had spent her final minutes making sure the map reached *me*—a debt-bonded scribe with no rank, no money, and no significance that any ledger in the empire would record. Unless the ledger was wrong. Unless there was a second column, hidden, running alongside the visible one all my life: my mother's disappearances, her pilgrimages, her lullabies in a tongue she claimed was just market-talk, her death on a road she'd never explained.

I closed the locket. Outside, far off, the Temple bells rang the ninth hour, and beneath their bronze certainty I thought I heard another sound—hooves, maybe, or wind, or the beginning of the thing that was coming for me either way.

Tomorrow, I decided. Tomorrow I would find out what my mother never told me.

Tonight, I packed.
