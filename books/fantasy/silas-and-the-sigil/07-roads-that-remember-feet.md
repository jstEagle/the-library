## 7. Roads That Remember Feet

The bells of Vessia rang the ninth hour, and Gideon Pemberton walked out of the Temple of the Veiled Mother for the last time.

He had rehearsed it for three days. Not the walking out—that part was simple; bonded scribes came and went on errands all the time—but the *not coming back*. The difference between an errand and a flight was a single decision, made once and never unmade, and he had spent those three days carrying the decision around inside him like a swallowed coal, waiting to see if it would burn through his stomach or settle into something he could live with.

It settled. Mostly.

He wore his ordinary coat, the grey one with the frayed cuffs, because nothing announced guilt like a new garment. Under his arm he carried a ledger—the quarterly tithe rolls for the parish of Saint Aldhelm's, a delivery so dull that no one would question it. And sewn into the lining of the coat, flat against his left side where a knife would sit if he were the kind of man who carried knives, was the map.

Sewing it in had taken him most of one night, working by candle stub with a sailmaker's needle he'd bought from a chandler near the fish market, haggling over the price like a man with nothing heavier on his mind than canvas thread. His stitches were ugly. Scribes were trained for vellum and ink, not leather and oilcloth, and more than once he'd driven the needle through his own glove and into the pad of his thumb. He'd sucked the blood away and kept sewing. It seemed fitting, somehow. The map had already cost one life. A little of his was only fair payment in advance.

*You could still turn back,* said the voice that sounded like the Temple provost, dry as old vellum. *Walk to Voss's office. Put the oilcloth on his desk. Say: Inquisitor, a dying man gave me this, I know nothing else, please don't ruin me.*

And then he'd be free. Debt absolved, maybe—Voss had hinted at it—or at least unpunished, returned to his desk, his ledgers, his forty remaining years of copying other men's numbers until his eyes went the way of his father's and they pensioned him off to some almshouse with a Bible and a blanket.

Gideon crossed the Temple courtyard under the ninth-hour bell and did not look back at the scriptorium windows.

*No,* he told the provost's voice. *I don't think I will.*

* * *

The smuggler's path began behind a tannery on Corder's Lane, and it began, as all the best illegal things in Corvane began, with a bribe.

The tanner was a woman named Hesk with forearms like ship's cable and a face that suggested she'd been carved from something the tanning vats had failed to improve. She'd known Gideon's mother, back before. That was the whole of their acquaintance—a handful of coins passed across a fence line twice a year since Gideon was nineteen, no questions asked, no questions answered. His mother had taught him which doors opened that way. It was nearly the only thing she'd taught him that had ever proved useful.

"You look terrible," Hesk said, not looking up from the hide she was scraping.

"I feel wonderful."

"Hm." She set down her scraping knife and studied him with eyes the color of dishwater. "Running or erranding?"

"Erranding," Gideon said. "For my health."

"For your health." She snorted. "Boy, I've watched you walk that fence line for eleven years. You walk like a man counting his steps so he doesn't have to count anything else. Tonight you're walking like a man who's finally stopped counting." She jerked her chin toward the gate at the back of the yard, beyond which the ground fell away into a ravine choked with alder and blackberry. "Coldwater Cut's open. The flood took the footbridge in spring, but there's a crossing at the old weir if you don't mind wet boots. After the weir you're on drover's ground. Nobody official goes there because there's nothing official to tax."

"Hesk."

"Don't thank me. Thanks are memorable." She picked up her knife again. "And boy—if anyone asks, you never came here. I've got a business."

"You've always got a business."

"That's why I've still got hands." She flexed them, all ten, scarred and gleaming with tallow. "Go on. Bell rang nine minutes ago. You want to be past the granary watchpost before the change of shift, when they're arguing about dice instead of watching the road."

He went. At the gate he paused, and against every instinct honed by eleven years of careful invisibility, he turned and said, "My mother used to talk about you. She said you were the only honest criminal in Corvane."

Hesk didn't look up. But her knife paused for exactly one heartbeat, and then resumed, and Gideon understood that he had just paid her a debt older than his bond.

* * *

Coldwater Cut earned its name within the first hundred yards.

The ravine swallowed the city's noise the way a throat swallows a pill—one moment the clank and murmur of Corvane, the next a green silence broken only by water talking to itself somewhere below. The path was barely a path: a shelf of packed earth worn into the hillside by generations of feet that preferred not to be remembered anywhere official. Gideon knew it the way he knew the smuggling routes he drew in the margins of his private notebook—not from maps, but from his mother's stories, told in fragments across years, half-lullaby half-lesson, about roads that remember feet.

*Roads remember, Gid. That's what people forget. A road doesn't care what the magistrates say. A road only cares who's walked it, and how often, and whether they were kind to the people along it. Walk a road kindly enough times and it'll carry you when you can't carry yourself.*

She'd said it like nonsense. Like a song. He'd thought, at eight years old, that it was just another of the strange things his mother said in that other language she sometimes slipped into when she thought he was asleep.

Now, climbing down through the dark with the map pressing warm against his ribs, he wondered.

The weir crossing was exactly as bad as advertised. The Coldwater ran high with snowmelt even this late in the year, and the old weir—a spillway built by some forgotten miller—turned the river into a sheet of white noise thirty feet wide. Gideon found the crossing stones by memory and moonlight, tested the first with his boot, and committed.

The water hit him at the knee and tried to take the rest of him with it. For three heart-stopping seconds he was a scribe again, helpless, bookish, drowning in a mountain stream with a stolen ledger floating away beside him—and then something older than eleven years of desk work woke up in his legs, some animal balance inherited from whoever his mother had been, and he found the second stone, and the third, and hauled himself up the far bank gasping and soaked to the thigh and *laughing*.

Laughing. Alone in the dark, dripping, wanted-or-soon-to-be-wanted, and laughing like a boy.

He sat down on a boulder and let the laughter run itself out, and when it was gone what remained underneath was not fear. That was the frightening part. What remained was something bright and greedy and awake, something that had been fed on ledger ink and bell schedules for eleven years and had apparently not died of it, and it looked out through his eyes at the black mass of the Thornfell foothills rising against the stars and it said, in a voice suspiciously like his mother's:

*Well. Finally.*

* * *

He walked through the small hours with the discipline of a man who has read enough travel accounts to know how people die in the wild: badly, stupidly, and of their own impatience. He rationed his pace. He ate at intervals. When the moon set he allowed himself one candle-end's worth of rest in the lee of a drystone wall, wrapped in his coat, the map a stiff presence against his side like a second heartbeat.

Sleep wouldn't come. Instead his mind did what minds do—it replayed.

Wren. The courier. The blood on his doorstep going cold while the Temple bells rang third bell, and him standing there with an oilcloth bundle in his hands and a dead stranger at his feet, making the first truly lawless decision of his life: not the hiding of the body—that had been panic, animal and quick—but the keeping of the package. The moment after, when any sensible man would have carried it straight to the Inquisitor's office, and Gideon had looked down at the bloodstained wrapping and felt the coal drop into his stomach.

*Burn it,* the provost's voice had said. *Or surrender it. Those are the only two clean choices.*

He had done neither. He had unwrapped it, alone, at midnight, like a child shaking presents.

The map. The symbols that matched no temple script he knew—which was saying something; Gideon had spent eleven years learning scripts the way other men learned drinking songs. And the locket. The locket with the miniature portrait inside, the woman with the dark hair and the high cheekbones and the eyes—

His eyes. His mother's eyes. Painted by someone who had loved her, or hated her, or both.

He'd told himself for three days that the familiarity meant nothing. Miniature portraits were commodities; faces repeated; grief and coincidence made strangers into family every day in a city the size of Corvane. He'd almost believed it.

Then Voss had come, grey and patient, asking about a courier on the north road, and Gideon had lied—lied smoothly, fluently, to an Inquisitor of the Temple of Vessia, with his debt-bond hanging over him like a blade—and discovered that lying fit him like the coat he stood in. Like something cut for him by someone who knew his measurements.

That was the real reason he was running, if he was honest. Not the map. Not the Sigil, whatever it was. He was running because for the first time in his adult life, deception had felt less like sin and more like *competence*, and he needed to get out of Corvane before he found out what else he was competent at.

* * *

Dawn caught him on the drover's road above the valley, and with the dawn came the thing he'd been refusing to think about.

He heard it before he saw it: hoofbeats, arrhythmic, wrong for a mail coach, wrong for farm traffic. He stepped off the road into a stand of gorse without hurrying—hurrying was what guilty men did, and the trick, he'd learned in three days of thinking about nothing else, was to move like a man with every right to be where he was—and waited, thorns snagging his sleeves, and watched the road behind him.

Two riders. Grey cloaks. Temple issue, though the grey was muted, travel-worn, the livery of men who didn't want to be identified as Temple issue. They moved at a trot, unhurried, scanning. One of them raised a hand and his companion reined in, and they sat their horses in the middle of the drover's road and looked—at the ground? At the walls? At the gorse?

At the weir crossing, probably. Wet boots left prints. Gideon looked down at his own trail with the eye of a man who had spent eleven years studying records: mud, pressed grass, a scuff on the crossing stones. He might as well have signed it.

But the riders didn't turn toward the Cut. They continued north along the drover's road at their patient trot, and after a long minute they dropped below the shoulder of the hill and were gone.

Not pursuit, then. Patrol. Or—worse, his mind supplied helpfully—pursuit that was ahead of him, sweeping the road north while he crouched in a bush south of it, having successfully hidden from the search by being in the wrong place entirely.

Gideon stayed in the gorse until his legs cramped, then climbed out, brushed himself off with as much dignity as a man covered in thorn-scratches could muster, and considered.

They were between him and the high passes. If they held the road, he'd have to go around—east through the sheep country, higher and harder, a route his mother's stories touched only glancingly. *The shepherd stairs*, she'd called it once, in that half-dreaming voice. *Steep as a sermon and twice as narrow, but the shepherds keep it clear because the clans pay wool-prices for salt.* Salt trails. Wool trails. Roads that remembered feet.

His mother had known these hills. Not as a pilgrim, not as a tourist—as a native knows them, the way you know a house in the dark. Every fragmentary story, every odd phrase in that other tongue, every unexplained absence of her childhood summers suddenly rearranged itself in Gideon's mind like letters resolving into words.

She hadn't been teaching him geography. She'd been leaving him a key.

He stood on the drover's road in the grey morning light, soaked, scratched, hungry, hunted, free—and the freedom hit him all at once, sideways, the way grief hits, and he had to put a hand on the drystone wall and breathe through it. Eleven years of bells. Eleven years of asking permission to exist. And now this: sky, wind, a road going north, and nobody's leave required.

It was glorious. It was theft—he had stolen this from the Temple, from the contract his father's debts had signed, from the orderly life that had been measured out for him in annual installments. Every step north was a step deeper into wrongdoing, and every step sang.

*Guilty freedom,* he thought. *Is there any other kind?*

He checked the sun, got his bearing, and turned east, toward the shepherd stairs, walking now like a man who counted nothing at all.

Behind him, far down the valley, a hawk turned once over the ridge and then flew south, fast, purposeful, as though carrying news.

Gideon didn't see it. But the road remembered, and the road, as his mother used to say, tells everyone.
