## 8. An Unwelcome Guide

The trouble with mountain trails in autumn was that they told you everything and promised nothing.

Sera Quill had learned that lesson at fourteen, hauling contraband brandy over the Serrat Pass with her father's old mule and her mother's old debts, and she'd never unlearned it. A trail showed you where feet had gone—worn stone, snapped heather, the particular way a switchback silted up when nobody used it anymore—but it wouldn't tell you when they'd gone, or how many, or whether they were still ahead of you or already dead in a ravine with their secrets spilled down the scree.

She crouched at the mouth of the shepherd's cut above Vael's Crossing and read the ground like a ledger.

One man. Heavy boots, city-made, the left heel worn outer-edge—a walker who favored his right knee, or a man who'd spent too many years hunched over a desk. He'd passed through the cut sometime after the rain, maybe six hours, maybe eight. He'd stopped once to rest against the leaning stone—there was the fresh scrape of a shoulder—and then he'd moved on fast, faster than a man carrying nothing should move.

But he was carrying something. She'd bet her ship on it. Well. She'd bet Kess's ship, which amounted to the same thing and was one of the many reasons Kess no longer let her near his ledgers.

"The scribe with the map."

That was all the client had said. No name, no description beyond "temple-bred, soft hands, will run north." The message had come through the usual channels—sealed wax, no seal-mark, payment in new-minted imperial crowns that hadn't passed through enough dirty hands to be traceable. Half now, half on delivery of *the map*, the man himself explicitly not required. In fact, the client had been very clear: *the scribe is incidental. Do him no harm if avoidable, but the map is the prize.*

Sera didn't like jobs where the client knew more than she did. She liked them even less when the client paid too well. This one paid like a man buying back something that could hang him—which meant either a temple official with sins to bury or an empire man with ambitions to feed, and either way, the kind of person who paid half up front had no intention of paying the other half.

Which was why she wasn't going to deliver anything to anyone until she knew exactly what she was carrying.

She straightened, rolled her shoulders against the cold, and started up the cut at a pace that would eat six hours of head start by nightfall.

* *

She caught sight of him at dusk, on the long exposed traverse below the Grey Stair, and for a moment she just watched him because watching was free and information was the only currency that never devalued.

He was doing badly. That was the first thing. He walked like a man arguing with every step, stopping to check the sun, checking it again as though it might have changed its mind about setting. His coat was good quality but wrong for the mountains—temple wool, dyed grey, the kind of thing that looked respectable in a scriptorium and got a man dead above the treeline. He had no pack worth the name. No bedroll visible. No weapon she could see, which in Thornfell country was less innocence than suicide.

And yet. He'd found the shepherd's cut. He'd found the traverse. These weren't roads you stumbled onto; they were roads you were shown, or roads you read out of something, and Sera would have staked a month's take that he was reading them out of something sewn into that coat, because he kept touching his left side the way a man touches a wound, or a letter, or a secret.

*There you are,* she thought. *The scribe with the map.*

What interested her more was what he did next. At the far end of the traverse, where the path split—one branch dropping toward the river road and the empire's comfortable patrols, the other climbing toward the Stair and clan territory—he stood for a long moment, a small dark figure against a sky going bruise-colored, and then he turned north. Uphill. Toward the clans, the cold, and every danger the empire couldn't reach him from but the mountains could.

Nobody ran *toward* Thornfell. Debt-runners went south, over the border, into the lowland towns where a man could change his name and disappear into honest misery. Smugglers went east and west, along the money. Only two kinds of people climbed into the high country in autumn: fools and people following instructions.

She wondered which he was, and decided it probably didn't matter, because both kinds died at the same altitude.

The wind picked up, carrying the smell of snow from peaks that still held last winter in their shadows. Sera pulled her collar up and began to close the distance. Not yet. She wanted him tired, cold, and grateful—or failing grateful, at least too frozen to argue. There was a waystation cave below the Grey Stair, an old smuggler cache with a fire ring and three decades of her family's grease on its walls. If he made it that far on his own, he'd light a fire he didn't know how to hide, and the glow would guide her in like a lighthouse.

If he didn't make it, well. Then she'd have a shorter walk and a colder corpse, and the client's second half-payment would die with him, and she'd have to decide what to do about that when she got there.

She moved along the traverse in the last of the light, quick and quiet, a shadow with opinions.

* *

He lit the fire exactly as predicted, and badly—too much smoke, no windbreak, positioned so the glow spilled out of the cave mouth like an invitation to every bandit between here and the pass. Sera gave him twenty minutes to get comfortable, then another ten to get desperate, and then she stepped into the firelight and said, "Your wood's wet," and enjoyed, briefly and thoroughly, the way he came off the ground like a startled cat.

He scrambled back against the cave wall, knocking over the little tin cup he'd been heating water in, and grabbed for his belt—for a weapon, she assumed, finding none, and settling for gripping empty leather with a look on his face that was equal parts terror and fury at his own terror.

"Easy." She held up both hands, palms out, and crouched by the fire to demonstrate how little she feared him, which was true, and how harmless she was, which was not. "If I wanted your purse I'd have taken it on the traverse. If I wanted your blood I'd have done it while you were snoring. Which, incidentally—you snore. Loudly. You're lucky the nearest clan scout is deaf in one ear."

"How—" His voice cracked; he cleared his throat and tried again, and this time it came out steadier, which was interesting. Under the panic there was a spine. "How long have you been following me?"

"Since Vael's Crossing. Before that, since the Temple district, if we're being thorough, but I'll admit the earlier part was luck rather than skill." She fished a strip of dried venison from her pocket and held it out. He stared at it like it might be poisoned. "It's not poisoned. Poisoning you would be a waste of perfectly good venison."

"Who are you?"

"Sera Quill." She let the name sit there. It carried weight in certain rooms—dockside rooms, mostly, and a few courtrooms—and she watched to see if it landed anywhere in his. It didn't. Temple-bred indeed. "Smuggler, guide, occasional rescuer of idiots who climb into Thornfell wearing indoor clothes. And you're Gideon Pemberton, late of the Temple of Vessia, currently listed as missing, currently very much of interest to several people whose interest you do not want."

Something flickered across his face at his own name—the smallest tightening around the eyes—and Sera filed it away. He took the venison finally, because hunger beats caution eventually, and ate it in the joyless mechanical way of a man fueling a machine.

"I don't know what you've heard," he said.

"I've heard you're carrying a map."

The silence that followed had texture. She watched him decide, in real time, between denying it and asking how she knew, and she watched denial lose, because he was visibly not built for lying—it sat on him like a child's coat.

"Everyone keeps saying that," he said instead, which was cleverer than she expected, and she revised her estimate upward slightly. "Everyone seems very confident about things I've never confirmed."

"Mm. And are they wrong?"

"Are *you* wrong?"

"I asked first."

"You're the one holding a knife."

She glanced down at her own hip, where the knife in question sat in its sheath, entirely unrevealed. "I'm always holding a knife. It doesn't mean I'm using it. Look—" She shifted, settling cross-legged by the fire, making herself smaller and warmer-seeming than she felt. Outside, the wind had begun to moan through the Stair, and the temperature was falling the way mountain temperatures fall, all at once, like a trapdoor opening. "Here's the shape of your evening, scribe. Option one: you keep walking tonight, in the dark, on ice, toward clan territory where a lone stranger gets a spear before he gets a greeting. Option two: you stay here, alone, with a wet-wood fire and no bedroll, and hope the cold is feeling charitable. Option three: you talk to me, we share this fire, and in the morning I show you a route over the Stair that doesn't end with you as a cautionary tale."

"And what does option three cost me?"

Straight to it. Good. Men who haggled were men who could be dealt with; men who gave things away for free were men who got people killed. "Conversation," she said. "Company on the road. Nothing you'll miss."

"That's what worries me."

She laughed despite herself—a short, real bark of a laugh—and saw him register it with surprise, as though he'd expected something more reptilian. Fair. She'd cultivated reptilian.

"Smart and rude," she said. "You'll survive longer than I guessed."

* *

They talked, in the way of two people circling a fire and a subject neither would name directly.

He gave her pieces, carefully rationed, and she noted each one. He was a bonded scribe—she'd guessed that from the debt-tattoo peeking at his wrist when he reached for the water tin, the little chain-and-quill mark the Temple inked into its property. Fourteen years served, six remaining, or so he said, with a bitterness that suggested he'd counted the days individually. He'd left the city quickly. He wouldn't say why. He was going north because—here he paused, choosing words like a man crossing stepping stones—"because south is where they'll look."

"*They* being?"

"People with grey coats and patient voices."

Voss. Had to be. Sera had heard the name whispered in three ports that season—inquisitor, temple hound, a man who closed accounts. If Malachi Voss was behind this scribe, then the anonymous client and the Inquisitor were either the same person or enemies, and Sera genuinely could not decide which possibility she liked less.

In exchange, she gave him pieces of her own, also rationed. She told him about the passes, the clan moots, the way weather worked in the high country—useful truths, offered freely, the currency with which smugglers bought trust. She did not tell him about the contract. She did not tell him about the phrase *the scribe with the map*, or the half-payment sitting in a false-bottomed strongbox in Kess's hold, or the fact that somewhere behind them, likely moving faster than comfort allowed, there were other people being paid to find him.

Some honesty was a gift. Some honesty was a knife you handed someone handle-first and hoped.

"Why Thornfell?" she asked, somewhere past midnight, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and his eyes kept closing and forcing themselves open. "Really. Not the answer you rehearsed. The real one."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up. Then he said, without looking at her:

"My mother used to sing songs in a language I never learned. She said they were pilgrim hymns. When I was older I heard a clan trader singing in the market, and it was the same language, and my mother went white and never sang again." He turned his hands toward the coals. "I'm going north because I think the answers to questions I've been carrying my whole life are up here. And because a dying woman told me to go, and I didn't listen to her while she was alive, so I suppose I'm listening now."

Sera looked at him—at the hollows under his eyes, the ink stains worked deep into his knuckles, the way he held his left side like a man guarding a wound—and felt the uncomfortable click of recognition she'd been avoiding all evening.

She knew that look. She wore it herself, most days, under the swagger. It was the look of a person carrying something heavy who had finally, recently, discovered exactly how heavy it was—and who had decided, wrongly or rightly, that putting it down was no longer possible.

*We're both running toward something,* she thought. *That's the worst kind of company. The kind that understands.*

"Get some sleep," she said aloud, gruffly, because sentiment was a leak in a hull and she'd patched hers years ago. "I'll take first watch. We cross the Stair at first light, and I mean first—after midmorning the ice sweats and people die."

He lay down on bare stone without complaint, curled around whatever was sewn into his coat like a man curling around a broken rib, and within minutes his breathing slowed into that telltale rumble.

Sera sat by the fire and did arithmetic.

The client wanted the map. The scribe wanted answers. Voss, presumably, wanted everything. And she, Sera Quill, wanted her brother back—Tamsin, taken by the Temple at nine years old, twelve years gone now, alive or not alive, a question she'd sold half her soul trying to buy. The anonymous client had promised her Tamsin's location. Promises from anonymous clients were worth roughly the paper they weren't written on, but hope didn't require collateral.

So: she could take the map tonight, while he slept. Cut it from his coat, be over the pass and gone before dawn, collect the second payment, force the client to make good on Tamsin. Clean. Efficient. Exactly what she'd been hired to do.

She looked at the sleeping man, at the firelight moving on his exhausted face, and she thought about a mother who'd gone white and silent, and a dying woman's last instruction, and the particular stubbornness of a man who walked toward the thing that frightened him.

Then she thought about what happened to hired knives who delivered early and asked questions later, and about the fact that a map without its bearer might be worthless—*the scribe is incidental*, the client had said, and clients who lied about small things lied about large ones—and she told herself that was the reason, the whole reason, the only reason, why she leaned back against the cave wall and kept watch over him until morning.

The wind rose through the Grey Stair, keening like something mournful and old.

And far below them, on the river road, torches moved—three of them, then five, riding north at a pace no honest traveler kept.

Sera saw them an hour before dawn, and did not wake the scribe, and did not sleep at all.
