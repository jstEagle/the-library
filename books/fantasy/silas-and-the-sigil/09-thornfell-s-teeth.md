## 9. Thornfell's Teeth

The mountain had been watching them for two days. Gideon could feel it the way a debtor feels a creditor's eyes—patient, certain of payment.

He said nothing about it to Sera Quill. He'd said almost nothing to her since she'd appeared on the trail three days ago, stepping out from behind a granite spur as if she'd grown there, offering partnership in a voice that made refusal feel like an invitation to be robbed later. He'd refused. She'd followed anyway.

"You're doing the thing again," she called from behind him.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you walk like you're being chased and pretend you're not." Her boots crunched closer on the frozen scree. "You've been staring at that ridgeline for half an hour. The one with all the snow sitting on it like a drunk leaning over a well."

"I'm looking at the path."

"You're looking at the snow above the path. There's a difference, scribe, and the difference is whether you live to see tomorrow." She came up beside him, breath steaming, and he hated how easily she read terrain the way he read ledgers. "We go through the Narrows before midday or we don't go through at all. Sun softens the pack by afternoon. Afternoon is when mountains shrug."

Gideon looked up at the shoulder of Thornfell looming over the pass—the mountain whose name was stitched into every rumor he'd ever copied for the Temple, always in the same hushed register reserved for plague ships and heretics. *Thornfell's teeth*, the clerks called the jagged skyline. Nobody explained why. Nobody in the Temple ever explained anything; they only priced it.

"Fine," he said. "Before midday."

She grinned, quick and sharp. "See? You can be taught."

* * *

Sera didn't trust him. That was the first thing she'd decided about Gideon Pemberton, back when she'd watched him count coins in a dockside tavern with the careful hands of a man who'd never owned enough to lose any. The second thing she'd decided was that he was carrying something heavy, because men carrying something heavy walked exactly the way he walked: shoulders squared against a weight nobody else could see, eyes flicking to exits, hands drifting toward whatever they'd hidden.

The third thing she'd decided was that she'd follow him off the edge of the world if she had to, because her client's coin was real and her brother's location was worth more than her comfort.

What she hadn't decided yet was whether she liked him.

The morning made the question academic. They entered the Narrows an hour after dawn, a slot of grey stone barely wide enough for two abreast, walls rising sheer on either side until the sky became a ribbon. Sera went first—not chivalry, just habit. In tight places you wanted your own hands choosing what to touch.

Halfway through, the mountain spoke.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. A sound like a giant clearing its throat somewhere above them, a low grinding groan that Sera felt in her molars more than heard. She stopped. Held up a fist. Behind her, Gideon froze with the obedience of a man used to taking orders.

Then the ribbon of sky went white.

"RUN!" She shoved him forward, both hands between his shoulder blades, and they ran—or stumbled, which is what running becomes when the ground itself starts moving. The roar behind them swelled from throat-clearing to avalanche, a freight of snow and shattered rock coming down the chute like the wrath of some god who'd finally found the receipt for their sins.

The light died. The world turned to thunder.

Sera saw it a heartbeat before it hit—a cleft in the left wall, a seam of darkness maybe four feet deep, half-hidden behind a leaning slab. She grabbed Gideon's collar, hauled him sideways, and jammed them both into the crack as the white wall arrived.

It didn't hit so much as *swallow*. Snow packed into the cleft around them, driving the air out of her lungs, bending her against the stone, filling her mouth, her ears, the space between thought and panic. For a long moment there was no world at all—only pressure and cold and the animal certainty that she was going to die packed inside a mountain like a letter no one would open.

Then the roar passed overhead, diminished, and stopped.

Silence. Total, ringing silence, the kind that has weight.

Sera spat snow and tried to breathe. Tried to move. Couldn't. The slide had packed them in tight, her back to Gideon's chest, both of them entombed to the shoulders in a plug of snow harder than mortar. Above her head, maybe a handspan of clear air remained between the slab and the drift.

"Gideon." Her voice came out muffled, absurdly calm. "Talk to me. Are you breathing?"

A pause. Then, close to her ear, ragged: "Yes."

"Good. Don't stop. It's a bad habit to start."

* * *

They dug themselves out in shifts, which took an hour and cost Gideon most of his composure. Sera worked like someone who'd been buried before—he caught the flat efficiency of her movements, the way she tested each armful of snow before throwing it, the way she kept talking the whole time, low and constant, not chatter but ballast. *Keep the mind busy and the body follows.* He filed the observation away the way he filed everything, in the vast silent ledger of his memory, and wondered how many graves she'd climbed out of to learn it.

When they broke free into the blinding afternoon, the Narrows were gone. The pass they'd been walking toward was a smooth white rampart fifty feet high, featureless, indifferent. Their route—*his* route, the smuggler's path he'd memorized from a dead courier's map and three nights of fevered study—was buried under a hundred tons of Thornfell's opinion.

Gideon stood in the churned snow and felt something crack quietly inside him. Not despair. Something worse: the loss of the plan. He'd had a plan. The plan had been the only thing between him and the screaming dark that had lived in his chest since Wren died in his doorway. Now the plan was under the mountain.

"Well," Sera said, dusting herself off. "That's inconvenient."

"That's—" His voice came out strange. He tried again. "That was the only way through."

"No." She was already scanning the walls, reading the rock the way he read script. "That was the fast way through. Mountains have more than one door. They just don't label them." She pointed up and left, to a shelf of wind-scoured stone angling along the canyon wall. "Old shepherd track. Goes up and over the shoulder. Cold, exposed, slow. But it goes."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been over worse with less." She glanced back at him, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes—something older than the grin she usually wore like armor. "You coming, scribe? Or are you going to stand there negotiating with the snow?"

He came.

* * *

The shepherd track nearly killed them anyway.

By dusk the wind had risen, funneling down from the high peaks with a shriek that made conversation impossible, and the temperature fell the way a blade falls. Gideon's fingers stopped answering him somewhere around the fourth hour. His thoughts went soft and warm and stupid, and he found himself thinking about the Temple scriptorium, about the smell of ink, about how cold the stone floors were even in summer and how he'd complained about it once and been whipped for insolence—and how, gods help him, he'd give anything to be kneeling on those floors right now instead of dying on this one.

He didn't notice himself stopping.

A hand closed on his collar and yanked. Pain flared across his cheek—Sera had slapped him, hard, twice.

"Don't," she shouted over the wind. "Don't you dare sit down. Sit down here and you're a statue by midnight."

"I was just—"

"You were just dying. I've seen it. I've *done* it." She shrugged out of her pack, pulled a coil of rope, and tied one end around his waist with brutal efficiency, knotting it to her own harness. "From here on, you don't walk alone. You fall, I fall. I fall, you're too heavy to drag, so don't."

They climbed. The track narrowed to nothing in places, a ledge of ice-glazed stone with a drop on the left that the darkness made infinite. Twice Gideon's boots skated and the rope snapped taut and Sera's grip held—her hands small and scarred and impossibly strong, hauling him back against the rock, holding him there while his heart tried to exit through his ribs.

At the worst of it, near the top of the shoulder, the ledge simply ended—a gap of six feet where the track had collapsed into the void, wind screaming through it. Sera looked at the gap, then at him, then did something insane: she backed up, ran, and jumped, landing on the far side in a spray of ice, then paid out rope and braced herself.

"Come on!"

"I can't—"

"You can. Three steps and jump. Your legs know how; it's your head that's arguing." Her voice dropped, and through the wind he heard something underneath it—not mockery, not command. Almost gentleness. "I've got the rope, Gideon. I'm not letting go. Jump."

He jumped.

He didn't remember landing. He remembered her arms, catching him past the rope's end, both of them slamming into the frozen rock in a tangle of limbs and breath, her heartbeat hammering against his own through two layers of wool. For a moment neither of them moved. The wind screamed past above them like it had lost interest in killing them tonight.

"You're heavier than you look," she said into his shoulder.

"You're stronger than you look."

"Everyone is. That's the trick nobody tells you." She pulled back, and in the blue dark he saw her face properly for the first time since the avalanche—frost-burned, exhausted, and wearing an expression he'd never seen on her: surprise. As if she'd expected to let go of the rope.

* * *

They found shelter an hour later—a hollow beneath an overhang, deep enough to sit, shallow enough to hate. Sera built a fire from scrub pine and a fire-striker she produced from somewhere in her coat, and they ate hard bread and smoked fish in a silence that had changed texture. It wasn't hostile anymore. It was tired.

Gideon stared into the flames and felt the day settling onto him like snow. Every part of him hurt. And underneath the hurt, unwelcome as a debt collector, sat a realization he'd been dodging for three days:

He would be dead. Twice over dead—buried in the Narrows, scattered on the ledge—if not for her.

"You keep a ledger," Sera said suddenly.

He looked up. "What?"

"In your head. I've watched you do it. Everything gets weighed. Everything gets written down." She poked the fire. "So write this down: you owe me. Two lives' worth. I'm not calling the debt tonight. But I keep accounts too, scribe, and mine don't have a forgiveness column."

"I know," he said quietly. "Neither does mine."

Something flickered across her face—satisfaction, or its cousin. She leaned back against the stone. "Your turn to talk. Avalanche rules. Whoever nearly dies tells one true thing."

"That's not a rule."

"It is now. I invented it." She waited. When he said nothing, she sighed. "Fine. I'll go first. One true thing: I'm not following you for the view. Someone's paying me to find you, and I intend to collect. There. Now you know I'm a liar and a mercenary, and you still ate my fish." She tilted her head. "Your turn."

Gideon looked at the fire. He could give her a true thing that was also a wall—a truth shaped like misdirection. He'd been building those his whole life. *I'm running from the Temple.* True. Safe. Revealed nothing.

But she'd held the rope.

"My sister died in my doorway," he heard himself say. "Three weeks ago. I didn't know I had a sister until that night." The words came out flat, ledger-flat, but saying them aloud cracked something loose in his chest that he hadn't known was sealed. "Everything I'm doing is because of what she gave me. And I don't fully understand it yet. That's the truth."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the wind changed its mind about something.

"You said you didn't know you had a sister," she said slowly. "Which means somebody hid her from you. Which means your family's got secrets with edges." Her eyes had gone sharp again, the merchant's gleam, the tracker's focus. "And whatever she gave you—it's why an empire's worth of people want to know where you're walking."

He said nothing. He'd learned silence young, and it had kept him alive longer than courage ever had.

But Sera Quill didn't need answers. He was beginning to understand that about her. She needed only fragments, and she assembled the rest the way he assembled meaning from a column of figures—from pattern, from what was missing.

"A courier died on the north road," she said, almost to herself. "Temple riders asking questions in every village south of the river. An Inquisitor in grey who pays better than he threatens. And a scribe who lies badly but runs well, carrying something sewn into his coat—I felt the stitching when I caught you on the ledge, don't look surprised, I've frisked better men than you." She smiled, thin and unbothered. "I don't need you to tell me, Gideon. I just need you to keep walking. Whatever it is, it's going to Thornfell. And everything I want is on Thornfell too."

The fire burned low. Gideon pulled his coat tighter around the shape hidden against his ribs and told himself she knew nothing for certain.

But as he drifted into an exhausted sleep, her last words followed him down like a bell tolling:

"Get some rest, scribe. Tomorrow we cross into clan territory—and believe me, that's a whole different kind of teeth."
