## 10. The Village That Pays Both Sides

Hollowmere announced itself the way a liar announces himself: with warmth.

We came down out of the tree line at dusk, and there it was in the valley's cup—thirty-odd houses with smoke rising straight from their chimneys because there was no wind to argue with, a mill pond flat as poured pewter, and lamps already lit in windows that faced the road. A village that wanted to be seen. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing I distrusted. Real hiding places don't light their lamps.

Sera stopped beside me on the last stretch of scree and looked down at it with the expression of a woman counting exits.

"Hollowmere," she said. "They'll sell us bread, beds, and silence, in that order, and they'll charge fair for all three."

"You've been here before."

"Everyone's been here before. That's the point of it." She nudged her horse forward. "Empire garrison comes through twice a year, collects its tax, drinks its ale, leaves. Clan riders come through maybe once a season, same thing. The village pays both and belongs to neither. It's not neutral, scribe. Neutral means you'd fight for either side if pushed. This place just wants everyone to go away."

I followed her down, and my body was grateful for the descent even as my mind kept turning over what she'd said, because it sounded like a description of me. Pay both sides. Belong to none. Want everyone to go away.

Three days since the avalanche. Two since she'd pulled me out from under a slab of snow the size of a temple roof and I'd coughed up half a mountain and thanked her by telling her nothing at all. She hadn't asked again. That frightened me more than asking would have. Sera Quill didn't stop asking questions; she just filed them somewhere sharp until they were useful.

The map rode against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my coat where I'd stitched it myself by lamplight in a waystation stable, using a curved needle and the steadiest hands I owned. My hands had been steady for twenty years. Copying ledgers does that. You learn to write numbers that belong to other people's sins without your fingers shaking.

The village took us in without ceremony. A boy ran ahead shouting that travelers were coming, and by the time we reached the well, a broad man with a miller's forearms stood waiting, wiping flour from his hands as though he'd been interrupted at something ordinary. He looked at Sera first—everyone did—and then at me, and his gaze snagged on my coat the way a fishhook snags on a sleeve.

"Room's four coppers. Stabling's two. Supper's included if you eat what we eat." He said it like a prayer he'd memorized. "No questions asked, no answers given."

"We'll want supplies for the high pass," Sera said. "Dried meat, hard cheese, rope if it's honest, lamp oil. And someone who knows the Thornfell road."

The miller's eyes went flatter. "Thornfell's not a road this time of year. It's a grave with weather."

"Then we'll want someone who knows the grave."

He chewed on that. Behind him, doors had opened along the lane—not wide, just a hand's width each, the village watching through its cracks the way I'd watched the Temple through mine for two decades. I knew that posture. I knew it so well it hurt.

"We'll talk terms after supper," the miller said finally. "Maren keeps the guest house. Tell her Tam sent you." A pause. "Don't tell her anything else."

* * *

Maren's guest house smelled of tallow and dried thyme, and Maren herself was perhaps seventy, small and bent like a question mark, with eyes that had missed nothing in seventy years and intended to miss nothing more. She fed us barley stew and dark bread and said perhaps forty words total, none of them wasted.

It was after supper, while Sera went to see to the horses and the terms with Tam, that things turned.

I'd spread nothing out. I want that understood—I had learned that much. But when I pulled off my coat to hang it by the fire, the stitching I'd done in the stable pulled loose at one corner, and a sliver of oilcloth showed at the hem like a wound opening. Just a sliver. An inch of waxed linen and the edge of a line drawn in ink that wasn't ink.

Maren set down the bowl she was scrubbing.

She didn't lunge. She didn't gasp. She crossed the room at the speed of old women everywhere, which is to say slowly and unstoppably, and she stood over me where I sat, and she held out her hand.

"Show me," she said.

"I don't know what you—"

"Boy." Her voice wasn't unkind. That was somehow worse. "I was reading edge-script before your mother drew breath. Whatever you're carrying, you've been carrying it wrong, and it's going to get you killed in a place where dying is the merciful option. Show me."

Every instinct I owned—the ones that had kept me invisible for twenty years, head down, pen moving, debts counted—screamed at me to deny, deflect, smile the small apologetic smile of a man who is nobody. I opened my mouth to do exactly that.

And then I thought about Wren bleeding out on my doorstep. About Voss's grey gloves folded on the table between us. About the way the map had felt in my hands, warm when everything around it was cold.

I unpicked the stitches.

* * *

She made me clear the table first. Made me bank the fire low. Made me check the shutters, which told me how serious she was, because no one checks shutters for a conversation about cartography.

Then she laid the map open under the last of the light and went still.

I watched her read it the way I watch scholars read—except Maren didn't read left to right or top to bottom. Her finger wandered the margins, the edges, the places I'd dismissed as decoration: the little hooked marks running along the border like bird tracks, the knotwork that framed the mountains, the tiny figures I'd taken for ornament. Her lips moved. Her color changed.

By the time her finger reached the lower border, Maren had gone the grey-white of old snow.

"Mistress—" I started.

"Where did you get this?" Not looking up. Voice very level, the way voices go when they're holding something heavy above deep water.

"A courier gave it to me. He died doing it."

"Did he now." She sat back. Her hands, I noticed, were trembling, and she pressed them flat against the table to stop them, an old habit fighting an older fear. "And do you know what you're carrying?"

"The location of something called the Sigil of Auren. I know the Temple wants it. I know the clans want it. I know men have died for it, including possibly the man who gave it to me."

"That's the shape of it," she agreed. "That's the shadow of it. Now let me tell you about the thing itself." She tapped the border marks, one by one, her nail clicking on the oilcloth. "These aren't decorations. These are warnings. Written by the last people who understood what sleeps under Thornfell, written so plainly that even a child of the keeping lines couldn't mistake them—if any child of the keeping lines still lived to read them."

My heart did something complicated. "Keeping lines."

Her eyes came up to mine then, and whatever she saw in my face made her go quieter. "You didn't know. No—you wouldn't, would you. They scattered your people so thoroughly that the empire forgot why it scattered you." She exhaled through her nose. "The Sigil isn't treasure, boy. It isn't a weapon either, whatever the Temple's priests whisper and whatever the clan hotheads hope. It's a lock. A seal on something that ended a war once—a war that ate a generation whole, before the empire was an empire, back when the clans and the lowlands bled each other white over these same mountains. The Sigil closed the wound. And a seal, boy, doesn't renew itself."

"What renews it?"

"Blood." She said it simply, the way you'd say rain. "Willing blood, of the right line. Every hundred years, the keepers renewed it, generation on generation, and the mountains stayed quiet and the war stayed dead. Then the empire came, and burned the shrines, and scattered the keeper families across the lowlands like seed thrown on stone, and for a hundred years the seal has been thinning. Thinning. Thinning." Her nail traced the border to a single mark near the map's corner—a spiral, broken. "This one here says the door is nearly open. And every hunger in the world is walking toward it, thinking it's a door to power, when it's a door to the thing that made the old war look like a tavern brawl."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, Sera laughed at something Tam said, quick and bright, a sound like glass.

"Burn it," Maren said.

I blinked. "What?"

"Burn it. Tonight. Burn the map, burn the locket if you have one, take the long road south, find a town with no name and a trade with no history, and live." She leaned forward and gripped my wrist, and her grip was stronger than her frame promised. "Because I'll tell you what happens otherwise. The Temple finds you, and they take the map and kill you slow. The clans find you, and they take the map and use you up and call it honor. Or you reach the shrine, and you open the door, and you learn what the seal asks of the blood that opens it—and by then it will be too late to be anything but what the map says you are."

"And what does the map say I am?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the laugh again, closer—Sera coming back up the path, boots on frozen mud.

"It doesn't say," Maren said softly. "That's the cruelty of it. It only shows the door. What walks through it has always decided for itself." She released my wrist. "Burn it. While you still can."

* * *

I didn't burn it.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the one debt I've never managed to escape. I sat by Maren's banked fire with the map in my lap and the knife in my hand that could have cut it into confetti, and I thought about burning, and I didn't.

Not because I doubted her. God help me, I believed every word. The dread she'd planted in me had gone down like a root, deep and cold, and I could feel it already feeding on something in my chest. But beneath the dread, underneath it, coiled around it like ivy around a dead tree, was another feeling entirely, and it took me until the small hours to name it.

Purpose.

Twenty years I had copied other people's accounts. Twenty years of ink under my fingernails and sins in my ledger that weren't mine, counting down a debt-bond like a prisoner scratching days into a wall. And here, at last, was a thing that was mine. A dead woman's trust pressed into my hands. A door only I could open—or so the old woman's face had suggested, in the moment before she caught herself. A warning written for a child of the keeping lines, and I was the only such child left standing in the world, apparently, holding the only map.

Wren had carried this through storms and checkpoints and whatever wounds finally killed her, and she had carried it *to me*. Not to a general. Not to a priest. To a scribe in a borrowed room who owed the Temple nine years. She had known something about me that I was only beginning to suspect, and she had bet her life on it, and lost, and passed the bet to me with her dying hands.

You can't burn that. I tried to explain it to myself in the dark and failed, and finally stopped trying. Some inheritances aren't property. They're instructions.

Near dawn, Maren found me still awake. She looked at the map, intact, sewn back into my coat with better stitches than my own—she must have done it while I slept, or pretended to—and she didn't rage. She just sighed, the sigh of a woman who had seen this exact stubbornness before, decades ago, on faces she'd loved.

"Your grandmother had that same look," she said. "Sit. I'll make tea. If you're going to die on Thornfell, you'll at least die warm, and you'll hear the rest of what I know—because I only told you the half, boy, and the half I kept back is the part that matters most."

Outside the window, far to the north, past the black teeth of the foothills, thunder rolled where there were no clouds.

Or something that sounded like thunder.

Maren's hands went still over the kettle, and she whispered a word in a language I almost—almost—recognized from a lullaby I'd spent thirty years forgetting.
