## 11. Voss Descends

The rain followed Voss down from the pass like a debt collector.

It came in sheets off the Thornfell foothills, turning the road to porridge and the world to grey, and Malachi Voss rode through it without complaint because complaint was a luxury and he had surrendered every luxury he owned twenty-two years ago, on a muddy field at Cael's Ford, when he'd knelt in the blood of his friends and sworn that no artifact, no prophecy, and no god's leftover trinket would ever again decide who lived and who died.

Hollowmere announced itself the way dying things announce themselves: with smoke. Cookfire smoke, hearth smoke, the thin blue exhalation of forty chimneys huddled against the weather. A village of maybe two hundred souls wedged into the crook of a river valley where empire road met clan trail, which meant it had spent three generations learning exactly how much loyalty it could sell to each side without either side burning it down.

Voss intended to find out how much was left.

He rode at the head of twelve men, six in temple grey, six in imperial black, and he let them be seen from a long way off. Fear traveled faster than horses. By the time they reached the village square, the streets were empty except for dogs and one old man sweeping water out of a doorway with the dogged persistence of someone who had decided that if the world ended, he would die mid-chore.

The headman met them under the eave of the meeting house. He was a broad, ruddy man named Aldous Crane, with the careful eyes of a merchant and the hands of a farmer, and he bowed the precise degree required by law—deep enough for an officer of the Inquisition, shallow enough not to insult the clan scouts who would hear about it by nightfall.

"Inquisitor." His voice was warm as fresh bread. "We're honored. We hadn't word of your coming."

"No," Voss said. "You wouldn't have."

He dismounted himself, which surprised his men; Inquisitors did not dismount for villages. But Voss had learned long ago that you saw more of a place on your feet, and people told you different truths when you stood among them instead of above them. He walked past Crane into the square, unhurried, letting the rain run off his hat brim, reading Hollowmere the way he read documents.

A shrine to Vessia with a fresh coat of whitewash over older paint—the older paint showing through in the shape of antlers. Clan knotwork carved into the lintels of half the houses, worn nearly smooth but not quite. A trading post with two ledgers visible through its window: one bound in imperial black, one in undyed homespun. And there, nailed to the well post, a notice offering a bounty for information regarding unauthorized travel on the north road, printed four years ago, sun-bleached, ignored.

"You're a border village," Voss said pleasantly.

"We serve the empire," Crane said.

"I didn't ask what you serve. I said you're a border village. You pay taxes here and tithes there, you fly the sun-disc on festival days and light the high cairn on clan ones, and everyone pretends this is normal because the alternative is a war nobody can afford." He turned. Rain dripped from his jaw. "I'm not here to punish arithmetic, Headman. I'm here about a courier."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes. Small. Fast. Buried like a coin in sand.

"Courier, Inquisitor?"

"A woman. Young. Dark hair, cropped short. She'd have been riding hard, riding hurt, perhaps. On the north road, within the last ten days." Voss watched him the way a surgeon watches a wound. "She may have passed through. She may have stopped. Someone may have given her bread, or a horse, or directions to a path the empire doesn't maintain."

"Travelers pass through all the time—"

"They do." Voss smiled, and it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Show me your guest ledger."

* * *

The ledger was a fiction, and both of them knew it. Names in a careful clerk's hand, dates, purposes of visit. Grain merchants. A tinker. Two pilgrims bound for the lowland shrines. Voss read it standing up, page by page, while Crane watched him with the rigid calm of a man watching a fire approach his granary.

"Your hand?" Voss asked.

"My daughter's. She keeps the books since her mother passed."

"She writes beautifully." He set the ledger down. "Now show me the other one."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Voss counted them. He counted everything; it was a habit from his soldiering years, counting seconds between lightning and thunder, between order and impact, between the moment a man decided to lie and the moment the lie came out of him.

"I don't take your meaning."

"The second ledger. The one in the trading post window. Homespun binding. The one your merchants keep for the clan caravans, because the clans won't trade on paper that bears the sun-disc." He tapped the well post, the bleached bounty notice. "You've been running two sets of books since before your daughter could hold a pen, Headman. I don't care. Do you understand me? The empire cannot afford to care. What I care about is one name in one of those books, or one bed in one of your back rooms, or one memory in one of your heads. A courier. Ten days ago. Riding north."

Crane's throat worked. Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as scripture.

"There was a woman," Crane said finally. "Three nights past. Soaked through, half-dead on her horse. My daughter fed her. She wouldn't give a name. Paid in old coin—clan mint, pre-annexation. Took the mountain trail at dawn, toward the Ellow waystation."

Voss felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped down a well. *Ellow.* The frozen shrine. It was on none of the imperial maps worth having, which meant it was on all the maps worth stealing.

"Did she say what she carried?"

"No. She barely spoke. But—" Crane hesitated, and Voss knew, with the certainty of a man who had heard ten thousand hesitations, that what came next was true. "But she was bleeding. From under the arm. And she kept her left hand pressed to her ribs the whole time, like she was holding something against her body. Like a mother holds a child."

Like a courier holds a map.

Voss thanked him. Genuinely. That was the thing his men never understood about him—he was grateful, sincerely grateful, to every person who gave him truth, even when he intended to burn their world down around the giving of it. Truth was the only currency he had never learned to counterfeit.

"One more matter," he said. "Where is Tam Ressler?"

* * *

They found the boy in the stable loft, and he ran, because he was seventeen and frightened and stupid, and fear makes animals of us all.

Two of Voss's blackcoats brought him down before he cleared the paddock fence. They marched him into the meeting house dripping mud, wrists bound, and set him on a bench before the hearth, and the whole village seemed to compress into that room—Crane, the daughter with her ink-stained fingers, a dozen others drawn in by the gravity of uniformed men, standing at the walls with the particular stillness of people hoping to be furniture.

Tam Ressler had sold information before. Voss knew it the way he knew most things—not from confession but from pattern. A boy from a border village, father dead in the levy wars, mother sick, no trade but strong legs and a fast mouth. Somebody had paid him once to watch the north road and count riders. Probably a clan scout. Possibly something worse. And three nights ago, when a bleeding stranger stumbled into Hollowmere asking after the mountain trail, Tam had done what he always did: he'd run to whoever paid him and sold what he'd seen.

Except this time, the buyer hadn't been a clan scout.

This time, one of Voss's own outriders had found the boy's message cached in a hollow oak—a scrap of parchment in a child's handwriting: *Woman, dark hair, wounded, took Ellow trail at dawn, alone.*

"Tell me who you sent that to," Voss said quietly.

Tam's eyes skittered around the room, looking for a door that wasn't there. "I don't know what you—"

"Boy." Voss crouched so they were eye to eye. Up close, the kid smelled of hay and terror-sweat. "I am going to tell you exactly how this evening goes, so that you can spend your last minutes honestly, which is more than most people get. You sold word of that courier to someone who is not the empire and not the clans. That person is now racing my riders to a shrine in the high passes, carrying knowledge that could unmake the peace your village has spent sixty years balancing on its nose. Every death that follows climbs, in part, through you. Now. Who paid you?"

"I never got a name." The words spilled out of him like water through a cracked dam. "A drop point, that's all. Under the split stone by the mill race. Coin and instructions, coin and instructions, never a face, never a name, I swear it on my mother's grave—"

"What instructions?"

"To watch the road. To write down strangers heading for the passes. Especially—" the boy swallowed, "—especially scribes. Temple scribes. Anyone with bonded marks on their wrists."

The room went very quiet. Voss heard the rain on the roof, the fire ticking, his own heartbeat, slow and even as a drum cadence.

Someone was hunting a scribe. Not just the courier—the courier was dead, he was certain of it now, certain with the cold certainty that lived in his gut below all thought. Someone wanted the next link in whatever chain that woman had died carrying. And they knew enough about the Temple to know that its scribes were its softest tissue: overworked, underfed, invisible, moving through the empire like dust motes.

Gideon Pemberton surfaced in his mind, unbidden. The little ledger-clerk with ink to the knuckles and a liar's steady eyes. *No, Inquisitor. No courier came to my door.*

Voss had interrogated four thousand people in his career. He had a memory like a warehouse and he catalogued everything, and there had been something in the scribe's breathing—not the words, the words had been perfect—that sat wrong, the way a single misaligned brick sits wrong in an otherwise sound wall.

"Stand him up," Voss said.

The blackcoats hauled Tam Ressler to his feet. The boy's legs barely worked. "I told you everything," he babbled. "I told you everything, I swear, I'll watch the roads for you, I'll—"

"Yes," Voss said. "That's the problem."

He drew his sword.

He did it slowly, deliberately, so that every soul in the room understood what was happening while there was still time to understand it. That was the point. That had always been the point, since Cael's Ford, since the day he'd learned that mercy, applied at scale, was just another word for negligence. An empire was a dam holding back a flood of everything people would do to each other if the dam failed, and a dam is not maintained by kindness. It is maintained by weight. By the visible, terrible, immovable weight of consequence.

"This boy sold the movements of travelers to an unknown party," Voss said, in the voice he used for verdicts, pitched to reach the back wall and every ear pressed near it. "Some of you knew. Some of you suspected. All of you will remember tonight. The empire does not require your love, Hollowmere. It requires your clarity. Know whose side holds your life. Because the people he served do not offer you clarity. They offer you coin until the day they need you dead."

"Inquisitor—" Crane started forward, and two blackcoats caught his arms, and his daughter screamed, and Tam Ressler was crying now, seventeen years old and crying, saying *please, please, my mother—*

"My men will see your mother provided for," Voss said to him, and meant it, and hated that he meant it, and did it anyway. "Winter coal. A pension. The empire remembers its debts even when its subjects forget theirs."

He killed the boy cleanly, one stroke, the way he'd been taught by a sergeant who died at Cael's Ford holding a line that should have broken. The body sagged between the blackcoats. They laid it on the bench with a gentleness that surprised the villagers, Voss could see it surprise them, because they expected monsters and got instead tired men who folded the dead's hands over his chest.

"Leave the coin for the mother," Voss said. "And leave the body where the clan scouts will find it. Let them know we know."

Then he walked out into the rain, and behind him he heard the wailing start, and he let it follow him into the dark like a second storm.

* * *

His tent went up at the edge of the square, and his lieutenants gathered inside it by lamplight: Captain Dree with her frostbitten ears, Brother Ossian with his ink and his ledgers, the quiet young acolyte Ferren who took notes and asked no questions, which Voss appreciated more than the boy would ever know.

"Ellow waystation," Voss said. "Dree, take six riders and the mule train. The pass will be snowed in within the fortnight—go now, go heavy, go prepared to winter if you must. Seal the waystation. Nothing crosses it in either direction without your countersign."

"And if our quarry's already past it?"

"Then you hold the far end of the gorge and send a bird every six hours." He turned to Ossian. "The archives. Everything on the Sigil of Auren. Not the public canon—I've read the canticles, they're nursery rhymes. The restricted folios. The campaign records from the annexation. What did the legions actually find in the mountains?"

Ossian's pen paused. "Inquisitor, the restricted folios are sealed by—"

"By the Conclave, yes. I am the Conclave's authority in this province, and I am telling you to break the seal." He held the old archivist's gaze. "You've read them already, haven't you? Years ago. Everyone with access reads them and then spends the rest of their careers pretending they haven't. Tell me what they say."

Ossian set down his pen. For a long moment the only sound was the rain hammering the canvas.

"They say the war didn't end," the old man said quietly. "It was *stopped*. There's a difference. The legion accounts describe a light on the Heartstone plateau that could be seen from thirty miles. Whole clans laying down arms mid-battle. And a line in General Corvane's private journal, written the night after: *'Whatever sleeps under that mountain, we did not defeat it. We merely inherited the debt of its keeping.'*"

"And the Conclave's position?"

"The Conclave's position is that the Sigil is a relic of pagan superstition, dangerous only insofar as it inspires rebellion." Ossian's voice was flat. "The restricted folios suggest otherwise. They suggest the annexation itself—the binding of the clans, the destruction of the shrines—was undertaken specifically to prevent anyone from ever reaching the Heartstone again. We didn't conquer the mountains, Inquisitor. We built a wall around a door."

Voss looked at his hands. Soldier's hands, scribe's calluses over the old sword calluses. Twenty-two years ago he had buried six friends in the mud at Cael's Ford, and the official account called it a skirmish, and the unofficial account—which lived only in his skull—called it what it was: the first skirmish of a war that the old powers had been keeping on a leash, and that leash was fraying.

People thought he hunted the Sigil out of ambition. Let them. Ambition was a language everyone spoke and no one feared.

But the truth was simpler and colder: Voss had seen what the mountains did to empires. He had carried the bodies. And if some artifact under the ice could wake the clans, could arm them with something older and stranger than grievance, then the peace would shatter—not into negotiation, not into treaty, but into a generation of slaughter that would make Cael's Ford look like a tavern brawl. Tens of thousands dead. Villages like Hollowmere burned to their foundations. Children like Tam Ressler dead in ditches by the ten thousand.

The Sigil could not be found. Could not be used. Could not be studied, negotiated with, or understood. It could only be destroyed—and if it could not be destroyed, then the door beneath the mountain must stay shut forever, and everyone who knew the way must be silenced, gently where possible and otherwise where necessary.

That was not cruelty. That was arithmetic. Someone had to do it, and everyone else was too squeamish, and so it fell to him, and he accepted the burden the way he accepted all burdens: silently, completely, and without the cowardice of doubt.

"Anything else, Inquisitor?" Dree asked.

"One thing." He picked up the outrider's report again, the boy's childish scrawl. *Especially scribes. Anyone with bonded marks.* "Send word ahead to every garrison between here and the city. I want the current whereabouts of every indentured scribe in the northern district. Names, postings, debts outstanding."

Ferren looked up. "Scribes, sir?"

"A courier died carrying something important," Voss said, rolling the boy's note closed. "Couriers are hired. Someone hired her. And whoever it was chose a route through the Temple's own territory, which means either great arrogance or great familiarity." He fed the note to the lamp flame and watched it curl. "Find me every scribe in the north, and find me which of them has recently begun lying."

Outside, the rain was easing. Somewhere north, in the white silence of the high passes, a dead woman's cargo was moving uphill on the shoulders of a man with ink under his fingernails—and Voss intended to be waiting at the top when he arrived.
