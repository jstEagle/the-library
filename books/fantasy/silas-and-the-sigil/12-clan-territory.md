## 12. Clan Territory

The border was not a line. That was the first thing Sera Quill understood about clan territory, standing on a granite shelf with the wind pulling at her braid and the whole grey spine of the Thornfells rising ahead of them like the back of something sleeping.

Empires drew lines. Empires loved lines—surveyed them, fenced them, carved them into stone markers and tax rolls and the margins of maps. The clans had no use for any of it. Their border was a slope where the pines changed shape, a river that ran colder than rivers should run, a smell in the air like iron and old snow. You knew you'd crossed because the land itself began to watch you.

She felt it now, the way she'd felt it once before, eleven years ago and a hundred mistakes younger: the prickle between her shoulder blades, the sense of eyes that belonged to no single face.

"You feel that too," Gideon said behind her. Not a question.

"Feel what?" She kept walking. "The cold? The altitude making you stupid?"

"The watching."

Sera didn't answer. Denying it would only tell him how much it bothered her, and admitting it would tell him more than that. So she said nothing and climbed, and let the silence do its work, which was most of what silence was ever good for.

They had been in the high country four days. Four days since the avalanche, since she'd hauled him out of the snow-choked narrows by his collar while he coughed up half a mountain, since he'd looked at her with those scribe's eyes—careful, cataloguing, missing nothing—and thanked her in a voice that made clear he intended to keep owing her rather than trust her. Smart man. Owing was safer than trusting. Trust got people killed, or worse, got them *kept*.

Four days since she'd started lying to him properly instead of just by omission. Every night she told herself she'd come clean about the client, the contract, the promise of her brother's location sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal. And every morning the words died somewhere between her lungs and her tongue, because the truth was simple: if she told Gideon Pemberton that someone had paid her to find him, he'd want to know who, and why, and what they wanted with the map—and she couldn't answer any of those questions without admitting she didn't know either. A smuggler who admitted ignorance was a smuggler out of business.

So she climbed, and lied by omission, and tried not to think about Tam's face at eight years old, being led away by Temple greycoats while she hid under a fish cart and did nothing.

*You're doing something now,* she told herself. *This is doing something.*

It didn't help. It never helped.

* * *

They crossed the river at midday, wading through water so cold it burned, and by late afternoon the trees had changed—the pines giving way to black-barked birch, the birch to bare slopes scattered with cairns. Stacked stones, waist-high, some older than the empire itself if the stories were true. Each one marked something. Sera didn't know what. That was the problem with clan territory: everything meant something, and none of it meant anything to her anymore.

Eleven years ago she'd known. Eleven years ago she'd run these passes with her father's crew, before the empire's tariffs strangled the lowland trade and pushed the smuggling routes north into clan ground, before her father decided the safest way through was to pay tribute to both sides and be trusted by neither. She'd been fifteen, quick with knots and quicker with knives, and the clan scouts had known her name. *Little Quill.* They'd let her carry messages between their camps because she was small and fast and because her father paid in salt and steel.

Then came the year the Temple took Tam, and her father stopped paying anyone, and Sera learned that debts in the mountains didn't expire. They compounded.

"They're going to know who I am," she said, when the light began to fail.

Gideon glanced at her. He'd gotten better at reading her over the past weeks—or maybe worse, depending on how you counted, because a man who read you well was a man who could hurt you precisely. "Know who you are how?"

"I ran cargo through these passes. Before." She adjusted the strap of her pack, buying a second to choose her words. "The clans keep records. Not paper—memory. There are women whose whole job is remembering faces and names and what those names owe. If one of them looks at me twice—"

"What do you owe?"

"That's not your business."

"It became my business the moment you saved my life under an avalanche." He said it mildly, the way he said most things, but there was steel under it now. The road had put it there. She'd watched it happen day by day, the temple mouse hardening into something with teeth, and she wasn't sure yet whether that was good for her or very, very bad.

"I owe three crews their passage fees," she said finally, because lying to him outright had started to sit wrong in her stomach, which was its own kind of dangerous. "I owe a blood-debt to the Ashvale family for a mule that died carrying their goods—a mule, Gideon, they're holding a *mule* over my head, except the mule was carrying their grain when the rope failed, so really I owe them a winter's food, except they've had eleven years to inflate the number. And I owe a man named Corragan a knife wound, which is the kind of debt that doesn't have a number, only a direction."

"A direction meaning—"

"Meaning Corragan wants to return it. To me. In person."

Gideon was quiet for a few steps. Then: "And you brought us in here anyway."

"There's no other way to the Heartstone. South is Voss and his riders. East is a week of dead scree with no water. West—" She gestured vaguely at the peaks. "West is worse than all of it. North is the only door, and this is the door."

"So we knock."

"We knock," Sera agreed. "And we hope whoever answers has forgotten my face."

* * *

Whoever answered had not forgotten her face.

It happened at dusk, in a fold of the mountain where the trail narrowed between two walls of stone. One moment the path was empty; the next there were four figures on it, and Sera hadn't seen a single one arrive. That was the thing about clan scouts that empire soldiers never understood—you didn't hear them coming because they weren't coming from anywhere. They were simply *there*, the way weather is there, the way night is there.

Three of them were young, spears in hand, dressed in wool and hide with iron torcs at their throats. The fourth was older—grey in his beard, a scar splitting one eyebrow, a longbow slung across his back with the ease of a limb. His eyes went past Gideon entirely and fixed on Sera, and she watched recognition move through his face like frost moving across a window.

"Well," he said. His voice was gravel rolled in honey. "The mountains spit you back out, Little Quill."

"Brannoc." Her mouth was dry. Of all the scouts in all the passes, it had to be him. Brannoc of the High Cairn line, who had taught her to read smoke signals when she was sixteen, who had carried her half a mile on his back when she broke her ankle on the Icefield Trail, who had stood at her father's funeral and spoken the clan words over the grave even though her father had been a lowland thief who deserved no rites at all. "You look old."

"And you look alive, which surprises me more." He circled her slowly, the way you'd circle a horse you were thinking of buying or putting down. "We heard you were dead. Or worse—we heard you were working for the Temple."

"I've never worked for the Temple."

"You're traveling with a bonded scribe." Brannoc's chin lifted toward Gideon, who had gone very still in the way that clever prey animals go still. "That ink on his wrist isn't decoration. So either you work for them, or you stole from them, and I confess I don't know which worries me less."

"Neither," Sera said. "He's running. Same as me."

"Everyone's running from something." Brannoc stopped circling. Behind him, the young scouts shifted, spears angling down—not lowered, just angled, the difference between courtesy and threat measured in inches. "Here's what I know, Little Quill. Two days ago, a rider came down from the imperial garrison asking after travelers on the north road. Yesterday, one of our own saw a column of greycoats at the foothills, moving slow but moving. And today, the two of you walk into our territory carrying the smell of the lowlands all over you, and one of you carries—" his nostrils flared, theatrical, "—something else. Something old. Don't ask me how I know. The mountain tells us things."

Sera's hand drifted toward her knife and stopped. Drawing steel here would be suicide, and worse, it would be rude, and rudeness in clan territory had a longer memory than murder.

"We're passing through," she said. "That's all. We want nothing from the clans and we'll take nothing."

"You already took something." Brannoc's eyes flicked to Gideon's coat—to the left breast, where the map lay sewn flat against his ribs. Sera felt her stomach drop. She'd thought the stitching was invisible. Apparently the mountains had better eyes than she did. "A map. Carried out of the lowlands by a dying courier, given to a scribe who should have handed it to his masters and knelt. Instead he runs north with it sewn into his coat like a sin." Brannoc smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "You want to know how I know all this? Because the courier passed through our ground nine days ago. Wren, she called herself. Clan-blooded, though she wore lowland clothes. She paid us in news, and the news she paid us with was that someone would follow her trail, and that when they came, we should look closely."

Gideon made a sound. Small, involuntary—the sound of a man hearing a dead woman's name in a stranger's mouth. Sera didn't look at him. Looking at him would tell Brannoc exactly how much that name cost, and she was suddenly, fiercely certain that whatever Wren had been to Gideon, it wasn't information the clans should have for free.

"She said to look closely," Brannoc went on, "and to judge whether the ones following were worthy of the road. So here is my judgment, such as it is." He planted his spear butt on the stone. "I could kill you both and take the map to the elders, and they would thank me, and the map would vanish into a strongbox for fifty years while old men argued about it. Or I could turn you back south, into the arms of your greycoat friends, and wash my hands. Or—" he paused, letting the third option hang, because men like Brannoc always let the third option hang, "—you come to High Cairn. Both of you. You stand before the moot, you speak your purpose, and you let the elders decide what you're worth. Alive or otherwise."

"And if we refuse?" Sera asked.

"Then the second option happens, just slower and further from my camp, where no one will hear anything."

* * *

They made camp that night under guard, which Brannoc called hospitality and everyone present understood to mean custody. A fire was permitted. Food was provided—barley bread, hard cheese, smoked fish that tasted of the cold deep water of the high lakes. Gideon ate mechanically, staring into the flames, and Sera watched him do sums behind his eyes.

"He knew Wren," Gideon said at last, quietly. "Or knew of her."

"Clans remember everyone who crosses. Especially clan-blooded ones wearing lowland clothes." Sera turned the fish in her fingers, not eating. "You knew her name."

"I told you. The courier who died at my door. She never gave a name." He hesitated. "But she looked like—"

He stopped. Sera waited. He didn't finish, and she filed the unfinished sentence away with all the other unfinished sentences Gideon Pemberton kept locked in that ledger of a head. The man had more redacted entries than a Temple archive.

"You realize what this is," she said instead, nodding toward the young scout at the edge of the firelight, pretending not to listen and failing completely. "High Cairn. The moot. It's not an invitation, it's a cage with better scenery."

"I know."

"We trade Voss's cage for theirs. At least Voss's cage had a known price. Debt-bond, seven years, done. These people—" She shook her head. "Their prices are whatever they decide you're worth, and they decide after they've heard you talk. You'll be standing in front of a ring of elders who think every lowlander is a thief with a baptismal record, and you'll have to convince them otherwise with nothing but your voice."

"My voice has convinced no one of anything in thirty-one years."

"Then it's overdue." She tossed the fish back on the plate. "Look. I've stood in front of clan moots before. When my father died, I had to plead the family's debts down or lose the route entirely. I was seventeen and I talked for two hours and I bought us three years. It can be done. But you have to understand what they respect."

"Which is?"

"Truth, mostly. Delivered like a blade. They don't care about your feelings and they'll smell a performance before you finish the first sentence." She leaned closer, dropping her voice beneath the fire-crackle. "Whatever you're carrying—whatever that map means, whatever Wren was to you—if there's a version of it you can say out loud to a room full of enemies, find it tonight. Practice it. Because tomorrow you get one speech, and after the speech, the rest of your life is whatever they decide."

Gideon looked at her for a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes, and behind the fear she saw something else—something that had been growing in him since the avalanche, since the narrows, since the first night he'd chosen the map over absolution. Resolve, maybe. Or the beginning of it. A muscle remembered.

"Why are you helping me?" he asked. "Really. Not the partnership speech. The real reason."

Because someone paid me to find you and I haven't told you. Because my brother is in a Temple cell and you're the coin that buys him out. Because every mile north I hate myself more and keep walking anyway.

"Because you saved nothing and no one by dying on this mountain," she said, "and neither did I. Get some sleep, scribe. Tomorrow you learn to fight with your mouth."

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin. Brannoc woke them before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and they climbed.

The trail to High Cairn switchbacked up through birch and then bare rock, and with every hour the air thinned and the world widened until Sera could see the whole northern reach laid out below—valleys stitched with smoke from clan steadings, the silver thread of the river, and far to the south, almost invisible, the smudge of the lowlands where the empire sat on everything it owned like a fat man on a chair. Somewhere down there, Malachi Voss was climbing too. She could feel it the way she felt the watching: a pressure in the air, patient and certain.

Near midday they crested the last shoulder and High Cairn opened before them, and Sera heard Gideon stop breathing.

It was not a city. It was older than cities—a vast shelf of stone jutting from the mountainside, ringed with standing monoliths taller than three men, each carved top to bottom with spirals and knotwork worn soft by centuries of wind. Between the stones, turf-roofed halls clustered like the mountain had grown them. Smoke rose from a dozen fires. People moved everywhere—herders, smiths, children racing between the monoliths, warriors drilling with wooden staves in a yard below. And above it all, at the ring's highest point, a great stone seat faced the sunrise, empty, waiting.

"The moot gathers at noon," Brannoc said, walking beside her now, his tone almost gentle, which frightened her more than the spears had. "Every clan line sends its voice. There will be two hundred people in that ring, Little Quill, and every one of them will be looking at you."

"At *me*?"

"You're the one who knows the roads. You're the one with debts. And you're the one who chose to bring him here instead of selling him to the Temple, which makes you either the bravest woman in the Thornfells or the biggest fool, and the elders will very much enjoy deciding which." He glanced back at Gideon, trudging behind with his coat buttoned tight over the map. "As for you, scribe—when they call you forward, remember one thing."

"What thing?"

Brannoc smiled again, and this time there was something almost like kindness buried in it, deep down, like a spring under stone.

"In that ring, a lie dies fast. But the truth, badly told, dies faster. Choose your words like your life depends on them."

"Doesn't it?"

"No," Brannoc said, and turned toward the fires. "Your life depends on the elders' mercy. Your words only decide whether they feel any."

Below them, a horn sounded—long, low, rolling off the monoliths like a wave—and the people of High Cairn began, unhurried, to gather.
