## 13. The Moot at High Cairn

They took my boots at the pass.

Brannoc's warriors had bound my wrists with braided horsehair—not rope, the scout had explained with something like courtesy, because rope was for animals and I was, at least technically, a guest—and marched us upward through two days of pine and scree to a place where the mountain opened like a cupped hand. High Cairn. I'd copied its name a hundred times in Temple ledgers, always in the column marked *unruly territories*, always followed by figures: tribute owed, taxes unpaid, garrisons requested and denied.

The ledgers hadn't mentioned that it was beautiful.

The village clung to the shoulder of the mountain like something grown rather than built—round stone houses roofed with turf, smoke rising straight in the windless cold, terraced fields stitched down the slope in green and gold. At its center stood a ring of standing stones older than anything the empire had ever raised, taller than a man, each one carved with spirals and interlacing knots that my scribe's eye itched to transcribe. Fires burned between them even though the sun was still up.

"They're deciding whether to kill us," Sera said beside me.

She looked worse than I felt. The climb had cost her color she hadn't regained, and her hands were cuffed in front of her like mine, but she stood with her weight on one hip and her chin up, surveying the crowd gathering around the stones as if she were appraising merchandise. "Any advice?"

"You're asking me?"

"You're the scholar." Her eyes cut sideways. "You told Brannoc you knew things about the old shrines. I'm hoping some of those things keep us breathing."

I hadn't told Brannoc anything of the sort. What I'd done, hauled half-frozen off the trail after the avalanche, was mumble something about waystation shrines and boundary marks while he held a knife near my throat, and he'd apparently decided it was worth carrying me up a mountain to verify. Now forty warriors and their families watched us from the edges of the fire-ring, and I understood with terrible clarity that whatever happened next would happen because of what came out of my mouth.

"I know ledgers," I said. "I know scripts. I don't know how to stop an execution."

"Then improvise." Sera smiled, thin and sharp. "It's what I do."

* * *

The moot convened at dusk.

An old woman struck a bronze bell that made no sound so much as a feeling—a vibration in the chest, in the teeth—and the crowd settled onto benches and blankets arranged in a wide circle. Brannoc stood us in the center, exposed, and took his place among eleven elders seated on carved wooden chairs that had clearly been old when the empire was young.

I catalogued them the way I catalogued everything, out of habit and out of terror. Three women, eight men. The eldest was a man so weathered his face seemed carved from the same granite as the stones behind him. A heavyset woman with silver rings on every finger kept looking at Sera with open hostility. And at the far end sat a younger man in a grey cloak trimmed with wolf fur, who never once looked at us—he looked at the elders, reading them, and I recognized the posture immediately.

He was a politician. Every institution has them. Even this one.

"The strangers were taken on clan ground," Brannoc said, "carrying a map written in the old script, fleeing the empire's hunters. I spared them under traveler's law. The law says they stand under my protection until the moot judges otherwise."

"The traveler's law is for travelers." This from the woman with the rings. "These are spies. The soft-handed one smells of temple ink. You can smell the Temple on them, like rot."

A rumble of assent moved through the circle. My stomach dropped through the mountainside.

"And the other one?" The eldest elder's voice was dry as old paper. "What does she smell of?"

The woman spat. "Sera Quill. Smuggler. She ran salt and steel past our watchers for three years and paid her tolls late every time. She owes debts here, and now she comes back leading a temple scribe by the hand."

Sera, to her credit, didn't flinch. "I paid every toll," she called out, "eventually. Which is more than your watchers can say—they drank half of it first."

Someone laughed. It broke the tension just enough. The ringed woman's glare could have curdled milk.

"The question," said the eldest, raising a hand, "is not what they owe. It is what they carry. Brannoc says a map in the old script. Show it."

My heart stopped. The map was sewn into my coat lining, and my coat was currently sitting in a warrior's hut fifty feet away, and for one bright moment I thought we might be spared the question entirely—

But Brannoc nodded to a young runner, who returned with the coat and handed it to the eldest with the reverence due a relic. The old man worked the stitches free with fingers like tree roots, drew out the oilcloth, and unfolded it across his knees.

For a long time, nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Wind moved through the standing stones and made a low sound, almost like humming.

"This script," the eldest said finally, "hasn't been read aloud in this village since my grandmother's grandmother. We have songs with fragments in it. Lullabies." His eyes lifted to mine, and they were pale grey, nearly white, and utterly without mercy. "And a temple scribe walks into our mountains carrying it sewn into his coat. So. Scribe. Before we decide whether you live—you will tell us where you found this, and what it says."

Every eye in the circle fixed on me. Sera went very still beside me.

I opened my mouth to say *I found it on a dying courier*, which was true, and *I cannot read it*, which was also true, and both truths together sounded exactly like what they already believed: a spy's cover story. I felt the whole circle leaning toward the verdict, felt the executioner's patience settling over the scene like snowfall.

And then something in me simply refused.

Maybe it was Voss's face, calm behind his desk while he offered to erase my debt. Maybe it was ten years of copying other people's numbers in other people's words, invisible, interchangeable, a hand attached to a debt instead of a name. Maybe it was the lullaby—the one my mother used to hum, the melody I'd heard again two nights ago when a clan woman sang a child to sleep in the next hut, the melody I had not been able to get out of my head since.

Whatever it was, I heard myself speak, and it was not the truth.

"I can read it," I said. "Some of it."

Sera's head turned a fraction of an inch. She said nothing. Bless her, she said nothing.

The eldest's eyebrows rose. "Then read."

I walked to him on numb legs and looked down at the map I had studied for weeks by candlelight, whose symbols I had traced and retraced until they lived behind my eyelids. I could not read them. But I had spent ten years in the Temple archives handling documents no one else could parse—I knew how dead languages behaved, how scripts carried grammar in their shapes, how repetition marked names and boundaries and blessings. And I had noticed, weeks ago, the pattern along the map's border: the same cluster of symbols recurring at intervals, always paired with a spiral mark.

Waystations. Boundary shrines. I'd guessed it in Hollowmere before the old woman went pale; I'd simply never said it aloud.

"This line," I said, touching the border without quite touching it, "is a road of shrines. Each mark is a resting place—a waystation for keepers walking the high passes. The spiral means a threshold. A door." I turned to the interior symbols. "This is the Heartstone of Thornfell. The script calls it—" I hesitated, grasping for a word that fit the shape of the symbol, the weight of it, and the word arrived from somewhere deeper than scholarship, from my mother's voice singing in a language she'd never taught me— "*the mountain's memory.*"

Silence. Then the ringed woman laughed, short and hard. "Convenient. A temple man who reads the forbidden script the moment his life depends on it."

"It isn't forbidden to me." The words came out steady, and that frightened me more than the knives had. "My mother sang in this tongue. I didn't know what it was until I heard it sung here, two nights ago, in your village. She died calling it pilgrimage. I think—" My voice caught, and I let it, because the grief was real even if the scholarship was improvised. "I think she was coming home."

The circle went quiet in a different way. I had spent my whole life learning that the most powerful lie is the one wrapped around a true thing, and here it was, working, and I hated myself and kept talking.

"I am not the Temple's man. I'm their debtor. There's a difference, and everyone in this circle knows what a debt does to a person." Murmurs of agreement—of course there were; these people lived under tribute. "The Temple's Inquisitor is hunting this map because he believes what's written on it should belong to the empire or burn. I believe it belongs here. That's why I ran. That's why I'm standing in front of you with no boots."

The eldest studied me for a long, long moment. Then he looked past me, to the elders' row, to the young man in the wolf-furred cloak who had finally, finally looked directly at me.

"He reads the mother-tongue," the young man said quietly, "and claims Pemberton blood." He rose from his chair. He was tall, broad-shouldered, maybe thirty, with a scar splitting one eyebrow. "There is one way to test a claim like that. The locket. Brannoc said they carried a locket."

My hand went to my collar before I could stop it. The locket hung there against my skin, warm from my body, and I realized too late that I had just shown the entire circle where it was.

"Give it here," the young man said, "and we'll send for Mairead."

"Who is Mairead?" Sera asked.

"The seer," Brannoc said, and there was something new in his voice—not warmth, exactly, but interest. "She reads bloodlines the way you read ink, scribe. If your mother was of the clans, Mairead will know."

The ringed woman objected loudly. Two others argued. The circle dissolved into debate, voices rising, and I stood in the middle of it holding my own wrists, cuffed, bootless, having just performed the greatest act of forgery of my life—inventing fluency in a dead language in front of the last people alive who might have truly known it.

Sera leaned close, her mouth barely moving. "You can't read it."

"No."

"You lied to eleven elders and forty warriors."

"Yes."

Her eyes glinted with something between horror and admiration. "Gideon Pemberton. You might survive after all."

*I might also have just invented a heritage I can't prove,* I thought. *And if this seer looks at my blood and finds nothing, we die on this mountain tomorrow.*

The bell sounded again, that deep vibration in the chest, and the eldest raised his hand.

"The moot is ended. The strangers live tonight. Tomorrow, when Mairead comes down from the high huts—" his pale eyes settled on me, and for just a moment, beneath the granite, I thought I saw something almost like pity— "we will learn what your blood remembers. Sleep well, scribe. If you can."

They led us to a hut and gave us broth and blankets and left a guard at the door. Through the turf walls I could hear the village settling, dogs, a child laughing somewhere, the ordinary music of people who did not yet know what was buried under their mountain.

Sera lay back and stared at the smoke-hole. "That performance," she said quietly. "Where did it come from?"

I thought about the lullaby. About my mother's hands, ink-stained like mine, humming a language she'd hidden for twenty years.

"I don't know," I said. And for the first time since Wren had died in my doorway, it was almost the truth.

Outside, far up the mountain, above the snowline, a single light appeared in the darkness—too high for any hut, too steady for a torch. It burned for a moment, then went out.

The guard saw it too. He crossed himself the clan way, thumb to brow to heart, and muttered something I couldn't hear.

I didn't need to hear it. Some part of me, the part that had spoken in a dead language without knowing why, translated it anyway:

*She is coming.*
