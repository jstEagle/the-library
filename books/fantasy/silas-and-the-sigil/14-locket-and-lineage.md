## 14. Locket and Lineage

The seer's name was Maren, and she was older than the longhouse they carried her into.

Gideon had expected a hut at the edge of the settlement, something apologetic and smoke-darkened, the way temples tucked away their inconvenient mystics. Instead Brannoc led them to the center of High Cairn itself, to a roundhouse built of black timber so old it had gone silver at the joints, and inside it, on a chair that might have been carved from a single root, sat a woman so small and folded that at first he thought she was a heap of blankets.

Then she opened her eyes.

They were pale as winter water, and they went straight past Sera, past Brannoc, and fixed on Gideon with an attention that made his skin prickle like the moment before lightning.

"There you are," Maren said.

"I—beg pardon?"

"Been waiting since moonrise." She clicked her tongue, impatient, ancient. "The mountain told me someone was coming who smelled of ink and secrets. Sit down before you fall down, scribe. You've been walking like a man carrying a stone in his chest for three days."

Gideon glanced at Sera. She gave him a look that said *I told you* — she'd been telling him things with her eyebrows since the moot ended, mostly variations on *these people are not going to let us leave* — and lowered herself onto a bench near the fire with the easy insolence of someone who had decided the room couldn't hurt her. Gideon sat more carefully, on the edge of a stool, knees together, hands in his lap. Twenty years of temple posture died hard.

Brannoc remained standing by the door, arms crossed, filling it the way a boulder fills a streambed. "The woman wants to see what they carry," he rumbled. "I said yes because I am curious too. Do not mistake this for trust."

"I wouldn't dream of it," Sera said sweetly.

Maren ignored them both. She extended one hand toward Gideon — a hand like a bundle of twigs wrapped in brown paper — and wiggled her fingers. "Give."

He hesitated. The locket had lived against his sternum since Hollowmere, warm from his body, and some superstitious animal part of him resisted handing it over, the way a man resists letting go of a rope over a drop. But that was precisely why he'd come. Three days on the mountain trail, three nights of turning the little portrait over in his mind until the familiar face blurred and sharpened and blurred again, and he needed someone else's eyes. Someone whose eyes weren't his own memory lying to him.

He lifted the cord over his head and placed the locket in her palm.

Maren weighed it. That was the only word for what she did — she held it up, closed her eyes, and *weighed* it, tilting it slightly as though judging coin. Her lips moved without sound. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, clan children were shouting at each other in a language Gideon didn't know but which, absurdly, felt like it knew *him*, the vowels landing somewhere below thought.

Then Maren opened the locket.

She looked at the miniature portrait for a long time. Longer than a glance warranted. Long enough that Gideon's heart began doing something embarrassing against his ribs.

"Well?" Sera asked, when the silence had gone on too long even for her.

"Hush." Maren tilted the portrait toward the firelight. "Come here, boy. Look at your own face and tell me what you see."

"That's not my face. It's a woman's."

"It's your face wearing a woman's years," Maren said flatly. "Look *properly*. You temple folk are trained not to look — I can smell the training on you. They teach you to read words and never faces, because faces tell truths the ledgers don't hold."

Against every instinct, Gideon leaned forward and looked.

He had been looking at the portrait in stolen moments for days, and every one of those moments had ended the same way: with the certainty that he knew her, and the inability to say from where. A young woman, dark-haired, painted in the flat style that predated the empire's fashion for shadow and depth. Serious mouth. Eyes that seemed faintly amused by whoever was painting her, or by the world in general.

Now, forced to look properly, he saw what he'd been refusing to see.

The jawline was his jawline. He'd have known it anywhere; he shaved it every morning. The set of the eyes, slightly wide, slightly watchful — his mother used to call him *her little sparrow watching the cats*, and here were those same eyes in another face. Even the small asymmetry of the brows, the left sitting a hair higher than the right, was repeated exactly, as if the painter had worked from a mirror held by Gideon's future self.

"That's—" His voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat. "That's impossible."

"Nothing is impossible," Maren said. "Some things are merely expensive." She turned the locket over in her fingers, and her thumbnail found something on the back — a mark worn nearly smooth, visible now in the raking firelight. "Ah. And there it is."

"What is it?" Brannoc asked from the door.

"A maker's mark. Clan work, and old." Maren traced it with a finger that trembled only slightly. "This is Cairn silver. My grandmother's pattern, or near enough. We stopped making lockets like this after the Binding Wars, when the empire decided our smiths should shoe its horses instead." She looked up at Gideon, and there was something in the winter-water eyes that hadn't been there before — a kind of reluctant pity. "You understand what I'm telling you, scribe? This woman wears your face. This locket was made by clan hands. Put those two facts together and stop flinching away from the sum."

"The sum is that someone painted a stranger," Gideon said. "Coincidence of features. Families run to certain looks across whole regions, any genealogist will tell you—"

"Show me your mother's name."

The question landed like a dropped glass.

"Why?"

"Because I asked," Maren said, "and because you've spent the last minute deciding whether to lie to me, which tells me the true answer is worth lying about. So. Your mother's name."

Gideon's mouth opened. Closed. He was aware of Sera watching him now, really watching, her earlier boredom burned away. He was aware of the fire, of Brannoc's shadow across the doorway, of the children's voices outside rising and falling like gulls.

"Mariel," he said finally. "Mariel Pemberton."

Something passed over Maren's face — not recognition exactly, but the shape of a door being tested and finding itself unlocked. She repeated the name under her breath, once, twice, tasting it. Then she began, very softly, to hum.

Three notes. Four. A descending phrase that circled back on itself, simple as a lullaby, because that was exactly what it was.

The floor tilted.

Gideon did not move, but everything in him moved — twenty years of sediment shifting all at once, the whole careful architecture of his memory groaning. Because he knew that tune. It had no words that he'd ever been given, just the melody, hummed low while her hands worked bread dough or mended his coat or smoothed his hair when fever kept him abed. His mother's song. A song he had never heard anywhere else in thirty-one years, in a city of a hundred thousand people, in a temple where the hymnals ran to four thousand pages.

And a clan seer in the mountains was humming it like she'd learned it at her grandmother's knee.

"Where did you hear that," he whispered.

"Where does anyone hear it?" Maren said. "From a mother. It's a cradle-song of the Blackwater line — one of the old keeper families, back before the clans scattered and the shrines went cold. Mothers sang it to their daughters so the daughters would know their own when they heard it, even if they never spoke of anything else." She closed the locket gently and held it out to him, and he took it with hands that did not feel entirely like his hands. "Your mother sang that song to you, didn't she. And she never told you what it was."

"She said it was nothing. A nonsense tune from her own childhood." His voice sounded far away to himself. "She said a lot of things were nothing."

Sera had gone very still by the fire. Even Brannoc had shifted forward off the doorframe, drawn despite himself.

"Mariel Pemberton," Maren said again, working the name like a knot. "Pemberton is not a clan name — it's a temple name, a taken name, the kind they give when someone comes in from outside and needs burying in the rolls. But Mariel..." Her eyes narrowed. "There was a Mariel of the Blackwater line. Long ago now — before your birth, certainly, but I was a girl, and girls remember scandal. A daughter of the keepers who went down out of the mountains and married into the lowlands, against every custom, and was struck from the line-rolls for it. Struck so thoroughly that within a generation no one spoke her name." She paused. "Except, apparently, in lullabies."

"You're saying—" Gideon stopped. Started again. "My mother was clan."

"My mother was *keeper-born*," Maren corrected. "Which is a different thing entirely, and a much heavier one. The Blackwater line kept the old shrines, scribe. All of them. Their blood was the hinge the doors swung on." She leaned forward, twigs-and-paper hand gripping her root-carved armrest. "Now think carefully, and answer me honestly, because I suspect your life has depended on not thinking about this for a very long time. Your mother. Did she disappear? Regularly? Days at a stretch, with explanations that never quite held their shape?"

The sediment shifted again. A memory surfaced whole, unbidden: his mother standing in the doorway in her grey traveling cloak, seven years old and clutching her skirt, asking where she was going, and her smile — that particular smile, amused and sad at once, the same expression frozen on the painted face in the locket — and her answer: *To see an old friend, sparrow. Watch the cats for me.*

She'd been gone nine days that time. When she came back there was mountain dust in the seams of her cloak that she never explained, and for a week afterward she hummed the lullaby constantly, like a woman reassuring herself of something.

"Yes," he said. It came out barely above a whisper. "Yes, she did."

"And how did she die?"

"On pilgrimage." The temple's official phrase, stamped into the death-roll in his own copying hand when he was nineteen, because the clerk short-staffed that season had asked him to fill in the entry and he'd written his own mother's death into a ledger like any other debt settled. "She traveled south and she died on pilgrimage."

"Mm." Maren's gaze was terrible and gentle. "South. Away from the mountains. Away from everything her blood was tied to. Isn't it strange, how the dying travel so faithfully in whatever direction their secrets point?"

The longhouse was silent except for the fire. Gideon became aware that his thumb was moving over the locket's worn maker's mark, around and around, and made it stop.

"There's one more question," Maren said. "And then I'll give you tea, because you've gone the color of porridge. The courier. The one who brought you that locket — the one whose blood was on it, if Brannoc's scouts reported true. Did you know her?"

"No," Gideon said. "I'd never seen her before in my life. She was dying and she pressed it on me and she said—" He faltered. What had Wren actually said, in those last wet seconds? *Take it. Take it to Thornfell. It has to be Pemberton blood.* He'd assumed it was madness, the delirium of a woman bleeding out. "She said it had to be Pemberton blood. I thought she was raving."

"Wren," Sera said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She shrugged, uncomfortable at having spoken. "The courier. Voss's people called her Wren. It was in the reports we — it was in the reports." She caught herself smoothly, but Gideon filed the slip away: *we*. Sera Quill had read Temple reports somewhere, somehow, and he was beginning to understand that her interest in this journey had never been simple.

"Wren," Maren repeated. She turned the name over the way she'd turned the locket, and her brow creased. "Wren is a lowland bird-name. But it could be a masking name — clan children of the scattered lines often took bird-names when the purges came, so the rolls couldn't follow them. Wren. Sparrow." Her winter eyes lifted to Gideon's face and held there. "Your mother called you sparrow, didn't she. I'd wager the house on it."

The floor was definitely tilted now. Or perhaps it was simply that Gideon had spent his entire life standing on ground he'd believed level, and was only now being shown the slope.

A courier named Wren, who wore no face he recognized but carried a locket with his mother's jawline and his mother's locket and his mother's song, who had crossed half the empire bleeding to put it in his hands, who had said *it has to be Pemberton blood* with her last breath — and who had known, somehow, exactly which door in the whole sprawling city to die against.

"Not a stranger," he said aloud, testing the weight of it. "She wasn't a stranger. She knew my address. She knew my name before I gave it. I thought — I assumed Voss's people had tracked her to me, that I was just the nearest—"

"You were not the nearest anything," Brannoc said from the door, gruff. "Temple quarter is a maze. A dying woman does not navigate a maze by accident."

"She chose you," Maren agreed. "Out of everyone. Which means she knew you — or knew of you — and knew what flows in your veins." She rose from her chair with the slow care of extreme age, took a kettle from the fire's edge, and poured tea into a wooden cup with hands that shook but did not spill. She pushed it into Gideon's grip. "Drink. And then listen, because I'm going to tell you the thing your mother should have told you thirty years ago, and you're not going to like it, and you're going to drink your tea anyway."

Gideon drank. The tea was bitter and resinous and steadied him the way a hand on the shoulder steadies.

"The Blackwater line were not priests," Maren said, settling back into her root-chair. "Forget everything the empire taught you about shrines and their keepers — incense and tithes and men in robes counting coins. The old keepers were something else. They were *doors*. Living ones. There are seals in the high places of this range, scribe, older than the clans, older than the empire, older than any god whose name would fit in your ledgers. And those seals do not open for keys or prayers or armies. They open for a particular blood, freely offered." Her eyes flicked to the locket in his hand, then back up. "The Blackwater blood."

"That's superstition," Gideon said automatically. It was the temple-trained reflex, and even as he said it he heard how hollow it rang — he was carrying a map covered in script no temple scholar could read, sewn into a coat he'd fled his bonded life in, sitting in a roundhouse full of people whose grandmothers had sung his mother's lullaby.

"Perhaps," Maren said, unbothered. "But here is what isn't superstition: your mother was struck from the rolls for marrying out. She went down the mountain, took a temple name, raised a son in the shadow of the Temple of Vessia itself — the one place in the world her blood would be least suspected — and she never once told him what he was. Ask yourself why a woman makes a choice like that. Ask yourself what she was hiding him *from*." Maren's voice dropped, and the fire seemed to lean in with her. "And ask yourself why, decades later, a dying girl with a wren's name and a keeper's errand came looking for the son she'd hidden — unless the thing she'd hidden him from had come back, and the hiding was over."

The cup was empty. Gideon didn't remember drinking it.

Outside, the children had gone quiet. In the new silence he could hear the wind moving through High Cairn's stone teeth, a low sustained note like a held breath, like the mountain itself waiting to hear what he would say.

His mother's face smiled sadly from the locket. *Watch the cats for me, sparrow.*

All those years. All those unexplained absences, the dust in the cloak seams, the song with no name, the way she'd gone rigid whenever temple processions passed their street — he had built a whole life on top of those cracks and called the foundation sound. And underneath it, all along, had been a woman running from something, hiding a bloodline in plain sight, humming a dead family's song into a child's hair so that someday, somewhere, someone might recognize it.

Someone had. Too late, and in blood.

"Gideon." Sera's voice, quiet, from beside the fire. Not mocking. Not sharp. For once, just his name. "Breathe."

He breathed.

Maren watched him with her winter eyes, and when she spoke again it was almost kind. "You asked where I heard the song. Now I'll ask you something, and take your time, because your answer decides what happens next — mine, Brannoc's, every soul in this valley's. That map you're carrying. The one sewn into your coat, because yes, child, we all watched you favor that seam for two days and none of us are fools." A dry click of her tongue. "The symbols along its border. Have you looked at them closely? Truly closely? Because I'm an old woman, and my eyes are failing, and even I can see what they are."

"They're shrine-script," Gideon said. "No temple lexicon matches them. I've tried—"

"They match nothing in your temple," Maren said, "because they were never temple script. They are keeper-marks, scribe. Blackwater marks. The same signs your ancestors cut above the doors of the high shrines, the same signs your mother would have known before she could walk." She leaned forward, and the firelight filled the hollows of her face. "Whoever drew that map drew it *for* a Blackwater. Not for a smuggler. Not for an inquisitor. For you — or for whoever carried your blood. And now I want you to tell me, honestly, with your mother's song still ringing in your ears: do you truly believe that courier was a stranger to you?"

Gideon opened his mouth to say *yes* — the safe word, the temple word, the word that kept the world in its neat columns and rows.

What came out instead, in a voice he barely recognized, was:

"She called me *little brother*."

The words hung in the smoky air. He hadn't remembered saying them at the time — hadn't trusted the memory through three days of storm and trail, had filed it under delirium with everything else. But now it stood up out of the sediment, whole and undeniable: the courier's hand pressing the oilcloth into his, her lips moving against the blood, and the words, faint as the last note of a lullaby — *run, little brother.*

Maren closed her eyes. Brannoc swore softly in clan-tongue. Beside the fire, Sera had gone white around the mouth, and Gideon realized distantly that she had done the arithmetic faster than he had — that a sister meant a mother who'd borne more than one secret, a family split and scattered like seed, a whole hidden architecture of lies with him living inside it his entire life.

"Then you'd best sit back down," Maren said quietly, "and tell me everything your mother ever told you. Every disappearance. Every excuse. Every night she sang that song. Because your sister died to put that map in your hands, scribe — and I am beginning to fear that what she died running from is not behind you." Her winter eyes went, briefly, to the north window, where the peaks of Thornfell stood black against the stars. "It's ahead of you. And it's waiting."

* * *

Later — much later, when the tea had gone cold in his hands and Maren had finally released him with instructions to sleep, which he knew he would not — Gideon sat alone in the guest-longhouse with the locket open in his palm.

The fire had burned to embers. Sera slept in the corner with her back to the wall and her knife loose in its sheath, because she was Sera. Outside, the wind had changed, and now carried a different note off the northern passes — thinner, higher, almost like singing.

He looked at the painted face. His jawline. His mother's mouth. His own watching eyes, a generation early.

"Who were you?" he whispered to her. "Either of you. Who were we?"

The embers popped. The wind sang. And in the space between waking and memory, the first images began to surface — his mother in the doorway in her grey cloak, the dust in the seams, the smile — and Gideon understood, with the terrible clarity of a man stepping off a ledge, that he was going to spend the whole night falling backward through his own childhood, and that nothing down there would ever look the same again.
