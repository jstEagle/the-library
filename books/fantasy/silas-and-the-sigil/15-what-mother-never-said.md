## 15. What Mother Never Said

Gideon did not sleep.

He sat in the guest hut the clan had given him—a round room of woven branches and smoked hides, a fire pit breathing low at its center—and turned the locket over and over in his hands until the portrait's painted eyes seemed to move. The seer's words were still lodged in him like a splinter too deep to draw.

*Pemberton features. Clan braids.*

He had spent thirty years believing he knew his mother. He had been wrong about everything, and the wrongness was not new—it had simply gone unnoticed, the way you don't notice a wall leaning until it finally falls.

* * *

She disappeared. That was the first memory, and it came back now with terrible clarity, stripped of all the soft explanations he'd wrapped around it as a child.

He was six. It was autumn, because he remembered the smell of rotting apples in the gutter outside their tenement in Vessia's lower quarter, and his mother was gone for nine days. Not eight, not ten—nine, because he had counted them on his fingers each morning, sitting on the step, watching the street.

When she came back she was thinner, and there was mud dried in the creases of her knuckles that no amount of washing would lift for days. She smelled of woodsmoke and something green and cold, like pine sap and stone. She knelt and held him and wept into his hair without explaining anything, and six-year-old Gideon had accepted this, because children accept everything, because they have no other choice.

*Your mother went on pilgrimage,* the neighbors said. *Devout woman.*

But pilgrims went to the Temple of Vessia, up the marble steps, two streets away. Pilgrims came back with blessing-marks in ash on their foreheads. His mother came back with mud under her nails and a look in her eyes he would not understand for another twenty-four years—the look of a woman who has seen something she cannot speak of and has decided, finally, to carry it alone.

* * *

The lullabies. He had forgotten the lullabies, or told himself he had, but they rose now whole and unbroken, every note intact, as if they had been waiting in him all along like water in a sealed jar.

She sang them at night, when the lamps were out, when the debt-collectors' footsteps had faded from the lane. Low songs, humming songs, songs with words that weren't Vessian. He had asked her once what language it was.

*"Our old tongue,"* she said, and smiled, and tucked the blanket under his chin. *"A secret language. Just ours."*

Just ours. As if it belonged to the two of them, mother and son, instead of—as he now understood—to an entire people who lived beyond the mountains, who braided their hair in patterns the seer recognized at a glance, who sang these same songs at moots and funerals around fires Gideon had never stood beside.

The Temple taught that the clan tongue was gutter-speech, that it had no grammar worth learning, that it was the language of rebels and savages. His mother had sung it to him as a lullaby. Which meant she had known what it was. Which meant she had chosen to make it small and secret and safe, a private music box instead of a heritage, because a heritage could be taken from you, and a lullaby was only a lullaby.

He tried to sing one now, there in the dark hut, and his voice cracked on the second line. He got the melody right. The words came back slower, half-remembered, and one phrase surfaced that he had never understood even as a child:

*Auren deth vael, auren deth mor.*

He had asked her once what it meant. She had stopped singing. Just stopped, mid-note, and in the firelight her face had done something complicated—grief and fear and love all trying to occupy the same expression—and then she'd kissed his forehead and said, *"It means sleep, little one."*

It did not mean sleep. He knew that now. Auren. The name on the map. The name pressed into wax seals and carved above shrine doors. Whatever the song was about, it was about *that*—and she had been singing it over his cradle while the empire she'd fled collected its taxes two streets away.

* * *

Her death. This was the memory that hurt worst, because it had never hurt before, and now it arrived all at once, decades late, with interest.

He was fourteen. She left on a grey morning in early spring, wearing her good cloak, and she crouched down to his height—which was nearly her own by then—and held his face in both hands.

*"If I'm longer than a month,"* she said, *"go to Master Corvin at the scriptorium. Tell him I sent you. He'll keep you."*

Not *if anything happens*. Not *I'll be back by the feast-day*. She had arranged things. She had made provision. A woman going on a simple pilgrimage did not arrange for her son's guardianship in advance, in detail, with a specific man's name.

She died on the road. That was what they told him. Fever, they said, caught at a waystation. The Temple recorded it in its ledgers in his own careful hand years later—*Maren Pemberton, deceased, cause: fever, status: pilgrim*—and Gideon had copied the entry himself without feeling anything, because grief, real grief, requires a body to stand in, and he had buried his standing up, without ceremony, at fourteen, because no one showed him how to do otherwise.

Now, holding the locket, he understood: she hadn't died of fever on some pious road. She had gone back. Back to the mountains, back to whatever she had fled—or been exiled from, or escaped—and she had known the risk, and she had gone anyway, and she had made sure her son would land somewhere safe before she did it.

And the locket. She must have left it behind, hidden, or worn it, and somehow—somehow—it had come to Wren. To the dying courier in his doorway. Who had known his name. Who had looked at him with an expression he had taken for desperation and now recognized as recognition.

* * *

Wren. He said the name aloud in the empty hut, testing it against the shape of his mouth, and it fit the way a key fits a lock you didn't know you carried.

His sister. Possibly. Probably. The seer had said the woman in the portrait wore Pemberton features, and Wren had worn those same features stretched across a different life—same narrow jaw, same dark eyes set close, the family face he'd only ever seen in mirrors and had assumed was merely his own. He had looked at her bleeding on his threshold and thought *she seems familiar* the way you think a stranger seems familiar, and dismissed it, because strangers are always familiar in cities, because cities are full of faces.

But she had come to *him*. Out of all the scribes in Vessia, all the doors in all the lower quarters, she had collapsed at his. And she had pressed the map into his hands with the last of her strength, and she had said—what had she said? He had barely heard it under the bells, under the storm, under his own panic.

He closed his eyes and pulled the moment back, frame by frame, the way he'd been trained to hold a page of text in his mind before copying it.

Her lips had moved. Two words. Barely voiced. He had thought it was a prayer, or a curse, or just breath leaving a broken body.

*"Little brother."*

That was what she'd said. Little brother. And he had knelt there holding an oilcloth bundle and thinking only of hiding the body before the third bell finished ringing, and he had not heard it, or had heard it and refused it, filed it away in the part of his mind where impossible things go to wait.

She had crossed half the empire, wounded, hunted, carrying a map and a locket and a message, to find a brother she had never met—or had met once, perhaps, when he was small enough to forget—and she had died on his doorstep having delivered exactly one word of it.

*Forgive me,* he thought, to a dead woman, in a dead language, in a hut full of smoke. *I didn't hear you.*

* * *

Near dawn, Sera found him still awake. She pushed through the hide door without knocking, which was apparently how Sera did everything, and stopped when she saw his face.

"You look like a man who's been reading his own funeral ledger."

"My mother was clan." His voice came out flat, strange, someone else's voice. "Not temple-born at all. Everything—the debt-bond, the indenture, the way she hid the language—all of it was cover. She was hiding us. From something." He turned the locket in his fingers. "And the courier. Wren. I think she was my sister."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. He expected mockery, or deflection, some blade of practicality to cut the moment down to size. Instead she lowered herself onto the bench across from him and studied the locket with a smuggler's eye—cataloguing, assessing—and then, unexpectedly, gently.

"The disappearances. The songs. Sending you to a specific man before she traveled." She ticked them off like cargo. "She wasn't devout, Gideon. She was *returning*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole map."

"I know."

"Do you? Because here's what I know about families." Her voice hardened slightly, the old scar tissue showing. "They leave you things. Riddles, debts, names you can't pronounce. And they expect you to carry them whether you want to or not." She stood. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow Brannoc wants you at the moot-fire again, and the elders have questions about your bloodline that 'scholar's knowledge' won't answer."

She left. The fire settled. Gideon sat alone with the locket open in his palm, his mother's painted face looking up at him across thirty years of lies, and felt the grief finally arrive—not the thin, dutiful sorrow of a boy at a funeral, but the full weight of it, late and enormous, the grief of a man mourning a mother he never actually met.

Outside, somewhere on the dark slopes of High Cairn, a horn sounded twice—short, short—the signal Brannoc had taught them meant *council gathers at first light*.

The elders would ask about his blood. And for the first time in his bonded, careful, invisible life, Gideon Pemberton had an answer worth giving.
