## 16. Sera's Price

The fire had burned down to coals by the time Gideon finally slept, and Sera Quill sat across from him with her back against a granite shelf, watching the rise and fall of his chest and hating herself in the quiet, methodical way she hated everything.

He slept like a man who'd never learned it was safe. Even unconscious, his hand stayed near the coat where the map was sewn, fingers curled an inch from the fabric, as if his body knew what his mouth wouldn't say. She'd noticed that on the trail. The scribe guarded the thing like a mother guards a child, and he didn't even seem to know he was doing it.

*You should tell him,* said the part of her that still had a conscience left, buried somewhere under fifteen years of dock work and knife fights and lies told so smoothly they'd worn grooves in her tongue.

*And then what?* said the rest of her. *He walks into the mountains alone and gets killed, and Tam stays wherever Tam is.*

She pulled her knees up and stared at the coals.

Her brother's name was Tammas. Everyone else called him Tam. He'd been seven years old the day the Temple's grey-cloaked assessors came through Saltmarsh with their ledgers and their scales, weighing children like cargo, calculating which families owed what to whom. Her father had died in a net-collapse two winters before, and the family debt had passed down like an inheritance nobody wanted. The Temple gave them a choice: the boat, or the boy.

Her mother had wept for three days and then stopped speaking entirely, which was its own kind of answer. Sera, eleven years old and already mean enough to survive Saltmarsh, had stood on the dock and watched the barge pull away with forty bonded children packed below decks, and Tam had pressed his small palm flat against the porthole glass until the ship turned and she couldn't see it anymore.

She'd been looking for that palm print ever since.

Fifteen years of asking questions in the wrong taverns. Fifteen years of running cargo past places she wasn't supposed to look at, trading favors with clerks and turnkeys and one very expensive night with a Temple archivist who'd drunk too much and mentioned, in passing, that the bonded scribes were kept in *wings*, that the promising ones got moved *upstairs*, that a clever boy named Tammas Quill had been noted for his hand.

Then, eight months ago, a hawk had come to a waystation outside Greyharbor with no signature and no seal. Just a letter, written in a fine clerk's hand:

*I know what you're looking for. I can give you the wing, the floor, the door. Bring me the map of the Sigil of Auren when it surfaces—it will surface—and the boy goes free.*

She'd spent a week telling herself it was a trap. Then another week telling herself it didn't matter if it was a trap, because a trap at least meant someone knew where Tam was, and knowing was more than she'd had since she was eleven years old standing on a dock.

So when word reached the smuggler's grapevine—a courier dead on the north road, a temple scribe gone missing with something valuable sewn into his coat—Sera Quill had done what she always did. She'd followed the money. And the money, this time, had a face: tired, ink-stained, too honest for his own good, walking into the Thornfell passes like a lamb touring a slaughterhouse.

*The map for the boy.* That was the bargain. That was all of it. Simple as a knife.

Except.

Except the lamb had a name now, and it wasn't *the scribe*. It was Gideon. Except he lied to Voss with his whole body shaking and did it anyway. Except he'd walked into clan territory knowing they might kill him, because some map somebody pressed into his dying hands mattered more to him than breathing. Except last night, half-asleep by the fire, he'd murmured something she couldn't catch—*Wren, wait, don't*—in a voice cracked open like an egg, and she'd felt something in her chest move that hadn't moved in fifteen years.

She was starting to like him. That was the problem. That was the whole rotting problem, sitting there in the coals' red light like a corpse she'd been trying not to look at for weeks.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Sera Quill had never once flinched from arithmetic: if she delivered Gideon and the map to her client, Tam went free. If she didn't, Tam rotted in a Temple wing until he was old and grey and forgot he'd ever had a sister. And if she warned Gideon—if she told him the truth about who'd sent her—he would run, or he'd burn the map, or he'd do something noble and stupid, and either way the bargain died and Tam died with it, slow, in ledgers.

One stranger against her brother. It shouldn't have been hard math.

It was becoming impossible math.

Gideon stirred in his sleep and she went still, hand drifting to the knife at her hip out of pure habit. But he only turned his face toward the fire, and in the coal-light she could see the resemblance she'd been refusing to see—the same hollows under the eyes, the same stubborn line of jaw. Wren, whoever she'd been, had left her mark on him. He carried his ghosts the way sailors carried ballast: low, necessary, never spoken of.

*He thinks you saved him back there,* she thought. *On the mountain. In the avalanche. He thinks you're on his side.*

She was on his side. That was the sick joke of it. She wanted him alive, wanted him to reach whatever shrine he was walking toward, wanted—gods help her—to see what his face looked like when he found whatever it was he was looking for. She just also wanted her brother back, and the world had arranged things so that she could only have one.

Hadn't it?

She turned the thought over again, the way she'd been turning it over for days, looking for a seam. *The client wants the map. Fine. What does the client want it FOR?* Nobody paid that kind of coin, that kind of reach—hawks, informants, knowledge of Temple wings—for a piece of old vellum. Whoever it was wanted the Sigil itself, or wanted it destroyed, or wanted it kept from someone else. And every one of those options meant the client didn't need Gideon alive after the handoff.

Which meant the moment she made the trade, she became the person who fed a decent man to the wolves. Which meant—

Which meant she needed a third option, and Sera Quill had survived fifteen years in her trade precisely because she never accepted that there was no third option. There was always a third option. It just cost more than the first two.

*Find out who the client is,* she thought. *That's the seam. You make the trade blind, you're a tool. You make it knowing whose hand is on the other end, maybe you find the crack. Maybe there's a way to get Tam without giving them Gideon's throat.*

The coals shifted. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the high pines with a sound like slow breathing. In clan territory now, Brannoc had called it. They were deep enough that the empire's roads were behind them and the empire's law was a rumor, and somehow that made her feel less safe, not more. Out here the only law was debt, and Sera Quill owed debts in three directions: to the client who'd promised her Tam, to the man sleeping across the fire who'd started trusting her despite every instinct he owned, and to the eleven-year-old girl on the dock who'd sworn, with a child's absolute ferocity, that she would get him back.

She took out the original hawk-letter, as she did most nights, and read it again by coal-light. *I know what you're looking for.* No name. No seal. Just that fine, patient clerk's hand—and one detail she'd noticed on the tenth reading and couldn't stop noticing since: the ink. Temple ink. The blue-black shade the scriptoriums used, the shade she'd seen drying on Gideon's own fingers.

Whoever held Tam's leash wore Temple colors. Of course they did. The Temple took him; the Temple alone could give him back. She'd known that going in. But seeing it written in that particular blue-black, night after night, made it real in a way her gut understood and her plans didn't.

Tomorrow they'd reach the high pass. After that, according to the map's logic—which Gideon explained in his dry, reluctant way, drawing shapes in dirt like a schoolmaster—the waystation shrine, and beyond it the Heartstone. After that, presumably, the client's patience ran out and the hawks came hunting.

She had until then. A few days, maybe a week, to find the seam.

Gideon woke near dawn, the way soldiers wake—all at once, hand moving before his eyes opened. He saw her sitting up and something in his shoulders eased, and she hated how much she liked that. How much she liked being the thing a careful man relaxed around.

"Bad dream?" she asked.

"I don't remember my dreams." He rubbed his face. "You've been awake all night."

"I sleep in shifts. Habit."

"You were watching me."

"I was watching the fire." She smiled, and it was the easy smile, the dock-side smile, the one that had talked harbor guards out of searching her crates a hundred times. "Don't flatter yourself, scribe."

He almost smiled back. Almost. Then his eyes went to the horizon, where the peaks had turned pink, and his expression did that thing it did—closing, settling, becoming the face of a man carrying something heavy uphill. "We should move. Brannoc said the moot gathers at High Cairn within days. If we're seen on the trails uninvited—"

"We'll be seen anyway. We're about as subtle as a house fire." She stood, stamped feeling back into her feet, and slung her pack. "Lead on, keeper of secrets."

His head came around sharply. "What did you call me?"

"A figure of speech." She stepped past him onto the trail, heart beating once, hard, against her ribs. *Careless. Careless, girl.* "Keep up."

Behind her, boots crunched on frost. Behind her, a man who trusted her followed her into the mountains, toward a thing worth killing for, not knowing that the price of his road had already been agreed upon—not knowing that the woman walking point was the knife, and the hand holding her belonged to someone whose face she'd never seen.

But she knew one thing now that she hadn't known eight months ago, watching that first hawk disappear over Greyharbor's rooftops: she was going to find out who held her brother's leash. And when she did—if they had lied to her about Tam, if there was no trade, if any part of the bargain was rotten—then gods have mercy on them, because Sera Quill had spent fifteen years learning exactly how little mercy she had left.

The trail bent north, into the teeth of the pass. She went first, as always.

She just no longer knew, entirely, who she was leading him to.
