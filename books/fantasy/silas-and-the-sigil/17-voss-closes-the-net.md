## 17. Voss Closes the Net

The interrogator's tent smelled of tallow and wet wool, and Malachi Voss had long ago stopped noticing either.

He sat with the two clan scouts between him and the brazier, and he let the silence do what silence always did. The younger one—Brannoc's man, by the knot of his hair—had stopped shivering an hour ago. That was a bad sign. Shivering meant the body still believed it might get warm again. When the shivering stopped, the body had begun to make other arrangements.

"Your names," Voss said, "are already written in my ledger. Whether they're written in ink or in ash is a matter I've left deliberately open."

The older scout lifted his chin. Blood had dried black along his jaw where the riders had taken him at the ford. "You'll write nothing we give you."

"I know." Voss turned his hands toward the fire, palms out, a gesture of almost comical harmlessness that he had learned, decades ago, worked better than any threat. "You'll give me nothing. You're brave men, sworn to your clans, and you'll die on your feet before you betray them. I have taken the confession of warriors who chewed through their own tongues rather than speak. I am not a fool, and I do not mistake you for cowards."

The younger scout's eyes flickered. Just once. Toward the older man.

Voss saw it. He always saw it.

"But here is what bravery cannot protect you from," he continued, in the tone of a man discussing weather. "It cannot protect you from arithmetic. Two of you were taken. Your war party scattered east into the pines. No one is coming. And the people you are protecting—the scribe and the smuggler—are already three days ahead of you, moving fast, because they believe no one knows their road." He smiled, thin and courteous. "I would like to keep it that way for as long as possible. Which means I need very little from you. A trail. A direction. Not even a name."

"You'll burn in the low dark," the older man said, "for what you carry into the mountains."

"Probably," Voss agreed pleasantly. "But not today."

He rose, crossed to the tent flap, and spoke quietly to the sergeant waiting outside. Then he came back, sat down again, and did the one thing that never failed: he waited longer than they could.

It took the younger one until the second watch. It always took the young ones less time. They had not yet learned that the empire's patience was more terrible than its cruelty.

* * *

By dawn, Voss stood over the map table in the command tent, and the picture was nearly complete.

A red thread ran north out of Hollowmere, bent west around the scree fields below Raven's Tooth, and climbed toward the high pass—the shrine pass, the old maps called it, though the Temple's cartographers had left it blank for two hundred years, which was itself a kind of testimony. Blank spaces on imperial maps were never accidents. They were wounds the empire preferred not to look at.

"They moved through Hollowmere," said Lieutenant Corren, a solid, unimaginative man whom Voss valued precisely for those qualities. "Bought flour and lamp oil. Paid in temple script."

"Of course they did. The scribe pays his debts even while running from them." Voss set two carved markers on the table. "Here, and here. The narrows above the treeline, and the shelf below the pass. If they take the northern route—and they will; the eastern trails are washed out this season—they must come through both."

"And if they don't?"

"Then my riders freeze on a mountain for nothing, and I look foolish in front of the column." Voss straightened. "I can survive looking foolish. I cannot survive them reaching whatever it is they're reaching. Send twelve to the narrows under Dain. Twelve to the shelf. Fast riders, light kit. They are to hold position, not engage—not unless the quarry tries to double back south. I want them boxed against the pass when the main column arrives."

Corren hesitated. "And the scouts, sir?"

"What of them?"

"The prisoners. Do we bring them with the column, or—"

Voss looked at the map for a moment. The red thread. The blank space beyond it.

"Leave them," he said. "The mountain will handle the paperwork."

Corren nodded and went out, and Voss remained alone with the map, and with the thing that had been sitting in the bottom of his dispatch case since Hollowmere, wrapped in oilcloth like something dead.

He had not wanted to look at it again. He looked anyway.

* * *

The fragment had come from the headman's house—or rather, from beneath it, from the cache the old woman had been too slow to burn. Three pages of vellum, brittle as autumn leaves, covered in the same pre-imperial script that marked the edges of Wren's map. Voss had taken them without ceremony, told himself he would hand them to the Temple archivists in Vessia, and then, somewhere on the cold road north, had broken his own instruction and read them.

He was not a scholar. He was an Inquisitor, which meant he knew enough old scripts to recognize heresy and enough theology to know when a text was dangerous. But these pages were not scripture. They were maintenance records.

That word had lodged in him like a splinter: *maintenance.*

He spread the pages now beside the brazier and went through them again, line by line, using the cipher-key the Temple used for pre-Imperial clan inscriptions—a key every Inquisitor learned and most forgot, because the clans' old writing appeared so rarely in the empire's business. The translation came slowly, in pieces, the way ice comes off a window.

*...the Sigil does not conquer. The Sigil binds. What was loosed upon the world in the age of ash could not be killed, only closed, and the closing was made with willing blood at the Heartstone...*

*...let no hand raise it as a weapon, for it is not a sword but a lock, and a lock turned wrongly opens...*

*...the seal weakens as all things weaken. Each hundredth winter it must be renewed by one of the keeper's line, freely given, or the door beneath the mountain stands unbarred...*

Voss read it three times.

Then he sat very still, the way he had taught himself to sit during interrogations, because stillness was the only honest response to certain kinds of information.

*A seal.* Not a weapon. Not the fabled engine of conquest that the Temple's own histories described—the relic that had made the mountain clans masters of the north in the age before the Empire, the thing the uprisings had always been fought over, the thing every general and every priest for two centuries had assumed lay sleeping under Thornfell like a drawn blade.

A lock. A door held shut. And dying, by the sound of it, the way all old things die.

He thought about what that meant, and the thinking was not comfortable.

If the Sigil were a weapon, then everything was simple. Whoever held it could break the empire, and therefore the empire must hold it, or destroy it, or both. That was the logic he had carried his whole career, the clean arithmetic of threat: find the artifact, deny the artifact, end the story. He had burned villages on that arithmetic. He had buried friends beneath it.

But a seal was different. A seal meant the danger was real but inverted. A seal meant the clans had not been hoarding power all these years—they had been *guarding* a debt. A seal meant that the empire's great victory, the binding of the mountain clans after the last uprising, had been built on top of a door that someone else's blood was holding shut.

And a seal meant, above all, that the truth was worse than the lie.

Because if the Temple learned the Sigil was a seal—if the archivists confirmed it, if the Synod debated it, if the fact traveled down through the ranks like water through cracked stone—then every faction in the empire would arrive at the same conclusion from different directions. The hawks would say: destroy it, collapse the shrine, damn the consequences, better a broken mountain than an open door. The pragmatists would say: seize it, control the renewal, turn the keeper's line into imperial property, breed them like hounds if necessary. And the zealots—the ones Voss feared most, because he had been one—would say: let it fail. Let the door open. Let the clans be blamed, and let the empire answer with fire, and let God sort the ashes.

There was no faction in Vessia that would say: leave it alone, honor it, let the mountain keep its own counsel.

Voss had served the Temple for thirty years. He knew its digestive system intimately. Any truth fed into it came out the other end as policy, and policy came out as orders, and orders came out as smoke over the valleys.

So he did the thing that Inquisitors are trained never to do.

He folded the pages, put them back in the oilcloth, and slid them into the coals of the brazier, and watched the pre-imperial script curl and blacken and go up as heat, which was, he reflected, the only form in which the empire could safely receive it.

No one must know. Not the Synod. Not the generals. Not Corren, good dull Corren, who would follow orders faithfully and report them faithfully and thereby kill everyone in this campaign. The knowledge would live in exactly one skull, and that skull would ride to the shrine pass and stand between the truth and everyone who might use it.

He told himself it was righteousness. He was careful to use that exact word, the way a man checks a knot twice.

*Righteousness:* the Sigil must never fall into imperial hands, therefore the quest for it must end at the pass, therefore the scribe and the smuggler must be stopped, therefore the seal—if there was a seal—must simply be allowed to die unwitnessed. An unopened door behind collapsed stone. Let the mountain keep its secret. Let the clans keep their grief. Let the empire sleep another century on top of a grave it didn't know it was standing on.

It was a clean solution. It required only that a handful of people on a frozen mountain cease to matter.

Voss noticed, distantly, that his hands were steady, and that this frightened him more than shaking would have.

* * *

They rode out at first light, the column snaking up the valley road in grey silence, breath steaming, hooves striking sparks from frozen ruts. Voss rode at the front, as he always did. An Inquisitor who rode at the back was a man asking his soldiers to believe in something he wouldn't stand in front of.

Corren drew alongside near midday. "Riders reached the narrows at dawn, sir. Dain reports position secured. Nothing moving on the trail yet."

"Good."

"There's—" Corren hesitated, which was unlike him. "The men are asking, sir. What's in the mountains. What we're chasing. They've heard the name Sigil. You know how it travels through a camp."

Voss watched the pass ahead, a white notch in the black shoulder of Thornfell, impossibly far and impossibly close.

"Tell them the truth," he said.

Corren blinked. "Sir?"

"Tell them we're pursuing thieves who stole Temple property, and that the property is dangerous, and that recovering it protects every village from Hollowmere to the sea." Each word was true. None of them was the truth. Voss had spent thirty years learning the difference, and only now, riding north into the blank space on the map, did he understand that the difference was a kind of abyss, and that he had spent his whole life walking along its edge calling it a road.

"That's what I'll tell them," Corren said, satisfied, and dropped back to the column.

Voss rode on alone at the head, and the mountain grew, and the cold found the seams of his coat, and he thought—not for the first time, but for the first time without comfort—of a night twenty-two years ago, crouched in a burning barley field outside Cairn Low with a dying clan woman's hand pressed flat against his breastplate, her fingers leaving blood-shaped prints, her mouth shaping words in the old tongue that he had later paid a scholar three silver marks to translate.

*You build your peace on our graves and call it order,* she had said. *One day the ground asks it back.*

He had filed the sentence away as the ravings of the defeated. He had been young. He had needed it to be ravings.

Now the pages were ash in a brazier in Hollowmere, and the ground was asking, and the only man in the empire who knew the shape of the question was riding at the head of forty soldiers toward a door in the mountain, carrying the answer locked behind his teeth like a stone.

Ahead, a hawk circled the pass—white against white, then gone.

Behind, the column followed, faithful and blind.

And somewhere above the treeline, three days out and climbing, a scribe with his sister's map sewn into his coat was walking toward the same door, believing he was outrunning the empire, not knowing that the empire had already arrived—that it was waiting at the narrows, patient as winter, wearing his own Inquisitor's face.

Voss touched his spurs to his horse and began the climb.
