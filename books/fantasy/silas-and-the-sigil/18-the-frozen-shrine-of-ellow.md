## 18. The Frozen Shrine of Ellow

The storm broke two hours before dawn, and Gideon woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath.

He crawled out of the lean-to they'd built against the rockface and found the world remade. Snow lay knee-deep and unbroken, blue-white under the last stars. The peaks of Thornfell rose around them like the walls of some vast cathedral, and above them—where the pass narrowed into a throat of black stone—hung a light that was not starlight.

"Sera," he said.

She was already awake. She was always already awake; he'd stopped wondering how she did it. She stood at the edge of the shelter with her arms crossed against the cold, watching the same light, her breath pluming in slow ribbons.

"I see it," she said. "Been seeing it since the wind dropped."

"What is it?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She turned to look at him, and even in the dark he could read the set of her jaw—the look she wore when she'd already decided something and was only waiting for him to catch up. "Waystation shrine of Ellow. Brannoc mentioned it before we split from his scouts. Said if we made the high pass by the new moon, we'd see its lantern burning." She nodded up toward the glow. "That's not a fire. Fires don't burn that color."

Gideon looked again. The light was pale gold shot through with something colder, a thread of blue like frost seen through amber. It didn't flicker. It simply *was*, steady as a heartbeat slowed to the pace of mountains.

"The map," he said.

Sera's mouth twitched. "The map."

They had been carrying the map for eleven days now—sewn into Gideon's coat lining, transferred there after Sera had pointed out, with brutal practicality, that a coat could be cut off a body faster than a satchel could be pried from frozen fingers. Eleven days of climbing through clan territory with Brannoc's warning ringing in their ears: *the moot has granted you passage, not protection. What you carry is yours to defend.*

Eleven days of the map's strange script resisting every attempt Gideon made at translation. He knew forty scripts—temple liturgical, imperial civil, three dead tongues of the old coast kingdoms, the knot-writing of the river deltas. None of them matched. The symbols were angular but flowing, each one seeming to contain the next, like rivers drawn inside rivers.

But last night, half-frozen and half-asleep, he'd dreamed of his mother humming over the washbasin, and he'd woken with a word in his mouth that belonged to no language he'd ever studied—and yet he knew what it meant. *Keeper.*

"Come on, then," Sera said, shouldering her pack. "Before the weather turns again. This mountain gives you a window and then it slams the shutter."

* * *

The climb to the shrine took most of the morning.

The path—if it could be called that—switchbacked up a face of granite glazed with ice, and Sera went first because she always went first on the bad stretches, driving her picks into seams Gideon couldn't even see. He followed in her handholds, counting his breaths the way she'd taught him, refusing to look down at the valley floor where the trees had shrunk to a carpet of dark moss.

Twice the mountain shook. Not an avalanche this time—something deeper, a long groan rolling up through the stone itself, as if the peak were turning in its sleep. Each time, Sera paused and pressed her palm flat against the rock until the trembling passed.

"It knows we're coming," she said the second time. She said it lightly, the way she said most unnerving things, but her eyes weren't light.

"Don't say that."

"Why not? Might be true." She hauled herself over a lip of ice-crusted stone and reached back for his wrist. "Everything else about this errand has been true. Why stop now?"

They came around the final shoulder of the ridge near midday, and there it was.

The shrine of Ellow stood in a hollow below the summit ridge, a small structure of grey stone no larger than a shepherd's hut, half-swallowed by snowdrift. Its roof was a single slab of slate furred with rime. And from its peak rose the light—a slender column of gold-blue radiance, silent, unwavering, pouring upward into the empty sky like water running backward.

No door. That was Gideon's first coherent thought. The shrine had no door, no window, no seam in its masonry that he could find. It looked less like a building than like a stone that had been persuaded, long ago, to take the shape of one.

Sera circled it twice, boots crunching, one hand never far from her knife. "No entrance," she reported. "No tracks. Nothing's been here in years—maybe decades." She stopped at the base of the light column and held her bare hand into it. Her fingers, blue with cold, warmed visibly, color flooding back. "It's warm," she said quietly. "Warm as a hearthstone. In the middle of all this—" she gestured at the white desolation ringing them, "—this."

Gideon approached more slowly. As he drew near the shrine wall, the map—sewn inside his coat—grew warm against his ribs. Not uncomfortably. Like a hand resting between his shoulder blades.

And the script began to move.

He cried out and staggered back, tearing open his coat. The oilcloth map lay against his chest where he'd tucked it that morning for safekeeping, and across its surface the angular symbols were *shifting*, rearranging themselves with a dry whisper like sand sliding, settling into new configurations—and then, impossibly, into legibility.

"Gideon?" Sera was at his side, knife out, scanning the ridgeline for threats that weren't there.

"It's reading me," he breathed. "Or—it's finished. Whatever it was waiting for, it—" He lifted the map with shaking hands. The script still moved at the edges, symbols drifting like fish under ice, but the central column had gone still and clear, and the words resolved into a tongue he somehow knew, the way you know your own name.

He read aloud, because silence felt unbearable:

"*Here stands Ellow, third keeper-station of the Thornfell line. Beyond lies the Heartstone, and beneath the Heartstone sleeps the Sigil of Auren, seal upon the wound of the world.*"

His voice cracked on the word *wound*. He hadn't known it would mean that. He'd read it and understood it all at once, the way you understand a blow.

Sera had gone very still. "Read it again."

"*Seal upon the wound of the world,*" Gideon repeated. His breath fogged and hung strangely in the shrine-light, as if the air itself were listening. "*The Sigil is not a blade nor a crown. It binds what was broken when the mountain bled. Once in a hundred years the binding thins, and once in a hundred years it must be renewed—by blood freely given, by a hand of the keeper's line, willing and unforced.*"

The wind moved through the hollow. Somewhere far below, ice cracked and resettled.

"Willing blood," Sera said flatly.

"That's what it says."

"Freely given." Her eyes came up to his face, sharp as awls. "Gideon. Whose blood renews a seal like that? A drop? An offering? A cupful? Or—"

"I don't know."

"—or all of it?" She stepped closer, grabbing his wrist, the map crumpling between them. "Look at me. You've been translating this thing in your head for days. You knew the script before today—I saw your face at Hollowmere when the old woman read those edge-symbols, you recognized half of them. So don't stand there telling me you don't know. Tell me what it says. All of it."

He wanted to lie to her. He was, by his own long accounting, a good liar now—he'd lied to Voss, lied to temple superiors, lied his way across half a mountain. But lying to Sera had gotten harder with every mile, and standing in the light of a shrine older than the empire, with the words still ringing in his ears, he found he couldn't do it.

"It doesn't say," he admitted. "Not here. There's more script further down the page—it hasn't settled yet. It might say. But Sera—" He turned the map toward her, pointing at the lowest line of text, which had just finished resolving. "There's this."

She read it aloud, slowly, sounding out words in a language she didn't know but whose meaning arrived anyway, carried on the same current that carried him:

"*Let none mistake the Sigil for a prize. It chooses no master. It holds no power for the taking. It is a debt, kept by keepers, paid by keepers, until the mountain itself forgets its grief.*"

Silence. The column of light poured upward, indifferent, beautiful.

"Well," Sera said at last, and her voice had a brittle brightness he'd learned to fear. "That's going to disappoint approximately everyone."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the shrine, because there was nowhere else and because, absurdly, the ground within ten paces of it stayed thawed—dark earth soft under their bedrolls while the rest of the world froze solid. Sera got a small fire going from their hoarded wood, and they sat close together, drinking melted snow flavored with the last of the pine tea, watching the light burn.

Gideon couldn't stop looking at the map. He'd spread it across his knees, and now and then another line would settle into clarity, another fragment of the old tongue locking into place. Most of it was liturgy—invocations, warnings, the names of stations along some ancient route that had once run the length of Thornfell. Waystation after waystation. Keeper after keeper.

"There were others," he murmured. "Look—Ellow is the third station. There are markings for at least nine. Shrines like this one, strung along the whole range. A whole network." He traced the symbols with a fingertip. "Someone maintained all of this. Generations of someones."

"And now there's one shrine left," Sera said. "One lantern still burning. What happened to the rest?"

He didn't answer, because the answer was already forming in him, cold and certain: *the empire happened.* Vessia's legions had climbed these passes a century ago to put down the clans, and empires did not tolerate things they couldn't tax or command. A network of shrines answering to no throne, tended by a bloodline outside imperial law—that wasn't faith. That was sedition. Of course they'd burned it. Of course they'd called it peace.

"The clans must have known," he said instead. "Some of them. Brannoc's people—the moot, the old laws, the way they talk about the mountain like it's a living relative. They're keeping pieces of it without knowing why."

"Or knowing exactly why and keeping it quiet." Sera fed a stick to the fire. "Empires punish memory, Gid. My mother used to say the surest way to survive Vessia was to forget on schedule." She glanced sideways at him. "Your temple certainly forgot a lot. Conveniently."

He let that pass. It was true, and they both knew it, and there was no longer any point in defending the institution that owned seven years of his life—or had, until he'd run.

The light shifted.

It was subtle at first—a deepening of the gold, a slow pulse like a heartbeat waking. Then the column of radiance bent, curving downward, and touched the map on Gideon's knees.

Every unresolved symbol on the page blazed into meaning at once.

He gasped. Sera swore and scrambled back as the map lit from within, lines of script glowing like veins of fire, and the whole design rearranged itself one final time—not into words now, but into a picture. A mountain. Thornfell, unmistakably, its double summit rendered in a few swift strokes. And beneath the greater peak, marked with a circle of interlocking symbols Gideon recognized from the locket's engraving—from his mother's locket, from Wren's locket—a chamber.

Beneath it, in letters that seemed to press up through the vellum like bruises:

*THE HEARTSTONE. THE SEAL. THE SLEEPING GOD'S CHAIN.*

And lower still, smaller, almost tender, as if written by a different hand:

*The hundredth year turns. The binding thins. Let the last of the line come home.*

The light withdrew. The map went dark and still, its work done, nothing left on it but ink and vellum and the shape of a mountain.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The fire crackled. Far off, the mountain groaned again, that deep tectonic sigh, and this time neither of them remarked on it.

"A sleeping god's chain," Sera said finally. "So it's real. The Sigil. Not a legend, not a temple scare-story. Real, and under that peak, and thinning." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Everyone who wants it thinks it's a weapon. Brannoc wants it to break the empire's hold. Voss wants it destroyed. Whoever hired me wanted—" She stopped. He watched her decide, again, not to finish that sentence, and filed it away with all the other unfinished sentences she'd been accumulating since High Cairn.

"And it's none of those things," Gideon said quietly. "It's a lock. And locks don't make anyone free. They just hold doors shut."

"Then why does everyone want it so badly?"

"Because nobody reads to the end of the document." He looked down at the map—at the small, tender line, *let the last of the line come home*—and felt the full weight of it settle onto his shoulders like a yoke fitted precisely to his frame. "Because everyone assumes anything worth guarding must be worth stealing."

Sera studied him for a long moment. In the firelight and the shrine-light her face was all planes and shadows, the wariness softened into something almost gentle. "You've changed," she said. "Since the city. Since the ledger office. The man I found on that trail wouldn't have sat here talking about locks and documents. He'd have been calculating how fast he could sell this thing and how far he'd get before anyone caught him."

"He probably still is calculating," Gideon said. "He's just getting answers he doesn't like."

She laughed—a short, real laugh, surprised out of her—and for a moment the cold, the exhaustion, the dread all receded, and it was just the two of them in a ring of impossible warmth at the top of the world.

Then the light pulsed again. Three slow beats, deliberate, unmistakable. A summons, or a countdown.

Sera's laughter died. She looked from the shrine to Gideon and back. "It's never done that before."

"No."

"You want to tell me what you think it means?"

He wanted to say *no*. He wanted to fold the map away, bank the fire, and sleep, and pretend that tomorrow they'd descend the south face and find some valley where none of this had ever caught up with them. Instead he heard himself say:

"I think it means the Heartstone is expecting us."

He looked up at the black bulk of the greater peak, invisible now behind cloud, somewhere beyond it the chamber marked on the map, and beneath the chamber the Sigil, and beneath the Sigil—

*Willing blood. Freely given. A hand of the keeper's line.*

The locket hung against his chest, beside the map, warm as a second heart.

"We should sleep," he said. "We climb at first light."

Sera didn't move. She was watching him with that terrible perceptiveness of hers, the smuggler's instinct for cargo that weighs more than it claims, and he could see the question assembling itself behind her eyes—*what aren't you telling me, Gideon Pemberton, what have you been carrying since the shrine started talking—*

But the mountain chose that moment to shudder again, harder than before, hard enough to rattle loose stones down the eastern scree, and whatever she'd been about to ask dissolved into the practical business of survival.

"First light," she agreed at last, pulling her blanket around her shoulders. She lay down facing the shrine, knife unsheathed across her hip out of pure habit. "And Gid?"

"Mm?"

"When we get wherever this road ends—whatever's waiting under that stone—you don't go through any door ahead of me. We go together or not at all. Swear it."

He looked at her, at the firelight moving on her face, and felt the promise form and die in his throat a dozen times before he could force out the smallest version of it that was still true.

"Together," he said. "As far as the door."

Something flickered in her expression—she'd heard the qualifier, he knew she had—but the mountain groaned again and the light pulsed its slow triple beat, and there was no more room tonight for questions either of them could survive the answers to.

Above them, the lantern of Ellow burned on, patient as a century, lighting the way to the Heartstone for the last keeper of the line—who lay awake beneath it, doing arithmetic with his own heartbeat, and finding the sum exactly enough.
