## 19. Willing Blood

The last line of the script took me three hours to translate, and I spent two of them pretending it didn't say what it said.

We had the shrine to ourselves. The waystation of Ellow was a hollowed tooth of granite on the mountain's shoulder, its walls furred with frost-lichen, its single chamber no bigger than Sera's cabin on the *Kestrel's Luck* had been—if she'd ever owned a cabin, which she hadn't. A broken altar. A dead brazier. And across every surface, carved into stone so old the chisel-marks had gone soft as river pebbles, the script that had followed us from Hollowmere like a patient dog.

I sat cross-legged before the altar with my notebook open and my coat around my shoulders, because the cold in here wasn't weather. It was architecture. It came up through the floor and settled in the bones and made itself at home.

Sera was outside checking the horses—or checking the treeline, more honestly, since she trusted horses less than she trusted people and people not at all. She'd been out there an hour. She gave me these hours without asking for them, and I understood it as a kind of mercy, though mercies from Sera Quill always came with interest accruing somewhere I couldn't see.

I looked at the line again.

*And the bearer shall give what cannot be taken.*

I'd translated it clean by the second hour. The grammar was archaic but not obscure—Temple scribes spent their whole lives reading dead men's sentences, and this one had a structure I knew. A conditional clause. An obligation laid on a specific party.

*The bearer.*

Not a warrior. Not a king. Not a willing volunteer drawn by lot from the faithful.

The bearer. The one who carried the map. The one whose blood had already opened things that should have stayed shut.

I turned back through my notes, page after page of careful transcription, and read the fragments in sequence the way you're supposed to read a contract: all of it, together, no cherry-picking.

*The Sigil sleeps beneath the Heartstone.*

*It is renewed each hundredth year by blood freely given.*

*The door knows its own.*

*And the bearer shall give what cannot be taken.*

Four lines. Together they built a house, and I had been walking toward it for weeks without once looking up at its shape.

Blood freely given. That phrase had seemed almost gentle when I first copied it—a kindness, even. The old faiths were full of blood offerings; the Temple of Vessia itself kept a ceremonial knife for the Feast of Remission, though nobody had bled into a bowl in four centuries. Freely given meant no slaughter. No conquest. It meant consent.

What I hadn't let myself notice was who the text addressed.

Every other passage in the script spoke in the third person. *They shall come. They shall climb. They shall carry.* But these four lines—the ones that mattered—shifted. Second person. Direct address. *You* who read this. *You* who hold the map.

It wasn't history. It was instruction.

And instructions are written for whoever finds them.

My hand was steady. That surprised me. I watched it rest flat on the notebook page, ink-stained fingers, the callus on my middle finger where the quill sat, and I thought: this hand has copied ten thousand lies for the Temple and never once trembled, why would it start now, over the truth?

Outside, wind moved through the pass with a sound like slow breathing.

I did the arithmetic because I couldn't stop myself. That's the curse of a scribe's mind—we total columns even when we know we'll hate the sum. Wren had carried the map. Wren had died in my doorway with frost in her lungs and blood on her lips, and her letter—her unfinished letter, still folded against my heart—had called me *little brother* and asked forgiveness *for the weight*. She had known. She had known what the weight was, and she had carried it as far as her body allowed, and then she'd passed it to the only person alive whose blood fit the lock.

She hadn't sacrificed herself. She'd run out of road. And the road ended at me.

*Forgive me for the weight.*

I pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes until colors bloomed.

Footsteps on stone. Sera came in sideways through the low doorway, ducking on instinct even now, shaking snow off her shoulders. She'd found firewood somewhere—I never learned how she found things—and she knelt at the dead brazier and started building a fire with the brisk economy of someone who'd done it in worse places.

"Your face," she said, without looking up. "Something's wrong."

"Nothing's wrong."

"Liar." Flint sparked. "You've got your ledger face. The one where you've balanced a column and don't like the number."

I almost laughed. Of course she could read me. She'd been reading me since the trail above Thornfell, cataloguing my tells the way I catalogued smuggling routes—out of habit, out of hunger, out of whatever it was she refused to name and I was too much of a coward to name first.

"The translation's done," I said. "Mostly. There are gaps. Old scripts rot in the middle; you reconstruct from context and hope."

"And?"

"And the Sigil sleeps under the Heartstone. Confirmed. The renewal rite requires blood given willingly—that's confirmed too. The door will know Pemberton blood, which we already knew, since mine opened the last one." I fed a piece of syntax into the lie the way you feed kindling into a fire, small pieces first. "There's a section about the bearer's obligations I can't resolve yet. The grammar's broken."

She looked up then. Firelight caught the scar through her eyebrow, the one she'd never explained and I'd never asked about.

"You're doing the thing again," she said.

"What thing?"

"Where you tell me nine true things so I won't ask about the tenth." She struck the flint again, and the tinder caught, and a small orange bloom grew in the brazier. "I count, Gideon. It's what I do."

"I know it's what you do."

"So." She sat back on her heels and looked at me across the little fire, and her eyes were the flat grey of the ice on the high passes—nothing hidden in them, nothing wasted. "The tenth thing."

For one whole breath I considered telling her.

Understand something about that breath. It wasn't short. In it I saw the whole conversation unfold: her face going still, then hard; her hand finding her knife out of pure reflex; her saying *no*, saying *absolutely not*, saying *we'll find another way* the way people say it in stories, and meaning it, and being wrong, because there was no other way, there had never been another way, the seal wanted what it wanted and the only currency it accepted was the one in my veins. I saw her argue until arguing failed. I saw her do something reckless afterward—Sera Quill did not survive twenty years of smuggling by accepting losses gracefully—and I saw that recklessness get her killed, or worse, get Brannoc's people killed, or worst of all, get the seal left unbroken for another century of the empire's peace, which was a peace the way a boot on a neck is peace.

Or I saw her look at me the way she'd looked at me in the cave during the storm, when the fever had burned her honesty loose and she'd told me about her brother, and I saw that look break.

I chose the lie. I chose it the way you choose to step off a ledge you know is there: knowing, and falling anyway.

"The bearer's obligations are ritual language," I said. "Fasting. Vigils. The usual priestly furniture. I need a day or two with the damaged section before I'll commit to a reading."

"A day or two." She held my gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then she shrugged, and the moment passed, and I learned that the worst part of lying to someone sharp is not the lie—it's watching them decide to believe you. "Fine. We have a day. Voss's column is two days behind us at most, Brannoc's rearguard bought us that much and paid for it, so tomorrow we climb and you finish your grammar on the way." She pulled her knife and began slicing dried meat onto a flat stone to warm. "Eat. You've been hunched over that rock since dawn like it owed you money."

"It does," I said. "Everything owes me money. It's the story of my life."

That got a real laugh out of her, short and rough, and I stored it away the way a drowning man stores air.

* * *

I didn't sleep.

Not from fear. Fear would have been cleaner. What kept me awake was the strange, humiliating discovery that beneath the terror there was a thread of something that felt disgracefully like relief.

I lay near the brazier with my coat over me and the locket open in my palm—the miniature portrait of the woman with Pemberton cheekbones and clan braids, my mother or whatever my mother had been—and I examined the relief the way I'd examine a suspicious clause in a debt-bond. Looking for the trap. Because there had to be a trap in feeling relieved.

Here was what I found: for thirty-one years I had been a man waiting. Waiting out a bond. Waiting out a life. Every year of my indenture I'd marked off in the margins of my ledgers like a prisoner scratching days into a cell wall, and I'd told myself the counting was patience, but it wasn't. It was suspension. I had never once believed I'd reach the end of the bond and walk free into some actual life. Freedom was a word I copied for other people. My future had always been a room I couldn't quite picture myself standing in.

Now the future had resolved into something concrete. One more climb. One more door. One act, final and defined, and then—what? The text didn't say. *Given what cannot be taken.* Perhaps death. Perhaps something stranger; the murals at the last door had shown keepers who seemed less dead than absorbed, faces grown serene inside patterns of light. I wouldn't know until I stood in it.

But it was a shape. It was a purpose with edges. And some starved part of me looked at that shape and whispered: *finally, something you were actually for.*

That was the trap. I saw it clearly. Relief is just despair wearing better clothes. A man who wants nothing doesn't leap at the first altar offered him. I made myself sit up in the dark and say it aloud, very quietly, so the words would have to exist outside my skull:

"You want this because living has hurt. That's not the same as this being right."

The fire had burned to embers. Sera slept three feet away, curled toward the wall, her knife-hand tucked under her cheek even in sleep, and her breathing was the only sound in the shrine besides the wind.

"But it might still be right," I added, to be fair to both sides of the argument. "Wanting it badly doesn't make it false."

That was the loneliest sentence I have ever spoken, and I have spoken a great many lonely ones.

Because here was the truth underneath everything, the truth I'd carry up the Heartstone alone: it didn't matter whether my motives were clean. The seal didn't audit intentions. It asked for willing blood, and my blood was willing, and if my willingness was tangled up with thirty-one years of quiet self-erasure, well—the mountain would take the tangle along with the rest. Wren had sent the map to me knowing exactly who I was. Maybe especially because of who I was. *Little brother,* she'd written. She'd known the boy who counted his years in margins. She'd known he'd say yes.

I closed the locket. Held it against my sternum, over the folded letter, over the heart that had maybe a few weeks left of being mine.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin. Sera was up before it, as always, kicking snow over the embers and breaking down camp with hands that never fumbled. I packed my notebook, and my hand paused over the page with the fourth line, and I did something I'm not proud of.

I tore the page out.

Folded it small. Tucked it into the lining of my coat, deep, behind the seam where I'd sewn the map on the night I fled Vessia. If I died on the mountain, fine—but if I lived past the rite somehow, if the texts lied or the loophole existed, then no one needed to know what the original said. And if Sera found it—

No. She wouldn't find it. I'd make sure of that.

"Gideon." She stood in the doorway with the pale sky behind her, breath steaming, and for a second she just looked at me, and I felt that gaze go through the coat and the lining and the folded paper and land somewhere near the truth. "Last chance for breakfast."

"Not hungry."

"You're never hungry anymore." She frowned. "Since Ellow. Since you finished the translation, you've been eating like a man settling accounts."

"That's absurd."

"Mm." She turned out into the cold. "Come on, scribe. The Heartstone's waiting."

I followed her out into the blue morning, up the white throat of the pass, and the mountain rose ahead of us like a verdict. Somewhere below, Voss was climbing with his righteousness and his riders. Somewhere behind, Brannoc's wounded held a gorge they couldn't hold. And somewhere in my coat lining, a torn page rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat, keeping the secret that was going to break her.

One more climb. I could carry it one more climb.

I just had to keep practicing my smile.
