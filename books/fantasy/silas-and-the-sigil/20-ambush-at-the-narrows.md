## 20. Ambush at the Narrows

*Gideon*

The Narrows announced themselves an hour before they arrived.

The trail, which had been generous all morning—wide enough for two riders abreast, stitched with wildflowers and the sound of water somewhere below—began to pinch. Rock walls rose on either side, grey and sweating with snowmelt, until the sky was a ribbon of blue no wider than a road. Brannoc rode ahead without speaking, but Gideon noticed the way his hand drifted to rest on the axe at his knee, casual as a man scratching an old itch.

"Bad ground?" Gideon asked.

"Old ground," Brannoc said. "That's worse."

Sera, riding behind him, snorted softly. "He does this everywhere we go. Brooding geography."

"I'm not brooding," Gideon said.

"You're composing a ledger entry about it. I can tell. Your shoulders do a thing."

Despite everything—the fever of the last three days still ghosting through her voice, the bandage under her coat he'd re-dressed that morning with hands that shook less than they had—he almost laughed. Almost. Then Brannoc raised a fist, and the column stopped, and the laughter died where it stood.

Ahead, the gorge bent sharply left around a shoulder of granite. The river ran loud below them, hidden by a lip of stone. And there was something else beneath the water-sound, so faint Gideon might have imagined it: the creak of leather. The shift of a horse's weight.

Brannoc's nostrils flared. "Woodsmoke," he breathed. "And oiled steel." He turned in the saddle, and for the first time since High Cairn, Gideon saw something like regret cross the clan warrior's weathered face. "They're already here. Voss's riders. They came up the south pass while we took the long way."

"How many?" Sera asked.

"Enough."

The word had barely left him when the first arrow came out of the morning.

It took the warrior behind Gideon—a young man named Coric who had spent the previous evening teaching Sera a drinking song—in the throat. He made no sound at all. He simply tipped sideways off his horse and was gone, swallowed by the roar of the river, and then the gorge erupted.

Riders poured from the bend ahead—grey cloaks, imperial steel, six of them, eight—and more were coming down the scree slopes above, dismounting to loose arrows from heights that made the clan's shields suddenly very small. A second arrow buried itself in Gideon's saddlebag inches from his thigh. His horse screamed and reared, and the world became teeth and hooves and the white flash of a blade.

"Off!" Brannoc bellowed. "Fight on foot! Horses are dead meat in the Narrows!"

Gideon didn't so much dismount as fall, hitting the stony track hard enough to drive the breath from his lungs. He rolled against the canyon wall as a grey rider bore down on him, sword rising—and then Brannoc's axe met the man mid-stroke, shearing through his guard and into his collarbone with a wet crunch that Gideon felt in his own bones. The rider slid past him, close enough to touch, and Gideon saw his eyes: not cruel, just doing what he'd been ordered to do, right up until the moment it killed him.

*This is what the map costs,* Gideon thought, scrambling back against the rock. *This is what it has always cost.*

"MOVE!" Sera hauled him upright by the collar, her face bloodless, her eyes blazing. "You fight or you run, scribe, but you don't stand still and die pretty! Which is it?"

"I—" An arrow shrieked off the stone above his head, spraying grit. "I don't know how to fight!"

"Then RUN!" She shoved him toward a crack in the canyon wall—a seam of shadow where the rock had split, narrow enough that a mounted man couldn't follow. "Up! Get up and keep moving east! We'll hold them here!"

"And leave you—" 

"That wasn't a question!"

But it was, and they both knew it, because in the space of one heartbeat Gideon looked at her—at this woman who had lied to him, used him, bled for him twice now—and understood that he could not take another step without her, and that staying meant dying, and that both of those things were true at once and did not cancel each other out.

"SERA—"

"GO!"

He went.

* * *

*Sera*

Three of them came at her at once, which was flattering, if nothing else.

She gave them the first one honestly—a feint low, blade up through his forearm, a boot to the knee that put him down screaming. The second she gave dishonestly, playing wounded, limping back until he overcommitted and found her knife waiting under his ribs. The third was smarter. He hung back, circling, letting her spend herself, and Sera could feel her brother's wound tearing open inside its bandage with every movement, warmth spreading down her side that had nothing to do with sweat.

*Don't think about it. Think about the blade. Think about the angle. Think about—*

The third man smiled. Behind him, on the slope, a horn sounded—two notes, repeated. Imperial signal. Reinforcements closing.

"Quill," the man said. He knew her name. Of course he did; Voss would have described her, paid for her description. "Inquisitor wants you alive. Him too. Come quiet and you both see spring."

"Generous," Sera panted. "Counteroffer."

She threw her knife at his face. He caught it—actually caught it, quick hands, veteran hands—but the catch cost him half a heartbeat, and half a heartbeat was all she needed to close the distance and put her short blade through the gap beneath his jaw.

He dropped. She stood over him, chest heaving, and looked up the gorge.

Brannoc and his remaining warriors had formed a wall across the track, shields locked, axes flashing over the rims, and they were dying well—each grey rider bought with clan blood, each yard of stone paid for twice. But there were more riders coming. There were always more riders coming. That was the whole arithmetic of empire: they could afford to lose ten men for every one of yours, and they knew it, and they never stopped counting.

A spear took Brannoc's shield-arm at the shoulder. The big man roared and kept fighting one-handed, and Sera saw the end of it written plainly in the geometry of the melee: two minutes, maybe three, and the wall would break, and everything behind it would be open ground.

Everything behind it included Gideon.

She found him thirty yards up the seam in the rock, hauling himself hand over hand along a ledge, coat torn, face grey with terror and effort. Below, two riders had dismounted and were climbing after him, sure-footed as goats.

"GIDEON!" Her voice cracked across the gorge. "LEFT! CLIMB LEFT!"

He turned. Saw them. Made the mistake every untrained man makes—froze, calculating distances he had no talent for measuring.

The first climber drew a knife.

Later, Sera would not be able to reconstruct the decision. There was no decision. There was only the ledge, and the drop, and the angle, and her body committing before her mind filed any objection. She went over the edge of the lower rocks in a controlled slide that wasn't controlled at all, hit the climber from above with both boots, and felt something in her ankle give with a sound like green wood snapping. They went down together onto the track, hard, and she was on her feet first because she had always been on her feet first, that was the whole of her life's strategy—

—and the second climber was already there.

She saw the knife. Of course she saw it. But her ankle folded when she tried to turn, and the world went slow and stupid, and the blade came in low and honest toward Gideon above her, and there was exactly one thing between it and him.

Her.

The point went in under her ribs, sliding along the bone, and it was strange how little it hurt at first—strange and cold, like plunging into the river in thaw-month. The man wrenched the knife free and Sera heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize, and then Gideon was screaming her name and there was a rock in his hand, a plain grey rock, and he brought it down on the back of the knifeman's skull with all the graceless fury of a man who had never hit anything in his life.

Once. Twice. The man fell and did not get up.

"Sera." Gideon was on his knees beside her, hands hovering over the wound, not touching, terrified of touching. Blood was coming fast now, hot between her fingers when she pressed them to it. "Sera, look at me, look at—"

"Took me long enough," she managed. "To make you useful."

"Don't. Don't talk. Brannoc! BRANNOC!"

The battle had turned. Whether it was the horn again—different notes now, deeper—or the war-cries rolling down the gorge from the eastern heights, Sera couldn't say through the roaring in her ears. But grey riders were wheeling, breaking, spurring back the way they'd come, and down the track came a wedge of clan warriors with painted shields and fresh axes, and at their head a woman with a raven feather in her braids whom Brannoc greeted with a shout that was half relief, half rage.

Reinforcements. Clan reinforcements. Too late by exactly one knife wound.

"Sera." Gideon's hands were pressing hers harder against the cut, and his face was wet, and he was talking fast, the way he talked when he was copying, words tumbling. "Stay with me. You stay with me, do you hear me? You don't get to do this. You don't get to bleed for me and then leave, that's—that's not how ledgers work, you owe me, you owe me a confession and a hundred miles and—"

"Ledgers," she whispered. The cold was spreading now, up through her chest, and the gorge was going soft at the edges. "Always... ledgers with you."

"Always," he said fiercely. "So stay in mine."

The last thing she saw before the dark took her was his face above hers, lit by a thin blade of noon sun falling into the Narrows, and it was not the face of the timid scribe she'd found watching her in a dockside tavern. It was someone new. Someone the road had built out of the wreckage of the man she'd been paid to find.

Someone worth the blood, she thought, and then even that was gone.

* * *

*Gideon*

They carried her up out of the gorge in a litter of spears and cloaks, and Gideon walked beside it the whole way with her blood drying on his hands, refusing every offer to ride.

Coric's body they could not recover; the river kept him. Two other clansmen dead. Six imperial riders left on the stones of the Narrows, and their horses scattered into the high country. Brannoc tallied it all in a low voice with the raven-feathered woman—his sister, Maerlin, commander of the Cairn watch—and Gideon caught fragments: *they knew the route, they knew the timing, someone told them our pace.*

His stomach turned over. Voss had scouts. Voss had captured scouts, Maerlin said grimly, taken on the south pass a week ago. Men who knew these trails. Men who could be made to talk.

Which meant the ambush hadn't been a guess. It had been arithmetic. And somewhere behind them, patient as winter, the Inquisitor was still counting.

At dusk they made camp in a hollow below the tree line, and the clan healer—a spare, silent woman named Iva—cut away Sera's coat and worked over her with needles and bitter-smelling paste while Gideon held a lantern and did not look away once, as if looking away might let the wound win.

"She'll live," Iva said at last, "if the fever doesn't take her. The blade missed the liver by a finger's width. Your woman is lucky."

"She's not my—" Gideon started, and stopped, because the word had come out of him too fast and too fierce, and Iva was looking at him with the faintest crease of a smile.

"The fever will speak for her tonight," the healer said. "Fever always tells the truth. Question is whether you're ready to hear it."

They laid Sera near the fire, and Gideon sat beside her through the whole of the night, holding her hand when the shivering took her, wiping her brow when the burning came. And just past the black hour before dawn, true to Iva's promise, the fever began to speak.

"No," Sera muttered, thrashing against some enemy only she could see. "No, you can't have him, he's mine to deliver, I earned him, I *paid*—"

Gideon went very still.

"Tam," she whispered, and the name cracked something open in the dark. "Tam, I'm coming. I'm coming, little brother. Hold on. Hold on, I found a way, I *found*—"

The fire popped. Somewhere above, wind moved through the pines like a long exhale. Gideon sat motionless, her secrets pooling out of her one fevered syllable at a time, and understood that by morning he would know everything she had never told him—and that she would wake knowing he knew, and that whatever stood between them after that would either be stronger than blood or gone forever.

Sera's eyes fluttered. For a moment, through the delirium, they seemed to find his.

"Gideon," she breathed—not the fever now, or not only the fever. "There's something... you need to know. About why I..."

Her eyes closed. Her breathing evened. The fire burned low.

Gideon leaned closer, heart hammering, and waited for dawn to bring the rest of it.
