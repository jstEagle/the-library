## 21. Fevered Confessions

The wound went bad on the second night.

Sera knew the signs the way she knew weather coming off the water—the heat rising off her skin like a kettle's breath, the gorge walls starting to swim and tilt, Gideon's face splitting into two faces and then three whenever he leaned over her. The knife had gone in shallow, thank every crooked god, but shallow cuts in mountain water and mountain filth festered all the same.

They were holed up in a shepherd's bothy above the Narrows, a stone hut with a sagging roof and a fire pit that smoked more than it burned. Brannoc's people had carried her here after the fighting, laid her on a pile of wool blankets that smelled of sheep and juniper smoke, and left them with a clay pot of some bitter paste and instructions in a language Sera didn't speak. Gideon had translated what he could. Boil the paste in water. Bind the wound clean. Change the binding at dawn.

She'd laughed at him when he said *dawn*. She was laughing now too, except the laugh came out wrong, high and thin, and she couldn't seem to stop it.

"Sera." Gideon's hand on her forehead, cool as river stone. "You're burning."

"I'm always burning," she managed. "It's my best quality."

He didn't smile. That was the thing about Gideon Pemberton—she'd spent weeks cataloguing his tells, the way a smuggler catalogs customs men, and she still couldn't get a clean read on him. He should have been furious with her by now. Or terrified. Instead he knelt beside her in the smoky dark with his sleeves rolled up and his hands steady, wringing cloth in boiled water like he'd done it all his life, and maybe he had. Temple scribes learned everything and got paid for none of it.

"Drink." He held a cup to her lips. The broth was thin and tasted of pine, but she drank because her body wanted it more than her pride did.

The fever came in waves. That was the worst part—not the pain, which was honest, but the way the fever pulled things up out of her without asking. Things she'd buried so deep even she'd stopped visiting them.

* * *

The first wave hit near midnight.

She was dreaming, or half-dreaming, of a dock in Corvane with rain hammering the tarps and a boy of nine years old standing at the end of the pier, waving. Not waving goodbye. Waving *come see*, the way children wave when they've found something—a crab, a dead gull, a coin in the mud. His hair was dark like hers. His front teeth were missing.

"Tamsin," she said out loud, and heard herself say it, and hated herself for it.

Gideon looked up from the fire. "Who's Tamsin?"

"No one." Her tongue felt thick. "No one you need."

But the fever had other plans. It always did. She'd seen men spill their whole lives at sea with a bad fever—names, debts, the location of buried cargo—and she'd used it, God forgive her, she'd sat beside dying sailors and listened for exactly this. Now the tide was pulling out of her and there was nothing she could do but watch it go.

"He was nine," she heard herself saying. The firelight swam. "Nine years old and he could gut a fish faster than any man on the quay. I taught him. I taught him everything and then I—" Her breath caught. Somewhere far away, Gideon set down the cup and moved closer. "Then the Temple collectors came through the harbor district. Debt seizure. Our mother owed on father's bond, and they don't take coin anymore, haven't for years. They take *people*. Bonded labor. Children count double because they last longer."

The words kept coming, smooth and terrible, like blood from a reopened cut.

"They took him off the pier. In front of me. I was fourteen and I bit one of them and they nearly took me too, but our mother threw herself between and said take the boy, take just the boy, and they did, because a bargain's a bargain even when it's made of rot." She was crying now, or the fever was; she couldn't tell whose tears those were. "I spent eleven years looking for him. Eleven years, Gideon. Every port, every ledger, every fixer who'd sell information for coin. And do you know what I found?"

Gideon said nothing. He was very still, the way he went still when Voss's name came up, and she thought distantly: *he already knows something is coming. He's braced.*

"Nothing. Eleven years of nothing. Until four months ago, when a broker in Greyharbor found *me*. Said a client was offering what I'd been hunting since I was fourteen years old. The temple registry entry for a bonded boy named Tamsin Quill. Where he was taken. Where he was sent. Whether he lived." Her voice cracked clean through. "All I had to do was find a scribe carrying a map."

* * *

She surfaced sometime after that, into grey pre-dawn light and the taste of pine broth forced gently between her lips. Gideon's face hovered above hers, drawn and sleepless.

"You were talking," he said quietly. "In your sleep."

"Were I." She tried for her usual grin and felt it slide off her face. "Anything incriminating?"

"You tell me."

And there it was. The moment. She'd rehearsed versions of this confession for weeks—in taverns, on trails, in the dark stretches of riding where lying to yourself gets hardest—and in every version she'd controlled it, rationed it, given him only enough truth to keep him moving south with her. But the fever had burned the script off her, and now he was looking at her with those careful grey eyes, and she understood with sudden clarity that if she lied to him now, right now, with her blood poisoning itself in a shepherd's hut, she would lose him. Not to anger. To something worse. To the quiet arithmetic of a man deciding how much a person can be trusted.

So she told him all of it.

"The client," she said. "The one who hired me to find the scribe with the map. I never met them. Messages through brokers, payment in temple-minted silver—that part I didn't question hard enough, and I'll hate myself for that until I die. They promised me Tamsin's registry record. Full record. Location, status, everything." She swallowed. "I found you in the Temple archive district. I watched you for two days before I ever spoke to you. I let you think it was chance on the north road. It wasn't chance. None of it was chance."

The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the mountain breathing.

"And the map," she went on, because stopping now would be worse than finishing. "I've known since Hollowmere what you're carrying. I've been trying to figure out how to take it from you or trade for it or steal it while you slept, and I keep not doing it, and I told myself it was because you'd be useless to me dead or robbed, but that's not true. That's not true, Gideon. I stopped wanting to take it from you somewhere back on the ice, and I don't know what to do with that, because everything I am is built on getting my brother back, and you're—" Her voice failed. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "You're the first person in eleven years who's made me forget, for whole hours at a time, that I'm supposed to be hunting something."

Silence. Long enough that she dared lower her hand.

Gideon was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on him before—not anger, not pity. Something rawer. Like a man hearing his own secret spoken in another voice.

"My sister died in my doorway," he said at last. "A courier named Wren. Bled out on my floor while I hid her body from the Temple bells." He turned the cup slowly in his hands. "Everything I've done since—the lie to Voss, the flight from the city, all of it—I told myself I was running from the Inquisitor. But I think I was running toward whatever she died trying to give me. So no." He looked up. "I'm not going to pretend I don't understand what you did. I understand it better than you know."

"That doesn't make it right."

"No. It doesn't." He set the cup down. "Your client used your brother as bait. Whoever they are, they dangled a child's life in front of you and watched you run. And now they've got a smuggler leading a hunted scribe straight through clan territory toward an artifact the Temple would burn cities to control." His jaw tightened. "You weren't just a hire, Sera. You were a leash. Someone put a collar on you and pointed you at me."

The words landed like cold water, and worse—they landed true. She'd been so busy drowning in guilt she hadn't climbed high enough to see the shape of it. A client with temple silver. A client who knew about the map before the map's own bearer did. A client who knew exactly which hook to bait.

"Voss," she whispered. "Gods below. It's Voss, isn't it? Who else has the registry access, the silver, the reason to—"

"We don't know that."

"We know enough." She tried to sit up and the hut tilted; Gideon's arm was behind her shoulders before she finished falling. For a moment she stayed there, leaning against him, too weak to be proud about it. "If it's him, then Tamsin's record is in his hands right now. Maybe it always was. Maybe there never was a record at all, maybe it was ash and lies from the first message, and I sold you to the man hunting you for a promise written in smoke."

"Hey." His hand found hers—scarred fingers, ink-stained even now, even here. "Look at me."

She looked.

"If it's Voss, we make him give us the record. If the record exists, we use it. If it doesn't exist—if Tamsin's dead, or lost, or grown and unfindable—then we tear the Temple's registries apart page by page until we know either way." His voice was low and absolutely certain, and it was the certainty that undid her, more than any comfort would have. "You confessed. That was the hard part. Now we do the rest together."

"Together," she repeated. The word felt foreign in her mouth, like a coin from a country she'd never visited.

"Together. You and me. We free him." A ghost of a smile crossed his face, tired and real. "But there's a condition."

"Of course there's a condition. There's always a condition with you scribes."

"You survive this fever." He pressed her hand once, hard, then let go and reached for the paste pot and the clean linen. "Because I'm not doing any of it alone. I spent thirty years invisible, Sera. I'm not going back to that. So drink the broth, let me change the binding, and fight. That's the price of the promise."

She laughed—weakly, honestly, for the first time since the knife went in. "Extortion. From a bonded scribe. Your Temple trained you well."

"The Temple trained me to keep ledgers." He dipped the cloth in the cooled decoction and began unwrapping the old binding with a gentleness that contradicted everything she knew about how carefully he guarded himself. "This is just bookkeeping. One debt, one payment. You saved my life twice on the ice. I intend to collect."

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it—faint, from the pass below the Narrows—came a sound that froze them both. Hooves. Many hooves, moving fast, muffled by snow but unmistakable to a woman raised on docks and a man who'd memorized every road that mattered.

Gideon blew out the fire with one motion and pulled his coat over her shoulders, and in the sudden dark his mouth found her ear.

"Whatever happens next," he breathed, "the promise stands. Remember that."

Down in the gorge, the riders began to shout in Imperial cadence, and the mountain answered with horns from the ridge above—Brannoc's horns, deep and angry, waking.
