## 22. The Empire's Version of Peace

The prisoner's name was Aldous Fen, and he had sold grain to clan couriers for eleven years.

Voss knew this because he had read the man's ledgers before the headman of Hollowmere had finished stammering his denials. He knew it the way he knew most things—by sitting still longer than anyone else was willing to sit, by reading what people wrote instead of what they said. Aldous Fen had kept meticulous accounts. Columns of dates, weights, prices. And in a cipher so simple it was almost an insult—a rotation of three letters, the kind of code a child might invent to pass notes in scripture class—the names of every courier who had ever carried a sack of his barley over the high passes into clan territory.

Voss did not need the confession. He had never needed confessions. Confessions were for magistrates and priests, men who required permission before they acted. What Voss needed was confirmation, and the ledgers gave him that, and now Aldous Fen knelt in the mud of his own dooryard with his hands bound behind him while the headman wept and the villagers watched from their doorways with the particular stillness of people who have decided in advance not to remember what they are about to see.

"Inquisitor." The headman again. Old man, wind-burned, sensible hands. "He's fed half this village through two bad winters. Whatever he sent over the mountain, it was flour, it was salt—it was never—"

"It was messages," Voss said. "Messages travel inside sacks as easily as grain does. Sometimes more easily."

"He has children."

"Yes," Voss said. "I saw them at the well."

He let the silence do its work. It always did. Silence was the cheapest instrument in the Temple's arsenal and the most reliable. Men rushed to fill it with things they would otherwise have carried to their graves.

Aldous Fen lifted his head. Mud on his knees, frost in his beard. He looked at Voss with something worse than hatred—with understanding, the terrible calm of a man who had known this day was coming for eleven years and had gone on selling grain anyway.

"You'll want the shrine road," Fen said quietly. "They're going to Thornfell. The scribe and the woman. If you take the low pass you'll lose them by a day and a half."

Voss crouched down to the man's level. Up close, Fen smelled of woodsmoke and turnips and honest labor, which was the thing about traitors that no ballad ever got right. They were almost always ordinary. Almost always tired. Almost always convinced, right up until the end, that they were the reasonable ones.

"Say that again," Voss said.

"The shrine road. Through the Narrows. I've drawn it for your riders before—" Fen stopped himself, but too late, and Voss watched him understand what he'd just handed over: not a plea, not a bargain, but a map. A dying man's last act of spite against the empire that had caught him, or perhaps a father's last attempt to buy his children a gentler winter. With collaborators you could rarely tell the difference, and it didn't matter. It never mattered.

"Take him," Voss said, standing. "Not here. Beyond the tree line. The village shouldn't have to look at it."

* * *

He made himself watch, of course. That was the rule he had set for himself thirty years ago and never broken, not once, not even the times when watching felt like swallowing coals. An Inquisitor of Vessia who ordered deaths he could not stomach was a man building a machine he would one day be fed into. So Voss stood at the edge of the fir trees with his hands folded behind his back and watched the sword fall, and when it was done he said a quiet word over the body—not a prayer; the Temple of Vessia did not pray for traitors, and neither, strictly speaking, did Malachi Voss—but a word nonetheless. A word like a stone laid flat.

Then he walked back down to Hollowmere and told the headman that the empire's gratitude depended on the village remembering nothing, and rode out before the light failed, because there were riders to dispatch and a pass to close, and because if he stayed another hour in that sad grey village he would begin to think about the last time he had stood among firs watching a man die for helping the clans, and that was a country he did not visit willingly.

But the mind is a traitor worse than any grain merchant, and that night, in the officer's tent at the mouth of the low pass, with the wind worrying the canvas and his lieutenants asleep, Voss dreamed of Kestrel Field, and so the memory came whether he willed it or not.

* * *

He had been nineteen. Second spear in Captain Dree's company, which meant he was the youngest man in the line and the last one the veterans bothered to learn the name of. They called him Quill then, because he'd had soft hands and a scholar's stoop and because soldiers are cruel in the small ways that keep them sane. He had hated the name. He would have given both hands, later, to hear someone call him it again.

The uprising had been three years burning before the empire finally answered it properly. Three years of burned tollhouses and murdered tax-men and clan war-bands coming down out of the white like weather, and the imperial answer, when it came, was Dree's company and nine others, marching into the Thornfell foothills in the wet spring to teach the mountains a lesson about arithmetic.

The lesson went badly.

It went badly because the clans did not fight like men who wanted to win. They fought like men who wanted the empire to bleed for every yard of stone it took, and there is a difference, and a nineteen-year-old with a spear learns that difference very quickly. At Kestrel Field—a meadow, truly, nothing more, a green shelf below the pass with a stream running through it where they'd stopped to water the horses—they learned it all at once.

The ambush lasted forty minutes. Voss remembered it in fragments the way he remembered all the worst days: the first arrow taking young Pellit through the throat mid-laugh, so that he died making a sound like a hiccup; the war-ponies coming down the slope with riders lying flat along their necks; Captain Dree shouting the reform and the reform never forming because the line had already come apart like wet rope. He remembered killing his first man, a clan boy hardly older than himself, braids and blue war-paint, and how the boy had looked surprised rather than afraid, and how Voss had knelt beside him afterward in the trampled grass without knowing why, and how Sergeant Hark had hauled him up by the collar and said, *Grieve on your own time, Quill.*

Forty minutes. Then the clans melted back into the high ground, having lost perhaps a dozen men, and left the empire ninety-one dead in the grass.

They buried them for four days.

That was the part no chronicle recorded, the part Voss had never spoken aloud to anyone, not in thirty years, not to superiors, not to priests, not in the dark to himself if he could help it. Four days of digging in ground that fought the spade. Four days of carrying what was left of Pellit and Dree and the twins Ossen and Osk, who had enlisted together and died within arm's reach of each other, so that they buried them in one grave to save the digging and no one thought it strange. Four days of the survivors working in silence because no one had voice enough for anything else, and the stream running red for the first day, and the crows arriving on the second in numbers that seemed less like birds than like a verdict.

On the third day they found the clan dead too—twelve of them, dragged partway up the slope by their own retreating kin and abandoned when the carrying grew dangerous—and a lieutenant whose name Voss had mercifully forgotten ordered them thrown in a pit at the field's edge, unwitnessed, unmarked.

And Voss—who was nobody, who was Quill, second spear, soft hands—had walked to the pit and stood looking down at the braided boy he'd killed, and done nothing. Said nothing. Went back to digging imperial graves.

He had told himself, then, that it was prudence. That a second spear did not contradict lieutenants. That the dead did not care where they lay.

He had been telling himself versions of that sentence ever since, and he had long ago stopped believing any of them, and he had also never once regretted the order, and both of these things were true, and this was the country Malachi Voss lived in: a country with no clean borders, garrisoned by a man who understood exactly what he was.

* * *

Because here was the thing the Temple never put in its catechisms, the thing he had learned at nineteen kneeling in the grass of Kestrel Field with a dead boy's blood cooling on his knuckles:

The Sigil of Auren had ended that war. Not the empire's nine companies. Not the arithmetic of spears. The Sigil—or rather, the fear of it, the memory of it, the old covenant that hung over the mountains like a held breath. The clans had risen because the seal was failing, because the hundred-year renewal had lapsed and the old shrines had gone dark, and the elders had read the silence of their god as license. And when the rising broke against the empire's weight and the mountains filled with graves on both sides, what finally settled the peace was not victory. It was exhaustion, and a treaty written in a hurry, and the mutual unspoken knowledge that the thing beneath Thornfell still slept, and might wake, and that whichever side woke it would inherit a war no treaty could contain.

Voss had spent thirty years studying that peace the way other men studied scripture. He had read every surviving account of the Sigil's last waking, every fragment of keeper-lore the Temple's archivists had hoarded or burned. He knew what the artifact was supposed to be—a seal, a covenant, a binding of willing blood—and he knew what it would become in the hands of men who wanted it to be a weapon, which was every hand that reached for it. Clan elders who dreamed of breaking the empire. Temple hierarchs who dreamed of binding the clans forever. Smugglers who dreamed of gold. Scribes who dreamed, perhaps, of nothing more than freedom.

It did not matter what they dreamed. It mattered what the thing *did*. A seal that renewed itself on blood would renew itself on any blood offered near it, and a power that great, loose in a world of hungry men, would drag the mountains back into the grave-digging years within a season. He had seen the field. He had dug the graves. Ninety-one of them, and twelve more in an unmarked pit, and he was the only man still living who remembered the smell of that meadow in spring, and he would burn every library in the empire before he let it smell that way again.

That was why the courier on the north road had to matter. That was why a scribe with soft hands and a smuggler with a knife wound could not simply be permitted to wander toward Thornfell with a dead woman's map. They thought they were carrying a treasure. They were carrying a fuse, and none of them—not the clans, not the Temple, not the girl with her brother's ghost riding her shoulder—understood what lit fuses did to the people standing closest.

He did not hate Gideon Pemberton. That was the misunderstanding everyone made about him, subordinates and prisoners alike: that Malachi Voss was a man of hatred. Hatred was cheap. Hatred was what the lieutenant at the pit had felt, contempt for the enemy dead. What Voss felt was heavier and colder and far more durable. He had buried friends in a meadow once, and he had watched the peace bought with those graves calcify into something fragile and dishonest and *held*, barely held, by treaties and garrisons and the empire's willingness to look away—and he knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent his whole life reading ledgers, that the peace was an account forever approaching zero. One artifact waking would empty it.

So yes. He had executed Aldous Fen in the trees above Hollowmere, and he had watched, and he had said his one flat word, and he had felt what he always felt, which was nothing that could be called satisfaction and nothing that could be called remorse either. Just the weight. The ledger-weight. Every name entered, every column balanced, and the total always the same: *so that it does not happen again.*

His lieutenants believed he served the empire. Let them. The empire was an instrument, and instruments were useful, but Malachi Voss served the meadow. He served the stream running red. He served the boy with the braids whom he had killed and not mourned and never forgotten, and the pit, and the four days of digging, and the promise a nineteen-year-old had made to the mud without knowing he was making it: *never again, whatever it costs, whoever pays.*

* * *

Near midnight, the tent flap moved and his scout-captain ducked in, snow in her hair, eyes bright with the particular hunger of good news.

"We found the waystation shrine at Ellow burned through—someone passed ahead of us, fast. And the Narrows show fresh tracks, two sets, one favoring a leg." She spread a scrap of hide on the campaign table, marked with charcoal. "Fen's road holds true. We can put riders at the Heartstone plateau within three days. Close the high pass behind them and they're in a sack."

Voss studied the charcoal lines. Somewhere beyond them, a scribe who had lied to his face with trembling lips was climbing toward a door he did not understand, carrying a dead sister's errand in his coat.

"Send six riders ahead," he said. "Light, fast, no banners. They hold the plateau and take the pair alive if it can be managed." He paused. "If it cannot, the map survives them. Nothing else needs to."

"And the clans, sir? Brannoc's war-band will contest the pass."

"Brannoc's warriors will die holding a gorge," Voss said quietly, "and the empire will write that they died rebels, and in fifty years no one will dig their graves at all." He rolled the hide closed. "That is what we are preventing. Go."

She went. The wind pressed at the canvas. Voss sat alone with the lamp and, for the first time in thirty years, took out the small thing he kept sewn into the lining of his field kit: a strip of blue cloth, war-paint stained, cut from a dead boy's braid on a spring morning when he was nineteen and had not yet learned to balance a ledger with lives in it.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he sewed the lining shut again, pinched out the lamp, and began, in the dark, to plan the ending of the world's oldest war—one grave at a time, if that was what it took, beginning with his own.
