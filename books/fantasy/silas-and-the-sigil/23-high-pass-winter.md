## 23. High Pass Winter

The storm came down on them like a fist.

Gideon heard it before he saw it—a low grinding roar from the peaks above the pass, the sound of a mountain clearing its throat. One moment they were picking their way along a shelf of blue ice with the valley yawning below; the next, Sera had his collar in her fist and was hauling him sideways off the trail.

"Cave," she shouted over the wind. "There. Move."

He moved. He had learned by now that when Sera Quill said *move*, you moved first and asked questions after. They scrambled forty yards through thickening white, her hand locked around his wrist, until a black seam opened in the rock face and she shoved him through it into darkness that smelled of old stone and older cold.

Behind them, the storm arrived.

It didn't howl so much as *consume*. The wind took the daylight, the trail, the sound of their own breathing, and ground it all into a white nothing. Gideon sat against the cave wall with his heart hammering and listened to the world end outside.

"Well," Sera said, shaking snow from her hair. "That's us for a while."

* * *

*Three days,* Gideon thought on the second day. Or maybe the third. Time went strange in the cave, measured only by the small rituals Sera imposed: melt snow, drink, eat a strip of dried meat, sleep in shifts, repeat. She ran the shelter like a ship's captain ran a deck, and he found he didn't mind being crewed. It was easier than thinking about what waited at the end of the pass.

The cave was deeper than it first appeared. On the first night Sera explored it by candle stub and found a back chamber where the wind couldn't reach, floored with dry gravel, its ceiling low enough to hold warmth. "Someone sheltered here before," she said, running her fingers over scratches in the wall—tally marks, dozens of them, some ancient enough to be worn smooth. "Clan travelers, maybe. Waiting out weather just like this."

"Did they make it out?"

She glanced at him. "You always ask the cheerful questions."

"It's a skill."

They built a fire at the mouth of the inner chamber, small and disciplined, fed with scrub pine Sera had stripped from a stunted tree near the entrance before the storm sealed them in completely. The smoke found a crack in the ceiling and left. Mostly.

It was on the third evening—or the fourth—that Gideon unwrapped the map again.

He hadn't meant to. But the silence pressed on him, and the map had become a kind of company, the one thing in his life that had never lied to him outright. He spread it across his knees in the firelight, and Sera watched him from across the flames with that look she got—the one that meant she'd already decided to involve herself in whatever he was doing.

"That script," she said. "You actually read it."

"Some of it." He turned the oilcloth toward the light. The symbols crawled along the map's borders like ivy, dense and deliberate. "It's not temple script. It's older. The shrine-tongue, Brannoc called it. I've been working it out since Hollowmere."

"You've been *working it out*." She said it flatly, but he caught the edge underneath. "You've been carrying that thing for weeks, and you never thought to mention you could read it?"

"I can read pieces of it. Slowly. Badly." He hesitated. "And every time I translate something new, I like it less."

Sera shifted around the fire until she was beside him, close enough that he could feel the heat of her shoulder through his coat. "Show me."

So he did.

He started with the basics—the character for *stone*, which looked like three strokes leaning together like tired travelers. The character for *water*, a downward hook with a tail. The character for *blood*, which was the same as the character for *river*, except drawn with a closed loop at the top, as if the river had been stopped up, held, kept.

"Huh," Sera said, watching his finger trace it. "Blood and river. Same word, almost."

"Not same. Related." Gideon frowned at it. "In temple script we'd call that a corruption. In this script I think it's the whole point. Blood *is* a river, to whoever wrote this. Something that flows from a source and has to keep flowing or—" He stopped.

"Or what?"

"Or everything downstream goes dry."

The fire popped. Outside, the storm ground on, patient as a siege.

"Teach me," Sera said.

He looked at her. In the firelight her face was all angles and shadows, the scar through her eyebrow pale as a scratch on glass. "Why? You sold passage on smuggling runs for ten years without learning a word of any dead language."

"Because," she said slowly, as if choosing each word from a narrow shelf, "you know things I don't, and I hate that. And because if something happens to you on that mountain, someone else should be able to read the damn map." She met his eyes. "I'm not losing this lead because you fall down a crevasse, scribe."

"There it is," Gideon said. "The sentiment. Buried under the commerce, where you keep it."

"Shut up and teach me the river-blood word."

* * *

*Sera*

He was a better teacher than she expected. That annoyed her, in the way that everything about Gideon Pemberton annoyed her lately—quietly, persistently, in a part of herself she preferred not to examine.

He didn't lecture. He made her do it. He drew the characters in the ash at the fire's edge with a stick and made her copy them until her fingers were grey, then made her sound them out, then made her string them into the short phrases that recurred along the map's border like a refrain.

"*Stone remembers. Water forgets.*" She sounded it out, halting. "What does that even mean?"

"I don't know yet. It might be poetry. It might be instructions." Gideon traced the phrase again. "Old scripts blur the line. Temple liturgy does the same thing—half of our rites are just very old directions for maintaining buildings, dressed up in incense."

"You're telling me your holy texts are maintenance manuals?"

"A surprising amount of holiness is maintenance." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a joke she'd ever gotten out of him unprompted, and she found herself absurdly pleased by it, and annoyed at being pleased.

They worked until the candle died. When he finally slept, curled near the fire with the map rewrapped against his chest, Sera sat awake and studied the characters she'd copied into her own memory, turning them over like stolen coins.

*Stone remembers.*

Her brother Tam had been seven when the Temple took him. Seven years old, with a stammer and a habit of collecting river stones, lining them up on the windowsill of their mother's house in Salt Harbor. She'd been twelve. She remembered the grey coats, the ledger book, the clerk's bored voice reading out the terms of indenture as if he were reading a laundry list. She remembered Tam looking back at her over his shoulder, and she remembered that she hadn't waved. She hadn't waved because she was crying too hard to lift her arm, and she had hated herself for twenty years because of it.

Somewhere in the Temple archives, if the client was telling the truth, there was a record of what became of him. A name in a column. Maybe a grave. Maybe—not. She'd let herself imagine *not* exactly once, on the boat north, and had spent the rest of the voyage punishing herself for the hope.

The client had promised her the record. All she had to do was find the scribe with the map and take it from him.

She looked at Gideon sleeping in the firelight, his face slack and younger than he ever let it be awake, his hands ink-stained even now, even here, as if the ink had soaked all the way through him years ago and would never come out.

*Take it from him.* That had been the job. Clean and simple.

Instead she was sitting in a cave learning a dead language from him, and the job felt like a word in a script she couldn't read anymore.

*Stone remembers,* she thought bitterly. *Let's hope not.*

* * *

*Gideon*

On the fifth day the storm showed no sign of ending, and the meat was half gone, and Sera announced that he was going to learn to climb ice.

"No," Gideon said.

"Yes."

"Sera. Look at me. Look at these hands." He held them up. They were scribe's hands, long-fingered and soft-palmed, hands that had held pens for fifteen years and had never once been asked to hold anything heavier. "These hands are for ledgers."

"These hands are going to get you killed on the Heartstone approach if you can't pull yourself up a frozen waterfall." She tossed him a coil of rope. "The last stretch to the plateau is ice. Brannoc told you that himself. You think the clan warriors climb it with style? No. They climb it slow and ugly, and you're going to learn slow and ugly right here, where falling means landing on gravel instead of landing on nothing."

"I'll land on gravel *repeatedly*."

"Yes," she said cheerfully. "That's the method."

There was a chimney of rock at the cave's rear, a vertical crack where meltwater seeped and froze into a ribbed sheet of blue-green ice. Sera drove two pitons into the stone above it, rigged a belay line, and demonstrated: feet flat, weight on the legs, swing the tool, commit. She made it look like walking. Then she came down, handed him the tools, and stood holding the rope with an expression of profound and cheerful skepticism.

Gideon swung the tool. It bounced off.

"Commit," Sera said.

He swung again. It stuck, barely. He pulled himself up six inches, his boots skittering, and gravity made its opinion known. The gravel broke his fall, as promised. So did his elbow, his hip, and his dignity.

"Again," Sera said.

Again. Again. By the tenth attempt he reached arm's height. By the thirtieth—he lost count somewhere in the failing light—he hauled himself to the top of the chimney, hung there gasping, and discovered that he was laughing. Actually laughing, breathless and disbelieving, dangling from two iron tools in a frozen cave at the top of the world.

"Don't get cocky," Sera called up. "Come down before your arms figure out what you did."

He came down, slower and uglier than he'd gone up, and collapsed onto the gravel, and she dropped the rope and sat beside him, and for a moment neither of them said anything. His forearms trembled. His palms were raw. He felt, bizarrely, wonderful.

"My mother tried to teach me to swim once," he said. "In the mill pond, the summer before she died. I screamed the whole time. She said—" He paused. The memory had resurfaced during the clan moot and had been surfacing ever since, piece by piece, rearranging itself. "She said, *the water won't hold you if you fight it.*"

"And you fought it."

"Every stroke." He flexed his ruined hands. "I've been fighting everything my whole life, I think. The debt. The Temple. Every door that opened, I assumed was a trap."

Sera was quiet for a moment. "And now?"

"Now I climbed a wall of ice because a smuggler yelled at me." He shook his head. "I don't know what that makes me."

"Tired," she said. "It makes you tired. Eat something."

But she was smiling when she said it, and he caught it, and neither of them looked away fast enough.

* * *

*Sera*

That night she woke to hear him talking in his sleep.

Not words, at first—just sounds, low and rhythmic, a lullaby in the clan tongue. He'd been humming it for days without noticing, she realized. The song his mother sang him. The song that had made the seer at High Cairn go still and stare at him like a ghost walking.

Then, clearer: "*Wren. Wren, wait—*"

His voice cracked on the name and he rolled toward the fire, and Sera was beside him before she'd decided to move, her hand on his shoulder. He surfaced slowly, blinking, disoriented, and for a heartbeat he looked at her without any of his walls up at all, and what was behind them was so bare and young that it hurt to see.

"You called out a name," she said quietly.

"Wren." He rubbed his face. "The courier. The woman who died at my door." He stared into the fire. "I dreamed she was teaching me the script. She was impatient with me. She kept saying I already knew it, I just wouldn't admit it." A hollow laugh. "Dreams are stupid."

"Dreams are honest," Sera said. "It's waking up that lies."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she understood that she had just said something true enough to frighten them both. The fire ticked. Somewhere far above, the storm had finally begun to lose its grip—the wind dropping, the pressure easing, the mountain exhaling.

"Sera." Her name, plain, no irony attached. "Why are you really here? Not the paid-to-find-the-map answer. The real one."

The question hung between them like the last smoke. She could lie. Lying to him had been easy for weeks; it was practically her native language. But he had just climbed a wall thirty times because she told him to, and he had laughed at the top of it, and he had called out his dead sister's name in his sleep, and lying suddenly felt like a stone she was tired of carrying.

"Someone I love is in the Temple's books," she said. "My brother. Taken when he was seven. Someone promised me his records—what happened to him—if I brought them the map." She watched his face carefully. "I didn't take the job planning to like you."

"No," Gideon said softly. "I don't imagine you did."

"I still might give you to them. If it comes to that. I need you to know that."

"I know." And the worst part, the part she'd remember, was that he did know—and stayed sitting beside her anyway, close enough that his shoulder rested against hers, as if her honesty were a door he'd rather walk through than away from.

"We'll find him," he said. "When this is done. Whatever's in those books—we'll find it together."

She didn't answer. She couldn't. She leaned her head against his shoulder instead, just for a moment, just because the storm was ending and the morning would demand everything of them both, and for one night more she wanted to be a person who was allowed to lean.

* * *

*Gideon*

The storm broke on the seventh day.

They emerged into a world remade—white and blinding and silent, the pass buried, the sky scoured clean and hard as glazed pottery. The air tasted like metal and mercy. Sera stood at the cave mouth breathing it in like a woman coming up from deep water.

"Two days' climb to the shrine pass," she said. "If the weather holds."

Gideon nodded and checked the map one last time. Along its lower border, where days ago there had been only tangled symbols, he could now read a full line, translated in the long cave hours, in ash and candlelight and Sera's stubborn voice making him say the words aloud:

*The seal is renewed by willing blood of the keeper's line, freely given beneath the Heartstone.*

He read it twice. Three times. Then he wrapped the map away against his chest, where it lay over his heart like a second heartbeat, and said nothing.

Below them, far down the valley they'd crossed a week ago, something glinted on the white slope. Not ice. Not snow-glare.

Steel. A thin line of it, moving. Riders, many riders, fanning out across the lower pass with the discipline of soldiers following orders sent ahead of them.

Sera saw it a moment later. Her jaw tightened.

"They knew the route," she said. "Before we did."

Gideon touched the map through the oilcloth and thought of Voss, and of willing blood, and of the two days of climbing between here and the Heartstone.

"Then we stop being behind," he said, and started up.
