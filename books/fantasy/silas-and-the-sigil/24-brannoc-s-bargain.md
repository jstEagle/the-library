## 24. Brannoc's Bargain

The fire in Brannoc's longhouse burned low and blue, fed by peat that smelled of the sea though the sea was three days behind us. I sat where he'd pointed me—a three-legged stool near the door, close enough to the cold to remember my place—and watched him turn the map over in hands like tree roots.

He hadn't asked for it. That was the first thing. Sera had laid it on the table herself, an hour ago, when the moot's last stragglers had drifted out into the snow and left only the chief, his two shield-kin, and us. She'd unrolled it with the confidence of a woman presenting a bill of sale, and I'd wanted to grab her wrist and stop her, and hadn't.

Now Brannoc studied it the way a man studies a wound—looking for how deep it goes.

"You say this came from a dying courier," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "On the north road. In empire territory."

"That's right," I said.

"And you carried it through my mountains without telling me what it was."

"I didn't know what it was. Not all of it." That much was true. What I knew of the rest—the Heartstone, the hundred years, the willing blood—sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and I pressed it down again, the way I'd been pressing it down since the frozen shrine at Ellow.

Brannoc looked up. His eyes were the pale grey of glacier ice, and they did not blink often enough to be comfortable. "A scribe who lies badly," he said. "That's a rare thing. Most men lie well or not at all."

Sera shifted beside me. Her arm was still bound from the Narrows; she'd refused the clan healers twice, which I understood better now than I had then. "We're not here to be interrogated, Brannoc. We're here because your scouts pulled us out of a gorge and your moot couldn't decide whether to kill us. We've been patient. Be patient back."

"Patience is for people with time." He set the map down flat and put one thick finger on the mountain range drawn across its middle, on the peak marked with the spiral symbol that had followed us for two hundred miles. "You know what this is?"

"A shrine," I said carefully. "An old one."

"It's *our* shrine." The word cracked like a whip on the table. One of the shield-kin flinched; the other went very still. "Before your empire drew its lines on paper, before Vessia's bells rang in any valley of these hills, the clans kept the high places. Every spring-tide, every solstice, we climbed. And then the empire came with its roads and its taxes and its god of ledgers—" his eyes flicked to me, deliberate, contemptuous, "—and told us the high places were closed. Told us our own gods were superstition. Put garrisons on the passes so we couldn't even bury our dead where our dead had always gone."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be either a lie or an apology, and I'd spent enough lies for one lifetime already.

"So." Brannoc leaned forward, and the firelight made his shadow climb the smoke-blackened rafters like something waking. "If there is a thing under that mountain—a sigil, a seal, whatever your dead courier died carrying the key to—it belongs to the clans. It was taken from us with everything else. You will give it to us when you find it."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind worried the eaves.

"That's not how it works," Sera said.

"It is exactly how it works. You are in my territory, under my protection, eating my food. Protection has a price."

"Protection has a price," Sera agreed, "but you don't get to name it after the fact. That's not protection, that's robbery with better manners."

One of the shield-kin laughed—a short bark, quickly smothered. Brannoc didn't smile. "You have a sharp tongue, smuggler. I've known men hanged for less."

"I've known men hanged for more. It never improved their manners either."

I should have said something. Instead I was looking at the map—at the spiral, at the little cluster of edge-symbols the old woman in Hollowmere had gone pale reading—and thinking about the way everyone who touched this thing reached for it like a drowning man reaching for a rope. Voss wanted it destroyed because he believed its return meant war. Sera wanted it because someone had promised her a brother. The clans wanted it because it had been stolen along with everything else. And Wren—

Wren had wanted it found because it needed finding. Because someone had to carry the weight. I still didn't understand her fully. Maybe I never would.

But Brannoc was waiting, and silence in a negotiation is just a debt accruing interest.

"Chief Brannoc," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, "may I ask you something?"

He grunted, which I took as permission.

"When you say the sigil belongs to the clans—what do you think it *is*?"

His jaw worked. "A weapon. What else would the old kings have buried under a mountain? A power strong enough to break the empire's hold. To make them give back what they took." He said it plainly, without shame, and I realized he wasn't lying either. He believed it the way Voss believed in destruction: completely, and with his whole grief behind it.

"And if it isn't a weapon?"

"Then it's worthless and you can keep it." He almost smiled. Almost. "But nothing is buried under a mountain for a hundred years unless it's worth killing for. Your empire taught us that lesson well."

*It's not a weapon,* I wanted to say. *It's a lock, and the lock needs a key, and the key is blood, and the blood is mine.* But saying it would mean explaining why I knew. It would mean the locket, the seer at High Cairn, the Pemberton crest on a door no Pemberton should have a claim to. It would mean handing Brannoc the one piece of leverage that mattered more than the map itself: that I could open what no one else could, and therefore I was worth more alive than the map was worth at all.

Or worse—that I was worth more dead, delivered to the right place at the right time.

Sera caught my eye. Just a flicker, just a half-second, but she'd gotten good at reading me in the weeks since the cave, and I saw the question form behind her eyes: *What aren't you saying?*

I looked away first.

"The moot gave you passage to the shrine pass," Brannoc said into the quiet. "That stands. My word doesn't bend with the weather. But hear the terms plain, scribe, because I'll only say them once." He rose, and up close he was enormous, a wall of wool and scarred leather. "You find this thing. You bring it down off the mountain—to me, not to your temple, not to your smuggler's buyer, not to any grave of your own choosing. The clans will decide what's done with it. Do this, and you'll have land, protection, a place among us if you want it. Refuse—" he shrugged, vast and terrible, "—and the pass south closes. Winter's coming. You've seen what winter does in the high country."

He was right. I had seen it. I'd nearly died of it twice.

"We need time to consider," Sera said.

"You have until dawn. After that, my scouts ride with you or against you." He rolled the map and held it out—not to Sera, but to me. I took it. Our fingers didn't touch; he made sure of that, releasing it the moment I had weight of it. "One more thing, scribe. The seer at High Cairn—Maeve. She looked at your mother's locket and went quiet for a full day. Maeve hasn't gone quiet in forty years. Whatever you're carrying besides that map, it's older and heavier than you let on." He leaned down, close enough that I could smell peat smoke and iron in his beard. "I don't need to know what it is. But I'll know if you lie to me about it. Clan noses are good for that kind of hunting."

Then he straightened, dismissed us with a flick of two fingers, and turned back to the fire as if we were already gone.

* * *

Outside, the night was the hard clean black of high altitude, stars sharp enough to cut. Snow squeaked underfoot. Sera walked beside me toward the guest hut they'd given us—a turf-roofed cell barely bigger than a coffin, but warm, and ours for one more night—and for a hundred paces neither of us spoke.

"He wants a weapon," she said finally.

"He wants his mountains back. He thinks the sigil is the lever."

"And is it?"

I stopped walking. The guest hut was twenty feet away, its single window glowing dull orange, and I stood in the cold between the firelight and the dark and tried to find a version of the truth I could survive telling.

"Sera."

"Don't." Her voice was soft, which was worse than loud. Soft meant she'd been rehearsing. "Don't do the thing where you go quiet and start composing. I've watched you do it since Ellow. Since before Ellow. You've got a face you make when you're holding something back, and you've been making it for two hundred miles, and I am *tired*, Gideon. I bled for you in that gorge. I told you things in that fever I've never told anyone living. So whatever Brannoc just saw in you that made him lean down and growl—I want to know if I'm the only person on this mountain who doesn't know."

The stars were very bright. My breath hung in front of me like a small ghost.

"If I tell you," I said, "it changes things. Between us. Maybe past fixing."

"Try me. I've had things change past fixing before. I'm still standing."

And I believed her. That was the terrible part. Somewhere between the avalanche and the fever and the long stormbound days teaching each other scripts and knots and the names of each other's scars, I had come to believe her completely—which meant the lie I was keeping wasn't protecting her anymore. It was only protecting me from the look that would come into her face when she understood what the translation at Ellow actually said. *Renewed every hundred years by willing blood.* Willing. As if the mountain could taste the difference. As if reluctance would sour the rite.

She thought I was carrying a treasure. She was planning, in whatever corner of herself she planned such things, how to trade it for her brother. Brannoc thought I was carrying a weapon. Voss thought I was carrying a plague. And none of them—not one—had guessed I was carrying myself, folded up small, addressed to a door beneath a mountain.

"Not tonight," I said. It came out rougher than I intended. "Tomorrow. When we're moving. If I tell you in this hut, with those walls that thin, and it goes wrong—I can't run anywhere. Give me the trail. Give me somewhere I can walk while you hate me."

Her eyes narrowed. "Hate you."

"Or whatever comes after."

For a long moment she just looked at me, and the wind moved her hair across the bandage on her arm, and I thought she was going to push—she always pushed, it was her best and worst quality—but instead she exhaled through her nose and nodded once, sharp.

"Dawn, then. Brannoc gets his answer, and I get mine. In that order." She started toward the hut, then paused with her hand on the door. "Gideon. Whatever it is—you should know I've already decided it doesn't change anything. So you can stop rehearsing."

She went inside. I stayed out a while longer, because the cold helped, because the cold was honest.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on the narrow bunk with the map across my knees and the locket open in my palm, and I did what I had done my whole bonded life: I counted.

Counted the ways out. There were none that ended with me walking free—if the rite required what the script said it required, then freedom was never on the table, only the manner of surrender. Counted the ways the sigil could end up in other hands. Brannoc's, if I gave it to him—and what would the clans do with a seal they couldn't renew? Prop it up in some high place and pray at it while it faded, another dead god, another grievance to hand their grandchildren? Sera's, traded to a client who was probably a fiction built around a promise someone never meant to keep? Voss's—no. Never Voss's. That road ended with the sigil broken and the mountain's peace broken with it, and ten thousand soldiers marching north to teach the clans the empire's version of quiet.

Everyone wanted it. That was the lesson of the whole journey, written in every campfire from the Temple district to this turf-roofed longhouse. The map attracted hunger the way a wound attracts flies. Empire hunger, clan hunger, smuggler hunger, grief hunger—they all wore different faces and they all reached for the same thing, and not one of them, I'd have bet my life on it, and possibly already had, knew what the thing actually cost.

Only Wren had known. Only Wren had carried it knowing, and died in a stranger's doorway rather than let it fall into the wrong hands, and sent it—sent *me*—the rest of the way with a letter she hadn't lived to finish.

*Forgive me for the weight.*

I closed the locket. Through the thin wall I could hear Sera breathing, slow and even, feigning sleep or finding it. Dawn was maybe four hours off. Brannoc would stand in his doorway with the firelight behind him and demand his answer, and I would have to give him one that kept us alive and moving and gave away nothing, and then on the trail I would have to give Sera the truth I'd been choking on since Ellow, and then—

And then the pass. And the door. And whatever waited behind it wearing my family's crest.

I sewed the map back into my coat lining with numb fingers, stitch by stitch, the way my mother must once have sewn secrets into hers, and outside the wind came down off the peaks carrying the faintest sound—so faint I might have invented it—of riders on the southern trail.

Not Brannoc's riders. They'd have used the high path.

Someone else was climbing toward the shrine pass in the dark, and they weren't slowing down for the weather.
