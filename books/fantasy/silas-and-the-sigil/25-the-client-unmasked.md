## 25. The Client Unmasked

The hawk came at dusk, dropping out of a sky the color of old pewter, and Sera knew before it landed that nothing good traveled on wings that tired.

She was sitting the second watch on a ledge above the waystation shrine, Gideon asleep below with Brannoc's warriors ranged around him like stones around a hearthfire. The bird spiraled twice, screamed once—a sound she felt in her back teeth—and settled on the message post they'd rigged between two pines three days ago, when Brannoc had grudgingly allowed them access to the clan relay lines.

Sera didn't move for a long moment. She watched the hawk instead, the way it preened and waited, unbothered, trained to patience by someone who paid well for patience.

Messages came to her all the time. Half her trade ran on birds: route changes, prices, warnings about which passes had grown teeth. But this one had flown through clan territory, over mountain walls in winter, which meant either extraordinary money or extraordinary desperation, and both of those wore the same face from where she sat.

She climbed down, took the tube from the hawk's leg, and fed the creature dried meat from her pack while she worked the wax seal loose with her thumbnail.

The seal wasn't wax, actually. It was resin, dark and hard, stamped with no sigil at all—which was its own kind of signature. Anonymous clients loved anonymity the way drunk men loved knives: it made them feel powerful right up until it got somebody hurt.

*Quill.*

*You have served faithfully and I honor that. The contract stands as written. Deliver the scribe and the map to the shrine pass before the first thaw, and your brother Tam will be returned to you whole.*

*I write now because circumstances have changed and you deserve honesty, which is rare currency in our trade.*

*Your original employer is dead. He died in Vessia City eleven days ago, throat cut in his own counting house, and his papers passed to me. Among those papers was your name and your price. I have honored both. You need never know who he was or why he wanted what he wanted. That knowledge would only endanger you.*

*What you should know is this: the Temple's Inquisitor rides for Thornfell with forty men and a writ that authorizes anything. If he reaches the Sigil before we do, there will be no bargain for your brother, because there will be no brother. Voss does not negotiate. Voss erases.*

*Move faster. Trust no one in Hollowmere. And do not let the scribe read the last chamber's script before I arrive—I will explain when I see you.*

*—A friend of Tam's*

Sera read it four times.

Then she read it a fifth time, slower, the way she'd been taught to read cargo manifests and customs writs, looking not at what was said but at what the saying revealed.

*A friend of Tam's.* Nobody was a friend of Tam's. Tam had been taken by the Temple when he was seven years old, one of the bonded children swept up in the debt purges of Harrow's Crossing, and in the nineteen years since, Sera had found exactly two people who even remembered his name: herself, and whoever had hired her last autumn with an offer too clean to refuse. *Find the scribe with the map. Bring him to Thornfell. Your brother walks free.*

She'd asked who they were. She'd been told it didn't matter.

Now this.

She turned the strip of paper over. Clan-relay paper, rough and grey-brown. The hand was neat, disciplined, the letters formed with the kind of precision that came from years of enforced practice—scribe's training, temple training, the handwriting of a man who'd been made to copy scripture until his wrist forgot any other motion.

Her stomach went cold and kept going.

There were maybe four hundred people in the empire with handwriting like that, and most of them were locked behind temple walls copying ledgers. Of the rest—the ones who'd graduated from copying to *commanding*—she could count the ones who'd care about a smuggler's contract on one hand.

And one of them had been in Vessia City eleven days ago. One of them had audited the Temple of Vessia personally, room by room, ledger by ledger, with authority to open every drawer and read every letter. One of them had stood across from Gideon Pemberton in a candlelit archive and offered to erase his debt in exchange for information, and been refused.

One of them had then intercepted a dead man's correspondence and found, folded among the shipping contracts and bribed-customs receipts, a smuggler named Quill and a price list that included a stolen brother.

Sera looked up at the darkening sky, at the first stars pricking through, and laughed—one short, ugly bark of a laugh that startled the hawk off its perch.

Voss. Malachi Voss. The Inquisitor himself had been her client since the beginning. Every mile she'd walked beside Gideon, every fire she'd shared, every night she'd talked him past his fear of the mountains—she'd been doing the Temple's work with her own two hands. She hadn't been shadowing the hunt. She *was* the hunt. The leash around Gideon's neck had run straight through hers, and she'd never once felt the pull because she'd been walking so eagerly toward the bait.

*Tam.*

Except—

She forced herself still. Forced the panic down into a box and sat on the lid, because panic was how people like Voss won. Read it again. Read what isn't said.

*Deliver the scribe and the map to the shrine pass.* Fine. Consistent with the original contract.

*Your brother Tam will be returned to you whole.* Returned. From where? Nineteen years in a temple bond-house and nobody had ever used the word *returned*. Bonded children weren't returned. They were worked until their debts converted to something permanent, or they died of the damp fever, or—in the rarest cases—they aged out into temple service and vanished into the hierarchy like a coin dropped down a well.

And if Tam had been in the Temple's hands all along—if Voss had known where he was since the day the boy was taken—then the promise had never cost Voss anything. It was a voucher drawn on money that was already his. Which meant the promise was worth exactly as much as Voss decided it was worth, at exactly the moment he decided it.

Which meant—

*He died in Vessia City eleven days ago, throat cut in his own counting house.*

Eleven days ago. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Eleven days ago she and Gideon had been two days out of Hollowmere. Voss had arrived in Hollowmere six days ago. The timing fit a man who'd killed his intermediary, seized his papers, and ridden hard to catch up to his own conspiracy before it walked off the edge of his map.

Or it fit a man telling whatever lie kept a useful tool moving forward.

Because here was the thing that scraped at her like a burr under a saddle blanket: *do not let the scribe read the last chamber's script before I arrive.*

Why? What was in the last chamber that a man willing to burn forty riders' lives on a mountain pass didn't want a debt-bonded scribe to understand?

Sera thought about the way Gideon had gone quiet at the frozen shrine of Ellow. Not frightened-quiet. *Resigned*-quiet. She'd seen that quiet before, in sailors reading weather, in debtors reading ledgers, in people who had just done arithmetic they wished they hadn't. She'd assumed it was the weight of the mission. Now she wondered what page he'd been reading.

She wondered what page *she'd* been reading, and whether she'd had it upside down the whole time.

* * *

"You're making that face."

Gideon was awake. Of course he was—he slept like a cat in a strange house, one ear always open, a habit she recognized because she owned it too. He'd built a small fire in the lee of the shrine wall, shielded by stone so its light wouldn't carry, and he was feeding it twigs with the careful economy of a man who'd spent his life being charged for everything he consumed.

"What face?"

"The one where you've read a letter and hated every word and are deciding how much of it to tell me." He didn't look up from the fire. "I'm a scribe, Sera. Reading people is just another script. Yours has gotten easier to translate lately."

"That so." She crouched across the fire from him and held the message out. "Read it."

He took it. She watched his eyes move, watched them stop at the same place hers had stopped—*a friend of Tam's*—and continue, and stop again at the end, and come back to the beginning. Watched his jaw tighten at *do not let the scribe read the last chamber's script*, because he was holding the last chamber's translation in his head already, had carried it since Ellow, and they both knew it.

"Voss," he said quietly.

"The seal's anonymous. The hand is temple-trained. The timing fits his ride from Hollowmere." She shrugged like it cost her nothing. "Could be coincidence. Could be some other grey-robed vulture with identical penmanship."

"It's not coincidence." Gideon folded the message along its existing creases, precisely, and handed it back. "When he questioned me in Vessia, he knew things about the north road he shouldn't have known unless someone had told him a courier was coming. Someone inside the smuggling network. Someone whose name would be in a broker's papers." His eyes lifted to hers, and there was no accusation in them, which was somehow worse. "I wondered, back then. How he knew. I told myself it didn't matter."

"Well. Mystery solved. Congratulations to us both." She fed the message to the fire and watched the resin-stamp curl black. "I've been working for the man hunting you since before I met you. I tracked you, I intercepted you on the trail, I—" Her voice snagged on something and she pushed past it. "Every kindness I did you on this mountain, I did on his coin. That's the shape of it. Say something."

Gideon was quiet for a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere below, one of Brannoc's sentries coughed.

"I say we use it," he said finally.

She blinked. "Use it."

"He thinks you're his. He thinks you'll deliver me to the shrine pass and stand aside while his riders take the ground." Gideon leaned forward, firelight carving his face into planes and hollows she'd learned the way she'd learned the map's script—slowly, then all at once. "So we let him think it. We know where he'll be, when he'll be there, and what he expects to find. That's not a leash anymore, Sera. That's a rope with both ends in our hands."

"And Tam?" The word came out flat and hard. "His voucher says my brother walks free if I deliver you. His *other* possibility—the one he doesn't put in writing—is that Tam's been dead for fifteen years and the promise was always ash. He wrote *or so I claim* in invisible ink between every line, Gideon. I can't tell which parts are true. That's the whole design of it."

"No," Gideon said. "It's not."

"How would you know?"

"Because I spent nine years learning how the Temple writes." He reached into his coat and drew out the map—not the oilcloth original, which stayed wrapped against his heart, but the copy he'd made at High Cairn, ink on treated linen, every symbol rendered in his own precise hand. "Temple documents are built like fortresses. Everything true is load-bearing. Everything false is decoration. A lie that carries weight—like *your brother will be returned*—is expensive to construct, because if it collapses, the whole structure falls and the reader stops believing anything else on the page. Voss wouldn't spend that kind of lie unless the promise was real enough to keep you moving."

"So you're saying he believes he can produce Tam."

"I'm saying he believes he can produce *something*." Gideon's voice dropped. "And I'm saying that whatever he produces, he'll produce it after he has the Sigil, and not one breath before. Promises from men like that are payments made in arrears, and the arrears are always adjusted."

Sera stared into the fire. Nineteen years. Nineteen years of keeping Tam's name alive in the only place it still existed, which was her mouth, and now the name lived in an Inquisitor's correspondence too, stamped and sealed and weaponized, and she couldn't decide whether that was the cruelest thing she'd ever heard or the closest anyone had ever come to giving her brother back.

"There's one more thing," she said. "The part where he tells me not to let you read the last chamber. You've read it. What's he afraid of?"

Gideon didn't answer immediately. He looked at the fire, and she saw him weigh something, and she saw him decide—she actually saw the decision happen, the small settling of his shoulders, the way his hands went still.

"When we get there," he said, "you'll read it yourself. All of it. I'll make sure of that."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that doesn't change what happens next." He met her eyes. "If I tell you now, you'll try to stop me. And it can't be stopped, Sera. It can only be done by me or not done at all, and if it's not done, Voss wins, and the clans burn, and everything Wren bled out on my doorstep to set in motion dies with us in this snow. So. When we get there."

*Wren.* He said the name differently now than he had a month ago. Like a wound he'd stopped trying to close and started trying to carry.

Sera wanted to shake him. Wanted to grab that careful scribe's collar and demand the whole truth, right now, in the firelight, consequences be damned. She'd earned it. She'd bled for it—there was still a ridge of scar tissue under her ribs where a knife had found her instead of him at the Narrows.

But she'd also spent her whole life reading contracts, and she knew what a conditional clause looked like on a human face. He'd give her the truth at the shrine. Whatever it was. And between here and there, pushing him would only teach him to hide it better.

"Fine," she said. "When we get there."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm furious with you. I'm furious with Voss. I'm mostly furious with myself." She pulled her cloak tighter. "But here's what I know that your fortress-documents don't cover. Voss wrote that Tam would be *returned whole*. Present tense plan, future tense delivery. Men don't phrase promises like that about dead people. Dead people get soft language—*at peace*, *beyond suffering*, *in a better place*. Voss is a professional liar and he chose the phrasing of a live hostage." She jabbed a stick into the coals. "Either Tam's alive, or Voss wants me to believe it badly enough that he studied how hope sounds. And I've met men who studied how hope sounds. They're rarer than the other kind. Rarer, and worse."

Gideon was silent. Then: "Which do you believe?"

"I believe I'm going to find out." She stood, brushing snow from her knees. "And I believe that when I do, Malachi Voss is going to wish he'd never learned to write."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat the rest of the watch with the hawk's empty perch in view and the burned message's ghost behind her eyes, turning the pieces.

Near dawn, the scout Brannoc had posted on the southern shoulder came down at a run, breath smoking, and the camp came awake around her in the particular silence of armed men waking.

"Riders," the scout said. "South pass. Forty, maybe more. Banners furled but the lead standard's grey." He spat. "They're not hurrying. They're herding. Like they want us to see them coming."

Grey. Voss rode under grey, the color of ash and accounting.

Sera found Gideon already standing, the oilcloth map's outline visible against his chest where his coat hung open, his face pale and set in the blue pre-dawn light. Behind him, the peaks of Thornfell rose black against the fading stars, and somewhere up there, higher than any of them had yet climbed, the Heartstone waited under its hundred years of ice with whatever it held—weapon, seal, or grave.

"He's early," Gideon said.

"He's *on schedule*," Sera corrected. "We're late. There's a difference, and the difference is that he thinks I'm still his." She swung her pack onto her shoulder and felt, absurdly, lighter than she had in weeks—as if the letter had burned away something heavier than paper. "So we climb. Today. We beat his column to the plateau, we open whatever door your blood opens, and when Malachi Voss arrives expecting a delivered scribe and a broken woman—"

She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, and she was glad there was no one but Gideon to see it.

"—he finds out what happens when you put a leash on a Quill."

Above them, the hawk screamed once and wheeled south, toward the riders, carrying nothing at all.
