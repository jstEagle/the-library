## 26. Heartstone Ascent

**Gideon**

The mountain stopped pretending to be climbable two hours past dawn.

What had been a trail became a suggestion, and what had been a suggestion became a wall of grey stone streaked with ice, rising so steeply that Gideon had to press his cheek against the rock to keep his balance. Above them the Heartstone plateau was still invisible, wrapped in cloud, but he could feel it now — or imagined he could — a pressure behind his sternum like a second heartbeat that wasn't entirely his own.

The map's script had burned itself into his memory over the long winter. *Beneath the Heartstone. Willing blood. The keeper's line.* He recited it silently as he climbed, the way other men prayed.

"Left foot there." Sera's voice came from above him, flat with exhaustion but steady. She'd driven herself harder than any of them since the hawk-message from Voss. Since learning her brother was dead — or that Voss wanted her to believe he was dead. Gideon had watched her carry both possibilities up three days of switchbacks without once setting either down.

He found the hold she meant, hauled himself up a body-length, and looked back down the gorge.

That was a mistake.

Far below, tiny as insects on a scroll, the column was moving. Voss's riders had abandoned their horses at the treeline and come on foot, maybe sixty of them in imperial grey, strung out along the lower trail like ink spilled down a page. They moved fast. Too fast for men carrying full kit.

"They've reached the second cairn," Gideon said.

Sera didn't look. She'd stopped looking an hour ago. "Then we have until midday."

Behind them, further down where the gorge narrowed to a throat of stone, Brannoc and eleven warriors were doing something quieter and far more terrible: waiting.

* * *

**Brannoc**

Brannoc had chosen the ground the way his father had taught him to choose ground — not for winning, because this ground could not be won, but for making the price obscene.

The Narrows above the third cairn funneled Voss's column into a lane six men wide, walled by scree slopes on one side and a drop on the other. Twelve clan warriors with bows, axes, and no intention of retreating could hold such a lane for hours. Maybe not even hours. Long enough.

"You could still come with us," Gideon had said last night, and Brannoc had laughed — a real laugh, rough and warm, the first Gideon had ever heard from him.

"And miss the chance to die on my own mountain? The moot gave you passage, scribe. The moot did not give you my death. That one I keep for myself."

Now, watching the first grey figures enter the Narrows, Brannoc spat over the edge, checked the bowstring his grandmother had braided, and said nothing at all. Beside him, young Cathal — sixteen, all elbows, who'd begged to be in the rearguard because his brother had died at the ambush in the gorge — was breathing too quickly.

"Breathe slower," Brannoc told him. "You'll want the air later."

"For what?"

"For screaming. It helps."

The first arrow he loosed took an imperial scout through the shoulder at forty paces, and the Narrows filled with shouting, and Brannoc settled into the old rhythm — loose, move, loose again — that his people had been practicing against empires for four hundred years.

They would not stop the column. He'd known that when he volunteered. But every quarter-hour bought here was a quarter-hour of climbing Gideon Pemberton would not have to do while arrows chased him, and if that was the whole arithmetic of Brannoc's life, then it was, at least, honest arithmetic.

"Cathal," he said, as the second wave came on. "Aim for the officers. Grey with the silver sash."

"Why?"

"Because killing a man who believes he's right is worth ten who only believe they're paid."

* * *

**Gideon**

They heard the fighting before they saw it — a thin, intermittent crack of sound rolling up the gorge like distant hail.

Sera climbed faster. Gideon struggled to keep pace, lungs burning, fingers cracked and bleeding from the cold stone. Twice she stopped and hauled him up a ledge by the wrist without a word, and twice he thought about how many times she had now saved his life, and how the debt between them had stopped being countable somewhere back in the winter cave, when she'd laughed at his terrible knot-work and he'd realized he was happy, actually happy, with frostbite threatening his toes and an empire hunting him.

"Sera." He caught her arm at the next ledge. "If we get separated—"

"We won't."

"If we do. You take the map knowledge and go south. You tell the clans, tell anyone who'll listen. Don't come back for me."

She looked at him then, wind-whipped hair across her face, and her expression was the one she wore when someone tried to cheat her at cards. "You've been saying goodbye since Hollowmere, scribe. I've noticed. I've decided to ignore it. Climb."

Above them the clouds tore open, and for the first time they saw it: the Heartstone plateau, a shelf of bare granite just below the summit ridge, and at its center a standing stone the size of a temple tower, black against the snow, veined with lines of faint gold that seemed — Gideon blinked, and they were still there — to pulse.

The Sigil slept beneath that stone. And something in his blood had begun answering it, a low hum behind his teeth that grew louder with every step.

"It knows we're coming," he whispered.

"Good," Sera said. "Let it put the kettle on."

* * *

**Voss**

Malachi Voss climbed like a man settling an account.

His soldiers were flagging; his officers advised rest; he ignored both. Thirty years in the empire's service had taught him that hesitation was simply defeat arriving politely, in installments. The clan rearguard in the Narrows had cost him nine dead and most of the morning, and he had ordered the wounded left behind with a field medic and pressed on, because the mathematics of this mountain permitted nothing else.

At the fourth cairn he paused, breathing hard despite himself, and looked up at the black stone crowned in gold light, and felt the old fear rise in him — the fear from the uprising, from the mass graves outside Redford, from the night he'd learned what happens when old powers wake and men who should know better kneel to them.

*It is a seal,* the captured scout had confessed under questioning. *Not a weapon. My grandfather's songs say it holds the war underground.*

Voss believed the scout. That was precisely the problem. A weapon could be seized, controlled, pointed by sensible men at sensible targets. A seal could only be renewed or broken — and renewal required blood, and blood required belief, and belief was the fire that had burned the borderlands twice in living memory.

Let the seal decay, and the clans stayed scattered, leaderless, harmless. Let it wake, and every ember of the old faith would catch at once.

There was no choice in it. There never had been.

"Inquisitor." Lieutenant Hask, pointing downslope. "Riders coming up behind us."

Voss turned. Far below, threading through the pass his own vanguard had cleared, came a second column — imperial grey, but moving wrong, too spread out, too fast, flying no banner he recognized.

Hawk-messages had gone out in his name for weeks. Apparently one of them had been answered by someone else entirely.

"Whose are they?"

"No banner, sir. Light kit. Moving like smugglers."

Voss watched them a moment longer, and something cold turned over in his chest. *Quill.* The smuggler woman had threads everywhere; he'd known that since the dockside reports. She'd found allies, or hired them, or promised them something.

It didn't matter. Whatever came up this mountain, the outcome was the same. He touched the iron medallion at his throat — Vessia's scales, worn smooth by thirty years of touching — and resumed climbing.

"Double pace," he said. "We reach the plateau before anyone else does."

* * *

**Sera**

The last stretch of the climb was vertical ice, and Sera led it because Gideon couldn't.

She drove pitons with hands she could barely feel, clipped the frayed rope she didn't fully trust, and talked the whole time — about the winter cave, about the terrible stew he'd made from pine bark, about anything — because she'd learned that Gideon climbed better when his mind was occupied and worse when it was counting costs. And he was counting costs now. She could see it in the set of his shoulders. He'd been composing his farewell speech for days, the stubborn, self-sacrificing fool, and she intended to interrupt it at every opportunity until the mountain itself ran out of patience.

Below and behind, the sounds of battle had changed. Thinner. Fewer arrows answering the imperial horns.

Brannoc's twelve were becoming fewer, and they both knew it.

"Don't," Gideon said quietly, reading her face as she hauled him over the final lip onto the plateau.

"I wasn't—"

"You were going to apologize for surviving. Don't. He wouldn't want it. He'd want us to be fast." Gideon stood, swaying, and stared at the Heartstone. Up close it was worse than awe — the gold veins ran beneath the surface like blood under skin, and the hum Gideon carried had become almost audible, a chord held by a hundred throats. Snow near the stone's base had melted in a perfect circle, exposing dark earth that steamed gently in the bitter air.

"There," Gideon breathed. At the circle's center, half-buried: worked stone. A seam. A door.

Sera saw it too — and saw, at the same moment, the thing that made her stomach drop.

On the plateau's southern edge, cresting the ridge with the sun behind them, the first of Voss's men appeared. Then more. Then a dozen, fanning out across the snow with crossbows levelled.

And on the northern edge, smaller, faster, half-hidden by the ridge shadow — the unbannered climbers. Smugglers' gear. Her people's gear, or close enough.

"Gideon." Her voice came out very calm, which was how she knew she was terrified. "We're out of time, and we're out of mountain."

He turned to look, and then he did the last thing she expected.

He smiled.

"Not quite," he said, and pulled off his glove, and pressed his bare palm against the black stone.

The gold veins flared.

The mountain rang like a struck bell, once, deep enough to feel in the marrow — and every man on the plateau, imperial and smuggler alike, staggered and clapped hands to ears. In the circle of melted snow, the ancient seam split with a sound like a held breath finally released, and darkness opened beneath the Heartstone, and stairs went down into a light that no torch had ever made.

Gideon stood at its edge with his hand still on the stone, lit gold from below, and looked back at Sera with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life — terror and peace wearing each other's faces.

"The door knows me," he said. "Come on. Before they find their feet."

Behind them, on the southern ridge, Malachi Voss began to run.
