## 28. Wren's Last Letter

The murals went on for longer than the chamber should have allowed.

Gideon walked them slowly, torch in one hand, Sera a half-step behind him with her wounded arm bound tight against her ribs. The corridor bent where no corridor should bend—walls curving inward like the inside of a throat—and on every surface, painted in pigments that still glowed faintly after centuries, the keepers marched.

He counted them as he passed. It was the scribe in him, the habit of inventory, and it gave his mind something to hold onto besides the roaring truth of the door: *Pemberton*. His family crest, worn smooth by ten generations of hands, set into stone that had opened at the touch of his blood.

Forty-one figures. He stopped counting when he realized they weren't all different.

Some of the faces repeated. A woman with high cheekbones appeared three times across what looked like centuries, each time standing before the same arch of stone, each time with her palms pressed flat against it. A broad-shouldered man with a crooked nose showed up twice. Gideon stood before the second rendering of that man for a long moment, because he had seen that crooked nose every morning of his childhood in the cracked mirror above the washbasin in their mother's house.

"That's you," Sera said quietly.

"It's not me."

"It's your face."

"It's my face," he agreed, "worn by someone else first."

The murals told the story without words, or perhaps the words were there and only Pemberton eyes could read them, because certain passages shimmered when he looked directly at them—flickers of script beneath the paint, legible for a heartbeat and then gone. He caught fragments. *The mountain sleeps. The seal holds. The blood returns.* A scene of armies clashing in a valley, rendered in blacks and rusts, and above it all a figure holding up a disc of light while the ground itself split open to swallow the war whole.

"The Sigil," Sera said. "That's it. That's what it did."

"Ended something." Gideon's voice came out flat. "Or buried it. Hard to say which from here."

They walked on. The offerings began after the third bend.

At first he took them for rubble—small shapes arranged along the base of the wall in careful rows—but the torchlight found metal, cloth, bone. Cups of hammered silver gone black with age. A child's shoe, leather still supple somehow, placed sole-up as if its owner had simply stepped out of it and into the stone. Rings. Combs. A bird skull resting on a folded square of linen embroidered with clan knots. Offerings, he understood. Things left by keepers, or for keepers, or by the pilgrims who had once climbed this far to press their foreheads to the door and ask the mountain for mercy.

Someone had kept this place. Someone had come here, generation after generation, and knelt among these objects, and done whatever it was keepers did.

And then, near the end of the row, someone had died doing it.

* * *

She was sitting.

That was the detail that undid him—not the desiccation, not the leathers gone stiff and dark, not the frost-pale skin drawn tight over the bones of her face. It was that she had sat down against the wall with her back straight and her legs crossed, deliberate as a scholar at a desk, and in her lap she had arranged a bundle of oilcloth with the corners folded inward, neat as a parcel ready for posting.

Gideon knew those hands. He knew them from a doorway in the rain, from fingers slick with blood pressing a wrapped shape into his chest, from a voice rasping *take it north, take it to Thornfell* before the light went out of her.

"Wren," he said.

Sera's hand touched his shoulder. He barely felt it.

He knelt. Up close, the resemblance was unbearable—not to the dying stranger in his doorway but to something older. Their mother's jaw. Their mother's way of holding her head slightly tilted, listening for something just past hearing. He had spent twenty years telling himself he remembered nothing of his sister, that she'd been too young when she left, that grief over a person you couldn't recall was a performance rather than a wound. Kneeling here, he discovered he remembered everything. The gap between her front teeth when she laughed. The scar on her left eyebrow from falling out of the pear tree. The way she used to braid her hair in the clan style their mother pretended not to notice.

"She came here first," Sera said softly. "Before you. She found it."

"She found it and she couldn't do it." Gideon reached out, then stopped, his fingers hovering over the oilcloth. "Whatever it is keepers do. She couldn't."

"Can you?"

He didn't answer that. He lifted the bundle instead, and it was lighter than it should have been, and the oilcloth crackled with age, and tucked against the folds was a second object—a locket, twin to the one Wren had given him, its chain snapped.

Inside the oilcloth: paper. Several sheets, covered edge to edge in a cramped, slanting hand he recognized from the map's marginalia. And beneath the pages, wrapped separately in waxed linen, a small iron stylus and a pot of ink frozen solid in its jar.

She had been writing when she died. Writing until she couldn't hold the pen anymore, and then she had folded the letter with the unfinished side hidden, because even at the end she hadn't wanted anyone to see the sentence stop mid-word.

Gideon sat down across from her, mirroring her posture without meaning to, and unfolded the pages, and read.

* * *

*Little brother.*

*If you're reading this, then I got the map to you, which means I got it wrong. I promised myself I'd carry it all the way to Thornfell myself. I promised Mother's ghost. But the pass took more out of me than I had, and when I saw your name on the temple rolls—Gideon, of course it was Gideon, our mother named you for the patient one—I understood why she sent me south instead of coming herself. She couldn't watch you learn this. Neither can I, and I'm already dead, so that tells you something.*

*You'll have questions. Let me answer the ones I can.*

*Yes, we are clan-blooded. Mother never lied to us exactly; she just let the empire's version of her stand. She was Ellow-kin, from the high valleys, keeper-line going back past counting. When she married Father she traded her braids for a wedding ring and her name for his, and she spent the rest of her life paying interest on that trade. The disappearances you remember—the weeks she'd vanish and come back thinner, quieter, smelling of pine smoke—she was walking north. Walking here. There was no pilgrimage. There was only duty, and duty doesn't send letters.*

*I know you remember the lullabies. You thought they were nonsense syllables. They're not. They're the binding rite, broken into pieces small enough to sing a child to sleep. She was teaching us the way her mother taught her, hoping neither of us would ever need it. I hope you were listening better than you think you were.*

*Here is what the shrine is. Not what Voss will tell you it is, not what the clans hope it is. The Sigil isn't a weapon. It's a seal, and the seal is a promise, and promises need someone to keep them. Every hundred years—or sooner, if the mountain starts to bleed—the keeper renews it. Willing blood, freely given, Pemberton line. That's the whole of the contract. Our ancestors wrote themselves into the mountain so that no army could ever dig the old war back up. Forty-one of them have held the door. The murals will show you. Count the faces. Some of them held it twice.*

*I found the shrine dying, Gideon. The light in the Heartstone is down to an ember. Whatever the seal holds has been pressing against it for years, maybe decades, and the wards are thin as winter ice on a spring stream. I felt it the moment I crossed the threshold—like standing in a house where the fire has gone out and the cold is coming through every seam. I sat down to rest before the rite and I could not get up. The climb, the cold, the years of running. My body made the decision my courage wouldn't admit to.*

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't reach you in time to explain. I'm sorry you'll learn this from paper instead of from me. I'm sorry for every year I stayed away when I could have written, could have come, could have been your sister instead of a rumor. Mother asked me to keep the distance—said the Temple watched you, said the less you knew the safer you'd be. She was right, probably. Rightness is cheap when you're the one who gets to walk away.*

*But here is the thing I need you to understand, and I need you to read it twice:*

*You don't have to do this.*

*The seal is failing, yes. The clans will suffer if it breaks, and the empire too, and everyone between. But you are a scribe with ink under his fingernails and four years left on a debt-bond, and you have spent your whole life being careful, and no one—not Mother, not the mountain, not the dead women in these walls—has the right to spend your life for them. If you burn the map and go home and live your small free life, I will not haunt you. I swear it on her grave. I will be the most content ghost in these mountains.*

*Find another way. Find Brannoc's people, find a scholar, find some loophole in the old law. The bond accepts willing blood—look for the willing part, little brother. Interrogate it. Our ancestors were cleverer than the empire ever gave them credit for.*

*And if there is no other way—if you read this and feel the door calling you the way I felt it—then know that I am proud of you, and Mother is proud of you, and forty-one keepers are waiting to stand beside you, and you will not do it alone even though it will feel like the loneliest thing anyone has ever done.*

*The keeper's line ends with you. Forgive me for the weight.*

*Your sister,*
*Wren*

*P.S. — The lullaby. Third verse. Sing it at the Heartstone and it will show you what it showed me. Don't be afraid of what it shows. It only shows the truth, and the truth is mostly kind, in the end.*

*P.P.S. — There's a man looking for the map. Grey coat, quiet voice, calls himself Voss. If he finds you before you understand all this, give him nothing. Especially don't give him the truth about what the Sigil is. He believes destroying it is mercy. Men who believe they're merciful are the most dangerous kind.*

The last page ended there. No closing flourish, no date. Just the pen-stroke trailing off at the bottom margin, a thin tail of ink like a thread pulled loose from a hem.

Gideon read it twice. Then a third time, because she had told him to, and because obeying her voice—even her written voice, even her dead voice—was the closest thing to hearing it he would ever get.

* * *

When he finally looked up, the torch had burned down two fingers' width and Sera was sitting against the opposite wall, watching him with the particular stillness she wore instead of sympathy.

"Well?" she said.

He handed her the pages. "Read it."

She read fast—he'd seen her skim cargo manifests the same way, eyes flicking, missing nothing—and he watched her face change as she went. At *the seal is failing* her jaw tightened. At *you don't have to do this* something flickered that might have been relief. At the postscript about Voss she exhaled through her nose, sharp and humorless.

"He knows," she said. "Or suspects. That's why the riders, why the net closing around us. He's not chasing a weapon anymore. He's chasing a secret."

"He thinks the Sigil should die." Gideon looked down at his sister's hands, folded now in her lap where he had replaced them, arranging the empty oilcloth over her knees the way she'd left it. "And he might even be right. From where he stands."

"And from where you stand?"

Gideon didn't answer immediately. He looked at the murals instead—at the forty-one faces, at the woman with his mother's cheekbones appearing again and again across the centuries, at the crooked-nosed man who had held the door twice because once apparently wasn't enough. He looked at Wren, who had carried the map a thousand miles and died within sight of the finish line, and whose last act of love was telling him he didn't have to follow her.

"I stand in a room full of my family," he said finally, "and every one of them is asking me the same question."

"Which is?"

"*Will you listen better than you think you did.*" He tapped the letter. "She says the bond takes willing blood. She says to interrogate the willing part. She says our ancestors were cleverer than the empire knew." He got to his feet, and his knees ached, and he noticed distantly that he wasn't afraid—or rather, that the fear was there but it had changed shape, cooled from panic into something with edges, something he could grip. "There's a Heartstone at the center of this place. And there's a lullaby I've known since before I could talk."

Sera rose too, wincing at her arm. "Gideon."

"Don't." He said it gently. "Whatever you're about to argue—we both know you're going to lose, and we both know you'll hate losing, and I'd rather save us both the time. But—" he met her eyes, and found he could hold them now without flinching, "—I meant what I said in the gorge. We do this together or not at all. So come on. Let's go meet the mountain."

They started down the corridor toward the light at its heart, and behind them Wren sat straight-backed against the wall, keeping her long vigil, and Gideon did not look back because he knew that if he did, he would kneel down again and never rise.

The glow ahead strengthened. And in it, faint but unmistakable, Gideon heard something that stopped him mid-stride—a sound threading through the stone like water through cracks.

Someone was singing. In the clan tongue. In their mother's voice.

The third verse.
