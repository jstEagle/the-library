## 29. Voss at the Threshold

Malachi Voss had outrun his own men for three days.

He had left them at the Narrows with a sergeant's salute and a lie about scouting the high route, and then he had climbed. Alone. No column, no banners, no grey-cloaked file of soldiers to announce him. Just his boots on the ice, his breath tearing white from his lungs, and the old scar along his ribs aching in the cold like a second heartbeat.

His men believed he was riding ahead to secure the pass for the empire. In a sense, he was. Just not the way they imagined.

The plateau opened before him at dusk, a bowl of stone cupped under a sky going violet at the edges. Wind came off the Heartstone's shoulder in long, keening gusts, and somewhere below the ice—Voss could feel it now, the way a man feels a storm behind a wall—the Sigil waited. He had known it would feel like this. He had prayed, these last weeks, that he was wrong.

He was not wrong.

The shrine door stood open.

That was the first thing that stopped him, twenty paces out, one hand drifting to the hilt of his sword out of forty years of habit. The great slab of mountain granite, carved with the Pemberton crest—a tower beneath a falling star—had been sealed when Voss was a boy. His instructors at the Temple academy had shown him sketches of it. *No key. No hinge. No way in.* And yet here it stood, swung wide into darkness, breathing out air that smelled of warm metal and old incense, as though the mountain itself had exhaled after a century of holding its breath.

And standing in that doorway, small against the light spilling up from below, was Gideon Pemberton.

The scribe looked terrible. Voss had last seen him across an audit table in the Temple of Vessia, ink-stained and apologetic, a man built entirely of careful apologies. Now his coat was torn at the shoulder, his face hollowed by wind and hunger, and there was blood—not his own, Voss guessed—dried brown along his sleeve. But his eyes were steady. That was the part that unsettled Voss most. Scribes did not have steady eyes. Fugitives did not have steady eyes. Men who had walked this far, through ambush and avalanche and clan country, toward something they knew was waiting for them—they had eyes like that.

"You came alone," Gideon said. Not a question. He had seen the empty snow behind him.

"My men are slower than I am." Voss stopped ten paces away and let his hands fall open, away from his sword. A calculated gesture. Everything about him, in this moment, was calculated. "They're holding the Narrows. Brannoc's warriors are still fighting them, I expect." He watched the words land. Watched the flicker of grief the scribe tried not to show. Good. Grief made men pliable. "I didn't want them here for this conversation."

"What conversation is that?"

"The one I should have had with you in Vessia, before you ran." Voss took another step, boots crunching on frost-brittle stone. "The one where I tell you the truth."

Gideon almost laughed. It came out cracked and bitter. "The truth. From an Inquisitor."

"I've told precious few lies in my life, scribe. Ask anyone who fears me—they'll confirm it with a shudder." Voss pulled off one glove, flexing stiff fingers against the cold. "I told your Temple superiors you were a thief. That was true—you stole their map. I told my riders you were dangerous. Also true, apparently; you cost me two good men at the gorge. But I have never once lied about what's under this mountain. Not to myself. Not to anyone."

"Then say it."

Voss looked past him, into the doorway. Light rose from deep within the rock, gold shot through with veins of blue, pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse. Even from here, even at this distance, he could feel it pressing against his teeth. The Sigil of Auren. The thing the empire had spent a hundred years pretending was a myth, because a myth could be dismissed, and a fact had to be dealt with.

"It's a seal," Voss said quietly. "Not a weapon. You know that now, don't you? You've read the murals."

Something moved behind Gideon's eyes—surprise, quickly mastered. "You knew."

"I've known since I was nineteen years old." The cold helped. Cold always helped; it kept a man's voice level when levelness mattered. "I stood where you're standing once. Different war. Same door."

* * *

He had been young, then. Young enough to still believe the empire's version of itself.

The last clan uprising had ended at a place called Cael Mor, and Voss had been a line soldier in the ninth cohort, seventeen years old, holding a spear he barely knew how to use while clan warriors came down the slope screaming names in a language the empire had outlawed. He had buried four friends in frozen ground too hard to dig properly. He had covered their faces with stones because there wasn't time or earth enough for anything else.

Afterward, his captain had marched the survivors up into Thornfell—to this very plateau—and shown them the sealed door, and told them what the old quartermasters' records said: that the peace they were bleeding to defend was not an empire's gift but a seal's work. That some power older than either crown held the clans and the lowlands from each other's throats, and that it was failing, and that the empire's answer was simple.

*Let it fail,* the captains had said. *When it fails, we finish what Cael Mor started. One war, won cleanly, instead of a hundred years of watching and waiting.*

Voss had stood in the snow with stones in his hands and thought: *no.*

Because he had seen what unfinished wars looked like. He had carried four of them down a mountainside on his shoulders. If there was a thing under the rock that kept children from burying their friends, then it was not the enemy. It was the only friend either side had ever had.

But he had also seen what the clans were. What they became when the old powers stirred—proud, armed, certain of their right to everything the empire had taken. And so he had spent thirty years walking a knife-edge of his own devising: protect the seal by making sure no one ever reached it. Kill the map. Burn the scholars. Silence the seers. Not because he wanted the seal dead.

Because he wanted it *left alone.*

"You think I'm here to destroy it," Voss said now. "You've been running from that since Hollowmere. I watched you flinch every time my name came up. So let me be plain, Pemberton, because you've earned plain speech if anyone has." He spread his hands again, deliberately empty. "I don't want the Sigil destroyed. I never have. My orders do. The Temple does. Half my own officers do. They believe a weapon sleeps under this mountain, and they want it in imperial hands before any clan elder can reach it. When they learn otherwise—" he nodded at the glowing doorway, "—when they learn it's a seal, a lock, a *dependency*, do you know what they'll conclude?"

Gideon said nothing. His jaw was tight.

"They'll conclude what my first captain concluded. That a lock is just a weakness wearing armor. That the empire is strong enough now to stand without it. That the cleanest solution is to break the seal, win the war it's postponing, and never again depend on the mercy of old magic." Voss's voice dropped. "There are men three days behind me who will burn this plateau to the bedrock if I give the order. I outran them on purpose. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Every hour I delay, I spend my own honor buying you time. This conversation is the last hour I have."

"Then say what you came to say."

* * *

Here it was, then. The bargain. Voss had rehearsed it on the climb, in the rhythm of his own footfalls, and every rehearsal had sounded like mercy. That was the trick of it—he had learned this late in life, in interrogation rooms lit by one candle—that the most effective temptation was the kind that arrived dressed as kindness. Men rarely refused mercy. Men refused demands. So he would offer no demand.

"Walk away," Voss said.

The word hung in the thin cold air between them.

"That's all. Walk away. Turn around, come down the mountain with me, and let the seal decay." He took a step closer, and lowered his voice, and made it gentle, because gentleness was a tool like any other and he had sharpened this one for decades. "You're a scribe, Gideon. A bonded man. Your whole life has been other people's ledgers, other people's debts, other people's ink. I read your file. Eleven years copying temple accounts for a debt your father incurred before you could walk. Nobody ever asked you what you wanted. Not once." He saw the words go in, saw them find the soft places he'd aimed for. "So here is the first person in your life asking. What do *you* want?"

"Don't." Gideon's voice was rough.

"A house," Voss pressed on, relentless, quiet. "Somewhere south, where the winters are short. A new name—I'm an Inquisitor, Pemberton, I can make a man unexist and reexist wherever I please. Your bond struck from the rolls. Your sister's death answered, if answers matter to you. And in exchange, you give me nothing but your absence. Let the mountain keep its secret until the seal fails on its own schedule. Maybe that's fifty years. Maybe a hundred. You'll be dust either way, warm and free and forgotten, and the empire will endure exactly as long as it was always going to." He paused. "Nobody dies today. Isn't that worth more than anything written on those murals?"

The wind gusted between them, flinging fine ice crystals across the stone, and for a moment neither man spoke.

Voss watched the scribe's face with the attention he usually reserved for liars. Because that was the thing about temptation—it worked best on honest men. Liars bargained. Honest men *wavered*, and wavering was all Voss needed. Give him a night, give him a fire and a hot meal and the memory of safety, and the scribe would come around. They always did. In thirty years of service, Malachi Voss had talked eleven people out of martyrdom, and every single one of them had thanked him afterward.

Gideon looked at the open door. Looked back at him.

"There's someone else coming up this mountain," he said.

"Sera Quill." Voss permitted himself a thin smile. "Yes. She's half a day behind me, if her wound hasn't slowed her further. Which is why we should conclude our business before she arrives. She complicates things. She has a habit of complicating things."

"She loves me."

It was not said as a boast. It was said the way a man reports weather—as a fact of the world, newly confirmed, still astonishing. And Voss felt something shift in his chest, some old splinter of the boy at Cael Mor twisting in the wood. Because he remembered being loved like that, once, by a woman who had died waiting for him to come home from a war the empire insisted was necessary. He remembered what it was to be someone's whole weather.

"All the more reason," Voss said softly, "to walk away with me. Think of what she gets, if you stay. A mountain. A story. A grave with no body in it. Think of what she gets if you come down. A man. A life. Thirty, forty years of mornings." He let that sit. "I'm not offering you cowardice, scribe. I'm offering you *her*. There is no version of staying that doesn't break her heart, and you know it. You've known it since the fever cave. I've read enough ledgers to recognize a man doing arithmetic he hates."

Gideon's breath came out ragged. For a long moment he simply stood there, swaying slightly, exhausted beyond endurance, and Voss thought: *now. Now he breaks.*

"You're wrong about one thing," Gideon said finally.

"Tell me."

"You said nobody asked me what I wanted. That's not true." He lifted his chin, and the light from the doorway caught his face, and Voss saw—with a sinking sensation he had not felt since Cael Mor—that the wavering was gone. It had perhaps never been there at all. "Wren asked. My sister asked, in the letter she left me. She asked me to forgive her for the weight. And the murals asked. Every keeper in that stone gallery asked, in their way. They all said yes, Voss. Generation after generation, they all said yes. Do you understand what that means?" His voice shook, but his eyes didn't. "It means I'm not the first man to stand in this doorway and hear someone offer him a way out. I'm just the first one you got to."

"The seal will fail regardless," Voss said. "One keeper more or less—"

"One keeper is the difference between a hundred years of peace and a war my mother's people lose." Gideon stepped backward, into the light. "You buried friends at Cael Mor. I've seen it in the visions—no, don't look surprised, the Sigil shows things—you buried them under stones because the ground was too hard, and you've spent your whole life making sure no one else has to. That's why you're really here. Not for the empire. For the stones." He paused. "We want the same thing, Inquisitor. We've wanted it the whole way up this mountain. You just can't bear the price, and I can."

"And Quill?" Voss's voice came out harder than he intended. "Does she get a vote? Or does she arrive tomorrow to find the door shut and you gone, having decided for her, the way every man in every age has decided for the women who love them?"

The words landed. Voss saw them land—saw the flinch, the guilt, the terrible honesty of a man who had already asked himself that question at three in the morning and failed to answer it.

"She'll hate me," Gideon said quietly. "For a while. Then she'll build something out of what's left. She's good at that. Better than I ever was." He put one hand against the doorframe, against stone that seemed to lean into his palm like a living thing. "Go home, Malachi Voss. Tell them whatever keeps your men off this mountain. Lie beautifully. You're clearly capable of it."

"I have never lied in my life," Voss said.

"No," Gideon agreed, and there was something almost kind in it. "You've just spent thirty years telling the truth to people who couldn't afford to hear it. Go home. Let me pay the family debt."

He stepped back into the light, and the great door began to move—not swinging, Voss realized, but *closing*, grinding inward on hinges of geology, the Pemberton crest sliding toward its frame like a star setting behind a tower.

"Wait." The word tore out of Voss before he could stop it, and he hated how human it sounded. "Pemberton. *Wait.*"

The scribe turned, half in shadow, half in gold.

"If you do this," Voss said, "and the seal holds, and the clans remember what freedom tastes like—the empire will come anyway. Not me. Not my lie. Someone younger, hungrier, with fewer stones on his conscience. Peace bought with one man's life lasts exactly as long as that man's memory. You're not ending the war. You're scheduling it."

"I know," Gideon said.

And he smiled—tired, terrified, certain—and the door closed, and the light went out, and the mountain settled over its secret with a sound like a long-held breath finally released.

* * *

Voss stood alone on the plateau for a long time.

Snow began to fall, fat and silent, filling his bootprints, erasing the evidence that anyone had ever stood here at all. Somewhere below, in the dark, in the warm gold dark, a scribe was beginning a rite that would make him a wall in the world's foundation. And somewhere below that, in the Narrows, men were dying for a lie Voss had fed them, and a woman with a knife wound was climbing toward a door that was already shut.

He thought about writing his report. *Shrine collapsed. Sigil destroyed. Nothing found.* He found, examining himself with the same cold precision he brought to everything, that he could write those words without his hand shaking. That was the worst discovery of the day. Worse than the door closing. He had become a man who could lie beautifully, after all—Gideon had seen it, named it, forgiven it—and he hadn't even noticed the change happening.

*Peace bought with one man's life lasts exactly as long as that man's memory.*

Then, Voss thought, turning back toward the descent, toward the fires of his camp and the faces of his men, I had better make sure someone remembers.

Far below, at the edge of the torchlight, a figure crested the ridge—small, limping, moving with the stubborn grace of a woman who had never once in her life done what she was told.

Sera Quill had reached the plateau. And she was looking straight at him.
