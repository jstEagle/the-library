## 30. Loyalty vs. Truth

Sera had killed men for less than what Gideon had just told her.

Not killed them—she corrected herself, pressing her palms flat against the cold stone of the shrine's antechamber to keep her hands from shaking. She had *wanted* to kill men for less. There was a difference, and the difference was that she was still standing here, and so was he.

"You're lying," she said.

"I'm not."

"You lie beautifully, scribe. It's one of your few talents. So lie now."

Gideon didn't flinch. That was the worst part. In the months since Hollowmere she had catalogued every tell he owned—the way his jaw tightened before a falsehood, the way his eyes went soft and distant when he was composing rather than confessing. None of it was there now. His face was just a face, tired and honest and utterly calm, and that calm made her want to put her knife through the wall beside his head just to see it crack.

"The rite requires willing blood," he said again, in the level voice he used for translating old script. "Pemberton blood, freely given, bound to the Heartstone until the seal is renewed. A hundred years, Sera. Maybe more. The keepers who came before—" He gestured at the murals behind him, the painted figures kneeling at the base of the great stone door, their hands pressed to the rock as light poured from between their fingers. "They didn't die on the first day. But they never left."

"Never left." Her voice came out flat. "You mean they were buried alive in a mountain and called it duty."

"I mean they stayed."

"They *stayed*." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter at all. "That's a beautiful word for it. Did you learn that word in the Temple? Is that what they taught you when they sold you into bondage at nine years old? That staying is a virtue?"

Something flickered across his face then—the first crack—and she hated herself for using it even as she leaned into the wound. You learned things about people in caves and gorges and frozen waystations. You learned where the soft places were.

"That's not fair," he said quietly.

"No," she admitted. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair." She pushed off the wall and crossed the antechamber in four strides, close enough to grab the front of his coat if she wanted to. She wanted to. "Wren knew. Didn't she? Your sister knew what this would cost, and she carried that map across half the empire anyway and died in your doorway with it, and neither of you thought I deserved to know."

"She asked me not to tell anyone. The letter—"

"Wren is dead, Gideon. Her wishes died with her. Mine are standing right in front of you, and I am telling you that you do not get to do this."

The chamber around them held its breath. They had reached the shrine of Ellow three days ago and descended through its frozen galleries into this place beneath the Heartstone, where the air itself seemed older than stone, where the murals glowed faintly without any source of light, where the Sigil slept behind a door marked with a crest Sera now understood was his birthright and his death sentence both. Somewhere above them, Brannoc's surviving warriors kept watch over the pass. Somewhere below them, or perhaps all around them, something vast and patient waited to be fed.

Voss was coming. They all knew it. They had perhaps a day, maybe two, before the Inquisitor's column fought its way up the last defile. Which meant, Sera realized with a horror that arrived late and hit hard, that Gideon had been planning to walk into that ritual tomorrow and let the mountain take him, and he had told her only because there was no longer any way to hide it.

"How long have you known?" she asked. "Exactly how long?"

He was silent long enough that the silence answered for him.

"Since Ellow," she said. "The waystation. Weeks. You've been walking toward your own funeral for weeks and teaching me to read ice and laughing at my terrible jokes and saying *when we get to Thornfell* like we were going anywhere together after." Her throat closed. She forced the words through it. "I confessed everything to you in that fever tent. My brother. My bargain. Every rotten thing I've ever done, because I trusted you, and the whole time you were carrying this."

"I was going to tell you."

"When? At the door? After?"

"After," he said simply. "When it couldn't be undone."

She hit him. Not hard—a blow meant for a man's shoulder, open-palmed, the kind of strike that said everything words couldn't—but he caught her wrist before it landed, and for a moment they stood there, her fist in his grip, both of them breathing hard in the ancient light.

"Let go," she said.

He let go immediately. He always did. That was the maddening thing about Gideon Pemberton: he never fought back, never grabbed, never held on to anything except, apparently, the conviction that his life was a currency someone else should spend.

"Run with me," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"You heard me." She was talking fast now, the plan assembling itself out of pure refusal, out of the same instinct that had kept her alive through twenty years of smuggling runs that should have ended her. "We take the map—or don't, we burn it, who cares—and we go south. Over the Salt Pass, down through the river country. Voss thinks the seal decaying means the empire endures; fine, let it decay, let it decay for a hundred years, we'll be dead of old age before it matters. The clans will hate us. Let them. I've been hated by better people than clan elders." She grabbed his coat front after all, hauling him closer. "There are roads, Gideon. I know roads that don't exist. Roads I've built myself, out of bribes and silence and twenty years of not being caught. No one finds people on my roads. Not Voss, not the Temple, not the mountain itself."

"Sera—"

"And in ten years, maybe fifteen, when the seal starts to fail and the passes start to shake, some other poor bastard with the right blood can come find it. Or not. Or the clans and the empire fight their war and settle it like people instead of feeding a mountain a scribe." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "Come with me. That's all. Just—come with me. Please."

The please cost her more than the confession had. She heard it hang in the cold air between them, naked and unarmored, and she knew—he saw her know—that she had never said that word to another living soul.

Gideon covered her hand with his own. Not pulling it away. Holding it there, against his chest, where she could feel his heartbeat, steady and infuriatingly alive.

"I can't," he said.

"You can. You literally can. Your legs work. I've watched them work for two hundred miles."

"It's not about my legs." He exhaled slowly. "It's about what happens if I use them."

"Nothing happens. Nothing has to happen—"

"The war happens." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "You've seen what's already moving. Voss's column in the pass. Brannoc's warriors dying on the rear line while we climbed. The clans arming every hollow and cave between here and High Cairn. If the seal fails—if the Sigil wakes unfed, unbound—the peace ends. Not eventually. Immediately. The clans will read it as the empire breaking the old compact, and they'll rise, and the empire will answer with fire, and I have *read the ledgers*, Sera. I know exactly what imperial fire costs. I spent six years copying the requisitions."

"So you'll copy yourself onto a wall instead."

"If that's what it takes."

"It doesn't take that! That's the point! That's the whole entire point!" She released his coat and spun away, pacing the width of the antechamber, her boots ringing on stone that had heard a hundred years of keepers say their goodbyes and had apparently decided to host one more. "You talk about the clans' freedom like it's a ledger entry. Cost: one heartbeat. Benefit: peace. As if you're a column of numbers and not a person. As if you're not—" She stopped. Turned back. "As if you're not the best thing that's happened to me since before my brother was taken."

He went very still.

"There," she said bitterly. "I said it. Out loud, in a hole in a mountain, with an army coming. Are you happy? Does the great sacrifice feel properly weighted now? Because I need you to understand that when I say the clans' freedom matters, I agree. When you say the peace matters, I agree. But you're leaving out a column, Gideon, and the column is me, and I am telling you that I cannot—" Her voice failed entirely. She stood there, hands empty, twenty years of practiced composure gone like smoke. "I buried a brother once. I'm not doing it twice. Not for anything. Not even for the world."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he crossed to her, slow, giving her every chance to step back, and took both her hands in his. His fingers were ink-stained even now, even here, some habits deeper than destiny.

"I believe you," he said. "About all of it. And if I thought running would save anyone, I'd run. You'd outrun Voss. You'd outrun the empire. We'd live, Sera—maybe ten years, maybe thirty, in some valley with a false name, and I would spend every one of those days grateful and every one of those nights listening for the sound of the mountains cracking open." He squeezed her hands. "The Sigil isn't a weapon. You know that now. It's a promise that was made a thousand years ago, that the blood that bound these clans and this empire together would keep holding. Wren gave her life getting the map to me. Brannoc's people are bleeding right now to buy us this hour. Everyone in this story has paid except me, and I'm the one the door was carved for."

"People aren't payments."

"No," he agreed. "But choices are. And I'm choosing."

"Then I'm choosing too." She pulled one hand free and pressed it flat against his sternum, over his heart, feeling it beat against her palm—stubborn, warm, mortal. "I choose to stand in that chamber tomorrow, and when the rite starts, I choose to drag you out of it bodily if I have to. I've hauled cargo heavier than you up cliff faces. Don't think I won't."

A ghost of a smile touched his mouth. "You'd fight me."

"With pleasure."

"And if you won? If you pull me out of the circle and we run, and forty years from now the passes are burning and the clans are ash and some survivor asks why the keeper ran?" He shook his head slowly. "I'd have an answer for them, Sera. 'I loved someone.' It's a good answer. It might even be true enough. But it wouldn't stop the fires, and I'd hear them every night for the rest of my life, and so would you, because you'd hear them in me." He lifted her hand from his chest and held it, gentle as a man handling something breakable, which under the circumstances she supposed she was. "You told me once that everyone wants the artifact for themselves. The empire wants it destroyed. The clans want it freed. The Temple wants it buried. Nobody, until this moment, wanted the *keeper*."

"Don't," she whispered.

"Someone does," he said. "So I'm asking you for the hardest thing I will ever ask anyone. Not to understand. Not to agree. Just to stay with me until the door closes. To be there. And then to ride south and tell the truth, because I can't carry it anymore, and the world needs to hear it from someone who can still walk away."

The chamber hummed around them, faint and patient, the murals' glow pulsing like slow breath. Above, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, a horn sounded from the pass—one note, then two. Brannoc's signal. Not the alarm. Not yet. But soon.

Sera looked at Gideon Pemberton—at the scribe who had lied to an Inquisitor to protect a stranger, who had crossed an empire with a dead woman's map sewn into his coat, who was asking her to help him die and calling it honesty—and she felt the two halves of herself finally stop fighting. The smuggler who survived, and the woman who had, somewhere on a frozen mountainside, stopped merely surviving.

"One condition," she said.

His eyes widened slightly. "Sera—"

"You don't get to decide alone anymore. Whatever happens in that chamber—whatever the Sigil shows you, whatever loopholes or warnings or last-minute mercies—you tell me. Everything. The whole truth, all the way to the end. You've spent your whole life invisible, Gideon. Not with me. Not tomorrow. Swear it."

The horn sounded again. Closer now. Three notes.

Gideon looked at the sealed door, at the crest that matched his blood, at the hundred years of dark that waited on the other side of his choice. Then he looked back at her, and nodded once.

"I swear it," he said. "Everything."

And that was when the third horn sounded—not Brannoc's signal at all, but the deep imperial bray of Voss's war-horns, echoing up from the Narrows, far too near.

* * *

They climbed the last gallery together in silence, because there was nothing left to say that hadn't been said, and because both of them were listening—to the horns, to the shouts drifting down through the mountain's throat, to the clash of steel somewhere above that meant Brannoc's rearguard had made contact.

At the mouth of the gallery, where frozen daylight spilled down the shaft from the plateau above, Sera stopped him with a hand on his arm.

"For what it's worth," she said, "if you're wrong about all of this—if the seal fails anyway, if the mountain takes you and gives nothing back—I want you to know I'll spend the rest of my life being furious at you."

"I'd expect nothing less."

"But if you're right," she said, and her voice steadied, "then I'll spend it telling everyone exactly who stayed."

Below them, the horns rose again, and the mountain began, very softly, to tremble.
