## 32. What the Sigil Shows

The light did not burn.

That was the first thing Gideon understood, and it undid him more than pain would have. He had braced for fire—for the white-hot annihilation the murals promised in their patient, painted way—but what came instead was warmth, spreading up from his opened palm like sunlight soaking into winter ground. The blood on the Heartstone drank itself back into the stone, and the stone drank him, and there was nothing to do but be drunk.

He was kneeling. He was fairly sure of that. The chamber floor had gone soft beneath him, or he had gone soft above it, and the distinction stopped mattering somewhere around the moment the sigil's glow filled his vision entirely and the walls of the shrine dissolved like sugar in water.

Then he was standing in a field of wheat that had never grown in Thornfell.

* * *

He knew it was a memory because the sky was wrong—too wide, too blue, unbroken by the grey teeth of the mountains. And he knew whose memory it was because the wheat was burning.

The war came to him not as history but as weather. It rolled over him in waves: the smell of smoke and cut grain, the thunder of hooves, a woman screaming a name in clan-tongue that he somehow understood without knowing how. He saw soldiers in imperial grey riding down clansfolk who had put down their weapons—who were kneeling, hands open, offering something small and bright to the riders. A disc. A circle worked in old gold, its face carved with interlocking rings.

The Sigil of Auren, whole and awake, held out like an olive branch.

And the riders cutting them down anyway, because an olive branch from a beaten people is just another kind of weapon, and the empire's generals had learned that lesson written in their own dead.

Gideon tried to look away. There was nowhere to look away *to*. He was inside the memory the way a man is inside a fever, and the fever showed him everything.

He saw the last stand at the mountain's root, where the clans made their final offer and the empire made its final answer, and the answer was fire. He saw a keeper—a woman with Pemberton cheekbones and braids threaded with river-stones—carry the Sigil up through the burning pass while arrows found her back, one after another, and she kept climbing because someone had to reach the Heartstone before the seal failed and the thing beneath the mountain woke hungry.

He saw her press her bleeding hand to the door he had opened two days ago, and he heard her speak the words he himself had spoken, and he felt—through her, as her—the seal close over the waking dark like a hand closing over a candle flame. Not smothering it. Holding it. Keeping it small enough to live with.

The war ended that day. Not because anyone won. Because the mountain itself stood up between the armies, groaning, avalanches falling on both sides alike, and every soldier who survived it told the same story afterward: *the land refused us.* They called it the Wrath of Auren. The priests built a theology on it. The clans built a silence on it.

It had been a woman dying on a doorstep of stone, holding the door shut with the last of her strength.

Gideon came out of that vision gasping, though there was no air to gasp, only light.

* * *

The keepers came next.

They came one after another, each one a lifetime compressed into a handful of images, and he began to understand that this was the true archive—not the murals, not Wren's letter, but this living succession passed from keeper to keeper down the centuries like a cupped flame. Every hundred years, a Pemberton knelt where he knelt. Every hundred years, the Sigil showed them what it was showing him now, so that no keeper would ever bind themselves blind.

A fisherman's son in the age of the second empire, who had walked away from a wife and two daughters and never told them why, and who wept for forty years inside the stone until the weeping wore smooth as river rock.

A twin who took the bond that her sister had been meant for, because her sister had children and she had none, and who greeted eternity with a shrug that Gideon felt in his own shoulders, absurd and human across three hundred years.

An old man, once, the only time the line ran thin—a grandfather who came up the mountain when his grandson was still a babe, saying *let him have the years I've already spent.*

Each of them had stood where he stood. Each of them had seen the loophole first.

Because here it was, unfolding in the light like a flower opening in fast motion: the terms of the bond, written not in words but in understanding that arrived whole, the way you know your own name. *The seal renews by willing blood. The bond accepts any soul of the keeper's line who comes freely, knowing the cost.*

Any soul. Not the eldest. Not the named heir. Not the one who happens to carry the map through the snow.

Any willing Pemberton.

Hope hit him like a physical blow. He staggered in the light—and there was no body to stagger with, but he staggered anyway—because for one blazing instant he saw the shape of it: Wren. Wren had come up this mountain. Wren had read these same terms. Wren had known, and had turned back, and had carried the map south to find him instead, and died in his doorway with the weight of it unfinished—

No. No, that wasn't hope, that was horror. She hadn't refused to save herself. She'd been dying already; the letter said so; the wound in her side had been days old when she reached the city. She had run out of time and passed the burden to him, and the guilt of that had ridden in her eyes when she pressed the oilcloth into his hands, and he had thought it was fear for him, and it had been fear *of* what she was asking.

But the hope had a second edge, and it cut deeper: Sera.

Sera was not Pemberton-blooded. He knew that before the thought finished forming, knew it the way the Sigil knew things now, with a certainty that lived in the stone rather than in him. The bond wanted the line. The line was him. His mother, dead thirty years. Whatever kin she might have had among the clans, scattered or gone—the seer at High Cairn had shaken her head when asked; the braids in the locket portrait belonged to a family reduced to a scribe in a temple garret and a courier dying in the snow.

There was no one left but him.

The Sigil showed him that, too. Showed him the line stretched thin as wire across four centuries, fraying, snapping, until only a single strand remained, and the strand was kneeling on the Heartstone with his blood running into the mountain, and the light was waiting, patient as all the keepers before him, for him to understand what they had all understood:

That the loophole was not a door out. It was only proof that the choice had always been real. That every keeper could have walked away, and none had, and that was the whole of the magic—not the gold disc, not the ancient script, but generation after generation of ordinary frightened people looking at the price and paying it anyway.

*You could stop,* the light seemed to say, in no voice at all. *You could pull your hand from the stone and walk down the mountain and let the seal decay and live whatever years are left to you beside the woman who fought an Inquisitor for you. That has always been allowed. That is what makes this worth anything.*

And underneath it, gentler, almost sorry: *But there is no one else.*

* * *

He tried to argue.

He was a scribe; argument was his native tongue; he had spent his life finding the clause, the exception, the second column in the ledger. He reached for objections the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood.

*The clans—Brannoc wants the Sigil free—maybe a clan child carries the blood, some bastard branch of the line—*

The light showed him the genealogy, complete and closed, a tree with every living leaf accounted for. One leaf.

*Sera would give anything—she'd find someone, she'd spend her life searching—*

And the light showed him Sera as the Sigil saw her, which was the cruelest gift of all: not a solution but a person, scarred and stubborn and half-dead of a knife wound taken shielding him, sleeping somewhere below the snowline or fighting her way back up through the storm toward him at this very moment. Showing him that if he stepped aside—if he handed the search to her, made his death her life's errand—he would not be sparing her grief. He would be conscripting her into it. Thirty years from now she would be an old woman still climbing passes, still reading other people's bloodlines, still carrying the weight Wren had carried and he would have handed her.

He loved her. Somewhere between the cave and the climb and the knife at the Narrows, without either of them naming it, he had come to love her, and love meant he did not get to make his ending her beginning.

*There has to be another way,* he thought, one last time, a boy's objection, a debtor's plea.

The Sigil did not answer. It simply went on shining, and in its shining he felt the thing beneath the mountain shift in its long sleep—not waking, not yet, but turning over, the way a sleeper turns when the hand pressing the blanket down begins to slacken. He felt how much of the peace of the world was resting on his open palm. He felt the exact weight of it.

It was heavier than a life. That was the terrible arithmetic. All those years he had spent counting down his debt-bond, measuring his freedom in seasons remaining, believing his life was the largest sum he possessed—and now the ledger lay open and his life was a rounding error against what the seal held shut.

Wren's words came back to him across the snow: *the keeper's line ends with you, little brother—forgive me for the weight.*

He forgave her. He found, examining himself with the strange clarity of the light, that he had forgiven her since the night he buried her body under the willow roots, and that what he had been carrying since then was not anger but inheritance.

*All right,* he thought. *I see it. I see all of it.*

The light gathered itself, pleased or sorrowful or both, and began to pour the binding deeper into him—

And that was when he heard her voice.

Not in the vision. In the chamber. Thin and ragged and furious, echoing off the Heartstone, cutting through the ancient light like a blade through gauze:

*"GIDEON!"*

His eyes—his real eyes, his failing, mortal, magnificent eyes—tried to open. Through the blaze he caught a fragment of the world: the doorway behind him, figures moving, steel catching starlight, and Sera Quill standing in it with blood on her coat and a drawn blade in her fist, screaming his name at a man coming fast across the chamber floor.

Voss. Voss had reached the plateau. Voss was *here*—

The rite lurched. The light stuttered around him like a guttering lamp, and Gideon felt the binding pause, poised, incomplete, waiting on a will that had just been shattered into a dozen pieces by the sound of the woman he loved walking into his death.

He had one heartbeat to choose again.

He chose.
