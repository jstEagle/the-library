## 33. Steel and Starlight

The chamber was drowning in light.

It poured up through the floor in slow columns, gold shot through with something older than gold, and every column hummed a note I felt in my teeth. Gideon stood at the center of it with his palms flat against the Heartstone, blood running from his opened hand down the stone's face like it had always belonged there. His eyes were closed. He was smiling.

I didn't have time to think about what that smile meant.

Voss came off the stairs like a landslide given legs.

I'd heard him coming—boots on the frozen steps, no attempt at quiet, because Malachi Voss had never once in his life tried to be quiet about what he believed—but hearing him and being ready for him were different animals. He crossed the chamber in six strides, sword already drawn, and the light bent around him as if the mountain itself flinched.

"Gideon!" he roared. "Step away from it!"

Gideon didn't move. Didn't open his eyes. The sigil beneath his hands brightened.

So it was me or nobody.

I put myself between them.

He was bigger than me. Faster than I'd expected, too—I'd watched him fight once before, back in Hollowmere, all economy and terrible patience, a man who treated violence like paperwork. But there was nothing patient in him now. He swung at me the way a man swings when he thinks the world is ending, which is to say: wide, hard, and without any interest in what happens after.

I caught the blade on my knife's crossguard and the impact went down my arm into my shoulder, into the wound Brannoc's surgeon had stitched three days ago, and white pain exploded behind my eyes. I gave ground. He pressed. Steel rang off steel twice more and each time my arm sang a little worse, and the light kept rising around us like water filling a hold.

"You don't understand what you're protecting," Voss said. He wasn't even breathing hard. "I've seen what happens when the old powers wake. I buried forty men at Redwater Ford because a clan shaman lit one candle of the old craft. One candle, Quill. This is a bonfire."

"Then let it burn," I said, and went for his knee.

He read it—of course he read it, the man probably read letters before they were sealed—and turned the cut aside, but turning cost him a half-step of balance and I spent it gladly, driving my shoulder into his chest. We went down together onto stone that was warm now, impossibly warm, warm as skin, and the mountain hummed under us like a struck bell that refused to fade.

His elbow found my ribs. My knife found his sword wrist. Neither of us accomplished anything we wanted.

We broke apart, circling, and I got my first good look at him in the rising light and understood, suddenly, why Gideon had said *he isn't evil, Sera, he's worse, he's certain.*

Voss looked afraid.

Not of me. Not of dying. He looked like a man watching a flood come for a village he'd spent his whole life building levees for, and the levees were failing, and he knew—he *knew*—that everything on the far side of the wall would drown.

"It doesn't have to end this way," I said. "Walk out. Report whatever you want. Nobody here is going to chase you."

"The Sigil wakes," he said, "and within ten years the clans remember what they were before the empire. Within twenty, the passes run red again. I have seen it. I have *buried* it." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated himself for it; I watched the hatred flicker across his face like weather. "There is no version of peace that survives this."

"There might be," I said. "You just won't live long enough to see it."

He came at me again.

This time I was ready for the width of his anger. I let him commit, let the big overhand swing carry his weight forward, and stepped inside it—the way Gideon had taught me, gods help us both, three weeks ago in a freezing cave, laughing, saying *you're stronger than everyone, so everyone will expect you to use it*—and drove my knife up under his guard.

The point stopped against his breastplate. Slid. Found the gap at his armpit where the plates didn't meet, the same gap every soldier has carried since men first started wearing steel, and went in two inches before his arm clamped down and trapped the blade.

He made a sound. Not a scream. A sound like a door closing.

Then he hit me with his fist, once, in the temple, and the chamber went sideways and soft, and I was on my knees with blood in my eye and my knife still buried in his side, and he was standing over me with his own sword raised, and the light was everywhere, and none of it mattered because I could not make my body answer.

"Sera—" Gideon's voice, distant, straining. "Sera, *no—*"

Voss looked down at me.

And didn't strike.

I'll think about that moment for the rest of my life, however long it turns out to be. The sword hung above me and the man holding it stood in a pillar of ancient gold light with my knife in his ribs and his whole certainty burning in his face, and he did not strike. Because Gideon had cried out. Because somewhere under thirty years of doctrine and dead friends and righteous arithmetic, Malachi Voss was still a man who heard a woman's name said in fear by someone who loved her, and something in him flinched.

Or maybe he just saw my eyes. Maybe there was something in them he recognized. I don't know. I've decided not to ask him.

His sword arm trembled.

Then Gideon said, quietly, "Malachi."

Just the name. No plea in it. And Voss turned his head, and I turned mine, and we both looked at the center of the chamber where Gideon Pemberton stood with his blood on the Heartstone and his eyes finally open, looking at us both with an expression I had never seen on him before—not fear, not resolve, but something like *pity*, and pity was the worst thing he could have worn, because pity meant he had already seen how this ended.

"It's not a weapon," Gideon said. "You know that. You've known since the pass—you read the waystation script faster than I did. It's a seal. It holds back exactly the war you're describing." He drew a breath. "Renewing it is the only thing keeping your empire's peace alive. You just can't admit it, because if the seal matters, then everything you did to the clans to keep them bound was unnecessary. They were never the threat. The forgetting was."

"Stop," Voss whispered.

"Say it," Gideon said. "Say what it is. Out loud. In this room. I think the mountain's listening."

The light pulsed. Under my knees the stone was warm as a living chest, and I swear to every god I've ever cheated that I felt it breathe.

Voss stood there with his sword shaking and my knife in his ribs and thirty years of conviction pressing down on him like snowload on a roof, and I watched the roof bend. I watched it crack. I watched a man begin, in real time, to lose the only story that had ever held his life together.

"I read it," he said at last, barely audible. "At the waystation. I read it and I burned the page." His voice came apart. "Because if it's true—if it's been true for a hundred years—then the uprising, the reprisals, the *bonding houses*, all of it—we did it to children, Quill. We did it to *children* to prevent a war that a rock was preventing anyway—"

"Malachi." Gideon's voice was gentle now, unbearably gentle. "Put it down."

The sword fell. It rang against the stone and the note it made joined the mountain's hum and faded into it, absorbed, forgiven, gone.

Voss sank. Not knelt—sank, the way a building sinks, all at once and slowly at the same time, until he was on his knees in the gold light with blood seeping between his fingers where he held his side, and he looked at Gideon the way a man looks at the son he failed.

"They'll ask me what happened here," he said. "The Temple. The Chancellery. They'll want a report."

"Tell them the truth," I said, hauling myself upright against the Heartstone's warmth. My head was still ringing. "For once in your life."

He laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and small. "The truth? That a scribe and a smuggler and a handful of clan savages defeated an Inquisitor of Vessia with a *rock*?" He shook his head. "They'll send more. You understand that. Whatever happens in this room tonight, they'll send more, and next time it won't be me, and I won't be here to lose slowly enough for you to survive it."

"Then lie," Gideon said.

We both looked at him.

"Lie," he repeated. "Tell them the shrine collapsed. Tell them the Sigil was destroyed in the fighting—that the pass came down, that nothing remains, that the threat is ended forever. Give them a story they can file and forget." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face. "You've spent your career deciding what the empire needs to hear. Decide this."

Voss stared at him for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—calculation, maybe, or surrender, or the first small seed of the bargain he would eventually make, though I couldn't have named it then. All I knew was that the sword stayed on the floor and the man stayed on his knees and the killing was over.

And then the mountain exhaled.

I don't know how else to say it. The whole chamber—the whole *mountain*, the whole dark spine of Thornfell beneath us—took a breath it had been holding for a hundred years, and let it go. The light didn't flare; it *settled*, sinking back down through the floor like water finding its level, and the humming note resolved into something low and vast and satisfied, and every hair on my body stood up, and I felt—this is the part I'll never manage to explain to anyone who wasn't there—I felt *noticed*. The mountain noticed us. All three of us. Weighed us. And found us, somehow, acceptable.

Gideon gasped.

I was moving before I decided to, dropping beside him, catching his shoulders as his knees buckled. He was heavy in a new way, dense, as if the mountain had already begun adding itself to him. His skin was cool and faintly luminous, veins of gold tracing up from his hands along his forearms like frost forming in reverse.

"Gideon. Hey. Look at me. Look at *me.*"

He looked. His eyes had changed—there was starlight in them now, actual starlight, pinpricks drifting slow in the brown—and he was still smiling, and the smile was calm in a way that terrified me more than any scream could have.

"It doesn't hurt," he said. "Sera. Listen to me. It doesn't hurt. It's not what I thought. It's not—"

"What is it?" I grabbed his face. "What did it do to you?"

"It made room," he said simply. "That's all. It made room, and asked me to fill it."

Behind us, Voss made a small broken noise. I didn't turn around. I couldn't spare the attention. Everything I had—all of it, every scrap—was in the man in my arms while the gold climbed past his elbows and the starlight spread through his eyes and the mountain hummed its long contented note.

"Stay with me," I said. "Whatever this is. Whatever it takes. Stay."

"That was always the price," he said. "I told you I'd pay it."

"You told me you'd *die* for it. Dying I could have survived. This—" My voice went to pieces. I let it. There was no one left in the world whose opinion of me mattered except him, and he already knew. "This is worse. This is you staying and me leaving."

His hand rose—slower than it used to move, deliberate as continental drift—and touched my cheek. Warm. Warmer than human. Warm as the stone.

"No," he said softly. "Look again."

And I did look, and I saw it: the door behind him, the great sealed door with the Pemberton crest, and it was no longer sealed. It stood open onto darkness that was not empty but *inhabited*—a threshold, a passage, a place where a voice could travel out and a voice could travel in. The binding hadn't shut him away. It had made him the door.

"Solstice," he said. "Come at solstice. Put your hand on the stone. I'll be listening."

"You'd better be," I said, and ruined it by crying, and didn't care.

The last of the light sank into the floor. The sigil dimmed to a faint warm glow beneath Gideon's palm, a heartbeat in the rock. Somewhere far below, or perhaps very near, the mountain settled into its new arrangement with itself, the way a house settles around a newly lit fire.

When I finally turned, Voss was still kneeling where he'd fallen, staring at the open door, at the murals beyond it, at centuries of Pemberton keepers painted in fading pigment reaching back further than the empire itself. His hand was pressed to his wound. His sword lay untouched at his knee. He looked like a man reading his own obituary and finding, to his horror, that it was kind.

"She beat you," I said to him, nodding at the painted figures. "All of them. Every keeper for four hundred years. They beat you before you were born, and you never even knew you were fighting."

He didn't answer. But he didn't reach for the sword either.

Outside, far down the gorge, I could hear the sounds of Brannoc's warriors finishing their grim work—shouts, a horn, the long silence after. Dawn was some hours off. There were wounded to tend and dead to bury and a story to invent that would buy these mountains a generation of peace, and I was the only one left standing who could tell it.

But first I sat with Gideon while the gold finished climbing, and he told me about the visions—the war, the keepers, the hundred years stretching ahead of him like a road—and I held his cooling-warming hand and memorized the sound of his voice, because I already knew that from tomorrow onward, I would only ever hear it through stone.

* * *

Dawn found the three of us at the plateau's edge: a smuggler with a bandaged head, an Inquisitor with a knife wound and no sword, and a keeper who could no longer feel the cold.

Below us the clouds had broken apart, and the whole world lay revealed—clan valleys smoking with morning fires, the imperial road a grey thread winding south, the shrine pass choked with the wreckage of battle and the small moving shapes of survivors. Somewhere down there, Brannoc was counting his dead. Somewhere down there, Sera Quill's brother had died years ago in a bonding house, and she didn't know yet, and soon she would, and I would not be able to hold her hand when she learned it.

"I'll need a week," Voss said. His voice was flat, emptied out, a well after the rope breaks. "To reach the garrison. To write the report. To—" He stopped. Started again. "The shrine collapsed. The Sigil is destroyed. The pass is impassable. I'll need witnesses willing to swear to it."

"You have them," I said.

He nodded once. Then he looked at Gideon—at the gold-veined hands, the starlight eyes, the impossible calm—and something moved through his face that I will spend years failing to name. Regret, maybe. Or envy. Or the beginning of whatever strange penance a man performs when he's been spared by the people he came to kill.

"For what it's worth," Voss said, "the empire owes this mountain an apology four hundred years overdue. I'm not the man to deliver it. But I know how to buy it time." He turned toward the stair. Paused. "Keeper."

Gideon lifted his head.

"When the next one comes," Voss said quietly, "and there is always a next one—make sure they find nothing worth climbing for."

"They won't," Gideon said. "Unless they come with clean hands."

Voss held that gaze for a moment longer. Then he went down into the morning, alone, carrying his lie like a lantern.

I stayed until the sun cleared the eastern peaks and lit the Heartstone gold, and Gideon's face with it, and I pressed my palm flat against the warm rock one last time before the climb down.

"Solstice," I reminded him.

"I know," said the mountain, in his voice. "I'll count the days. I've got nothing but time now."

"Arrogant," I said, wiping my face. "Insufferable."

"I learned from the best."

I shouldered my pack, checked my knives out of habit, and started down the trail toward the valley, toward the dead, toward the long ride south and the truth I was now the only living soul equipped to carry.

Behind me, faint and steady as a second heartbeat, the mountain hummed—and if I'm honest, which I rarely am, I'd swear the note it chose sounded almost like a lullaby.
