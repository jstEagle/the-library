## 35. Aftermath at the Plateau

Dawn came up the color of old bone.

Sera stood at the edge of the Heartstone plateau with her arms wrapped around herself and watched the light crawl down the far peaks, and for a long time she could not make herself turn around. Behind her was the sealed door. Behind her was the mountain that had swallowed Gideon Pemberton whole and left nothing behind but warmth in the stone and a silence she could feel through the soles of her boots.

She had buried men before. She'd dug graves in mud outside Vessa harbor, in frozen ground above Kell's Crossing, once in the hold of a ship when there was no other choice and the sea got what it got. She knew how to do this work. That didn't mean her hands wanted to start.

But the dead were waiting, and the living were watching, so she turned around and started.

There were nine of them. Brannoc's warriors who had held the narrows while she and Gideon climbed — eight clan-folk and one young scout whose name she never learned, though Brannoc said it twice over the bodies last night, low and formal, the way you say a name when you're putting it into the ground along with the man. Nine graves to dig in plateau soil that was half stone and all of it cold.

Brannoc worked beside her without a word. He had taken a sword-cut across the forearm and refused every offer of binding beyond a strip of linen he tied himself, one-handed, teeth pulling the knot tight. He dug like a man punishing the earth. Sera let him. She'd seen grief do that before too — seen it in her own mirror, years ago, after the Temple took Tam. You either wept or you swung a shovel, and swinging a shovel at least produced something.

The clan women sang while they worked. Not a dirge — Sera had expected a dirge — but something older and stranger, a rising-and-falling line of sound that seemed less like mourning than like instruction, as if they were telling the dead where to go and how to get there. The words were in the old tongue. She caught maybe one in ten, and the ones she caught were about doors and roads and coming home by another way.

She thought: *he can probably hear this.* And then she thought: *I hope he can hear this,* and then she hated herself a little for hoping, because what kind of person hoped a man could hear his own funeral sung from inside a mountain?

The kind of person who had watched him walk into the light, that was who. The kind of person who had stood in that chamber with blood running down her side from Voss's knife and screamed his name and been answered — not with words, not at first, but with the sigil flaring under his palm like a heartbeat made visible, and then, just before the stone took him, with his voice, calm as a man saying goodnight:

*It doesn't hurt. Sera. It doesn't hurt.*

She put her back into the digging and did not think about it again until the third grave.

* * *

They laid the dead facing the mountain. Brannoc explained it in his flat, gravel way: the keepers slept below, and warriors preferred to sleep within shouting distance of their allies, even dead ones. Especially dead ones. He cairned each grave with stones from the plateau itself, and when the last cairn was raised he stood before it a long moment and then did something Sera hadn't seen a war-leader do — he knelt. All the way down, forehead nearly to the frozen ground, and stayed there while the wind moved his hair.

Sera looked away. Some things weren't meant for an audience, even an audience of one smuggler with a stitched-up knife wound and nowhere else to be.

The wounded came next. Voss's column had broken in the gorge — Brannoc's delaying action had done its terrible work — and what remained of it had surrendered at first light, eleven soldiers kneeling in the snow with their weapons stacked and their eyes on the clan warriors circling them. Two of the soldiers were hurt badly. One had an arrow through the meat of his shoulder; the other had taken a clan axe across the thigh and was gray-faced and shivering, bleeding through a field dressing that was more mud than cloth.

"Kill them," one of Brannoc's younger warriors said. Not angrily. Almost reasonably. "They came to burn the shrine."

"They came because a man told them to," Sera said. She heard her own voice come out harder than she intended. "And that man is lying up there right now with a broken arm and a worse conscience, and he's not going anywhere. These ones are just tired."

"Tired men with swords burned Hollowmere's granary two winters past."

"And if you kill them, their mothers burn your villages for a generation. I've watched how empires grieve." She stepped between the young warrior and the prisoners, which was stupid, given the state of her side, and everyone present knew it was stupid, which was perhaps why it worked. "You want peace? Peace starts with somebody being the first one to put the knife away. Usually it's whoever's tired of burying people."

Brannoc ended it with a grunt and a word in the old tongue. Prisoners' wounds would be tended. Their weapons would stay with the clans. They would be walked down off the mountain alive and released at the border with a message for the empire: *the shrine stands, the keeper keeps, come again and the passes will eat you.*

Sera knelt beside the gray-faced soldier with the thigh wound and did the work herself. Cleaned it, packed it, bound it, gave him watered wine from her own flask when he shook too hard to hold the cup. He kept looking at her face like he was trying to place her.

"You're the smuggler," he said finally. "Quill. There's a price on you in three provinces."

"There's a price on everybody worth knowing." She tied off the bandage. "Hold still."

"Why?" His voice cracked. "Why help us? We came to kill your—"

He stopped. Didn't know the word. Neither did she, quite. *Friend* wasn't big enough anymore. *More* wasn't shaped right.

"Because someone once helped me when I didn't deserve it," she said, "and I've been paying it forward badly ever since, and today I'm going to do it properly. Drink your wine."

* * *

Voss sat apart, at the plateau's eastern lip, where the ground fell away toward the empire he had served for thirty years. They had splinted his arm where the rockfall had caught him — Sera had done it, because no one else would touch him, and because his broken radius was set crooked and it offended her craftsmanship. He hadn't thanked her. He hadn't spoken at all since the chamber. Since Sera had beaten his sword out of his hand and stood over him with his own knife at his throat and found, to her lasting surprise, that she couldn't push.

Not mercy. She wanted to be clear with herself about that. It wasn't mercy. It was that killing him felt like letting him win — like confirming everything he believed about the clans, about her, about the whole bloody world being a contest where the strong did the necessary thing and called it justice. Gideon had spent his whole life refusing contests like that. She wasn't going to lose one on his behalf now.

So Voss sat with his crooked-set arm and watched the sun come up over the empire, and Sera left him to it, because she had somewhere better to be.

The sealed door waited at the heart of the ruined shrine, and it was beautiful, which was somehow the worst part. The explosion of light during the rite had cracked the old chamber open to the sky, torn away centuries of dark, and now morning poured down into the deep place like water finding its level. The door itself — the Pemberton door, the one his blood had opened — stood intact amid the ruin, black stone shot through with veins of silver that pulsed, very slowly, very faintly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Like someone breathing in his sleep.

Sera crossed the threshold alone. The others knew better than to follow. Even Brannoc hung back, and Brannoc feared nothing she'd ever seen.

She sat down with her back against the stone, right where the veins ran closest together, and pulled her knees up, and for a while she just breathed. Her side ached. Her hands were raw from digging. Somewhere behind her, the clan women had begun singing again, softer now, a working song or a mending song, and the sound drifted down into the chamber and pooled around her like warm water.

"Right," she said finally. Her voice sounded enormous in the quiet. "I don't actually know how this works. So I'm just going to talk, and you're going to listen, and if you can't hear me then I'm talking to a door, and honestly, Gideon, after everything, I've had worse conversations."

The silver vein nearest her cheek brightened. Just slightly. Just enough.

Her breath went out of her all at once.

"You can hear me." She laughed, and it came out wet, and she wiped her face with the back of her hand, impatient with herself. "Of course you can hear me. Of course. Why would the universe give me one easy thing and one impossible thing when it could give me both impossible things at once?"

*Hello, Sera.*

His voice didn't come through the air. That was the strange, wonderful, unbearable truth of it. It came through the stone, through her spine where she leaned against it, through the bones of her skull, as if she were hearing him speak from inside her own chest. Calm. Warm. Unmistakably him.

"How are you doing this?" she whispered.

*Practicing. I've had several hours and nothing else to do. The mountain and I are still negotiating the terms.*

"What are the terms?"

*A long silence, and then:* *Mostly it wants to know what I know. I'm teaching it the roads. Smuggling routes, mostly. It finds them fascinating. Apparently no one has ever told the Thornfell range about the ferry crossing at Marrow Bend.*

Despite everything — despite the graves on the plateau above her and the ache in her side and the eleven enemies sitting in the snow — Sera laughed. A real laugh, startled out of her, echoing off the ancient walls.

"You're teaching the mountain to smuggle."

*I'm teaching the mountain geography. What it does with the information is its own affair.* A pause. *How is your side?*

"Stitched. Ugly, but stitched. How's yours?"

*I don't have a side anymore. Or I have all of them. It's difficult to explain. I can feel the snowmelt starting in the high passes. I can feel Brannoc's people walking on the south trail — their footsteps come up through me like rain. I can feel you best of all.* The vein beside her brightened again, gently, like a hand reaching. *You're leaning on my left shoulder.*

She shifted, absurdly, to check. The silver ran thickest exactly there.

"Gideon—"

*Don't.* Gentle. Firm. *Don't apologize. Don't grieve at me yet. It's barely been a night, and I need you to be angry at me a little longer, because anger will keep you moving, and moving is how you survive the winter, and surviving the winter is the only thing I ask.*

"That's not the only thing you ask. You asked me to ride south. You asked me to tell the truth. You asked me—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "You asked me to leave you inside a mountain, Gideon. Nobody asks that of a person and then gets to dictate terms."

*Fair,* he said softly. *Counteroffer, then. Come back often. Tell me about the road. Tell me about the ledgers. Tell me about whatever fool scheme you're building, because I know you, Sera Quill, and you've already got one.*

She stared at the glowing stone. "How do you know that?"

*Because you haven't cried since I finished speaking. You've been crying, yes — I felt it, don't argue — but the moment I finished, you stopped. Which means you've turned the grief into fuel, which means there's a plan, which means somewhere in that clever criminal head of yours you've decided what my death is *for*. So tell me. I'd like to know what I'm for.*

Sera pressed her forehead against the stone. It was warm. It was warm the way a hand is warm.

"The ledgers," she said. "Your copies. Every bonded scribe in the Temple district, every debt forged, every term extended illegally, thirty years of it, written in your neat little hand and hidden in the lining of your coat, which I have, because you made me take the coat." She swallowed. "I'm taking them to Vessa. To the Temple itself. I'm going to stand in front of whoever runs that marble mausoleum and read your ledger aloud until they either free every bonded scribe in the city or arrest me, and either way, the copies go out first. Twelve couriers. I've already sent word to four of them."

Silence in the stone. Then, quietly:

*Twelve couriers. You found my network.*

"I found your network, I recruited your network, and by midwinter your network is going to burn the debt-bond system to the ground in your name." She lifted her head. "That's what you're for. That, and being the most stubborn, decent, impossible man I ever met. Are you satisfied?"

Another silence. Longer. When his voice came again, it had gone rough in a way she'd only heard twice before — once in the cave, in the storm, when he'd taught her the word for *home* in the old tongue and pretended it was just vocabulary.

*Satisfied isn't the word.*

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

Above her, faintly, the clan song changed key. Sera listened for a moment, and then she laughed again — quieter this time, wondering.

"Gideon. They're singing about you. Brannoc says the old verses have a place for keepers. Says there hasn't been one to sing about in a hundred years, and the song's been waiting." She traced one silver vein with her fingertip, and it warmed under her touch like skin. "You're in the song now. You understand that? A hundred years from now, clan children are going to learn your name before they learn their own grandmothers'. *Silas and the Sigil* — that's what they'll call it, mark me. The story always needs a name, and yours is the one that stuck."

*Silas,* he repeated, tasting it. *Wren used to call me that. When we were small. Before I knew she was Wren.* A pause, and when he spoke again there was wonder in it, and sorrow, and something that was neither. *She knew. All those years, she knew, and she carried it alone to my doorstep, and she died in the snow rather than give the map to Voss. And now the mountain tells me she's here too, Sera. In the stone. The keepers don't leave. They wait. She's been waiting a hundred years for someone from our line to climb high enough to say hello.*

Sera closed her eyes. "Then say it for both of us."

*I do. Every day. But I'd rather you said it too. She'd have liked you. She had your exact talent for telling powerful men exactly how disappointing they are.*

"Oh, she and I would have gotten on *famously*."

The light in the veins pulsed — and Sera would have sworn, would have bet her ship and her name on it, that it was laughter.

They stayed like that a long while, as the sun climbed and the chamber filled with gold. She told him about the graves, and the cairns, and Brannoc kneeling. She told him about the prisoner with the arrow whose name was Aldous and who had wept when she bandaged him, nineteen years old and terrified, and how she'd told him the same thing someone once needed to tell her: *you're not what they made you do.* She told him about the plan, all of it, the couriers and the timing and the risk, and he listened the way he had always listened — completely, without once interrupting to tell her she was being reckless, which was how she knew he understood it was already decided.

When she finally rose to go, her legs were stiff and the light had gone full morning, and she stood a moment with her palm flat against the door.

"I'll be back before solstice," she said. "Probably sooner. Don't teach the mountain anything I wouldn't do."

*No promises. The mountain has excellent instincts.*

She was three steps toward the light when his voice came one last time through the stone, quick and quiet, the way a man speaks when he's remembered something urgent and doesn't want to make a ceremony of it.

*Sera. One more thing. Voss — before you ride south, talk to him. Truly talk. I've been listening to him all morning through the plateau's edge, and there's something breaking loose in him. Something that started in that chamber when the Sigil showed him what it shows. He came up this mountain certain, and certainty is the most dangerous thing a man can carry, and I think he's setting it down.* A pause. *Be careful. But be there.*

She looked back at the door, at the slow pulse of silver, and nodded once — knowing he'd feel it, knowing he'd know what it meant.

Then she climbed out into the daylight, and went to find the Inquisitor of the Empire of Vessia, sitting alone at the edge of the world with a broken arm and a war's worth of certainty coming apart in his hands.
