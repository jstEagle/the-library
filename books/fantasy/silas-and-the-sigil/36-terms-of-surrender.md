## 36. Terms of Surrender

Malachi Voss had knelt before emperors, before archons, before the veiled statue of Vessia herself in the deepest vault of the Temple, and he had never once felt small.

He felt small now.

He sat on a stone at the edge of the Heartstone plateau with his hands bound behind him—not roughly; the clans did not waste cruelty on the defeated—and watched dawn come up over Thornfell like a slow fire. The mountain was breathing. He could not have said how he knew that, only that the air moved wrong for wind, in and out, patient as a sleeping animal, and every time it exhaled the snow on the far ridges glittered as though someone had shaken a jar of stars.

Behind him, through the sealed door of black stone, something vast and quiet had finished waking.

His men were dead or scattered or kneeling in rows under the watch of clan warriors who still held their spears as if they expected the empire to rise out of the snow and take the pass back by force of numbers alone. Brannoc stood apart from his warriors, one arm bound to his side, blood frozen black along his jaw. And Sera Quill—the smuggler, the thief, the woman who should have been nothing more than an instrument—stood ten paces away with her sword point resting on the stone between them, not raised, not lowered, watching him the way you watch a snake you've decided not to kill yet but haven't forgiven either.

"You've been sitting there an hour," she said.

"I'm composing myself."

"Take longer. I'm enjoying it."

Voss almost smiled. That was the obscenity of it—that he *could* almost smile, here, at the end of everything he'd built his life around. Thirty years in the Temple's grey service. Twenty-two years since the uprising at Redwater Ford, where he had buried four friends in frozen ground and told himself the lie that would organize the rest of his existence: *this must never happen again.* Every interrogation, every execution, every village burned on paper rather than in fact because he preferred paper—he had done all of it so that the mountains would stay quiet and the lowlands would stay whole and no more boys like the boy he had been would come home carrying their friends' names stitched into their sleeves.

And now the Sigil was awake anyway. The seal was renewed. The peace he had murdered to preserve had been preserved instead by a scribe with ink stains on his fingers who had walked into a mountain to die.

The joke was old enough that Voss could appreciate its craftsmanship.

Footsteps crunched behind him. He turned as far as his bonds allowed. Sera Quill crouched, took a knife from her belt, and cut the cords from his wrists in two strokes.

"Brannoc wants to talk," she said. "Before they decide what to do with you."

"And what are my options?"

"Fewer than you'd like." She stood, sheathing the knife. "But more than you deserve. Come on. Get up slow."

* * *

They made him stand before the sealed door.

It was worse up close. The Pemberton crest carved above the lintel had filled with light since the rite completed—a soft gold, banked like coals, and when Voss looked at it too long he felt the same sensation he'd felt once as a child, pressing his ear against the temple floor during the consecration of a new altar: that the stone was not empty. That something on the other side knew he was there.

He kept his face still. Thirty years of practice. His face was the best weapon the Temple had ever given him, and he used it now out of habit more than hope.

Brannoc faced him across a stretch of trampled snow. The clan chief's warriors formed a loose crescent behind him, and beyond them, down the slope, the dead lay where the fighting had left them—clan and imperial together, indistinguishable under the fresh fall, which struck Voss as the first honest thing the mountain had shown anyone.

"The man inside," Brannoc said. "The keeper. You came to kill him."

"I came to destroy the Sigil," Voss said. "Him being inside it was—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "No. That's not true. By the end, I knew what the rite required. I came anyway."

Murmurs along the crescent of spears. Brannoc didn't move.

"Why?"

"Because I believed a renewed Sigil would bring war." Voss let the words out evenly, one at a time, the way he'd been trained to deliver confessions extracted from others. "I believed the empire could not survive its return. I believed the clans would use it to break us, and the breaking would cost a hundred thousand lives on both sides of these mountains. I believed it so completely that when I learned the truth—that it was a seal, not a weapon—I decided no one could be trusted with that truth. Not my own superiors. Not the Archon. No one."

"And now?"

Voss looked past Brannoc, at the door, at the light in the crest.

"Now I think I spent thirty years guarding a door that was already guarded," he said. "By better hands than mine."

* * *

They gave him a fire, a blanket, and a bowl of barley soup, which was the most hospitality the Temple of Vessia had ever extended to a prisoner and therefore, Voss reflected, another mark against it. He ate because his body insisted, and while he ate, Sera Quill sat down across the flames from him without asking leave, which he supposed she had earned.

"You knew," she said. "About the seal. Before we reached the shrine."

"I suspected after Hollowmere. I confirmed it three days before you did." He set the bowl down. "My scouts captured a waystation keeper on the north trail. An old woman who tended the Ellow shrine before the Temple closed it. She talked. They're usually eager to talk, once they understand what happens if they don't."

"She told you the renewal needed willing blood."

"She told me the renewal needed *Pemberton* blood. The rest I worked out." He watched the fire. "I sent riders to close the pass because I intended to stop the rite. Not because I wanted the Sigil for the Temple. Because I wanted it gone, and the only way to make certain it stayed gone was to make certain no one ever reached it again."

"But then you learned there was no destroying it. Only renewing it. Only feeding it."

"Yes."

"So your choice was a living seal with a scribe inside it, or a dying seal and a war." Her eyes were flat and dark in the firelight. "And you chose the war."

"I chose what I believed was the lesser death." Voss met her gaze. "You disagree. Good. Someone should. But don't flatter yourself into thinking I wanted it. I have buried enough people, Quill. I simply believed I was choosing the smaller grave."

"And now?"

"Now I'm choosing differently."

She leaned forward. "Why should I believe anything you say? You forged a contract to use me. You told me my brother was dead—you didn't even check, did you? You just said it because it was useful."

Voss held her stare and did not look away, because looking away would have been mercy and she hadn't asked for any.

"No," he said. "I didn't check. Your brother's name was Tam Quill, taken by the Temple's foundling levy eleven years ago, assigned to the scriptorium at Cairnlow. I had a clerk pull the record when I needed leverage on you, and I used the ambiguity of the record to tell you what would hurt most. For what it is worth—which I know is nothing—the record shows a transfer to a lay school in the river provinces six years ago. If he lived, he likely still lives. Whether he remembers having a sister, I cannot say."

Sera Quill went very still. It was the stillness of a woman deciding whether to cry or kill, and Voss had seen it often enough to respect it, and this time—only this time—he wished he weren't the cause of it.

"That's the second true thing you've given me today," she said finally, voice rough. "What do you want for it?"

"To negotiate," said Malachi Voss. "Terms of surrender."

* * *

Brannoc heard him out without interrupting, which Voss counted as a good sign. Clan chiefs who interrupted were performing for their warriors. Clan chiefs who listened were actually considering.

"My report to the Temple will read as follows," Voss said. "The Inquisitorial column engaged clan forces at the Narrows and pursued remnant fighters to a high shrine in the Heartstone passes. The shrine housed the artifact known as the Sigil of Auren. In the course of the engagement, the shrine's foundations failed. The chamber collapsed. The Sigil was destroyed. Casualties included the bonded scribe Gideon Pemberton, the courier known as Wren, and"—he paused, and made himself continue—"the majority of my own command. I will carry scars consistent with the account. I have several already. I intend to acquire the rest honestly."

Silence, except the wind.

"The mountain collapsed?" Brannoc said at last. "This mountain?" He gestured at the entire breathing bulk of Thornfell, serene under its snow. "They'll send surveyors."

"They'll send surveyors to the wrong pass. I will give them the wrong pass. I have thirty years of experience telling the Temple what it wishes to hear about these mountains, and the Temple has thirty years of experience believing me, because the alternative is admitting it knows less than its own Inquisitor." Voss folded his hands. "There will be no expedition. There will be no second column. There will be a commendation, possibly a pension, and a closed file. I will see to all of it personally."

"Why?" It was one of the younger warriors, a woman with a bandaged forearm, spear still in hand. "Why would you lie for us? Yesterday you tried to kill us."

"Yesterday I tried to prevent a war," Voss said. "Today I am trying to prevent the next one. It is the same work. I have merely changed methods." He turned back to Brannoc. "Understand what I am offering and what I am not. I am not offering friendship. I am not offering conversion. I am an instrument of an empire I no longer fully believe in, and I will go on serving it, because the alternative is chaos and I have seen chaos eat villages. What I am offering is a generation. Perhaps two. Long enough for children on both sides of these mountains to grow up without learning each other's names from across a battlefield. Long enough for the clans to strengthen, for the debt-bonds to rot, for the Temple's grip to loosen the way all grips loosen eventually. When the truth surfaces—and it will surface; truth is patient—it should surface into a world that can survive knowing it."

"And if the Temple orders you to lead them back here?" Brannoc said.

"Then I will lose the file. Misfile it. Bury it under forty years of grain tariffs. I am very good at burying things." A ghost of dry humor crossed his face. "Ask anyone I've interrogated."

Brannoc studied him for a long moment. Then he spoke a few words in the clan tongue to the woman with the bandaged arm, and she spat—Voss chose to interpret it as ritual rather than personal—and stalked away.

"The moot must agree," Brannoc said. "Not just me. But I think they will. A lying enemy is worth ten honest ones, my grandmother used to say." He crouched to Voss's level, and up close his eyes were the pale grey of glacier ice. "One condition. The keeper. The man in the stone. His sister died getting the map to him. His mother died keeping the secret. My people bled holding the pass so he could finish it. He does not become a rumor. Whatever else the world forgets, the clans remember Gideon Pemberton. We will sing him. We will teach his name to our children. And someday, when the truth comes out, your empire will learn that the man you reported dead is the reason their grandchildren sleep safely—and your lie will be remembered as what it is. A gift. From us, and from him, to you."

Voss bowed his head. It was the closest thing to absolution anyone had offered him in thirty years, and it came from a man whose language he'd once forbidden in the temple courts.

"Agreed," he said.

* * *

They released him at midmorning with a horse, three days' rations, and his sword returned unbloodied, which Sera Quill handed over personally with an expression suggesting she'd argued for the other outcome and lost narrowly.

At the edge of the plateau, where the trail dropped toward the southern passes, Voss stopped. Turned. Looked back once at the sealed door with its banked golden light, at the warriors standing easy guard around it, at the thin line of woodsmoke rising from the camp where the wounded of both sides were being treated by the same hands.

"Gideon Pemberton," he said aloud, to no one, testing the weight of it. Then, quieter, in the direction of the stone: "I buried better men than me at Redwater Ford. I won't insult you by apologizing. But I'll keep your secret until my last breath, and I'll spend the years it buys you making sure they were worth something. That's the only prayer I know."

For a moment—half a heartbeat, no more—he thought the light in the crest brightened. Warmth touched his face like a hand.

Or perhaps it was only the sun clearing the ridge. He was no seer. He was a functionary with a talent for lies, and he had just told the largest one of his career, and the strange thing—the thing he would carry south with him like a coal in his chest—was how much lighter it made him feel than any truth he had ever delivered.

He mounted, and rode down out of the high country, and did not look back again.

* * *

Three days later, at a waystation below the snowline, Malachi Voss sat by a small fire and began to write his report in the beautiful, precise, temple-trained hand that had condemned dozens of souls and would now, for the first time, save a mountain.

*To the Archon of the Temple of Vessia, greetings. It is my duty to report the destruction of the artifact designated the Sigil of Auren...*

He wrote through the night. And far to the south, in a city of bells and ledgers, a smuggler with a stolen horse and a head full of names was already riding toward the Temple gates with a different kind of document sewn into her coat—and neither of them knew yet how soon their roads would cross again.
