## 37. The Long Ride South

The road south took eleven days. Sera counted them the way she'd once counted coin—by feel, by weight, by what each one cost her.

She rode a clan mare Brannoc's people had pressed on her at the plateau, a grey with frost-colored mane and an opinion about bridges. She rode alone. Voss had offered her an escort as far as the imperial border, and she had looked at him for a long moment before saying no.

"You'll need me in that city," he'd said. "You don't know how the Temple works from the inside."

"I know how it works from the outside," she'd answered. "That's the only view that matters."

He hadn't argued. He'd given her a sealed letter instead—a confession of sorts, or the closest thing a man like Malachi Voss could produce: testimony that the Sigil of Auren was destroyed, that the shrine beneath Thornfell had collapsed in the final assault, that no artifact remained. A lie written in his own careful hand, sealed with his own authority, buying the mountain a generation of peace. She carried it in her coat next to the other papers, the ones that mattered more.

The ledger copies. Forty-one pages in Gideon Pemberton's small, exact script, copied over years and hidden in the false bottom of a scribe's writing case—the same case he'd carried out of the Temple the night he fled, the same case she'd nearly sold twice before deciding not to. Names. Dates. Bond amounts. Interest rates that turned a five-year debt into twenty. And in the margins, in that same patient hand, the arithmetic of it: *Bonded at age nine. Debt accrued to age nineteen: triple the original sum. Projected freedom: never.*

Gideon had kept the ledgers the way he'd once mapped smuggling routes—as a private habit, a quiet rebellion, ink under the fingernails. He'd never told her they existed. She'd found them the morning after the binding, going through his things because going through his things was the only way left to touch him.

She'd sat on the cold stone of the plateau with forty-one pages spread around her like fallen leaves and cried until the sun came up. Then she'd dried her face, packed the pages in oilcloth, and started planning.

Grief was a currency. She'd learned that young, when her brother was taken. The Temple had taught her that sorrow buys nothing unless you spend it somewhere sharp.

So she rode south to spend it.

* * *

Vessia's capital smelled the way she remembered: coal smoke, river mud, incense drifting down from the Temple district like a lie told politely. She came in through the dock quarter, where nobody asked names, and took a room above a tavern called the Drowned Bell, which was exactly the kind of place she'd have chosen for a job even if she weren't hiding from half the empire's clerks.

She spent two days doing nothing but listening.

Taverns were ledgers too. Every drunk was an entry; every complaint, a column total. By the second night she knew that the Temple's debt registry office burned its duplicate records every seventh year—a "purification," the clerks called it—and that the last purification had been four years ago. She knew that three bonded scribes had died in the past winter of "fever," though fever rarely left bruises. She knew that Inquisitor Voss had returned from the north ten days after her, pale and quiet, and had reported to the Archon behind closed doors, and had come out with his rank intact and his eyes looking like a man who'd buried something alive.

She also knew, by the third night, that someone else was asking questions in the dock quarter. A woman. Plain dress, plain speech, asking after records, after bonded persons, after the Temple's registry practices. Asking carefully, the way you ask when you've been warned before.

Sera found her on the fourth evening, sitting alone with a cup of wine she wasn't drinking.

"Rosa Quill," Sera said, sliding into the opposite chair.

The woman went very still. "Nobody's called me that in fifteen years."

"I know. I'm the one who stopped calling you it." Sera set the oilcloth packet on the table between them, wrapped tight, tied with cord. "Hello, Tam."

Her sister stared at the packet, then at her, then at the packet again. Fifteen years since the Temple's collectors had taken a boy named Tam Quill off a smuggler's boat and entered him into the bond rolls as property. Fifteen years since Sera had hunted every record, bribed every clerk, followed every rumor south and found nothing but locked doors. Fifteen years, and here was the answer sitting across from her in a plain grey dress, with calluses on her hands that no smuggler ever earned.

"They keep the bonded in the undercroft of the Registry Annex," Tam said finally, voice flat as slate. "You'd never find it if you didn't already know. I found it because I was put in it." She turned her cup slowly. "I got out because a clerk misfiled my death certificate during the fever winter. On paper, Tam Quill is dead. So I became Rosa, and I've been working my way toward the inside ever since. Copying what I can. Waiting for something I could use."

"And then a stranger starts asking questions in your taverns."

"And then a stranger starts asking questions." Her sister's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I thought maybe I'd finally gone mad and invented you."

"No such luck." Sera pushed the packet across. "Open it."

Tam untied the cord with fingers that shook only slightly, and read. Sera watched her read—watched the stillness deepen, watched her lips move over Gideon's marginal arithmetic, watched her reach the page where a boy of nine had been entered into bondage against a debt of eleven crowns and emerge, on paper, owing ninety.

"This is one scribe?" Tam whispered.

"One of hundreds. Those are copies. The originals are in the Temple's own vaults, in Gideon's hand, matching the registry seals. Unimpeachable." She leaned forward. "And the man who wrote them gave everything he had so they'd see daylight. So we're going to make them see daylight."

"How? The Temple owns the courts. The Temple owns the censors. Half the Archon's council holds bonds themselves—they lend at interest through third parties. You'd be handing this to the wolves and asking them to audit their own teeth."

"I know." Sera tapped the packet. "Which is why we're not giving it to the courts. We're giving it to the printers."

Tam looked up sharply.

"There's a press in the Tanners' Row that's been printing broadsheets against the bond system for two years. Small runs, anonymous, nailed to doors at night. The Temple calls them sedition. I call them a start." She let herself smile, thin and sharp. "They've been waiting for proof. Nobody prints sedition without proof. Now they'll have forty-one pages of it, in the Temple's own authorized hand, with the registry's own countersignatures."

"They'll burn the press."

"They'll try. But paper's faster than fire, little brother. You taught me that—or I taught you, I forget which." The old joke landed wrong and right at once, and for a moment both sisters sat with it, the years between them heavy as wet rope.

"The bonded," Tam said quietly. "The ones still inside. What happens to them when this breaks?"

"That depends on how loud it breaks. Quietly, the Temple buries the scandal and the scribes stay in the dark. Loudly—" Sera spread her hands. "Loudly enough, and the Archon can't afford the optics. There's a faction on the council that's wanted the bond system reformed for years. They just needed a reason they couldn't be punished for wanting it. This is the reason. It arrives with a dead courier's seal, a living Inquisitor's complicity, and a martyr's name attached."

"A martyr's name," Tam repeated. "Whose?"

Sera looked at the window, where the Temple district glowed gold against the dusk, beautiful and rotten.

"Gideon Pemberton," she said. "Bonded scribe. Died in service to the realm. His last act was documenting the crime that owned him." She turned back. "We're going to make sure everyone in this city knows it."

* * *

It took six days to set the machine in motion, and every one of them felt like walking a plank over open water.

The printer was a squat, fearless woman named Odile who chewed tobacco and read the first three pages in silence while Sera stood in her shop breathing ink fumes. When Odile finished, she spat into the sawdust and said, "Where's the man who wrote this?"

"Dead."

"Shame." She squared the pages. "Dead men write best. Nobody can buy them." Then she named a price that would have emptied Sera's purse twice over, and Sera paid it without blinking, because some money was meant to be spent the way grief was meant to be spent—somewhere sharp.

They printed eight hundred copies. Tam's network of ex-bonded and sympathizers folded them through two sleepless nights, and on the morning of the seventh day, Vessia woke to find them everywhere. Nailed to the Registry Annex doors. Slipped under the Archon's council chamber. Scattered across the Temple steps like autumn leaves, so many that the sweepers gave up by noon and simply walked on them.

THE DEBT THAT GROWS IN THE DARK, said the headline, in letters tall enough to read from horseback. Below it, reproduced line for line, the bond entry of a nine-year-old boy. Below that, the arithmetic. Below that, the names—dozens of them, drawn from the margins of Gideon's copies, each one a life measured in interest.

By midday, a crowd had gathered at the Registry Annex that the city watch didn't dare disperse. By evening, three council members who held bonds through third parties had publicly divested—an act of cowardice dressed as principle, and Sera took it gladly, because leverage doesn't care what it wears.

By the second day, the Archon himself announced an inquiry.

"He moved fast," Tam observed, watching the proclamation being read aloud in the market square. "Too fast. He's not investigating. He's performing investigation. He'll sacrifice a few clerks, reform a clause or two, and bury the rest."

"Then we don't let him bury it." Sera pulled her collar up. "There's a hearing. Public, because he can't afford it to look secret. Which means witnesses. Which means us."

"You're not a citizen. You're a known smuggler with two outstanding warrants."

"I'm also the last person who saw Gideon Pemberton alive, the bearer of an Inquisitor's sealed testimony, and the only witness to what happened at Thornfell." She smiled without warmth. "Warrants are just debts, Tam. And I've learned recently what you do with debts."

"What's that?"

"You renegotiate them. From strength."

* * *

The hearing was held in the Hall of Reckoning, a chamber built to make petitioners feel small, all stone ribs and echoing height. Sera walked in wearing her best coat and her worst expression, and gave her testimony standing, without notes, in a voice that carried.

She told them about the courier Wren, who died carrying a map she'd bled for. She told them about a scribe indentured at nine for a debt of eleven crowns who served eighteen years and was offered freedom only in exchange for betraying a dying woman's trust. She told them about the undercroft of the Registry Annex, and watched the Archon's chancellor go grey, because that detail had never appeared in any broadsheet—it existed only in the Temple's private knowledge, and its presence in a public witness's mouth told the council exactly how deep the rot ran.

She did not tell them about the Sigil. She did not tell them about the shrine, or the keeper bound beneath the Heartstone, or the mountain that breathed. Some truths were not leverage. Some truths were sacred, and she had learned the difference at the cost of everything.

When the chancellor tried to discredit her—*a smuggler, a criminal, a woman of no fixed address*—she produced Voss's sealed letter and let the herald read it aloud. The chamber heard an Inquisitor of the Temple attest that the northern expedition's object had been destroyed, that the operation had concluded, and that certain irregularities in the debt registry had come to his attention during the course of his inquiry. Irregularities. From Voss. It was the driest possible confession, wrung out of a proud man one syllable at a time, and it landed in that chamber like a dropped chandelier.

Afterward, in the corridor, Tam caught her arm. "The bond rolls are being opened. All of them. There's a decree drafted—emancipation of minors' bonds, capped interest, the works. It won't pass clean. It'll pass gutted. But it'll pass."

"Gutted still frees people."

"It frees *some* people." Tam's voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "Sera. The undercroft. If they open the rolls, they have to open the undercroft. There are people down there who've been inside so long they don't remember their own names."

"Then we'll remind them." Sera looked out the high narrow window, south, toward mountains she could not see but could feel, the way you feel an old scar in cold weather. "One name at a time."

* * *

She went back to the Drowned Bell that night and allowed herself, for the first time in weeks, to sit still.

The tavern noise washed over her. Somewhere a fiddle played badly. Somewhere a merchant complained about the price of wool. Ordinary life, grinding on, indifferent to empires—which was, she supposed, the whole point of what they'd done. Not glory. Just the ordinary, defended.

She took out the letter she'd been carrying since the plateau. Not Voss's confession—the other one. Gideon's. Written in the cave at High Pass Winter, in his precise hand, tucked into her pack while she slept because he'd known she'd refuse it if he handed it to her.

*If you're reading this, it worked, or it didn't, and either way you're free of me now,* it began. *Don't waste the freedom on mourning. Spend it. You always knew better than anyone I ever met how to turn a loss into a weapon. That's not cynicism, Sera. It's craftsmanship.*

*I kept the ledgers for twelve years and never showed a soul. I told myself it was habit. It was hope. Hope that someday someone would care about those names besides me. I think I always knew it would be you.*

*Free them. All of them. Every name in every margin.*

*And come south in summer. The passes will be clear. I'll leave a light burning—you'll know where.*

She read it twice, folded it along its worn creases, and put it away.

Then she took out fresh paper, dipped a pen, and began to write—not to the council, not to the printers, but to the mountain. To the keeper beneath the Heartstone, who would feel her words the way he now felt weather, if the seers were right, and who had once told her, in a frozen cave with ice in his hair, that he'd never had anyone to write to before.

*Dear Gideon,* she wrote. *You should see the Temple steps. There's ink everywhere. Yours, mostly.*

*The rolls are opening. The undercroft next. Tam is home—I'll explain when I see you, and yes, I'm coming at solstice, so stop pretending you can't wait, because I can feel you pretending from here.*

*Forty-one pages, and every name in the margins walks free or will soon. You kept the ledger. I'm collecting the debt.*

*The light you left burning—I see it. Keep it lit.*

She signed it, sealed it, and set it aside for the rider heading north at dawn.

Outside, the fiddle found its tune at last, and the city went on being saved, one stubborn day at a time.

In the mountains, snow was falling on a plateau where a door stood sealed in stone. Inside it, something ancient and patient glowed warm at the thought of summer.

Sera smiled at the thought, finished her wine, and started making lists.

There was still work to do. There would always be work to do.

That, she'd decided, was the best kind of inheritance.
