## 39. The New Pilgrims

The first pilgrim arrived in spring, and she was nine years old.

Sera saw her coming from the plateau's edge—a small figure on the switchback trail below, moving with the deliberate, careful steps of a child who had been told a hundred times not to fall. Behind her came her mother, and behind them two more figures, then four, until Sera counted eleven souls winding up the mountain like beads on a string.

She didn't go down to meet them. She'd learned that much in three years of keeping this place. The mountain did its own welcoming.

"Someone's climbing," she said to the sealed door, because she still said things to it, out loud, when no one else was near enough to hear. "Eleven of them. One's carrying a drum."

*Clan children,* came the answer—not sound exactly, but something that lived in the stone under her palm and behind her ribs, warm as a hand pressed flat against her chest. *I can feel their feet. They walk like Brannoc walked.*

"They do," Sera said. "Brannoc's sister sent word. Half the high valleys are sending children this year."

*A whole generation that will grow up knowing the shrine is open.* A pause, and then, with the dry humor she'd come to love across three years of stone: *Voss would hate this.*

"Voss is teaching history at the Temple academy now." She settled onto her usual spot—a flat boulder worn smooth by her sitting, by her talking, by the slow patient work of her presence becoming part of the place. "Retired. Officially. He sends a letter every solstice. I don't answer."

*You could.*

"I know what he'd want me to say, and I'm not saying it."

The door didn't laugh—stone couldn't laugh—but she felt the warmth shift, the particular flicker that meant Gideon was amused, and that was enough. It had always been enough. That was the bargain they'd made without ever speaking it aloud: she got the world, and he got her voice through the rock, and between those two things they built something that neither of them had a better name for than *this*.

The children reached the plateau as the sun cleared the eastern ridge. Their teacher—an old clan woman named Maren, who had fought beside Brannoc at the Narrows and carried the scar to prove it—stopped at the boundary stones and waited, as custom now required, for the warden's welcome.

Sera stood. Her knees cracked. Three years of mountain winters were writing themselves into her body, and she resented every line of it.

"You're welcome here," she called, in clan tongue first, then empire speech, then the trade-pidgin that half the borderlands spoke. "All of you. Water's at the cistern. Don't touch the sigil-stone unless you mean it."

Maren smiled the way people always smiled at that last instruction—the smile of someone who thought it was ritual language, empty words passed down from an older rite. But the eldest girl, the nine-year-old with the drum, looked at the sealed door with an expression Sera recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone who could feel something waiting on the other side.

"What's behind it?" the girl asked.

"A friend," Sera said.

*Tell her my name,* Gideon said, and there was something in the warmth of it that made Sera's throat tighten.

"Gideon," she said. "His name is Gideon Pemberton. He kept the door open so you could come through it."

The girl put her palm flat against the stone, right over the place where the sigil glowed faint and gold beneath the surface, and Sera watched the light pulse once—gently, the way you'd squeeze a child's hand—and watched the girl's whole face change.

"He's warm," she said, wonder-struck.

"He's always warm," Sera said. "That's the whole point of him."

* * *

They came all that summer, and by autumn the plateau had changed beyond anything Sera could have predicted.

It started with practicalities. Pilgrims needed water, shelter, a fire-ring that wouldn't scorch the ancient stonework. Sera built the shelter herself, the first week, hauling timber up the switchbacks with a stubbornness that made her shoulders scream—she'd smuggled cargo heavier than lumber across worse terrain, but she'd been ten years younger then. By midsummer, freed scribes from the Temple reform movement had arrived with tools and plans, and the shelter became a lodge, and the lodge became two lodges, and someone planted a kitchen garden in the sheltered hollow where the snow melted first.

Sera supervised. That was the word they used—*warden*, and beneath it, unofficially, *supervisor*—and she wore both titles like a coat that almost fit.

"You could say no," Gideon told her one evening, after the last pilgrims had bedded down and the fires had burned low. "You never wanted to keep anything. You spent your whole life not keeping anything. Boats, cargo, promises—you moved through all of it."

"I know what I spent my life doing."

"Then why stay?"

She turned the question over. Below them, the valley lights of Hollowmere glimmered—Hollowmere, which paid both sides no longer, because there weren't two sides anymore, just roads and the people on them. Somewhere south, the Temple's debt-bond ledgers sat in ash-lined archives, struck through in red ink by clerks who still didn't fully understand how the copies had reached the Chancery. Somewhere east, clan children learned empire letters alongside clan script, and nobody rioted about it.

"Because somebody has to remember how we got here," she said finally. "And everybody else who remembers is either dead or Voss."

*That's a terrible reason.*

"It's the only honest one." She lay back on the boulder, arms behind her head, and let the mountain's warmth seep up through her spine. "Also, if I leave, who's going to tell you gossip?"

*I have the mountain. I hear everything within twenty miles. Last week a shepherd lost a goat and found it again and told the whole pass about it.*

"That's not gossip, that's livestock news. Completely different discipline."

The warmth flickered—amused again—and Sera closed her eyes and let herself have the moment, the whole moment, the fire-smell and the star-thick sky and the impossible fact of his voice living in the stone beside her. Some nights the grief of it still ambushed her, sharp as the knife wound at the Narrows. Other nights—and these nights were winning, slowly, season by season—it wasn't grief at all. It was just the shape of their life. Strange. Unchosen. Hers.

*There's a rider on the south road,* Gideon said suddenly. *Alone. Moving fast. He'll reach the switchbacks before midnight.*

Sera sat up. Old instincts fired down her spine like lit fuse. "Temple?"

*No. He rides like a courier but carries no satchel. And he's crying, Sera. I can feel it in the way he rides—like a man trying to outrun something inside himself.*

She was already pulling on her boots. "Crying couriers at midnight. Wonderful. This is exactly the kind of trouble I swore off when I took this job."

*You didn't take a job. You stayed.*

"Same thing, apparently." She grabbed her cloak and the short blade she still wore out of habit, though the last person she'd drawn it against was a bear, two summers ago, and the bear had mostly wanted the smoke-fish. "Keep the fires low. Wake Maren if the lodges stir."

*Sera.* The warmth caught her at the boundary stones, gentle but insistent. *Be careful.*

"I'm always careful."

*Liar.*

"Goat news," she said, "and now you're a mind-reader. The mountain's made you insufferable."

But she was smiling as she went down into the dark, and the stone behind her held the smile like a coal holds heat, long after the fire's gone.

* * *

The rider collapsed at the foot of the switchbacks, which was, Sera reflected grimly as she knelt beside him, a familiar way for trouble to arrive. People kept falling down at thresholds in her life. It was practically a tradition.

He was young—temple-clerk young, ink-stained fingers and the soft hands of a man who'd never hauled anything heavier than a ledger. His horse stood lathered thirty yards back, grazing indifferently, and the man himself was curled around a leather satchel like it was a wounded animal.

"Hey." She turned him over. "Hey. You're on sacred ground, son, so either you're dying or you're praying, and either way talk fast."

"The ledgers." His eyes were wild, red-rimmed—he'd been crying for hours, days maybe. "The original debt-bond ledgers. The ones before the purge. I was an archivist. I found—" He swallowed. "They're not all destroyed. The Chancery thinks they are. Someone kept copies. Someone *high*. And they've been using them."

Sera went very still.

"Using them how?"

"To rebuild it." The clerk's voice dropped to a whisper, as if the words themselves might be arrested. "The bond system. Quietly. New contracts, new names, same terms. There are scribes indentured right now—in Vessia Province, in the southern houses—who don't even know the practice was outlawed. They think it's normal. They think it's always been normal." He pushed the satchel toward her with shaking hands. "I copied everything. Names, dates, signatures. I ran because they'd kill me for this. They killed the last archivist who asked questions."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, the sigil-stone flared gold.

Sera looked up at the sealed door—at the light pulsing through the ancient carvings like a heartbeat—and felt the mountain's attention sharpen to a point.

*Gideon?*

*I heard.* His voice was cold in a way she'd almost forgotten it could be. Cold, and underneath the cold, something molten. *Every bonded scribe in the empire—I can feel the roads, Sera. I've felt the traffic on them for three years and never understood why the southern routes carried so many young travelers with temple marks on their wrists.*

"Oh, Gideon." She said it aloud, to the stone, forgetting the clerk entirely. "Oh, love."

*We broke this,* he said. *We broke it and someone swept up the pieces and rebuilt it in the dark.*

"We'll break it again." She said it the way she said everything that mattered—plainly, without ceremony, the way you'd promise weather to a farmer. "Louder this time."

The clerk was staring between her and the glowing door, mouth open. "What—what is—"

"Warden business," Sera said, standing, shouldering the satchel. It was heavy. Full of paper. Full of names. "Get up. You'll sleep in the lodge tonight, and tomorrow you'll tell me every name in that bag, and the day after that we ride south."

"South?" The clerk's face crumpled. "I can't go back south. They'll—"

"Not to the Temple." Sera looked up at the plateau, at the lodges full of sleeping pilgrims, at the garden gone to seed, at the whole fragile living thing they'd built on this mountain out of grief and stubbornness and a dead woman's map. At everything Gideon had bought with his freedom, and everything she'd promised to carry because he no longer could.

"To the Chancery. To the newspapers. To every freed scribe from here to the coast." She tightened the satchel strap across her chest. "This time we don't just burn the ledgers. This time everyone reads them."

Behind her, the sigil pulsed—steady, warm, furious—and far above, in the high passes where the snow never melted, the mountain itself seemed to lean south, like a man turning his head to listen.
