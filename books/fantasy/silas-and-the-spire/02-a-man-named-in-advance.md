## 2. A Man Named in Advance

Silas Vane had been lost in exactly forty-one cities, and he had developed a theory about it: that being lost was not a failure of navigation but a failure of agreement between a place and the person walking through it. Some cities simply declined to know you. They kept their streets turned inward, their doors shut to your particular shape of stranger. You could walk them for years and remain a rumor even to yourself.

The city of Corvane's Reach was not one of those cities. The Reach knew him. He could feel it in the way the cobbles seemed to tilt, very slightly, toward the house he was looking for — as though the whole street were leaning in to listen.

He checked the card again. *Marta's kitchen, behind the dyers' lane, sundown.* His sister had written it in her round, flour-dusted hand, and beneath it she had added, because Marta had never once let him escape into ambiguity: *Her name is Lena. Be honest. Be on time. Don't map the tablecloth.*

That last instruction was fair. He was aware it was a habit — the mapping. When a room made him nervous, he drew it. Corners, exits, the distance between the door and the window, the weight of the light. He'd filled eleven sketchbooks with rooms he'd eaten dinner in, inns he'd slept one night in, harbors he'd meant to leave from and hadn't. A cartographer with no fixed address; he carried his maps the way other men carried homes, folded into oilcloth against his ribs, and when people asked where he was from he said *elsewhere* in a tone that invited no follow-up questions.

Tonight he was on time. That alone felt like an omen, or perhaps a symptom.

The house behind the dyers' lane was narrow and tall, its shutters painted the blue that dyers' wives always chose, the blue of cloth before the mordant took it. Smoke rose from the chimney in a plume that smelled of rosemary and something darker underneath — burnt sugar, maybe, or old paper. He stood in the street for a moment longer than a punctual man should, letting the evening settle around him. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the rooftops. The Spire stood against the darkening east like a needle holding the sky shut, and Silas looked at it the way he looked at all landmarks: professionally, briefly, without any sense that it was looking back.

Then he knocked.

* * *

Lena Delacroix opened the door the way people open doors when they have already counted the cost of what's behind them.

She was smaller than he'd expected, or perhaps just more contained — a woman built like a closed ledger, everything important filed away out of sight. Her hair was dark and pinned with what appeared to be a pencil. She wore a good dress that had clearly been argued with, and she held a dish towel in one hand like a weapon she was choosing not to draw.

"You're on time," she said.

"I'm told that's suspicious."

"It is." But the corner of her mouth moved, a small tectonic event, and she stepped aside. "Marta said you'd be either early or never. Come in before the bread decides it's waited long enough."

Inside, the house was warm in a way that felt deliberate, engineered, the warmth of someone who understood that cold rooms make cold conversations. Marta — broad, floury, formidable, wearing an apron that had survived several wars — emerged from the kitchen doorway, looked at Silas, looked at Lena, and performed the smallest possible version of a curtsy, which from Marta was roughly equivalent to a standing ovation.

"Silas," she announced. "This is Lena. Lena, this is Silas. I'm going to be in the kitchen, where I can hear nothing at all." And she withdrew, radiating the satisfaction of a woman whose life's work was approaching completion.

The dining room was small. A table set for two under a lamp with a green glass shade. Bread, still steaming. A stew that smelled like it had opinions. And books — books everywhere, not arranged but *stationed*, stacked on chairs and windowsills and one precarious tower by the door, spines cracked, corners soft with handling. Ledgers, most of them. He recognized the breed. He'd seen ledgers in counting houses from here to the Salt Coast, and these had the same look: patient, exact, slightly menacing.

"You keep accounts," he said, taking the chair she indicated.

"I keep accounts," she agreed, ladling stew with the precision of someone defusing something.

"What kind?"

"The kind nobody else wants to keep."

He laughed, and meant it, and watched something in her posture loosen by a degree she probably thought he couldn't measure. He could measure it. He measured everything; it was the only prayer he knew.

They ate. He told her about the Salt Coast, where they sold maps of places that didn't exist yet and bought back maps of places that had stopped existing. He told her about the ferry town where every building leaned the same direction, like a congregation listening to a sermon only the river could hear. He did not tell her that he'd redrawn the layout of her dining room twice in his head already, or that he'd noted the window's latch, or that the bookshelf by the stairs had a gap in its dust shaped like a book recently removed and recently returned.

"And you?" she asked. "Where are you from? Marta wouldn't say. She said it was your story to tell."

"Elsewhere," he said, and then, surprising himself, added: "Everywhere, briefly. Nowhere for long. My mother traveled. I was born somewhere between two towns, on a road, in the back of a cart. She used to say I never quite finished arriving anywhere." He turned his spoon over. "I've never had an address that outlasted a season. It's not tragic. It's just — provisional. Everything about me has always felt provisional. Like I'm staying somewhere on sufferance, and the lease is up sooner than anyone will say."

The words came out more honestly than he'd planned. He watched her receive them — not with pity, which he'd have forgiven, nor with curiosity, which he'd have tolerated, but with a kind of grim recognition, as if he'd read aloud from a page she knew by heart.

"That's how I feel about the guildhouse," she said quietly. "And I've lived there twenty years."

Before he could ask what guild, the lamp flickered.

Not guttered — *flickered*, once, like an eye blinking. And in the silence that followed, Silas heard a sound he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe: a soft, papery exhalation, the sound a book makes when it opens itself.

It came from the stack beside the door. From a ledger bound in grey leather that he was almost certain had been closed when he sat down — certain, because he'd mapped it, because he mapped everything, and closed books have a particular silhouette, a particular gravity, and this one had changed.

Lena went very still. Stillness, on her, was louder than movement.

"That book doesn't do that," she said, mostly to herself. Then, rising, to the book, in the voice one uses for a dog caught on the table: "You don't *do* that."

She crossed the room and picked it up, and Silas saw her face change as she registered the cover — saw recognition and dread arrive together, twins holding hands. "This is the prophecy registry," she said. "It hasn't accepted a new entry in — " She stopped. Recalculated. "In a hundred years."

She brought it to the table, because whatever else she was, she was a woman who kept accounts, and an account opening itself mid-dinner demanded reconciliation. She set it down under the green lamp. The pages lay open without her help.

The entry was dated tonight.

Silas read upside down, because cartographers learn to read upside down the way sailors learn to swim, and so he got there first — got there while her breath was still catching, while her finger was still hovering an inch above the ink as though the ink might be hot.

*On this night,* the entry read, in a hand that was not the registry's usual clerk-hand but older, rounder, almost childlike, *a man comes to dinner. His name is written before his birth. Silas Vane. His soul is owed, and winter is short.*

The lamp hummed. The stew cooled. From the kitchen came the ordinary, oblivious sounds of Marta washing dishes, and somehow that was the worst part — that the world was continuing its small domestic business three yards away while the page lay there saying *his soul is owed* in handwriting a century dead.

"Lena," Silas said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him; inside, something vast and quiet had begun to fall, the way snow falls in windless country, covering everything. "That's my name."

"I know." Her finger finally touched the page, lightly, the way you touch a wound to confirm it's yours. "I know it is."

"How — " He stopped. Started again. "I've never met you before tonight. I've never been to this city before this week. And there's a book in your house that's known me since before my mother ever — "

He didn't finish, because he was looking at her face and she was looking at the page and both of them had arrived at the same impossible arithmetic at the same moment. Two strangers at a table. A date arranged by a foster sister with flour on her hands. And a ledger, a hundred years sealed, that had opened itself to the exact hour of their meeting and written his name in advance of his birth.

"We've met before," Lena said slowly. "Haven't we. Before the meeting."

It sounded like madness. It sounded, also, like the truest thing either of them had said all evening, truer than the compliments and the travel stories and the careful, negotiated warmth of two lonely people performing openness. Silas looked down at the entry again — *winter is short* — and felt the strange, lifelong provisionality of himself suddenly acquire a deadline. All those borrowed rooms, all those seasons in other people's towns. He had always felt like a guest in his own life.

Apparently he'd had reason.

From the kitchen, Marta's voice floated out, bright and unaware: "Is anyone going to want the honey cake, or shall I defend it myself?"

Neither of them answered. The lamp burned green and steady now, as if satisfied. And Lena Delacroix closed her hand around the edge of the page — gently, the way you close a hand around something that might bite — and looked up at Silas with an expression he would come to know very well in the weeks ahead: the expression of a woman deciding, against every rule she owned, to tell the truth.

"There are things in my keeping," she said, "that I am forbidden to speak of. Guild law. Oath-bound." Her jaw tightened. "But the book is already speaking, and I find I'm tired of pretending I don't hear it. Sit down, Silas Vane. Your soul is owed — and I think I'm the one who keeps the account."
