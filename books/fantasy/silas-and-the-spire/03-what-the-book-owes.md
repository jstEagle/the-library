## 3. What the Book Owes

The prophecy ledger did not want to be opened.

Lena had handled enough of the guild's books to know the difference between a volume that was merely old and one that was *reluctant*. Old books yielded. Reluctant books negotiated. This one — bound in grey calfskin gone soft as moth-wing, its spine stitched with thread that might once have been hair — sat on the reading stand in the archive alcove off her office and radiated the particular stillness of something pretending to be asleep.

She had pulled it down because of the name.

*Silas.*

It was not an uncommon name. She knew that. In forty years of ledgers she had catalogued three Silases: a Silas who sold his shadow to a glassblower in Vell, a Silas who traded his mother's lullabies for safe passage across the Weeping Strait, and a Silas whose entire entry consisted of the word *returned* in someone else's handwriting, which was the kind of entry that kept junior clerks awake at night. Names repeated. The guild's business ran on names repeating, on debts echoing down bloodlines like bells rung in towers a hundred miles apart.

But this Silas was sitting in her kitchen, eating the second half of a pear tart he had complimented twice and meant both times, and this ledger had opened itself to a page dated tonight.

Lena stood with her hand flat on the cover and listened to the house breathe around her. Guildhouses breathed; that was the first thing they taught you. Stone took air in through the mortar and let it out through the chimneys, slow as a sleeping animal, and if you were raised inside one the way she had been, you learned its rhythms the way farm children learned weather. Tonight the breathing had changed. Quicker. Attentive.

*You are being ridiculous,* she told herself. *The book is a book. The man is a man. Coincidence is the guild's oldest currency and you have never once paid it.*

Her hand lifted the cover anyway.

The pages smelled of cold iron and dried violets, the signature scent of contracts written under the old Archivist, dead now sixty years. The paper was heavy, rag-edged, each leaf ruled in faded red — the color reserved for entries concerning souls, which were, per the founding charter, *the only commodity that cannot be honestly weighed and must therefore be honestly described.* Lena had memorized that clause at nine years old, standing recitation before Vess Corvane while her foster siblings played in the courtyard below. She could still hear Vess's voice delivering it: *Honesty is our only luxury, child. Everything else we can counterfeit.*

The page dated tonight was written in a hand she did not recognize, though every archivist's hand in this book was supposed to be logged in the front matter. That was the first wrong thing. The ink was brown-black, iron-gall gone amber with age, and it had been written by candlelight — she could see where the letters leaned when the flame guttered.

She read.

---

*Entry the 214th of the Winter Ledger, in the year the Spire last slept.*

*Item: one soul, unbegotten at time of sale, to be begotten of the line of Maren Voss-adjacent, cartographic trade, no fixed address. Sold pre-birth by the father, payment received in full: one road out of debt, one winter without hunger, and the erasure of the father's name from all records thereafter.*

*Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain, by its standing agent.*

*Brokered through this house. Commission taken: one favor, uncalled, held in perpetuity.*

*Terms of collection: the soul shall be gathered into the Spire's shadow when the Spire aligns, at winter's end, upon the completion of its hundredth turning. The bearer will feel no pain. The bearer will feel nothing at all, which is the Court's idea of mercy and has been for as long as the Court has existed.*

*Note, in a different hand, smaller, pressed hard:* *The father wept. I record it because no one else will.*

---

Lena read it twice. Then a third time, because her eyes kept sliding off the date — *winter's end* — and finding instead the small note at the bottom, the weeping father, the archivist who had bothered to write him down. Sixty years dead or more, that hand. And still more honest than anyone she worked beside.

Winter's end was eleven weeks away.

She closed the book. Opened it again, because closing it felt like lying, and the entry was still there, patient as water wearing at stone. *One soul, unbegotten at time of sale.* Somewhere in the world, a man had been hungry enough and frightened enough to sell a child who did not yet exist, and the guild had taken its commission, and the commission was still *held in perpetuity*, which meant somewhere in the vault beneath her feet there was a slip of paper worth a favor from the Court Beneath the Mountain, and she had walked over it every working day of her adult life.

And the man himself was in her kitchen.

She became aware, gradually, of the sounds of him: the scrape of his chair as he shifted, the small clink of his spoon against the cup Marta's tea had left behind, his voice humming something tuneless and contented because he thought he was alone and unobserved. He hummed the way people hummed who had never had a reason to stop. It made her chest hurt in a way she could not file under any heading the guild taught.

Guild law was clear. Article Nine, Disclosure: *No partner shall reveal to any client or subject the contents, existence, or terms of any entry touching that client or subject, save by instrument of formal notice served through proper channels.* She had sworn it with her hand on steel, which made it swordspeech-adjacent, which made breaking it not merely dishonest but *wounding*. Oaths spoken over blades did not forgive. They scarred.

Proper channels would take until midwinter. Proper channels would send Silas a letter written in language designed to be misunderstood, delivered by a courier trained to deflect questions, followed by a summons to a negotiation room where Vess Corvane would offer him tea and terms in the same breath and he would sign something he did not understand because everyone always did, everyone always signed, and then he would go home — wherever that was, a cartographer with no fixed address — and wait for winter's end feeling nothing at all, which was the Court's idea of mercy.

Lena looked at the entry. At the weeping father. At the word *mercy* sitting there in century-old ink like a fly preserved in amber.

She thought about the way Silas had said *you feel familiar* at the door, embarrassed, waving it off before she could answer. She thought about how the prophecy ledger had opened itself — not fallen open, not been nudged, but *opened*, the way a dog wakes when its name is said in another room. Books in this house did not do things by accident. The house had wanted her to read this tonight, while he was close enough to hear the truth from her mouth rather than receive it in an envelope.

Or the house wanted her to break her oath, and wanted witnesses.

Both, probably. The guildhouse had never distinguished between temptation and test.

* * *

She carried the ledger back to the kitchen instead of returning it to the stand, which was itself a violation — restricted volumes did not leave the archive — and she noted, distantly, that noting the violation gave her no pause at all. That was the strange arithmetic of oaths: they did not weigh themselves until after you broke them.

Silas had found the tin of shortbread Marta had forced on them and was eating it with the guiltless efficiency of a man who had learned never to refuse food, and he looked up when she came in, and smiled, and the smile did something to the light in the room that Lena refused to examine.

"You've got your work face on," he said. "I can tell. It's the same face you made at dinner when the wine was bad and you were too polite to say."

"The wine was *criminal*."

"See, and there it is." He brushed crumbs from his fingers. "Is everything all right? You've got the look of someone whose books don't balance."

Lena set the ledger on the table between them, and the sound it made was heavier than a book should make, and Silas stopped moving entirely. He was, she was learning, quick in exactly the ways his looseness disguised. He read rooms the way he read coastlines.

"What's this?" he asked.

"Before I show you," Lena said, "I need you to understand something about me." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, in the flat register she used for figures that would not flatter anyone. "I keep accounts for a company that trades in things most people don't believe exist. Miracles. Curses. Years of life. Names. Souls." She watched him for the flinch, the laugh, the polite withdrawal toward the door — the three standard responses — and got none of them. He just waited. That was worse. "There are laws governing what I may tell clients. There are laws governing what I may tell *anyone*. I swore them on steel, which means they're not rules to me, they're bones. If I break one, I don't get a fine. I get a wound that doesn't show."

Silas glanced at the book, then back at her face. "And you're about to break one."

"I'm deciding whether to."

"Can I help?"

The question landed somewhere beneath her ribs and stayed there. *Can I help.* Not *should you*, not *what happens to me*, not even the self-interested *am I in danger* — though he was, enormously, though the danger had been accruing interest since before his father ever wept — just: *can I help.* As if whatever she was carrying might be lighter for two sets of hands.

"No," she said. "That's the point. Nobody can help. That's why the law exists — so that the weight stays with the person paid to carry it." She drew a breath. "But I'm going to tell you anyway, and I need you to remember, later, when it matters, that I chose this. That nobody made me. That if there's a price, it's mine."

"Lena." He said her name carefully, like a word in a language he was only beginning to learn. "Whatever it is — you've been looking at me all night like I'm a sum that won't come out right. Just say it."

So she opened the ledger to the marked page and turned it to face him, and told him the truth, plainly, in the order it happened, because she had spent her whole life believing that honesty was the only luxury and tonight, for the first time, she intended to spend some.

"Before you were born," she said, "your father sold your soul. He sold it to the court beneath the mountain — yes, the real one, the one from the stories, the one under the Spire on the southern horizon that no map of mine has ever managed to hold still. The sale was brokered through my guild. My house took its commission. The contract says the debt comes due when the Spire completes its hundredth turning and aligns, at winter's end. Eleven weeks from tomorrow." She kept her voice level, kept her hands flat on the table, kept her eyes on his because he deserved that much. "When it aligns, they collect. You'll be walking somewhere, or sleeping, or laughing at a joke, and you'll simply stop feeling anything at all. Forever. The contract calls it mercy. I've read ten thousand contracts and I have never once seen a crueller word used so gently."

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire ticked. Outside, the city went on with its ordinary noises, carts and dogs and someone singing badly two streets over, the whole oblivious machinery of a world that did not know one of its own had been entered into a book.

Silas looked down at the page. She watched him read it — watched his lips move slightly on the hard parts, *unbegotten at time of sale*, watched him reach the note at the bottom, the weeping father, and stop there. His hand came up and rested on the edge of the page, not touching the words, the way people touched holy things or dangerous ones.

"He wept," Silas said finally. His voice was strange, sanded down. "Whoever wrote this. They wrote down that he wept."

"Yes."

"Why does that—" He stopped. Started again. "I've spent my whole life feeling borrowed. That's the only way I've ever known how to say it. Like a coat that fits but isn't mine. Every town I map, I map it like I'm leaving, because I am, because staying has never — I thought it was temperament. Wanderlust. A failing." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "It's not a failing. It's a *term of the loan*."

"Silas—"

"Eleven weeks." He was doing the arithmetic already; she could see it running behind his eyes, a cartographer calculating distance by dead reckoning. "What happens at eleven weeks? Is there — is there a process? An appeal? People must have fought these contracts before. There must be precedent, there's always precedent, that's what maps are, maps are just precedents for ground—"

"There are precedents," Lena said quietly. "And I'm going to find every one of them. But I need you to hear the rest of it first, because you deserve the whole shape of it, not the comfortable part." She turned her hands over on the table, palms up, empty — the gesture of a clerk presenting clean books. "By telling you this, I have broken my oath. When the house notices — and it will notice, the house notices everything, it noticed us before you knocked — there will be consequences, and they will fall on me, and possibly on you, and I need you to know that I knew that when I opened this book. I need you to know that whatever happens next, someone chose you over the rules. That's not nothing. Hold onto it. There may be a season coming when it's all either of us has."

He looked at her for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face and he let it, unflinching, and she understood suddenly that here was a man who had spent his whole provisional life practicing for terrible news, and that the practice had not made him ready, only *still*.

"You broke your bones for me," he said, "and we met four hours ago."

"You feel familiar," Lena said. "You said it yourself at the door. I'm starting to think neither of us was wrong."

The ledger lay open between them, and as they watched — as they both watched, unable to look away — the ink of the date at the top of the entry darkened, wet and fresh, as though the century between its writing and this moment had been no time at all. And from deep beneath the floor, beneath the vault, beneath the foundations and the older foundations under those, the guildhouse exhaled, long and slow, like something turning over in its sleep to look at them.

Every lamp in the kitchen guttered at once.

Down the corridor, unhurried, deliberate, came the sound of the front doors unlocking themselves.
