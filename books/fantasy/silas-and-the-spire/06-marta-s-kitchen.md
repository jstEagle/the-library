## 6. Marta's Kitchen

Marta knew something was wrong before she opened the door, because Lena never knocked.

In thirty-one years of sisterhood — the kind written in ink and gruel and shared beds in a guildhouse attic, not in blood — Lena Delacroix had crossed this threshold perhaps four hundred times, and every one of those times she had simply appeared, the way weather appears, the way debt appears. She did not knock. Knocking was a thing you did when a house belonged to someone else, and as far as Lena was concerned, Marta's house had belonged to both of them since the winter they were nine and eleven and had huddled under one blanket in the guild dormitory, inventing a family out of thin air because the guild had given them everything except one.

So when the knock came — three soft raps, almost shy — Marta set down her paring knife, wiped her hands on her apron, and felt her stomach drop through the floorboards.

She opened the door to find her sister standing on the step like a stranger at a strange door, and behind her, half-hidden in the lamplight, a man.

"Marta," Lena said. Her voice was doing something Marta had heard only twice before: once at their foster mother's funeral, and once the night the guild took back the ledger-room key Lena had been promised at eighteen. It was the voice of someone holding a door shut from the inside. "Can we come in?"

"Since when do you ask?" Marta said, and stepped aside, because whatever else was happening, there was a man on her step who looked like he hadn't eaten a proper meal in a month, and Marta Voss had never in her life let that stand.

* * *

The man's name was Silas, and he moved through her kitchen the way a cat moves through an unfamiliar room — gracefully, attentively, touching nothing but noting everything. He had the kind of face that made you want to feed him: sharp-cheeked, tired around the eyes, with a smile that arrived a half-second after it should have, like a letter delayed in transit. A cartographer, Lena said. No fixed address. He'd drawn maps of places that didn't exist yet and places that no longer did, and he carried his whole life in a satchel that looked lighter than a life should be.

Marta put him to work within ten minutes, because idle guests made her nervous and busy ones told her things. He peeled potatoes with surprising competence. He accepted the task of shelling peas without complaint. And when she handed him the wooden spoon and pointed him at the stew — "stir, don't think about it too hard, thinking makes it bitter" — he laughed, an actual laugh, startled out of him, and something in Marta's chest went soft and wary at the same time.

Because she could see what Lena couldn't. Or rather, she could see what Lena was pretending not to see, which for Lena amounted to the same catastrophe.

Her sister stood at the counter chopping onions with the precision of a woman defusing something, and every time Silas turned his head, her eyes came up to him and away again, quick as a pickpocket. Thirty-one years old, fluent in eleven currencies, three dead languages, and the entire grammar of impossible debts — and she looked at this man like she was trying to read a word in a language she'd refused to learn.

And underneath all of it, under the onions and the careful small talk, there was the other thing. The wrongness. Lena kept glancing at the window, where the guildhouse spire was not visible from here but might as well have been, and her hand kept drifting to the satchel at her hip — the ledger-satchel, the one Marta hadn't seen her carry outside the guild walls since her oath-day.

Something had happened tonight. Something with teeth.

"So," Marta said brightly, dumping carrots into the pot with more force than the carrots deserved. "How did you two meet? Because my sister does not do blind dates. My sister does not do *dates*. My sister once scheduled a courtship the way other people schedule dental work, and then cancelled."

"Marta." Lena's voice had a warning in it, thin as wire.

"It was arranged," Silas said, and there was something odd in how he said it — not embarrassed, but puzzled, like a man reading a map whose roads had shifted overnight. "By a matchmaker. An old woman in the Lantern Quarter. I paid her fee and honestly forgot about it until this morning. Then I remembered, and I thought — why not? Worst case, dinner with a stranger." He glanced at Lena, and the delayed smile arrived, softer this time. "Best case, apparently."

"And who sent you to the matchmaker?" Marta asked, casual as salt.

He blinked. "I don't know that anyone sent me. I found her card in—" He stopped. His brow creased, and Marta watched him reach for the memory and find the shelf empty. "I don't remember. Isn't that strange? I remember the card. Cream paper, red border. But I can't remember where it came from."

The kitchen went very quiet. The stew murmured. Outside, the wind worried the eaves.

Lena set down her knife with exaggerated care, the way she did everything when she was frightened, and said, "Marta. Can I talk to you in the pantry."

It was not a question. Marta dried her hands, told Silas to mind the pot, and followed her sister into the little cold room off the kitchen where the preserves lived in rows like a sleeping congregation.

* * *

Lena told her all of it, in a low rapid voice, standing between shelves of plum jam and pickled fennel: the prophecy ledger opening itself at dinner. The entry dated tonight, a century old, bearing Silas's name. The soul sold before his birth to the court beneath the mountain. The Spire aligning at winter's end. The guild's claim. Guild law forbidding disclosure — and Lena's voice catching, just slightly, on the words *I told him anyway.*

"You told him," Marta repeated. "You broke disclosure."

"I told him." Lena's jaw was set, but her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "He has a right to know what's coming for him. Whatever the guild says, whatever the fine is, whatever they— Marta, they're going to collect him. At winter's end. Like a debt called due. And I sat across from him at my own table and read it to him out of a book, and he just — he thanked me. For telling him. Who thanks someone for that?"

"A man who's spent his whole life feeling borrowed," Marta said quietly.

Lena stared at her. "What?"

"I've met his kind before. Orphans of a different ledger." Marta reached out and gripped her sister's shoulders, feeling the tension in them like bridge cable. "Listen to me. Listen. You know what happens now. You broke disclosure on a sealed entry. The guildhouse knows — it always knows, the walls are its ears, the doors are its eyes. By tomorrow morning, maybe by midnight, Vess will have your name in front of her and his name beside it, and she will be so kind, Lena. She'll be so gentle. She'll offer him protection, custody, comfort. She'll make him feel safe the way a cage feels safe, and he'll take it, because he's got nowhere else, and that's how they get everyone. That's how they got us."

"They raised us."

"They *bought* us." The words came out harder than Marta intended, hard enough that Lena flinched, and Marta softened her grip but not her voice. "I know. I know what they were to us. Soup in winter and a roof and teachers and — and Vess, when she found me stealing bread at twelve, she didn't call the watch, she taught me knife-work and gave me a kitchen. I love her too. Gods help me, I love her still, some ruined corner of me will love her until I die. But you and I were assets, Lena. We were entries. They fed us because fed assets appreciate. They taught you swordspeech because a speaker is worth more than a clerk. Every kindness had a column number. You know it's true. You've *written* the columns."

Lena was silent. In the dark of the pantry, her face was very pale and very young, and Marta hated the guild a little more for putting that look back there — the look Lena had worn at nine years old, before competence grew over it like bark over a wound.

"If I run," Lena said finally, "I lose everything. My place. My work. Her. Twenty-two years, Marta. If I run with him, I'm not breaking a rule. I'm breaking the oath. The whole architecture of me."

"Yes," Marta said. "Exactly. And?"

Lena laughed — a broken, startled sound, nothing like Silas's laugh, a laugh with a crack down the middle of it. "That's your advice? *And?*"

"My advice is that you already ran." Marta took her sister's face in both flour-dusted hands. "You told him. You broke disclosure before you ever walked out my door tonight. Whatever you decide about tomorrow, that part's done, and the guildhouse already knows, and there is no version of this where you go back to being what you were yesterday. So the question isn't whether to betray the guild. That bill's already posted. The question is whether you betray *him* too — whether you walk him to Vess's office yourself and dress it up as protection — or whether you get him out of this city before the doors finish rearranging themselves."

"The doors—"

"Were rearranging when you left. Weren't they." It wasn't really a question either. Lena's silence answered it. Marta nodded slowly, the way you nod at a diagnosis. "Then we have hours, not days. Eat first. Nobody runs well hungry; I won't have it. And then—" She stopped, because saying the next part aloud made it real, and real things demanded payment. She said it anyway. "And then I'm coming with you. As far as the mountain road, at least. There are things in my kitchen that travel. There are wards in my stockpot, if you can believe it, and charms in the spice tins, and I know the ferryman's moods better than any living soul, and you—" her voice wobbled and she steadied it with fury, "you are going to need someone watching your back who isn't bound by your oath or his debt. Someone *family.*"

"Marta, if the guild finds out you helped us—"

"They'll what? Take my kitchen?" Marta smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "Let them come. I've been feeding people the guild considers property for twenty years. I've slipped extra bread to the retrievers' indentured grooms and doctor notes into the ledgers' margins for clerks too proud to ask. I have been committing small treasons against that house since before you had your oath, little sister. This is just the largest one. The recipe scales."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Then Lena did something she had not done since they were children: she leaned forward and pressed her forehead against Marta's, eyes closed, breathing. The old sign. The one they'd invented in the dormitory bed, forehead to forehead, *you and me against the arithmetic.* Marta felt her sister's breath shake once and steady.

"I'm scared," Lena whispered.

"Good," Marta whispered back. "Scared means you're cooking with heat. Now come on. Your man's stirring my stew, and if he thinks about it too hard it'll go bitter."

* * *

They ate at the big table by candlelight, because Marta refused to eat important meals under lamplight — candles made people honest, she'd always maintained, something about the way fire moves when truth is near. The stew was good, dark with mushrooms and slow with marrow, the kind of food that argued quietly with despair. Silas ate like a man who had learned never to assume there would be a second helping, and Marta kept refilling his bowl without comment until he began, self-consciously, to slow down.

"You're feeding me like I'm staying," he said at last.

"Eat," Marta said.

But the words worked on him anyway; she watched them work. He set down his spoon and looked around the kitchen — at the copper pots hung like instruments, the bundles of drying herbs, the jar of honey with a sprig of rosemary standing in it like a flag — and something in his expression folded inward.

"I've never had a table like this," he said. "A fixed one. I've eaten in a thousand kitchens, mapping towns, and they were all lovely, and none of them were mine, and I never minded, I told myself I never minded, because a cartographer belongs to the road, that's the romance of it—" He stopped, turned his spoon over. "Tonight I minded. Tonight it felt less like visiting and more like remembering something I never had."

"That's because you're among family," Marta said simply. "Even if it's a new one. Even if it's only hours old."

"Marta." Lena's voice was soft with warning and something else.

"What? It's true. Family isn't blood and it isn't paperwork, whatever the guild's contracts say. Family is whoever feeds you when you're falling apart and doesn't ask you to be less hungry than you are." She pointed her ladle at Silas, then at Lena, then, absurdly, at herself. "I decided you two were mine the moment you knocked on my door like a stranger, Lena Delacroix, and you, cartographer, the moment you stirred my stew without asking if it was beneath you. So. Family meeting. First order of business."

She laid it out for him then, plainly, because he deserved plainness: what the guild was, what it had done to children like Lena, how it bought orphans cheap and raised them expensive and called the interest love. How Vess Corvane would smile at him and mean perhaps a third of it. How protection, in that house, was a word with a chain inside it. Silas listened without interrupting, and Marta gave him credit for that; most men flinched or argued or looked to Lena for permission to believe her.

When she finished, he was quiet a long moment. Then: "The matchmaker. The card I can't remember receiving. The date that landed on the exact night a century-old book named me." He laughed, without much humor in it. "I wasn't sent here to meet her. I was delivered. Like a parcel."

"Now you're seeing the shape of it," Marta said.

"And if we run — where? The mountain is where they're taking me. Running toward it seems—"

"Running toward it with *choice* is different from being collected without it," Lena said. "There's a village. People like you. Sold, not collected. If there's a way to break this — a way the guild doesn't control — it'll be there, under the mountain's shadow, among the ones who've had a century to study the problem."

Silas looked between them — the hedge-witch cook with her ladle and her treasons, the oath-bound speaker with her cracked-open face — and Marta saw the moment the loneliness in him lost the argument. It was visible, almost physical, like a knot slipping loose.

"All right," he said. "All right. Tonight, then. Before the house finishes waking up."

"There's one more thing," Marta said, and went to the pantry, and came back with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: bread that would not stale, a flask of broth that refilled itself so long as you believed it would, a twist of salt charmed against ill roads, and a small iron key gone green with age. "My mother's. Not my guild mother — my first one, the one I don't talk about. It opens locks that were closed with regret. Don't ask me how I know. Just — if you find a door that shouldn't open and needs to, try this before you try anything loud."

Lena took the bundle like it was communion. "Marta—"

"Ah-ah. Second order of business." Marta held up a finger. "Nobody thanks anybody until we're past the city walls. Gratitude is for survivors. Right now we're just ingredients."

Silas laughed again — that delayed laugh, arriving late and warm — and helped her stack the dishes, and for a few minutes the kitchen held the three of them in its steam and candlelight like a cupped hand, and Marta let herself pretend, briefly, that this was all it would ever be: a dinner, a sister, a stray man learning to sit still at a fixed table.

Then the candles guttered, all six at once, though no door had opened.

And from the direction of the guildhouse — from the heart of the city, through stone and street and shuttered window — came a sound Marta had heard only once before, on the night Lena took her oath: a deep, resonant hum, like a struck bell that never finished ringing. The guildhouse, waking. The guildhouse, noticing.

Marta blew out the candles and said, very calmly, into the dark:

"Coats on, loves. We leave by the cellar."
