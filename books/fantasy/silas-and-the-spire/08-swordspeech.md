## 8. Swordspeech

The sealed stair had changed everything, and Lena could not stop looking at Silas as though he were a ledger entry she'd misread.

He sat at the long table in her rooms above the counting floor, hands wrapped around a cup of Marta's spiced wine he hadn't touched. The candlelight did something unkind to his face — hollowed his cheeks, sharpened the shadows beneath his eyes — and she realized with a small, cold shock that this was what he actually looked like. The charm of their first dinner had been a kind of light he carried; now that light was guttering, and underneath it was a man who had learned, three days ago, that he was not entirely real to himself.

"You keep staring at me," he said. "Is there ink on my face?"

"No." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, and then stood again, because sitting felt like surrender and standing felt like accusation, and finally she settled for leaning against the mantel where the fire could warm her back and she could pretend the posture was casual. It wasn't. Nothing about tonight was casual. "I need to tell you something. And I need you to understand, before I tell you, that I am breaking more rules by saying it than I broke when I told you about the debt."

Silas set down the cup. "More rules."

"The guild has laws the way a body has bones. Most of them you never see unless something breaks." She turned her hands over, studied her palms as if the words might be written there already, waiting. "One of them concerns me specifically. My oath. The reason they trust me with the ledgers at all."

"Your ledgers are honest," he said slowly. "You said that. You said it like it was — like it cost something."

"It does." Lena pushed off from the mantel and crossed to the window. Below, the guildhouse courtyard lay silvered under a rising moon, and beyond its walls the city went on with its ordinary business: lamplighters, late carts, a drunk singing somewhere near the tanneries. A world that did not know what was kept in the building behind her. "Do you know why a guild that trades in impossible things needs honest books? Not accurate — honest. There's a difference. An accurate ledger records what happened. An honest one records what's *owed*. Every miracle we broker, every soul-debt, every bottled storm and borrowed year — if the books lie about any of it, the whole edifice comes down. The contracts would eat themselves. So the founders built a safeguard into the office of Keeper."

She heard him shift behind her. "Which is you."

"Which is me." She laughed, but there was no humor in it; it came out like a cough. "When I was fourteen, Vess brought me into the vault beneath the counting floor. There's a sword there — old, older than the guild, older maybe than the mountain. They call it the Adjudicator. Nobody trains with it. Nobody displays it. It sits on a stone plinth in the dark, and once a year, on the night of the winter solstice, each Keeper before me knelt before it and spoke."

"Spoke what?"

"One sentence." Lena turned to face him. The firelight caught the scar along her jaw — the one she'd never explained, the one he'd been too polite to ask about. "A true sentence. Spoken through steel, it becomes law. Not metaphor-law, not court-law. *Law*. If I say *the river runs north*, the river runs north until the sentence is spent or superseded. If I say *this debt is paid*, it is paid, whatever the contract says, wherever the debtor hides. The words go into the world the way a blade goes into flesh, and the world closes around them and heals around them and they stay."

Silas had gone very still. That was a thing she liked about him, she thought distantly — he listened with his whole body, the way cartographers must, taking bearings on every word. "And you've done this. Spoken through it."

"Every solstice since I was fourteen. Nine sentences." She held up her hands, counted them off, an accountant's habit she couldn't break even now. "The first was to seal my apprenticeship. The second bound the ferry-contracts on the Graywater so no spirit could raise its toll mid-crossing. The third—" Her voice caught, and she smoothed it flat again. "The third closed a breach in the salt flats where something had gotten out of a bottle. Four people died before I spoke. After I spoke, nothing else did."

"And the rest?"

"Ledgers. Always, eventually, ledgers." She came back toward the table but didn't sit. "Here is the price, Silas. Here is the part Vess never says plainly, because Vess believes plainness is a kind of weakness. I get one sentence per year. One. And a sentence spoken falsely doesn't just fail — it unmakes the speaker. So every word I spend must be true, must be *necessary*, and must be worth going without for however long remains until the next solstice. This year's sentence is spent. I spoke it in autumn, binding the annual audit, so that no partner could quietly amend the books while the auditors slept. It holds until spring."

The silence stretched between them like a drawn wire.

"So you can't—" Silas began.

"I can't speak law for you. I can't speak law against the guild. I can't stand in front of the Undercourt, if it comes to that, and command anything at all." She braced her hands on the table's edge and leaned toward him, close enough to see the candle flame doubled in his eyes. "Whatever happens at winter's end, when the Spire aligns and they come to collect you — I will have no weapon except the ones I was born with. Two hands, a working mind, and a talent for finding the flaw in any contract. That's all. I want you to know that now, while there's still time for you to run. Because if you stay, you're staying with a woman who has already given away her only sword."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "You said nine sentences. What was the ninth?"

Lena straightened. Of all the questions he might have asked — *can we escape, can we fight, can you steal me back* — he had asked that one, and something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"The ninth," she said, "was last solstice. I knelt in the dark in front of that sword, and I meant to bind the eastern tolls, or seal the archive, or do any of the hundred prudent things a Keeper is supposed to do. And instead I heard myself say—" She stopped. Started again. "*Let there be one person in this house who sees me.*"

Silas rose from his chair.

"That's not how the gift works," she said quickly, backing up a step. "It's not a wish-granting engine, it's — the sentence has to be about the world, about debts and boundaries and—"

"And then a blind date arrived at your door," Silas said, "on the exact night your prophecy ledger named me. Three days ago. Sent by your sister, who has been trying to marry you off for years, and who swears to this day she chose my name out of a hat." He was smiling, but it was the unsteady smile of a man standing on a ledge. "Lena. I'm a cartographer. I map things. And I am telling you, with every professional faculty I possess, that I have been mapped onto your life since before you spoke that sentence. Whatever brought me here — it was moving toward you long before you asked it to."

"*Don't*," she whispered. "Don't make it mean something. If it means something, then spending the tenth sentence on you won't be a choice, it'll be — arithmetic. And I can't do that arithmetic yet. I need the winter to decide whether I'm the kind of woman who burns a year of law for a man she's known three days, or the kind who lets the Spire take him and keeps her oath intact and hates herself for exactly as long as she lives."

"Or," Silas said softly, "the kind who finds a third way. You told me yourself — the court honors theft. Sef said the doors open to a speaker's word. Maybe the answer isn't your gift at all. Maybe it's your flaw-finding. Every contract has a seam, you said. Even theirs."

"Even theirs," Lena agreed, and hated how much hope the words cost her. "But seams need hands, Silas, and time, and right now we have neither. Winter's end is six weeks away. The retrievers are already asking after us in the river towns. And I—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, against the ache there. "I have spent my whole life being fluent in every currency but this one. Debts, yes. Obligations, penalties, interest compounded hourly. But this—" She gestured between the two of them, at the small warm room, at the untouched wine, at the distance neither of them seemed able to cross. "This I can't ledger. I don't know the exchange rate."

"Then don't calculate," Silas said. He crossed the room — three steps, no more — and stopped just short of touching her, the way a man stops at the edge of a map where the paper ends and the blank space begins. "Just tell me one thing. When you knelt before that sword last solstice, alone in the dark, and the sentence came out of you sideways — did it feel like a debt?"

"No," Lena said. "It felt like the only true thing I'd said all year."

"Then it's already spoken," he said. "Everything since is just interest."

Outside, far below, a bell began to ring — not the guildhouse bell, which tolled in fifths, but a higher, thinner sound, urgent and wrong. Lena knew it instantly and her blood went cold: the counting-floor alarm, the one that sounded only when a ledger opened itself unbidden.

Not *a* ledger.

*The* ledger.

She was moving before she finished thinking, snatching her coat from its hook, and Silas was beside her, matching her stride, because that was the thing about rootless people — they learned fast which way the wind was blowing. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at him.

"When we get down there," she said, "whatever the book shows us — remember that I have no law left to speak. Whatever it demands, whatever it threatens, we face it unarmed. Are you still staying?"

Silas took the cup of cold wine off the table and drained it in one swallow, like a man fortifying himself for a crossing he'd already decided to make.

"I stayed before I knew about the sword," he said. "I'm staying now that I know what it costs you."

Together they went down, toward the ringing, toward the book that had been waiting a century to be read aloud — and somewhere beneath the city, in the dark beneath the dark, something ancient turned a page.
