## 11. Village of the Sold

The village had no name on any map Silas had ever drawn, and he had drawn most of them.

He noticed that absence first — the way a cartographer notices anything, as a blank space where ink should be. For three days they had walked out of the salt flats and into the foothills of the mountain, following Elder Sef's directions, which were less directions than a riddle: *walk until the road forgets it was a road, then keep walking until it remembers something older.* The path beneath their boots did exactly that. It began as packed clay, became sheep-track, became nothing at all, and then — abruptly, between one step and the next — became flagstone, ancient and lichen-eaten, laid by hands that had wanted people to find this place and then, apparently, changed its mind for a century or two.

"There," Lena said, and her voice came out strange, flattened, the way sound behaves in a room built to absorb it.

The village sat in a fold of the mountain's skirt like an egg in a nest of grey wool. Forty houses, perhaps fifty, all of stone, all of them low, all of them with roofs of slate so dark they seemed to drink the afternoon light rather than reflect it. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, unhurried columns. Gardens grew in neat terraces. Laundry hung between doorposts. Everything about it said *lived-in*, and everything about it said *waiting*, and Silas could not reconcile the two, because waiting was not a thing a place could do, except that this place was doing it, patiently, the way a kettle waits, the way a held breath waits.

"Is it — " he started, and stopped, because he didn't know how to finish the question. *Is it real? Is it a trap? Is it where I've been going my whole life without knowing?*

Lena's hand found his. Her fingers were cold; three days of salt wind and bad sleep would do that. But her grip was certain, and she had not let go of his hand since the ferry, and Silas suspected she never would again if she could help it. He understood. He felt the same way about her voice — hoarded now, rationed, every word she spent a small treasure he catalogued against the day the treasury ran empty. She had told him, on the flats, in the storm, wrapped in one blanket while the retrievers' lanterns swept the horizon behind them: *one true sentence per year, spoken through steel. I already spent this year's keeping the ledgers honest. Whatever happens at the mountain, I will be silent when it matters.* And then she had smiled, that dry, crooked smile, and added: *so don't do anything stupid enough to require me.*

He had promised. He intended to break the promise. It seemed, lately, like the only honest thing left to do.

They walked down into the village together, and no one came out to meet them, and that was the first wrong thing.

* * *

People watched them. That much was clear from the beginning — faces in windows, figures in doorways, a woman kneeling in a garden bed who lifted her head and tracked them with her eyes while her hands went on working the soil as though her body had been left running and her attention had gone elsewhere entirely. But no one approached. No one called out a greeting or a challenge. In every village Silas had ever mapped — and he had mapped hundreds, from fishing hamlets on the drowned coast to shepherd camps above the snowline — strangers were an event. Strangers were news, entertainment, threat, opportunity. Strangers drew a crowd inside of ten minutes.

Here, the crowd simply watched, and the watching had a quality he had no word for until later, when he lay sleepless and finally found it: they watched the way people watch weather. Not with fear. Not with hope. With the weary expertise of those who have seen this particular storm before and know precisely what it will and will not do.

"They know why we're here," Lena murmured.

"How?"

"Look at you." She turned to face him fully, and there it was again — that look she gave him sometimes, as if checking that he was still there, still solid, still his. "You're fading. I can see it. Anyone who knows what to look for can see it."

He had been trying not to look. But the evidence accumulated whether he attended to it or not. His shadow had begun arriving late — a half-second delay, as though it needed to remember whose it was. Food tasted of less each day, flavor draining out of bread and cheese and stream water like dye out of cloth. And in the last day, reflections had started to trouble him: not because they showed anything wrong, but because they showed him *slightly too well*, sharper than life, as though the world had begun rendering him in higher contrast in preparation for some final reading. The Spire's alignment was weeks away. Winter's end. The court beneath the mountain would come to collect what had been sold before he was born, and it was already warming up its hands.

A door opened ahead of them. An old man stepped out — old in a way that took Silas a moment to parse, because the man's face was unlined but his eyes were ancient, set in a smooth young-old face like river stones in fresh snow. He wore a coat of undyed wool and carried nothing, and he stood in the middle of the flagstone street and waited for them to come to him, because of course he did. Everyone here waited. Waiting was the local industry.

"You'll be wanting the registry house," the man said. His voice was mild, worn smooth as beach glass. "Everyone wants the registry house. First thing, always. Come."

They followed him through lanes that smelled of woodsmoke and turnip greens and something else underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a glacier. The houses were beautiful in their plainness — fitted stone, carved lintels, doors of ironwood grown glossy with generations of hands. Someone had loved this place once. Someone had built it to last. And yet every garden gate hung open, every tool sat where it had been set down mid-task, as though the whole village were a stage set between performances.

"Where is everyone?" Silas asked. "I mean — everyone's here. But where is everyone?"

The old man glanced back at him, and something moved behind the ancient eyes. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"Inside themselves," he said. "Most days. You'll understand soon."

* * *

The registry house stood at the village center, larger than the others, its lintel carved with a frieze of interlocking hands — dozens of them, hundreds, a chain of grasping palms that circled the doorway and met itself above the threshold. Lena stopped dead when she saw it.

"I know that mark," she said quietly. "It's in the guild's oldest ledgers. The founding seal." Her thumb pressed hard against Silas's knuckles. "This village was built by the guild."

"Built by us," the old man corrected gently. "For us. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Come in. My name is Tam. I keep the books now, the way my mother kept them, and hers before her. Somebody has to. That's the whole of the law here: somebody has to keep the books."

Inside, the registry house was one long room, and the long room was lined with shelves, and the shelves held ledgers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Volumes bound in grey leather, spines cracked with age, arranged in date order stretching back into shadows deep enough that Silas could not see the end of them. A lamp burned on a central table, its flame perfectly steady, fed by oil that never seemed to lower. Before the lamp lay an open book, and beside the book, a pen, and beside the pen, a chair that had been recently vacated — the cushion still held the shape of someone patient.

Tam crossed to the shelves with the unhurried gait of a man who had done this ten thousand times, ran a finger along the spines, and pulled down a volume near eye level. He blew dust from its cover — a gesture so ordinary, so domestic, that it made what came next worse, somehow. This was bookkeeping. This was a man doing his job.

"What year were you born?" Tam asked.

Silas told him.

Tam turned pages. The sound of the turning filled the room like slow rain. Lena had gone very still beside him, and Silas realized she was holding her breath, and that she knew — had known since the lintel, probably, since the founding seal — what they were going to find, and was bracing herself anyway, the way you brace for a wave you cannot stop.

"Here," Tam said.

He turned the book around on the table so they could read it, and Silas read, and the world got very quiet and very large at the same time.

The entry was written in a fine, careful hand, brown ink gone amber with age:

*Item: one soul, male issue of the woman Wren, sold in utero by the father, price two hundred crowns and forgiveness of debt. Term: collectible at alignment, winter's end, one hundred years hence. Buyer: the court beneath. Broker: the Guild of Impossible Things, commission retained.*

Below it, in a different ink, newer, a line had been added:

*Name given at birth: Silas. Status: pending.*

Pending. His whole existence, summarized. One line item among thousands. He stared at the words *sold in utero* and felt them physically, a pressure behind the sternum, because he had spent his entire adult life feeling borrowed — provisional, temporary, a guest in his own story who kept waiting for the true owner to arrive and ask him to move along. He had told Lena this on the salt flats, in the storm, his mouth against her hair: *I've never felt like I belonged anywhere, not even inside myself.* He had thought it was temperament. Wanderlust. The cartographer's curse, loving places and leaving them. It had never occurred to him that it was a condition of purchase. That somewhere, a clerk had recorded his rootlessness as a feature of the goods.

"How many?" Lena asked. Her voice was rough.

"In this volume? Four hundred and eleven entries." Tam laid his palm flat on the page with something like tenderness. "In the registry entire — " he gestured at the shelves, the shadows, the centuries — "we stopped counting at forty thousand. The counting seemed beside the point after that."

Forty thousand. The number refused to mean anything. Silas tried to make it mean something — forty thousand souls, forty thousand lives sold before birth, forty thousand people walking around the world feeling borrowed and provisional and unable to say why — and his mind slid off it, the way a cartographer's eye slides off terrain too vast to survey from any single vantage point. You had to map it in sections. You had to take it one contour at a time. And even then you only ever made a picture of it. You never actually held it.

"The guild brokered all of these," Lena said. It wasn't a question. Her hand had gone to the hilt of her sword — not drawing, just resting, the way a person touches a wall to steady themselves. "My guild. The family that raised me. They built this village. They've been — warehousing the consequences for a century."

"The guild doesn't think of it as consequences," Tam said mildly. "The guild thinks of it as inventory. That's not cruelty. It's worse than cruelty. It's tidiness." He closed the ledger with a soft, final sound. "You'll want to meet Sef. She's our elder. She knew your name was coming, Silas — we all did. Your entry has been read aloud here every winter's end for a hundred years. A little ritual. We read the pending names aloud so they aren't forgotten." He smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing Silas had seen in a life that had recently contained a great many sad things. "You've been part of this village longer than you've been part of anywhere else. We just never expected you to walk in through the front."

* * *

Sef received them in her kitchen, because of course she did; every important thing in Silas's new life seemed to happen in kitchens. She was small and round and quick-moving, with flour on her forearms and eyes the same river-stone grey as Tam's, and she looked up at Silas from her dough with an expression he couldn't read at all — joy and grief ground together into a single fine powder.

"There he is," she said, as if he were late for dinner rather than a hundred years early for his own collection. "Wash your hands. Sit. You're skin and bone, both of you, and whatever's coming, nobody meets it hungry."

And that — absurdly, unexpectedly — was what broke him. Not the ledger. Not the forty thousand. Not the word *pending*. But a stranger telling him to wash his hands, in a warm kitchen that smelled of bread, in a village full of people who had read his name aloud every year of his life. His eyes stung. He washed his hands. He sat. The bread, when it came, tasted of almost nothing — another thing the Spire was taking, quietly, installment by installment — but he ate it anyway, because Sef watched him eat with such fierce satisfaction that refusing seemed unthinkable.

Around the table, as the light failed outside, the villagers gathered. They came in ones and twos, drawn by some signal Silas couldn't perceive, filling the benches and the corners and the doorway, until the kitchen held thirty people, fifty, more. They didn't speak much. They looked at him. And under their looking he began to understand what Tam had meant by *inside themselves*, because these were not empty people, exactly — they were people whose insides had been repossessed, whose depths had been sold off, leaving them long-lived and functional and shallow as a puddle. They remembered their childhoods the way you remember a book you read once. They loved their neighbors the way you love a chair. They had been waiting, all of them, some for decades, some for generations, for a winter's end that would either free them or file them away, and they had gotten so good at waiting that waiting had become the shape of their souls — the small remaining portion that hadn't been sold.

An old woman touched his hair as she passed, gently, the way you touch something you don't expect to see twice.

"He has his mother's color," she said to no one.

"You knew her?" Silas asked, and his voice cracked on the second word.

"Wren? Everyone knew Wren. She lived in the blue house by the mill race, before the guild moved her on. Sold women don't get to stay put — bad for the asset." The old woman's smile was terrible in its mildness. "She used to sit right where you're sitting, big as a barn with you, and swear she'd buy you back someday. She saved for it. Every coin. The guild took the savings too, in the end. Breach of contract, they called it. She'd signed away the right to save."

Silas looked at Lena. Lena was looking at the table, and her jaw was set in the particular way it set when she was filing something away to be paid for later, with interest, and he knew — the way he'd known since the prophecy ledger opened itself on their first night — that she was going to burn the whole guild down for this, or die trying, and that she would consider it a fair trade.

"Sef," Lena said. "Tell us how to steal him back."

And Sef set down her rolling pin, and wiped her hands, and looked at the two of them for a long moment with those river-stone eyes, and said: "Ah. So you *do* want the truth, and not just the bread. Then hear it — but understand, child, that the truth has a hook in it, and once it's in you, it doesn't come out."
