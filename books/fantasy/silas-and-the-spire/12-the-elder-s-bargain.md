## 12. The Elder's Bargain

Sef had kept the registry of the sold for sixty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one thing above all others: hope was a debt like any other, and it collected interest.

She felt it accruing now, in the hour before dawn, as she climbed the crooked stair from her cellar with a lamp in one hand and the village registry under her arm. The book was heavy — heavier than paper had any right to be, because every name written in it carried weight that had nothing to do with ink. Nine hundred and forty names. Nine hundred and forty souls sold before their owners drew breath, entered into her ledger by the mothers and fathers who had signed away what they could not see, could not hold, could not imagine missing until it was gone.

Silas Vane's name sat on page four hundred and twelve, in her own careful hand, copied out thirty years ago from a traveling peddler's duplicate contract. She remembered writing it. She remembered thinking, *another one*, the way a woman thinks *another winter* when she is tired of cold.

And now the man himself was sitting in her kitchen, drinking tea he did not taste, while the swordswoman who had brought him stood at the window watching the mountain with the particular stillness of someone counting exits.

Hope, Sef thought, settling into her chair with the groan of old joints. Here it comes. Let us see what it costs.

* * *

The kitchen of Elder Sef was the warmest room in the Village of the Sold, which was not saying much. The village sat in the mountain's long shadow like a thing that had given up arguing with the weather — forty houses, a well, a meeting hall nobody used anymore because meetings required believing something might change. Its people were famous, in the way of such places, for longevity. They aged slowly. They died reluctantly. A soul sold at birth came with certain clauses, and one of them was durability: the court beneath the mountain did not want its property wearing out before collection day.

So the villagers endured. That was the word Sef preferred to *lived*. They endured their long gray decades, their hollow mornings, the peculiar lightness behind the breastbone that every one of them could describe and none could cure. They kept gardens they did not love. They mended roofs. And once a year, on the night of first frost, they lit lamps in every window facing the mountain, so that if anything down there ever looked up, it would see that they were still here, still waiting, still — in the only sense available to them — home.

"You've read the entry," Sef said now, turning the registry toward Silas so he could see his own name. "Page four hundred and twelve. Sold by your father, six months before your birth. Price: forgiveness of a gambling debt and passage south." She tapped the margin, where she had noted the broker's mark. "Guild transaction. Third-hand. Your soul changed owners twice before it reached the court's vault."

Silas looked at the page the way a man looks at an X-ray of himself. He was younger than most who came to her table, and prettier than was useful, with the restless hands of someone who could not stop drawing things — she had noticed him sketching her kitchen on the corner of a map scrap while she poured the tea, the table, the lamp, the steam, as though naming a place by rendering it could keep it from dissolving.

"That's my whole life," he said quietly. "Six months of negotiation. I cost less than a horse."

"You cost exactly what your father owed," Sef said. "Which is how these things always go. Nobody sells a soul for profit. They sell it for survival, and survival never pays market rate." She folded her hands on the registry. "Now. You came down out of the salt flats pursued by guild retrievers, you crossed our boundary stones without being turned away — which, incidentally, has happened twice in sixty years — and you are sitting in my kitchen asking me how to get your soul back. So let me tell you what I know, and then let me tell you why knowing it has made this village what it is. Which is to say: a waiting room."

Lena Delacroix turned from the window. Sef studied her openly, the way she permitted herself to study few people. A swordspeaker. She had met three in her life, and all of them had the same quality: a silence around the mouth, as if the body understood that words were ammunition and kept itself unloaded. This one wore her competence like armor over a wound. And she had not left the man when the ledger named him. That was the interesting part. That was the part Sef intended to use.

"The court beneath the mountain," Sef began, "is older than the guild, older than any kingdom whose coins you've handled. It does not rule land. It rules *obligation*. Every promise ever sworn carelessly, every debt defaulted, every vow broken at a crossroads — the court holds the residue. Over centuries it became the great repository of what people owe each other. And somewhere along the way, it learned to trade. Souls, mostly. Bottled voices. Unspent years. If it can be promised, the court can hold it, and if the court can hold it, the court can sell it."

"And the guild brokers for it," Lena said.

"The guild *is* its child," Sef said. "Founded by someone who went down into that mountain and came back up with the idea that obligation could be inventory. But here is the thing your guildmasters will not have told you, swordswoman, because it is the one law even they cannot bend." She leaned forward. "The court honors only theft."

Silas frowned. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Sef said, "that you cannot buy back what the court owns. Not with gold, not with years, not with another soul offered in exchange. The court's entire architecture is built on the sanctity of contract. A sale is a sale is a sale. Walk down there with a wagon of treasure and they will weigh it, admire it, and keep both the treasure and you. Every family that has ever tried repurchase has failed. Some of them are buried in our churchyard. Some of them are still down there, paying installments."

"Then how—" Silas started.

"Theft," Sef said again, savoring the word the way one savors a bitter medicine known to work. "The court recognizes theft as the one force that can unmake ownership. It is the flaw in their foundation, the crack they built their temple over and called a feature. A thing stolen from the court is *freed*, absolutely and irrevocably, because the act of theft breaks the chain of legitimate transfer. The contract doesn't transfer. It shatters. And the court — here is the beautiful, terrible irony — the court must honor the shattering. Their own law demands it. They worship obligation so completely that they are obligated to respect its destruction."

Silas set down his cup. His hands had gone still, which Sef suspected was rare. "Then we steal my soul back. Fine. I'll learn to pick locks."

"No," Sef said gently. "You misunderstand. The court's doors do not open to thieves. They open to *witnesses*. To enter the Undercourt at all — to stand before the chandelier of contracts where your soul hangs — requires a word of opening. A binding word. A word spoken by someone with the authority to make it true merely by saying it." Her eyes moved to Lena. "A speaker's word."

The silence in the kitchen changed texture. Sef had spent a lifetime reading silences — the hollow ones, the frightened ones, the ones full of arithmetic — and this one was doing sums. She watched the swordswoman arrive at the total and try to pretend she hadn't.

"One true sentence per year," Sef said softly. "That is the swordspeech oath, isn't it? Spoken through steel, it becomes law. And yours is already spent — I can see it in how carefully you aren't talking. You fed it to the ledgers, didn't you? Kept the guild's books honest with the only honest voice you had." She shook her head slowly. "They always take it that way. The guild loves a swordspeaker. A woman who can make truth enforceable, and who spends her one sentence a year making sure the accounts balance. What a bargain. What a *theft*, really — though no one ever calls it that."

Lena's jaw tightened. When she spoke, her voice was low and level. "Spring. My next sentence renews at the spring equinox. Winter ends in eleven weeks. The alignment—"

"—is at winter's end," Sef finished. "Yes. Eleven weeks. Three weeks too late." She let that sit. "Unless."

"*Unless,*" Lena repeated, flat.

"Unless the sentence you already spoke can be *unspoken*." Sef rose, joints protesting, and went to the cupboard where she kept the things she showed almost no one. From its depths she took a small iron box, and from the box a strip of vellum gone amber with age. "I was young when a swordspeaker passed through here — the last one, before you. Fleeing the guild, like you. Dying, unlike you. She taught me the deep grammar of the gift before the fever took her. And she told me about revocation. A swordspeaker can recall a spoken sentence — take it back into the steel — but the price is ruinous. The words don't return clean. They return *burned*. Whatever the sentence bound is released, yes. But the speaker loses the ability to speak a new one until the year turns, and the recalled sentence itself is spent forever — it can never be spoken again, in any form, for any purpose."

"So she'd have nothing," Silas said. "Even when spring comes. Even if we survive."

"She'd have nothing," Sef agreed. "No swordspeech. No binding word. Ever again, if she recalls now — because the recall consumes the current year's allotment, and the revocation clause burns the sentence itself out of existence." She looked at Lena directly. "You would be an ordinary woman. For the rest of your life. The last of the oath-bound line, ending not with a triumph but with a blank page."

"I don't understand," Silas said, and his voice cracked on it. "If recalling frees her sentence, how does that help me? We need a word of opening. We need—"

"We need her to speak it *now*," Sef said. "Here. Tonight. Before the recall." She laid the amber vellum on the table between them. On it, in faded brown ink, was a single line of formal script — the opening formula, the witness-word that unlocked the court's outer door. "One sentence remains to her. One true sentence, spoken through steel, that becomes law. If she speaks the opening formula — binds herself as witness and thief both, declares intent to commit theft against the Undercourt — the doors will open. The court will be *obligated* to admit her. And once inside, the law of theft does the rest." She paused. "But she must speak it tonight, and then recall it — no. Forgive me. An old woman's tongue outruns her memory." She corrected herself with the precision of someone who had rehearsed this explanation for sixty years. "She speaks the opening word tonight. It opens the door. And the door, once opened by a binding sentence, stays open — the sentence continues to bind, you see, it works *forward*, holding the door until it is fulfilled or recalled. She walks you in. She takes your soul. And if anything goes wrong — if the court traps you, if the theft fails — she can recall the sentence and the door closes and the binding releases and you are both locked outside, alive, defeated, but alive."

"And if she recalls it after we succeed?" Silas asked.

"Then the door closes behind you on the way out, sealing the court's loss permanent," Sef said. "A perfect crime, in the language of contracts. Open with a word, close with its absence." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "There is only one problem, and you have already guessed it."

Lena had gone very still. "The recall burns the sentence forever. If I spend my opening word tonight and then recall it to seal the door behind us — I lose it either way. Spend it or save it, the sentence dies. And with it—" She stopped.

"With it the gift," Sef finished. "Recall requires the sentence to pass back through the steel, and a burned sentence scars the channel. No more swordspeech. Not in spring. Not ever. She told me that too, the dying one. *The gift is a loan, and recall is default.*" Sef sat back down, heavily, and folded her hands. "So. There is your bargain, swordswoman. Speak your last true sentence to open a door for a man you have known nine days, and spend the rest of your life voiceless in the only way that has ever mattered to you. Or keep your gift, keep your oath, keep your place in the guild that raised you — and watch the Spire collect him at winter's end."

* * *

The fire needed tending. Sef tended it, because old hands wanted occupation when the room was deciding something, and because it gave her an excuse not to watch their faces.

She heard the kettle tick. She heard Silas say, quietly, "Don't. Don't you dare. I'm one person. You're the last of—"

"Don't finish that sentence," Lena said.

"—and I'm not worth—"

"You were named in a book a century before you were born," Lena said, and there was something in her voice Sef recognized from her own long-ago griefs: the sound of a woman furious at the universe for making her care. "Do you think I walked across the salt flats for something that wasn't worth it?"

Sef put another log on the coals and let the flare of light cover her expression. Sixty-one years of registry entries, and in all that time only four souls had ever been stolen back out of the court's vaults — and every one of those thefts had been paid for by someone who loved the thief, or the stolen, or both. Hope was a debt. Love was the collateral. She had watched the interest compound her whole life.

"There is one more thing," she said, turning back. "And you will want to throw me out of my own kitchen for it, so I'll say it quickly." She met Lena's eyes. "The opening formula must be spoken as a *true* sentence — that is the nature of swordspeech, truth enforced. And the formula contains a declaration of identity. *I, [name], come as witness and thief.* To speak it truly, you must already believe yourself a thief. Not intend to become one. *Be* one." She picked up the registry, closed it, held it against her chest like a shield. "You broke guild law the night you told him what the ledger said. You have been stealing from them ever since — his custody, his secrecy, this flight. The question is not whether you can become a thief by morning. The question, swordswoman, is whether you can finally admit what you have been since the moment you opened that prophecy book and chose him over your oath."

Outside, far off across the frozen fields, a light moved along the ridge road. Small. Steady. Wrong for a shepherd, wrong for a courier, moving with the patient deliberation of something that had all winter and knew exactly where it was going.

Sef saw it over Lena's shoulder, and her old heart sank with a strange, weary satisfaction — the feeling of a ledger balancing at last.

"They've found us," she said. "So whatever you're going to decide, decide it before that light reaches the gate."
