## 13. Retrieval

Vess Corvane had not slept in three days, and she did not intend to begin now.

Sleep, in her experience, was a currency like any other, and she had never been a generous spender. She sat in the upper study of the guildhouse with the windows shuttered against the salt wind and the ledgers of the Delacroix account spread before her — not Lena's ledgers, which were immaculate, but the older ones, the ones written in her own hand across forty years of careful acquisition. She read them the way other people reread letters from dead lovers.

The breach had been logged at the ninth hour of the evening. *Unauthorized disclosure to a named client.* The guildhouse itself had reported it, the way a body reports a wound: doors rearranging themselves down the east stair, the prophecy ledger slamming shut with a sound like a jaw. Vess had felt it in her teeth. Somewhere beneath her feet, the house had begun to digest.

And Lena Delacroix — her Lena, the orphan she'd pulled half-frozen from the river district at nine years old, the child who'd learned to count coins before she'd learned to trust anyone — had committed the oldest sin on the books.

Vess turned the page. Her own handwriting marched across it, young and certain: *Item: one soul, unbegotten, sold in utero by the father Corvane line to the court beneath the mountain. Purchase price: negligible. Projected yield at alignment: considerable.*

She had bought Silas's debt for less than the cost of a good horse. That was the joke of it, the private joke she'd carried for thirty years while the entry gathered dust: that the guild's most valuable holding had been acquired as an afterthought, a speculation by a younger Vess who understood, even then, that souls compounded better than gold. The court beneath the mountain always paid its debts. It simply preferred to pay them late, with interest measured in whole human lives.

Now the Spire's shadow lengthened toward winter's end, and the investment was coming due, and the asset had walked into her guildhouse and sat down to dinner like any ordinary man.

*He seemed kind,* Vess thought, and was disgusted with herself for thinking it. Kindness was not a line item. Kindness was what clients said when they wanted better terms.

She rang the small brass bell on her desk — once, twice, a third time with a pause between — and heard the house answer. Floorboards shifted in the halls below like a great animal turning over. The retrievers were already assembling. She could hear them: the soft industrial click of their harnesses, the whisper of chain, the particular silence they made when they stopped breathing so as to listen better.

"Master Corvane." The senior retriever stood in the doorway without having knocked. They all did that. It was part of the training — or perhaps part of whatever they were made from; Vess had signed those contracts too, long ago, and had wisely declined to read the full text. "The trail is warm. The Delacroix woman and the asset crossed the south wall at moonrise."

"The salt flats," Vess said. "They'll make for the mountain road."

"They'll die on the salt flats," the retriever said, without inflection. "If you prefer."

"I prefer them alive." Vess closed the ledger. "Both of them. The woman is guild property — oath-bound, trained, irreplaceable. The man is collateral. Bring back the collateral intact and the property unspoiled, and there will be bonuses."

"And if the property resists?"

Vess looked out through the shutters, at the thin blade of lamplight lying across the floor like something spilled. She remembered a girl of eleven, standing in this same study, refusing to cry after the binding-oath had burned her tongue. *It doesn't hurt,* the girl had lied, in the flat voice children use when they have decided that pain is a tax and they will simply pay it. Vess had admired that lie more than she had ever admitted.

"If she resists," Vess said quietly, "remind her of the terms. Remind her that everything she is, we paid for. And then bring her home."

* * *

On the third night of the flight, the storm found them.

Vess knew it before the reports came — the house told her things now, grudgingly, the way a wronged servant tells on the household. Wind off the flats hard enough to strip paint. Visibility nothing. The retrievers were blind in weather; they tracked by scent and contract-sense, and a storm scrambled both. But contracts did not care about weather. Contracts cared about *where*, and the mountain road narrowed at the ferry crossing, and there was nowhere else to be.

She stood at the map table while junior partners scurried at its edges, moving carved markers across the painted flats. The marker labeled RETRIEVAL crept eastward. The markers labeled DELACROIX and ASSET had stalled near the river.

"They've reached the ferry," someone said.

"Then they're paying the toll," Vess said. "Everyone pays the toll."

She wondered, briefly and unwelcome, what Lena would pay. Memory, probably. The ferry spirit took memory the way toll-takers took coin, and Lena had memories worth stealing — Vess made sure of that. She had spent twenty years making sure that every warm thing in the girl's life had been provided by the guild, so that every memory the guild might later reclaim would point back to the guild like a compass needle. It was not cruelty. It was architecture.

But tonight, watching the markers crawl, Vess permitted herself a different thought: that she hoped the girl would pay with something else. Something that wasn't her.

She crushed the thought the moment she identified it. Sentiment was a leak in the hull, and she had kept this hull afloat for forty years precisely by bailing such water before it accumulated.

"Double the teams," she said. "I want them cornered at the village. Not taken — cornered. A creature runs fastest when it still believes in escape, and I want to know where they run *to*. Who else knows about the asset? Who else has been talking?"

No one answered, because no one knew, and that frightened Vess more than she showed. Someone had taught Lena about Elder Sef. Someone had fed her the old law — *the court honors only theft* — and there were perhaps four living people who knew that law, and Vess intended to have a conversation with each of them.

The bell above the map table trembled. Then it rang — not her summons, but the house's own alarm, high and thin, a sound Vess had heard only twice in her life.

"What," she said, "is that."

A junior partner ran to the window and pulled back the shutter, and the wind came in carrying salt and something else, something warm and impossible: the smell of bread.

"Baking," the partner said stupidly. "Someone's baking. In the—"

"In the kitchens," Vess finished, and went cold.

Because Marta Voss — hedge-witch, cook, foster sister, the one guild member whose loyalty had never been contractual because Vess had never thought her important enough to bind — was supposed to be locked in her kitchen under watch. And Marta Voss's magic was the small, despised, domestic kind: rising dough and softened butter and water that remembered being rain. Useless magic. Kitchen magic. The kind of magic no one bound, because no one feared it.

Vess had made many calculations in her life. She had, in this one, failed to price the bread.

* * *

The report came in fragments over the next hour, and each fragment was worse than the last.

The retrievers had cornered the fugitives at the edge of the village of the Sold, just as ordered — six of them in a crescent, chains singing taut, the night lit orange by their contract-lanterns. The asset had been fading visibly; the Spire's pull was on him now like a tide going out. Two more hours and he would have been too thin to carry.

And then the wind had changed, and brought the smell of fresh bread across the flats, and Marta Voss had walked out of the storm.

Vess reconstructed it afterward from the survivors' accounts, and even reconstructed it made no sense. The woman had come barefoot through the salt — barefoot, on ground that ate flesh — carrying nothing but a covered basket and an expression the senior retriever described, in his flat report language, as *"maternal."*

She had set the basket down. She had lifted the cloth. And the smell that came out of it had undone them.

Because here was the thing about kitchen-magic, the thing Vess had never bothered to learn: it was the oldest bargain there was. Bread was the first contract. Flour and water and fire and time — *give me your hunger and I will give you rest* — and every retriever in that crescent had been hungry for longer than they could remember, hungry in the way that made things are hungry, hollow where their appetites used to live. Marta's bread smelled like the one meal they had ever been given freely, back when they were still people enough to be given things.

Three of them sat down on the salt and wept. One began to eat his own contract-lantern. The senior retriever alone held formation, chains raised, advancing on the fugitives —

— and Marta stepped between him and them, and said, in the voice she used for burnt pots and stubborn apprentices:

"No. You'll sit down and have something first. That's just manners."

And the senior retriever — a construct worth more than the guildhouse wing he stood in — *sat down.*

That was when Vess understood the shape of her defeat, and it was not the bread. It was the arithmetic. Marta had traded herself. While the retrievers sat entranced, the fugitives had run — Lena half-carrying the fading Silas, stumbling up toward the mountain's throat — and Marta had stayed, basket open, feeding monsters, until the spell thinned and the chains came for her instead.

The last fragment of the report was the worst. The senior retriever, resuming function, had asked her — genuinely puzzled, in the tone of a machine encountering an unscheduled variable — *"Why?"*

And Marta Voss, in chains, bleeding from her salt-burned feet, had smiled at him the way she smiled at everyone, and said:

"Because somebody has to feed my family. Even the parts of it that don't know they're mine yet."

* * *

Vess dismissed the junior partners and stood alone with the map table for a long time.

The markers told the story plainly enough. DELACROIX and ASSET: entered the mountain. MARTA VOSS: held, guildhouse, cell four. The retrieval teams: recalled, demoralized, two of them requesting — *requesting* — reassignment to kitchen duty, which had never happened in the history of the guild and which Vess did not have the energy to punish.

Cell four. Vess went down to see it herself, because she was honest with herself in the way that cruel people often are, and honesty required looking.

Marta sat on the cot with her hands folded in her lap, flour still in the creases of her knuckles. She looked up when Vess entered, and she was not afraid, and that was the most infuriating thing in the world.

"You should have run with them," Vess said.

"I'm not built for running." Marta flexed her burned feet and winced, cheerfully. "I'm built for feeding people. Besides, someone had to stay behind and keep an eye on you."

"On *me*."

"Someone always has." Marta looked at her with those terrible clear eyes, the eyes of a woman who had raised orphans and therefore could not be lied to. "You loved her, Vess. In whatever way they let you love things down here. You kept her close for twenty years and called it custody. Well. Custody's ending. And I want to watch what you do when it does."

Vess stood in the doorway of cell four and felt, for the first time in forty years, the full weight of the ledger she had written — every soul purchased cheap, every affection converted to collateral, every kindness filed as investment. The chandelier of contracts hung somewhere beneath the mountain, and she had spent her life adding links to it, link by link, telling herself it was architecture.

"She'll come back for you," Vess said finally. "Lena. She'll trade herself for you. It's what I trained her for — she always balances the books."

"No," Marta said gently. "You trained her to survive. I taught her to share. There's a difference, and you're about to learn it the expensive way."

Vess left the cell without answering. In the corridor, the guildhouse groaned around her — doors rearranging, floors shifting, the whole vast organism of the place restless with a change it could feel coming and could not name. Far below, deeper than the cellars, deeper than the vaults, something ancient stirred in response to the mountain's stirring, two great machines of debt turning toward each other across the dark.

Vess climbed back to her study and opened the Delacroix ledger to a fresh page. Her pen hovered over it.

Whatever she wrote next would decide whether she was the guild's master or merely its longest-serving prisoner. And down in cell four, humming softly, Marta was already baking again — the guards letting her, the guards *asking* her — filling the guildhouse with the smell of bread, as if the building itself were being slowly, patiently, lovingly fed its way free.
