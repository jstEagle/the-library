## 14. Descent

**Silas**

The mountain's throat did not open so much as admit them.

Silas had expected a door — some great slab of stone with a lock worth stealing, something Lena could put her shoulder against while he talked his way past it. Instead the cliff face simply grew a mouth. The rock ahead of them rippled like water disturbed by a dropped stone, and a passage unfolded inward, breathing out air that smelled of cold iron and old paper and, beneath it all, faintly of honey.

Lena stood at its edge with her sword drawn and said nothing. She had said very little since Marta's capture — since the moment the retrievers' net closed over her foster sister and Marta had shouted at them both to *run, you idiots, run* — but Silas was learning to read her silences the way he had once learned to read coastlines. This one meant: *I am deciding whether to go in after my sister or forward for your soul, and I hate that I cannot do both.*

"Forward," he said quietly. "Marta told us forward. She'd haunt us if we turned around."

"She'd haunt us anyway." But Lena sheathed the sword, and stepped through the mountain's teeth first, because that was the kind of person she was — she would take the darkness on herself before letting him walk into it alone.

Inside, the passage was lit by nothing they could name. The walls gave off a pale luminescence, like moonlight soaked into chalk, and Silas saw that they were not walls at all but shelves — strata of stone packed with objects, thousands upon thousands of them, receding beyond sight: rings, keys, letters sealed with wax gone amber, a child's shoe, a wedding veil folded with terrible care. Every object hummed faintly when he passed, the way a struck tuning fork hums after the sound has left human hearing.

"What are they?" he asked.

"Lives," Lena said. "Or the parts of lives people sold to get through winters." Her voice echoed strangely, as if the mountain were repeating it back to itself somewhere far below. "The guild calls this the Antechamber. Vess called it our warehouse once. I thought she was being poetic."

They walked down. The slope was gentle and endless, and the air grew warmer rather than colder, which felt like a violation of everything mountains owed the world. Somewhere below, water moved — not rushing, but turning, slow and deliberate, like a page being smoothed flat by a careful hand.

Silas noticed it first as a lightness in his own footsteps. Then as a thinning of the world's edges — the shelf-objects losing their detail, blurring like ink touched by a wet thumb. Then as a strange double vision, in which he could see himself walking beside himself, one figure solid and one rendered only in outline, the way cartographers drew coastlines whose interiors no surveyor had yet reached.

*Lena,* he wanted to say. *I am becoming an unexplored territory.*

But when he opened his mouth, what came out was: "How much farther?"

And he understood, with a calm that frightened him more than panic would have, that the question had been asked by whichever version of him still had enough substance to ask it.

* * *

**Lena**

She counted his fading the way she counted coin: precisely, without flinching, because flinching cost time they did not have.

It began at his hands. By the second hour of descent, Silas's fingers had gone translucent at the tips, candlelight passing through them as though they were wax. He kept flexing them, testing, and each time he caught her watching he smiled — that easy, unmoored smile that had disarmed her across a dinner table a lifetime ago, eleven days ago, whatever time was worth down here.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Do the thing where you make it fine."

He considered this. "You do the thing where you make it fine too. You've done it since I met you. I'm just better at it."

"You're not better at anything than me."

"There it is," he said softly. "There's my Lena."

*I am not yours,* she almost said, and did not, because the mountain was listening — she could feel it listening, the way a creditor listens, the way the guildhouse listened — and some things spoken here might be entered into evidence. Instead she reached out and took his hand, the solid part, and found that even the solid part felt like holding a memory of a hand: warmth without quite enough weight.

The stair ended where the market began.

She had read about the Undermarket in the guild's restricted catalogues, in entries written by partners who had descended to negotiate and returned changed, quieter, some of them never speaking above a whisper again. None of the entries had prepared her for the sound. It was not noise. It was voices — hundreds of them, thousands, murmuring from every direction, and when her eyes adjusted to the pearl-grey gloom she saw why.

Bottles. Shelves and stalls and carts and strings hung between stalactites, all of them crowded with bottles of every shape glassblowers had ever dreamed: long-necked, round-bellied, faceted like gems, squat as onions. And inside each bottle a voice moved like a goldfish, a small coiling brightness that pressed against the glass when a passerby came near.

A vendor — or something shaped like a vendor, tall and grey-skinned with eyes like coins sunk in wells — leaned toward them from behind a stall of bottled laughter. "Voices," it said, in a voice that sounded borrowed from three different people. "Singing voices. Lying voices. A mother's voice saying a child's name, very rare, very dear. What will you trade?"

"We're not buying," Lena said.

"Everyone buys," the vendor said pleasantly. "Everyone who comes down here is missing something. That is the whole of commerce, swordspeaker. We simply keep honest inventory."

She pulled Silas past stalls selling bottled courage, bottled grief (aged twenty years, guaranteed mellow), bottled last words. In one corner a merchant displayed a single bottle glowing white-hot, and the label read *A MAN'S NAME, SPOKEN BY HIS FATHER AT HIS BIRTH*, and the price was not written in any currency Lena recognized but in years, in distances, in names-of-other-things.

Silas stopped walking.

She felt him stop before she saw it — the tug on her grip, gentle as a tide going out. He was staring at a bottle on a low shelf, ordinary as a jam jar, and inside it a voice coiled and pressed against the glass, and the voice was *his*. She knew it the way you know your own handwriting. It was his laugh, his way of saying *well then*, the particular music of him, trapped and dimming.

"That one's mine," he said. His voice, the live one, came out very small. "That's — Lena, that's mine, it's already here. They already took it."

"No." She turned him by the shoulders. "No. Look at me. If they'd collected it, you wouldn't be standing here arguing with me about it. That's a copy. An echo. The court makes them — decoys, inventory, proof of claim. Your soul is still theirs to collect, which means it hasn't been collected." She heard how the logic sounded and hated it. "It means we're in time."

"In time," he repeated. He was looking at his hands. The translucence had climbed past his wrists now, and when he lifted them toward the market's grey light she could see the bottles behind them, ghosted through his skin. "Lena. When we get there. Whatever happens—"

"Don't."

"—whatever happens, I need you to know that I was provisional my whole life. Borrowed. I moved towns the way other people change coats, and I always thought someday someone would come to collect me, and now I know why, and the strange thing—" His voice cracked, and the bottle on the shelf flared briefly, sympathetically, as if the two voices shared one wound. "The strange thing is that being collected doesn't scare me half as much as it used to. Because now there's someone who'd come after me. Do you understand? I've been unclaimed property for thirty-one years and now I'm *contested*, and it's the best thing that ever happened to me."

Lena Delacroix, who could price a miracle to the copper, who had spent her life fluent in every currency but this one, did the only thing she knew how to do with a surplus she couldn't ledger. She kissed him, there in the market of stolen voices, surrounded by everything people had given up, and held on to the solid parts of him while the mountain watched and made its notes.

* * *

**Silas**

The river appeared without warning, the way rivers do in dreams.

One moment they were threading between stalls; the next the market fell away behind them and there it was — a broad, slow flood of silver-white filling the cavern floor to ceiling, moving with the patience of something that had never once been late. It made no sound. That was the worst of it. A river that size should have roared, and instead it flowed in perfect silence, and Silas realized he could see through it to the riverbed, and the riverbed was made of faces. Not skulls. Faces — sleeping, dreaming, mouths slightly open, generations deep, silted over with time.

"The river of unspent years," Lena whispered. "Elder Sef said—"

"I remember. Everything the sold never got to do. It goes back to the mountain eventually." He crouched at the bank. His reflection looked up at him, and the reflection was worse than his hands — it was faint, a sketch of a man, an artist's first attempt abandoned halfway. "Does it hurt them? The ones in the river?"

"It's not them. It's what they would have been. There's a difference." She said it firmly, and then, quieter, honestly: "I don't actually know if there's a difference."

A boat was coming. Of course a boat was coming. It slid out of the silver silence without wake, poled by a figure wrapped in layered grey cloth, faceless — or faced everywhere, Silas couldn't decide; the wrappings shifted and suggested features the way clouds suggest ships. The ferryman brought the boat alongside the bank and extended one wrapped hand, palm up.

Not for coin. Silas understood that immediately, the way you understand things in the last hour of yourself — quickly, totally, without argument. The ferryman's palm was a small dark hollow, like the opening of a bottle.

"Toll," Lena said flatly. "In memory."

The ferryman inclined its head.

"How much?"

The ferryman regarded Silas for a long moment. Then it gestured — at all of him, the thinning outline of him — and made a small weighing motion, as if to say: *him, cheaply. He is mostly paid for already.*

"No," Silas said, stepping forward. "Take mine. Take — I've got good ones. The night I met her. The map I made of my mother's garden when I was six, every row of beans. Take those, take all of them, I'll swim the rest of the way if I have to—"

"Stop." Lena's voice went through the cavern like her sword through air. Even the river seemed to still. "You don't get to spend yourself down to nothing to save me the cost. That's not how this works anymore. We're not separate accounts." She rolled up her sleeve, and Silas saw her forearm, and saw the faint silver scarring there — old marks, oath-marks, the ledger of a woman who had paid for things her whole life in pieces of herself.

She took the ferryman's hollow palm in both her hands, and Silas watched her choose. He watched her lips move silently, offering up memories one by one like coins tested between finger and thumb, discarding some, and then she went still, and he knew the instant she selected the payment because her face did something he had never seen it do: it grieved in advance.

The ferryman drank.

Lena swayed. Silas caught her — caught most of her; his arms passed through her elbow before closing on solid wrist — and for a moment she stared at the middle distance with the expression of a woman reading a book and finding pages torn out.

"What did it take?" he asked. "Lena. What did it take?"

She blinked slowly. "Someone," she said. "Someone important. I can feel the shape of where they were." Her jaw tightened. "Vess. It was Vess. I paid with Vess — the good years, the early ones, when she taught me my letters and let me sleep in her study during storms." Her voice didn't shake, but it went thin as wire. "I can remember that she mattered. I can't remember why."

"You didn't have to—"

"Marta is in a cell upstairs because of me. You are dissolving beside me because of a contract signed before your grandmother was born. I am not spending *your* memories to cross a river, Silas. You'll want all of them where we're going. You'll want to know exactly what we're fighting for." She stepped onto the boat, steady, terrible, magnificent, and held out her hand to him. "Come on. We're nearly there, and I intend to arrive with everything of you that's left."

He took her hand and boarded, and the silent river carried them down into the dark, past the sleeping faces of every year anyone had ever failed to live.

* * *

**Lena**

The far bank was a door.

Not a grand one — that was the horror of it. After the market and the river, after all that scale, the door into the court beneath the mountain was a plain iron-bound thing, the height of a man, set into living rock without arch or ornament. It looked like the back entrance of a counting-house. Which, Lena thought, was exactly what it was.

The Spire's alignment was close. She could feel it in her teeth, in the oath-scar on her arm, in the way Silas's outline flickered now with every heartbeat, guttering like a lamp low on oil. Above them, miles of stone, winter was ending. The stars were sliding into their appointed places. Somewhere in the deep of the mountain, something vast was opening its books.

Silas stood before the door and pressed his fading palm flat against the iron. "Locked," he said. "Of course it's locked."

"It's not locked." Elder Sef's words, learned in a village of the hollow-eyed, rose in Lena's memory with the weight of law: *The court honors only theft. Its doors open to no buyer, no beggar, no penitent. They open to a thief — and a thief must be named by a speaker's word, spoken true, spoken through steel.* "It's waiting," she said aloud. "For me."

She drew her sword.

The blade caught the pearl-light of the river and threw it back silver, and along its fuller the old oath-marks gleamed — every binding sentence she had ever spoken through steel, etched there by her own voice, a lifetime of law laid down in metal. One sentence per year. Hers already spent keeping ledgers honest, keeping clients bound, keeping the guild's impossible accounts balanced.

Except.

Her heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs. Except the year was not yet out. Winter's end — the alignment — the turn of the season. The old reckoning measured the speaker's year from solstice to solstice, and the solstice had passed twelve nights ago, in the salt flats, under storm, while she slept against Silas's shoulder and dreamed of nothing at all.

Twelve nights ago, a new sentence had become hers. She had not known. She had been carrying it unspent through the market and the river like a coin she didn't know was in her pocket.

"Silas." Her voice came out strange. "Step back from the door."

He turned, and even fading, even two-thirds ghost, he read her face the way he read maps — instantly, wholly, seeing the whole country from the single contour. "You have a word," he breathed. "You have a word right now."

"One," she said. "One word, and then never again. Not in spring, not ever. Whatever I say through this steel becomes law, and then the gift is gone, and I go with it — the speaking part of me, the part that binds. I'll live. I just won't... *weigh* anything I say ever again." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Vess always said my word was the most valuable thing in the guildhouse. She was right. She just never imagined I'd spend it on *you*."

"Lena—"

"Get behind me. And whatever comes through this door — whatever's left of the court, whatever the founder became — you stay behind me until the theft is done. Promise me."

"You know I can't promise you that."

"I know," she said, and raised the blade, and set its point against the iron door, and felt the mountain lean in to listen. "That's why I'm not asking."

Somewhere far above, the last star of winter slid home into its socket, and the Spire aligned, and the door beneath her swordpoint began — softly, patiently, like a clerk unlocking a shop at dawn — to open.

Behind it, in the dark, something that had been waiting a hundred years said her name.
