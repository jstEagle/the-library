## 15. The Court Beneath

The last stair did not end so much as surrender.

Lena felt it in her boots first — the stone going soft beneath her tread, the way packed snow softens before a thaw — and then in the air itself, which thickened until each breath arrived pre-loaded with meaning. Not incense. Not rot. Something older and more patient: the smell of a library that had been left to dream for a hundred years.

Silas's hand found hers in the dark. His fingers were cold, and colder than they should have been, and she could feel the fading in them now the way you feel a fever coming on — not yet visible, but unmistakable to anyone paying attention. She was paying attention. She had been paying attention since the salt flats, cataloguing every hour of him like a debt she intended to collect personally from whatever god kept such books.

"Tell me what you see," he said quietly. He couldn't see much himself; the dark down here ate light rather than merely lacking it. But his voice was steady, and she loved him a little more for the steadiness, and hated herself for doing arithmetic about how many more hours of steadiness the Spire would leave him.

"Stairs," she said. "And below the stairs—"

Below the stairs was a cavern so vast it had weather. Lena felt a wind move past her cheek that had no business existing underground, carrying the mineral breath of deep places, and when she lifted the lantern Marta had pressed into her hands at the mountain's mouth, the light simply refused to travel. It lit three feet of air and gave up, embarrassed, like a messenger who has realized the size of the room he's been sent into.

So they descended by touch and by sound, and by the strange luminescence that began to seep out of the walls themselves — veins of pale gold running through black stone, pulsing with a rhythm almost but not quite like breathing. Contract-ink, Lena realized. The whole mountain was laced with it. Every foot of tunnel they walked was written on, somewhere, in some language older than the guild's oldest ledger, and the ink remembered being alive.

"How far down are we?" Silas asked.

"Far enough that the sky is a rumor."

"Good." He said it lightly, but his grip tightened. "I've always wanted to be somewhere the sky couldn't find me. It never once looked away."

She understood what he meant. She had spent her whole life under the guildhouse skylights, those great glass eyes Vess had installed in every reading room, and she knew the particular exhaustion of being perpetually observed. Down here, for the first time since the prophecy ledger had opened itself on her dinner table, no one was watching them.

That lasted another quarter mile.

* * *

They heard the court before they saw it: a sound like pages turning, multiplied ten thousandfold, a dry whispering tide that rose and fell in the cavern dark. Then the tunnel opened its throat and set them down at the edge of a gallery of black stone, and Lena stopped breathing.

The Undercourt was not a room. It was a held breath.

The cavern arched overhead higher than any cathedral, ribbed with stalactites that had been carved — or had grown, she could not tell which was worse — into the shapes of columns, of gallows-posts, of quills the length of masts. And hanging in the center of all that darkness, suspended from nothing she could identify, was the chandelier.

It was made of contracts.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Parchment scrolls curled into buds and blossoms, vellum sheets folded into the geometry of petals, wax seals clustered like berries, all of it strung together on chains of what looked like hair and hung in tiers descending toward the cavern floor — a vast inverted tree of paper and promise, glowing faintly gold from within, because the contract-ink in its fibers was still alive, still dreaming, still *binding*. Each time the whispering tide rose, Lena understood that it was the chandelier turning slowly on its chain, and each scroll was murmuring its own terms to the dark.

She recognized the handwriting on the nearest tier. Of course she did. She had spent eleven years keeping ledgers in that hand, learning its slants and tempers, its way of pressing hard on names and soft on dates. The Undercourt's chandelier was written in the founding script of the Delacroix Guild.

*Our* script. The guild's first hand, the one Vess made Lena practice until her own writing forgot how to be anything else.

"Lena," Silas said. His voice had gone thin. She turned and saw why.

He was fading. Not metaphorically. The lantern light was passing through the edges of him now — through his sleeve, through the ends of his hair, through the fingers that had held hers so firmly on the stairs. The Spire's alignment was close. Hours, perhaps. The mountain was already beginning to read him.

"We're here," she said, and heard how useless the words were, and said them anyway, because someone had to fill the silence and she refused to let it be the chandelier. "We came all this way. Whatever happens next, we *came*."

"I know." He managed something that was almost his smile — the one from the blind date, crooked and unmoored, the smile of a man with no fixed address. "I keep thinking I should be more frightened. But mostly I'm just — relieved? Is that terrible? Like I've been waiting my whole life to be collected, and now that it's happening, at least it's *happening*. At least the waiting's over."

"It's not over," Lena said, and put everything she had left into the sentence. "I didn't walk across a desert and rob a ferryman and lose—" she caught herself on the memory of Vess, paid out at the river without witness, "—lose things, to watch you get collected politely. We are here to commit a crime, Silas. Try to look criminal."

That earned a real laugh, brief and startled, echoing off the contract-columns. And it was still hanging in the air when the monarch arrived.

* * *

There was no throne. That was the first thing Lena noticed, and the thing she would think about most afterward. For all the theater of the place — the vastness, the chandelier, the ink-veined dark — the founder of the Delacroix Guild stood waiting for them on the bare cavern floor like a shopkeeper who had stepped out from behind the counter.

She was small. That was the second thing. A woman perhaps sixty, perhaps six hundred, dressed in plain grey wool gone soft with age, her white hair braided back in the style Lena had seen only in the portrait above the guildhouse hearth — the founder's portrait, the one every orphan raised in the house was taught to curtsey to. Her face was the portrait's face, aged honestly. Her eyes were brown and warm and terribly, catastrophically kind.

"You must be tired," the woman said. Her voice was low and unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it. "The salt flats are cruel this time of year. And the ferryman — ah, I felt the toll paid at the river. A memory, wasn't it? They always take memories now. In my day it was years." She smiled, and the smile reached her eyes, and Lena felt the floor tilt under her certainty. "You have her hands, child. Vess's. She holds a pen the same way. As if the paper might argue."

"You're her," Lena said. It came out flat, an accusation wearing the clothes of a question. "You're the founder. You've been dead for two hundred years."

"Dead." The woman considered the word like a coin of doubtful weight. "Archived, perhaps. The guild keeps what it cannot use. Including me." She spread her hands, and Lena saw that the fingertips were stained gold — contract-ink soaked so deep into the skin it had become the skin. "I built the first ledger, you know. Before there was a guild, before there was a Spire, there was a woman with a debt she could not pay and a talent for making lists. Everything else grew from that. Empires usually do."

Beside her, Silas swayed. Lena caught his elbow and felt the wrongness of him — solid where she gripped, translucent where the lantern touched, a man becoming a draft of himself.

"He's nearly due," the founder said gently. "Yes. I can see it. The alignment is tonight, and the Spire is impatient, and you have come all this way to do something foolish and brave, and I would very much like to save us all the trouble of pretending we don't know what it is." She turned those warm brown eyes fully on Lena, and Lena felt the full weight of them, and understood suddenly why Vess Corvane — who feared nothing Lena had ever seen — kept the founder's portrait dusted daily.

"So let me make you an offer instead," the founder said. "A real one. No riddles, no trials, no theft required."

* * *

She led them to the base of the chandelier, walking slowly, and Lena noticed that the scrolls nearest the woman leaned toward her as she passed, the way flowers lean at a window. The founder stopped beneath the lowest tier and gestured upward, and one contract detached itself from the chain and drifted down into her palm like a leaf deciding to fall.

"This one," she said, "is your young man's. Or rather — this is the *original*. The master copy. Every soul sold to the court beneath the mountain exists here first, in this hand, in this ink, and the Spire merely enforces what this page says." She held it out so they could see. Lena read it in a single glance, because she had been trained her whole life to read contracts in a single glance, and this one was short, and old, and bore a name in a firm infant's-cry scrawl: *SILAS. SOLD PRE-BIRTH. TERM: ONE CENTURY OR UNTIL ALIGNMENT.*

"The century is up," the founder said. "The alignment is tonight. By morning this page will be fulfilled and he will belong to the court entire — voice, memory, name, the lot. Unless." She paused, and the pause was masterful, and Lena thought with a distant, cold clarity: *Vess learned everything from this woman. Everything.* "Unless the page is superseded. Contracts bind both ways, child. That is the first law and the last one. If a greater instrument supersedes a lesser, the lesser dissolves. And there is no instrument in the world greater than the Delacroix ledgers."

Lena's heart began, very quietly, to break.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to. I can see them in your face. Mercy always looks like this. It's why mercy is dangerous."

The founder's smile deepened, and there was — Lena searched for it and found it and wished she hadn't — genuine affection in it. "Then hear them anyway, for the sake of the argument. Sign over the guild's ledgers to me. All of them. Every impossible debt the house has brokered in two centuries — every miracle traded, every life extended, every name held in trust, every soul like his." She nodded at Silas, whose translucence had crept past his wrists now. "Bring me the ledgers, and I will burn this page myself, tonight, before the alignment completes. He walks out of this mountain whole. You walk out with him. Everyone is saved. No oath broken, no theft committed, no war with the retrievers, no more running. Just a signature. One signature, from the finest ledger-keeper the house has produced in a generation — and Vess told me about you, child, she writes to me, did you know that? She has written to me every solstice for thirty years. She thinks I'm listening. Perhaps I am."

The words settled over Lena like snowfall, soft and suffocating.

Because here was the poison of it, and the genius of it, and the reason her hands were already trembling toward yes: it *would work*. That was the unbearable part. Sef's law said the court honored only theft, that souls must be stolen back, that purchase and bargain were traps. But Sef had never seen the master copy. Sef had never stood beneath the chandelier and watched a contract drift down like an autumn leaf into the founder's patient hand. If the ledgers superseded the soul-contracts — and they would, they *would*, the guild's instruments were the strongest binding-magic in the world, Lena had spent her career enforcing exactly that hierarchy — then Silas would go free. Truly free. Tonight. Whole.

And every other soul in those ledgers would pass into the hands of the woman who had invented the trade in impossible things. Every debtor, every depositor, every orphan whose name was collateral, every village like the Village of the Sold with its registry of hollow-eyed waiters. Lena would buy one man's freedom with the deeds to ten thousand captives, and the signature would be hers, and it would be legal, and it would be binding, and it would be called salvation right up until history finished counting.

"That's the offer," the founder said softly. "One book for one boy. Or rather — one library for one boy, though I suspect you'll find the arithmetic kinder than it sounds. Ledgers are just paper, child. Paper can be rewritten. Souls, as you can see—" she tipped her head at Silas, who was now luminous at the edges, his outline dissolving into the gold of the ink-veins, "—are considerably harder."

"She's right," Silas said.

His voice startled her. He had been quiet so long, fading so gracefully, that she'd half-forgotten he could still speak. He turned to her, and his eyes were still entirely his own, and that was somehow the worst thing in the cavern.

"Lena. Look at me. It's *me*. One signature and I'm free, and you — you keep your oath. You keep everything. Your family, your gift, your year-word, all of it. You don't have to be voiceless. You don't have to be exiled. You don't have to lose anyone else." He smiled his crooked smile, and it cost him visibly, the effort of smiling while being erased. "I've been borrowed my whole life. I know what I cost. Don't pay ten thousand people for me. I'm not worth —"

"Don't." The word came out of Lena like a blade leaving a sheath. "Don't you dare finish that sentence. Don't you dare tell me what you're worth. I keep the ledgers, Silas. *Me.* And I am telling you that the value column on you is blank, because you are not for sale, and neither am I, and neither—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—neither are the ten thousand."

The founder watched all of this with the expression of a teacher watching a student arrive, late but correctly, at the answer.

"There it is," the woman said. "There's the Delacroix spine. Vess wrote to me about that too. 'The girl will either be my successor or my ruin,' she said, 'and I genuinely do not know which.'" The founder folded Silas's contract closed between her ink-stained fingers. "So. You refuse. I expected you might. Then understand what happens next, child, because I am not cruel, whatever the ledgers say. When the alignment completes, the Spire collects him, and the collection is not painless, and it is not quick, and he will experience every page of himself being read aloud. And you will stand here and watch, because the court does not permit interference during collection, and the court's law is older than your guild, older than your oath, older than the alphabet your ledgers are written in."

She turned to go, then paused, and looked back over her shoulder with something that in a younger face would have been hope.

"Unless," she said again, "you have thought of something I haven't. You have until the chandelier finishes turning. It turns once a century. It is nearly done."

Above them, the vast inverted tree of contracts groaned through the last degrees of its rotation, ten thousand scrolls whispering their terms, and among them — Lena could hear it now, threading through the tide, growing clearer as the alignment approached — one page murmuring a single name, over and over, in a firm infant's cry:

*Silas. Silas. Silas.*

Lena took her sword off her back.

She did not draw it. Not yet. She only held it, feeling the steel remember her hand, feeling the one true sentence she had hoarded since spring sitting behind her teeth like a swallowed star — and beside her, Silas watched her grip the hilt and slowly, slowly understood what she was about to spend.

"Lena," he whispered. "*No.* There has to be another way."

The chandelier turned. The ink in the walls pulsed faster. Somewhere far above, the Spire began, at last, to align.

"There isn't," Lena said. "But that was never the question. The question was always whether I'd pay it."

She raised the blade.
