## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The queen's breakfast arrived at half past seven, as it always did, and Mirabel Nystrom tasted it as he always did: one bite of everything, in order of least likely to kill.

Bread first. Then butter. Then the poached eggs, the smoked trout, the honey from the royal apiaries that had once, three years ago, come laced with monkshood by a gardener with a grudge and a dead wife. He took a measured grain of each dish's risk onto his tongue and waited, counting heartbeats the way other men counted coins. Forty-one beats for the trout. Fifty-two for the honey. Nothing bloomed, nothing numbed, nothing whispered up the back of his throat in the voice of arsenic or worse.

"Clean," he said, and the under-steward wrote it down, and somewhere above them the queen ate her breakfast without ever learning Mirabel's name.

That was the arrangement. The crown owned his body—had bought it at fourteen, when a taster with his particular constitution was worth more than a horse—and in exchange for his mornings, his stomach, and eventually probably his life, they left him alone the rest of the day. Nobody asked where he went. Nobody asked about the second ledger he kept, the private one, bound in grey leather and hidden behind a loose brick in his rooms on Candlemaker's Row. Nobody asked why a food-taster needed to record doses in tenths of grains, or why some entries were dated years before his employment began.

He had started dosing himself at eleven. His mother had sold him to a poisoner's guild for a winter's coal, and the guild had discovered quickly that the boy didn't die like other boys died. So they'd fed him the curriculum instead: hemlock on Mondays, foxglove in increments, a slow education in every venom the city could source until his blood became a ledger of its own, forty poisons deep, each one catalogued and paid for in pain. It made him nearly useless as a victim and invaluable as an instrument. Kings liked instruments. Instruments didn't ask questions.

Mirabel asked questions all day long. He just never asked them out loud.

* * *

By noon he was crossing the Weavers' Bridge with his collar up, moving through the fish-market crowd the way water moves through a net—present everywhere, caught nowhere. Invisibility was a craft like any other. You practiced it. You kept your shoulders rounded and your eyes down and your face arranged into the expression of a man thinking about lunch. For nineteen years it had worked so well that guards forgot him between doorways and clerks misfiled his papers and once, memorably, a whole council session proceeded while he stood in the corner holding the queen's wine, entirely unremarked.

The Gilded Thorn occupied a narrow house on Vellum Street that looked, from outside, like the offices of a moderately successful importer of dyes. Inside, it smelled of ink and beeswax and, faintly, of things that had no business existing: a jar of last year's summer, sealed and labeled; a cage containing something that hummed in a key no instrument could match; a drawer, which Mirabel had never opened and never would, marked simply *BEFORE*.

He let himself in through the accounting door, because that was what he was. Not an enforcer, not a broker, not one of the masked collectors who went out at night to repossess promises. He was the man who balanced the books of impossible things.

"Three hours late," said Odile Vane, the guildmaster, not looking up from her correspondence. She was sixty and sharp as a paper cut, with silver hair pinned like a threat and rings on eight fingers, none of them decorative.

"The queen wanted kippers."

"I don't pay you to be interesting." She slid a stack of folios across the desk. "Ashcombe's account is wrong again. He's been selling weather."

"Selling it?"

"Rain, mostly. To farmers east of the walls. Small amounts. Unlicensed." Her mouth thinned. "Weather is a Thorn monopoly, Mirabel. Fix the numbers, note the infraction, and tell Ferro I want Ashcombe's rain-jars inventoried by Friday."

It was ordinary work, and he loved it the way some men love carpentry—the fit of numbers against each other, the satisfaction of a column that came true. He settled at his desk in the back room, uncapped his pen, and began. Debts in black. Payments in red. One entry for a widow who had purchased her husband's last words and was paying them off at a copper a month. Another for a merchant who'd bought twenty years of good luck and was discovering, now, the interest.

He was halfway through Ashcombe's falsified rainfall figures when the light in the room changed.

Not dimmed. Changed—as if the afternoon had been replaced by an older afternoon, one from before the city, before the kingdom, before anyone had thought to name the mountain that loomed over everything. The lamp flames bent sideways. The humming thing in its cage went silent.

Then someone knocked on the front door of the Gilded Thorn, and every ledger in the building opened itself to the same page.

Mirabel felt it happen. He felt it in his teeth, in the old poison-scars of his gut, in the grey book's presence behind the brick of his rooms four streets away—a tug, like a hook set in him. He walked out to the entry hall with the rest of them, Odile already there, Ferro the enforcer looming by the stairs, and together they looked at the child standing in the doorway.

A girl, seven or eight, barefoot, dressed in clothes that had been mended too many times to have a country anymore. Dust on her. A long way walked in her. She looked at the assembled power of the Gilded Thorn—Odile with her eight rings, Ferro with his iron-tipped staff—and she was not afraid, which was the first impossible thing about her.

She spoke.

No one understood her. That much was clear from their faces. The language was nothing any of them knew—not the trade-tongue, not high court speech, not the gutter cant of the river districts. It sounded older than speech, if speech could be imagined young again: vowels like water over stone, consonants like roots cracking pavement. She said perhaps thirty words, patient, as though she had walked a very long way to say exactly these.

"What's she saying?" Odile demanded. "Anyone. Ferro?"

Ferro shook his head. The clerk who handled foreign contracts shook his head. The girl waited, watching them with eyes the color of the space between stars, and tried again, slower, and this time—

This time Mirabel understood her.

Not translated. Understood, the way you understand your own name. *I am owed,* she was saying. *I am called. Open the door that was opened for me.*

His hands went cold around his pen. Because he knew the script. He had seen it once, four years ago, in an entry written in a hand so fine it seemed drawn rather than written—an entry in the Thorn's oldest ledger, the one chained shut in the vault—and Odile had watched him read it and then ordered it burned, page and binding both, and stood over the fire until the ash was cold, and told him that if the words existed anywhere but in his memory, she would see him rendered down to his components and sold by the dram.

*I am owed. I am called.* The same phrasing. The same grammar that predated kingdoms. An entry he had been ordered to burn, standing in the doorway in dust and mended clothes, seven years old.

"Mirabel," Odile said, and her voice had gone quiet in the way it did before decisions got expensive. "You've gone pale. Do you know this child?"

Every instinct he owned—nineteen years of practiced invisibility, of being furniture, of being no one—screamed at him to shake his head. To shrug. To be nothing.

But the girl had turned. She was looking directly at him now, past Odile, past Ferro, straight through all his careful nothing, and she said one more sentence in that root-and-water language, and this time she smiled.

*There you are,* she said. *I found you.*

"No," Mirabel heard himself say. His voice sounded strange in his own ears, loud, visible. "I've never seen her before in my life."

The lie sat in the room like a dropped glass. Odile looked at him for a long moment, and then at the girl, and Mirabel saw the arithmetic begin behind her eyes—the same arithmetic he performed daily in her ledgers, debts and payments and interest accruing.

"Close the door," Odile said to Ferro. "And fetch me the vault key. It seems we have a delivery."
