## 2. What Was Sold Beneath

The child slept in the guild's counting room, curled on a bale of ledger paper like a cat that had decided the danger was worth the warmth. Mirabel watched her from the doorway with a cup of cold chicory going bitter in his hand, and he did not sleep at all.

He had tried. He'd gone home to his rooms above the tannery, locked the door, set his usual precautions—a hair across the latch, flour on the sill—and lain down in the dark. But every time he closed his eyes he heard the language again, that dry rustle of consonants like wind through a dead field, and behind it, fainter, the memory of ink: the entry he had burned seven years ago because the guildmaster told him to and because burning things was, in the end, the only job he was any good at.

By the third bell he was back at the guild with a lamp and a lockpick, letting himself into the archive room where the ledgers of impossible things were kept under triple lock and a superstition no one had ever explained.

The Gilded Thorn kept its books in triplicate. One copy for the client, one for the vault, one for the fire—because some debts were meant to be forgotten by everyone except the mountain. Mirabel had spent eleven years balancing those books, and in all that time he had learned one rule above the others: *the third copy is never truly destroyed.* Paper remembers. Ash remembers. And a man who has trained his tongue to detect one part poison in ten thousand parts honey learns to taste what is missing from a thing.

He found the scar of it in the vault copy—the second ledger, the one that wasn't supposed to exist. A page razored out close to the spine, but razors leave ghosts. He tilted the page against the lamp and read the impression left in the paper beneath, letter by letter, the way he'd been taught to read a seal's imprint in wax.

*Fortieth year of the Long Register. Item: one soul, unborn, female line. Seller: Maren Voss, miller's daughter, of the Lowfield houses. Buyer: the Court Beneath, office of the Registrar of Debts Outstanding.*

*Consideration received: one harvest, abundant beyond natural measure, sufficient to stay famine in the city of Vail for the space of three winters.*

*Terms: the debt matures upon the soul's first breath drawn free of the mother's body—or upon the soul's first word spoken in the Old Tongue, whichever comes later.*

*Whichever comes later.*

Mirabel sat down slowly on a crate of confiscated love letters and put his head in his hands.

Forty years ago a woman had been starving along with everyone else, and she had gone to the mountain and sold the only thing she had that the mountain wanted: her daughter, before her daughter existed. The harvest came. The city lived. Maren Voss presumably died believing herself a savior, or a monster, or both, and the debt went into the deep files to wait.

It had waited forty years. It had waited until a mute child walked out of nowhere to the door of the very guild that held her contract, opened her mouth, and spoke the Old Tongue for the first time in her life—which meant the debt had come due last night, at closing, in front of six witnesses and a man who tasted poison for a living.

She hadn't come looking for sanctuary. She'd come because the words finally worked, and the moment they did, everything that owned her could hear her.

Mirabel looked up at the ceiling, toward the counting room, toward the small sleeping weight of her, and felt something crack quietly in his chest that he suspected would not mend.

* * *

In her sleep, the child dreamed in pieces.

*A kitchen with a black stove. A woman humming off-key, flour to her elbows. The woman says a word the child cannot yet say, and the word tastes like iron and like honey, and the woman presses a thumbprint of dough into the child's palm and says, "When you're ready. Not before. When you're ready."*

*A stairwell going down farther than stairs should. Cold that isn't temperature. A voice like a ledger being balanced: "Item received. Item received. Item—"*

*Maren Voss, old now, dying, holding the child's face in papery hands: "They'll come for you when you speak. So don't. Don't speak, little wren. Stay small. Stay silent. I bought you forty years with my whole life; spend them slow."*

*And then the kitchen again, empty, the stove cold, the word sitting in the child's mouth like a stone she has carried so long she has forgotten it is not part of her body—until the night at the guild door, when the door opened and the word fell out of her, and somewhere far beneath the city, something enormous turned over in its sleep and said: "There you are."*

* * *

The collectors arrived at noon.

Mirabel knew them before he saw them, the way he knew arsenic in almond cream—not by sight but by the wrongness underneath. Three of them came up Copperlane on foot through the market crush, and the crowd parted around them without knowing why, the way water parts around a dropped stone. They wore gray. Not livery gray, not mourning gray, just gray, the color of a page after the writing has faded off it. Their faces were pleasant and unfinished, like portraits where the painter lost interest at the eyes.

They did not knock at the guild door. They stood outside it, and the door opened itself, which Mirabel took as a very bad sign about who else held keys to the Thorn.

The guildmaster, Ossian Vael, met them in the front hall with his rings on and his smile loaded. Mirabel stood at his shoulder in his usual place—invisible, useful, holding nothing—and listened.

"The account of Maren Voss," said the middle collector, in a voice like paper tearing slowly. "Matures today. Delivery is owed."

"Of course," Vael said smoothly. "The Thorn honors its filings. Though there are procedures—"

"There are no procedures. There is the item, and there is the debt."

"The item," Vael repeated, tasting the phrase, "is a child. Under guild charter, consignments of persons require—"

"The charter," said the collector, "is a document. Documents live above ground." Its unfinished eyes moved, unhurried, around the hall, cataloguing exits, staff, the weight of the strongbox, and then—they stopped on Mirabel.

Just for a moment. Just long enough.

Mirabel had spent his whole life being overlooked; it was his craft, honed finer than his palate. But something in him must have flinched, because the collector's gaze snagged on him like a hook on silk, and its mouth made a small adjustment that was not quite a smile.

"You keep your books well," it said to Vael, still looking at Mirabel. "Someone here reads deeply."

"They do their work," Vael said, and there was an edge in it now, because a guildmaster does not enjoy being reminded that his best asset can be seen.

"You have until moonrise," the collector said. "Delivery at the low door of the mine-shaft called Widow's Throat. Bring the item. Bring no names." It turned. At the threshold it paused. "Or bring them. Names travel well down here."

The door shut itself behind them. The hall exhaled.

Vael stood very still for the length of ten heartbeats. Then he turned and looked at Mirabel directly—something that happened perhaps twice a year—and said, in the mild voice he used for arithmetic:

"You've read the Voss file."

It was not a question. Mirabel kept his face at taster's neutrality. "I balance the books, Guildmaster. I know what's in them."

"Then you know we don't refuse the mountain. The mountain holds paper on half the noble houses in Vail. We hand over the girl, the harvest stays harvested, the Thorn keeps its standing, and nobody above ground ever knows there was a girl at all." Vael's voice stayed gentle. That was the worst of him; he was gentle the way a knife is smooth. "You're the one who found her. You'll be the one to bring her. Moonrise. Widow's Throat."

"And if I don't?"

Vael smiled with real warmth, which was how Mirabel knew he was already afraid. "Then the mountain collects from us instead, and it will start with whoever it can see. And Mirabel"—the name landed like a coin on a counter—"you are the most visible man in this building, to the right eyes. Don't make yourself more so."

* * *

He went up to the counting room. She was awake, sitting cross-legged on the bale of paper, watching the door with the patience of someone who had spent a lifetime waiting for exactly this.

"I'm supposed to bring you to a hole in the ground at moonrise," Mirabel said. He crouched to her level, forearms on his knees. "There are three of them and they don't have proper faces, and if I don't, they'll take the whole guild apart and probably the palace after, and honestly the palace might deserve it."

She blinked at him. Then, deliberately, she raised one small hand and pointed at him, then at her own chest, then made a shape in the air with both hands—a scale, weighing two things.

"You want to know what you weigh against me." He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "That's the problem, little wren. I've spent thirty years making sure I weigh nothing. No name anyone uses, no face anyone keeps, doses of poison twice a day so even my own body belongs to somebody else's safety. If I vanish tomorrow, the queen's breakfast gets a new taster by supper and the guild's books get a new hand by morning, and the world doesn't lose a single line."

Her eyes were very dark and very steady. She reached out and took his wrist—his pulse jumping under her fingers like a caught bird—and shook her head once, hard.

"No?" he said softly. "You don't believe me?"

She picked up a scrap of ledger paper and the stub of pencil Mirabel always carried, and she wrote—not in the Old Tongue, but in trade-script, badly, the letters wobbling like a child's:

*YOU ARE HERE.*

"That's ink," Mirabel said. His voice came out rough. "Ink fades. Ink gets burned. Believe me, I've burned enough of it."

She wrote again, pressing harder this time, nearly tearing the page:

*I REMEMBER YOU. THAT'S ENOUGH.*

And Mirabel Nystrom, who had survived forty poisons and eleven years of other people's secrets, discovered that the one thing he had no antidote for was being believed in by a child.

He stood. He crossed to the vault, took out the second ledger, and turned to the Voss entry—the true one, the living one, the line that said *item: one soul, delivery pending*. He dipped his pen. And with the steadiest hand he had ever used for forgery, he wrote beneath it, in the cramped official script of the Registrar's office, a line that did not exist:

*DELIVERY DEFERRED — ITEM RELOCATED TO VAULT OF THE LONG REGISTER — CLAIM ONLY BY BEARER OF THE ORIGINAL SEAL.*

A claim no one alive held. A deferment with no expiry. A lie written in the language of the mountain itself, which he had learned, over eleven years, the way he learned everything: quietly, completely, and for reasons he hadn't understood until tonight.

He blotted the page. He hid the ledger. Then he took Wren's hand—it fit inside his like a secret—and blew out the lamps.

"Moonrise is in four hours," he whispered. "We're not going to Widow's Throat. We're going under it. There are ways down that aren't on any map, and I've read all of them."

Somewhere far below the city, patient as interest accruing, the mountain began to count.
