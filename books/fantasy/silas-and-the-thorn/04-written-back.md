## 4. Written Back

The light was wrong when we came out of the mountain.

I had spent so long in the dark that the sun felt like a hand pressed flat against my face, and I flinched from it, and Mirabel—Mirabel caught me. His arm around my shoulders, steadying me on the scree above the old mine-mouth. His sleeve smelled of ink and copper and the deep cold stone smell of the court below.

"It's morning," he said, like it mattered. Like anything up here could matter after what he'd done.

He had no name anymore. I knew it the way you know a burn is a burn: the registrar's pen had gone through him and come out the other side, and where his name used to be there was now a smooth blank place, like a page scraped clean. The mountain had taken it. He had given it. *Take mine instead,* he'd said, in that voice he used for ledgers, level as a ruled line, and the registrar—who had seen every trade since the first stone was laid—had hesitated. Had almost smiled.

*No contract anticipated this,* the registrar said. But contracts are what they are. Ink is ink.

We climbed down through the spoil heaps to the road, and nobody looked at him. That was the first sign. A carter passed us with a load of turnips and his eyes slid over Mirabel like water over glass, catching only on me—the mute girl, the debt come due, the child who wasn't owed anymore. The carter tipped his hat to me. To me. And walked straight through the space where Mirabel stood.

Mirabel didn't say anything. He just kept walking, one foot then the other, the way he did everything: precisely, quietly, as if being erased were a task to be completed with good posture.

The city gates took my breath twice—once because I'd never seen them from outside, once because the guards waved me through without a word. No papers asked. No tolls. Whatever the mountain had written into the world when it released me, it had written *free* beside my name in letters everyone could read but me.

And beside his name, nothing at all.

* * *

The Gilded Thorn looked smaller from the street than it ever had in my head. Maybe because I'd imagined it as a palace, all those nights in the dark, the place where the kind taster worked late with his lamps and his columns of figures. It was just a building. Green door. Brass knocker worn smooth by a hundred years of hands.

Mirabel stopped across the street and looked at it for a long time.

"I should go in," he said. "They'll need—" He stopped. Started again. "There are accounts. Quarter-end is in nine days. Someone has to—"

"Come on," I said.

It was the second thing I'd ever said to him out loud. My voice still came out strange, rusted from disuse, each word costing something. He turned and looked at me like I'd struck a bell inside him.

"You talk," he said.

"When it matters," I said.

Inside, the guild was loud with ordinary life. Clerks arguing about freight rates. Someone frying onions in the back. A woman counting coins into stacks with the speed of long habit. Nobody looked up. Nobody looked at *him*. A clerk walked directly through his shoulder in the doorway and apologized—to me, for crowding me—and Mirabel stood very still while it happened, the way a man stands still when he's decided not to bleed where anyone can see.

The guildmaster's office door was open. Master Corvane sat behind his desk with the great ledger open before him, and when he saw me his face did something complicated—relief and fear and guilt all fighting for the same ground.

"Wren." He knew my name. Of course he did; the guild had sheltered me those weeks while Mirabel hid me, forged entries, lied to crown agents and enforcers both. "Child. You're—gods. You're back." His eyes went past me, through me, to the empty doorway, and returned. "You came alone?"

"She didn't," I said.

Corvane's gaze flicked sideways, found nothing, came back. "Wren—"

"Check your book," I said.

He frowned, but he was a guildmaster; books were his scripture. He pulled the Gilded Thorn's master ledger toward him and turned pages back, back, back, to the year Mirabel Nystrom first set foot in this building as a boy of fourteen with quick hands and a talent for numbers no one wanted to examine too closely.

The pages were blank.

Not torn out. Not burned. Blank—parchment smooth and pale as an egg, every line he'd ever entered simply *not there*, forty years of columns dissolved like sugar in rain. Corvane turned the pages faster. Somewhere in the middle they became normal again, dense with other clerks' hands, but the gap yawned where Mirabel's work had been, a wound of white paper.

"That's not possible," Corvane said. "He balanced the Vellamy account. He—he was here last week, he—"

He stopped. His brow creased. I watched it happen, watched the memory try to surface and slide away like a fish under ice. "He was—" Corvane pressed two fingers to his temple. "There was someone. There was a taster who—no. The palace taster is a man called Doss. Has been for years."

"Doss can't taste nightshade," I said. "Doss will be dead within the month if the queen's enemies have their way, and the queen with him. Because the man who kept her alive for eleven years is standing in your doorway and none of you can see him."

Silence. The onion-frying smell drifted in from the back. Corvane stared at the blank pages, and I watched him want to believe me and be unable to find the shape of it.

Behind me, Mirabel spoke. "It's all right, Master Corvane."

Nobody heard him. Nobody but me. That was the bargain's fine print, the part even the registrar hadn't bothered to explain: a traded name doesn't just vanish from records. It vanishes from minds. From mouths. From memory itself. He had paid with the only coin that made him real to anyone, and now he was spending eternity as a rumor with no teller.

Except me.

Because my soul had been in his hands when he traded. Because the registrar's pen had passed through both of us and bound us together in the ledger's deep grammar, the way two entries on the same line belong to each other forever. When the world forgot him, something in me held the shape of him like wax holds a seal.

I remembered everything. His handwriting, slanted right, heavy on the downstrokes. The way he counted under his breath when he was nervous. The exact words of the entry he'd been ordered to burn, which he hadn't burned, which he'd copied into his private book in case a day came when it mattered. The way he'd looked at me the first night, at the guild door, and understood my language before I understood I still had one.

"Corvane," I said. "Give me a pen."

* * *

The back room was where he'd taught me letters. Three months hidden in the guild, waiting out the collectors, and every evening he'd sat with me at this scarred table and shown me how the alphabet worked, how a word is a small machine for carrying meaning across distance and time. *Ink is a contract,* he told me once. *Every sentence commits its writer to something. Be careful what you promise.*

I took the guild's master pen. Good goose quill, cut fresh. I dipped it in the iron-gall ink he'd mixed himself, the batch that browned slightly with age, and I opened the master ledger to the blank wound where his life used to be.

"What are you doing?" Corvane said.

"No contract anticipated this," I said, and wrote:

*Mirabel Nystrom.*

My hand shook on the first letter and steadied on the rest. Below the name I wrote what I knew of him, line after line, in a child's careful script: *food-taster to the queen, eleven years. Keeper of accounts, Gilded Thorn. Forty poisons survived. One soul saved.* I wrote the date. I wrote *entered by Wren, who remembers,* because a ledger entry needs a witness and I was the only one left.

The ink sank into the parchment and the parchment seemed to breathe.

It started small. Corvane blinked, and blinked again, and his eyes went to the doorway and this time they *caught*—snagged on a thin man with ink-stained fingers standing half in shadow, and Corvane's face went through shock the way a lock goes through tumblers.

"Nystrom?" he said. "Gods' teeth, Nystrom, where have you—we thought—" He stood up so fast his chair went over. "The books. Your pages, they were—"

"Gone," Mirabel said. His voice cracked on it, just barely, the first imprecision I'd ever heard from him. "Yes. I know. I paid them."

Corvane crossed the room in four strides and gripped his shoulder like a man confirming solid ground. Around us the guild stirred and reassembled—clerk by clerk, glance by glance, the world stitching him back into itself along the lines I'd drawn. The cook dropped a spoon. The woman counting coins looked up and said, "There you are, we've been—didn't you balance the Vellamy—"

"I did," Mirabel said.

And then he turned and looked at me, and everyone else in the room went quiet and far away, because he was looking at me the way a drowning man looks at shore.

"You wrote me back," he said.

"You wrote yourself out," I said. "For me. So." I shrugged, because I didn't have better words yet; I'd only had a voice for two days. "Now we're even."

"We are not remotely even," he said, and laughed—a short, broken, astonished sound—and then he knelt down on the floor of the Gilded Thorn's back room, in front of everyone, in front of the ledger, and bowed his head like a man before an altar. "Wren. Daughter of Ashka, whose soul was sold beneath the mountain. I am in your debt. Formally. On the record. Forever."

"Ink is a contract," I said.

His head came up. He was smiling. It transformed his whole careful face.

"Ink is a contract," he agreed.

* * *

Later—much later, after Corvane had shouted himself hoarse and embraced him twice and demanded the full story and gotten perhaps a third of it—the palace messenger arrived at the green door, breathless, with news that spread through the guild like fire through dry grass.

Queen Sarelle was dead.

Not poisoned—or not only poisoned. Found at dawn in her chambers with a black-edged summons lying on her breast, sealed in wax the color of deep stone, addressed in a script older than any kingdom. A script the palace scholars couldn't read.

A script I could.

Mirabel read the message over the messenger's shoulder, and I watched the color leave his face, and he reached for my hand without looking, the way you reach for the one fixed point in a turning room.

"They're not finished," he said quietly. "Debts don't end, Wren. They only change hands."

I looked at the messenger's paper, at the elegant descending characters I'd spoken before I could speak anything else, and felt the mountain's attention settle over the city like weather.

"My mother signed more than one entry," I said.

Mirabel closed his eyes. "Then we'd better open the ledger," he said, "and find out what else she sold."
