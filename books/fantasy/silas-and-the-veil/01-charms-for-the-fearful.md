## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The night market on Bellows Street smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool, of fried onions gone cold in their pans, of the particular sourness that clung to a city afraid. Rafael Okonkwo had learned to read Corvane the way other people read weather, and tonight the air said *storm coming*, though the sky above the rooftops was clear and salted with stars.

She arranged her wares with the care of a woman laying a table for guests she did not trust. Luck-knots braided from river grass. Iron nails wrapped in red thread. Little cloth poppets stuffed with lavender and dried nettle, stitched shut with knots that meant nothing at all—or nothing that anyone could prove. She set each one down gently, as if it might bruise.

"Charm against cramp," she murmured to herself, positioning a knot of knotted cord beside a knot of knotted cord. "Blessing for safe passage over water. Ward for the milk going sour." Her voice was low, half a prayer and half an inventory. Three years in this city, and this was what her gift had been whittled down to: small comforts, hedge-tricks, the kind of magic so old and so humble that even the crown's clerks had never bothered to name it illegal. *Superstition*, they called it. *Folk custom.* They taxed it like fishmongering and thought no more about it.

Rafael knew better. She knew exactly how much power slept in her hands, coiled beneath her skin like a second pulse, and she spent every waking hour pretending it wasn't there.

The trick of hiding, Serah had taught her—back when Rafael still called the old woman *mistress* instead of *the reason I ran*—was not to smother the gift but to starve it. Never work anything large. Never work anything that sang. A luck-knot made honestly, with a whisper of intent folded into the braid, would carry a man through a bad week. The same hands, given leave, could have mended bone, quieted fever, turned aside a knife. Could have done things Serah's grimoire described in diagrams that still visited Rafael's dreams uninvited.

So: luck-knots. Lavender poppets. A woman with ink-stained fingers and a stall that smelled of herbs, selling comfort to the frightened at two coppers apiece.

"You've got the good ones out tonight."

Rafael looked up. Doss Harker stood at the edge of her blanket, cap in his hands, turning it the way he always turned it when he wanted something but hated asking. He was a dockworker built like a mooring post, forty years of hauling rope written into his shoulders, and he'd been buying charms from her since her first winter in Corvane, back when she'd still flinched at every crown livery she saw.

"Doss." She smiled, and let it reach her eyes, because Doss was one of perhaps four people in the city whose smiles she trusted enough to answer honestly. "What is it tonight? Your knee again?"

"My knee can go hang." He crouched, joints cracking like green wood, and studied the luck-knots with the gravity of a man inspecting cargo. "It's my girl. Marta. She's carrying, Rafa. Four months now, and the midwife says the baby sits wrong."

Rafael's hands went still on the blanket.

Here it was, then—the old hunger, rising in her like water into a hold. She could feel the shape of what Marta needed, could almost see it: warmth drawn along the spine, the body persuaded rather than forced, a working soft as breath. She could do it in ten minutes. It would take almost nothing from her, a cupful from an ocean.

And if anyone ever learned she could do it, everything she had built—this stall, this careful gray little life, this invisibility—would burn.

"A charm, then," she said, and heard how steady she kept her voice, and hated herself a little for the relief in it. "For the mother, not the baby. You tie it at the headboard, left side, and you don't untie it until the child comes. It won't fix what's wrong, Doss. But it'll help her rest, and resting helps more than most midwives will admit."

He paid her four coppers without haggling, which told her how scared he was, and closed his fist around the knot like it was something alive. "You're a good woman, Rafa Okonkwo."

"I'm a woman who sells string," she said lightly. "Go home to your Marta."

He went, shoulders easing already, believing—and this was the part that sat in Rafael's chest like a swallowed stone—that he'd bought something honest and small and harmless. He had. That was the whole point. Everything she sold was honest and small and harmless, and everything she *could* sell stayed locked behind her ribs where not even she liked to look at it.

* * *

The market thickened around her as the evening deepened. Bellows Street ran from the fish-landing down to the tanners' quarter, and after dark it filled with everyone the daylight city had no use for: off-shift dockworkers spending wages too fast, washerwomen trading gossip, pickpockets working the crowds with the lazy confidence of men who owned the dark by lease. Lanterns swung on wires overhead. Somewhere a fiddle played a tune that couldn't decide whether it was a reel or a lament.

Rafael sold a ward-for-sour-milk to a dairyman's wife. Sold a traveler's blessing to a carter heading south, though the roads south were no worse than usual, which was to say merely dangerous. Sold three cramp-charms to a laundress whose hands she noticed, professionally, were swollen at the knuckles—she could have fixed those too, in minutes, and instead she sold the woman a knot and instructions to soak them in salt water, and the laundress thanked her warmly, and Rafael smiled and felt the familiar double weight of it: guilt on one side, survival on the other, balanced on a scale so old she'd stopped noticing it hurt.

That was the bargain she'd struck with herself three years ago, running south from Serah's cottage with a stolen cloak and a belly full of terror: *small magic only. Be a hedge-witch, be a fraud, be nothing.* Because Rafael Okonkwo had seen what happened to witches who were *something*. She'd grown up watching Serah weigh every kindness against its risk, watching the old woman turn away desperate mothers and bleeding men because the working needed was too bright, too visible, too much like the thing the old stories warned about. *They burned my sister in Vellmark*, Serah used to say, not looking up from her mortar. *Not because she cursed anyone. Because she cured someone, and the curing got talked about.*

So Rafael had learned the lesson twice over—once at Serah's knee, once in the hard school of her own flight—and she wore her smallness like armor.

Which was why, when the first crier's bell rang out at the mouth of Bellows Street, her body understood before her mind did.

* * *

The sound cut through the market noise like a blade through rope. Not the tax-collector's bell, not the watch's patrol rattle—a proclamation bell, bronze and official, the kind that rang for coronations and wars and things that changed the shape of ordinary lives.

The crowd didn't part so much as congeal, heads turning all at once, the fiddle faltering mid-phrase. Down the street, torchlight moved in formation—not the loose stagger of a patrol but the measured tread of men who had rehearsed this. And ahead of them, mounted on a cart dragged into position beneath the great crooked sign of the Bell and Billet, stood a crier in crown colors, unrolling a scroll with the crisp ceremony of a man who enjoyed his work.

"Hear ye! Hear ye! By decree of His Majesty King Aldric, Protector of the Realm—" The crier's voice was trained, carrying effortlessly over the crowd, warm as a physician announcing an amputation. "—let it be known that a registry shall be opened in every quarter of the city of Corvane. All persons possessing the gift of sorcery, hedge-craft, cunning-work, or any art beyond natural means, being of age, shall present themselves before the registry clerk within thirty days, to be counted, examined, and entered upon the rolls of the realm."

The words landed in the crowd like stones into still water. Rafael watched the ripples move outward: the dairyman's wife glancing sideways at her neighbor; the carter's hand drifting unconsciously toward the blessing-charm at his throat; a boy near the front—no older than Tam would have been, if Rafael had known Tam then—going very pale and very still.

*Counted. Examined. Entered upon the rolls.*

There was no such thing, in the history of kingdoms, as a registry that stopped at counting.

"All persons so registered," the crier continued, "shall receive the king's protection and license to practice within lawful bounds. Those who conceal the gift, being discovered after the term of grace, shall be subject to—" he consulted the scroll, though Rafael would have bet her stall he knew it by heart— "the penalties provided under statute."

*Penalties provided under statute.* The old statutes. The ones nobody had invoked in living memory because invoking them required proof, and proof required hunting, and hunting required a crown willing to spend blood on superstition.

The crier rolled up his scroll. And then, as if remembering an afterthought that had been sewn into the hem of the decree all along, he lifted his voice again:

"Let it further be known: following the thirty days' grace, the king's officers shall make inquiry throughout the realm. Let none shelter the unregistered. Let none conceal the gifted. The hunt begins at month's end, by the king's own word."

*The hunt.*

The word went through the market like a current through a shoal of fish. Conversations died mid-sentence. Somewhere a pot lid clattered. The fiddler packed his instrument and was gone before the echo faded, and no one remarked on it, because everyone was doing the same arithmetic Rafael was doing: *who do I know? Who knows me? How long has someone been watching my stall?*

Rafael stood behind her blanket of harmless charms and felt three years of careful invisibility tear down the middle like wet paper.

They had never come for hedge-witches before. That had been the whole architecture of her safety—built not on secrecy alone but on insignificance, on the crown's long indifference to women selling string. Kings hunted enemies. Kings did not waste riders on luck-knots.

But this decree didn't distinguish. *Any art beyond natural means.* The phrase was wide enough to drive a wagon through, and Rafael could see the wagon coming, torchlit, all the way down Bellows Street.

Her hands, she realized distantly, had gone cold. Beneath her sternum, the thing she never touched, never named, the ocean she kept dammed up behind a wall of smallness—it stirred. Not waking, exactly. Turning over in its sleep. As if it had heard the word *hunt* and recognized, with some ancient instinct, that it was the kind of thing hunts were for.

*Be calm,* she told herself, the way Serah had taught her, breathing slow, grounding through her feet. *You are a woman who sells string. You are nobody. You have been nobody for three years and nobody survives.*

But her eyes were moving over the crowd now, cataloguing without meaning to: the dairyman's wife who bought wards and asked too many questions about where Rafael learned her trade; the carter who'd once offered triple price for "something stronger, if you take my meaning"; the boy gone pale at the front, whose stillness had the particular quality of a person listening to something no one else could hear.

Thirty days. Then inquiry. Then the hunt.

She began packing her wares with hands that only slightly trembled, folding the blanket over luck-knots and lavender poppets, over two coppers' worth of harmless comfort that had just become evidence.

* * *

She was three streets away, taking the long route home through the tanners' reek because it was empty and empty was safe, when she heard the second bell.

This one came from the direction of the fish-landing, and it wasn't a proclamation bell. It was the watch-bell, rung hard, rung urgent, and beneath it rose voices—not the ordinary quarrelsome noise of the waterfront but a sound Rafael knew from stories her mistress had told by firelight, stories that always ended the same way. A crowd finding a target. A crowd becoming a weapon.

She should have kept walking. Every instinct she'd honed for three years screamed *keep walking, be nobody, go home, bar your door.* Instead she climbed the steps to the ropewalk bridge—because apparently three years of caution could be undone by one bad night—and looked down at the landing.

Torchlight again, but different now. Looser. Hungrier. A knot of maybe thirty people had gathered around the customs house, and at its center, held between two men, was a figure Rafael recognized with a sick drop of her stomach: Old Nan Perch, the shrimp-seller, seventy years of weather in her face, who read tea leaves for dock girls for a penny and told fortunes so vague they couldn't frighten a gull.

"—marked her stall myself," one of the holding-men was saying, breathless with the joy of righteous work. "Cursed goods, she had. Poppets. Bones. My missus bought a love-charm off her last spring and look what came of it!"

"She's a fraud, is all she is," someone else protested, halfhearted.

"She's *registered*-adjacent," the first man snapped, mangling the new vocabulary with relish. "The criers said. Any art beyond natural means. Thirty days, and she's been at it twenty years. What's she waiting for, eh? What's she *hiding?*"

Nan Perch was saying something—pleading, by the pitch of it, though the words were lost under the crowd's roar. Rafael's hands gripped the bridge rail. She could see the old woman's stall already overturned, shrimp and shells ground into the mud, and she could see what was coming next with terrible clarity, because Serah had shown her drawings of it, woodcuts from Vellmark, images she'd made Rafael study with the grim instruction *know what they do, so you know why we hide.*

The first stone flew. Then the torches moved in.

Rafael turned away. There was nothing she could do—less than nothing; intervention would be suicide, would light a beacon over her own head that every Grey Cloak in Corvane would see—and knowing it didn't help at all. She walked fast, then faster, cutting down Tanner's Lane with the screams fading behind her, and the whole way home the same thought beat in her skull like a second heart:

*Nan Perch read tea leaves. Tea leaves. If they take her for tea leaves, what will they take me for?*

* * *

Her room above the chandler's shop on Corder Street was one window, one bed, one stove, and forty hidden caches of everything that mattered. She lit no lamp. She stood in the dark with her back against the door, breathing, listening to the city sounds—the watch-bell still ringing somewhere, a dog, the ordinary machinery of a city trying to convince itself the night was normal.

Then she crossed to the loose brick behind the stove and drew out the bundle she had sworn, three years ago, she would never touch unless touching it was safer than leaving it.

Serah's pages. Not the grimoire itself—she'd left that behind, fled from it, and some nights she still woke with the memory of its diagrams crawling behind her eyes—but six leaves she'd torn out in the moment of her running, reasons she'd never fully admitted even to herself. Binding-script. Counter-signs. Notes in her own younger handwriting, frantic, half-legible: *the stone beneath the throne, if the stories are true, if she actually—*

She hadn't looked at them in three years. She'd built a whole life on top of the place where she'd buried them.

Now she sat on the narrow bed with the pages in her lap and the decree ringing in her ears—*counted, examined, entered upon the rolls; the hunt begins at month's end*—and understood, with the flat certainty of a woman watching a tide come in, that her carefully invisible life was over. Not threatened. Over. Thirty days was not grace; it was a countdown, and registries had only ever been lists for burning.

Somewhere across the city, Nan Perch was screaming or silent, and Rafael had walked away from her, and tomorrow the whole of Bellows Street would know that the crown meant it, and the day after, someone would remember the witch-woman with the ink-stained fingers who sold luck-knots and never, ever worked anything strong.

She looked down at her hands in the dark. Ordinary hands. Small-magic hands.

Beneath her skin, patient as the sea, the gift waited—and for the first time in three years, Rafael wondered whether hiding it had ever truly been a choice, or just the slower way of being found.

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a single bell tolled the midnight hour. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came another sound: boots. Many boots. Moving through Corder Street in step.

Stopping below her window.
