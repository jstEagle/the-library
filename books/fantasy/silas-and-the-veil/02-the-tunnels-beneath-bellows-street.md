## 2. The Tunnels Beneath Bellows Street

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Rafael knew the rhythm of Bellows Street by heart—the milk cart before dawn, the cobbler's hammer at midmorning, the drunk who sang off-key outside the Drowned Rat every night but Soulday. So when fists hammered her door in the grey hour between midnight and morning, when even the singing drunk was asleep, she was awake and moving before her mind finished the thought.

*Registry sweep. Third floor up.*

She had heard them coming for two streets, actually—had felt it as a wrongness in the walls, a tremor like a struck tuning fork that only she could hear. The tenement's bricks had been murmuring to each other all night, nervous as sparrows, and Rafael had lain in her narrow bed telling herself it was nothing, that she was imagining it, that three years of careful invisibility had made her paranoid.

Then the fists on the door, and the voice: "Open in the name of the Crown Registry of Gifts."

They were on the stairs already. She could hear boots, many boots, and beneath them something worse—a low, grinding hum she'd never felt before, like millstones turning. Whatever they carried with them, it was hunting magic the way a hound hunts scent.

Rafael did not open the door. She went out the window.

Three years of living above a chandler's shop had taught her the building's bones: the drainpipe that would hold if you didn't trust it with your whole weight, the awning over the dyer's yard two floors down, the gap between the dyer's fence and the tannery wall where no lamp reached. She dropped through the dark with the economy of long practice, barefoot, her coat thrown over her nightdress, her satchel—the one thing she always kept packed—clutched against her chest.

Inside the satchel: forty silver bits, a change of clothes, a knife, and a bundle of half-finished charms that could hang her if anyone looked too closely. She should have left the charms. She knew that. But her hands had grabbed them without asking her permission, the way a mother grabs a child from a burning house.

Behind her, above her, the door of her room splintered open. A man's voice, flat and bored: "Empty. Bed's still warm."

"Search the street."

Rafael pressed herself into the tannery's shadow and did not breathe. The hum grew louder. Through the fence slats she saw them pass—six men in grey cloaks, and between the fourth and fifth, a thing on a wheeled frame that looked like a brazier turned sideways, its coals burning a color coals should not burn. Grey fire. It cast no warmth. When it swung toward her end of the alley, every hair on her arms rose, and something deep in her chest—the well, the spring, the place where her gift lived—flinched like a snail touched by salt.

She clamped down on it. Hard. Wrapped it in silence the way Serah had taught her, twenty years ago, in a marsh cottage that seemed now to belong to another life. *Bury it deep, little bird. Bury it so deep even you forget where you put the shovel.*

The grey fire passed over her. Paused. Passed on.

Rafael waited until the boots faded, then waited longer, until the sky went from black to the bruised purple of almost-morning. Then she ran.

* * *

She did not go back to Bellows Street. That was the first rule of hiding: never return to a place the hunters have touched. But rules, she discovered as she crouched in the reeds of the Corvane riverbank watching the sun rise over the smelter smoke, left a person nowhere to go. Her charm-stall was gone. Her room was gone. Every neighbor who might have lied for her had seen the Grey Cloaks carry her mattress out into the street and burn it in the gutter—she'd watched that too, from a rooftop, before the sun came fully up. Burning a witch's bedding. Sending a message to the whole street.

*We know one lived here. We will find the others.*

So Rafael sat in the reeds with mud soaking through her trousers and did the arithmetic of desperation. Forty silver bits. No references, no papers stamped with the registry's seal—and the criers had been clear yesterday, their voices cracking across the market square like whips: *All persons possessing unregistered gifts must present themselves within seven days. After that day, concealment is treason.* Seven days. She'd had three years of careful, small, hidden life, and the crown had dismantled it in an afternoon.

There were places to run, of course. South to the marshlands, back toward Serah—but Serah was the last person on this earth Rafael wanted to see, and besides, the roads out of Corvane were already choked with checkpoints. She'd seen them from the rooftops. Men in grey, checking hands, checking eyes, running that grey-fire contraption along queues of frightened people like a shepherd counting sheep.

Or there was the other option. The one whispered about in the night market, always in a lowered voice, always with a glance over the shoulder. *If the hunt comes for you, look for the mark on Bellows Street. Under the third arch of the old aqueduct. If you can see the mark, you're meant to find it.*

Rafael had always assumed the whisper was a trap. What kind of fool believed in a sanctuary? Sanctuaries were stories told to children and fools, and Rafael Okonkwo had stopped being either the day she'd run from Serah's cottage with a satchel and a burned hand and a secret she'd carried ever since.

But the aqueduct was close. Twenty minutes' walk along the river, maybe less. And the alternative was standing in a registry line with her gift shriveled behind her teeth, praying that whatever machine the Grey Cloaks used couldn't taste the difference between a hedge-witch's tricks and the real thing.

She didn't believe it could tell the difference. She knew, with the certainty of someone who had spent her whole life being underestimated, that it couldn't. But certainty was a luxury, and machines were made by men, and men made mistakes, and she had exactly one life to lose.

Rafael got up out of the reeds, wrung the river water from her hem, and walked toward the old aqueduct.

* * *

It was a ruin from the imperial age, the aqueduct—three tiers of weathered stone arches marching across the low quarter of the city like the spine of some buried giant. The lower arches had been walled up centuries ago and now served as storage, stables, and homes for the sort of people who couldn't afford to be particular. The third arch from the river end was bricked shut, its face furred with moss and pigeon droppings, indistinguishable from its neighbors except—

Except.

Rafael stood across the lane from it for a long moment, feeling foolish, feeling watched. Then she let her sight slip, just slightly, just enough—the way she'd been taught to look at the world since childhood, the way she had spent three years training herself *not* to look.

The world shivered. Colors deepened. And there, on the bricked-up face of the arch, written in chalk that no ordinary eye would ever see, blazed a sigil.

It was beautiful work. Simple—a spiral crossed by three bars, drawn with a confidence Rafael recognized because she'd been trained by someone with the same confidence—but layered, keyed, alive. As she watched, it pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat. Like it had noticed her noticing.

Her mouth went dry. Sigils that responded to attention were not hedge-craft. This was work done by someone with real strength, and it had just tasted hers, or tried to.

*Turn around,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had kept her alive for three years. *Walk away. Take your chances with the registry.*

But the registry line stretched for a mile, and the grey fire smelled like the inside of a coffin, and Rafael was tired—tired in a way that three years of caution had not cured but only deepened, tired of burying the best part of herself so far down that some mornings she woke and genuinely wondered if she'd imagined it all. The marsh. Serah's kitchen. The light under her own skin.

She crossed the lane. She laid her palm flat against the sigil.

The chalk flared white. The brick face rippled like the surface of a pond disturbed by a thrown stone—and opened.

Not a door. The bricks themselves slid apart, grinding, revealing a passage sloping down into darkness, breathing out air that smelled of candlewax, woodsmoke, and underneath it, faintly, the river. Rafael stood on the threshold for exactly as long as it took to understand that she could still turn back.

Then she stepped through, and the bricks closed behind her with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

The passage descended in a long spiral, lit at intervals by candles stuck to iron spikes driven into the mortar. Someone maintained this place. Someone swept it—there were no cobwebs, no rat droppings, though rats she could hear, scurrying somewhere below, and the stones around her murmured their usual gossip, *cold cold cold, deep deep deep*, the way stone always talked when you listened. She didn't listen closely. Listening cost control, and control was everything now.

At the bottom, the passage opened into a vaulted chamber that had clearly once been a cistern, imperial masonry rising in perfect arches overhead, dry now for centuries. It had been transformed. Cots lined the curved walls, a dozen of them, most occupied—she counted figures wrapped in blankets, sleeping or pretending to sleep. A cookfire burned in a ring of stones, vented through a shaft that must have connected to some chimney above, and the smell of barley porridge hung warm in the air. Crates were stacked against one wall, marked with smugglers' chalk codes. A rack of tools. A shelf of books—actual books, which in itself told her this was no ordinary hideout.

And standing at the center of it all, arms crossed, watching Rafael with her single eye, was a woman built like a siege engine.

She was perhaps fifty, grey-shot hair cropped short, a scar running from her jaw down into her collar. Her left eye was gone—not patched, simply absent, the lid sewn or scarred shut over the hollow. She wore a soldier's coat with the insignia cut away, and a sword hung at her hip with the casual placement of someone who had worn one so long it had become part of her skeleton.

"You're bleeding," the woman said.

Rafael looked down. Her feet, she realized, were leaving red prints on the flagstones. In the dark of the flight she'd cut them badly and hadn't noticed. Adrenaline, she supposed. It was always adrenaline.

"I'm looking for shelter," Rafael said. "I was told—"

"You were told to look for the mark." The woman's voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone conducting an inspection. "Which means whoever sent you trusts you, or wants you dead, or both. Come here where I can see you. Not the wound. You."

Rafael came. Standing close, she saw the woman's remaining eye was pale grey, sharp, and missed nothing—it traveled over Rafael's face, her satchel, the mud on her clothes, the way she held her weight off her cut feet.

"Name?"

"Rafael."

"Rafael what?"

"Just Rafael."

Something flickered at the corner of the woman's mouth. "Careful girl. Good. Odile Voss. This is my house, such as it is. Now." She jerked her chin at Rafael's feet. "Maretta! Wake up. We've got a runner with her soles hanging off."

From one of the cots rose a round, middle-aged woman with flour on her sleeves and kind, exhausted eyes. She took one look at Rafael's feet and clicked her tongue. "Sit. Sit, girl, before you fall down. How long have you been walking?"

"Since before dawn," Rafael admitted, and let herself be steered to a stool by the fire.

Maretta knelt and began cleaning the cuts with something that stung ferociously. Around them the chamber was waking—blankets shifting, faces emerging from hoods, a dozen pairs of eyes fixing on Rafael with expressions ranging from curiosity to suspicion. She counted again, properly this time. Eleven besides Voss and Maretta. All ages. A boy of perhaps twelve sitting cross-legged on a crate, staring at her with frank interest. An old man with ink-stained fingers. Two young women holding hands.

Fugitives. All of them. People like her—or near enough.

The realization landed strangely. Three years she had believed herself alone in Corvane, singular, a secret of one. And here was a whole cellar full of secrets, sleeping in blankets, eating porridge, alive.

"So," Odile said, dragging a crate over and sitting on it across from Rafael, elbows on knees. "The mark found you, or you found the mark. Either way, you saw it, which means you've got the gift. Question is how much, and what flavor. Everyone here works for their keep. Cook, clean, haul, mend—and if you've got craft worth anything, you use it carefully and only when I say. First rule of my house: nobody works magic without leave. Second rule: nobody lies about what they are. Both rules keep us breathing." The grey eye fixed on Rafael. "So. What are you?"

Here it was, then. The question, arriving on schedule.

Rafael had rehearsed versions of this answer for three years, in mirrors, in the dark, in the pauses of conversations. *Hedge-witch. Charm-seller. Small things, luck and mending, nothing the crown would bother with.* It was even true, mostly. It was true the way a coin with the middle filed out is still mostly a coin.

"Hedge-craft," she said. "Charms, mostly. Luck-knots, fever-wards, finding lost things. I sold them in the night market. Small stuff." She shrugged, and made the shrug convincing by letting her shoulders ache with it. "I'm not strong. Never was. Enough to make a living off folk who want a little hope in their pocket, that's all."

Silence. Maretta's hands paused on Rafael's ankle. The boy on the crate tilted his head, listening to something—stones, Rafael would have guessed, if she'd let herself guess such things.

Odile's eye hadn't moved from her face. "Small stuff," she repeated.

"Small stuff."

"The Cloaks swept your street with a seeker-engine. Those things taste power like a bloodhound tastes fear. You got past one."

"Luck," Rafael said, and smiled, and hated how easy the smile came. "Ironic, isn't it? A luck-charm seller with actual luck."

Odile studied her a long moment. Somewhere in the chamber a log shifted in the fire, sending sparks up toward the vaulted dark. Rafael kept her breathing slow, kept the well of her gift buried, buried, buried—though she could feel it pressing up against its grave now, restless, as if it knew it was among kin.

"Everyone lies here, at first," Odile said finally. "About how much they've got, about what they can do. They think if they seem small, they'll seem safe." She leaned forward. "I'm going to tell you something, Rafael-just-Rafael, and you can take it however you like. In this house, small isn't safe. Small is what gets people killed, because small means we don't know what you can do when the Cloaks come down that stair, and they will come. I don't need your confession tonight. But I'll know eventually. I always know. And when I do, I'd rather hear it from you than from the rubble."

She stood, apparently satisfied, and called across the chamber: "Tam! Show the new girl where the empty cot is. And get her some porridge before Maretta eats it all."

The boy slid off his crate and came over, grinning, missing a front tooth. Up close Rafael saw that his eyes were an odd, deep brown, almost the color of wet earth, and that he moved with his head slightly tilted, always listening.

"I'm Tam," he said. "You're the charm-seller. The stones told me you were coming—they've been gossiping about it since yesterday. They say you walk quiet." He lowered his voice to a conspiratorial whisper. "They also say you're louder than you think you are. Inside, I mean. They say you glow."

Rafael's blood went cold.

Maretta laughed from her knees. "Don't mind him, love. Boy talks nonsense. Porridge?"

"Yes," Rafael said, and her voice came out steady, and she marveled at that, at the actor she had become. "Porridge. Thank you."

She ate. It was good porridge, thick with honey, and it was the first food in two days that tasted like anything. Around her the household settled into its rhythms—Maretta bandaging a coughing old man, the two young women repairing a net, Odile at the crate-table marking a map with charcoal, Jules—no, not yet, she didn't know him yet, a lean man sharpening knives by the tool rack who had not once looked at her and somehow looked at her constantly.

A family. That was the word that came, unbidden, unwelcome, and Rafael pushed it away the way she pushed everything else down. Families were for people who stayed. She was going to rest two nights, three at most, and then slip south before the city tightened further, alone, invisible, the way she survived.

That was the plan.

But as she lay on the borrowed cot that night, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's life, listening to the soft chorus of strangers' breathing, Rafael felt the strangest sensation. The stones beneath her were humming—not the nervous gossip of the tenement, but something low and steady, almost like a lullaby. And buried in her chest, deep in its grave, her gift turned over, uncurled, and—for the first time in three years—went to sleep.

She told herself it was exhaustion. She told herself she'd be gone in three days.

Above her, far above, Corvane rang its bells for the morning watch, and somewhere in the palace a king who did not sleep signed another name to another list, and the hunt went on.
