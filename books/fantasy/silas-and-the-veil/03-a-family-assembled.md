## 3. A Family Assembled

Rafael woke to the smell of onions and the sound of someone arguing with a stone.

The voice was young, high, and utterly unselfconscious about its absurdity—a boy's voice, pitched in the particular exasperation of someone who has been contradicted one too many times. She lay still on the narrow cot Odile Voss had grudgingly assigned her the night before, the blanket scratchy against her chin, and listened.

"It doesn't *work* like that," the boy said. "You can't just—no. No. I'm not asking you to. I'm telling you what she said."

A pause. Then, in a tone of profound disgust: "Well, that's *rude*."

Rafael pressed her face into the pillow to keep from laughing. The sound came out anyway, a small snort, and the voice beyond the curtain stopped.

"You're awake," it said accusingly.

"I'm awake," Rafael agreed.

The curtain twitched aside and a boy peered in at her—ten years old, maybe eleven, with a shock of copper hair and dirt ground so permanently into his knuckles that it looked like part of him. He studied her with the frank, unnervingly direct gaze of a child who had never learned that some things were better left unseen.

"Odile says I'm not supposed to ask you questions," he announced. "So I'm not going to."

"That's very disciplined of you."

"It is." He shifted his weight. "What's your name?"

"Rafael."

"Tam." He said it like he was handing her something. Then, after a visible struggle: "I'm not asking you this either, but the wall behind your cot says your magic smells like rain on hot iron, and it's never said that about anybody before, and Maretta says hers smells like bread, and mine smells like—"

"TAM." The voice came from deeper in the tunnel, a woman's voice, warm and worn as an old kitchen cloth. "Let the poor girl dress."

Tam looked at Rafael. Rafael looked at Tam.

"The walls talk to me," he said, as if confessing to a crime. "And the floors. And sometimes the stairs, but stairs are boring, they only ever complain about feet." He backed out through the curtain. "I'm not asking you anything. I just thought you should know what the wall said."

His footsteps pattered away. Rafael lay staring at the ceiling beams—old ship's timbers, salvaged, still bearing the ghost-marks of cargo stenciled in faded paint—and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched.

Three years. Three years of hiding, of shrinking, of performing hedge-tricks for coins while the real thing sat leashed and silent inside her. And now, on her first morning underground, a child had told her the walls could smell her gift, and the world had not ended.

She got up slowly and dressed, and did not let herself hope. Hope was a coin you spent carefully, and she had learned to count.

* * *

The tunnel home revealed itself to her by degrees, the way a shy animal comes out of cover.

It lived in the bones of old Corvane—the city beneath the city, where smugglers had run goods since before Aldric's grandfather wore the crown. Odile's stretch of it centered on a vaulted chamber beneath Bellows Street where three passages met, and someone with patience and no fear of hard work had made it livable. Lanterns hung from iron hooks driven into ancient mortar. Shelves lined one wall, crammed with jars, tins, bundles of dried herbs, coils of rope. A stove squatted in the center like a fat black idol, its pipe running up through a shaft that breathed the smoke out through a chimney grate somewhere above, disguised among a hundred others.

And people. That was the thing Rafael kept stumbling over. People, moving through the space with the easy choreography of long habit.

Maretta she found at the stove—a broad, gray-streaked woman with forearms like a blacksmith's and a burn scar that climbed her left wrist and vanished up her sleeve. She stirred a pot the size of a cauldron and hummed something tuneless, and when she saw Rafael hovering at the edge of the firelight she didn't startle or stare. She just lifted her chin toward a stack of bowls.

"If you're going to stand there, you can carry these to the table," she said. "Third shelf. Mind the chipped one, it leaks."

It was such an ordinary instruction, so casually given—as if Rafael were already part of the furniture of the place—that she obeyed before she thought to be wary. The bowls were mismatched, collected from a dozen different lives. One had roses painted on it, worn nearly away. One was fine porcelain with a hairline crack. One was tin.

"Sit anywhere," Maretta said behind her. "Jules will glare at you no matter which chair you pick, so don't waste time choosing."

"Charming household," Rafael murmured.

"He's not charming. He's right, most of the time, which is worse." Maretta tasted the pot, added salt, tasted again. "He lost people. You'll hear it eventually or you won't. Either way, eat first. Nobody argues well hungry."

The table itself was a miracle of salvage—two doors laid across trestles, scrubbed pale, scarred with candle burns and knife-nicks and one deep gouge shaped like a crescent moon. Around it stood chairs that matched nothing: a bench, a barrel with a cushion, a proper spindle-backed chair that had clearly been loved by someone once.

Rafael set the bowls down and ran her thumb along the crescent gouge without meaning to.

"My daughter did that with a mattock," said a voice at her shoulder, "when she was six and angry about bedtime."

She turned. Jules stood there—or rather, Jules had arrived without her hearing him, which in itself was a kind of answer about him. He was lean and silver-templed, with a face built for scowling and eyes that went through her like a customs officer's hands through luggage. A knife hung at his belt with the casual permanence of a limb.

"Your daughter," Rafael repeated.

"There was a daughter. There was a village, before Maretta's." His voice was flat, unhurried, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "There isn't now. There's this." He gestured at the room, the shelves, the boy's abandoned boots by the passage mouth. "Which is why I ask every new face the same question, and I'll ask you yours now so we can stop pretending otherwise."

"Ask."

"What are you running from, and how much trouble does it weigh?"

Rafael held his gaze. Every instinct she'd honed in three years of night markets rose up and offered her the practiced answers: *nothing much, nothing heavy, just bad luck, just wrong place.* The lies were good lies. They fit in the mouth easily.

But the wall behind her cot knew what her magic smelled like. And the boy who heard stones was somewhere in these tunnels, and the woman at the stove had burn scars from a village that wasn't anymore, and Rafael was so tired of carrying the weight alone that her shoulders ached with it.

"From my mistress," she said. "Serah. A hedge-witch, out past the fen country. I ran three years ago." She paused, and gave him the truth in miniature, the shape of it without the weight. "I found something in her books I shouldn't have seen. Something that scared me more than being caught ever has."

Something flickered across Jules's face—not softening, exactly. Recalculation.

"And how much trouble does it weigh?"

"Not yet any," she said. "That's the truth. But it might. Someday. If it follows me here, you can put me out into the street myself."

Jules considered her for a long moment. Behind them, Maretta had gone quiet at the stove, listening with the entire back of her body.

"Dinner's at the seventh bell," Jules said finally. "If you're eating with us, you're helping. Tam will show you where the water is." He turned away, then paused. "The chipped bowl leaks. Maretta will tell you it adds character. It does not. Take the tin one."

* * *

The water was fetched from a cistern fed, Tam explained at length, by a spring that "grumbled" but was basically friendly. He talked the whole time they worked—about the tunnels, about which passages flooded in spring, about the rat named General who had achieved a truce with the household through sheer audacity. Rafael carried buckets and let the talk wash over her, and realized halfway through the second trip that she was smiling, actually smiling, at a boy's monologue about a rat.

Careful, she told herself. Careful. You've been somewhere warm before. You know how it ends.

But Serah's cottage had never been warm, not really. It had been a schoolroom, a leash, a place where Rafael's power was measured and catalogued and quietly, incrementally, claimed. She had told herself for three years that leaving had been cowardice. Standing in a smuggler's tunnel watching a child argue with a spring, she began to suspect it had been the first honest thing she'd ever done.

By the seventh bell the table was set, and the family assembled around it the way water finds its level.

Odile came last, up from the southern passage, smelling of lamp oil and river mud. She took the head of the table without ceremony—it was simply her place, the way the keel is a boat's place—and set down a canvas sack that clinked.

"Two barrels of Vellish brandy and a chest of silk," she reported, "and a Grey Cloak patrol on Bellows Street that's gotten twice as thick in two days. Eat while it's hot."

"They're sweeping wider," Jules said. It wasn't a question.

"They're sweeping wider." Odile's eye found Rafael, briefly, then moved on. "Which means everyone eats fast tonight and nobody goes up alone. Maretta, the list?"

Maretta recited names as she ladled stew—names Rafael didn't know, families sheltered in side-tunnels, a glassworker, two sisters who read fortunes badly, an old man who could coax green things out of dead wood. Eleven households. Eleven little flames cupped in hands, hidden from a wind that was rising.

Rafael ate and listened and understood, gradually, what she had walked into. Not a hideout. An ark.

"This is good stew," Tam said, around a mouthful.

"It's adequate stew," Jules corrected.

"It's *good* stew and you know it."

"It's adequate," Jules said, "which is the highest praise food can earn, because praise makes cooks lazy." But he took a second helping, and Maretta caught Rafael's eye across the table with a look that said, plainly: *you see what I live with.*

And Rafael—Rafael laughed. Out loud, unguarded, into the middle of the meal. It startled her more than anyone. The sound seemed to hang in the lamplight, unfamiliar, like a word in a language she used to speak.

Nobody made anything of it. That was the mercy of it. Tam grinned and went back to interrogating the table leg about whether it missed being a door; Maretta refilled her bowl without asking; Odile talked routes and patrols with Jules in low voices; and Rafael sat in the middle of it all with a tin bowl in her hands and let herself be, for one hour, one meal, simply a person at a table.

She tried to remember the last time. Her mother's kitchen, maybe, before the fever took her—before Serah came for the gifted orphan the villages whispered about. She couldn't find the memory's edges. It had been that long.

*After,* whispered the careful voice inside her. *This ends after. Everything does.*

She ate her stew anyway.

* * *

Later, when the bowls were cleared and Tam had been bullied toward his cot, Rafael found Maretta banking the stove and offered to help, mostly to have something to do with her hands.

"You healed someone," Maretta said, without turning. "Before you came here. Recently. I can always tell—healing leaves a smell, like crushed green stems. On you it's faint. Old."

Rafael's stomach tightened. "A dockworker. A gashed arm, weeks ago. Small work."

"Mm." Maretta closed the stove's grate. "Small work, done well, is still work. I burned a village's worth of trust learning that." She straightened, and in the banked firelight her burn scar caught orange. "They came for me with torches because I saved a lord's son and let a laborer's daughter die—so they said. What really happened is I chose, and choosing is the one thing small folk aren't allowed to do with power. So now I choose down here instead." She looked at Rafael steadily. "Whatever you're hiding about how strong you are—I'm not asking. But don't hide it from yourself. That way lies bitterness, and I've enough bitter people at this table."

"I'm not strong," Rafael said. The lie came automatically, smoothly, and died in the air between them half-spoken, because Maretta was looking at her the way no one had looked at her in three years: as if she were worth the truth.

"I'm stronger than I've said," Rafael amended quietly. "To everyone. Including myself, maybe."

Maretta nodded once, satisfied, and squeezed her shoulder—brief, practical, devastating. "Good. Sleep, then. Odile puts the new ones on water duty. Dawn shift."

"Dawn shift," Rafael repeated. "Of course."

"Family rule," Maretta said, and the word landed in Rafael's chest like a coal in dry grass.

She lay that night on the narrow cot under the talking walls, listening to the sounds of the household settling—Jules checking the passages, Odile's boots, Tam's muffled argument with his pillow about whether it was listening properly. Above her, far above, Corvane slept uneasily under criers' proclamations and thickening patrols. Down here, eleven households of hidden flame breathed in the dark.

She was almost asleep when the stone behind her headboard spoke—not to Tam, but in the general murmur stones apparently kept up among themselves, a vibration more felt than heard, and Tam's sleepy voice drifted through the curtain, translating absently, half-dreaming:

"The deep rock says... somebody's been asking the old roads about the palace..." A pause. A yawn. "...says the roads haven't been asked that in twenty years..."

Rafael lay wide awake in the dark, her heart beating slow and cold, and thought of a diagram in a leather-bound book, ink-brown with age, of a king bound kneeling beneath a throne.

Twenty years. Someone had asked the roads about the palace twenty years ago.

Someone had helped forge those chains.
