## 4. What the Registry Costs

Jules had always measured the city by its sounds. Corvane at dawn was a creak of ropes and the groan of warehouse doors; by midmorning it was the clatter of the fish market, the barking of gulls fat enough to barely lift off the cobbles. He had learned those sounds in twenty years of running cargo through Odile's tunnels, learned them the way a sailor learns weather—without thinking, without fear.

So when the bells began ringing out of order on a grey Tuesday morning, he knew before his eyes opened that something had gone wrong.

He lay in the narrow bunk above the storeroom and listened. Not the alarm bells from the harbor towers—those rang fast and even, a pattern every smuggler's child could recite in their sleep. These were slower. Heavier. The great bell in Tanner's Square, tolled by hand, one stroke at a time, the way it was rung for executions and plagues.

Beside him, Odile was already awake. He could tell by her breathing—the shallow, deliberate rhythm she fell into when her mind was working through maps. Her eye, the good one, was open, fixed on the ceiling beams as though they might rearrange themselves into an answer.

"Tanner's," Jules said quietly.

"I know."

"Who's in Tanner's Quarter?"

Odile closed her eye. "Everyone we couldn't fit down here."

* * *

They heard the truth of it in pieces through the day, carried down into the tunnels by runners and rumor, each version worse than the last. The registry had gone up on the church doors three weeks ago—a list, the criers said, merely a list, names and addresses of the gifted so that the crown might "extend its protection." Some had registered out of fear. A few, incredibly, had registered out of faith, believing the king's seal meant what it had always meant.

One of them was Anselm Pike.

Jules knew him distantly, the way you know a man whose shop sits along your route. Pike kept a chandler's shop off Cooper's Lane, a squat stone building that smelled forever of tallow and honey. He made candles that burned without dripping and lamps that gave light the color of late afternoon. Small magics. Kind ones. Jules had bought a storm-lamp from him two winters back and still used it on the long hauls beneath Bellows Street, where the dark got thick enough to chew.

Pike had registered because he had a wife with weak lungs who needed a physician, and physicians asked questions, and a registered man had nothing to hide. That was the arithmetic of it. That was how the crown had designed it—not to catch the powerful, but to make safety look like surrender, one small kindness at a time, until the list was full.

The Grey Cloaks came for him at noon.

Jules didn't see it. Nobody in the tunnels saw it directly, which somehow made it worse, because what came down instead was fragments: a runner who'd been two streets over and heard the shutters break; a fishwife who swore the Cloaks dragged Pike out first and then went back in; a boy who claimed—and this was the part that stopped the whole household cold—that they hadn't taken Pike away at all. That they'd carried him back inside. That they'd nailed the doors.

The fire started an hour past sundown.

From the mouth of the tunnel behind the tannery, where the family gathered because they couldn't help gathering, Jules could see the glow of it over the rooftops, orange against a sky the color of wet slate. Smoke rolled east in a fat black column. The crowd noise reached them even underground—a low animal sound, hundreds of people making the same noise without agreeing to, grief and horror compressed into something that wasn't quite screaming.

"They burned him in his own shop." Maretta's voice came from beside him, flat and strange. She had her arms wrapped around herself, knuckles white. "With him inside it. They nailed the doors and burned him with his wife watching."

"Where's the wife?" Tam asked. The boy's voice cracked on the word *wife*, and Jules remembered, not for the first time, that Tam was eleven years old and learning what cities did.

"Gone," said the runner, a hollow-eyed girl named Bess. "Or dead. Nobody knows. The Cloaks cleared the street before the flames caught proper."

Odile stood apart from all of them, arms crossed, her single eye reflecting the fire-glow so that it seemed the burning had gotten inside her too. She said nothing for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was the one she used for orders, the one that permitted no argument, and Jules felt his stomach drop before she'd finished the sentence, because he already knew what she was going to say.

"Nobody goes up tonight. Nobody goes near Cooper's Lane. We keep our heads down and we keep working."

"That's it?" Jules turned to face her fully. Around them, the little crowd went quiet in the particular way of people who can smell a fight coming. "Anselm Pike is ash, Odile. Ash. And your answer is *keep working*?"

"My answer is that there are forty-one people on the registry list in this district alone, and most of them are still alive, and half of them will come looking for us if we start running around with torches and grief." Her eye found his and held it. "You want to march? March toward what?"

"Away!" The word tore out of him louder than he intended. He lowered his voice, but only a little. "Away. That's what I've been saying since the registry went up. This city is a trap closing by inches, and every day we stay, the walls get closer. Take the ones we have and go south, go to Marsh Hollow, go anywhere the king's writ runs thin—"

"And leave the rest?" Odile's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the terrible thing about arguing with her; she got quieter and quieter until you realized you were shouting alone. "Leave the Maretts and the Pikes still hiding in their cellars, still hoping someone comes? You want us to be the kind of people who ran?"

"I want us to be *alive* people!"

The echo came back off the tunnel walls, and in the silence after it, Jules became aware of how many faces were turned toward them. Tam, pressed against Maretta's side. Bess the runner, hugging her elbows. Rafael Okonkwo, standing at the edge of the lamplight with her hands folded, watching them both with an expression Jules couldn't read—something older than her face should have allowed.

"The Cloaks raided the upper entrances on Fetter Street yesterday," Jules said, softer now, pleading now. "They're mapping us, Odie. They don't need to find us all at once. They just need to keep taking doorways until we've got nowhere left to stand."

"I know how sieges work, Jules. I fought in one." Something moved behind her eye—old smoke, old screams, a war she never talked about. "And I know what happens to people who scatter. They die alone, in ditches, in ones and twos, and nobody ever learns their names. We hold together or we mean nothing."

"We hold together and we burn together, is what you mean."

"Maybe." She said it simply, without flinching, and that simplicity frightened him more than any argument could have. "Maybe that's the price. But I'll pay it here, with these people, before I'll pay it running."

She turned away from him then, deliberately, and began giving quiet instructions—double the watch on the north passages, move the grain stores deeper, no one surfaces before full dark tomorrow. Orders, orders, orders, the armor she wore instead of grief. And Jules stood in the firelight leaking up from the world above, furious and heartsick and unable to name which feeling was winning, and understood that he loved this stubborn, impossible woman precisely because she would nail herself to a burning building before she'd let one more person be taken alone.

That didn't mean he had to like it.

* * *

He found Rafael an hour later, deep in the tunnels, and what he saw stopped him in the dark like a hand on his chest.

The passage behind the old cistern was one of Tam's places—the boy liked it because the stones there were "chatty," whatever that meant, and he'd sit for hours with his palms flat against the walls, listening to things no one else could hear. Tonight he sat exactly that way, except that his right hand lay in his lap at an angle hands don't go. Broken, and badly. Jules had seen enough breaks in twenty years of rough work to know a bad one: the swelling already dark, the fingers wrong.

Rafael knelt in front of him. And her hands were glowing.

Not blazing—whispering. A pale gold light, soft as candlelight through honey, pooled in her palms and sank into the boy's ruined hand, and where it sank, the flesh remembered itself. Jules watched the swelling fade. Watched the fingers straighten, one by one, like flowers opening in reverse of wilting. Watched Tam's face go from white-knuckled pain to slack wonder.

"You shouldn't—" Rafael's voice was tight, strained, nothing like the easy market patter she used upstairs. "Hold still. This is delicate work, bone wants to remember wrong if you let it—"

"It doesn't hurt anymore," Tam breathed. "It doesn't hurt at *all*. Maretta said broken bones take six weeks—"

"Maretta sets bones. I unbreak them. Different thing entirely, and much more stupid, so don't you dare tell her—" Rafael swayed. Caught herself. Kept working.

Jules stepped forward, and a pebble shifted under his boot, and Rafael's head snapped up.

For one naked moment, before she could arrange her face, he saw her clearly: exhausted, frightened, caught. A woman holding a lamp she'd spent three years insisting was a candle.

"He fell in the north passage," she said. "Slipped on the wet stones. I was just—"

"Rafael." He crouched down across from her, slowly, the way you approach a spooked animal. In the gold light her eyes were very bright. "I ran cargo past witch-fires in the border wars. I know healing when I see it. Real healing. Hedge-wives can soothe a fever and draw out a splinter. What you're doing is the other thing. The big thing."

Her jaw tightened. The glow guttered, steadied.

"How strong are you?" he asked quietly. "Truly. Not the charms-and-knots version. Truly."

Silence. Tam looked between them, sensing the weight of the question if not its shape. Somewhere far off, water dripped its slow count into the cistern.

"Why does it matter?" she said at last.

"Because Odile won't ask. Because Maretta would ask gently and you'd lie to her gently. And because today they burned a man who could make candles that didn't drip." Jules held her gaze. "If you're what I think you might be, then you're not just another mouth to shelter, Rafael. You might be the most dangerous person in this city. To them. Which means they will hunt you harder than anyone, and it means—" he hesitated, hating the next words and saying them anyway, "—it means maybe you're the reason to stop hiding. Or the reason we all have to run faster. Either way, I'm done pretending I don't see what I see."

The light in her hands dimmed as she finished—she drew the last of it back into herself with visible effort, folding it away like a merchant folding cloth—and Tam's hand lay whole and pale in his lap, flexing, disbelieving.

"I hid for three years," Rafael said finally. Her voice was low, and it shook only a little. "Before Corvane, I hid for longer than that. Do you know what I learned? That power isn't a gift. It's a scent. Everything that hunts can smell it, and everything that needs it wants to own it, and there is no amount of being careful that makes you safe. There's only smaller. Smaller and smaller until you disappear." She looked down at her empty palms. "I got very good at disappearing."

"And now?"

"Now they're burning chandlers in their shops." She lifted her head, and something in her face had changed—hardened, the way metal hardens, in fire. "Now the small life is dead anyway, isn't it? They killed it when they nailed those doors. So I suppose you deserve an answer, Jules." She took a breath. "I am stronger than Serah ever was. Stronger than anyone I've met. And I have spent my whole life being ashamed of it, and today I watched a kind man burn for a gift a tenth the size of mine, and I am done being ashamed."

The words hung in the dark between them. Above, muffled by forty feet of earth and stone, the city still murmured its grief.

"Don't tell Odile yet," Rafael added. "Let me tell her myself. She carries enough."

"She carries everything," Jules said. "That's her whole tragedy." He stood, knees cracking, and offered Rafael his hand. After a moment she took it, and he pulled her up, and Tam scrambled up between them cradling his new-old hand like a treasure.

"Does it count as magic," Tam asked solemnly, "if the stones say thank you?"

Rafael blinked. "Do they?"

"All the time." He patted the wall fondly. "They liked you. They said you shine."

Jules exchanged a glance with Rafael over the boy's head—one of those glances that contains an entire negotiation—and what passed between them, unspoken, was this: *the game has changed, and we both know it, and God help us all.*

* * *

That night, Jules couldn't sleep. He climbed to the watch post at the Fetter Street entrance instead, where the tunnel mouth opened behind a false wall in a derelict cooperage, and sat with his back against the stone and listened to Corvane breathe its damaged breath.

Smoke still hung over the Tanner's Quarter. He could smell it even down here—tallow and char and something sweeter underneath that he refused to identify. Somewhere up there, Anselm Pike's wife was dead or fled or kneeling in the ruins of her life, and somewhere up there, Grey Cloaks were writing down addresses, and somewhere up there, thirty-nine other registered souls were lying awake doing the same arithmetic Jules was doing: *how long, how long, how long.*

Footsteps on the ladder. Odile emerged into the watch post without ceremony, handed him a cup of the bitter chicory they drank instead of tea, and settled beside him. For a while neither spoke. Twenty years of nights like this had taught them both the value of silence.

"I meant what I said," Jules offered eventually. "About going."

"I know you did."

"And you meant what you said."

"Every word." She drank. Below them, faintly, the tunnels hummed with the small sounds of the living—Maretta banking her hearth-coals, Bess humming to keep the dark away, Tam talking softly to walls that answered. "Ask me if it's worth it, Jules. Go on. Ask me if forty strangers are worth your life, my life, all of ours."

"Is it?"

"I don't know." She said it without shame. "But I know what we are if we leave. And I've been the thing that runs before. It fits poorly." Her eye reflected the thin line of torchlight that leaked through the false wall. "Besides. Something's changed. You've seen it too—I can tell. The girl. Rafael."

Jules turned his cup slowly in his hands. "What about her?"

"Tonight, when everyone was grieving, she sat with Tam for two hours. And when she left him, the boy was laughing." Odile's voice was thoughtful, distant. "Broken hand at supper, laughing by midnight. I've known healers my whole life, Jules. Good ones. None of them do that."

The chicory had gone cold in his cup. Jules stared into the dark of the shaft and thought of golden light whispering through a child's fingers, of a woman who shone so brightly the stones themselves had noticed, and of the price such brightness always, always commanded.

"Odie," he said carefully, "there's something you need to know about her. But it's hers to tell, and she'll tell it soon. I think—" he searched for the shape of it, the thing he'd seen hardening in her face by the cistern, "—I think today broke something loose in her. I think she's done hiding. And I think whatever she decides to do next, this family is going to follow her straight into the fire."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed—short, dry, without much humor in it, but with something underneath that might have been hope.

"Good," she said. "Because I'm tired of deciding everything alone."

Far above them, muffled by stone and distance, a church bell began to toll again—slow, heavy, one stroke at a time. Counting. Jules listened and felt the grief in his chest compress, condense, turn by some alchemy of exhaustion and love into something harder and more useful.

Somewhere in the dark below, Rafael Okonkwo was telling the stones her secrets. And the stones, Tam would say later, were listening very, very closely.
