## 5. The King Who Does Not Sleep

Aldric Veyran had not slept in twenty years.

He remembered sleep the way a drowned man remembers air: as something that had once belonged to him, warm and ordinary and unremarkable. He remembered the weight of blankets, the slow dark behind his eyelids, the mercy of forgetting. Now there was no forgetting. There was only the long vigil of his own body, watched from the inside like a prisoner watching a courtyard through a slit of window, and the thing beneath his will that never slept either—never needed to, never wanted to, that wore his exhaustion like a costume it had learned to imitate.

In the mornings it let him yawn. That was one of its small cruelties, or perhaps one of its kindnesses; he had never decided which. It arranged his face into the shapes of a tired man, because a king who did not look tired would draw questions, and questions were dangerous to it. So each dawn Aldric stood before the silvered glass in his dressing chamber and watched his own mouth open in a false yawn, watched his own eyes water on command, and thought: *I am a puppet learning to puppet itself.*

"Your Majesty." Chamberlain Dreth bowed at the door, gray-robed, gray-faced, his eyes the flat color of river ice. "The council awaits."

"Of course it does," said Aldric's mouth.

He walked. He was permitted to walk, so long as he walked where the Veil wished, and this morning the Veil wished the long gallery, past the portraits of his ancestors, all of them dead and free. His boots sounded on marble worn hollow by a century of royal processions, and beneath the sound—beneath everything, always—he could feel it coiled at the base of his skull, patient as sediment, vast as the dark under a lake. It did not speak in words most days. Words were crude tools for it. It spoke in pressure, in inclination, in the subtle tilting of his own desires until he could no longer be certain which wants were his. That was the worst of it. Not the commands. The *editing*. Twenty years ago he had known his own mind the way a man knows his own hands. Now his mind was a document someone else kept amending, and the original text was lost.

*Good morning, little king,* it said, in the way it said such things—not words but a warmth along his spine, a settling of weight, the affection of a hand resting on the back of a neck.

Aldric did not answer. Answering encouraged it. In the early years he had screamed at it, bargained with it, wept at it, and it had absorbed all of it the way the sea absorbs stones. Silence was the only dignity left to him, and he guarded it like the last coin of a bankrupt kingdom.

* * *

The council chamber smelled of tallow and ink and, faintly, of rot—the rot was new these last three years, since the ban, since the burnings began. No one else seemed to smell it. Perhaps only he could, the way a man whose house is haunted smells the haunting.

"The northern districts resist the registry," said Lord Cassel, spreading a map across the table. He was a broad man with a voice built for drill yards, and he spoke to Aldric the way one speaks to a wall that occasionally issues decrees. "Bellows Street especially. The smugglers move them through the old cisterns. We've taken eleven this month, but for every one we catch, two vanish."

*Take more,* the Veil inclined him to say, and Aldric felt his own throat prepare the words, felt the sentence assembling itself in his chest like a bird taking shape from smoke.

He fought it. He always fought it, though the fighting had never once changed the outcome; it only made the outcome cost more. But the fighting was his. It was the one property they could not seize. So he stood inside his own body and pushed against the inclination the way a man pushes against a door bolted from the other side, and when he finally spoke, his voice came out level and cold and exactly wrong:

"Increase the sweeps. Double the Grey Cloaks' stipend. And post the names of the taken at the market crosses—the rest will come forward when they see we are serious."

Cassel smiled. Somewhere in the city, Aldric knew, a family would be torn apart by that smile, and his own mouth had made it possible, and the thing beneath his will purred along his vertebrae like a cat being stroked.

There had been a time—he clung to the memory the way a climber clings to a root over a drop—when Aldric Veyran had been twenty-two years old and desperate, kneeling in a marsh hut while a plague ate his father's kingdom and his own lungs filled like bellows. There had been a hedge-witch there, or something older than a hedge-witch, and another presence behind her, vast and cold and *offering*, and he had been so afraid of dying that he had not asked enough questions about what living would cost.

*I will carry you,* it had promised. *You will never fall.*

It had kept the letter of the bargain perfectly. He had never fallen. He had also never been alone in his own skull again, and the plague years had ended, and the kingdom had flourished, and somewhere in the flourishing the Veil had begun to eat. First it ate his sleep. Then his dreams. Then, slowly, with the patience of a root splitting stone, it had eaten his reign—turning his justice into cruelty, his prudence into paranoia, his love of his people into a slow systematic harvest. Because that was what it fed on. Magic. The gift in human blood, the bright thread woven through certain souls. It outlawed what it devoured, and it used his crown, his seal, his beloved face to do the outlawing.

Every death warrant bore his signature. Every pyre was lit in his name. And every night—for there were no nights for him anymore, only the hours when the palace grew quiet and the Veil's attention drifted toward the city like a shark cruising beyond a reef—in those hours Aldric lay in his bed, eyes open in the dark, and hated himself with a purity that would have astonished the theologians.

* * *

The warrants came to him at midday, forty of them, bound in black ribbon.

This part was ritual now, almost liturgy. The scribes brought the documents; the Veil guided his hand; the quill scratched; names became corpses. Aldric had learned, over twenty years, to do two things at once: to sign, and to watch. To occupy the small room at the back of himself where the Veil's attention did not bother to reach, because why would it? What harm could a prisoner do in a room with no door?

But there was a door. There had always been a door. It was ink.

The Veil understood power—raw gift, witchcraft, the blood-bright thread—but it did not understand paper. It did not understand the thousand small courtesies of statecraft, the marginalia, the formulaic blessings, the conventional flourishes that cluttered every royal document. To it, language was a lever and nothing more. It read for command and compliance and never for anything hidden between the lines, because nothing in its long hungry existence had ever dared hide from it.

Aldric hid.

He had begun small, years ago, testing like a prisoner tapping walls. A misplaced accent here. An inverted initial there. He had studied the old court cipher his grandfather had used for letters to his mistresses—a cipher buried so deep in dynastic trivia that no living clerk knew it—and he had rebuilt it in his cell of a mind, letter by letter, and then he had begun to write.

Today the third warrant was for a woman named Odile Voss, described as a smuggler of the Bellows Street tunnels, harborer of the unregistered. Aldric read the description twice while his hand signed the first warrant without him. *Voss.* He filed the name away the way he filed all of them, in the crowded archive of the people his hand had condemned, and turned to the third page.

His pen moved through the formal recitation—*by grace and by necessity, let it be known*—and threaded through the conventional phrases, invisible unless you knew to look, ran the true sentence:

*THE KING IS NOT THE KING. THE BARGAIN WAS MADE IN THE MARSH YEAR. SEEK THE ONE WHO WROTE IT. SHE LIVES. I AM ALIVE. HELP.*

He had written some version of that sentence four hundred times in six years. Into warrants, into trade charters, into a funeral oration, into the dedication of a bridge. Four hundred messages cast into the sea of bureaucracy like bottles, and for six years, silence. Either no one read the margins, or no one who read them believed them, or—this was the thought that woke him at 3 a.m., if waking were a thing he still did—no one who believed them had survived long enough to act. The Grey Cloaks answered to the Veil directly; he had learned that much by watching which of his own coded complaints had resulted in disappearances. Once, early on, he had written *I AM PRISONER* plainly, in desperation rather than cipher, in the corner of a grain tariff. The clerk who transcribed that tariff had been found floating in the canal within the week. The Veil did not punish Aldric for it. It had simply shown him the body, in a dream it constructed for the purpose, and let him understand.

So he wrote smaller now. Sider. Deeper in convention. And he waited, and hoped, and hated the hoping, because hope in a cell is a slow poison and he drank it daily anyway.

His hand finished the fortieth warrant. The scribes gathered the black-ribboned bundle. As the lead scribe bowed, Aldric heard himself say, in the dry ceremonial tone the Veil preferred:

"Let them be posted by week's end. The city should know its king protects it."

*Protects it.* From the very magic the Veil farmed out of them, harvested through fear, driven underground so it could hunt the desperate ones who used it anyway. Protects it. His mouth said the word; his soul gagged on it.

*You did well today, little king,* the Veil murmured, pleased, drowsy, its attention already sliding toward the smell of some gifted fugitive hiding in the cisterns below Bellows Street. *Rest now.*

Rest. He walked back through the gallery alone—no, never alone, never once alone in twenty years—and paused before the portrait of his father, a stern man with kind eyes who had died believing his son would be a good king.

"I'm trying," Aldric whispered, so quietly that even the thing inside him might not hear. "Father, I'm still trying."

The portrait said nothing. Portraits were free. Everything else in the palace belonged to it.

* * *

Night. The Veil's attention stretched thin after feeding—it had fed tonight, he could feel it, a greasy satisfaction radiating from his own marrow, someone's small gift snuffed out somewhere in the lower city—and in those thin hours Aldric was most himself. He sat at the writing desk in his private study, the one room the Veil never bothered to furnish with surveillance because kings wrote nothing of consequence themselves, and he took out the plain practice folio in which he kept his cipher drills, and he worked.

Not messages tonight. Tonight he planned.

Six years of bottles in the sea had taught him patience, but lately the calculus had changed. The burnings were accelerating. The registry would strip the city of every gifted soul it could find, and the Veil grew stronger with each harvest, and there would come a threshold—he could feel it approaching the way one feels weather in an old wound—past which it would no longer need his body at all except as ornament. Past which the man named Aldric might be set down entirely, the way a child sets down a toy grown boring.

And yet. Something else had changed too. He could feel it through the Veil's own hunger, the way a fisherman feels a storm coming in the behavior of gulls. Something in the deep city. A knot of the gifted, hidden and stubborn. And threaded through the Veil's recent satisfactions, a new note he had never tasted before: *wariness*. Small, faint, deniable—but there. Something below Bellows Street had made the ancient thing cautious.

Hope was a poison. He drank it anyway. He uncapped his ink and began, in cipher, in the margin of a draft decree nobody would read closely, to plan the only rebellion left to a man imprisoned in his own flesh: to make himself useful to whoever might come. To leave doors unlocked in the architecture of the state. To know, when the moment came, exactly where the binding was anchored—because he had been present, half-conscious and drowning, when it was forged, and he remembered the marsh, and the stone, and the voices, and above all he remembered *her*: the woman who had held the pen. Serah, they had called her. The one who wrote it.

*SHE LIVES,* he had written four hundred times. He was almost sure she lived. The Veil would have gloated otherwise; it loved to gloat.

Below the window, Corvane glittered, ten thousand hearths, and somewhere among them, he prayed, someone was reading margins. Someone was connecting a smuggler's name to a king's strange phrasing. Someone was deciding to believe the impossible thing written in the voice of the man who burned them.

"Hold on," Aldric whispered to the city, to the dark, to himself—to the small unbroken room at the back of his soul where a twenty-two-year-old still knelt in a marsh hut making the worst bargain in the history of crowns. "Hold on. I'm leaving the door open."

Behind him, at the base of his skull, the Veil stirred in its shallow doze, and turned over, and dreamed—of tunnels, and chalk sigils, and a girl with too much light hidden under hedge-tricks—and its dreaming hunger sharpened like a knife being drawn slowly from a sheath.

Aldric felt it wake, and reach, and fix upon a scent.

And for the first time in twenty years, the thing wearing his crown was afraid.
