## 6. The Message in the Margins

The stones had been singing wrong all morning.

Tam noticed it first in the walls of the common room, a thin discord running through the old brick like a splinter under skin. He'd learned to read the city's moods through its bones—the deep contented hum of Bellows Street's foundations, the grumble of the aqueduct, the bright chatter of cobbles warmed by sun. But today the ledger sitting on Odile's table was *loud*, and it was loud in a way that had nothing to do with weight or age.

He should have been minding the drying racks. Maretta had set him to turning the valerian bundles twice daily, and she always knew when he cheated, some healer's sixth sense that made her scold him with her eyes rather than her mouth. But the ledger called to him the way a locked door calls to a boy with quick fingers, and Tam had never once in his twelve years walked past a thing that whispered.

It lay among the haul from last week's job—a crate of confiscated goods lifted from a customs warehouse, mostly worthless: rotted silks, a case of spoiled ink, tax records nobody would ransom. Odile kept it on the table as a reminder, she said, of what the crown thought valuable enough to seize. *Paper,* she'd said, spitting into the gutter. *They burn witches and they hoard paper.*

Tam dragged his stool across the flagstones—apologizing silently to them for the scrape—and opened the ledger's cracked leather cover.

The first hundred pages were exactly what they appeared: inventories of seized property, columns of names, dates, values. Boring as sermon. He was about to close it when his thumb caught on the edge of a page near the back, where the paper changed texture. Newer. Thinner. Almost trembling.

And there, crammed into the margin beside an entry for forty bolts of linen, was writing so small it might have been decoration.

Not decoration. Tam could hear stone speak, but he couldn't read witch-script—and this was witch-script, he was sure of it, because the letters *hurt* to look at, the way standing too close to a struck bell hurts. They seemed written in something darker than ink. When he tilted the page toward the lamp, the characters cast shadows that didn't match the flame.

"Rafael," he said, before he'd decided to say anything at all.

She was in the corner mending one of Jules's shirts, needle flashing, and she looked up with the wariness she still wore like a second cloak after two weeks among them. "What?"

"There's writing in here." He held the ledger out. "In the margins. It's not normal."

Rafael set down the shirt. She crossed the room slowly, and Tam watched her face do the thing it did whenever magic came near her—that careful blankness, the shutters coming down over windows. She took the book from him like it might bite.

For a long moment she said nothing at all. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, cart wheels rattled over the grate on Bellows Street, and the stones carried the vibration down to Tam like distant thunder.

"This is royal hand," Rafael said finally. Her voice had gone strange. Flat, but with something underneath, the way ice looks solid until you notice the water moving beneath. "Or—it's meant to look like it. This is a state inventory seal." She turned pages with increasing speed. "There are more. Here. And here. Dozens of them."

"What does it say?" Tam asked, leaning in. The script buzzed against his senses like a trapped fly. "Can you read it?"

"Some of it." She sat down heavily on the bench, pulling the lamp closer. "It's a cipher layered over formal binding-notation. Whoever wrote this wanted it readable only by someone who knew both court script *and* craft-work." Her finger hovered over the margin without touching it. "It's a message."

"From who?"

"I don't know yet. Give me time."

* * *

By the time Odile came down the tunnel stairs that evening, Rafael hadn't moved from the bench except to fetch more lamps. Tam had abandoned all pretense of chores and sat across from her, chin on his fists, watching her lips move as she worked through the cipher. Jules leaned in the doorway with his arms crossed, wearing the expression he reserved for things he intended to distrust.

Odile took one look at the table—the ledger, the scattered notes, Rafael's face—and started unbuckling her sword belt. "Somebody found something."

"The ledger," Tam said. "From the customs house. There's hidden writing all through the back section."

"We were going to sell it for waste paper," Jules said flatly.

"Well, we're not now." Odile pulled up a crate and sat, elbows on knees, in the posture Tam privately called her captain-sitting, though she'd been a soldier twenty years ago and hated any mention of it. "Rafael. Talk."

"It's a plea." Rafael's hands were steady, but her voice wasn't quite. "Hidden in state records, which means whoever wrote it had access to documents passing through crown channels—and means to alter them without being seen doing it. It repeats, with variations, maybe thirty times across these pages. The core of it is the same every time." She looked up. "*I am not free. Find the binding. Beneath the throne. I am not free.*"

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"That's a trap," Jules said immediately. "That's the oldest trap there is. Bait for witches, written in their own script, planted in goods we're known to steal." He pushed off the doorframe. "The Grey Cloaks can't find us, so they invite us. We go chasing a mysterious prisoner under the palace and walk straight into a cell."

"You don't write thirty messages in a cipher that hard for a trap," Rafael said. "A trap needs one convincing lure. This is—" she gestured helplessly at the ledger—"*desperation*. Look at the variations. Early ones are formal, careful. By the end the hand deteriorates. The last one is barely legible. Someone has been hiding these for months, maybe years, terrified every single time, and getting no answer."

"Or someone wants us to think that."

"Jules." Odile's voice cut clean between them. She was staring at the open ledger, at the cramped desperate script, and her jaw had gone tight in a way Tam recognized from the night she'd told them about Maretta's village. "The king. You think it's the king."

"The seal is his chancery's. The access is his. And the words—" Rafael exhaled. "*I am not free.* Who else signs death warrants and hides pleas for help in the margins of his own paperwork?"

"The king isn't a prisoner," Jules said. "The king is the prison. Everyone knows Aldric hasn't been right since the ban. Everyone knows the Veil speaks through him. That's not captivity, that's—"

"That's exactly what captivity looks like from inside," Odile said quietly.

Tam looked between them, then at Rafael, and saw something he'd never seen on her face before. Not fear exactly. Recognition. She was staring at the marginal script—at the structure of it, the notation beneath the cipher—and her expression had gone somewhere far away and very cold.

"Rafael?" he prompted.

"These aren't just pleas." She turned the ledger toward them, toward a page dense with looping characters that made Tam's teeth ache. "This part isn't the message. This part is the writer showing his work. He copied out fragments of the binding that holds him—or what he's been able to reconstruct of it from inside. He's asking whoever reads this if they recognize it. If anyone alive knows this work."

"And does someone?" Odile asked softly.

Rafael didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice came out small, stripped of all its market-stall charm.

"Yes. I've seen this notation before. Three years ago, in my mistress's grimoire—the oldest book she owned, the one she kept chained shut. There were diagrams in it. Workings for binding a living will into a living body. I told myself I'd misunderstood. I told myself a lot of things, three years ago." She closed her eyes. "Serah helped bind the king. Or she knew who did. Either way, the past just reached out of a stolen ledger and put its hand on my throat."

Nobody spoke. The lamps burned. Above them Corvane went about its evening, ten thousand hearths lit, ten thousand families eating supper while their king sat in a palace full of stones that Tam suddenly understood had been screaming for years, and none of them—not the granite, not the marble, nothing—had ever sounded this afraid.

"So," Jules said at last, heavily, into the silence. "Trap or truth. Someone decide, because either way we're already in it."

Odile reached out and rested one scarred hand flat on the ledger, over the desperate little script, the way you'd steady a shaking shoulder.

"Then we decide fast," she said.
