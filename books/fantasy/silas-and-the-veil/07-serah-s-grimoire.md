## 7. Serah's Grimoire

The ledger still lay open on the table where Tam had found it, its margins crawling with that thin, patient script. Rafael had not touched it since. She kept her hands wrapped around a cup of chicory she wasn't drinking, letting the warmth bleed out of the clay into her palms while the tunnel household argued around her in low voices.

"A trap," Jules said for the third time. He stood with his arms crossed, shoulder against the doorframe as if he might need to bar it. "A king doesn't smuggle messages out through confiscated shipping manifests unless he wants them found by exactly the wrong people."

"Or unless he has no one else," Maretta said softly. She sat with her hands folded, the burn scars on her forearms catching the lamplight like old wax. "You weren't in Vellin when the purges came, Jules. You learn to write on whatever will carry. You learn to pray someone reads."

"He hears stone," Odile said, nodding at Tam, who sat cross-legged on the floor beside the ledger with his chin on his fists. "The boy says the binding-script is real. Not decoration. Real."

"It's real," Rafael said.

Her voice came out flatter than she intended, and the room turned toward her. She set down the cup because her hands had begun to shake, and she would not let them see that—not yet, not before she said what she had come here, over three years and two hundred miles of hiding, never intending to say to anyone.

"I've seen this script before." She swallowed. "In my mistress's grimoire. The oldest book she owned. And I know what it was for."

* * *

Three years ago, in the marsh house where Serah took in girls nobody else wanted, Rafael had been the one who dusted the shelves.

It was a small duty, and she had loved it—the only chore she'd ever loved. Serah's cottage smelled of tallow and river-mud and dried feverfew, and its single long shelf held perhaps forty books, most of them cheap almanacs and herbals with cracked spines. But at the far end, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a cord that Serah herself always untied, never Rafael, stood the oldest book. A grimoire bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its title worn away to nothing. Serah read from it sometimes at night, hunched over the table with the shutters barred, and when Rafael came downstairs for water she would close it without hurry, without guilt, the way you'd close a letter that was none of a child's business.

Rafael had been sixteen, and curious, and careless—three things that in a witch are not flaws but fuel.

Serah went to the marsh villages for three days that autumn, healing a fever that had taken hold of the eel-catchers' families. She left Rafael with instructions: feed the fire, mind the wards, touch nothing on the high shelf. And on the second night, alone with the wind worrying the reeds, Rafael climbed onto the stool and cut the cord.

She told herself afterward, for years, that she'd only meant to look. That was even true, at first. She meant to look and put it back and never speak of it, the way you promise yourself about so many things.

The early pages were what she expected: weather-workings, charms against bog-lights, a recipe for a salve that made hands able to bear cold water. Serah's own annotations crowded the margins in her sharp, slanted hand. Then, somewhere past the middle, the handwriting changed. Older. Denser. The diagrams stopped being circles of protection and became something else—circles of *containment*, drawn not around a place or an object but around a person. Around a living, breathing, crowned person.

Rafael remembered sitting down on the stool because her legs stopped agreeing to hold her.

The diagram was beautiful. That was the worst part. Whoever had drawn it—and Serah's notes said *we*, we bound him, we seven—we had drawn it with a loving hand, every line balanced, every sigil nested inside the next like rings of a tree. At the center, where a charm against rheumatism might name its subject, the script named its subject too. Not by name. By title.

*A king who bargained. A king who must be held.*

And beneath it, in Serah's younger handwriting, tighter than her later notes, almost angry: *He asked for it. Gods forgive us, he asked.*

Rafael had read the pages until the candle drowned in its own wax. She learned the shape of the working: seven witches, a stone beneath a throne, a bargain struck willingly and then chained unwillingly. She learned that the binding fed—that it was designed to feed, drawing power from everything magical within its reach, which was why the old kingdoms had withered, why hedge-witches hid, why the gift itself felt like holding a coal in your chest your whole life long. The binding did not cage the Veil only. It caged *everything*, and called the cage law.

She did not understand all of it. She understood enough.

When Serah came home on the third evening, mud to her knees and tiredness carved deep around her mouth, Rafael was waiting at the table with the grimoire open between them like a wound.

Serah looked at the book. Then at her. For a long moment the older woman's face did something complicated—fear and grief and a terrible weary resignation, all arriving together like weather.

"You read it," Serah said. Not a question.

"You helped do that. To a living man."

"To a thing wearing a man," Serah said, and her voice cracked on it, and Rafael would spend three years wondering whether that crack had been a lie. "Child, you don't know what he was going to become. What it promised him. We were seven old women with a war behind us and nothing left to lose, and we chose the lesser evil, and we have paid for it every day since."

"Then unbind him."

"We can't." Serah's hands flattened on the table. "That's the cruelty of it. The binding takes a witch to make and a witch to break, and breaking it means taking the chains into yourself. Do you understand? Someone has to *wear* it, Rafael. Someone has to lie down in that dark in his place, forever, and no witch alive owes that man enough to—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "No one has volunteered in twenty years. So the book stays shut, and the king stays bound, and the world stays merely cruel instead of ending."

Rafael had looked at her mistress—at the woman who had pulled her half-drowned out of a canal at nine years old, who taught her to knot luck-charms and brew febrifuges, who was the closest thing to family the world had ever offered her—and felt the love in her chest curdle into something colder.

"And the magic it eats," Rafael said. "The witches it burns so the Veil stays fed. Are they the lesser evil too?"

Serah didn't answer. That silence answered.

Rafael packed that night. She took nothing but her mother's knife, a change of clothes, and the luck-knot pattern she could work in her sleep. At the door, Serah caught her wrist—not gripping, just resting there, light as a bird's foot.

"If you run," Serah said quietly, "run all the way. Don't stop in any city under the crown. And Rafael—if you ever meet another witch who asks what you know about bindings, lie. Lie until your tongue bleeds. Because the moment anyone knows what you know, you stop being a girl and start being a key."

Rafael had pulled her wrist free and walked into the marsh dark, and she had lied beautifully for three years.

* * *

"—ael. Rafael."

Odile's voice pulled her back. The tunnel kitchen resolved around her again: the lamp, the ledger, the four faces watching her with varying degrees of alarm. Tam had gone very still, the way he did when the stones told him something. Maretta had reached across the table and covered Rafael's cold hands with her warm scarred ones, and Rafael hadn't even noticed her doing it.

"I'm sorry," Rafael said. "I was—elsewhere."

"You said you know what the script is for," Odile said. Her eye was steady on Rafael's face, patient and unblinking, the eye of a woman who had waited out sieges. "So tell us. All of it. Whatever it costs."

And that was the thing about Odile Voss, Rafael thought. Other people asked questions to trap you. Odile asked them to free you.

So Rafael told them.

She told them about the marsh house and the high shelf and the cord she'd cut. About the diagrams drawn with a loving hand. About *a king who bargained*. She told them about the fight, and the silence that was an answer, and walking out into the reed-dark with her whole life folded into a bundle on her back. When she reached the part about the binding feeding—about the Veil eating magic to keep its chains strong, about the registry and the hunt and the burned shop on Candlemaker's Row being not policy but *appetite*—Maretta made a small sound and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

Jules had uncrossed his arms somewhere along the way. He was staring at the ledger's margins now as if they might bite him.

"The king wrote this," he said slowly. "The real king. Aldric. He's been leaving these in state documents for—how long?"

"Years, maybe," Tam said from the floor. His voice had the distant quality it got when he was translating. "The stone under the palace remembers him writing. It remembers him *asking*." The boy looked up, and his young face was grave beyond its years. "It's not a trap. Stones don't lie about asking."

"No one reaches the palace archive," Jules said, but the fight had gone out of his voice; he was arguing with himself now, not with them. "It's the most warded building in Corvane. It's the *throat* of the thing."

"Then we find the counter-ritual somewhere else," Maretta said. "If Serah helped forge the binding, the method of unmaking it exists. It must exist. Nothing is chained that cannot be—"

"She told me it couldn't be broken," Rafael said quietly. "Only transferred. Someone takes the chains. Someone lies down in the dark in his place." She looked at her own hands, the charm-scarred fingers that had hidden their true strength for three years. "That's why no one's done it. It's not a lock problem. It's a sacrifice problem."

Silence settled over the kitchen, thick as the river silt overhead. Somewhere far up the tunnels, water dripped its slow count.

Odile broke it. She pushed back her chair, crossed to the ledger, and studied the margin-script for a long moment with her single eye.

"Your mistress," she said. "Serah. Is she alive?"

"I don't know. Three years, and I never once looked back." Rafael's throat tightened. "I was afraid if I looked back I'd go home."

"Then we look for her," Odile said simply. "Because either she knows how to unmake this thing, or she knows where the knowledge lives—and either way, she owes this family an answer, and you owe yourself one too, girl. Guilt shared is guilt halved. That's not wisdom, that's arithmetic. I've done the sums."

Rafael laughed—a wet, broken sound she hadn't expected—and Maretta's hand tightened over hers, and Tam leaned his head briefly against her knee the way he did with Odile, and Rafael understood that she had just handed them the worst of herself and the table had not overturned. The walls had not come down. They were all still here, in the warm lamplight, with the burden spread among them like bread.

"But the archive," Jules said, and now he sounded less like a doubter than a planner, which was worse and better. "Even with Serah—even with a counter-ritual written down somewhere—the palace archive holds the original records. The terms of the bargain. Maybe the names of the seven. If we're going to do this, we do it knowing everything, or not at all." He rubbed his jaw. "Someone has to get inside."

"Someone will," Odile said.

"Not someone." Rafael heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it came out. "Me. I'm the one who read the grimoire. I'm the one who knows binding-script when it crawls across a page. If there's a counter-ritual in that archive, I'm the only one who'll recognize it—and if there's a trap written in the same hand, I'm the only one who'll smell it." She met Odile's eye. "You gave me a home. Let me be worth the roof."

For a moment the ex-soldier said nothing. Then the corner of her mouth moved—not quite a smile, but kin to one.

"Worth the roof," she repeated. "Gods help me, I've sheltered worse reasons." She straightened, and the commander came up in her spine like a blade. "Then hear me, all of you. Nobody moves tonight. Tomorrow, Jules finds out when the palace opens its doors to outsiders—feasts, deliveries, petitions, anything. Maretta, I want a list of what we'd need for a journey to the marshlands and back. Tam—" the boy looked up— "you ask the stones under Bellows Street whether there's a way into the palace grounds that doesn't go through a gate."

"And me?" Rafael asked.

Odile's eye found hers, steady and warm and utterly without pity, which was the only kind of mercy Rafael had ever been able to accept.

"You sleep," Odile said. "Tomorrow you start telling me everything that grimoire said, word for word, line for line, until I could recite it drunk. Because if my people are walking into the throat of that thing, they walk in knowing it better than it knows itself."

Later, lying in the narrow bunk that was hers now—hers, with her name unofficially scratched into the wood beside Tam's and Maretta's—Rafael stared at the ceiling stone and felt the guilt shift in her chest. Not lighter, exactly. Just no longer hers alone to carry, the way a heavy beam is lighter when four shoulders take it up together.

She thought of Serah's voice: *lie until your tongue bleeds.* Well. She had bled the truth instead, and the world had not ended, and tomorrow they would begin.

Above her, faint through fathoms of earth and brick, the city murmured—and beneath the murmur, so faint she might have imagined it, Rafael felt something vast turn over in its sleep, and begin, slowly, to pay attention.
