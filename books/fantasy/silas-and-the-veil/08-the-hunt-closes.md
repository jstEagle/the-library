## 8. The Hunt Closes

Odile Voss had learned to sleep the way soldiers sleep: shallow, one ear open, her body cataloguing sounds before her mind ever woke enough to name them. The drip of water through Bellows Street's old brick. The settling groan of the tenements above. Tam's breathing from his corner pallet, quick and birdlike even in dreams. Jules turning over beside her with a grunt, muttering half a word about ledgers.

So when the sound came—the wrong kind of silence, the silence of boots moving in unison where there should have been the scattered shuffle of drunkards and dockworkers—she was awake before her eye was fully open.

She lay still in the dark of their room behind the counting-house, listening. Above them, through three floors of rotting timber, Corvane held its breath.

"Jules," she said quietly.

He was already sitting up. That was one of the thousand small reasons she loved him; he woke like a blade leaving a sheath, no fumbling, no questions. "How many?"

"Too many to be honest and too few to be loud." She swung her legs off the cot and reached for her boots. Her left knee announced its opinion of the hour, as it always did—old shrapnel from a war fought for a king who no longer existed, if he had ever existed at all. "Company strength. Moving in step."

Jules lit no lamp. He didn't need to; twenty years in these tunnels had given him a smuggler's eyes, and hers had been remade by fire into something that saw well enough in the dark. He dressed in silence while Odile went to the door and pressed her palm flat against the wood.

The stone spoke to some people here. To Tam it murmured like an old friend. To Rafael it apparently sang whole histories. To Odile it only ever gave the soldier's truth: vibration, weight, direction. Boots on Bellows Street. More on Tanner's Row. And beneath all of it—a pressure she felt in her back teeth, a wrongness like a struck tuning fork that never quite stopped humming.

They'd felt it for days now, ever since the criers had read the second proclamation. *Registry concluded. Hunt commenced.* As though the first had merely been the polite knock and this was the shoulder against the door.

"They've found the upper entrances," she said.

"You don't know that."

"I know they're not sweeping randomly. I know they've got men on Tanner's Row and Bellows both, and those are our doors." She turned. In the darkness Jules's face was a pale smudge, but she could feel him looking at her, weighing what she would say next, because he knew her too well. "And I know we have eleven people sleeping down the east passage who can't hear boots through forty feet of clay."

* * *

The tunnels beneath Bellows Street had been dug for coal a century ago and abandoned when the seam proved thin, then reclaimed by smugglers, then by Odile, who had found them half-collapsed and full of drowned rats and seen in them the thing she had been looking for since the war ended: ground worth holding.

Now they were home to eleven fugitives besides her own household. A dyer's apprentice whose hands stained everything blue. Two sisters who could start fires with a word and had burned down nothing but their own reputations trying to hide it. An old man who dreamed true and hated it. Maretta, who had come to them with burn scars up both arms from a village that decided healing was witchcraft when it worked too well. Tam, who heard stone speak. Rafael, who claimed to be barely gifted at all and lied about it badly, bless her terrified heart.

Eleven people who would be dead or registered within the week if the Grey Cloaks found the doors.

Odile moved through the passages rousing them, her voice pitched low and hard, the voice that had once moved two hundred soldiers across a frozen ford without a single lamp showing. "Up. Quietly. Take your bundle, leave the rest." She watched them stumble into wakefulness—fear moving through the little camp like cold water through cloth—and she hated herself a little for how practiced they'd all become at this, how quickly hands went to bundles, how children of the hunt learned to dress in the dark.

Maretta was already up, already moving among the others with her healer's calm, pressing a steadying hand here, a whispered word there. She caught Odile's eye across the passage.

"The east shaft?" Maretta asked.

"East shaft to the river gate. Jules takes the lead group. I take rear."

"And you?"

"I'm last out. Always last out." It was not bravado. It was arithmetic. Odile Voss was the slowest runner in the tunnel and everyone knew it, and the rearguard belonged to whoever could best afford to be caught fighting rather than fleeing. She flexed her sword hand, feeling the old ache of it. Twenty years since she'd drawn steel in earnest. She'd hoped to die without doing it again.

Tam appeared at her elbow, small and solemn in the dark, his hand finding hers without looking. The boy navigated blackness better than anyone she'd ever known; the stones told him where everything was, he said, told him stories about the weight of feet above them.

"Auntie Odile." He said it very seriously. He'd started calling her that a month ago and she still hadn't recovered from it. "The walls are scared."

"The walls don't get scared, love."

"These do." His face tipped up toward hers. "There's iron coming down. Lots of iron. Iron makes them go quiet, and when they go quiet all at once—" He swallowed. "It's like when the birds stop singing."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she raised her voice just enough to carry. "Change of plan. We don't wait for the east shaft. Everyone moves *now*. Jules—first group, go, go. Maretta, you're with me and the boy."

Jules kissed her once, fast, his hand cupping the back of her skull the way he did, thumb against her hairline, as though checking she were still solid. "River gate. Don't be a hero."

"I've never been a hero in my life. I'm a quartermaster with a sword."

"That's what worries me. You'll ration yourself into a grave." But he was already moving, gathering the blue-handed boy and the fire-sisters and the dreamer, herding them down the eastern passage with the low urgent murmur of a man who had done this in worse places.

* * *

They nearly made it.

Later—much later, in the weeks after, when she would lie awake replaying every choice like a merchant recounting coins that had all come up counterfeit—Odile would trace the failure back to the tanner's grate. The upper entrance on Tanner's Row had been sealed with rubble and a false wall for eight months. Nobody used it. Nobody should have known it mattered.

But the Grey Cloaks hadn't come down through the entrances at all. They had come down through the *city*, tearing up cellars and wells, following whatever sense it was they followed—that pressure in the teeth, that humming wrongness—and they had opened the tanner's grate from above like surgeons opening a wound.

Odile heard them before she saw them: the particular clink of iron fittings, the disciplined hush. She pushed Maretta and Tam into a side alcove and put her back to the brick, sword out at last, and looked down the passage at the grey shapes emerging from the broken grate with lanterns hooded to slits.

There were six of them. They wore cloaks the color of ash and rain and old cobwebs, and their faces above the collars were ordinary faces—young men mostly, one woman with a jaw like a shovel—and that was the worst part, Odile thought. Not monsters. Just men with iron badges and a warrant signed in a king's hand by something that wasn't a king.

"Halt," said the nearest, and his voice was young too, and uncertain, and that almost made her laugh. Almost.

She considered the geometry. Six Cloaks in a passage built for two. One old soldier with a good blade and bad knees. Behind her, a healer and a child who heard stone speak.

"Maretta," she said softly, without turning her head. "The side passage, thirty paces back, drops to the sump level. Take the boy. Do not wait for me."

"No," Maretta said.

"That wasn't a request, healer."

"And I'm not one of your soldiers." Maretta's voice was calm as still water, which Odile knew meant it was anything but. "You'll need someone to stitch you back together after."

"I need you alive more than stitched."

"And I need you to stop deciding for everyone." Maretta stepped forward, past Odile's shoulder, into the lantern light, and lifted her scarred arms slightly away from her body—not surrender, exactly. Presentation. "I'm the one you want. I'm registered in the parish rolls of Millbrook. Maretta Solenne, hedge-healer, convicted of unlawful craft. There's a price on my head. You can smell it on me, can't you? All that working, soaked into the scars."

The Cloaks' leader shifted. Odile saw the hunger move through him like wind through wheat. Whatever sense they used, it was real, and Maretta was blazing with it—deliberately, Odile realized with a lurch, *deliberately*, the way a woman might flare a lamp in a dark field to draw the moths from a doorway.

"What are you doing," Odile breathed.

"Buying time," Maretta said, not looking at her. "You're always buying time for us, Odile. Let someone else pay once." And then, louder, to the Cloaks, with a healer's gentle authority that seemed to confuse them more than any spell could have: "I'll come quietly. No one needs to bleed tonight. But those two are nothing—old woman, lost child. I found them hiding down here. They're not worth the paperwork."

It was beautifully done. It was so beautifully done that Odile wanted to scream.

The Cloaks conferred in murmurs. The young leader wanted the easy prize; the woman with the shovel-jaw kept glancing past Maretta at Odile, at the sword, at the shape of the alcove, and Odile saw the arithmetic running behind her flat eyes and knew it wouldn't hold. Knew it even as Maretta walked forward into their midst with her wrists offered, even as they bound her with iron-fitted manacles that made her flinch—the iron, always the iron—even as they turned back down the passage toward the grate, dragging their prize.

Knew it, and could do nothing, because the woman with the shovel-jaw stayed behind, and because Tam was crying silently in the dark, and because six lives hung on Odile not being a hero.

"Go," Maretta said over her shoulder, one last time, smiling at Odile with bloody determination. "Stitch yourself this time."

Then the lantern-light swallowed her, and the grate scraped, and she was gone up into the city that burned witches.

* * *

They ran.

Odile took Tam's hand and they went down through the sump level, through water to mid-shin that stank of the tanneries, through passages so low they crawled, and the whole time the boy's grip on her fingers was iron-tight and the whole time she listened for pursuit that mercifully never came. The Cloaks had their prize. Prizes ended hunts, usually. Usually.

At the river gate—a rusted hatch that opened onto the mudflats below the water-stairs—Jules was waiting with the others, and the look on his face when he counted heads and came up one short was a look Odile would carry longer than her scars.

"Where is she?" he asked, though his eyes had already answered him.

"Gone." Odile climbed out onto the mud and stood in the cold river wind with her sword still bare in her hand and realized she was shaking. Not from fear. From the specific fury of a commander whose plan had worked perfectly and saved everyone except the one person who had volunteered to break it. "She gave herself up. Walked into their hands like it was a market transaction. Bought us the passage."

"She what?" Jules came up beside her. Behind them the fugitives huddled against the sea-wall, the blue boy weeping quietly, the fire-sisters holding each other, old Wenna the dreamer staring at the water with tears running freely down her face. "Odile. They'll take her to the Cloak house. You know what happens in the Cloak house."

"I know what happens in the Cloak house."

"They'll work her until she tells them everything. Every entrance. Every name. Every face." His voice cracked on the last word, and Odile understood that he was not only afraid for Maretta. He was afraid of her. Of what love would pull out of a person under iron and pain.

"She won't talk," Odile said.

"Everyone talks."

"Not her." But even as she said it, Odile heard the lie in it, the same lie every soldier tells, and she turned away from Jules and looked north along the river toward the distant bulk of the palace, black against the lesser blackness of the sky, and felt the humming wrongness reach all the way out here, faint as a scar's ache before rain.

*They will take her to the Cloak house,* she thought. *And the Cloak house answers to the palace. And the palace answers to—*

To what? To the king who did not sleep, according to Rafael's white-faced reading of a stolen ledger. To the Veil, whatever that was. To something that ate magic and called the eating law.

"We can't stay on the flats till dawn," Jules said. "The tide."

"I know."

"Odile." He touched her arm. "We have to run. Properly run. Out of Corvane, tonight, while they think the nest is cleared. If we go now—"

"If we go now, we abandon every other soul hiding in this city who heard the same proclamation we did." Her voice came out flat and hard and she couldn't soften it. "The dyers' cellars off Cordwain Lane. The chapel crypt where old Fen shelters three families. The woman under the granary—Rafael knows her, the one with the singing hands. Maretta bought our lives tonight. I am not going to spend them running away."

"And if they take you too? If they take Tam?" Jules's voice dropped to a whisper. "If they take *me*? What's left of this family then, Odile? Ash and a list of names?"

She looked at him. Twenty years, and he still could cut her to the bone with a question, because he aimed them where the armor wasn't.

"Then there's ash and a list of names," she said, "and every name on it will be a person who was free for a little while, because somebody built them a door." She sheathed her sword at last. Her hand shook doing it. "Get them to the salt cellar under the fish market. You know the one. I'm going back in."

"*Odile—*"

"I'm going back in to close the doors they didn't find, and warn the ones they haven't reached yet, and then I'm going to find out where they've taken her." She pulled her cloak's hood up against the river wind. "And Rafael is going to tell me everything she knows about kings and Veils and binding-script, because I am done hiding from this hunt. It found us anyway. So we stop being prey."

Behind her, in the dark, Tam's small voice said, "The stones say she's still alive, Auntie Odile. The stones say the iron hasn't won yet."

Odile closed her eye. Opened it again.

"Then we'd better be quick," she said, and turned toward the burning city.
