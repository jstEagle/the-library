## 9. Bargaining with Thieves

The message came by way of a dead drop behind the tannery on Sallow Row: a strip of paper no wider than a finger, folded eight times, smelling of ink and something older beneath it—smoke, maybe, or the memory of smoke. Odile read it once, read it again, and then set it down on the tunnel table as though it might bite.

"Mother Quill wants a meeting," she said. "Tomorrow. Midnight. The Gilded Cage."

Jules's chair scraped back an inch, all of him gone rigid. "No."

"It wasn't a question."

"It was a *trap*." He said the word like he was spitting out gristle. "You don't walk into the Cage. You don't even look at the Cage. You know what her people did to my—"

"I know." Odile's voice was quiet in the way that made everyone else go quiet too. Her single eye found Jules and held him there. "I know what you lost. I've never once asked you to forget it. But we need passage into the palace district, Jules, and there is exactly one person in Corvane who can get us through the Feast Night checkpoints without our throats opened. That person is Mother Quill. And Mother Quill does not grant favors."

Rafael sat at the far end of the table, hands wrapped around a cup of chicory that had long since gone cold, and said nothing. She had learned that much in three years of hiding: silence was armor. But Odile turned to her anyway, because Odile always knew where the weight of a decision actually rested.

"They want you," Odile said. "Not me. Not any of us. The note names you. *The charm-witch of Bellows Street.*"

Rafael looked down into her cup. The chicory had a skin on it, thin and grey, like something drowned.

*They see me,* she thought, and the old dread rose up its familiar spine. *Three years of being small, of being nothing, of selling knots and buttons and harmless little lies—and they still see me.*

"Then I'll go," she said, and heard how steady her voice came out, and hated how easy the lie of steadiness had become.

* * *

The Gilded Cage was not a cage at all, or not only one. It had been a bird-seller's warehouse once, before the ban, when Corvane still allowed small bright things for pleasure rather than only for war. Now the cages hung empty from the rafters in their hundreds, brass and iron, catching candlelight and throwing it back broken, so that walking beneath them felt like wading through a shoal of cold fish. Rafael counted her heartbeats instead of the cages. It helped. A little.

Mother Quill received her in a room above the warehouse floor, behind a door flanked by two women with crossbows held low and ready. The crime-lord herself was smaller than Rafael had imagined—a compact woman in her sixties with silver-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared, on close inspection, to be actual quills—and she sat behind a desk built from the hull of some wrecked ship, its wood still bearing the pale scars of salt.

"The charm-witch." Mother Quill's voice was dry paper. "Sit."

Rafael sat. The chair across from the desk was deliberately lower than the crime-lord's own. She noted it, filed it away, refused to let it show.

"You've been careful," Mother Quill said. "Three years in my city, selling your little knots and buttons, and you never once crossed me, never once shorted a payment, never once drew the crown's eye until the crown drew it for you. Do you know how rare that is? Carefulness?"

"I imagine it's rarer than talent."

Something moved at the corner of Mother Quill's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, perhaps—the shape a smile leaves in ash.

"The Grey Cloaks took four of my people last week," she said. "Two of them were mine in name only, but two were mine in truth, and I do not forgive debts against my own. The Veil's hunters are bad for business. Bad for *all* business." She laced her fingers together atop the wrecked-ship desk. "So. You want into the palace district on Feast Night. I can put you inside a wine delivery, past three checkpoints, through the servants' gate. I can give you faces that will pass inspection and papers that will pass better than inspection. And in exchange—"

"You want a charm of true luck."

Mother Quill's eyes sharpened. "You know what I want."

"I know what the rumors say you want. There's a difference." Rafael kept her hands flat on her knees, kept her breathing even. "True luck isn't a knot tied right or a coin carried in the left shoe. It's a working. It reaches into the thread of a life and *bends* it. And every bending has a cost, and the cost comes out of the witch who works it. That's the price. That's always been the price."

"And you're telling me this why? To raise your fee?"

"To make sure you understand what you're buying." Rafael leaned forward, just slightly, enough to make the smaller woman's eyes narrow. "Because if you think you're purchasing a trinket, you'll treat it like a trinket, and when it costs more than you expected you'll come looking for someone to blame. I'm telling you the price so there's no debt between us afterward. No debt means no reason for your people to find me in an alley."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, a cage swayed on its chain and knocked softly against another, a sound like distant bells mourning something.

"You have your mistress's tongue," Mother Quill said at last. "Serah of the marshes. The hedge-witch who taught half the cunning folk of the river country before the ban, and who vanished off the face of the earth." Her gaze was a fishhook, patient and barbed. "Yes, witch. I know whose student you are. I know everything that happens in my city. Including—" and here her voice dropped, went almost gentle, which was infinitely worse, "—including what happened in the palace twenty years ago. Including what sleeps under the throne."

Rafael's blood went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Mother Quill agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Yet." She unfolded her hands. "Here are my terms. One working of true luck, worked on a vessel of my choosing, strong enough to turn a death into a survival. In exchange: passage for two into the palace district on Feast Night, papers, faces, and my silence about the tunnels on Bellows Street—for a season. Do we bargain?"

Rafael thought of Tam's hand, knitted whole beneath her palms. Thought of Maretta's cell, wherever it was. Thought of the ledger page with the king's coded plea hidden in its margins, and the binding-script that matched Serah's grimoire stroke for stroke.

"One condition of mine," she said. "When the Grey Cloaks come—and they'll come, whatever you trade me—you warn the tunnels first. An hour's warning. That's part of the price."

Mother Quill studied her for a long moment, and this time the smile arrived properly, thin and sharp as a blade drawn slow.

"Oh, I like you," she said. "Terms accepted. Come back in three days with the vessel ready. And charm-witch—" Rafael paused at the door. "Wear gloves. What you're about to work will show in your hands."

* * *

She told them that night, all of it, around the tunnel table while the lamps burned low. The true-luck working. The cost. The three days.

"A true-luck charm," Maretta said softly, and crossed herself in the old way, thumb to sternum, though the gods she'd been raised on had been outlawed longer than magic had. "Rafael, those stories aren't superstition. My grandmother saw one worked, when she was a girl. The witch who made it—" She stopped. Started again. "It aged her ten years in an hour. Some say it killed her."

"It won't kill me."

"You don't *know* that."

"I know what I can carry." Rafael met the healer's eyes across the table. "And I know what we're carrying already. Maretta, they have you in a cell somewhere. They have Tam. Every day we wait, the Cloaks dig deeper into the tunnels, and every day Aldric sits on that throne signing death warrants with a hand that isn't his own. If a working can bend one death into one survival—if it buys us the road to the binding-stone—then I'll pay what it costs."

"You always say that," Jules said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't spoken through the whole discussion; he'd sat with his arms crossed and his jaw set, wearing the expression he wore when he was deciding whether a newcomer was worth the air they breathed. Now he stood, and his voice came out rough.

"You always say *I'll pay what it costs*, like cost is a purse you can count out and be done with. Like it doesn't keep coming after." He jabbed a finger toward the table, toward all of them. "I watched cost eat this family once already. I'm not watching it eat you too."

"Then help me do it right," Rafael said.

He stared at her. "What?"

"You heard me. If I'm going to work something this dangerous, I'm not doing it alone in a corner hoping nobody notices when I collapse. I need someone watching the thresholds. Someone who knows when a working starts pulling harder than it should. Someone stubborn enough to drag me out of it if I go under." She held his gaze. "You hate Mother Quill. Fine. Hate her. But you said you'd watched cost eat this family. So you know its teeth better than anyone here. Help me."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the tunnel mouth. For a moment Jules looked, not angry, but young—caught between grief and something he didn't have a name for yet.

"Quill's people burned my brother's shop," he said finally, quieter. "Before the ban, when it was just thieves stealing from thieves. He owed them money. They decided the debt was worth more as a lesson than as coin." His jaw worked. "I swore I'd never lift a finger to help that woman. And now you're asking me to stand guard while you hand her the thing that keeps her alive."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you're not doing it for her." Rafael reached across the table and, before she could lose her nerve, laid her hand over his fist. His knuckles were scarred; his fingers were cold. "You're doing it for the family she'd burn next. For Tam. For Maretta. For the people in the tunnels who'll get an hour's warning because I bargained it into the price. You're doing it for *us*, Jules. Quill's just the road we have to walk."

He didn't pull his hand away. That was answer enough, or near enough.

"Three days," he muttered. "If you so much as *tremble* wrong during this working, I'm pulling you out whether you finish or not. Swear it."

"I swear."

"And Rafael." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw that behind the distrust there had always been a watchman's care, patient and unsentimental and fierce. "Thank you. For not lying to us about the cost. People lie about cost. It's how they get you."

* * *

They worked it in the deep gallery, the oldest part of the tunnel system, where the stone had been cut before Corvane had a name and the walls sweated mineral light. Odile stood at the gallery mouth with a loaded crossbow, not because she expected Cloaks but because standing guard was how she loved things. Maretta prepared a pallet and a basin of watered wine. Tam, released and hollow-eyed and refusing to speak of his cell, insisted on sitting nearby because, he said, the stones here told him they liked Rafael, and he wanted to hear what they said while she worked.

Rafael rolled up her sleeves and set the vessel on the cloth before her: a small silver bell, no bigger than her thumb, that Mother Quill's messenger had delivered that morning along with instructions. *For my granddaughter,* the note said. *She sails for the free ports in a month. Keep her alive on the water.* Even thieves, it seemed, had granddaughters. Even monsters had soft places, and Rafael supposed that was either the world's mercy or its cruelest joke.

"Tell me what to watch for," Jules said, crouched beside her.

"When the working takes hold, my breathing goes shallow. That's normal. If it stops—that's not. If my lips go grey, if my hands start shaking and won't stop, if I say a word that isn't part of the working—" she looked at him, "—break the circle. Break it however you have to. Don't ask permission. Don't be gentle."

"Don't be gentle," he repeated. "Right. I can manage that."

She smiled despite everything. Then she closed her eyes, laid both bare hands on the silver bell, and began.

A true-luck working was not cast so much as *woven*. Rafael reached into the bell's cold metal and found the threads waiting—not the bell's threads, but the granddaughter's, the life the bell would ride in, faint and far away across the water, a girl of perhaps sixteen with salt in her hair and her grandmother's stubborn chin. Rafael gathered those threads the way Serah had taught her to gather anything living: gently, gently, as if holding a bird that might decide to die of fright.

Then she began to bend them.

The cost came immediately, the way it always did, like cold water flooding into a boot. It started in her fingertips—a numbness, a fading, as though her hands belonged to someone farther away than the ends of her arms. She fed the working from her own reserves, drawing up power the way one draws water from a deep well, and felt the level drop, and drop, and drop. Around her the gallery dimmed, though no lamp had gone out; the mineral light in the walls seemed to lean away from her, wary.

*Bend the thread here,* she told herself, *and here, and here. The storm that would have found her hull—turn it aside. The fever she would have caught in port—turn it aside. The rope that would have frayed, the reef that would have risen, the knife in the tavern in the third port—*

The knife stopped her.

Bending a death was nothing like bending weather. Weather was indifferent; it could be persuaded. Death had *weight*, and intention, and when Rafael seized the thread of that particular ending and hauled it sideways, the ending hauled back. She felt her own thread shudder in sympathy, felt the working reach hungrily past her reserves into deeper stores she hadn't known she had—into the marrow-things, the root-things, the place where gift lived.

*Rafael.* Tam's voice, very far away. *The stones say you're going grey.*

She couldn't answer. Her lips wouldn't move. The working had her now, and it pulled, and she gave, and somewhere beyond the roaring in her ears she heard Jules shouting her name, heard Odile's boots, heard—

Hands. Strong hands closing on her shoulders, breaking her posture, dragging her backward out of the weave. The bell rang once, high and clear and impossibly sweet, and fell silent forever, its work done. Rafael came back to herself on the cold stone floor with Jules's arm braced behind her shoulders and Maretta pressing wine to her lips, and the first thing she saw was her own hands.

They were old.

Not ancient—not ruined—but older. The fine skin over her knuckles had loosened. Two new lines scored themselves across her left palm, deep and permanent, the record of a death deferred. She stared at them, and the gallery tilted, and she laughed—a cracked, exhausted, half-hysterical sound.

"Well," she managed. "That's going to complicate the disguise."

"Drink," Maretta ordered, and Rafael drank.

Odile crouched down beside them, and for a moment her one eye moved over Rafael's face with an expression she usually kept for wounded soldiers and lost children. "Report," she said, because tenderness, in Odile Voss, always wore the uniform of duty.

"She lives," Rafael whispered. "The granddaughter. Whatever the sea meant to take from her, it won't now. The bell will keep her." She swallowed, tasted iron. "And Quill keeps her word. I could see it in her, Odile. Under all the brass and the cages—she keeps her word. We sail into the palace district in a wine cart, and no one in that warehouse will breathe a word about Bellows Street."

"For a season," Odile said.

"For a season." Rafael let her head fall back against Jules's arm, too spent to hold it up, and found she didn't mind. "After that, with any luck, none of us will need hiding places at all."

Jules snorted. "Luck."

"Exactly." She closed her eyes. "I'm rather an expert on the subject now."

They let her rest, then, there in the old stone gallery with the family gathered close—Maretta counting her pulse, Tam whispering to the walls, Odile standing watch, Jules staying exactly where he was, arm still braced behind her shoulders, having apparently decided that letting go could wait until morning.

But sleep, when it finally took her, brought a dream: the binding-script from Serah's grimoire, glowing in the dark like veins of fire in black rock, and beneath it, in handwriting she had not seen in three years and knew instantly, a single line added in fresh ink:

*The counter-ritual is incomplete. Come to me before Feast Night, child. Before you unmake what I helped make — there is a piece you do not have, and the Veil knows you're coming.*
