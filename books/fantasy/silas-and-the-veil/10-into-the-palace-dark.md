## 10. Into the Palace Dark

The masks were Mother Quill's idea, or rather her price. Two faces of lacquered paper, a falcon for Jules and a moth for Rafael, gray and silver and utterly forgettable—the kind of masks that servants wore to feasts they weren't invited to, so that no one would look twice at them carrying wine through the crowd.

"Remember," Jules murmured as they joined the river of guests flowing up the palace steps, "you're nobody. Nobody is the safest thing to be in Corvane tonight."

"I've been practicing for three years," Rafael said.

He glanced at her sidelong, and even behind the falcon's beak she could see the ghost of his crooked half-smile. It was strange, how quickly she had come to read him—three weeks ago he had barely tolerated her presence, and now his shoulder brushed hers in the crowd like a promise: *I'm here. I'll get you out.*

The palace of Corvane had been built by men who wanted the world to know they were not afraid of anything. Its façade climbed white and gold against the evening sky, its windows blazed with a thousand candles, and from within came music—strings and horns, bright and relentless, the sound of a city pretending nothing was burning.

But Rafael felt it before she crossed the threshold.

It rolled over her like cold water off a glacier, so thick she stumbled on the second step and had to catch Jules's arm. The stones themselves were *saturated*—soaked through with hunger the way a sponge is soaked with water, every block and pillar and flagstone dripping with an appetite so old and vast that it had stopped feeling like appetite at all and become simply the texture of the place. She had sensed magic before, small warm things: Serah's hearth-charms, Tam's murmuring stones, her own gift humming under her skin like a struck bell. This was none of those. This was a bell the size of the sky, rung once, long ago, still ringing.

*Rafael.* Jules's grip tightened on her arm, steadying her. His voice came low through the mask. "What is it?"

"It's everywhere." She kept her lips barely moving, her eyes forward, smiling at nothing the way one did at feasts. "The whole building. It's—it's drinking."

"Drinking what?"

"Everything. Everyone." She swallowed. "Keep walking."

They walked.

* * *

Inside, the great hall was a sea of silk and candlelight, and Rafael had never seen anything so beautiful or so wrong.

The guests danced and laughed and toasted, but their laughter had a strange quality, like echoes arriving a half-beat late. And the servants—gods, the servants. They glided between the tables with trays held perfectly level, their faces smooth and empty as eggs, their eyes fixed on some middle distance no one else could see. A woman refilled a lord's cup; her hand never trembled; her face never changed. A boy gathered plates, dozens of them, moving with the fluid economy of a marionette whose puppeteer had grown very skilled over many years.

Rafael watched a serving girl stand motionless beside a pillar for the length of an entire song. Not resting. Not waiting. Just *standing*, candlelight pooling in the hollows of her cheeks, until some silent command released her and she drifted back into motion.

"They're not asleep," Rafael whispered. Her mouth had gone dry. "They're awake inside. I can feel it—a little flicker, way down deep, like someone knocking on a door from the other side."

"You're saying the king does this to them?"

"The king doesn't do anything." The words came out colder than she intended. "That's the whole point of him."

A chamberlain in silver livery beckoned them toward the service corridor with two fingers, and Rafael understood suddenly why Quill's masks worked: the palace expected hired help, and hired help was invisible, and invisibility was the one magic Rafael had spent three years perfecting. She lifted a tray of wine cups from a sideboard because a tray made hands look purposeful, and Jules fell into step beside her, and together they became furniture among furniture.

The corridors behind the great hall were quieter, lit at long intervals, hung with portraits of dead kings. Rafael counted doorways, matching them to the map Odile had drawn from a bribed laundress's memory: the council chambers there, the royal apartments beyond the guarded stair, and—

"There." Jules tipped his chin toward an unassuming door of dark oak, iron-banded, set into the wall like an afterthought. No guard. Why would anyone guard a library? Books could not assassinate anyone. Books could only tell the truth, and the Veil had evidently decided that was no threat at all.

Or had been content, until now.

Jules tried the handle. Locked. He produced two thin pieces of bent steel from his sleeve—he was a smuggler first, a skeptic second, and a lockpick always—and knelt, working by feel, his breath shallow. Rafael stood over him holding the wine tray like a shield, watching both ends of the corridor, feeling the hunger in the walls press closer, curious, patient, a whale circling a rowboat.

The lock clicked.

"In," Jules breathed. "Quickly."

* * *

The royal library was vast and cold and smelled of dust and vellum and, beneath it all, faintly, of rot.

Moonlight fell through high leaded windows in pale blades. Shelves rose three stories into the dark, ladders on brass rails, and the silence here was different from the silence of the hall—not the silence of pretense but the silence of neglect. Rafael set down the tray with trembling hands and let her gift unfurl, just a little, just enough to taste the air.

Here the saturation thinned. Not gone—nothing in this palace was ever free of it—but diluted, the way blood dilutes in a river. The books had been warded once, generations ago, by librarians who understood what they were keeping company with. The wards were nearly dead now, threads of old intention fraying in the dark, but they had held a pocket of clean air, and Rafael breathed it in like a drowning woman.

"Odile said the archive of state rituals would be in the east range," Jules whispered. "Restricted stacks. Look for the seal of Aldric's grandfather."

They split the aisle between them, running fingers along spines. Most of the books were histories, genealogies, tax ledgers stretching back centuries—the tedious machinery of rule. But the restricted stacks, behind a gate of black iron, held stranger things. Grimoires chained to their shelves. Folios wrapped in oilcloth. One shelf held nothing but jars of preserved things Rafael chose not to examine.

Her fingers found it by accident—or perhaps not by accident. Perhaps the book had been waiting. A slim volume bound in gray leather, unmarked except for a sigil stamped into the cover that made her heart stop and then start again, hammering.

She knew that sigil.

She had seen it drawn in candle smoke on Serah's kitchen table, seen it inked into the margins of a grimoire she had burned in her nightmares a hundred times. The binding-script. The same looping, interlocking geometry that caged a living soul inside a bargain.

"Jules." Her voice cracked. "I found it."

He was at her side in three strides. Together they carried the book to the window, into the moonlight, and Rafael opened it with the care one gives a sleeping animal.

The early pages were Aldric's grandfather's hand—proud, precise, a king cataloguing the protections of his house. Then the handwriting changed, younger, more hurried, and Rafael realized with a lurch that these later pages were *Aldric's own*. The king had studied this book. Had annotated it. In the margin beside a diagram of the binding-stone, in a cramped and desperate script, someone had written:

*It grows stronger each year. The scribes think my hand shakes with age. I am thirty-nine years old. If any loyal soul reads this: the stone beneath the throne. Everything is the stone.*

"He's been leaving messages," Rafael whispered. "For years. Praying someone would find this book."

"And no one did." Jules's jaw was tight behind the falcon mask. "Because no one looks at libraries when there are executions to watch."

Rafael turned the pages faster now, hunting. There—the rite of binding, laid out in full, and her stomach turned as she read it: the bargain struck in blood, the entity summoned and given a throne to inhabit, a body to wear, a kingdom to feed upon. And Serah's name was not written anywhere, but the *hand* was there, in the ritual's architecture, in choices of phrasing that Rafael had memorized against her will during three years of trying to forget. Her mistress had helped write this. Had helped build the cage. And the cage had become the kingdom.

Then, near the end, in Aldric's increasingly ragged hand: the counter-ritual.

*Unbinding of the Stone and Soul.*

Rafael read it once. Then again, slower, because the first time her eyes had refused to truly see.

The unbinding required three things. The binding-stone itself, beneath the throne. A witch of sufficient power to work the severance. And—

And the chains, once loosed from the king, could not simply fall away. Chains did not vanish. Chains *transferred*. The rite demanded a vessel: a soul strong enough to take the binding entire into itself, to hold the Veil's tether in flesh and spirit, indefinitely, perhaps forever. The text was explicit, merciless in its old formal language:

*Let the witch who works the unbinding understand: the binding must go somewhere. Into stone, if stone be prepared across a lifetime. Into another vessel freely offered. Or into herself—for the worker of the rite stands closest, and the chains will know her hand, and will settle upon her as a cloak settles upon the shoulders nearest the door.*

"No," Jules said.

She hadn't spoken aloud. She realized she'd gone white, that the book was shaking slightly in her hands, that Jules had read the passage over her shoulder and understood it faster than she had, because Jules always understood costs faster than anyone.

"No," he said again, and took the book gently from her grip. "We find another way. We prepare a stone. We find a vessel. We—"

"It says *freely offered*." Rafael heard her own voice as if from far away. "You can't force someone into this. It has to be given. Who gives their soul to hold a thing like that, Jules? Who *volunteers*?"

Silence. Somewhere far above, the music swelled and faded, swelled and faded, a heartbeat belonging to someone else's body.

"We'll figure it out," Jules said finally, and his voice was rough in a way she'd never heard. "That's what we do. That's what Odile always says—we figure it out, because the alternative is letting people burn. But you are not—" He stopped. Started again. "You've already given too much. The luck-charm for Quill. Healing Odile. You're still hollowed out; I can see it. You don't get to be the one who—"

The candles went out.

Not all of them. Just the ones near the door—a soft exhale of flame, six points of light snuffed in perfect unison, and the darkness beyond the doorway seemed to lean inward, attentive.

Rafael's skin prickled from wrist to nape. The hunger in the stones, which had been ambient, diffuse, a weather—had *turned*. Like an eye opening somewhere in the fabric of the building. Like a vast attention sliding along the corridors, tasting the air, and finding, for the first time in twenty years, a flavor it recognized.

Magic. Hers. Unshielded, unwary, spilled across the library like perfume.

"Jules," she breathed. "It knows we're here."

"What knows—"

The doors at the far end of the hall, thirty yards distant, swung open without a sound.

Down the length of the shelves, framed in the doorway, stood a figure in a gray cloak. And though Rafael could not see the face beneath the hood, she felt it looking at her—felt it the way one feels a hand on the back of the neck—and beneath the terror, absurdly, she heard Tam's voice in her memory, small and certain: *the stones talk to me, Rafi. They say the palace is hungry. They say it's waking up.*

The figure stepped into the library. Behind it, faint and terrible, came the sound of footsteps—many footsteps, unhurried, coming down the corridor like a tide.

Jules slammed the grimoire shut and thrust it inside his coat. "Back window," he whispered. "Now."

Rafael ran.
