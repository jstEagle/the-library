## 11. The Thing Wearing the Crown

Aldric had learned, over twenty years, to measure his captivity in small humiliations.

It was not the death warrants—though he had signed so many that the wax had become a kind of weather to him, red rain falling on parchment, season after season. It was not even the hunger, which was worse than any hunger of the flesh: the Veil's appetite moving through him like a tide, drawing up through the stones of the palace, through the roots of the mountain itself, drinking whatever thin magic still lived in Corvane's shadows. No. The truest humiliation was smaller than all that. It was this: he could no longer feel his own face.

He knew it existed. He saw it in mirrors when the thing allowed him mirrors—a broad, grey-streaked man with a king's bearing and a stranger's eyes. But the feeling of it, the warmth of his own skin, had gone out of him decades ago, the way a limb goes numb and then goes absent entirely. When the Veil smiled with his mouth, Aldric felt nothing. When it wept—which it did sometimes, theatrically, at funerals, at coronations, at the burning of witches—he felt nothing then either. His body had become a glove, and someone else wore it.

Tonight the glove was being dressed for a feast.

He stood—or rather, it stood, and he rode within it like a passenger rides a horse it cannot steer—in the high chamber where servants laid out the ceremonial regalia. Silver mask, chased with night-blooming vine patterns. The black doublet stitched with thread that had been dyed in something Aldric preferred not to think about. And the crown, always last, always lowered onto his head by Chancellor Veyra's white-gloved hands, as though crowning were a mercy and not a muzzle.

*There is a witch in the palace,* the Veil said.

It did not speak in words, exactly. It spoke in certainties. The knowledge simply arrived inside him, fully formed, the way you know your own name. But after twenty years Aldric had learned to translate its certainties back into language, because language was one of the few territories it had never bothered to take from him. It found his inner voice tedious. It let him keep it the way a man lets a spider keep her web in a corner of the cellar.

*A witch,* he answered, careful to make the thought small and dull, a pebble among pebbles. *Here? How alarming.*

Do not play at irony with me. It is young. It is frightened. Its power is— the certainty paused, and Aldric felt the thing taste the air of the palace, tasting through his tongue, through the stone, through the hundred wards woven into the walls. *Old. Older than its years. It has been hiding.*

Something in Aldric went very still. He had learned to keep his stillness deep, below the level where the Veil fished. But hope was a difficult thing to hide; it glowed. He thought of the coded messages he had left in the margins of state documents for fifteen years—the little acrostics, the inverted seals, the binding-script copied into shipping ledgers and grain inventories, prayers folded into bureaucracy. Most men would have called it madness. A prisoner scratching tally-marks on his cell wall. But he had never stopped believing that somewhere in the dark beneath his kingdom there might be one pair of eyes that read closely enough.

Is it one of yours? he wondered, and hated himself for the wondering, because if it was—if some poor girl or boy had come following his scribbled pleas—then he had lured them into the wolf's den with his own hand.

The Veil did not answer. It rarely answered questions about itself. It slid into the silver mask, and Aldric felt the cold weight of it settle over his features, and then Chancellor Veyra was bowing them out of the chamber and down toward the feast, and the king of Corvane went to hunt.

* * *

The Great Hall blazed with a thousand candles, and Aldric hated every one of them.

Not because of the light. Because of what the light cost. Every candle on the long tables, every torch in every sconce, was tallow rendered from fat that should have fed the poor districts, wax that should have been sold to shrines, and the feast itself—this was the part that made him want to scream, though his throat was only ever borrowed for state speeches—was theater. The Veil held feasts the way a farmer holds feed out in winter: not from generosity but to draw things in. Magic clung to celebration. Joy was a scent, and the Veil hunted by scent. Somewhere in that glittering crowd, behind some half-mask, there would be a hedge-witch who could not help blessing the wine, a charm-seller whose fingers itched to knot a luck-thread for a pretty stranger. They came to feasts because they were hungry too, hungry for a single evening of ordinary delight, and the Veil set its table for exactly that hunger.

Aldric sat at the high table and watched his own hands lift a goblet he could not taste.

The music was a pavane. The dancers turned. Chancellor Veyra leaned close to murmur about the eastern garrisons, and Aldric hung on the words the way a drowning man hangs on driftwood—not for their content but because they proved that somewhere under the mask, under the possession, the machinery of a human kingdom still turned, still required a chancellor's voice, still had business that was merely business. As long as the paperwork continued, he told himself, Corvane was not yet dead. Only sick. Only dreaming in chains.

Then the music faltered.

It was nothing anyone else noticed—one violinist missing a beat, a dancer turning her head toward a door as though she'd heard her name. But Aldric felt it go through the hall like a fish through water: the Veil's attention, which had been spread wide and lazy over the whole glittering room, snapping suddenly narrow. Focused. A hound lifting its head.

*There,* the certainty said, and Aldric felt his own legs begin to move.

He rose from the high table. Veyra fell silent mid-sentence. The Veil walked him along the edge of the hall, unhurried, gracious, pausing to touch a countess's shoulder, to exchange three polished sentences with the ambassador from Vell—and all the while its attention streamed ahead of the body like a lantern-beam through fog, narrowing, narrowing, toward the service corridor beyond the minstrels' gallery. Toward the library stair.

Toward the royal library.

Aldric's heart—if he still had a heart, if the muscle in his chest still belonged to him in any way that mattered—lurched. The library. His library. The place where he had spent the first ten years of his captivity searching, secretly, desperately, through every text on binding and unbinding, before the Veil had grown suspicious and begun to watch him read. The counter-ritual, if it existed anywhere, existed there. And now something was in those stacks tonight, tonight of all nights, small and quick and terrified, and the Veil was walking him toward it with his own feet.

*Please,* he thought, and did not care anymore whether the thing heard him. He had passed shame long ago. *Please be nobody. Please be a servant stealing candlesticks. Please be a thief, a lover, a drunkard—anything but—*

But he had felt the Veil's attention sharpen, and he knew what it sharpened for. Twenty years of living inside the hunter had taught him the shape of its hungers. It had scented a witch. And it was going to use his body, his crown, his smiling masked face, to run her down.

They descended the library stair together, king and captor, and the candles guttered as they passed.

* * *

The royal library at night was a cathedral of shadow. Shelves rose four levels high, iron galleries circling the walls, and the books breathed their old-paper breath into the dark. Aldric loved this room more than he had ever loved anything, and the Veil knew it, and that was why the Veil kept it locked except when it wanted to hurt him.

His feet moved silently across the mosaic floor. Ahead, between the shelves of the law section, something shifted—a whisper of fabric, the softest intake of breath. The Veil stopped. Let the silence stretch. It enjoyed this part; Aldric could feel its pleasure the way you feel heat off a brazier. It liked fear the way gourmands liked rare vintages. It liked to let the prey hear the footsteps stop.

Then it rounded the shelf corner, and there she was.

A young woman, dark-skinned, dark-haired, pressed back against a case of tax codices with her arms full of stolen paper. Her eyes went wide above her half-mask. And Aldric, looking out through eyes that were not his own, felt something crack open in him that had been sealed for twenty years.

Because he knew that look. He had worn it once, in a marsh at nineteen years old, when the thing that had promised him salvation showed him its true face. That look said: *I am seeing something I cannot survive.* That look said: *the world just broke.*

She had papers in her arms. He could not read them upside down, but he could see the diagrams—the circles, the chained sigils—and his mind, his poor caged human mind, leapt at them like a dog at a gate. Binding-script. She had found binding-script. She had come for—

*For me,* he thought, wild and hopeless. *Oh, you fool. Oh, brave fool. You came for me.*

The Veil spoke with his voice, and the sound of it filled the library like a landslide filling a valley.

"Little thief."

The woman flinched. The Veil let the words hang, savoring, and then it began to walk toward her, slow, letting the silver mask catch the candlelight, letting her see the crown above it. Aldric screamed inside himself, threw everything he had against the walls of his own skull, tried to seize his own arm, his own tongue, his own breath—*stop, stop, she's just a child, she's—*

And then, impossibly, he felt it.

Her gaze met his. Not the mask. His. Through the silver, through the possession, through twenty years of frost, her eyes found his eyes, and she looked at him the way no one had looked at him since the binding—searching, horrified, compassionate. And in that instant of contact, something of him leaked through. He didn't know how. Perhaps terror thinned the Veil's grip; perhaps pity was a kind of key. But he felt himself rise to the surface of his own face like a drowning man breaking water, and he poured everything into the only channel he had left:

*Run. Run now. Don't fight it. RUN.*

Whether she heard it in his eyes or simply understood what any sensible person understood—that the King of Corvane was walking toward her wearing a smile like a knife—she bolted.

She was fast. She was cleverer than fast: she hurled a shelf-end of scrolls into the aisle behind her, ducked through the gap between two cases, went low where he would have to go around. The Veil pursued without hurry, without anger. It swept the fallen scrolls aside with one regal hand and walked, and its walking was more terrible than running, because it communicated, in every step, the absolute certainty that she could not escape. Prey that ran was prey already caught. It had all night. It had all the corridors of the palace, and every door in those corridors answered to the crown.

Aldric rode inside the hunt and wept, silently, in the only place he could—inside.

* * *

She led them down through the gallery levels, past the map room, past the portrait hall where the faces of his ancestors watched with painted indifference. Twice she nearly made the stairs. Twice the Veil turned him with terrible ease, cutting angles, herding, patient as erosion. And all the while it grew stronger, feeding on her fear, and Aldric felt its pleasure thicken in his veins like honey, and he hated it with a hatred so pure and so old that it had gone beyond burning into something geological.

In the portrait hall she made her stand.

She had backed against the far wall, cornered at last, and her hands were moving—quick, desperate gestures, and Aldric felt the air change. Charms. Stored workings, knotted into threads and tokens, the hoarded arsenal of a hiding witch spending everything at once. The first charm detonated in a bloom of light that seared even the Veil's borrowed retinas; the second wrapped his legs in bands of clinging golden force; the third sang a high broken note that cracked two panes of glass in the windows.

The Veil walked through all of it.

That was the horror Aldric had never managed to make peace with, in twenty years: how little the world's magic meant to the thing that ate magic. Her charms broke against it like waves on a cliff. He felt each one dissolve against his skin, felt the Veil drink them, felt it grow fatter and more delighted, and he understood that she was not fighting it—she was feeding it, and she did not yet know.

"No," he thought, straining toward her with everything that remained of his will. "No, child, don't give it more—"

But she had nothing else. He could see it in her face—the arithmetic of desperation, the last tokens coming out of her pockets. And the Veil reached the center of the hall and stopped, and let the silver mask tilt, and said in Aldric's gentlest voice, the voice it used for pardons and for traps:

"You came so far. Show me what you found."

And she—gods, she was brave—she lifted the stolen pages slightly, as if she might actually bargain, and in that moment her eyes met his again, and this time there was no mistaking it. This time she saw him. He felt the contact like a hand gripping his wrist through twenty years of stone. Her expression changed. The terror stayed, but something else arrived beside it, something that looked almost like grief, and her lips parted, and though no sound came, he watched the shape of the word form on her face:

*You're still in there.*

Yes, he screamed at her, silently, with all that he was. Yes. I am here. I have been here for twenty years. I am the king and I am the prisoner and I am so sorry, so sorry, that my messages brought you to this—

The Veil felt the leak. He felt it notice—the sudden cold attention turning inward, checking its boundaries, finding him pressed against the wall of himself like a man against a cell door. And in the half-second of its distraction, the girl moved.

She snatched a candelabrum from the wall and hurled it—not at him, at the great window, and the leaded glass burst outward into the night, and cold wind roared into the hall, scattering papers, snuffing candles, plunging everything into blue darkness. In the darkness she ran. He heard her boots on the gallery stairs, heard a door bang, heard the small human sounds of flight diminishing down through the palace bones.

The Veil stood in the ruined window-light and did not pursue.

That was the worst part. Aldric had expected rage, pursuit, the hunt spilling out into the streets. Instead the thing simply stood, and he felt it thinking, and its thinking was vast and slow and utterly without mercy, the way winter thinks.

*Let it run,* the certainty came at last. *It has read the pages. It will seek the counter-working. And when it comes to the binding-stone—it will come to us. Everything that grows comes to the root.*

And then, softer, in a tone Aldric had not heard from it in many years—a tone almost like curiosity, almost like unease:

*Its eyes reached you. That should not be possible.*

The Veil turned his body from the shattered window and walked him back toward the feast, unhurried, composing apologies for his absence, rehearsing the king's warm laugh. And Aldric went with it, as he always went, as he would always go—but for the first time in twenty years, he carried something new inside the prison with him.

Hope with a face. Hope with dark eyes and quick hands and stolen pages full of binding-script.

*Forgive me,* he thought toward her, wherever she fled in the dark beneath his kingdom. *Forgive me for what my face is about to do to your world. And if you find the way—child, if you find the way—I will spend the last of what I am helping you.*

Far below the palace, in tunnels he had never seen, a chalk sigil waited on a wall that only witches could see. And somewhere in the margins of a stolen ledger, an old prayer of his still waited to be read.

The feast received its king back with bowed heads and bright music, and no one saw that behind the silver mask, a man was praying.
