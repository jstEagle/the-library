## 12. Ashes of the Safehouse

Odile Voss had smelled a great many terrible things in her life—burning pitch at the siege of Harrow's Ford, the inside of a plague cart, her own flesh the day she lost her eye—but nothing had ever smelled like this.

Charred chalk. Burnt sage. The sweetish reek of scorched wool and something underneath it all, faint but unmistakable: burnt hair.

She stood at the mouth of the Bellows Street entrance with Jules at her shoulder and Rafael behind them both, and she did not move for a long moment. The tunnel beyond was black. Not shadowed—*black*, painted from ceiling to floor in soot, as though someone had taken the dark itself and set it alight.

"Odile." Jules's voice was very quiet. He knew better than to touch her when she was like this. "Odile, wait."

She didn't wait. She never waited. It was the flaw at the center of her, the crack that had gotten soldiers killed under her command and would likely get her killed in these tunnels someday too. She walked into the ruin of her home with her one eye streaming and her hand pressed flat against the wall where the stones were still warm, still *warm*, hours after whatever fire had swept through here, and she listened the way Tam had taught her to listen.

The stone said nothing.

That was the worst part. Tam swore the stones always spoke—that they grumbled about rain and roots and the weight of the city above, that they gossiped like fishwives about what passed over their backs. But when Odile laid her palm against the wall of the main chamber and asked, silently, *who did this, which way did they go, is my boy alive*—the stone gave her back only silence, dense and dead as a held breath.

The main chamber was unrecognizable. The long table where Maretta had ladled stew, where Rafael had first sat hunched and suspicious with her bowl held close like a shield—the table was ash and three blackened legs. The shelves of Maretta's medicines had burned hot enough to crack the stone shelf-brackets. The blankets, the lanterns, the little tin soldier Jules kept on his bunk since he was a boy—all of it gone, melted into the general gray sludge that covered the floor like dirty snow.

And the chalk sigils—the ward-marks, the warning spirals, the old smuggler's signs that said *safe passage, safe rest, family within*—every one of them had been deliberately, methodically defaced. Scratched out. Overwritten with a new mark she'd seen only once before, branded into the shoulder of a captured witch two winters ago: a vertical line crossed by three horizontal strokes, drawn in something that glittered faintly even through the soot.

Silver. They'd used silver dust. It would keep any witch from redrawing the wards for a month.

"They knew exactly what they were doing," Rafael said behind her. Her voice was thin. "This wasn't a raid. This was an erasure."

Odile crouched by the cold hearth. Maretta's kettle lay on its side among the ashes, its handle pointing toward the eastern passage like a compass needle, and Odile felt something in her chest go tight and strange, because Maretta was the sort of woman who would leave a sign even while being dragged away—a kettle-handle, a turned shoe, anything—and the handle pointed east, toward the salt cellar, toward the Cloaks' holding cells beneath the customs house.

Or it pointed nowhere at all and Odile was reading tea leaves in the wreckage of her life.

"Jules," she said. "Count them."

He counted. She heard him moving through the dark, turning over debris, checking the sleeping alcoves one by one. When he came back his face was gray in the light of the lamp Rafael had kindled—Rafael's hands cupped around a small flame that needed no wick, the first time Odile had seen her use her gift openly, and how bitter a thing that the girl should finally stop hiding on the worst night of all their lives.

"Tam's bed hasn't been slept in," Jules said. "His boots are gone. His coat is gone." A pause. "Maretta's shawl is still here."

"Then Tam walked out," Odile said. "On his own feet."

"Or was made to."

"He wouldn't have been made to walk anywhere without leaving a fight." Her voice cracked on the word *fight*. "Boy hears stone talk. Stone would've warned him. He'd have run, or hidden, or—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eye until stars burst behind it. "He'd have done something clever. He's always doing something clever."

"Maybe there wasn't time to be clever." Rafael knelt beside the hearth, studying the ash with the particular intensity she brought to everything, as if the world were a text she could decode if she stared hard enough. "Look at the burn pattern. It started here—at the hearth—and spread outward against the airflow. Someone lit it from inside. Someone who knew the room."

"A prisoner lighting a diversion," Jules said slowly.

"Or a boy buying time for whoever wasn't home." Rafael looked up at Odile, and her eyes in the witchlight were steady and sorrowful and far too young. "Where were you all when it happened?"

Buying Mother Quill's favor. Bargaining away a charm of true luck. Walking the long road back from the Quill's hall with their pockets empty and their bargain struck, while a twelve-year-old boy and a burned-out healer held the house alone.

Odile had commanded soldiers for eleven years. She had sent men into breaches knowing some would not come out; she had written letters to their mothers in her own careful hand; she had learned to carry the arithmetic of sacrifice the way other people carried coins, always counting, always weighing. But the arithmetic had never before involved a child who called her *Voss* because he thought it was funny, who left river-stones on her bunk shaped like little forts, who had told her once, gravely, that the walls of the tunnel liked her best of everyone because she was "heavy-footed and warm."

"I should have been here," she said.

"You couldn't have known—"

"I should have been *here*." The words came out of her like something torn. "I'm the one who built this place. I'm the one who told them it was safe. I looked a child in the face and said *nothing gets past me*, and I went off to trade favors with a crime-lord while the Grey Cloaks walked right down my front stairs."

Jules put his hand on her shoulder then, rules be damned, and she let it stay there because her legs had begun to shake and she could not afford to shake. Not yet. Not in front of the girl.

* * *

They searched for three hours.

It was Jules who found it, in the eastern passage forty paces past the second junction: a scorch-mark on the wall at the height of a man's torch, and beneath it, scratched into the stone with something sharp—a nail, a belt-buckle, a desperate fingernail—three words in Tam's careful, schoolroom hand:

**THEY HAVE MARETTA TOO**

Beneath that, smaller, almost apologetic:

**DONT COME ITS BAIT**

Odile read it twice. Then she turned and put her fist through the wall, and the stone—warm, silent, treacherous stone—cracked around her knuckles, and she stood there with blood running down her fingers and did not care, did not feel it, felt nothing but a fury so vast and clean it was almost peaceful, the way the eye of a storm is peaceful.

"Bait," she repeated. "For us. For *me*." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "They took a child to fish for smugglers. That's the crown's great war on magic now. Snaring children."

"The Veil's war," Rafael corrected quietly. "Not the crown's. There's a difference, and we need to remember it, because the difference is the whole point of everything we're trying to do."

"And what *are* we trying to do?" Odile rounded on her, and saw the girl flinch, and could not stop herself. "You're going to walk into the palace with a stolen ritual and pull a king apart. That's the plan. That's the whole mad plan. And now they've got Tam and Maretta in a salt-and-iron cell under the customs house, and every hour you spend on your king is an hour they're spending in the dark asking questions, and I am supposed to—" Her voice failed. She stood in the ruined tunnel with her bleeding hand and her burning eye and understood, suddenly and completely, why generals went gray.

Because both things were true. Both things were urgent. And she could not be in two places at once, and whichever child she abandoned would know, forever, which one she had chosen.

"Odile." Jules again. Steady. He had been steady for twenty years, through her rages and her nightmares and the year she drank herself half-blind after Harrow's Ford, and he was steady now. "Say the choice out loud. All of it. Then we'll decide together."

So she said it. She stood in the ashes of her home and said it plainly, the way she'd once stood before frightened conscripts and told them the truth about the battle ahead, because lies got people killed faster than truth did.

"We split," she said. "Rafael and the mission go one way—the palace, the binding-stone, the ritual, all of it, because if the Veil isn't caged there will be no end to nights like this one, only more of them, forever, until there are no tunnels left and no witches left to hide in them. And the rest of us go east. Into the customs house. After our children."

"That's suicide," Jules said. "You know it's suicide. Two of us against a garrison—"

"Three." Rafael stepped forward. "No. Don't look at me like that. You'll need a witch to get through their wards, and I'm the witch you have. I can blind their sentries. I can open their locks. I can—" She faltered, and Odile watched her gather herself the way she gathered herself before every confession, every cost. "I can do enough. And Tam healed for me when I first came here, do you remember? He sat with me for hours when I had fever, listening to the stones tell him stories because he thought it would help. I'm not letting him sit in a cell alone."

"No," Odile said.

"It's not your choice alone—"

"It is absolutely my choice alone, because I am the one who runs this family, and I am telling you *no*." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and made it gentle, because the girl deserved gentleness even now. Especially now. "Rafael. If you're caught, the ritual dies with you, and the king stays chained, and everything Serah confessed to, everything you've paid already to Mother Quill—it's all for nothing. You are the only person alive who can do what you're going to do. I am not. Jules is not. We're replaceable. You're not."

"You're not replaceable," Rafael said fiercely. "None of you—"

"To the mission, girl. To the mission." Odile held up her bandaged hand before the argument could spiral. "Listen to me. Here is how it will be. Jules goes with you to the palace—he knows the feast-cycles, he can pass for a servant, and he'll keep you alive when your conscience tries to get you killed. I take the east route with anyone else willing to follow me, and I bring our children home. We don't argue about whose fight matters more, because both fights matter, and the Veil wants us divided, wants us squabbling over scraps of ourselves while it eats the whole loaf. So we split. We split, and we finish, and we meet again on the other side of it."

The tunnel was quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled by thirty feet of stone, a church bell rang the third hour of the morning.

"And if you don't come back?" Rafael whispered.

"Then you come back anyway," Odile said. "You come back, and you finish it, and you live in a free city, and you remember us. That's the order. That's the last order I'll ever give you, so listen well."

Jules made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief. He crossed to Rafael and, awkwardly, ferociously, hugged her—this man who distrusted every newcomer on principle, who had needed a full month to speak to Rafael in anything but monosyllables.

"Don't let her burn anything she can't afford to lose," he told Odile over the girl's head.

"Same to you."

* * *

They parted at the third junction, where the passages forked toward the river and the customs house like a wishbone waiting to be broken.

Odile watched them go—Jules with his satchel of forged papers, Rafael with her hood up and her charms sewn into her hem, two small figures swallowed by the dark toward the palace—and then she turned east, toward the salt and the iron, and felt the weight of the choice settle onto her shoulders like a yoke.

Behind her came footsteps. Six of them. Fugitives from the outer bolt-holes, summoned by runners in the night: a glassworker named Perrin whose gift was cooling molten sand; twin sisters who could taste lies; old Wenna, who had been hiding in Corvane since before Odile found these tunnels, moving stiffly but carrying a wood-axe with the air of a woman with strong opinions about Grey Cloaks.

Seven, counting herself. Against a garrison.

Madness. She'd have called it madness, once, and meant it. But she had learned something in the years since the army, something the army had never taught her: that a family was just a battle you chose to keep fighting, and that love was not a soft thing at all but a hard thing, a siege-thing, a thing that dug in and held the wall and refused, absolutely refused, to yield the people behind it.

They had taken her boy. They had taken her healer. They had burned her home and salted her wards and left a child's handwriting on a wall saying *don't come.*

She was coming.

"Voss?" Perrin, at her elbow, young and scared. "What's the plan?"

Odile rolled her shoulder, testing the ache of it, and looked down the eastern passage where the dark ran deep and the stones, for once, seemed to lean toward her, listening.

"The plan," she said, "is that they think they've set a trap." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile, and did not care. "Let's show them what happens when the trap has teeth."

Far away, beneath the palace, she would learn later, a binding-stone began to hum.
