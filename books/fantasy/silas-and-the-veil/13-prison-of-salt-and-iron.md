## 13. Prison of Salt and Iron

The cell was made of salt and iron, and Tam hated it the way he had never hated anything in his short life.

Not because it hurt—though it did hurt, in a low grinding way he could feel behind his teeth, like biting tinfoil. Not because it was cold, though the damp crept up through the floor and settled into his bones and stayed there like a guest who would not leave. He hated it because of what it *did* to the stones.

Everywhere else in his life, stones talked. Pebbles in a road muttered about the feet that had crossed them. Foundation-stones hummed old songs about the buildings they held up. The tunnel walls beneath Bellows Street gossiped all night long, warm and chatty as a room full of aunties, and Tam had fallen asleep more nights than he could count with their murmurs wrapped around him like a second blanket. The stones were how he knew he wasn't alone. The stones were how Odile always found him when he wandered too far—they told her where he'd gone, grumbling about the boy who listened.

But this cell had been built by people who knew about boys who listened.

Salt packed into the mortar between the blocks. Iron filings ground into the plaster itself. The walls here were deaf and dumb, sealed off from every other stone in Corvane, and when Tam pressed his palm against them—as he had been doing for hours, or days; time went strange without windows—all they said was *salt salt salt*, flat and dead as a slapped cheek. It was the loneliest sound he had ever heard. It was almost no sound at all.

He sat on the plank bed with his knees drawn up and his arms around them, making himself small, and he counted his breaths the way Maretta had taught him for the bad nights. In for four. Hold for two. Out for six. His broken hand throbbed against his ribs where he'd tucked it—broken three days ago, back before, when the world still had Odile in it and hot stew and Jules complaining about the price of lamp oil. He hadn't let anyone look at it since they took him. He didn't want the Grey Cloaks touching it. He didn't want them knowing it hurt.

Because that was the other thing about the cell, the thing he understood now after however many days: everything in here was designed to make him talk. The cold. The dark that wasn't quite dark—a single hooded lamp burned behind iron slats, just bright enough to keep sleep shallow and thin. The silence where the stones should have been. And under all of it, so faint most people would never notice, a whispering. Not the stones' whispering. Something else. Something that came through the salt itself, patient as water dripping, saying over and over:

*tell us tell us tell us*

Tam pulled his arms tighter around his knees and whispered back, into the dark, in the smallest voice he owned:

"No."

* * *

They came for him twice a day. He knew because meals came twice a day too—bowl of grey porridge shoved through the slot in the door, cup of water—and the interrogations came right after, as if they thought a full belly loosened tongues. Maybe it worked on grown-ups. Tam ate every scrap anyway. You couldn't be brave hungry. That was another thing Maretta taught him, back when she was teaching him to grind willow bark: *a body fed is a body that can think.* He held onto that like a rope.

The one who came was called Warden Hesk. Tam knew his name because the man's boots announced him—the left heel clicked wrong, worn crooked, and the flagstones outside the cell complained about it every time: *clack-drag, clack-drag, there goes that heavy fool again.* The stones couldn't hear past the salt, but they could hear everything leading up to it, and so Tam always knew when Hesk was coming before the key turned. It was the only advantage he had, and he guarded it fiercely, arranging his face each time into blankness. Scared but not too scared. Dumb but not rude. A boy who was nothing special.

Hesk was a big man gone soft in the middle, with a grey cloak that smelled of wet ash and a voice like gravel poured slow. He carried a ledger and a little brass instrument that looked like a compass crossed with a tuning fork, and he set both on the table beside the door every time, in the same place, and Tam watched them instead of watching Hesk's face because faces lied but habits didn't.

"Morning, boy." Evening, actually. Or maybe morning. "Let's try again, shall we? How many others are in the tunnels under Bellows Street?"

"I don't know any tunnels," Tam said. "I'm a chimney sweep's boy. I got lost."

"You got lost three levels underground, past a door warded shut."

"I got very lost."

Hesk sighed, the sigh of a man doing tedious work, and picked up the brass fork. He struck it against the table edge and it sang—a thin, sour note that made Tam's fillings ache—and he walked it slowly along the wall, watching its needle. The needle twitched near the bed. Near the floor drain. Near Tam himself.

"Your hands are dirty," Hesk observed.

"I dig chimneys."

"Chimney soot doesn't have quartz dust in it." He crouched, bringing the singing fork close, and Tam saw the needle straining toward him like a plant toward light. "You've been working stone. Talking to it, maybe. The salt keeps your friends out, boy, but it can't unmake what you already are. You're a listener. We know that much. The question is how good."

Tam looked at his knees. "I don't know what you mean."

"The Veil knows what you mean." Hesk said it mildly, like a man mentioning weather, and something cold slid down Tam's spine, because that was wrong. That was *wrong*. Everyone knew the Grey Cloaks served the crown—Odile said so, everyone said so, hunters wearing the king's grey, carrying the king's writ. But Hesk hadn't said *the crown knows* or *the king's men know*. He'd said the Veil. As if the Veil were a person. As if the Veil were his master.

Tam kept his face still. Chimney sweep's boy. Nothing special. But inside, his mind turned the sentence over and over like a stone with a fossil in it.

When Hesk left—he always left eventually, frustrated, promising worse tomorrow, tomorrow a man named Cutter would come with tools—Tam lay on the plank bed and stared at the hooded lamp and thought about what the warden had said.

The Veil knows what you mean.

He'd heard the name before, in fragments, in the tunnels. Rafael's face going pale when the registry was read out. Odile's jaw tightening. *The thing wearing the crown,* someone had whispered once, thinking he was asleep. Tam had spent his whole life learning that things which seemed solid—walls, floors, kings—were mostly hollow, mostly stories, mostly stone with something moving underneath. If the Cloaks answered to the Veil and not to Aldric, then the writs they carried were lies, and the law they enforced was no law at all, and that meant—

That meant being brave wasn't treason. That meant Odile's family weren't criminals. That meant the fear he'd been swallowing since they dragged him up out of the tunnels, bag over his head, was fear of something that had no right to be obeyed.

It was a small thing. It was a small, hard seed of a thing. But Tam curled around it in the dark the way he'd curl around a warm rock in winter, and he decided to keep it.

* * *

On the fourth day—or the fifth; the meals blurred—the whispering changed.

*tell us tell us tell us* became, for one moment, something else. A different pressure against the salt, coming from the other side. Fainter than faint. Tam thought at first he'd imagined it, dreamed it, gone soft in the head from the grey porridge. Then it came again, and this time it had a shape he knew.

*Maretta.*

She was somewhere below him—below and to the left, deep, deeper than his cell. He couldn't hear her words; the salt strangled everything. But he could feel the rhythm of her, the way you can feel a heartbeat through a wall if you press your ear flat enough. Maretta hummed when she worked. She hummed when she was frightened, too, old marsh songs with no words, and Tam had heard that humming across a hundred quiet evenings in the tunnel kitchen while she stitched wounds and stirred broth. The rhythm pressing up through the stone was one of those songs. Slowed down. Dragged out. But hers.

She was alive. They had taken her alive.

Tam pressed both palms—one whole, one splinted and screaming—to the floor and pushed everything he had toward that rhythm, the way you'd push warmth into cold hands. *I'm here. I'm here. I'm here.* He didn't know if she could feel him. The salt ate most of it. But the humming faltered, just once, and then steadied, and then—this was the part that made his eyes sting—it changed. Shifted key. Picked up a phrase from a different song entirely, one Odile used to sing while mending nets, and Tam laughed out loud, alone in the cell, wet-eyed and grinning, because that was an answer. That was Maretta saying *I hear you, boy, I'm here, hold on.*

Hold on. He could do that. Holding on was most of what he'd ever done.

After that the days had a shape. Meals. Interrogation. Counting breaths. Listening for Maretta's song through the floor, and answering with tapping—little rhythms knocked out on the boards with his good hand, patterns she'd recognize, patterns that meant *still here, still me, still stubborn.* Once, far away, he felt another flicker of presence, brief and strange: something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the palace, a hunger so large it registered less as sound than as absence, a hole in the world's noise. Tam pulled back from it fast, heart hammering. Some doors you didn't knock on.

And once—only once—Hesk brought someone else.

* * *

The visitor wore a grey cloak finer than Hesk's, cut close, and no hood, and Tam wished fervently that he had worn one, because the man's face was the kind of face that made you want to look anywhere else. Smooth. Too smooth. Like a portrait of a face rather than a face, like something that had studied how features fit together and arranged them competently but without quite understanding why people had them. His eyes were grey all the way through, iris and white alike, and they did not blink often enough.

"This is the listener?" the smooth man asked.

"Strong for his age," Hesk said. "Doesn't talk."

"They rarely do, at first." The smooth man crouched to Tam's level, and up close his skin had a texture like river-polished stone, and Tam's stomach turned over, because he had felt that texture before—not with his hands, with his gift, deep under Corvane, in the places where the old stones went silent and afraid. This man felt like those places. This man felt like the hole in the world's noise, walking around in a coat.

"Hello, Tam," the smooth man said gently. "You're wondering how I know your name."

Tam said nothing. His mouth had gone dry as the salt in the walls.

"You're wondering a great many things. Children always are. Let me save you the trouble of the important ones." He folded his hands, unhurried, a tutor beginning a lesson. "You believe you were arrested under the king's law. You are not. There has been no king in Corvane for twenty years—only a shape the Veil wears, the way I wear this face, the way your friend the soldier wears her scars. The Grey Cloaks are the Veil's hands. I am its voice. And the Veil is *hungry*, Tam, as it has been hungry for a very long time, and it feeds on exactly what you have. So here is my lesson, freely given: whatever loyalty you feel to the woman Voss, whatever hope you hold that someone is coming for you—set it down. Hope is heavy, and you cannot carry it where you're going."

Tam looked at him. His whole body wanted to shake. His whole body wanted to weep, and confess, and beg. Instead he heard Maretta's song in his memory, steady under everything, and Odile's voice saying, the night the Cloaks first swept Bellows Street: *Fear's just weather, Tam. It passes through. What you choose in it—that's yours.*

"What you choose in it," Tam repeated out loud, because his voice worked even when nothing else did, "is mine."

The smooth man blinked—once, slowly, like a lizard. For a moment something moved behind the grey eyes, something enormous and irritated, the way a pond moves when a big fish turns under the surface. Then the portrait-face smoothed again.

"Yes," it said. "For now."

He stood, told Hesk the boy was to be kept fed and untouched—"the Veil prefers its vessels unbruised"—and left. The clack-drag of Hesk's boots faded. The door boomed shut. And Tam sat in the salt-and-iron silence with his heart going like a drum and the smooth man's words ringing in his ears.

*Whatever hope you hold that someone is coming for you.*

The man had meant it as a knife. But Tam turned it over carefully, the way he turned over every stone, looking at the underside—and there it was, glinting. The Veil knew about Odile. Knew about hope. Which meant the Veil was *watching for them*, which meant they mattered, which meant—

Which meant someone was coming. You didn't watch for nothing.

Tam lay back on the plank bed, cradling his broken hand, and began, very softly, to tap Maretta's song against the wall with his good knuckles. *Still here. Still me. Still stubborn.* Far below, faint as a star through cloud, the humming answered.

Above him, muffled by fathoms of stone, Corvane went on burning. Somewhere in it, he was sure—stone-sure, bone-sure—boots were moving through the ash, following the only map that mattered: a boy's knocking, a healer's song, a thread of stubbornness running down through the dark.

"Hold on, Maretta," he whispered to the deaf salt walls. "We're both getting out of here. I heard it in the stones."

The stones said nothing at all.

Tam smiled anyway, small and fierce, in the iron dark—and far above, faint through the salt, through the iron, through everything they had built to keep him alone, he thought he felt the first tremor of footsteps that were not wardens'.
