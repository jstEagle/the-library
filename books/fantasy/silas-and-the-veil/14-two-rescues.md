## 14. Two Rescues

The prison of salt and iron sat at the bottom of a drowned street.

That was what the stones called it—Tam had told her once, before they took him, that every building in Corvane had a name in its bones, and the Grey Cloaks' holding house had been built on a flooded cistern from the old kings' days. The water still lived down there beneath the flagstones, black and patient, and it whispered up through the mortar to anyone who could hear it: *cold, cold, cold.* Rafael heard it now as she crouched in the shadow of a collapsed granary across the lane, and she thought of Tam alone among all that whispering dark, and her hands would not stop shaking.

"You're bleeding again." Jules's voice came low beside her, barely more than breath. He was pressed against the granary's rotted doorframe, and in the grey hour before dawn his face was all angles and old scars, the distrust worn down to something harder and more useful. "Your nose."

She wiped it with the back of her wrist. Blood, thin and rust-dark. She'd been leaking power for three days now, ever since Mother Quill's charm of true luck, ever since she'd learned how empty a witch could be and keep walking anyway. "It's nothing."

"It's not nothing. It's your body telling you what you won't say out loud." He didn't look at her when he said it. He kept his eyes on the prison's single gate, where two Grey Cloaks stood with their lanterns hooded, their faces pale ovals above grey wool. "Odile does the same thing. Bleeds rather than admits she's spent. It's a disease you both have."

"Is that concern, Jules?"

"It's arithmetic." But his jaw tightened, and after a moment he added, quieter, "If you fall in there, I can't carry you and the boy both. So don't fall."

Above them, on the ridge of rooftops along Bellows Street, Odile Voss was about to set half the district on fire.

* * *

The plan had been made in the ruins of the safehouse, around a table scorched by the Cloaks' fire-oil, with ash still sifting down from the ceiling beams when anyone moved too fast. Odile had spread a stolen guard roster across the charred wood and put one scarred finger on the map of the holding house like a woman pinning a snake's head.

"Forty-one cells," she'd said. "Two levels below ground. They'll have Tam on the lower level—that's where they keep the ones they want to question slowly—and Maretta will be near him, because they took them the same night and they'll want them together for leverage." Her eye had been steady, her voice flat as slate, but Rafael had seen the tremor in the finger. Odile Voss did not tremble. Except when it was Tam. Except when it was family. "The gate is suicide. The gate is always suicide. So we don't use the gate."

"The cistern," Rafael had said.

Odile's eye had come up to hers, and there had been something almost like approval in it. "The cistern. The old flood-tunnel runs under the east wall. It's barred, but bars are just iron, and iron is just metal, and our witch here bends metal when she has the strength to stand afterward." A pause. "Which is why I need you to save your strength. Every drop. You hear me? You go down that tunnel, you bend those bars, you find my boy, and you do *nothing else* unless someone is dying. Nothing else."

Rafael had agreed. She had meant it, too, in the way one means things in the warm light of a ruined kitchen with the people you love still alive around you.

Now, in the cold, she watched the first flame climb the roofline of the tannery three streets north, and she understood exactly how plans died.

* * *

It started small, the way Odile did everything—a single oil-soaked rag lobbed onto dry shingles, a bloom of orange in the pre-dawn grey. Then a second fire, west, near the chandlery. Then the bell in the watchtower began to ring, wild and arrhythmic, because Odile had cut its rope and tied it to a falling beam, and the sound it made was less an alarm than a scream.

The gate guards turned toward the fires. One ran. The other stood his post for eleven heartbeats—Rafael counted—before duty or fear pulled him after his fellow.

"Now," Jules breathed.

They crossed the lane low and fast, boots silent on wet cobbles, and slipped through the gap where the granary wall met the prison's outer yard. The holding house rose above them, a squat block of black stone with slit windows, and even from outside Rafael could feel it—the wrongness soaked into the walls, the same hungry residue she'd felt in the palace stones, thinner here but unmistakable. The Veil's fingerprints. The Grey Cloaks didn't serve the crown. They served the thing beneath it, and their prison was built like a throat.

Jules went to the grate over the cistern shaft and worked the lock with picks he'd stolen from a locksmith who owed Odile money. Rafael knelt beside him and laid her palms flat against the iron bars of the flood-tunnel mouth below—cold, so cold, the water whispering behind them—and *pushed.*

Metal remembered being ore. That was the secret no hedge-witch taught you, the thing Serah had shown her once in a marsh hut full of drying herbs: everything wanted to return to what it had been. Iron dreamed of earth. Rafael gave the dream a little help, and the bars groaned and bent outward like fingers uncurling, and the cost went through her like a hook behind the sternum.

She gasped. Kept her feet. Wiped the fresh thread of blood from her lip before Jules could see.

He saw anyway. He said nothing. He went first into the dark, lamp shuttered to a coin of light, and she followed him down into the water-voice and the cold.

* * *

The tunnel was thigh-deep in places, black water moving with a slow tidal breath. Rats tracked them along the ledges with bright indifferent eyes. Rafael counted her steps to keep from counting her fears: forty steps, sixty, a hundred, the ceiling lowering until Jules had to stoop and the air went thick with mineral rot. And all the while the stones murmured to her—not words, never words, but pressure and mood, the way a sleeping animal shifts—and what they murmured was *wrongness, wrongness,* something caged and suffering somewhere ahead.

Then light. Not their lamplight—grey, sourceless, seeping through cracks in a wall of fitted blocks ahead. Jules killed the lamp entirely and they crept to the seam between the stones, and Rafael put her eye to the gap.

A corridor. Torchlit despite the hour. A door of banded iron standing open, and beyond it, visible in slices, a cell: straw, a bucket, a small figure sitting cross-legged on the floor with his head bowed and his lips moving.

Tam. Alive. Whole, as far as she could see. Talking to the floor.

Her chest cracked open with relief so sharp it was nearly pain.

"He's talking to the stones," she whispered. "They've been keeping him sane."

"Where are the guards?" Jules breathed back.

As if the question summoned them, boots rang in the corridor—two Cloaks passing the open door, lanterns swinging, and Rafael flattened herself against the cold blocks and held her breath and listened to them pass. Their voices drifted back, low and ugly:

"—the witch-boy asked for his mother again. Captain says tell him she's dead."

"She isn't, though."

"Captain says tell him she is. Breaks 'em faster."

Rafael felt Jules go rigid beside her. In the shuttered dark his hand found her sleeve and gripped it, not for comfort—for restraint. She could feel the fury coming off him like heat off a forge.

"They'll rotate again within the quarter-hour," he managed. "When they pass, we take the door. You bend the lock, I break the heads. Then we find Maretta and we go back the way we came, and we do not deviate, and we do not be heroes."

"I remember the plan, Jules."

"No you don't. Nobody remembers the plan. That's why plans have me in them." But there was something underneath the acid now, something that hadn't been there three weeks ago when he'd looked at her like a stranger carrying a lit fuse into his home. Something that might, in better light, have been called trust.

The boots returned. Passed. Faded.

Jules slipped through the crack in the wall—they'd pried it wider with a pry-bar in the days of preparation, a wound in the masonry just wide enough for a man with courage and a narrow chest—and Rafael followed, bending the last obstructing bar aside with a squeal she prayed the torches would swallow.

The corridor smelled of salt and iron and old fear. The open cell door stood three strides away. Inside, Tam's head snapped up at their approach—he couldn't have heard them, but the stones must have shouted—and his face, hollow-cheeked and bruised at the jaw, broke into a look of such naked disbelief that Rafael's eyes stung.

"You came," he whispered. "I told them you'd come. They said—" His voice wobbled. "They said Odile was dead."

"Odile," Rafael said, kneeling to grip his shoulders, "is currently setting fire to half of Bellows Street out of pure maternal spite. Up. Can you walk?"

"I can run." He was already up, swaying, thirteen years old and stubborn as bedrock. "Maretta's four doors down. I hear her through the wall. She hums when she's scared—she thinks nobody knows—and she's been humming for six days."

Four doors down. Past cells whose occupants Rafael tried not to imagine, past a stairwell climbing up toward the world of the living and another descending into worse. They moved fast, Jules leading with his cudgel loose in his fist, Tam's hand locked in Rafael's, the boy's palm ice-cold and trembling.

At the fourth door, Jules bent to the lock and Rafael laid her hand over his and the mechanism surrendered without a fight, tumblers melting soft as wax. Inside: Maretta, curled on a shelf of straw, grey-faced, wrists raw, humming a lullaby through cracked lips. When the door opened she flinched so hard she struck the wall, and then her eyes found Tam and the humming stopped, and she made a sound Rafael had only ever heard from mothers.

"Mama," Tam said—it was a joke between them, had been since the day Odile collected them both, and it wasn't a joke at all—and Maretta gathered him in with chained hands and wept into his hair without a single loud note, the way healers cry, quietly, economically, saving everything for later.

"Keys," Jules demanded, and Rafael bent the shackles instead, iron parting like bread. "Stairs are up. We go up?"

"We go down," Rafael said, and hated herself for it.

Both of them stared at her.

"The flood-tunnel's compromised—I felt it on the way in, the east section's settling, the water's rising. We'd drown in the dark." It was half true. The other half was that her legs were shaking and she didn't trust the deep water anymore, didn't trust anything, wanted sky. "There's a sally-gate on the south side, into the tannery yard. Odile's fires will have emptied it."

"That's not the plan," Jules said flatly.

"The plan also assumed I wouldn't be this empty." She showed him her hands. They were trembling visibly now, and there was blood under her nose again, and Jules looked at her hands for a long moment and then at Tam and Maretta, doing arithmetic, and Rafael watched him lose the argument with himself.

"South gate," he said finally. "And if we meet anyone—"

"I'll handle it."

"With what, exactly?"

"Charm," Rafael said, and pulled the last of her stored workings from the lining of her coat: a knot of red thread that had taken her a week to make and held enough compulsion to stop a man's will for ten heartbeats. Ten heartbeats was a long time, if you had a cudgel.

* * *

They nearly made it.

The stairs rose, the corridor turned, and the south sally-gate stood ahead of them—barred, but Rafael could feel the lock inside it, simple, mortal, *bendable*—when the torchlight behind them doubled and a voice like a file drawn across steel said, "Stop."

Three Grey Cloaks. Then five. Then more, pouring up from the lower level, called back early from the fire because someone had counted prisoners and found the count wrong. Their captain stepped forward, a tall woman with a burn-scarred cheek and the flat eyes of someone who had stopped seeing people years ago, and she smiled without any warmth at all.

"The witch," she said. "The Veil wants you specifically, girl. It's been tasting you in the stones for days. Come quietly and the others live."

Rafael stepped in front of Tam and Maretta and raised the red thread, and the captain laughed.

"Charms," she said. "We burn witches. What do you think a charm is worth?"

What it was worth was ten heartbeats. Rafael loosed the working and the captain's face went slack mid-laugh, and Jules moved like a landslide, cudgel cracking down twice, and Tam grabbed Maretta and ran for the gate, and Rafael bent the lock screaming open and the door swung and—

—and then the world ended, briefly, in white.

She came back to herself on her knees in the tannery yard with her ears ringing and her vision full of sparks, and Jules hauling her up by the arm, and the doorway behind them choked with Cloaks who were not following, because the whole eastern sky had gone orange.

Not the tannery. Not the diversion fires. The prison itself—its upper floors vomiting flame, black smoke rolling from the slit windows, and somewhere in the roaring Rafael heard a sound she knew: the whistle-and-crack of fire-oil igniting, the Cloaks' own weapon, used at scale.

"Odile," Tam screamed, and broke from Maretta's grip and ran toward the burning building, and it was Rafael who caught him, Rafael who wrapped her arms around the thrashing boy and held on with everything left in her hollowed-out body. "She's IN there, she was supposed to draw them OFF, she promised—"

"She drew them off," Jules said. His voice was terrible and steady. "Look at the gate. Look."

No one was pursuing them. Every Cloak in the district was running toward the fire—or away from it, dragging wounded, coughing, blind. Whatever Odile had done, she had done it completely. There would be no pursuit tonight. There would perhaps be no prison tomorrow.

But there was also no Odile Voss emerging from the smoke.

* * *

They found her in the alley behind the chandlery, twenty minutes later, because Tam heard her.

The boy dropped to his knees on the filthy cobbles and pressed both palms flat to the ground and went utterly still, listening, tears cutting tracks through the soot on his face, and then he scrambled up and pointed. "There. Under the crates. She's breathing. She's breathing but it's wrong, the stones say it's wrong—"

They dug her out from under a collapsed awning and a spill of broken barrels, and Rafael's heart stopped at the sight of her.

Odile Voss, one-eyed veteran of two wars, the woman who had carried Rafael's terror up out of the tunnels and given it a bed and a bowl of stew—lay crumpled like a discarded uniform. Her left side was a ruin. Fire-oil burns from hip to shoulder, the skin gone black and split, and beneath them, worse, the deep crush of a collapsing beam. Her remaining eye found Rafael and focused with agonizing slowness.

"Told you," she rasped. "Distraction. Works... every time."

"Shut up," Rafael said. "Shut up, don't talk, don't—"

"Boy?"

"I'm here." Tam crawled into the crook of her good arm, and Odile's hand came up—shaking, blackened, determined—and rested on his head, and something in her face eased.

"Good," she whispered. "Good. Now everyone stop... looking at me like that. Witch. You know what to do."

Rafael knew. That was the horror of it. She knelt in the alley filth and put her hands on the burned flesh and she *knew*, and she also knew what was left in her: fumes. Dregs. Three days of leakage, Quill's charm, the locks, the shackles, the compulsion-knot—all of it paid out of a purse already near-empty. To heal this—to pull Odile Voss back from the edge of a death that was already leaning on her—she would have to reach past empty. Past the bottom of herself, into whatever lived underneath, the place no teacher ever named because witches who went there sometimes did not come back.

"Rafael." Jules's hand on her shoulder. "How much?"

"All of it," she said. "And then some."

"And what happens to you?"

"I don't know." She met his eyes, and found that she was smiling, and the smile felt strange on her face, like a garment borrowed from someone braver. "But I know what happens to her if I don't. Jules—hold my shoulders. If I start to drift, hold me here. Don't let me follow it down."

He gripped her. Hard. Grounding her the way you ground a lightning strike.

Rafael closed her eyes and reached.

The healing began gently, the way all her work did—warmth flowing into torn flesh, coaxing, persuading, reminding the body of its own wholeness. Burns softened. The crushing weight in Odile's ribs eased. And Rafael felt the current running through her, felt it thinning, guttering, and pushed deeper, past comfort, past pain, into the cold vault at the center of herself where the last of her gift lay coiled like a dying ember.

*Take it,* she told it. *All of it. She's worth more than I am. She's worth all of us.*

The ember flared—and fed.

It was like being opened. Like something reaching into her chest and drawing out not just power but the *shape* of power, the architecture of her gift, load-bearing walls coming away in great handfuls. She felt her senses stretch and tear; felt the stones' whispers recede as if a door were closing on them forever; felt, distantly, Jules shouting her name and his hands crushing her shoulders, holding her in the world. Odile's flesh knit. Color flooded back into the grey of her lips. Her spine straightened under Rafael's palms by degrees, and Rafael poured and poured and poured until there was nothing left to pour, and then she kept pouring, scraping the vessel, giving the dregs, the sediment, the self—

—and then Odile's hand closed around her wrist, strong, alive, impossibly strong, and dragged her back.

"No," the old soldier rasped, her voice whole again, her eye blazing. "*Enough.* Enough, girl. You come back. You come back NOW."

Rafael came back.

* * *

Dawn found them in the smugglers' deep tunnel, three miles from the burning prison, in a smuggler's cache that smelled of lamp oil and old cheese. Maretta—her healer's hands steady now, her chains gone, her voice rough with six days of humming—moved among them with salves and water. Tam slept against Odile's side, one fist knotted in her shirt, and Odile sat propped against the wall with her new-pink skin and her arm around the boy, watching Rafael with an expression she didn't bother to hide.

Because Rafael was empty.

She sat apart, wrapped in a blanket, and she could feel it the way an amputee feels the missing limb: the silence where the stones had whispered. The stillness where the currents of the world had brushed against her since childhood. She reached inward, out of habit, and found smooth blank walls, a house swept bare, and beneath the grief of it a strange floating lightness—as if she'd set down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Can you feel anything?" Jules asked quietly, crouching beside her. Somewhere in the last hours he had stopped pretending not to care, and neither of them remarked on it.

"Nothing." She said it steadily. "Maybe it comes back. Maybe it doesn't."

"Rafael—"

"Don't." She touched his arm. "Jules. Look at her." Across the cache, Odile had bent her head to press her lips to Tam's hair, her one eye closed, her scarred hand cradling the back his skull, and Maretta sat close against her other side with her chin on Odile's shoulder. All of them alive. All of them together. "I'd empty myself a hundred times for that. You know I would. You'd do worse."

He was quiet a long moment. "Yes," he admitted finally, and it sounded like it cost him. "I would."

It was Tam who broke the moment—Tam, lifting his head from Odile's chest with the sudden alertness of a boy who hears what others cannot, even now, even with Rafael deaf to it. His eyes went wide.

"What is it?" Odile demanded, hand going to her knife. "What do you hear?"

"The stones," Tam said. His voice shook. "Deep down. Under the city. They're singing." He looked at Rafael, and his young face was full of a wonder and a fear she couldn't yet understand. "Rafa—they're singing *your name.*"
