## 16. How to Unmake a King's Chains

The marsh did not forgive, and Serah had never asked it to.

She had lived in its gray embrace for thirty years—since before Rafael was born, since before the binding, since she had been a woman with a name that mattered in certain rooms and a reputation that opened doors she should have kept shut. The marsh took everything eventually: the boards of her walkway rotted on a schedule of its own devising, the reeds swallowed her fences each spring, the water rose into her cellar and left its salt-scum signature on every jar. She let it. It seemed only just that the land should consume her slowly, by inches, when she had helped build something that consumed a king.

Now they were all here, crowded onto her sagging porch and into her single great room that smelled of dried yarrow and peat smoke and old mistakes. Odile Voss stood by the door with her one eye cataloguing exits out of habit. Jules sat sharpening a knife he had no intention of using, because the motion calmed him. Tam had fallen asleep against Maretta's shoulder, the boy's fingers still curled around a river stone he'd carried from the prison cell like a talisman, though Serah suspected the stone was the lesser charm and the boy himself the greater. Maretta's wrists still bore the red rings of iron manacles; she moved her hands carefully, as if the air itself might bruise them.

And Rafael stood at the center of the room, arms crossed, wearing the expression Serah knew better than her own reflection—the look of a girl who had decided something and would not be argued out of it.

Serah had seen that look before. She had worn it herself, once, standing in a candlelit chamber beneath an unfinished throne, telling four other witches that she would be the one to hold the chains.

She had been wrong about what that would cost. She intended to be right this time.

"The grimoire is not enough," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to, scraped raw by three days of confession and the long walk back from the place where she had buried her own courage decades ago. "You've read the diagrams. You think you understand the working. You don't. No one understands it who hasn't stood inside it."

"Then explain it," Rafael said. "All of it. No more riddles, no more *you're not ready*. I'm done being managed."

Serah almost smiled. Almost. There had been a time when she would have boxed the girl's ears for that tone, and a later time when she would have wept at hearing it again after three years of silence. Now she only nodded and went to the chest beneath her bed.

* * *

The chest was oak, bound in iron, warded so thoroughly that Tam had flinched when he first entered the house and asked, puzzled, why the floorboards were *shouting*. Inside lay the things Serah had not been able to destroy and had not been able to keep visible: her mother's ring, a lock of hair from a friend dead forty years, and six candles of black tallow that had never been lit because lighting them served no purpose except to mark the beginning of something terrible.

She set the grimoire on the table between the candles and opened it to the pages she had tried, for twenty years, to stop dreaming about.

"This is the binding-stone," she said, tapping the diagram. A circle of interlocking script, dense as woven cloth, wrapped around a shape like a heart—or a fist. "Not a metaphor. An actual stone, cut before Corvane had walls, before Aldric's grandfather's grandfather drew breath. The old kings built their throne room above it because the stone holds the land's memory, and a throne that sits on memory is hard to unseat. They thought it made their rule permanent." Her mouth twisted. "They were right about that, at least."

"And the Veil?" Jules asked. He had stopped pretending to sharpen the knife.

"The Veil was already there. Coiled around the stone like a tapeworm in a gut, feeding slow, growing slow. It predates the city. It predates the language we use to name it. What my circle did—" and here Serah's hands began, traitorously, to shake, so she pressed them flat against the table, "—what we did was not summon it. We didn't bring it. We *bound* it. To a living vessel. Because a thing that feeds must feed on something, and if it was anchored to one man instead of drifting through a whole kingdom's dreams, we could starve it. Contain it. Watch it."

"Aldric," Maretta said quietly. "He was young."

"He was nineteen," Serah said. "And he volunteered. That is the part I need you to carry, all of you, because you will want to make me a monster and him a victim, and the truth is uglier than either. He knelt on that stone and gave his consent freely, with his whole heart, because the plague years were killing his people and the Veil promised—if promised is the word—that a bound king could hold the sickness back. And it worked. The plague broke. The harvests came. Twenty years of peace." She looked up, and let them see her face without mercy or excuse. "I have told myself for two decades that the price was worth it. I have told myself that a caged god-thing is better than a loose one. And every night I dream of his eyes when the chains closed, and I know that what I told myself was the sound a conscience makes when it is drowning."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a marsh bird cried its one flat note.

"So the unbinding," Rafael said. Her voice was steady. Too steady. Serah recognized that steadiness too—a girl holding herself rigid so she wouldn't shatter. "How?"

* * *

Serah turned the page. The next diagram was simpler and worse: a single line running from the stone outward, ending in a shape like an open hand.

"You cannot simply cut the chains," she said. "A binding of this depth doesn't break. It *transfers*. The chains are not rope around the Veil—they are woven from consent. Aldric's consent, given on the stone, is the warp of the whole working. To undo it, someone must stand where he stood, on the stone, and take the weave into themselves. Unpick it thread by thread while the Veil fights you, because it will fight—it has spent twenty years getting fat and clever inside a king's body, and it knows exactly what is coming the moment a witch sets foot on its anchor. And when the last thread comes free, the chains don't vanish. They have to go somewhere."

"Into the witch doing the unbinding," Rafael said.

"Into the witch doing the unbinding," Serah confirmed. "Chained into your soul, as it was chained into his body. You would carry it until you died, and it would gnaw at you every hour of every day, whispering, hungry, patient. Some vessels it breaks in a year. Some it takes decades to crack. But it does not release. There is no second unbinding. Whoever takes those chains keeps them."

Silence. Then Odile, arms folded, jaw set: "There has to be another way."

"There is one other way," Serah said, "and it is written in the margin here, in my own hand, thirty years younger." She touched the faded ink. "The transfer requires a vessel that accepts the chains *freely*. Freely is the operative word—the working will not force itself on an unwilling soul. So the witch who unpicks the binding can, at the final moment, offer the chains to another who consents to receive them. A substitution. One soul stepping in front of another."

"That's not a way," Jules said flatly. "That's just choosing who dies slower."

"It is exactly that," Serah agreed. "Which is why I wrote it down and then prayed no one would ever read it."

Rafael stepped forward. "I'll do it."

"No," Serah said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. I know what you're going to say. You're going to say you're strong enough. You're going to say you've hidden your power for three years and you know how to endure, how to be small, how to survive things that should have broken you. You're going to say the family needs Odile's cunning and Maretta's healing and Tam's ears and Jules's stubbornness, but nobody needs a hedge-witch's charms, so the sacrifice costs the least coming from you." Serah's voice cracked on the last words, and she hated it, hated the tremor, hated that thirty years of discipline dissolved the moment this girl stood in front of her. "I raised you. I taught you every trick you know. Do you imagine I can't predict you?"

"Then predict this," Rafael said, and her composure finally fractured—not into tears but into fury, white-hot, three years of it. "You don't get to decide. Not anymore. You lost that when you lied to me for fifteen years. When I found the diagrams in your book and asked you what they were and you looked me in the eye and said *old theory, nothing more*. When I ran because I couldn't bear to look at you and couldn't bear to stop loving you, and both of those things nearly killed me. You had your chance to protect me by telling the truth. You chose silence. So no—you don't get to volunteer for my death like you're settling a debt. This isn't yours."

"It is entirely mine," Serah said, and now she did raise her voice, and the candles guttered, and Tam woke with a start and Maretta hushed him. "Girl, listen to me. I built those chains. My hands wove half that script. My consent is in the weave alongside his—I gave it freely on that stone, as witness and co-author, and that means there is a thread in the binding with my name on it. Not metaphorically. Literally. The working knows me. When you stand on that stone and begin to unpick, the chains will recognize the hand that tied them, and they will come to me *gladly*, the way a dog comes to its master. I can take them without a struggle. You would have to wrestle a god for them."

"That's not penance, that's suicide," Odile said.

"Yes," Serah said simply. "It is. I am sixty-eight years old. I have perhaps ten winters left even if I hide in this marsh until my bones give out, and every one of those winters would be spent knowing what I did and what it cost. Versus a girl of twenty-three who has barely begun." She turned back to Rafael, and gentled her voice, because anger was a poor argument against love and always had been. "Child. I have watched you since you were small enough to fit in a bread oven. I taught you to knot luck-charms and read weather in bird-flight and lie beautifully to tax collectors. Everything good in your craft came from me, and everything good in mine came from wanting to give you a world that wasn't rotten. Let me finish the work I started. Let me carry what I made. That is not you dying for me. That is me, at last, paying what I owe."

"And what about what *we* owe?" Rafael's voice dropped low, shaking. "You think you're the only one with debts? You think I ran from you and stayed away three years and didn't pay for it every single day? I sold fake charms to frightened people while I could have healed them. I hid while Maretta's village burned. I hid while Tam was taken. Hiding is what I *do*, Serah. It's the one skill you gave me that I mastered completely. And I am done hiding. If I stand on that stone and take those chains, it won't be debt. It will be the first free choice I've ever made with my whole self. Doesn't that count for anything? Doesn't that matter more than your arithmetic of guilt?"

"It matters," Serah whispered. "God help me, it matters more than you know. That's why I can't allow it."

"You can't *allow*—"

"I am the elder witch of this line. Like it or not, girl, you are my heir, and there are laws older than crowns. The maker of a binding has first claim on its unmaking. It is not courtesy. It is structure—the working itself will bend toward me whether you wish it or not. If you try to take the chains while I live and stand willing, the transfer will *fight* you, and a fight on that stone, with the Veil awake, is a fight you lose." She held up a hand before Rafael could speak. "But hear the rest. Hear all of it, because I did not spend thirty years studying this working to misuse it now."

She turned the grimoire toward them all, to the margin, to the open-hand diagram.

"There is a third path. It is narrow and it is cruel and it may be nothing, but I found it when I was young and arrogant and looking for loopholes, and I have never forgotten it. The chains require a consenting vessel—but consent does not require a living soul's full strength. A vessel already hollowed. Already emptied. A soul that has given nearly everything else and has only its willingness left." She looked at Rafael, and her eyes were wet and steady. "You told me you'd pay any price. Well. The unbinding will demand your gift—all of it, burned away as payment for the working, that is fixed and unavoidable, the stone takes the witch's power as its toll. If you do the unbinding, you come off that stone powerless. Empty. And an empty vessel, freely offering what remains—" her breath caught, "—might hold the chains lighter than any full soul could. Might. It is a thread of hope thinner than spider silk, and I will not pretend otherwise. But it means the sacrifice is shared. Your strength pays the toll. My claim takes the chains. Neither of us walks away whole, and neither of us carries it alone."

The room was very quiet. Tam had crept forward to stand beside Rafael, and the boy put his small hand in hers without looking at anyone, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

"That's why you really called us here," Rafael said slowly. "Not to argue. To offer this."

"I called you here to teach you the working," Serah said. "What you do with the teaching is yours. But I will tell you what I told four other witches thirty years ago, kneeling on cold stone with a terrified boy between us: whatever we choose, we choose it together, or it fails. The binding was forged by many hands. So will its ending be."

Maretta spoke first, soft and fierce. "Then we choose tonight. Before the road. Before the Cloaks find this marsh. Together."

Odile nodded once. Jules set down his knife. Tam squeezed Rafael's hand.

Serah closed the grimoire and looked at her student—her daughter in every way that mattered—and saw that the girl was crying silently, and that she was also smiling, and that both of those things were true at once, which was the whole of what Serah had learned in sixty-eight years and never managed to say aloud.

"Then light the candles," Serah said. "We begin at moonrise. And Rafael—" she paused, gathering the last of her courage, the kind that mattered more than any working, "—when we reach the throne room, whatever happens on that stone, you will not be alone. I swear it on the chains themselves."

Outside, far to the north, past the marsh and the dark fields, the sky over Corvane glowed a dull, hungry orange—the city burning, the Veil baiting its traps, the road ahead shorter than any of them wished.

Moonrise was three hours away.
