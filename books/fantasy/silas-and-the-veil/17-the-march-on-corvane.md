## 17. The March on Corvane

The news came to them on the wind, the way bad news always did—carried in fragments, half-heard, growing worse with every telling.

Odile Voss stood at the edge of the marshlands where the reeds gave way to hard ground, one eye squinting against the grey morning light, and watched a peddler's cart rattle toward them along the levee road. The horse was lame. The man driving it was worse. He pulled up when he saw the ragged encampment by the water—thirty-odd souls now, fugitives who had trickled out of Corvane over the past weeks like blood from a wound—and his hands were shaking so badly he could barely work the brake.

"City's closed," he said, before anyone could ask. "Walled at dawn yesterday. Martial law. The criers are saying—" He stopped, swallowed, started again. "They're saying any witch found within the walls will be burned in the Grand Square. Publicly. Starting tomorrow."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved through the reeds behind Odile with its dry whispering, and somewhere among the tents a child began to cry and was hushed.

"Starting tomorrow," Maretta repeated quietly. She stood at Odile's shoulder, arms crossed against the cold, her healer's hands wrapped in clean linen as they always were these days. "Not arrests first. Not trials."

"They're not hunting witches anymore." The peddler's voice dropped, as if the words themselves might be overheard by something vast and listening. "That's what folk are whispering. They're *baiting* them. The burnings are for show. Anyone who comes into the city to save someone—that's who they want. That's who they're waiting for."

Odile felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She thought of Rafael's face three nights ago, lit by marsh-firelight, when she had laid out what the grimoire demanded: the binding-stone beneath the throne, the unbinding, the price. She thought of the way Rafael had not hesitated. Had not even blinked.

*They know,* Odile thought. *Whatever wears the king's crown felt us coming, and it has set a trap made of other people's screams.*

She turned from the road. "Jules. Tam. Get everyone moving. We're going to Corvane."

---

They argued about it, of course. They always argued; it was how the family loved each other.

"You heard him," Jules said, pacing the length of the largest tent, his lean frame coiled tight as a snare-line. "It's a trap. It is the most obvious trap in the history of traps. We should go north. Over the mountains. There are enclaves—"

"There are no enclaves," Odile said flatly. "There are places that will hide us until the Grey Cloaks find them, and then there are ash-stains. I've run before, Jules. I ran from the border wars until there was nowhere left to run, and you know what I learned?" She held up her scarred hand, counting fingers. "One: they don't stop. Two: running makes you smaller every year, until there's nothing left of you but the hiding. Three—" she folded the fingers down into a fist— "I'm done being small."

"The burnings start tomorrow," Jules said. "People we sheltered. People whose names we know. Fenwick the cooper. Old Sable with her weather-sense. The twins from Tanner's Row. They'll be dragged into that square and—"

"I know their names," Odile said, and her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and she saw Jules hear the crack and soften. Twenty years they had been together, and he still could not bear the sound of her breaking. It was the one weapon she had never wanted.

He stopped pacing. Stood in front of her. "Then we go," he said. "But we go smart. And Odile—" his hand came up, calloused palm against her cheek, thumb brushing the old scar where her eye had been, "—you don't get to die for all of them. You only get to die for me. Those are the terms."

A wet, ugly laugh escaped her. "Terrible terms."

"I know. Take them anyway."

Behind them, unnoticed until now, Rafael stood at the tent flap with Serah's grimoire clutched to her chest like a shield, watching them with an expression Odile couldn't name—something between grief and hunger, the look of a woman seeing what a family looked like and knowing she'd have to help break the world to keep hers.

"We leave within the hour," Odile said to her. "All of us."

"All of us," Rafael agreed softly. "Even the ones who can't fight."

"*Especially* the ones who can't fight," Odile said. "That's the whole point of the exercise."

---

The column formed on the levee road as the sun climbed, and it was not an army. Odile looked back down its length and felt something twist in her chest that was equal parts despair and fierce, stubborn pride.

Thirty-seven souls. A handful of ex-soldiers like herself, grey-haired and stiff-jointed. Maretta and two apprentice healers. Tam, thirteen years old, walking beside Rafael with his hand resting on the stones of the levee wall as though greeting old friends. Jules with the supply cart and the crossbow bolts he'd been fletching all night. A dozen hedge-witches and charm-sellers and village cunning-folk who had never held anything sharper than a pruning knife, walking toward a city full of soldiers because their people were inside it.

And at the head, when she allowed it, Rafael—small, dark-eyed, hollowed from the working that had bought their passage past Mother Quill's tolls, carrying a dead woman's book and a living king's plea.

"We're not an army," Odile told them, pitching her voice to carry. She stood on the levee wall, one eye on the road ahead, one on the faces below. "In case anyone was wondering. We are thirty-seven people with bad weapons and worse odds. If any of you have changed your minds, the north road is right there, and no one will think less of you. I'll think less of you, but I'll say nothing."

Nervous laughter rippled down the line. Good. Fear laughed at or it curdled.

"But here is what we are," she went on. "We are everyone the Veil tried to erase. Every one of you was supposed to be registered, or burned, or buried in some hole where no one would remember your name. And instead you're standing on this road in broad daylight, walking toward the thing that hunted you, because your people are in that square tomorrow and you refuse to let them die alone. That's not an army. That's something the Veil has never once faced in twenty years of wearing our king." She let the silence sit. "It doesn't know what to do with us. Remember that, when your hands shake. It doesn't know what to do with us."

Tam raised his voice, thin and clear: "The stones say the same thing. Sort of. They say—the city's foundations are scared, but they remember being built by hands like ours, and they're waiting."

"The stones are waiting," Odile repeated. "Well. Let's not keep them."

They walked.

---

They gathered more as they went. That was the thing Odile had not predicted, though perhaps she should have.

Word traveled faster than feet. Refugees who had fled Corvane in the first weeks of the hunt—huddled in ditches, in barn lofts, in the root cellars of sympathetic farmers—saw the column pass and came out blinking into the daylight. A tanner with smoke-scars up both arms. An old woman who read fortunes and had hidden her cards in a loaf of bread for four months. Two brothers who could quiet animals with a hummed note, leading a small menagerie of dogs and one deeply confused goat.

By the second evening they numbered sixty. By the third, ninety.

And Odile learned something on that road that she would carry the rest of her life: fear is contagious, but so is courage, and courage spreads slower and holds longer. Each new soul who joined straightened the spines of the ones already marching. The column stopped looking like flight and started looking like intent.

Rafael walked the line each evening, and Odile watched her do it. The young witch would stop at each fire, sit with each frightened stranger, and listen—not heal, not charm, just listen—and by the time she moved on, the person she'd sat with sat taller. Odile had spent twenty years commanding soldiers and ten more commanding smugglers, and she knew leadership when she saw it, and it looked nothing like hers. Hers was iron. Rafael's was gravity.

"She doesn't know she's doing it," Jules murmured that third night, settling beside Odile at the fire while the camp settled around them. Ahead, far off across the dark plain, Corvane glowed on the horizon—not with lamplight but with something redder, angrier. Pyres, maybe. Or districts burning. Impossible to tell at this distance, and the not-knowing was its own torture.

"She knows," Odile said. "She just thinks it's nothing. Thinks power is the lightning, not the pull." She turned her good eye toward him. Firelight caught the grey in his beard. "You've come around on her."

"I came around on her in a prison corridor, when she bled herself white putting Maretta back together." Jules fed a stick to the flames. "I just didn't want to say it out loud in case the world took it as a challenge."

Odile huffed. Beyond the fire, Rafael sat with Tam asleep against her shoulder, the grimoire open on her knees, her lips moving silently through the counter-ritual yet again. Memorizing. Preparing. Odile knew what the girl intended. Knew about the vessel, the chains, the price. Knew that Rafael meant to carry the Veil into her own soul and pay whatever the paying cost.

*Over my corpse,* Odile thought, and then, honestly: *or over hers, if it comes to choosing, and gods forgive me for which one I'll pick.*

No. Not that. Never that. There had to be another way. There had to be.

"Get some sleep," she said aloud. "Tomorrow we reach the outer farms, and after that, the walls."

---

The farms around Corvane had emptied. They passed holdings with doors standing open, meals rotting on tables, livestock wandering unfenced—a whole countryside that had simply fled or been taken. On the fourth day they found the first refugees from the city itself, and the stories they told made the march go silent.

The Grey Cloaks were not waiting for witches to come to the burnings. They were going door to door. Dragging people out by name—registered names, names given in good faith during the first weeks of the registry, when the crown had promised registration meant safety. Promised protection. Promised.

"They used the registry as a list," whispered a laundress who had escaped through the sewer grate Odile's own crew had kept open for years. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking. "Every name. Every address. They knew exactly where we lived, because we *told* them."

Maretta sat with the woman for a long time afterward, holding her hands, doing whatever healers did for wounds no salve could touch. Odile stood apart and let the fury build in her the way she'd once let it build before battles, feeding it log by log, because fury was useful, fury was fuel, and grief without fury was just drowning.

At dusk Rafael came to stand beside her at the column's head, looking down the long road toward the red smear of the city.

"My mother's name was on the registry," Rafael said quietly. "Before Serah took me. Before I could even do magic on purpose. Just—born marked, in a village that kept lists." She paused. "Serah burned the list. That was the first thing I ever saw her do. First thing that showed me what she was." A breath. "I used to think it was the most frightening thing I'd ever seen. Now I think it might be the kindest."

Odile said nothing. There was nothing to say that the road ahead hadn't already said better.

"Odile." Rafael's voice steadied. "If it comes to it—if the ritual needs a vessel and I'm not—I need you to promise me something."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. Whatever it is, the answer is no. You want me to promise I'll let you throw yourself into the fire alone. I won't promise that. I'll promise you this instead: nobody carries that thing alone. Not you, not Serah, not me. Alone is how the Veil wins. Alone is the only weapon it's ever had." She turned her good eye on the young witch and held her gaze. "We go in together or it doesn't happen. Swear it back to me."

For a long moment Rafael searched her face, looking, Odile supposed, for the loophole, the softness, the crack. She wouldn't find one. Odile Voss had held a bridge for six hours against three times her number with a spear through her thigh; there was no crack.

"...Together," Rafael said at last. "I swear it."

"Good." Odile clapped her shoulder, rougher than affection, gentler than she felt. "Now get some sleep. Tomorrow we knock on the devil's door, and I want everyone rested enough to be rude about it."

---

They came within sight of the walls at midday on the fifth day, and Odile called a halt in the shadow of a ruined granary, and the whole column stood looking at what waited for them.

Corvane rose against the sky, grey and vast and crowned with smoke. The great gates were shut. Along the battlements, figures moved—soldiers, and among them others, tall and wrong, robed in the color of old ash. Even at this distance Odile could feel it, or imagine she could: a pressure on the air, a hunger in the stones, the sensation of being *watched* by something that did not use eyes.

From the city, faint on the wind, came a sound that froze the blood of everyone standing on that hill. Singing. Hundreds of voices, chanting in unison—no hymn Odile knew, no tongue she recognized. The Veil's voice, multiplied through throats it wore like gloves.

Beside her, Tam pressed both palms flat to the earth. His eyes had gone distant, the way they did when the stones spoke loudly.

"What are they saying?" Odile asked him. "The chanters. What is it?"

Tam's face was pale as milk. "It's not words," he whispered. "It's—it's an invitation. The stones under the square are screaming, Odile. Something big is going to happen tomorrow, and the stones are trying to warn everyone, and nobody can hear them except me." He looked up at her, and his chin trembled, and his jaw set, and Odile's heart broke and hardened in the same instant. "They say the binding-stone is awake. They say it knows she's coming." He pointed one small finger—not at the walls, not at the gates, but at Rafael. "It's been waiting for *her*."

Silence fell over the column. Ninety people, and not one of them breathed.

Rafael stepped forward, to the front of them all, and looked up at the smoking city with an expression Odile had last seen on the night she'd sworn her oath at the marsh fires—terror and resolve fused into something that was neither, something stronger than both.

"Good," Rafael said. "I've been waiting for it too."

Odile closed her eye. Opened it. Drew breath.

"Well," she said, to ninety frightened souls on a hill beneath a burning sky. "You heard the lady. Let's not keep the devil waiting."
