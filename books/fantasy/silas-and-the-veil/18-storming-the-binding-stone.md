## 18. Storming the Binding-Stone

The sewers of Corvane smelled of everything the city wanted to forget, and Rafael had learned three years ago that a city's forgotten things were the safest places to stand.

She walked at the front of the column with Serah's grimoire open against her forearm, the pages warped with marsh-damp, the binding-script glowing faintly whenever they drew near one of the old way-marks. Behind her came the family—what remained of it, and more than she had ever expected to have. Odile moved like a blade wrapped in leather, one eye catching the witchlight Rafael held cupped in her palm. Jules carried Maretta on his back, the healer still gray-lipped from her weeks in the Cloaks' cells, her wrists bandaged where the iron had eaten into them. Tam walked between two freed fugitives, his small hand pressed flat against the sewer wall, listening.

"The stone hears us," he said for the third time since the marshes. "It's scared."

"Stones don't get scared," muttered one of the fugitives, a ferryman whose gift was calling fish.

"This one does," Tam said simply, and no one argued with him after that.

Rafael felt it too, now that they were close. The Veil's hunger saturated Corvane above them—it had for years, though she'd only learned to name it recently—but here beneath the palace district the hunger had a shape, a direction, like current pulling toward a drain. And threaded through that pull was something older and stranger: a pulse. Slow. Patient. The heartbeat of the binding-stone, beating out the rhythm of twenty years of captivity.

Aldric's heartbeat, or what remained of him.

*Keep walking,* she told herself. *Don't think about what it will cost. Think about the door.*

There was always a door. Odile had taught her that in the first week, when Rafael had been too frightened to sleep in the tunnels. *Every wall has a door, little witch. Your job is to find it before the wall finds you.*

The first door betrayed them exactly as Mother Quill had promised it would.

They came up through the cistern access beneath Tanner's Row, following the route Quill's people used to move contraband within sight of the palace walls, and found the grate already open—and already watched. A Grey Cloak stood in the torchlight beyond it, tall and faceless in his ash-gray hood, and beside him stood Quill's lieutenant, Doss, wringing his soft hands and not meeting anyone's eyes.

"I'm sorry," Doss said, and he sounded like he meant it, which somehow made it worse. "She gave me a choice. My people or yours. I've got forty souls under the Sable Stair, Rafael. Forty against your—" he counted, wincing, "—eleven."

Rafael didn't answer him. She was watching the Cloak, watching the way the hood turned toward her with the slow, tracking attention of a predator scenting blood. She could feel the thing inside it now—the Veil's attention, thin and cold as wire, reaching for her gift the way a hand reaches for a flame.

*THERE,* the attention said, without words. *THE ONE WHO CARRIES THE COUNTER-SONG.*

"Odile," she breathed.

Odile was already moving. "Jules—Maretta down, behind the pipe. Tam—" but Tam was already scrambling back into the dark, stones whispering around him like a flock of protective birds, and the fugitives were scattering along the walls, and the Cloak raised one gray-gloved hand and the torchlight bent toward it like iron filings to a lodestone.

What happened next happened fast, and Rafael would spend the rest of her life remembering it in fragments.

Jules threw Maretta clear and put himself between the Cloak and the boy, roaring something wordless, and the Cloak flicked its wrist and the darkness itself went solid—a whip of shadow that caught Jules across the chest and hurled him into the cistern wall hard enough to crack stone. Odile came out of the dark low and fast, the way she'd come out of the dark at men twice her size for thirty years, and her knife opened the Cloak's throat before the shadow-whip could reform.

But the hood did not fall. There was nothing under it. Nothing at all, just a hollow where a man should have been, and the gray robe kept standing, kept turning, kept *reaching*, and Rafael understood with a lurch of horror that killing these things was going to be much harder than anyone had promised.

"Down!" Serah's voice, cracked and old and absolutely certain.

Her old mistress stepped past her into the torchlight, and Rafael saw—for the first time since the marshlands, since the confession, since the half-forgiveness she still didn't know how to finish—Serah as she must have been fifty years ago. The hedge-witch planted her staff on the wet stone and *pulled*, and every drop of water in the cistern answered her. The sewer itself rose up: black water climbing the walls in ropes, condensing out of the air in sheets, wrapping the second and third Cloaks who came boiling up out of the access tunnel like gray moths. The robes thrashed. The hollow hoods screamed without mouths, a sound like wind through a keyhole, like the last breath of a dying fire.

"GO," Serah snarled over her shoulder. Her white hair was lifting in the wind of her own working, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "All of you. The grate. Now. I'll hold the door."

"You can't hold it alone—" Rafael started.

"I held a king's chains for twenty years, girl. I can hold a sewer." Serah's mouth twisted—not quite a smile, not quite an apology. "This is my penance. Let me *pay* some of it."

Rafael wanted to argue. She wanted to grab the old woman and drag her bodily into the tunnels and never let go, because Serah had been mother and jailer both, because forgiveness was supposed to have time to grow into something whole, because—

Because there was no time at all.

More robes in the tunnel behind. The pulse of the binding-stone quickening overhead, frightened, calling. Tam screaming her name. Jules hauling himself upright with blood sheeting down his face, one arm hanging wrong, saying through gritted teeth, "Get her to the stone, Rafa. Get her *up there*. That's the only door that matters."

So Rafael ran.

* * *

The way up was a service stair built for scullery maids and corpse-bearers, narrow and spiraling, and Rafael took it two steps at a time with the grimoire clutched to her chest and witchlight streaming off her skin because she no longer had the strength to hide it. Behind her, muffled by stone, she heard the battle continue—the roar of water, the keyhole-screaming, Serah's voice chanting in the old tongue, steady as a millwheel.

Then, abruptly, unsteadily.

Then silence.

Rafael stopped on the stair with her heart slamming, and Tam, who had caught up to her on legs far quicker than hers, pressed his palm to the wall and went very still.

"She's alive," he whispered. "Barely. The stones say she's... folded up small. Hiding inside a working. But she's alive, Rafael. She's *alive*."

"Then we finish this," Rafael said, "and we come back for her."

She made herself believe it. She made it true by deciding it was true, which was, she was learning, most of what courage was.

Above the service stair, the palace opened around them like the inside of a jewel gone rotten. They emerged through a laundry chute into corridors hung with banners Rafael didn't recognize—black silk worked with a pattern like ripples on deep water, the Veil's sigil replacing the crowned stag of Aldric's line. Servants glided past with trays and candles, their faces smooth as eggs, their eyes fixed on nothing. Sleepwalkers. The feast-hall thundered somewhere to the east; the Veil was entertaining the ambassadors it hadn't yet devoured, buying one more night of ordinary appearances while its hunters tore the city apart looking for her.

Tam tugged her sleeve. "That way. Down. It's *loud* down there, Rafael, it's so loud—"

"I know." She could feel it through her boots. The pulse had become a drumbeat, and beneath the drumbeat was a voice—or not a voice, but a pressure, a vast attention coiling beneath the throne room floor, and it knew she was coming. It had known since the cistern. Maybe since the marshes. Maybe since the day Serah first taught a girl of nine to knot a luck-charm and Rafael's knots came out *true*.

They crossed the antechamber to the throne room under cover of a procession of sleeping servants, Rafael cloaking them in a working of un-noticing that cost her more than she dared count. Jules came last, carrying Maretta still, refusing all help, his broken arm bound to his chest with a strip of banner-silk, his eyes burning with a fury Rafael had only seen once before—the night his shopkeeper neighbor burned.

The doors of the throne room stood open. Of course they did. It wanted her to walk in.

"No," Rafael whispered, and turned aside, and led them instead to the place the grimoire had described in Serah's own cramped hand: *the priest's door, behind the rood-screen, where the kings of old went down to swear their oaths upon the stone.*

The hidden stair behind it was older than the palace, older than Corvane, cut from rock that predated the city's founding. The walls sweated. The air tasted of iron and salt and something else—something like the moment before lightning. Tam whimpered and buried his face in Odile's neck when she caught up to them at the top of the descent, the smuggler arriving bloody and breathless with three freed fugitives at her back and a story about dead Cloaks in the laundry corridor that Rafael didn't ask her to tell.

And then the stair ended, and there it was.

The chamber beneath the throne was round and vast, its ceiling lost in darkness, its floor a single sweep of living rock. And in the center, rising from the rock like a tooth from a jaw, stood the binding-stone.

It was smaller than Rafael had imagined—waist-high, rough-hewn, unremarkable, the kind of standing stone that dotted every hillside in the northern provinces. But it *pulsed*. Light moved beneath its surface in slow waves, silver-gray, the color of the Veil, and chained around it—actual chains, iron gone green with age, each link thick as her arm—were bindings carved in script she knew better than her own name.

Serah's script. Serah's hand, thirty years younger, cutting those runes by torchlight with a young king watching and weeping and consenting.

"Oh," Rafael said softly. "Oh, Aldric."

Because she could feel him now. Faint, flickering, at the very bottom of the stone's pulse like a drowned man's face beneath ice. Twenty years of captivity, and still—still—something of him remained, curled around his own mortal will like a coal keeping itself alive under ash.

The Veil's attention crashed down on her like a wave.

*YOU BROUGHT IT HERE.* The voice filled the chamber, filled her skull, ground against her teeth. *YOU BROUGHT THE COUNTER-SONG INTO MY HOUSE. I WILL WEAR YOUR SKIN FOR A THOUSAND YEARS, WITCH. I WILL—*

"Rafa." Odile's hand on her shoulder, warm, real, callused. Grounding her the way she'd grounded Rafael through every terror of the last months. "Whatever you're hearing—don't listen. It lies. It's *made* of lying." Her one eye was fierce and wet. "You go to that stone. You do what Serah taught you. And we—" she gestured at the ragged, bleeding, exhausted remnant of their family, at Jules setting Maretta gently against the wall, at Tam standing straighter than any child should have to stand, at the fugitives forming a trembling line between Rafael and the stair, "—we hold the door. All of it. Every second you need."

"It might kill me," Rafael said. "The unbinding. Serah said—"

"Serah says a lot of things." Odile almost smiled. "So do I. Here's mine: you come back. That's an order, little witch. First one I've ever given you. You come *back*."

Rafael looked at them—really looked, letting herself see it, because if this was the last thing she saw she refused to see it blurred. Jules, who had hated her, bleeding for her. Maretta, who had healed strangers in a village that repaid her with fire, smiling at her through split lips. Tam, who heard stones speak, pressing his small hand briefly against her heart as he passed, whispering, "The stone likes you. It says hurry."

Family. Not the one she'd been born to. The one she'd chosen, and been chosen by, in tunnels and safehouses and shared bread.

"All right," Rafael said. Her voice didn't shake. She was almost proud of that. "Hold the door."

She walked to the binding-stone alone.

The chains hummed as she approached, recognizing her, recognizing the counter-song in her blood. She set both palms against the rough surface, and it was warm—warm as a living body, warm as a fevered brow—and the pulse beneath her hands stuttered, then steadied, then reached for her like a drowning man reaching for a rope.

Behind her, at the head of the stair, the darkness began to pour downward. Gray robes without end. The keyhole-scream rising to a shriek. Odile's war-cry, Jules's roar, the crackle of Maretta finding one last healing-turned-destroying working in her ruined reserves, Tam's high clear voice shouting words no child should know and every stone in Corvane obeyed.

Rafael closed her eyes, opened the grimoire to the page Serah had marked with a lock of her own white hair, and began to speak the unbinding.

The stone drank her light. The chains began, link by link, to glow.

And deep beneath the ice, the drowned man opened his eyes.
