## 19. Unbinding

The binding-stone sang when Rafael laid her palms against it.

Not a sound the ear could catch—a vibration that climbed through her wrists, her elbows, her ribs, until it hummed in the roots of her teeth. The chamber beneath the throne was older than Corvane itself, older than the crown, a hollow of black granite ribbed like the inside of some enormous throat. The stone before her was no larger than a millstone, but it *moved*, its surface crawling with script she had first seen in Serah's grimoire three years ago and had dreamed of every night since. Chains of light lay coiled through it, dense as rope, and at their center something breathed.

Behind her, at the top of the stair, Serah's voice rose in a language Rafael half-knew—wards being thrown up against the Grey Cloaks who would come, were already coming. Jules's boots hammered somewhere above, carrying wounded. And below, through the throne room's floor, through yards of marble and granite, she could feel the king.

She had felt him since she entered the palace. A second heartbeat under the city's own, slow and wrong, like a drum struck underwater. Now, with her hands on the stone, she could feel how thin the wall between them truly was.

"Rafael." Odile's voice, rough and low, from the stair. The one-eyed woman stood with a stolen sword in her fist, blood on her collar that might have been hers or someone else's. "Whatever you're going to do—do it fast. They're coming."

"I know." Her mouth was dry. She thought of Tam hearing stone speak, of Maretta's burned village, of the neighbor whose shop had burned around him, of Aldric's coded pleas in the margins of ledgers: *I am here. I am still here.* "Get everyone clear. If this goes wrong, I don't want you under it."

Odile held her gaze a moment longer—one eye fierce, one socket empty and unashamed—and then did something Rafael did not expect. She gripped Rafael's shoulder, hard, once, like a soldier acknowledging an order she hated.

"Don't go wrong, then," she said, and was gone up the stairs.

Rafael turned back to the stone.

The ritual was not complicated. That was the horror of it. Serah had taught it to her in the marshes in a voice gone quiet with shame: *unmake the knot as it was tied*. The binding had been forged with blood freely given and power freely spent; it could only be unwoven the same way. A witch of sufficient strength would open herself to the chains, take them into her own soul, and hold them there—forever, or until another vessel freely accepted them. There was no third door. Serah had argued for it as her penance; Rafael had refused to let the old woman walk into that dark alone, and they had fought, and in the end Rafael had simply been faster, and stronger, and here.

She drew the knife from her belt. Old iron, bought from a night-market stall years ago for two coppers, used since for nothing more sinister than cutting twine for luck-knots. It seemed absurdly small.

"Right," she whispered, to no one, to herself, to the thing breathing in the stone. "Let's see what you're made of."

She opened her palm along the old scar-line and pressed her bleeding hand flat to the binding-stone, and spoke the first word of the unbinding.

* * *

Aldric had not seen the sun in twenty years.

He saw it sometimes in memory—the grey-gold light of the training yard at dawn, when he had been a prince and his body had belonged to him and the worst bargain he had ever considered was which horse to ride. But the sun itself, the true sun on true skin, had been taken from him the day he knelt beneath this very stone at nineteen years old and said yes to a voice that promised to save his dying kingdom.

*Save,* the Veil had said. *And be saved.*

It had not mentioned the price. Or rather—it had mentioned everything except the only price that mattered.

Now he sat in the dark behind his own eyes, in the small cell of self the Veil allowed him, and felt the thing wearing his body move through the world. Tonight it moved strangely. Restless. It had abandoned the feast above—the masked lords and sleepwalking servants—and come down, down, drawn by something it feared. He had felt its fear only twice in twenty years, and both times, afterward, witches had died.

But tonight the fear smelled different. Tonight, beneath the Veil's cold fury, he felt something else. Something like hope, and it hurt worse than despair ever had, because hope was the thing he had trained himself out of, hope was the hook that kept a prisoner a prisoner—

The stone flared.

Light poured through the chamber, gold-white and searing, and Aldric felt the chains shudder. Not the chains that held *him*—the chains themselves, the great knot of binding that ran through the stone and through his soul and through the thing coiled around it. Someone was pulling at the knot. Someone was *untying* it.

*No,* the Veil screamed, and for the first time in twenty years, it screamed through his mouth, in his voice, and the sound was so humanly afraid that Aldric laughed—laughed for the first time since his coronation, a cracked and rusty sound in the dark of himself.

*What is it,* he called into the dark. *What are you afraid of?*

The Veil did not answer. It tore free.

* * *

Rafael felt the king's body die before she understood what was happening.

The chains came loose all at once—not gently, not in the ordered sequence Serah had described, but tearing, like roots ripped from soil—and the vast cold presence that had ridden them for twenty years poured out of the failing flesh it had worn and turned on her.

She had prepared for many things. For wards, for hunters, for the stone resisting. She had not prepared for *this*: the air of the chamber thickening into something almost solid, pressing on her from all sides, a pressure without shape or face but with attention, terrible and total, the way a hawk attends a field mouse. The Veil had left the king because the king was nearly spent anyway, because a body kept alive unnaturally for two decades had nothing left to give—and because she, Rafael Okonkwo, charm-seller of Bellows Street, was a better vessel. Younger. Stronger. Freely offered.

*Freely offered,* she reminded herself, gasping, as the pressure crushed the breath from her lungs. That was the hinge of the whole working. The chains needed a willing soul. The Veil could crush her, could kill her—but if she died unwilling, the binding would fail, and the thing would be loose in the world with nothing to cage it.

So it would not kill her. It would make her *want* to let it in.

Cold slid into her mind like water finding cracks. It showed her Bellows Street at night, the market lamps, the smell of fried dough and river mud. It showed her Odile's table, Tam laughing, Maretta humming over her herbs. Then it showed those things burning. It showed the Grey Cloaks finding the tunnels. It showed Tam in a salt-and-iron cell, screaming. *All of this ends,* the cold whispered, in a voice like pages turning. *If you open. All of this continues, if you do not. You are already tired. You are already hollowed. Let me carry it.*

"I know what you are," Rafael ground out, kneeling, her hand still welded to the stone by will and blood. "You're the thing that makes people think surrender is wisdom."

She spoke the second word.

The chains answered. She felt them turn toward her the way iron filings turn toward a lodestone—felt the first link touch her soul, and it was like touching winter, like swallowing a river in flood. Her gift rose up in her to meet it, all the power she had hidden for three years behind hedge-tricks and luck-knots, all the power she had never dared show anyone, and the chains began to wind into it, into *her*, and it was agony and it was homecoming and she screamed and did not stop speaking.

The third word required something more than words. It required her to look into the binding—to go *inside*—and find the man at its center and ask him.

She went.

* * *

Inside, there was no up or down, no light except what she brought with her, which was little and flickering. Rafael stood—or existed—in a darkness that was not empty but *occupied*, and across it, faint as a figure seen through deep water, stood a man.

He was younger than the coins with his face. That was her first stupid thought. Nineteen, maybe twenty, though his eyes were ancient, worn smooth as pebbles in a stream. He wore no crown. His hands, she noticed, were shaking.

"You're real," she said. Her voice sounded strange here, close and echoless.

"So are you." He studied her the way a starving man studies bread. "You're the witch. From the margins. You read them."

"The ledger. Tam found it. We—" Her throat closed. Twenty years of coded pleas scratched into state documents, smuggled out in confiscated goods, prayed over by a prisoner who could not know if anyone would ever read them. "We found you. We're sorry it took so long."

Something moved through his face that he clearly did not trust enough to call hope. "The Veil. Where is it?"

"It left you. It's—" Above them, beyond the walls of this inner dark, she felt the pressure redouble, furious now, the whole weight of the ancient thing throwing itself against her refusal. "It's trying to get into me instead. I'm taking the chains. That's the working. But I need—" She steadied herself. "The binding was tied with your consent. It can only untie with your consent too. Your will is the last lock, Aldric. Yours, or nothing holds."

He was silent a long moment. In the dark, she watched him understand—that freedom meant the chains went elsewhere, that they went into *her*, that this stranger who had read his marginal prayers was about to spend her life's power caging the thing he had carried for twenty years.

"No," he said.

Rafael's heart dropped through the dark. "Aldric—"

"You'll die," he said. "Or worse. I've lived inside it. I know what it does to a soul, year over year. I won't—" His voice cracked, nineteen years old suddenly, a boy who had said *yes* once to save his kingdom and had regretted it with every breath since. "I didn't escape my chains just to hand them to someone else. Find another way."

"There is no other way. There's only this, or it walks free."

"Then let it walk free and damn me—"

"Aldric." She crossed the dark to him—however crossing worked here—and took his shaking hands in hers. Up close he was translucent, worn thin, a candle burned to its last quarter inch. She understood suddenly how little of him was left. The Veil hadn't just ruled his body. It had been *keeping him alive*, feeding him its cold sustenance for twenty years, and the moment the chains finished moving, the moment it fully left—he would gutter out like this, like a lamp with no oil.

He knew it too. She saw it in his face.

"That's why you said no," she said softly. "You think your death buys me nothing. You think if you refuse, you save me from the chains and yourself from the ending."

"Isn't that reason enough?"

"No." She squeezed his hands. "Listen to me. I have a family. Did you know that? Three months ago I had nobody—I sold luck-knots and hid in plain sight and I was so *lonely*, Aldric, lonelier than you, because at least you knew what you'd lost. And then a boy heard stones talking about me, and a one-eyed soldier took me in, and a healer fed me soup, and a man who hated me learned to stand beside me in a fight. I know exactly what I'm fighting for. Do you?"

His eyes were wet. Kings, she supposed, were not permitted tears even in their own souls.

"My kingdom," he whispered. "My people. I made a bargain to save them and it became the thing devouring them."

"And the only way to save them now is to say yes one more time. To me. Not to it." She lifted his hands, folded them around hers. "Give me your will, Aldric. The last of it. Not because you owe anyone—because you *choose*, one final time, freely, and that choice is the thing the Veil has never been able to take from you. Don't let it win by making your last act a refusal. Make it a gift."

The dark trembled. Far above, muffled through layers of stone and soul, she heard Serah's wards cracking, heard Odile shouting, heard the Grey Cloaks coming down. There was no time. There had never been any time.

Aldric looked at her—at this stranger, this charm-seller, this witch who had crossed a burning city to reach him—and something in his worn-smooth eyes kindled.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my mother told me the measure of a king is what he gives away freely." A ghost of a smile. "I gave badly, at nineteen. Let me give well now."

"Yes," Rafael breathed.

"Then take it. Take all of it—my will, my strength, whatever's left of me. Finish it." His grip tightened, mortal-strong for one last moment. "And witch—when it's done, when you've paid—live. Live loudly. That's an order from your king."

"I never registered you as my king," Rafael said, crying and laughing at once.

"Good," said Aldric. "Then consider it a request from a friend you met in the dark."

And he opened himself, and gave.

* * *

Strength poured into her like dawn into a valley.

It was not much—she could feel how little remained of him, a cup scraped to its dregs—but it was *freely given*, and freely given was the coin this working traded in, and the last lock turned. The chains leapt. Rafael felt them pour out of the dying stone, out of the space where a king had knelt for twenty years, and into her—link after link after link, cold and vast and endless, winding through her gift, drinking it, and her power went into them the way water goes into sand, gladly, completely, all of it, everything she had hoarded and hidden and feared for thirty years.

She held the Veil as it came.

It fought. Of course it fought—it threw its whole ancient weight against her soul, whispering, promising, threatening, showing her the burning tunnels and then, when that failed, offering her the crown itself, offering her Corvane kneeling, offering her everything, and Rafael Okonkwo, who had spent her whole life making herself small, stood in the center of her own soul and said, in the voice of a woman who had finally stopped hiding:

"No. You're mine now. Be small."

And the Veil—coiled, furious, ancient—was chained.

* * *

Above, in the ruined chamber beneath the empty throne, Rafael Okonkwo knelt over a dead stone and a dying man.

The pressure was gone. The cold was gone. The silence that remained was the ordinary silence of deep places, and it rang in her ears like bells.

Aldric lay where the Veil had dropped him, and he was *old*—twenty years arriving all at once, silver flooding his hair, his face slack and human and terribly, peacefully finite. His eyes found hers. They were just eyes now. Just a man's.

"Free," he whispered. The ghost of the smile again. "It's so quiet."

"I know," Rafael said. She gathered him up, his head against her shoulder, this stranger she knew better than anyone alive. "I've got you. You're not in the dark anymore."

"Did we—" His breath was shortening. She could feel it, the lamp-flame fluttering. "The city. My people."

"Will be free. I promise. I'll make sure of it."

"Live loudly," he murmured. "That was... a request. From a friend..."

"From a friend in the dark," she said. "I remember."

He smiled—all the way, this time, a young man's smile on an old man's face—and Aldric, King of Corvane, died in her arms with his own name in his own mouth, and the last breath out of him was not fear.

Rafael held him as the light faded, and reached for her gift—the warm current that had run beneath her skin since childhood, her secret, her burden, her worth—

And found nothing there.

Only silence. Only her own two hands, empty and human and free.

Somewhere above, boots on stone, and Odile's voice calling her name, ragged with fear—and Rafael, hollowed and grieving and unbearably light, began to laugh, and to answer.
