## 20. The Price of the Crown

The binding-stone sang.

Rafael felt it through her knees, through her palms pressed flat against its surface, a note so deep it was less sound than pressure—the hum of twenty years of chains wound tight around a living man. The chamber beneath the throne was small and round and old, older than Corvane, older than the crown, and the walls wept a slow condensation that caught the stone's pale light and scattered it like drowned stars.

She had thought the unbinding would feel like cutting. It did not. It felt like *unraveling*—like picking apart a knot with fingers gone numb from cold, each loop looser than the last, each one fighting her.

And the Veil fought her.

It came at her as cold first, then as hunger, then as a voice made of every voice she had ever feared: Serah's, the criers', her own. *You are not strong enough,* it said. *You were never strong enough. Give me the king and go home to your little charms.*

"His name is Aldric," Rafael said aloud, because naming things had always been her armor. "And you don't get to keep him."

The chains rose up out of the stone like serpents of black glass, and she took them.

That was the working—that was what Serah's voice in her memory had taught her, what the counter-ritual demanded, what no grimoire could make gentle. A witch of sufficient power must take the binding into herself. Not break it. *Hold* it. Become the vessel, the lock, the cage. She had volunteered in the marsh-light with Serah arguing and Odile silent and Tam gripping her sleeve, and she had meant it then, and she meant it now, with the Veil pouring into her like winter pouring into an open door.

It hurt in ways she had no words for. It hurt in places she hadn't known a person could be hurt—old memories, future hopes, the shape of her own name. She felt it tasting her, cataloguing her, learning the geography of Rafael Okonkwo so it could wear her the way it had worn a king. She felt it find her gift, that deep warm river she had hidden for three years behind hedge-tricks and luck-knots, and fasten onto it with something like delight.

*Yes,* it breathed. *This will do.*

"No," Rafael said, and pulled.

The last chain came free of Aldric all at once, and the recoil threw them both across the chamber floor.

* * *

For a moment there was only silence, and the ringing absence of the stone's song, and Rafael on her back staring at the vaulted dark, unable to move, unable to feel her hands.

Then someone groaned beside her, and she turned her head, and it was him.

Not the thing wearing the king. The king. Aldric lay on his side in the pooled light, and he looked—Rafael searched for the word and found only the plainest one—he looked *lighter*. Twenty years of held breath leaving a body all at once. His face, which she had seen twisted into the Veil's terrible rictus in the corridors above, was slack with exhaustion, and human, and old. He was younger than Serah. He looked older. He looked like a man who had been carrying something enormous and had finally set it down.

"You're still here," he said. His voice was rusted from disuse—from *his own* disuse, she realized; how long since he had spoken words that were entirely his? "The witch. You're still here."

"I'm still here." She crawled toward him. Her arms shook. Everything shook. "So are you. Just you. I felt it let go."

"It didn't let go." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one used to live. "You took it. I felt you take it. Foolish, brave girl." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it stopped his breathing sounded wrong. Thin. Like wind through a cracked window. "I signed my first death warrant at nineteen. Did you know that? My hand. My hand moved, and I watched it, and I have watched it for twenty years."

"I know," Rafael whispered. "I read your letters. In the margins. We read them."

Something in his face broke open—not grief exactly, or not only grief. Relief so vast it looked like grief's twin. "Someone read them," he said. "All those years, I told myself: if even one soul reads them, then I am not alone in here. Then the man is still the man." His eyes found hers, and they were clear, so clear, brown and tired and *free*. "What is your name?"

"Rafael."

"Rafael." He said it carefully, like setting down something fragile. "Thank you is too small a word. I want you to know that. Whatever I say next—and I suspect I haven't much next left in me—know that thank you is too small."

"Aldric—" She reached for his hand. His fingers were cold. When had they gotten so cold? "Stay with me. The others are coming. Jules is carrying Odile up through the sewers right now, and Tam—there's a boy who hears stones talk, he'll want to meet you, he'll—"

"The Veil kept me alive." He said it simply, the way a man reports the weather. "Twenty years. It needed the body. It fed the heart when it faltered, mended the lungs when they tore, kept the blood moving because the blood was *its* blood by then." He drew a breath, and Rafael heard the rattle in it and hated it with her whole self. "You've taken it out. All of it. And what's left is just—" he smiled properly now, and it transformed him, made him briefly young, "—just me. Just Aldric. Do you understand? I am dying as *myself*. No one has died as himself in this palace in twenty years."

"Don't." Her eyes were burning. "Don't talk like that. Maretta can—she's a healer, the best I've ever—"

"She cannot heal what isn't wounded, child. I'm not wounded. I'm *empty*." He squeezed her hand, and his grip was stronger than she expected, brief and fierce. "Listen to me. There isn't much time and I have spent twenty years with nothing but time, so indulge a king who is finally, finally only a man. Listen."

She listened.

"The registry will burn. Order it burned—it's in the archive, third shelf, under my seal. The Grey Cloaks answer to the Veil, not to me; without it they'll scatter like smoke, but some will fight to keep their power. Let Odile Voss hunt them. She'll be better at it than any soldier I ever commanded." Another breath, thinner. "And the ban. The ban on magic. Lift it. Not for the witches—for the city. Fear is a poor foundation for a kingdom. I built on fear because it wasn't *my* building anymore. Build on something else. Build on—" his gaze drifted to the binding-stone, dark now, dead, "—build on whatever she carries. She'll know what to do with it."

"I will," Rafael said, though her voice cracked in the middle. "I promise. I'll see it done. Every word."

"Good." He closed his eyes. Opened them again, and there was wonder in them, genuine childlike wonder, as if the ceiling of the chamber were the most astonishing thing he had ever seen. "It's quiet," he murmured. "Do you know how long it's been quiet? There was always... it was always *talking*, always hungry, always coiled under my tongue waiting to speak through me. And now—" he laughed, a small broken sound, "—now there's just me in here. Just me. It's the finest thing I have ever felt. Better than coronation. Better than love. Just *me*, at the end."

Tears slid down Rafael's face and dropped onto their joined hands. "I'm sorry," she said. "I'm sorry it cost you everything."

"It cost me nothing." His voice was fading now, going soft and far away, but he smiled with every word. "It gave me everything. An hour of myself. That's more than most kings ever get." His fingers tightened once around hers. "Rafael. The man in the margins says thank you. Tell your strange, brave family—the smuggler, the healer, the boy who hears stones—tell them a king owes them a debt no crown can pay. Tell them..." His breath caught. Held. Released. "...tell them he was glad, at the end. Glad and free and *himself*."

The light in the chamber seemed to gather toward him—or perhaps that was only Rafael's failing sight, blurring with tears. His hand went slack in hers, gently, the way a bird goes still, and the rattling breath slowed, and slowed, and stopped.

Just Aldric. At the end. Free.

Rafael bowed her head over his hand and wept without sound, because sound would have been too small a thing to hold what she was holding.

* * *

She didn't hear them come down. Later she would learn that Jules had carried Odile the last half-mile on his back, that Tam had run ahead through the dark guided by the stones' whispering, that Maretta had followed the smell of old magic to the sealed door—but in the moment there was only the sudden presence of light and voices and then Tam's small hand on her shoulder, and Odile's voice, rough with pain and something softer:

"Rafa. Rafa, look at me. Is it done?"

"It's done." Her voice came out sanded flat. "He's free."

Odile stood over the king's body for a long moment, one eye bright in the stone-light, and then she did something Rafael had never seen her do: she knelt. Slowly, wincing from her wounds, she knelt beside the dead king and bowed her head, and Jules—Jules, who trusted no crown and no king—removed his cap and stood silent, and Maretta murmured the old words over him, the ones burned out of her village along with everything else, and Tam asked in a whisper, "Was he nice?" and Rafael, wiping her face, answered honestly: "He was tired. And kind. And finally himself."

They buried him would come later—no, they would not bury him; he was a king, and kings belong to cities, and Corvane would want its grief public. But for now they wrapped him in Odile's cloak, the good wool one, the one she'd carried through three wars, and none of them mentioned it, and none of them would ever mention it, and it was the truest royal shroud any king of Corvane had ever received.

It was walking out through the sewers, Jules steadying her elbow, that Rafael noticed.

The silence inside her.

Not the Veil's silence—that horror was caged, chained into the vessel she had offered, locked beneath layers of working and will in the deepest part of her soul where she could feel it like a swallowed stone, cold and patient and *hers to hold*. No. This was another silence. Where her gift had lived—that warm river, that deep current she had drawn from since childhood, the sense of threads and knots and the sweet pressure of power gathering in her palms—there was nothing. Smooth, quiet nothing, like a riverbed after drought.

She stopped walking. The tunnel dripped around them. Tam looked back at her, sensing it before anyone, the way he sensed everything the earth felt.

"Rafa?"

"I can't feel it," she said. Her own voice sounded strange. "My gift. It's gone."

Maretta reached for her hands, professional instinct, checking pulse, checking warmth. "Payment," the healer said quietly. "The ritual demanded a witch's power as price. You knew that."

"I knew." And she had. She had known in the marsh-light, known when she volunteered, known every step of the way here—and knowing had not prepared her for the sheer *absence*, the way a phantom limb aches with everything that isn't there. She flexed her fingers. Nothing gathered. Nothing warmed. She was, for the first time since she was six years old, simply a woman. "I knew. I just didn't know it would feel like this. Like losing a language. Like forgetting my mother's face."

Tam slipped his small hand into hers. "The stones say you're still you," he offered. "They say the you-ness doesn't live in the magic part."

Rafael laughed—a wet, cracked, astonished sound—and pulled him close, and Odile put her arm around both of them, and Jules muttered something about sentiment while conspicuously not letting go of Rafael's other arm, and they walked together up through the dark toward the burning, waking city.

Above them, dawn was coming. Somewhere in the palace above, clerks would find the king dead in his chambers and a decree under his seal ordering the registry burned. Somewhere a city that had feared its own shadow for twenty years would wake to freedom it didn't yet understand. And somewhere deep beneath the throne room, in a small round chamber older than crowns, a dead binding-stone sat in silence—while inside Rafael Okonkwo, in the hollowed place where her gift had been, something ancient and chained and patient settled slowly into the dark, and waited, and was held.

Held by a woman with no magic left at all.

Only love. Which, she was beginning to understand, might have been the stronger working all along.
