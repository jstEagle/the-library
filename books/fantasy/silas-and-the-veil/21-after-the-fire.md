## 21. After the Fire

The smoke over Corvane had finally thinned to a grey memory by the third morning after, and Odile Voss stood in the doorway of the rebuilt tunnel kitchen and watched Rafael Okonkwo burn her hand on a kettle.

Not badly. Just the ordinary scald of a woman reaching without thinking, snatching her fingers back, swearing under her breath in the particular vocabulary of someone who had once been able to make water cool itself with a word and now could not.

"Here." Odile crossed the room, took Rafael's wrist, and held it under the pump spout. The water ran cold and clean through the new pipes Jules had bartered off a plumber who owed Mother Quill money. "You'd have done that differently, once."

"I'd have done it differently yesterday," Rafael said. Her voice was dry as old paper, but there was no bitterness in it. That was the thing that kept catching Odile sideways, like a stair that wasn't where her foot expected it. Three weeks since the binding-stone. Three weeks since Rafael had walked out from beneath the throne with the Veil chained into her soul and her gift burned to ash in the paying, carrying a dead king's weight in her arms and her own body still upright through nothing but stubbornness. Odile had seen soldiers survive things that should have killed them, and she had never once seen one come out the other side looking *settled*.

Rafael looked settled.

"You could have gone," Odile said. It wasn't the first time she'd said it. She said it the way she checked the tunnel braces—routinely, because routine was how you kept a thing true. "Quill would have gotten you papers. New name, passage south, anywhere past the border mountains. By midwinter nobody in Corvane would remember your face."

"By midwinter nobody in Corvane will be able to spell my name anyway." Rafael flexed her fingers under the water. The scald was nothing; it would heal like any ordinary hurt heals, slow and human. "And I told you. I'm staying."

"People say that when they've got nowhere else."

"Yes." Rafael turned her head and looked at Odile straight on, the way she almost never had in those first weeks in the tunnels, back when she'd been all lowered eyes and hedge-tricks and lies about how small she was. "People do. But I'm not saying it for that reason, and you know it, and I think you keep asking because you want me to say it again."

Odile grunted. Twenty-two years in the army had taught her every way there was to hide behind a grunt, and she used them all shamelessly.

Below them, faint through the floor, Tam's voice rose and fell. He was down in the old chapel gallery—the wide chamber where they'd sheltered forty fugitives during the worst of the hunt—teaching stone-listening to three children whose mothers were still deciding whether Corvane was safe enough to surface in. He'd come back from the Grey Cloak cells thin as a whipstock and quiet for a month, and then one day he'd started talking to the walls again, and the walls, he reported, were very glad the bad thing was caged. Maretta moved among the wounded upstairs in the safehouse proper, her hands steady, her gift intact and legal-or-soon-to-be, teaching two apprentice healers everything the village that burned her had tried to stamp out.

And Serah was in the ground up on the hillside above Bellows Street, under a rowan tree, in a grave Odile had dug herself because digging was the only part of grief she knew how to do with her hands.

* * *

They buried her on a Tuesday, because Serah would have hated the fuss of a weekend.

Odile had argued for the tunnels. *She was one of us at the end,* she'd said. *She held the Grey Cloaks off at the binding-stone door and it cost her everything. She belongs below.* But Rafael had shaken her head, and Jules—who had distrusted the old woman from the moment she'd limped into their lives trailing guilt like a torn cloak—had surprised them all by siding with Rafael.

"She spent fifty years above ground pretending to be ordinary," Jules said. "Hedge-row, herb-garden, chickens. Let her have the sky over her. She earned the pretense."

So they carried her up through the smuggler's door at dawn, seven of them plus three fugitives who owed the old witch debts they'd only just learned about, and Odile dug while the light came up grey-gold over the rooftops of a city still smelling of its own burning.

Serah had lasted nine days after the binding-stone chamber. The Cloaks' iron had done something to her that even Maretta's gift couldn't untangle—or perhaps, Maretta said quietly, wouldn't; perhaps the old woman had simply decided she'd paid enough and it was time to put the account down. In those nine days she'd talked almost constantly, hoarse and unsentimental, dictating corrections to the grimoire while Rafael wrote. Half of it was counter-rituals for bindings she'd helped forge. Some of it was recipes. One page, near the end, was just instructions for keeping rosemary alive through a Corvane winter, written in a hand that shook so badly Rafael had to finish the sentence for her.

"She never asked forgiveness," Rafael had said at the graveside, standing in mud with the rowan's bare branches scratching the sky. "I noticed that. Fifty years of guilt and she never once asked. She just—" Rafael had stopped, and pressed her palm flat against the raw earth, and Odile had watched her decide not to cry. "She left the rosemary page. I think that was her asking."

Jules had spoken the burial words—not any rite Odile recognized, something older, from whatever village had made him before Quill's rivals unmade it—and they'd filled the grave in with their hands because shovels felt wrong, and Tam had knelt and put his ear to the soil and reported, solemnly, that the hill was honored to take her.

Odile had stood at the edge of it all, one eye on the mourners and one on the street below, because even in grief she was a soldier first, and she had thought: *this is what a family funeral looks like. I've been to real ones. This is better.*

Her real ones had been ranks of strangers in dress uniform and a folded flag and a lie about how her brother died. This was mud under the fingernails of everyone she loved and an argument about rosemary.

Now, three weeks later, she climbed the stairs from the kitchen to the long room where Maretta worked, carrying two cups of chicory because tea cost more than the family could justify and coffee had stopped arriving when the harbor burned.

Maretta sat by the window with a girl of maybe ten, one of the chapel-gallery children, holding the child's arm in both hands. A burn scar, old, from the fire that had taken the girl's street. Maretta's fingers moved over it and glowed faintly, gold-green, the way they did, and the scar didn't vanish—it never vanished, that was the difference between Maretta and the miracle-mongers—but it faded from angry rope to pale thread, and the girl's pinched expression loosened into wonder.

"There," Maretta said. "That's yours to keep mended. Body does the rest if you feed it right. Go on, tell your mam dinner's at six."

The girl fled happily. Maretta sagged back in her chair and accepted the chicory with a grunt of thanks.

"Council sent a man yesterday," she said. "Proper council, city council, not the crown's. Wanted to know if I'd stand in the square on Repeal Day and heal publicly. Free, in front of witnesses."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'd think about it." Maretta blew on her cup. Her face did something complicated. "Three years ago I'd have spat at him. Burned out of my own village by neighbors I'd healed for free my whole life, and now they want a demonstration, like I'm a horse at market." She sipped. "But there'll be girls like that one, Odile. Dozens. Growing up thinking the gift is a death sentence. Somebody's got to stand in the square and let them see it's not."

Odile leaned against the doorframe. Below, faintly, Tam laughed at something, bright and unguarded, and the sound went through her like a splinter going in—painful and welcome at once.

"The ban's not lifted yet," she said. "Council talks. The regency council talks slower than anything alive."

"It'll lift. The Veil's caged, the Cloaks are scattered, half the nobility is claiming they opposed the registry all along." Maretta's mouth twisted. "Cowards move fast when the wind changes."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is why we keep the tunnels braced and the escape routes mapped, and you know it as well as I do." Maretta looked at her over the rim of the cup, healer's eyes, missing nothing. "You haven't slept properly since the raid. You're doing the thing where you count everyone twice a night."

"I do not count—"

"Tam. Then Rafael. Then me, then Jules, then the gallery, then the doors. In that order. Every night. The boy hears your boots, you know. He says the stones tell him you're coming before you get there. He thinks it's funny."

Odile drank her chicory and said nothing, which was agreement, and they both knew it.

* * *

She found Jules in the counting room, which was what they called the narrow brick vault off the main tunnel where the ledgers lived—supplies, debts, favors owed and owing, the whole fragile economy of a household that had survived three years underground by knowing exactly what it had.

He was hunched over the newest book, quill scratching, and he didn't look up when she came in. He never did anymore. That was new too, and good, and strange: for two years Jules had flinched at her footsteps, some old reflex from the years before they found each other, and sometime during the march on Corvane—she couldn't name the day—the flinching had simply stopped.

"Quill's runner came," he said. "We're clear. All debts from the prison raid are considered paid, and she's offering a standing arrangement—protection for the upper entrances, in exchange for first refusal on anything we salvage from the Cloaks' storehouses."

"We're not salvagers."

"We're not, but she doesn't know that, and the offer costs us nothing to hold in reserve." He set the quill down and rubbed his eyes. Ink on his fingers, always ink. "There's more. The regency council published the repeal draft. Public comment period. Thirty days."

Odile pulled up a crate and sat. "Real?"

"As real as anything they write. Rafael's name isn't in it—no amnesty named, no registry annulled yet, just the ban repealed 'in respect of the changed circumstances of the realm.'" His mouth went sour. "Lawyers' language. But it's a door. First door anyone's opened in twenty years."

"Changed circumstances," Odile repeated. Somewhere far beneath them, through sixty feet of rock and clay, the binding-stone hummed its low note that only Tam and Rafael could hear, the sound of a god-sized prisoner breathing behind a wall built from a dead woman's penance and a living woman's sacrifice. Changed circumstances. Yes.

"They'll want the witches registered anyway," Jules said quietly. "Mark my words. Softer registry. Kinder cage. Same bars."

"Probably."

"And you'll argue we should stay anyway."

Odile looked at him. Her partner of eleven years, her second-in-command, the man who had wanted to flee Corvane in the first month of the hunt and stayed because she'd refused to leave, and who had carried wounded men through burning streets in the final assault and never once said *I told you so*, though he'd earned it a hundred times over.

"I'll argue we should let people choose," she said. "Same as always. Door's open both ways. Always has been."

"Some of them will go south. Maretta's apprentices. Maybe Maretta."

"Maybe."

"And you'll dig the escape routes deeper regardless."

"Definitely."

Jules smiled—thin, tired, real—and turned back to his ledger. On the open page, in his careful hand, under a column headed *HOUSEHOLD*, he'd written the names. Not supplies. Not debts. Names: Odile. Jules. Maretta. Tam. Rafael. And beneath them, in smaller script, the gallery families, eleven households now, with notes beside each—*wants work, has skills, owes nothing, afraid of crowds*—the whole tender bureaucracy of sheltering people.

Odile read it upside down for a moment and then stood, because there was a lump in her throat and she was too old a soldier to let anyone see it.

* * *

She found Rafael at dusk on the roof of the safehouse, which was not a place anyone was supposed to go, and which Rafael had been going every evening for a week.

The city spread out below, scarred and mending. Scaffolding on the harbor warehouses. The black gap where the registry office had burned—by rioters, the third night after the Veil fell, though officially it had been an accident of the martial-law withdrawal. Lanterns coming out along Bellows Street, ordinary lanterns, ordinary people walking home under them without checking the shadows for grey cloth.

Rafael sat against the chimney with her knees drawn up, watching the lights, and Odile lowered herself onto the tar-paper beside her with a grunt her knees insisted on.

"Roof's not structural," Odile said.

"Nothing in this building is structural. Jules says the whole street is held together by habit."

"Habit's held worse together." Odile settled. For a while neither spoke. The evening cooled around them, and somewhere below a fiddler started up in a tavern, badly, cheerfully.

"I buried a king today," Rafael said at last. "Or what was left to bury. The palace gave him a state funeral—empty casket, full honors, the regents all wearing faces like wet paper. But Jules and I took the actual ashes upriver this morning. There's a bend in the water where the willows grow, past the last tollgate. He described it to me. Inside the binding, at the end. It was the place he remembered being happiest, before—" She shrugged. "Before the bargain. Before the Veil. He was nineteen and he'd skipped a hunting party to swim there, and he said it was the last time he ever did anything just because he wanted to."

"That's a hell of a thing to carry."

"I'm getting rid of it, bit by bit. Giving it to the river." Rafael turned her cup in her hands—chicory again, cold now. "Twenty years, Odile. Twenty years awake inside his own body, watching his hands sign the warrants. And at the end, when I was drowning in the binding and the Veil was crushing me, he reached out with what was left of his mortal will and he *held the door*. Gave me the last of himself so I could finish the working. I didn't know him. I'll never know him. And I loved him anyway, the way you love someone you've suffered beside." She looked out at the lights. "Is that mad?"

"No," Odile said. "That's the least mad thing I've heard all year."

Rafael laughed—a short, surprised sound, like the laugh had escaped custody. Then quiet again. Down in the street, the fiddler stumbled through a reel and recovered.

"Quill offered me passage," Rafael said. "Did Jules tell you? Papers, escort, a house in Callowmere with a garden. She said a woman who caged a god shouldn't have to live above her own prison." She paused. "Maretta thinks I should go somewhere the air doesn't hum. She's probably right. Every time I stand over the deep galleries I can feel it, the stone's note, the thing I carry answering the thing I chained. Some nights it's like sleeping next to a hearth. Some nights it's like sleeping next to a wolf."

"And?"

"And I'm staying." Rafael said it simply, without performance, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "I'm staying because Tam needs someone to teach him the theory behind what the stones tell him, before some academy gets its hooks in him and turns listening into a science. Because Maretta's going to stand in that square on Repeal Day and she shouldn't stand alone. Because Jules's ledger has my name in it, under household, and I read it when he wasn't looking and I'm not giving that up." She turned to Odile then, and her eyes in the dusk were steady and clear and entirely, ordinarily human. "Because three years ago I hid my gift so hard I nearly disappeared, and I thought the hiding was survival, and it was, but it was also dying slowly. And then a one-eyed soldier followed a chalk sigil down into the dark and fed me soup and lied to me about how much room there was in the tunnels, and I learned the difference between surviving and living, and it wasn't the magic that taught me. It was the table."

Odile said nothing. Her throat had that lump again, and this time she let it sit there.

"You keep waiting for me to leave," Rafael went on, gentler. "Every time I'm quiet, you check. Every time I look at the road south, you brace. I can see you doing it—you think you hide it well, and you don't, you hide things like a barn door hides things. So I'll say it plain, once, so you can stop bracing: I am not staying because I'm powerless and have nowhere else. I'm staying because this is mine. You're mine. The boy, the healer, the suspicious man with the ledger. I chose you before I had power to offer, and I choose you now that I have none, and if the choice proves anything it's that it was never about the power at all."

The fiddler below finished the reel. Someone clapped. A dog barked twice, joyfully, at nothing.

"Well," Odile managed, after a moment. "Good. Because I redug the east escape route last week and I'd hate to have wasted the labor."

Rafael grinned, and it transformed her face, made her look nineteen herself, made her look like someone who had never hidden from anything.

They sat until the stars came out over the mending city, two women on a rooftop, one armed with nothing but a knife and thirty years of soldiering, one armed with nothing at all, and between them and beneath them and all through the bones of the dark, the binding-stone hummed its patient note—and above it, in the long room, a boy listened to the stones and reported to his family that everything below was calm, and held, and home.

Down on Bellows Street, a lantern flickered in a window that had been dark for twenty years, and lit.
