## 1. The Bone That Sings

The knife knew the way before Noor did.

That was how he thought of it, anyway — not that his hands guided the blade, but that the blade remembered. Bone had grain the way wood did, only quieter, and if you held a piece up to the lamplight and turned it slow, you could see where it wanted to be carved. A curl of smoke. A bird mid-flight. The curve of a child's cheek.

This particular piece was a shinbone from a milk cow, boiled clean that morning in the big copper pot behind his workshop until the whole street smelled like soup and regret. It sat in the vise now, pale and patient, while Noor shaved off thin curls with the small hooked knife he'd owned since he was nine years old and too young to know better.

"Keepsake," he said to himself, because saying things out loud made them true. "A keepsake for Maren Ostrow. Nothing strange about it."

Nothing strange at all.

He'd been carving keepsakes for eleven years — little tokens of bone for people who had lost someone and needed somewhere to put their hands. A brooch shaped like a fishing boat for a drowned husband. A hairpin with a wren on it for a mother who used to whistle at birds. People came to him because he was good, and he was good because — well. Because of the other thing. The thing nobody knew.

Noor Nystrom heard the dead through the bones he carved.

Not all bones. Not always. But when he worked a piece meant for a grieving person, when his knife found the grain and followed it down into the marrow of the thing, sometimes — often — a voice would rise up through the shavings like warmth through floorboards. And the voice would talk.

He had never told anyone. Not once. Not in Thornfell, before it fell, and certainly not here in Grayharbor, where the Kingdom of Vess had ruled for forty years and the word *doorkeeper* was still, occasionally, scrawled on walls next to warnings nobody explained anymore.

*You carve,* his mother had told him, the last time they spoke properly, her hands over his on the knife. *You never tell. Carvers who tell get visited by men with gray cloaks, and then their families stop having names.*

She'd been right about that, in the end. Though not in the way she'd meant.

Noor shook the memory off the way you shake water off your sleeves and bent back over the bone.

* * *

Maren Ostrow arrived at dusk, which was when she always arrived, because grief kept farmer's hours even when the farming was done. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, with flour permanently in the creases of her knuckles and eyes that had the particular shine of someone who had cried herself dry and was now just waiting for weather.

"You've got the smell again," she said, standing in his doorway. "Boiled bone."

"It's a popular smell."

"It's a terrible smell." She came in anyway, hanging her shawl on the peg he'd installed at exactly her height two years ago, because that was the kind of thing you did for Maren Ostrow whether you admitted it or not. "Is it finished?"

"Nearly." He covered the half-carved shinbone with a cloth, an old habit, pointless. She'd seen him work a hundred times. But there were things under the cloth that weren't the carving, and those things were best left alone. "Sit. Tea's hot."

"I don't want tea. I want to know if it looks like him."

"It will look like him."

"Noor." She lowered herself onto the stool across from his bench, folding her hands like a woman preparing to negotiate a price. "It has been four months. Four months, and I have nothing. No body — they said the river took him, but Tam couldn't swim, so what was he doing *in* the river? No letter. No last words. Just a constable with bad breath telling me to accept the gods' judgment and stop asking questions." Her chin trembled and steadied, the way it had learned to. "I need something of him I can hold. You understand?"

He understood. He understood better than she would ever know.

"I'll finish tonight," he said. "Come tomorrow, after market."

She nodded, and then, because she was Maren, she leaned forward and gripped his wrist with flour-rough fingers. "You're a good boy, Noor Nystrom. Your mother raised you right, wherever she ended up."

Wherever she ended up. He smiled and didn't answer, and after a moment she let go, collected her shawl, and went home through the blue evening to a house with one plate too many in the cupboard.

Noor stood in the doorway a long time after she'd gone.

Then he shut the door, banked the lamp higher, uncovered the bone, and picked up his knife.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hearing the dead: it is not like the stories. There is no cold wind, no flickering candle, no ghost rising with its arms out. It is warm. That's the horror of it. The voice comes up through the bone like heat from a hearthstone, familiar as bread, and it settles into your skull the way a cat settles onto a lap — comfortable, entitled, certain it lives there now.

Tam Ostrow had been talking for three days.

He wasn't a chatty ghost, at least. He mostly hummed. He'd been a fisherman's son before the fish trade dried up, and he hummed the way fishermen hum, tunelessly and forever, a low drone that Noor had been listening to since the first cut of the knife. He'd gotten used to it. He'd even, shamefully, started humming along once or twice before catching himself.

But tonight, as the knife traced the line of a jawbone — Tam's jaw, broad and stubborn, rendered in the polished curve of a cow's shin — the humming stopped.

And Tam Ostrow said: *"Cold."*

Noor's knife slipped. He caught it before it gouged the cheek, barely, heart slamming. He set the blade down flat on the bench, the way you do when your hands are being stupid, and waited.

*"Cold,"* the voice said again, warmer than the word deserved. *"Water's cold this time of year. Didn't think it'd be that cold."*

"Tam," Noor said quietly, to the empty room, to the bone. "Don't."

*"You're the carver."* The voice had no direction. It came from everywhere the bone was. *"Ma said you'd make me pretty. Make me look handsome, eh? I was handsome. Ask Dalla Ferrow. Ask anyone."*

"You're dead," Noor said. It came out harsher than he meant. "I'm sorry. You're dead, and I'm making a keepsake for your mother, and you should — rest. Or whatever it is you're supposed to do."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Outside, a gull complained about something gulls find worth complaining about, which is everything.

Then, softer: *"I didn't fall in."*

Noor closed his eyes. *Don't ask*, said the part of him that had survived forty years of silence. *Don't ask, don't listen, don't know. Knowing is how it starts. Knowing is the door.*

"Didn't you?" he asked.

Because he was weak. Because he was lonely. Because in eleven years of hearing the dead, he had never once been able to leave one unanswered, and every single time he told himself *this is the last time*, and every single time he lied.

*"Men came,"* Tam said. *"At the warehouse. Night before the river. They asked about letters. I carry — carried — letters sometimes, for extra coin. Down-country routes. They wanted to know who gave them to me and who I gave them to."* A pause, long enough that Noor thought he'd gone. Then: *"They wore gray."*

The knife on the bench seemed suddenly very loud, though it hadn't moved.

Gray cloaks. The inquisition. The men who had burned Thornfell's shrines and salted its god-stones and taken its carvers away in wagons, forty years ago — disbanded, the histories said, dissolved by royal decree when the job was done. His mother's warnings had been about them. His mother's *death* had probably been about them, though he'd been seven and the story he'd been given was fever.

"They hurt you?" Noor asked. His voice sounded far away.

*"Threw me in after. Said drowning leaves no marks."* The warmth in the voice flickered, guttered, like a lamp running low. *"Ma shouldn't know. It'd break her worse than the river did. Let her think it was an accident. Promise me, carver. Promise."*

"I—" Noor's throat worked. "I promise."

*"Good."* And there it was again, the humming, faint and tuneless, already receding, a man walking away down a long corridor. *"Make me handsome. Dalla Ferrow liked my— "*

Gone. The bone sat in the vise, white and silent, just bone again.

Noor sat down hard on his stool and put his face in his hands.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He tried. He lay in the loft above the workshop with the blanket pulled to his chin, staring at the rafters, and every creak of the house became a footstep, every footstep became a gray cloak, and around the third hour he gave up and went downstairs and lit the lamp and looked at the finished carving.

It was good work. Even shaken, even hollowed out, his hands had done what his hands did. Tam Ostrow grinned up from the polished bone — broad-jawed, bright-eyed, a wren perched on his shoulder because Maren loved wrens and Noor had decided the dead could share. It was, objectively, the finest keepsake he'd ever made.

It was also evidence. If such a thing could be called evidence. Of what, he couldn't say. Men in gray asking about letters. A murder dressed as a drowning. A dead boy's request, whispered through a cow's shinbone to the only man alive who could hear it.

Noor set the carving down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw stars.

This was the arithmetic of his life, the sum he'd been carrying since he was nine years old: the gift was real, and the gift was useful, and the gift was a death sentence if it was ever known, and so he lived alone in a workshop above a town full of people who thought he was merely a fine craftsman with a fondness for sad customers. Eleven years of taking comfort into his hands and giving it back transformed, and never once — not once — being able to say *I spoke with him. He hummed. He loved someone named Dalla Ferrow. He forgave you for not finding him.*

Loneliness, he had learned, was not the absence of people. It was the presence of a truth with nowhere to go.

Somewhere below the floorboards, in the dark of the harbor district, the tide came in. Somewhere beyond the hills, in the high passes he'd be guiding smugglers through come week's end, the old roads of Thornfell wound through country that officially did not exist. And somewhere — he felt it rather than heard it, a pressure behind the breastbone like weather coming — something else was stirring. Something older than the inquisition, older than the kingdom, older than the silence he'd kept all his life.

The bone on the bench was still. All the bones were still.

But for the first time in eleven years, Noor Nystrom had the distinct, unshakable feeling that the silence was listening back.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark until dawn, holding a finished carving of a murdered boy, and wondering what, exactly, he had promised to keep quiet — and what would happen to Maren Ostrow, and to him, if the men in gray ever came asking their questions again.

Outside, far up in the black hills, something howled. Once. Then nothing.

Noor told himself it was a wolf.

He almost believed it.
