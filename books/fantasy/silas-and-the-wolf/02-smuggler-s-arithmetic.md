## 2. Smuggler's Arithmetic

The trouble with smuggling, Noor had learned over eleven years of doing it badly and then nine years of doing it well, was that it was mostly arithmetic.

Everyone imagined it was about nerve — dark cloaks, whispered passwords, blades in the night. In truth it was about counting. Counting the hours between patrols. Counting the weight a mule could carry versus the weight that would make it stumble on scree. Counting the coins you'd earn against the fines you'd pay if caught, the years in a Vess work-camp if the fines couldn't be paid, and the odds of any of your bones coming home if the work-camp had opinions about Thornfell-born smugglers.

Noor did his counting at dawn, sitting on an overturned crate behind the tannery in Lowmark with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands and a slate balanced on his knee. The slate was old, its frame cracked, its surface ghosted with the chalk-marks of a hundred trips he'd mostly wiped away but never entirely. Some habits you kept even when they served no purpose. Some habits were all you had left of a country.

"Three mules," he murmured, chalking numbers. "Fourteen bolts of Vessish linen, which is funny enough that I almost respect it. Salt pork. A chest of something Drenna swears is candlewax and I swear is not candlewax."

"It's candlewax," said Kesh, dropping onto the crate beside him and stealing his tea.

It was not candlewax. They both knew it was not candlewax. This was part of the arithmetic too: what you said out loud, and to whom, and where the walls had ears. The walls of Lowmark had excellent ears. Half the town survived on what they heard through them.

Kesh was fourteen, small for it, all elbows and opinions, with hair she cut herself using a fish-gutting knife and a face that suggested she was personally offended by the sunrise. She drank his tea like it owed her money. He let her. That was also arithmetic — the cost of one cup of tea against the worth of a girl who could smell a patrol three switchbacks before anyone else and who had never once, in two years, given him up.

"You've got the route wrong," she said, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand.

"I have the route exactly right."

"You've got us crossing at Shepherd's Ford." She tapped the slate with a grimy finger. "Shepherd's Ford in autumn. The water's down. Which means it's crossable. Which means *everyone* crosses there, which means that's where the Vessish border-watch sits with their little fire and their little soup and their big crossbows."

Noor looked at her. She looked back with the flat patience of someone who has explained the same thing to a slow child many times.

"The ford's watched," he admitted. "But the ridge path above it is washed out since the spring melt."

"So we go the long way. Under the Hanging Stones. There's a shepherd track."

"There's no shepherd track under the Hanging Stones. There's goat droppings and a drop of four hundred feet."

"Goats use tracks," Kesh said. "Tracks exist because goats make them. You're just too heavy to be a goat."

"I am, tragically, too heavy to be a goat."

"Then walk light." She shrugged. "Or take the ford and explain to Drenna why her not-candlewax is sitting in a Vess evidence shed."

He should have argued longer. A good teacher argues longer; a good smuggler counts the cost of pride and finds it always comes due with interest. Instead he rubbed out his chalk marks and drew the long way round, and Kesh nodded once, satisfied, as though she had personally invented geography.

That was the other thing about the arithmetic. Somewhere in the last two years, without his noticing exactly when, the sums had changed. It used to be him alone against the ledger: risk weighed against coin, coin weighed against survival. Now every calculation had a small sharp variable in it that would not stay put, that wandered off toward danger whenever his back was turned, that stole his tea and his sleep and, though he'd have denied it under torture, most of his heart.

He chalked the last figure, blew the dust from the slate, and tried not to think of it as a family budget.

* * *

They left Lowmark before the third bell, when the fog off the mere was thick enough to chew. The caravan was seven souls and five animals: Drenna the factor, fat and cheerful and deaf to anything she didn't want to hear; her two drovers, brothers who communicated entirely in grunts and elbow-jabs; a nervous wool merchant named Pell who had never done anything illegal before this week and kept apologizing to the mules; and a quiet young woman with a lute case who paid double to travel unasked questions and asked none herself.

And Noor, walking point. And Kesh, walking everywhere, because staying still was apparently beyond her.

The first miles were easy. The old road — the real road, the Thornfell road — ran straight and true beneath them, buried now under forty years of gravel and Vessish re-grading, but Noor felt it the way you feel an old scar: a pull, a memory in the soles of the feet. Everyone else saw a goat track climbing into the gray shoulders of the mountains. Noor saw the King's Way, or the spine of it, the road his mother had walked with salt-fish on her back and a song in a language the Kingdom had forbidden on pain of fine.

He didn't sing it. But he knew where every milestone lay under the dirt, and he steered by them.

"Left here," he said at a cairn that looked like every other cairn.

"That's not a junction," said Pell nervously. "That's just rocks."

"The rocks disagree," Noor said, and turned left, and thirty paces on the goat track widened into a shelf of road smooth as a table, running level along the cliffside, and Pell made a small sound like a man finding a coin in his boot.

Drenna grinned around a strip of dried apple. "Every time," she said. "Every time, carver. You find roads that aren't there. What's your secret? Witchcraft?"

"Good boots," Noor said.

It was the answer he always gave, and it was nearly true. The truth was a thing he kept folded up small and deep, next to other things he kept folded up small and deep: that he had grown up in a house where bone was carved and bone remembered, that his mother had taught him to listen to what the dead left behind, that the fall of Thornfell had taken her and the country and the language and left him only this — a map drawn in marrow, a nation stored in his hands. He walked roads no map showed because he had, in a sense, *been* those roads. Or been carried down them. Or buried beside them.

He did not say any of this. He said "good boots," and Drenna laughed, and the caravan climbed.

By midday they were above the tree line, in a world of gray stone and thin wind and sky so wide it made a person feel like an apology. Kesh ranged ahead as always, a scrap of motion against the rock, and Noor let her, because the alternative was her ranging ahead without permission, and he had learned to choose the version of disobedience he could see.

The Hanging Stones rose in the early afternoon: a field of standing rocks, tilted and leaning, some as tall as three men, left by glaciers or gods depending on who you asked. The Vessish maps marked them as impassable. The Vessish maps had been drawn by men who had never been hungry enough to try.

Kesh came bounding back down the slope with the particular gait she used for bad news delivered as triumph.

"Fire," she said. "Smoke, past the stones, maybe half a mile east. Small. Cook-fire size. And there's a horse line strung between two boulders."

Noor stopped. Behind him the mules stopped, because mules are wiser than people. "How many horses?"

"Four. Maybe five. Saddles on two of them." She paused, savoring it. "Border-watch. Told you. Shepherd's Ford."

"We're not at Shepherd's Ford."

"We're near enough that they're sweeping. They moved the fire because the water's down. I *told* you—"

"Yes," Noor said. "You told me. You were right. Write it on my headstone: *He Was Told.*"

A ghost of a grin crossed her face and vanished, embarrassed to be seen. Then she was all business again, crouching, sketching in the dirt with a stick: the stones, the fire, the horse line, the shape of the ground beyond. She drew fast and clean. Two years ago she'd drawn like a child. Someone had taught her to draw like a scout, and Noor supposed, with a pang, that someone was him, and that this was what teaching was: making yourself gradually unnecessary.

"They'll have a picket out," he said, studying her sketch. "One rider, probably, working the far side of the stones. The rest sit by the fire because it's cold and soldiers are cold and lazy in that order."

"So we go through the middle of the stones while they watch the edges."

"So we go through the middle of the stones," he agreed, "quietly, single file, and nobody sneezes."

Pell sneezed within the hour.

* * *

It happened the way disasters happen: slowly, then all at once.

The middle of the Hanging Stones was a cathedral nobody had built — pillars of granite leaning together overhead, gaps showing slivers of white sky, the floor carpeted in dry moss that swallowed footsteps whole. Noor led, reading the ground. Kesh came second with the lead mule. The others followed in a rope of held breath.

They were halfway through when Pell sneezed — a huge, wet, catastrophic sneeze, the kind of sneeze that has ambitions, that wants to be remembered — and somewhere east of them a voice shouted, and a whistle went up, two notes, sharp as a knife.

"Move," Noor said, calm as Sunday, and broke into a trot.

What followed was twenty minutes that Noor would later remember in pieces: the mules' ears flat, the rattle of the wax-chest straps, Kesh swearing with a fluency that would have made a drover blush, the whistle sounding again closer, a hoofbeat echoing weirdly among the stones so that it seemed to come from everywhere. The track bent south where Noor knew — *knew*, the way he knew his own knuckles — that it bent, and dropped through a notch barely wider than the mules, and came out into a shallow valley with a stream at the bottom and willow scrub along its banks.

"Scrub," Noor gasped. "In. All of you. Mules down, heads down, hush."

They huddled in the willows while the hoofbeats circled above them, along the rim of the valley, close enough that they could hear the rider muttering to his horse. Pell was shaking. Drenna had gone very still and very pale, her cheerfulness folded away like goods in a panic. The lute-player closed her eyes and breathed slow, and Noor noticed her hand resting on the lute case in a way that suggested the case contained more than a lute, and decided firmly not to know that.

Kesh was gone.

He realized it in stages, the way you realize you've lost something: first a vague wrongness, then a count, then the cold drop. He counted heads twice. Four adults, five mules, zero apprentices.

She had slipped out of the willows. Upstream, he judged, moving low. Toward the rider.

Noor's whole body went cold and then hot. He thought, absurdly, of the slate, of chalk dust, of the neat column of figures where every entry balanced. There was no column for this. There was no number for a girl walking toward a crossbow because she had decided, in her ferocious orphan way, that the best defense of the caravan was to remove its threat, and that rules were for people with less nerve.

The hoofbeats stopped directly across the stream. Through the willow stems Noor could see the rider: young, Vessish blue-and-gray, bow unstrung across his back, letting the horse drink. A boy, really. Nineteen perhaps. Cold, bored, and in about ten seconds about to have the worst afternoon of his military career.

Because Kesh stood up out of the scrub on the far bank, twenty feet from him, empty-handed, and said in flawless border-Vessish:

"Sir! Sir, please — is this the road to Marrowbridge?"

The rider nearly fell off his horse.

"What — girl, what are you—"

"I'm lost." She was crying. Noor watched it happen and marveled; tears arrived on command, huge and helpless, streaming down her filthy face. "Me and my da, we got separated, he went to market at Marrowbridge and I followed the wrong sheep and now I can't find the road and I've been walking since yesterday and—" she hiccuped, magnificently, "—please, sir, I'm so hungry."

The soldier's whole posture changed. Noor watched suspicion arrive and be dismantled piece by piece by a fourteen-year-old's runny nose. The boy dismounted. He dug in his saddlebag — Noor's heart stopped — and produced, of all things, a sausage.

Kesh took the sausage with both hands like a starving saint receiving a relic, thanked him in a wobbling voice, asked the way to Marrowbridge, thanked him again, and trotted off downstream, sniffling, in precisely the opposite direction from the caravan.

The soldier watched her go. Then he mounted, whistled once — the all-clear, two notes, lazy now — and rode back toward his fire and his comrades and his conscience, having fed a lost child and accomplished nothing whatsoever.

Ten minutes later Kesh came up the streambed, eating the sausage, wearing an expression of unbearable smugness.

"You," Noor said, when the others were out of earshot, "are going to be the death of me."

"He gave me a sausage."

"You walked toward an armed soldier."

"He was *armed*," she conceded, "but he was *lonely*. Lonely soldiers feed lost girls. It's in the manual. I've read the manual."

"There is no manual."

"There's always a manual." She bit the sausage. "You just haven't read this one yet. Also, we should move — he'll mention the girl at supper, and if anyone asks questions tomorrow, better we're two valleys gone."

And that — that instinct, that cold clean read of consequence wrapped in sausage theft and cheek — was the moment Noor stopped being afraid for her and started being afraid *of* her, in the way you fear weather, or the sea, or anything else magnificent and indifferent to your plans.

"Two valleys gone," he agreed. "And Kesh? Next time, tell me before you invent a plan."

"If I tell you, you'll say no."

"I would have said no."

"There," she said, mouth full. "See? Manual."

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below a ruined waystation — a genuine ruin, Thornfell-made, its walls still standing to shoulder height, its hearthstone black with fires older than the Kingdom. Noor chose it deliberately. Ruins attracted no patrols; Vess soldiers avoided old Thornish places, superstitious in the official way, the way institutions are superstitious when they've done something wrong and fear witnesses.

The travelers made a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Drenna shared out dried apple and hard cheese and, after a decent interval of pretending otherwise, the not-candlewax, which turned out to be honey-brandy, and which improved everyone's mood and everyone's opinion of everyone else. Even Pell relaxed, apologizing to the mules with slightly less sincerity.

Kesh sat hip-to-hip with Noor on the fallen lintel of the waystation gate, their backs against stone that had been cut and fitted by hands forty years dead. Below them the firelight touched the faces of their strange temporary family: the grunting brothers arguing silently over a card game, Drenna humming, the lute-player actually playing now, something soft and minor that might have been a Thornish air worn smooth of its words.

"Do you remember it?" Kesh asked suddenly. "Before. Thornfell."

Noor was quiet a moment. Above them the stars came out in the fierce high-altitude way, crowded and cold.

"I was eight when it fell," he said. "I remember bread. I remember my mother's hands. I remember a festival where they floated candles down the river and everyone sang, and I remember thinking the singing sounded like the mountain talking." He turned his cup. "I don't remember it well enough. That's the trouble. Every year it gets more like a story I heard than a place I lived."

"My mother used to sing," Kesh said, to the fire rather than to him. "I don't remember the song. Just that she did. Just that her voice went up at the end of lines, like a question." She shrugged, armor sliding back into place. "Anyway. Countries end. People keep walking."

"People keep walking," Noor agreed.

"But you find the roads." She glanced at him sideways, quick and sharp. "Roads that aren't on maps. Roads that got burned off them. How?"

"Good boots."

"You're a liar with good boots." But she let it go, the way she let most of his silences go — cataloguing them, he suspected, in that ferocious memory of hers, filing each evasion away for a day she might need it. He found he didn't mind. Trust, he was learning, wasn't telling everything. It was being certain the other person would still be there tomorrow, secrets and all.

Below, the lute-player shifted into a melody Noor knew — knew in his spine, in the scar-tissue of him — an old walking song, words long stripped away, and without meaning to he hummed the line beneath it, low, in a dead tongue, and Kesh went very still beside him, listening, saying nothing, giving him that.

When the song ended she bumped her shoulder against his. "For the record," she said, "if they ever catch us, I'm telling them you forced me into a life of crime with promises of sausages."

"Noted. And I'll tell them you ate the evidence."

"Every word of it."

Above the ruined waystation the mountains stood black against the stars, the passes sleeping, the borders holding. Somewhere south, in a country that no longer appeared on maps, old stones waited under new gravel for a man with good boots to come find them. Noor finished his brandy, banked the fire, and slept without dreams, which was, in those years, the closest thing he had to peace.

He would remember that, afterward. That he had slept well, that last ordinary night. That the worst thing in the world, then, had been a boy with a sausage and a girl with no fear.

Three days later, back in Lowmark, a stranger stopped at his stall in the market — a traveler with dust on his cloak and an accent from nowhere — and set down on the counter, among the combs and buttons and carved keepsakes, a small tablet of bone.

It was carved all over, edge to edge, in tight, careful letters.

Noor knew at a glance that it was Old Thornish. He hadn't seen the script in twenty years. He hadn't heard the tongue spoken aloud since his mother died.

The stranger smiled, paid for nothing, and walked away into the crowd.

And the bone, lying there in the daylight among the honest goods, began — very softly, like a sleeper turning over — to pray.
