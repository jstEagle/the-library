## 3. Prayer in a Dead Tongue

The market at Grayhollow smelled of wet wool, frying onions, and the particular desperation of people trying to sell things before the autumn rains came. Noor Nystrom had held the same corner stall for six years now — third row from the fountain, between a woman who sold candles that smoked in seven colors and a man whose entire business was pickled eggs — and in all that time he had learned one iron law of commerce: never let them see you counting.

So he whittled.

He always whittled when trade was slow, small shapes from offcuts of antler and riverbone: birds mostly, because birds sold, and the occasional lopsided bear for children who had more coins than sense. His knife moved without his full attention, which was good, because his attention was on the crowd, reading it the way other men read weather. A knot of Vess soldiers by the fountain — three of them, bored, which was worse than hostile. Bored soldiers went looking for trouble the way cats went looking for high shelves.

"Bone-man." The candle-woman, Marta, leaned over the gap between their stalls. She was seventy if she was a day and had the voice of a landslide. "You've got a customer. Been standing there a while."

Noor looked up. A figure stood at the edge of his stall's shadow, hooded against the gray drizzle, and had indeed been standing there long enough that Noor felt a flicker of professional embarrassment. He hadn't noticed the man arrive at all, and noticing people was supposed to be his gift.

Not *that* gift. His public gift was carving. His private gift was something else entirely, something he kept folded up inside himself like a knife wrapped in cloth, and no one in Grayhollow knew about it except Kesh, who had guessed, and the widow Amalie Sorn, who suspected, and God — or whatever passed for God these days, since the old gods of Thornfell had gone silent forty years ago along with the country itself — help him if anyone else ever found out.

"Morning," Noor said, easy as anything. "Looking for something? I've got birds. I've got bears. I've got one wolf that came out looking more like a sad dog, but she's honest work."

The stranger didn't answer right away. He was tall, broad-shouldered under a traveler's oilskin, and when he pushed back his hood Noor saw a face weathered to the color of old saddle leather, with eyes that did the thing Noor hated most: they looked at Noor like they were measuring him for something.

"You're the carver," the man said. "The one they say can put a face on grief."

"I put faces on bones," Noor said. "Grief does its own work. I just give it somewhere to sit."

It was a line he'd used a hundred times, warm and practiced, the kind of thing that made widows smile sadly and open their purses. This man didn't smile. He reached into his coat and set something on the stall's counter with a care that made Noor's hands go still around his knife.

It was a piece of bone. Not antler, not riverbone — something older, yellowed like paper left too long in sun, and carved all over its surface in fine, tight lines. Script. Letters, dozens of them, worked so small and precise that Noor's fingers itched with professional envy even before his mind caught up with what his eyes were seeing.

He knew those letters.

Every child of Thornfell had known them once, before the fall, before the Kingdom of Vess annexed the ruins and burned the books and declared the language itself a vector of sedition. Forty years, and Old Thornish survived only in smugglers' marks, in gravestone rubbings hidden under floorboards, in the heads of people old enough to remember and stubborn enough not to forget.

Noor was thirty-eight. He remembered.

"That's not for sale," the stranger said quietly. "That's an offering."

"Marta sells candles," Noor said, keeping his voice light while every hair on his arms stood up beneath his sleeves. "I sell carvings. You want the stall two rows down, fellow does eggs—"

"It's for you." The man's eyes didn't move from Noor's face. "You're the last one who can read it. Maybe the last one alive who can read it and hold a knife straight."

Around them the market went on — onions frying, Marta loudly informing someone that her blue smoke cured headaches and her red smoke caused them, the bored soldiers laughing at something near the fountain. The ordinary world, humming along. And in the middle of it, this one quiet spot where the air had gone strange and heavy, like the moment before summer thunder.

"Who told you to come here?" Noor asked.

"No one living," the stranger said.

And then he simply walked away, into the crowd, unhurried, and Noor watched him go and did not follow, because following would have been admitting how badly his heart was hammering.

* * *

He didn't touch the bone for an hour. That was discipline, or cowardice; after six years he'd stopped pretending there was much difference.

Instead he sold a bird to a grandmother, a bear to a boy who paid in buttons and got it anyway, and let Marta gossip at him about the soldiers ("Vess garrison's doubled at the north gate, my nephew says, and nobody doubles a garrison for nothing") until the words had worn smooth and the morning had turned toward noon.

Then, when the crowd thinned, he picked up the offering.

It was lighter than it should have been. That was the first wrongness. Bone has weight, has grain, has the honest density of something that lived. This felt like holding a breath. He turned it under the flat gray light and read the script, slowly, his lips moving the way they hadn't since he was nine years old and his mother rapped his knuckles for mumbling:

*O Vell-who-walks-behind-the-fog, mother-of-the-nine-names, we hid your door and could not unhide it. We are few now. We are fewer. Hear us anyway.*

Below the words, smaller, almost an afterthought: *If the carver reads this, the carver is asked. Answer us.*

Noor set the bone down very carefully, the way you set down a sleeping snake.

"Answer us," he muttered. "Sure. Lovely. I'll get right on that, shall I, next to the pickled eggs."

But his hands weren't steady, and the joke died in the air, and he found himself looking at the crowd — really looking — wondering which of these faces belonged to the few, the fewer, the remnant of priests who had once served gods that no longer had a country to be worshipped in.

Kesh arrived at midday with lunch and opinions, in that order.

She was fourteen, all elbows and attitude, with a shaved patch on her scalp where she claimed a dog had bitten her and everyone else suspected she'd done it herself for effect. She dropped onto the stool behind the stall, handed him half a meat pie, and immediately spotted the bone.

"What's that?"

"Nothing."

"You're holding 'nothing' like it's going to bite you."

"I'm holding nothing like it's a valuable piece of inventory I don't want greased with pie fat."

Kesh leaned over and squinted at it. To her credit — and Noor felt a complicated pride about this — she didn't reach for it. She'd been his apprentice three years, and in three years she'd learned the shape of his silences the way he'd learned the shape of roads: thoroughly, and without being taught.

"Old letters," she said. "Thornish."

"Don't say it out loud."

"We're literally the only two people in earshot and one of us is eating."

"The walls in this town have better hearing than most priests." He wrapped the bone in cloth and tucked it into the locked box under the counter, and Kesh watched him do it with narrowed eyes, and mercifully said nothing else, which for Kesh was roughly equivalent to a vow of silence sworn on a holy book.

They spent the afternoon packing up early. Rain was coming — real rain, the kind that emptied markets — and Noor wanted to be home before dark, though he couldn't have said why, only that the sky had the same bruised heaviness as the air around the stranger, and he trusted his skin's opinion more than his head's.

* * *

Their rooms above the tannery were small, cold, and smelled faintly of chemicals no amount of boiling could evict, but they were theirs, and they had a stove, and Kesh had a genius for getting fires lit out of wood that seemed personally opposed to burning.

Noor waited until she was asleep. That was the rule he'd made for himself years ago, after the widow's son, after the night he'd sat shaking until dawn with a dead boy's last words stuck in his throat like a fishbone: *never listen alone, never listen tired, and never, ever listen hoping.* Because the gift answered hope. It fed on it. Hope was the handle by which the dead took hold of the living.

He wasn't hoping tonight. He told himself that firmly, twice, while he unwrapped the bone by candlelight.

He was just curious. Curiosity was safe. Curiosity was a craftsman inspecting another craftsman's work.

The script was beautiful up close. Whoever had cut these letters had done it with love, or desperation, which in Noor's experience amounted to the same steadiness of hand. He traced one line with his fingertip — *we hid your door and could not unhide it* — and felt the familiar low hum rise in the bone, the way it rose in everything he touched that had once been part of something living. Usually the hum stayed a hum. Distant. Like voices through a wall.

Tonight the wall fell down.

*—hear us—*

Noor's candle guttered though there was no draft.

*—carver, hear us, we have been so long unheard—*

It wasn't one voice. That was the horror of it, the thing that dropped the floor out of his stomach. When he touched a keepsake bone he heard the dead person it belonged to, one voice, one grief, one story with edges he could grip. This was many. Dozens. Hundreds, maybe, layered and overlapping, prayers spoken into this bone across years by throats that had since gone silent, all of them still going, still praying, a congregation that refused to disperse even though the church was ash and the god had not answered in forty years.

*—mother-of-nine-names, walk behind our fog—*

*—she cannot hear, none of them can hear, the door is shut—*

*—the carver can hear. the carver hears. carver, carver—*

"No," Noor whispered. His back was against the wall; he didn't remember moving. "No, I'm not — I'm a tradesman. I make birds."

*—you are the doorkeeper's blood—*

"I'm a smuggler's guide and a souvenir-seller," he said, louder, and Kesh stirred in the next room, and he clapped a hand over his own mouth like a fool, like a child, because the voices didn't come through the air, they came through the bone and up his arm and into the place behind his sternum where he kept everything he never said out loud.

*—we hid the door ourselves, do you understand, we begged it sealed, we thought—*

One voice separated from the chorus, closer than the rest, an old man's voice cracked as dry riverbed:

*—we thought we were saving them. We gave the inquisitors the rites. We helped them chain our own gods beyond the border of the world. And it worked, carver, it worked, and then we understood what we had done, because a land without its gods is a land with the soul scooped out, and we had done the scooping—*

"Stop." Noor's voice shook. "Stop, stop—"

*—and now the chains are rusting, carver. Now something on the far side has begun to pull. We can feel it through the seal. Something remembers us. Something is learning our names again—*

The candle flame stretched tall and thin, impossibly thin, and in its light the shadows of the room bent inward, toward the bone, as if the whole room were a bowl and the bone its lowest point.

*—answer us. Answer her. She asks for you by name—*

"She doesn't know my name," Noor said, and heard how young his voice sounded, how frightened. "Nobody knows my name. That's the whole point of me."

*—Vell knows. Vell has always known her doorkeepers. Vell is coming to ask you herself, little carver, and may the nine names have mercy, because we could not refuse her either—*

Then silence — total, ringing silence, the kind that has weight — and the candle snapped back to its ordinary size, and Noor Nystrom sat on the floor of his cheap room above a tannery with a dead language's prayer in his lap and the absolute certainty that his life, which he had spent forty years building into something small enough to hide, had just been measured for something much larger.

He didn't sleep. Around the hour before dawn he heard Kesh pad to the doorway and stop there, arms crossed, watching him hunched over the cold stove with the bone still in his hands.

"Well?" she said finally. "What did it say?"

He looked up at her — sharp-tongued, sharp-eyed Kesh, who had guessed his secret years ago and never once made him say it aloud — and found that for once he wanted to say it aloud. Wanted it desperately.

"It said," Noor managed, "that a goddess is coming to find me. Personally."

Kesh considered this with the gravity it deserved, which was none, and then said, "Did you offer her a bird? You've got loads of birds. Some of them are nearly symmetrical."

And Noor laughed — a cracked, exhausted, genuine laugh — because that was the thing about Kesh: she could stand in any darkness and make it feel survivable.

But when she'd gone back to bed, he looked down at the bone one last time, at the fine desperate letters, and beneath the humor his blood ran cold, because the voices had said one thing more, at the very end, so softly he might have imagined it:

*She is nearer than the fog, carver. She is already asking.*

And on the shelf above the cold stove, among the unfinished carvings waiting for morning — the half-done heron, the lopsided fox, the antler crown he'd been shaping for a festival that wouldn't come — something stirred.

Something began, very faintly, to speak.
