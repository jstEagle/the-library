## 4. The God Who Forgot Her Name

The antler had been sitting in the bottom of Noor's trade chest for three years, wrapped in oilcloth like something shameful.

It was a good piece of material — that was the problem. Red deer, shed clean at the crown, dense as river stone and pale as milk gone to cream. A carver could spend a whole life waiting for antler like that. He'd bought it off a Vess trapper who didn't know what he was selling and wouldn't have cared if he had, and then Noor had carried it from camp to camp, town to town, through two bad winters and one very bad spring, and never once put a knife to it.

Because it was Thornish antler. He knew the grain of it the way you know a face across a crowded market — the faint gray marbling near the base where the old herds used to winter in the high passes, before the passes were closed and the herds were scattered and the country they belonged to was struck from every map between the Salt Coast and the Marrow Hills. Bone remembered where it grew. Everyone knew that, in the way everyone knows a thing without letting themselves think about it.

Noor knew it differently.

He sat in the workshop behind Widow Brann's dye-house, where he rented space by the week and paid on time precisely so no one would ever have a reason to come looking for him, and he unwrapped the antler for what he told himself was the hundredth and final time. Lamplight moved along its length. Somewhere above him, the widow's looms clacked their patient rhythm, and the smell of madder root and urine-vat drifted down through the floorboards, sharp enough to cut through the tallow and bone-dust smell of his own little room.

"You're just an antler," he said to it.

The antler did not answer. Antlers never answered. That was the entire architecture of his survival: things made of bone held echoes, the way a bell holds the shape of its ringing, and if you were born wrong the way Noor was born wrong, you could hear those echoes when you worked the material — but only when you worked it. Unworked bone was silent. Unworked bone was safe.

He had built a whole life on that distinction. Keepsakes, mostly. Lockets of carved horn with a lock of hair sealed inside; small animals for children's graves; the mourning-beads that had made his modest reputation in three border towns, each bead carved with a name too worn to read unless you already knew it. Grief-work, the traders called it. Honest work. Work that touched the dead only glancingly, the way you might touch a hot pan through a folded cloth.

And underneath the honest work, in the locked chest, in the oilcloth: the materials he did not carve. The rib that had come out of a barrow mound. The tooth of something nobody could identify. And the antler, which was not even human, which should have been nothing, which was only dangerous because of where it had grown and what had once been prayed at under the trees that grew there.

Three years. He picked it up. It was lighter than it looked, the way all bone is light, the way everything the dead leave behind turns out to weigh almost nothing.

"Kesh would laugh at me," he told it. "She'd say I'm superstitious as a fishwife." His apprentice had a genius for saying cutting things in a cheerful voice, which was either a flaw or a survival trait depending on the day. "She'd say, *you'll die with a full chest and empty hands, old man.*"

The antler lay across his palms, cool and patient.

He thought — as he had thought a hundred times — about simply selling it. Passing the problem down the road to some other carver, some other pair of wrong-born hands. But there were maybe four people left alive who could work bone the way he could, and he suspected, in the wordless way he suspected most things, that the other three had better sense than to buy it. So the antler stayed, and he stayed, and the chest stayed locked, and that was the arrangement. That was the arithmetic of a careful life.

That was before the prayer.

* * *

The stranger had come to his market stall six days ago, in the rain, when the other traders had packed up early and Noor had stayed because packing up meant walking home past the shrine-stones and he'd been avoiding the shrine-stones since the night the carved bone sang.

He'd looked like any traveler — gray road-cloak, hood back, rain flattening his hair. Middle years, forgettable face, the kind of man markets forgot before he'd finished paying. He'd set a small object on Noor's counter among the mourning-beads and said, in accented but passable Border Common:

"For repair."

It was a prayer-token. Noor knew prayer-tokens the way a locksmith knows locks. This one was old — forty years old at least, older — a flat oval of carved horn, worn nearly smooth, with a single line of script around its edge. Old Thornish. The flowing, rooted, tree-like letters of a language that had been dead since the fall, beaten out of a generation of children by men who believed that words could hold gods the way a jar holds smoke.

"I don't work in that style," Noor had said. Which was true, and also the closest he could come to screaming *put that away, are you mad, there are eyes everywhere.*

"It doesn't need skill," the stranger said. "Only mending. The crack goes through the words." And he'd paid triple rate in old coin — real Thornish coin, silver gone black with forty years in the ground — and walked away into the rain without waiting for change.

Noor should have refused the commission. He knew that now, sitting in his workshop with the mended token on the table in front of him, the crack sealed with hide-glue and wax, the dead letters running around the rim like a fence around a field.

He should have refused because the moment his knife had touched the crack, he had felt the words. Not heard them — felt them, the way you feel a chord held low on a string, a vibration in the teeth and the breastbone. The prayer had still been *in* the token. Forty years, and the words hadn't evaporated. They'd been waiting, the way the antler had been waiting, the way everything from that vanished country seemed to be waiting, patient as seed.

He'd finished the mend because finishing was safer than stopping — a half-mended prayer-token was evidence, and evidence got people burned — and then he'd sat with it in his hands for a long time, reading the letters by lamplight, sounding them out the way you sound out a lullaby your mother sang before you understood language.

He could still read Old Thornish. Of course he could. His mother had taught him, in the years when teaching it was already a hanging offense, tracing the letters in wood-ash on the tabletop and wiping them away before supper.

*Vell atha morren,* the rim said. *Vell atha morren, kel veth.*

He had not spoken or read those words in twenty-two years, and his mouth had shaped them anyway, silent, like a reflex. Like flinching.

*Vell atha morren.* He turned the phrase over. It was a common formula — the opening line of half the prayers in the old rite. *Lady of the [something], we [something] you.* His mother's ash-tablet lessons had covered the grammar and stopped short of the liturgy, deliberately, the way you teach a child to swim in the shallows and never mention the sea.

He'd put the token in his chest with the rest of the unsold work, meaning to keep it until the stranger came back, and he'd told himself the shaking in his hands was just the cold.

* * *

Now it was midnight, and the cold had nothing to do with it.

It started as a hum. That was how it always began — not sound exactly, more a pressure in the quiet, the feeling of a room where someone has just stopped speaking. Noor looked up from the spoon-handle he was carving — honest work, birchwood, nothing that could talk — and the pressure thickened, and the lampflame leaned though the window was shut.

The antler was humming.

Not the antler, he corrected himself. The *token*, lying in the chest beside the antler, and the antler answering it, the way one bell answers another. Two pieces of a dead country, six inches apart in a locked chest, calling to each other across the oilcloth.

"No," Noor said aloud, to the chest, to the room, to whatever was listening. "No, absolutely not."

The hum deepened. It found the floorboards. It found the water in the jug, trembling rings across the surface. Above him the looms had gone silent — Widow Brann asleep hours ago — and the silence made the hum enormous, filled the little room like water fills a lung.

He should have taken the token outside. Thrown it in the river. Buried it under the midden where nothing holy would ever look for it. He knew all of this with perfect clarity, and instead he sat frozen at his bench with a half-carved spoon in his hand, because underneath the fear was the other thing, the thing he hated about himself, the thing that had gotten his mother killed:

He wanted to listen.

The hum broke. What came through was not the smooth chorus of the dead that lived in worked bone — the murmured names, the small domestic griefs, a boy describing his own murder in a voice like wind through reeds. This was different. This was *vast* and *broken*, a voice like a cathedral with the roof torn off, and it came not from the token alone but from the antler, from the marrow of the antler, from the memory of the trees and the herds and the high white passes, and it said:

"—cold. Why is it cold. Where are my—"

Noor's chair went over backward. He caught himself against the wall, heart slamming, and the voice kept going, indifferent to him, a tide dragging wreckage:

"—forty years, is it, is it forty, I counted the winters on the other side of the door and lost count at nine because counting requires a self and mine is coming apart at the seams, who sealed it, who *dared*—"

"Stop," Noor whispered.

"—they took my name. Do you understand? They did not kill me, killing is a mercy, they took the *name*, and a god without a name is just weather, just hunger, just—" The voice fractured, dropped, became suddenly, horribly small. "—please. Please. Someone is there. Someone is *listening*. I can feel ears. Oh, I haven't felt ears in—"

His mother's face rose in front of him. Not the way she'd looked alive — quick, laughing, flour on her knuckles, tracing letters in ash — but the way she'd looked at the end, in the cell they'd let him see her in once, before the fire. Her wrists ringed with iron. Her voice hoarse from the questions. And the last thing she'd said to him, the thing he'd carried for twenty-two years like a stone in the boot:

*"Don't carve anything that talks back, little wren. Promise me. Whatever hears you — don't let it know you can hear it."*

He had promised. He'd been fourteen, and terrified, and he had promised, and he had kept that promise for over two decades through every keepsake and every grave-marker and every bead, touching the dead only through a folded cloth, never letting anything know that behind his hands there was a listening mind.

"Noor," said the voice in the antler, and his blood went to ice, because he had not said his name, he had not thought his name, and—

No. It wasn't reading him. It was fumbling, blind, casting about the way a drowning man casts for a rope. "Carver. Bone-man. Door-something. You have the *door* in you, I can taste it, the old blood, the hinge-blood, please, please, I've forgotten what I'm the lady *of*—"

"I can't help you," Noor said. His voice came out cracked and young, fourteen years old again. "I'm a tradesman. I make beads."

"A tradesman." Something moved through the voice that might have been laughter, if laughter could rust. "Yes. And I am a gust of wind. Carver — *hinge-blood* — listen to me, I will be brief, I am brief whether I wish it or not, I come apart when I speak, do you see, this is what they left me—"

"Don't," Noor said. "Don't tell me things. I don't want to know things."

Silence. Then, softer, and somehow that was worse — the vastness folding itself down small, the way a great bird folds itself into a cage:

"She died for it, didn't she. Whoever taught you to flinch like that. I can hear her shape in your refusal. She's on the other side of the door, carver. They're all on the other side of the door. Even your dead. *Especially* your dead. Did you know that? When your country fell, your dead had nowhere to go. The door was theirs to pass through, and your inquisition nailed it shut, and so they linger. All these years. In beads and spoons and little carved birds, pressing their faces to the seam of the world, waiting for someone with the blood to open—"

"Shut *up.*"

The word came out of him like a thrown stone, and the room rang with it, and the voice — the goddess, the vast broken half-mad thing in the antler — went quiet.

In the quiet, Noor slid down the wall until he was sitting on the cold boards, the overturned chair beside him, the lamp guttering. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. He could smell bone-dust and tallow and, faintly, through the floor, the ghost of madder-root from the widow's vats, the ordinary smells of the ordinary world, and he held onto them like a drowning man holds a spar.

His mother had known. That was the thing he kept arriving at, decade after decade, the truth he kept burying and kept unearthing. She had known what he was before he did — the hinge-blood, the doorkeeper's line, whatever ancient name the gift went by — and she had spent his childhood teaching him to be small. To carve beads. To flinch. To survive. And when the Gray Cloaks came for her anyway, when they dragged her out of her own kitchen in front of him, she had not screamed at them. She had looked at him over their shoulders and mouthed one word through the window glass:

*Quiet.*

So he had been quiet. For twenty-two years. Quiet as a bead, quiet as a spoon, quiet as a man who sold grief-work in border towns and never slept two nights in the same bed. And it had worked. It had *worked*, and here was a voice in an antler trying to undo it in a single night, trying to hand him a purpose like a live coal, and he would not take it. He would not. Purpose was how they found you. Purpose was a bonfire and you were the fuel.

"I'm sorry," he said to the antler, and meant it, and hated that he meant it. "Whatever you were. Whoever you were. I can't be what you need. There's nothing here. Just a tradesman and his beads."

The antler hummed once, low, almost tender.

"*Liar,*" it said gently, and went silent.

* * *

He did not sleep. Toward dawn he did the only useful thing his terror could produce: he packed. Not to run — not yet, running was a confession and confessions drew wolves — but to be *ready*. The trade chest repacked with the antler at the bottom and the prayer-token wrapped separately, double-wrapped, buried under stock. His good knives. The false-bottom box with his mother's letters, which he did not read, because he could not, because the last letter ended mid-sentence and he had never once managed to finish it.

Kesh arrived with the gray light, banging the door open the way she opened every door, a paper sack of fried dough in one hand and an opinion already loaded in the other.

"You look terrible," she announced, with the frank delight she took in his suffering. "Like something dug you up and put you back wrong. Here." She tossed him the sack. "Eat. Also, have you seen the notice-board? Because someone's nailed new bills up overnight, and they're strange even by the usual standard of strange."

Noor took the dough and did not eat it. "Strange how."

"Official-strange. Wax seal and everything." She flopped onto his stool, all elbows and appetite, thirteen years old and sharper than any blade in his chest. "Kingdom seal, not town. *By order of the Holy Office of Vess, citizens are reminded that the practice of relic-carving in the old manner remains forbidden, and that persons having knowledge of so-called doorkeepers are bound by law to report—*" She recited it with relish, then shrugged. "Doorkeepers. Forty years, and they're still scared of a word. Anyway. Widow Brann says the dye shipment comes today, and if you make me sort buttons again I'm running away to be a pirate."

Noor stood in the middle of his workshop, in the wreck of his sleepless night, and looked at his apprentice — loud, alive, entirely unaware of what she was standing in the middle of — and felt the fear in his chest rearrange itself into something harder and more familiar. Not fear for himself. Fear had stopped being about himself somewhere around the second year of Kesh.

*Persons having knowledge of doorkeepers.*

They weren't hunting a rumor. Rumors didn't get fresh bills and kingdom wax. Somebody had given them a reason to remember an old word.

"Kesh," he said. "How fast can you get word to Doss and the caravan?"

She stopped chewing. That, more than anything, told her the morning had changed shape. "Fast. Why?"

"Because we may need to move stock. Soon." He turned toward the chest, toward the oilcloth and the silent antler and the wrapped token, and made himself say the rest of it lightly, the way his mother would have. "Also, we're taking the long road north this week. There's a buyer in Hollowmere."

"Hollowmere?" Kesh grinned, suspicion already dissolving into appetite for the road. "That's exile country. Half the people up there still swear oaths on Thornfell."

"I know," Noor said quietly.

Somewhere in the bottom of the chest, under the oilcloth, under the stock, under twenty-two years of careful silence, something waited — patient as seed, hungry as winter — and Noor Nystrom, last of the hinge-blood, keeper of nothing, began to plan how a quiet man runs from a god.
