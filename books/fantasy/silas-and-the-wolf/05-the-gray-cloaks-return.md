## 5. The Gray Cloaks Return

Kesh had three rules for market days, and she'd learned all of them the hard way, which was the only way she ever learned anything.

One: never let Noor handle the money, because he'd give a discount to anyone who looked sad, and in Saltmere everyone looked sad by midmorning. Two: never let him carve at the stall while customers watched, because he went somewhere else when he worked — his eyes going soft and distant like a man listening to music nobody else could hear — and people found it unsettling, and unsettled people didn't buy keepsakes. Three: always know where the back ways out of the square were.

Rule three wasn't about the money.

It was about days like this one.

She was haggling with a fishwife over the price of a memorial comb — "He's got your husband's jaw exactly, look at it, that stubborn chin, and you'll haggle over a chin?" — when the bells started. Not the market bells, which rang cheerful and arrhythmic because the bell-ringer was paid in ale and usually drunk by noon. These were the watch bells, three sharp strikes, pause, three more, and Kesh's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world before her head even caught up.

Gray cloaks.

She'd never seen them herself. Nobody under forty had. But she'd grown up on the stories the way other children grew up on lullabies: the Gray Cloaks came to Thornfell before Thornfell fell, and they wore ash-colored wool and carried iron censers, and where they walked, prayers stopped working. Where they *stopped* walking, worse things happened. The stories said the order disbanded after the fall, that Vess had no more use for god-hunters once there were no gods left to hunt, just forty years of quiet and rubble and forgetting.

The stories, Kesh reflected, shoving the comb into the fishwife's hands at half price just to be rid of her, had clearly been written by an optimist.

* * *

The shrine was at the far end of the square, tucked against the tanner's wall where it had stood for as long as anyone could remember — which, Kesh now realized, was itself suspicious. It wasn't much to look at. A shelf of river stones, each one rubbed smooth and marked with a spiral that nobody living knew the meaning of. A chipped bowl for offerings. A strip of old cloth, sun-bleached past color, tied to a nail. Old-timers touched the stones for luck. Younger folk did it too, mostly without thinking, the way you'd knock wood or spit twice. Nobody prayed there. Praying would have required knowing who you were praying *to*, and that knowledge had gone into the ground with Thornfell.

Six men in gray stood around it in a loose semicircle, and their leader — a woman with a face like a closed ledger — was feeding the stones into an iron brazier one at a time.

Not throwing them. Feeding them. Carefully, almost gently, the way you'd lay flowers on a grave. That was the part that made Kesh's skin crawl. Anyone could burn a thing in anger. It took a special kind of person to burn it with *reverence*.

A crowd had gathered at what everyone judged to be a safe distance — close enough to see, far enough to run. Saltmere crowds were good at that arithmetic. The fishwife beside Kesh clutched her new comb and whispered, "Inquisition," in the tone other people used for plague ships.

"They're disbanded," said the tanner's son, who was seventeen and knew everything.

"Tell them that," said the fishwife.

The woman in charge finished with the stones and turned to the crowd. She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to; the square had gone quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet when they're afraid and don't want to admit it.

"By authority of the Crown of Vess and the Office of the Vigilant Faith, restored this season by royal decree." Each word landed like a coin on a counter. "The shrines of the fallen nation are instruments of sedition. The practices surrounding them are instruments of sorcery. You are not punished. The Crown does not punish ignorance." She let that hang there, generous as a slap. "But hear this."

Her aide began nailing notices to the posts along the square's edge. Kesh edged closer, using a knot of gawkers as cover, and read over a drover's shoulder.

**NOTICE OF INQUIRY**

*The Office of the Vigilant Faith seeks information concerning persons known as DOORKEEPERS: individuals who traffic with the dead through carved objects, who hear the voices of the departed in bone, horn, and stone, and who may serve as conduits for the forbidden gods of Thornfell.*

*Such persons are to be reported. Harborers will be charged as accomplices.*

*Reward offered.*

Below the text was a drawing. Not a portrait — a diagram, almost clinical. Two hands holding a small carved figure. And beneath the hands, a list of signs to watch for:

*A carver who works alone. A carver whose pieces are sought by the grieving. A carver who listens while he works.*

Kesh felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth.

She thought of the stall. Of Noor, sitting cross-legged behind it with a widow's keepsake in his hands, head tilted, eyes gone soft and far away, listening. Of how every piece he sold was bought by someone grieving. Of how he worked alone, always alone, because — she'd assumed — he was shy, or private, or simply strange in the way artists were strange.

*A carver who listens while he works.*

"Oh," Kesh said out loud, very quietly, and the drover glanced at her, and she made her face do something bored and stupid, because rule four, the unwritten one, was *never let them see you think.*

* * *

She didn't go straight back to the stall. That was the whole point of rule three: you never approached your own campsite from the direction people expected. She looped around through the fish market, cut down the alley behind the cooper's, and came at the square from the west, moving slow, a girl with nowhere to be.

Noor was packing up early. Good. Smart. Except he was doing it wrong — folding the cloth with those careful, absent movements of his, taking forever, like a man in a dream. He hadn't heard the bells. He never heard anything that mattered unless it was spoken in a language thirty years dead.

Two gray cloaks were drifting along the row of stalls. Not hurrying. Looking. One of them held a notice, and every few steps he'd glance from the paper to the carvers and tinkers lining the row, comparing.

Kesh covered the distance at a walk that wanted to be a sprint. "Uncle," she said loudly, arriving at the stall with her best market grin, "you promised you'd help me carry the basket home, come on, you're useless, you always say that—" She grabbed his elbow and steered him, talking the whole time, a stream of complaint about prices and weather and the fishwife's terrible chin-comb, loud enough to bore any listener into looking elsewhere.

Noor blinked at her, surfacing from wherever he'd been. "Kesh?"

"You're deaf today. Come on." She hoisted the basket onto her hip and pulled him toward the alley, and it wasn't until they'd turned the corner and put two streets between themselves and the square that she let the grin fall off her face.

"What's happened?" he asked. He was watching her the way he did when a carving told him something bad — carefully, like approaching a wounded animal.

"The Gray Cloaks are back."

He stopped walking.

"Burned the shrine in the square. Posted notices." She recited it flat, the way she'd memorized it, because saying it fast meant not feeling it yet. "*Persons known as doorkeepers. Individuals who traffic with the dead through carved objects. Who hear the voices of the departed in bone.*" She made herself look up at him. "Noor. That's you. That's — word for word, that's *you*."

For a long moment he said nothing. They stood in the alley between a cooper's yard and a dyer's, the air smelling of sawdust and sour urine, and Noor Nystrom looked older than she'd ever seen him look, all the softness drained out of his face and something else left behind. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like a man hearing his own name called from a great distance.

"I know," he said finally.

"*You know?*" Her voice cracked up an octave and she strangled it back down. "You *know*? How long have you—"

"Since the notices went up. I saw one on my way to the stall this morning." He started walking again, faster now, and she had to trot to keep up. "I thought if we packed up calm and left calm, no one would—"

"That was your plan? Pack up *calmly*?"

"It was a workable plan."

"It was a *terrible* plan! Your plan was to be calm at people who burn things!" She ducked around him and planted herself in his path. "Listen to me. Listen. There were two of them walking the carvers' row. Comparing faces to the notice. And the notice says — it says a carver who works alone, whose pieces the grieving seek, who *listens while he works*. Everyone at that market has seen you do that. The fishwife. The tanner. Half of Saltmere has bought something you listened to first, even if they don't know that's what they were buying!"

"Then we leave tonight," he said. "Quietly. The caravan goes north in three days; we can be with them by tomorrow evening if we take the river road—"

"And go where? They're not just in Saltmere, Noor. Royal decree means everywhere. Every market, every town, every border crossing between here and the sea. You can't outrun a *decree*." She heard her own voice getting high and thin and hated it, and made herself stop, breathe, count the barrels stacked against the dyer's wall the way she did when she needed her hands to remember they belonged to her. Seven barrels. Eight. "We need a real plan."

"We need to get out of this alley," Noor said, "and then we need a real plan."

That, at least, they agreed on.

* * *

By the time they reached the rented room above the chandler's shop — reached it, per rule three, by a route involving two courtyards, a drainage ditch Kesh refused to discuss further, and a bribe of one carved hairpin to the chandler's daughter — the light was going amber and the bells had stopped ringing, which somehow felt worse than the ringing.

Noor lit the lamp and sat down at his workbench, and Kesh's heart did something complicated and painful, because the workbench was covered in half-finished pieces. A songbird for the miller's daughter. A wolf, nearly done, that he'd been whittling for weeks without saying who it was for. Little things. Keepsakes. Bones and antler and river stone, each one waiting for his hands.

Each one, possibly, evidence.

"You should stop," she said. "Carving. Until we're clear."

His hands went still over the wolf. "These are commissions."

"These are *notices* waiting to happen. You think they won't search the room? You think some neighbor won't sell us for the reward? There's a *reward*, Noor. Reward money finds people faster than bloodhounds do." She paced the narrow room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn. "You have to tell me what this is. All of it. Whatever it is you hear, whatever it is you *are*, because I can't protect you from a thing I don't understand, and right now the thing I don't understand is the most dangerous thing in this room."

He looked at her for a long time. In the lamplight his face was unreadable, and then it wasn't — it was tired, and lonely, and afraid in a way that had clearly been afraid for a very long time, so long it had worn smooth, like one of his river stones.

"Kesh—"

Someone knocked on the door below. Three strikes. Pause. Three more.

They both froze.

"Not the watch pattern," Kesh breathed. Watch was two-and-two. This was the market bell pattern, the alarm pattern, except here, now, at a private door, which meant either the person knocking was incompetent or they wanted them to know exactly what they were.

Noor rose without a sound and moved to the shuttered window, easing it open a finger's width. Below, in the chandler's yard, lamplight caught the hem of a traveling cloak.

Gray wool.

"Noor," Kesh whispered, and couldn't finish the sentence, because there was nothing to finish it *with*.

The knock came again. Then a woman's voice, level and unhurried, pitched to carry through old floorboards:

"Noor Nystrom. We know you can hear us." A pause, and then, softer, almost kind: "We also know you haven't told the girl. Open the door, doorkeeper. Or shall we discuss it where she can listen too?"

Kesh looked at Noor. Noor looked at Kesh. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, she understood two things with perfect clarity: that everything she thought she knew about the man she'd followed across half a country was about to change —

— and that whoever was downstairs already knew she didn't know.
