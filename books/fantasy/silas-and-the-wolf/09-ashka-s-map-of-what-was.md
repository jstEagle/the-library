## 9. Ashka's Map of What Was

The tent at the center of Hollowmere was larger than any building Noor had slept in since Thornfell fell, and it was full of things that should not have survived.

He noticed that first — before the map, before the general herself, before the brazier smoke that smelled of juniper and something older underneath. He noticed the *things*. A child's rocking horse with one ear chewed off. A wall of framed portraits, faces gone gray with damp but stubbornly present. A shelf of books whose spines he could read from across the room because he had shelved half of them himself, once, in a library that was now a crater full of rainwater and nettles.

"You keep them like relics," he said.

"They *are* relics," said General Ashka Vey, without turning around. She was standing at the far end of the tent, her back to him, doing something to a map table that required both hands and her whole attention. "Everything in this tent outlived its country. That makes every one of them a survivor. I don't believe in throwing survivors away."

Noor had been in the camp three days now, and he had learned three things about Ashka Vey. One: she remembered the name of everyone who had died at the fall of Thornfell, all eleven thousand of them, and would recite them under her breath when she couldn't sleep, which was often. Two: she had a laugh she deployed like a coin tossed to beggars — bright, quick, and costing her nothing. Three: everything she said sounded true, which was the most dangerous quality a person could have.

"General," he said, "you sent for me at dawn."

"I did." She turned. She was not a tall woman, but she occupied space the way a cliff occupies a coastline. Her hair was iron-gray and cropped short, and there was a scar along her jaw that she touched, absently, the way other people touched prayer beads. "I wanted you to see this while the light was good."

She stepped aside, and Noor saw the map.

It covered the entire table — no, it *was* the table, painted directly onto the wood by someone who had loved Thornfell enough to get it exactly right. The Silverwash River ran down through the middle in a thread of actual silver leaf. The passes were inked in brown so dark they looked bruised. And across the top, in Old Thornish letters that made his chest ache in a way he refused to examine, were the words: *THORNFELL, WHOLE*.

"Where did you get this?" His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"I commissioned it." Ashka traced the river with one finger. "From memory. Mine, mostly. I spent thirty years defending these borders; they're printed on the inside of my skull. But I had help." She glanced up at him, and her eyes were sharp and warm at the same time, which should not have been possible. "A cartographer from Lowmark who claimed her grandmother was Thornish. A shepherd who remembered where every shrine stood between here and the coast. And a bone-carver who guided smugglers along roads that officially do not exist." She tapped the eastern passes. "Your roads, Nystrom. They're on here. Every one. The widow told us what you can do. The land remembers you."

"The land doesn't remember me," Noor said automatically. "I remember the land. It's different."

"Is it?" She smiled. "Then why did the pass open for you last week? Kesh told me the road reshaped itself around your words. Folded a rockslide out of your way like a servant drawing back a curtain."

Kesh, he decided, was going to receive a very thorough lecture about discretion. Possibly involving chores.

"It's not—" He stopped. Started again. "General, whatever you think I am, I'm a carver. I make keepsakes for grieving people and I guide wagons past border patrols. That's the whole of it."

"Mm." Ashka turned back to the map, and for a moment she simply looked at it, and Noor saw something crack very slightly in her posture — a hairline fracture in the cliff. "Do you know what I was doing when Thornfell fell, Nystrom?"

"Fighting, I'd assume."

"Holding a bridge." Her finger came to rest on a spot near the southern border, a crossing so small it didn't have a name on the map. "The Vess army was coming over it, and behind me were four hundred civilians who couldn't walk fast enough to outrun anything. So I held the bridge. For six hours. With two hundred soldiers, most of them boys." She touched her jaw scar. "When I finally retreated, I looked back, and the country behind me was already burning, and I thought: *I will spend the rest of my life making this right.*" She looked up. "Thirty years, Nystrom. That is how long I have been making it right. And then a bone-carver walks into my camp with an orphan apprentice and a wagonload of contraband, and the old roads wake up under his feet, and I think — perhaps the rest of my life is about to be shorter than I feared."

Noor's mouth was dry. "That sounds like a threat."

"It's a hope," Ashka said. "There's a difference. Threats are for people who owe me something. You don't owe me anything yet." She let that word — *yet* — sit in the air between them like a coin balanced on its edge. Then she clapped her hands once, briskly, and the fracture sealed itself shut. "But come. There's something else you need to see. Something I've shown no one else in this camp."

* * *

She led him to the back of the tent, past the bookshelf, past the portraits, to a chest of black oak bound in iron. Two guards stood beside it — real guards, not the ceremonial kind, their hands never far from their weapons — and Ashka dismissed them with a flick of two fingers.

"No one has opened this but me," she said, kneeling. "Not since I recovered it." The locks were intricate, and she worked them slowly, and Noor realized she was savoring it, the way a man savors the last glass of a bottle he'll never replace. "Eleven years ago. A Vess salvage team pulled it out of the ruins of the royal vault at Cael Amarth. I bought it from their quartermaster for more money than I will ever admit to my accountants, and I have hated every day of not knowing whether I'd been robbed."

The lid came up.

Inside, on a bed of faded blue velvet, lay a bone.

It was long — longer than Noor's forearm — and pale as winter milk, and even before he leaned close he could feel it. Not hear it, exactly. Not yet. More like standing outside a house where music is playing: pressure against the ribs, a sense of voices just past the threshold of understanding.

"That's—" He stopped, because his mouth had gone dry again, worse than before.

"King Aldric's femur," Ashka said softly. "Recovered from the vault where the last priests hid it during the fall. The man himself died on the palace steps holding the door against the Gray Cloaks. They burned everything else. His body, his crown, his records. But the priests smuggled this out, because a king's bone is a king's bone, and bones are what your family works in, aren't they, Nystrom?"

Noor didn't answer. He was looking at the femur, and the femur, he was almost certain, was looking back.

*Careful,* said a voice in the back of his skull that might have been his mother's and might have been his own caution wearing her voice like a borrowed coat. *Careful, little door.*

"Ashka," he said carefully, "what do you want me to do with it?"

"What I brought you here to ask." She rose, and took his hands in hers, and her grip was strong and dry and terribly sincere. "Carve it. Carve a key-bone. The goddess who spoke to you — the antler, the fragments — she is trying to reach us. The roads wake for you. The dead gather around you like moths. Everything in the old songs says the same thing: *when the doorkeeper comes, he will need a key, and the key must be carved from the king.*" Her eyes were bright, fever-bright, and Noor understood suddenly that he was not looking at a general. He was looking at a woman standing in front of a bridge she had been holding for thirty years, and she had finally, finally seen reinforcements on the far side. "Carve it, and we open the way home. All of us. Eleven thousand names, Nystrom. We say them all at the border, and the border opens, and Thornfell lives again."

The tent was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a camp dog barked twice and gave up.

"No," Noor said.

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well, and Noor listened for the splash and heard none. That was the trouble with talking to zealots, he thought wildly. Even the stones went quiet.

Ashka's expression did not change. That was the frightening part. Her smile stayed exactly where it was, warm and patient, the smile of a woman listening to a child explain why he couldn't possibly eat his vegetables.

"No?" she said pleasantly.

"No." He stepped back, and her hands slid from his, and he immediately missed the warmth of them, which annoyed him. "General — Ashka. What you're describing isn't a door. It's a demolition. I've felt what happens when someone carves a god badly. My mother—"

"Your mother was a doorkeeper too," Ashka said. "Yes. I know."

The temperature of the conversation changed. Noor felt it on his skin like a draft from a window he hadn't known was open.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I knew her." Ashka said it simply, and somehow that was worse than if she'd confessed to spying. "Maren Nystrom. She carved wards for the resistance in the last year of the war. Small things — charms, blessings, little protections for refugees on the roads. She wouldn't carve anything bigger. I asked her once — I begged her — to carve the way open. She refused me too." Something moved behind Ashka's eyes, some old arithmetic being done one final time. "Three weeks later she was dead, and the border stayed closed, and I have had thirty years to wonder whether I should have pushed harder."

"She refused," Noor said, "because carving gods kills carvers. It killed her anyway, in the end, for carving *small*. And you want me to take the femur of a dead king and use it to pry open a wound in the world." He shook his head. "I won't. Not for a country. Not for eleven thousand names. Not for you."

Silence. Ashka looked at him for a long moment, and Noor braced himself for anger, for the coin-toss laugh turning to iron, for the guards to be recalled through the tent flap.

Instead she nodded slowly, and said, "You're afraid."

"I'm *sensible*."

"You're afraid," she repeated, gently, horribly, "and I understand why. Your mother died for this work. Of course you flinch from it. Anyone would." She closed the chest, and the click of the lock sounded obscenely final, and Noor exhaled a breath he hadn't known he was holding. "I won't force you, Nystrom. I'm not the Gray Cloaks. I don't burn people into obedience." She walked him toward the tent flap, hand light on his shoulder, and her voice dropped into something almost intimate. "But I'll tell you what I told your mother, and I'll leave it where she left it — in your ear, growing. Every year we wait, another hundred of the old ones die in exile without ever seeing home. Another hundred names added to the list I recite at night. You have a gift that could end that list. Think about what refusing it actually costs, and who pays."

At the tent flap she paused.

"Oh — and the widow, Sera Ilsette, asked me to give you this." From inside her coat she produced a small parcel wrapped in oilcloth. "She said you'd know what it means."

Noor took it. Through the cloth he could feel the shape of a bone — small, curved, a knucklebone or a bird's wishbone — and faintly, faintly, like a bell rung in another room, it hummed.

"She also said," Ashka added, and now the smile was back, warm as noon, "to tell you that her son's route isn't finished. That there are priests still waiting at the end of it. And that she knows you'll do the right thing." She patted his shoulder once, firmly, the way one pats a horse one has decided to buy. "Get some breakfast, Nystrom. You look pale. Being needed is heavy work."

* * *

Outside, the morning was cold and gold, and Hollowmere was waking — cookfires, hammering, children chasing each other between tents with the fierce seriousness of children everywhere. Someone was singing an old Thornish harvest song, badly, in a dialect forty years dead, and Noor stood in the middle of the camp and let the sound wash over him and tried to feel flattered instead of trapped.

He failed.

"There you are." Kesh materialized at his elbow, as she did, holding two bowls of porridge and looking like she'd slept in a hedge, which she probably had. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you get when rich people talk to you." She shoved a bowl into his hands. "Porridge. It's terrible. Eat it anyway. So? What did the General want?"

Noor looked down at the porridge. He looked at the parcel in his pocket, humming its small, patient note. He looked east, toward the mountains, where the morning fog was peeling off the peaks like bandages coming off a wound.

"She showed me a map," he said. "And a bone."

"Yeah? Whose bone?"

Noor hesitated. Around them the camp went on — the singing, the hammering, the children shouting — and beneath it all, so faint he could almost pretend otherwise, he thought he heard the fog on the mountains whispering in a language he had promised himself he'd forgotten.

"An old man's," he said finally. "Come on. Eat your porridge. We're leaving tomorrow."

Kesh's eyebrows went up. "Leaving? Ashka's going to love that."

"No," Noor said quietly, "she really isn't."

And that night, alone in the narrow tent they'd been given, surrounded by the half-finished carvings he'd carried out of habit and couldn't bring himself to unpack, Noor began — very softly, very distantly, from somewhere below the floor of the world — to hear them.

All of them.

Every unfinished piece, whispering at once.
