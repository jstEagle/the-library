## 10. Voices in the Workshop

The trouble with a gift that lives in your hands is that you can't lock it in a drawer at the end of the day.

Noor had tried. He had tried everything short of breaking his own fingers: wrapping unfinished pieces in wool, burying them in sand, singing loudly and badly over them while he worked. The dead were patient people, on the whole. They could wait out wool. They could wait out sand. They could wait out his rendition of "The Miller's Lament," which even the living had asked him to stop.

So he had learned rules instead. Never carve after dark. Never leave a piece half-finished overnight if he could help it — finish it or set it aside face-down under a cloth, the way you'd cover a birdcage to convince the bird it was nighttime. And never, ever carve anything with a mouth.

The little wooden fox on his bench had a mouth. He'd been proud of it, three days ago. A commission for a child in the low town, a birthday gift, sanded smooth as river stone, with a clever pointed snout and just the suggestion of a grin. He'd given it a mouth because children liked mouths. Children talked to their toys, and toys ought to look like they might answer.

He sat across the workshop from it now, at the far wall, with his back against the cold stone and his knees drawn up, watching it the way a man watches a dog that has begun to stare at an empty corner.

"You're wood," Noor told it. "You are pine. You cost me four coppers and a splinter. You do not get to have opinions."

The fox said nothing, of course. It was the whispering underneath the silence that was the problem.

It had started at dusk, when the light went thin and gray through the one window and the mountains around Hollowmere turned the color of old bruises. At first he'd thought it was wind in the eaves. Then he'd thought it was the river. Then he'd thought, with the particular sinking feeling of a man recognizing an old illness by its first symptom, *oh no.*

Not one voice. Not even ten. The whisper came from everywhere at once — from the fox, from the half-carved spindle whorl in the corner, from the bundle of antler blanks, from the bowl of bone buttons waiting to be polished, from the shavings themselves, curled on the floor like small sleeping things. Thousands of voices, all speaking at once, all saying variations of the same thing, so layered and overlapping that they resolved into something less like language and more like weather.

*Home. Please. We only want — is anyone there — my daughter had a red coat — home, home, we only want to go—*

"No," Noor said aloud, to all of them, uselessly. "No, I'm not — I'm closed. It's late. Come back never."

The whispering didn't stop. It never stopped; that was the thing no one understood about hearing the dead. Living voices ended. Doors closed between conversations. But the dead had nowhere else to be, and all the time in the world to be there, and they pressed against whatever thin place he offered them the way water presses against a crack in a dam — not maliciously, not even urgently, most of them. Just *present*. Just *there*, the way cold is there.

He pulled his knees tighter and did the breathing his mother had taught him before he knew what she was teaching him for. In for four. Hold for four. Out for eight. Name five things you can hear that aren't voices.

He got as far as two — the river, and his own heartbeat — before the whispering swallowed the count.

*—the road past the mill, I know the road, tell them I know the road—*

*—it's dark here, why is it always dark—*

*—my hands, someone took my hands, I had hands—*

*—home. Home. Home.*

"Stop." His voice cracked in the middle of the word, which embarrassed him even though there was no one to hear it, and then the embarrassment curdled into something worse, because there were thousands to hear it, thousands of dead people crammed into a room full of wood and bone, and every one of them had heard the crack in his voice and every one of them now knew — the way they always seemed to know — that he could hear them.

The whispering changed. It sharpened. It found its focus the way a crowd finds its focus when someone faints.

*—he can hear us—*

*—you can hear us—*

*—please. Oh please. Please, please, please—*

They began to say his name. He didn't know how they knew it. They always knew it. *Noor. Noor Nystrom. Carver. Doorkeeper.* That last word arrived like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without landing, and he clapped his hands over his ears even though the voices weren't coming through his ears, they were coming through the marrow of him, through the same channels that let his knife find the shape sleeping inside a piece of bone.

His mother had died with her chisel in her hand. He had been eleven. He remembered — he made himself not remember — he thought about the price of salt instead, and the mending Kesh needed done on her left boot, and whether he'd remembered to latch the goat pen, and none of it worked, none of it ever worked, because the dead were not thoughts you could replace. They were a tide, and he was standing in it, and tonight the tide was high.

He didn't remember getting off the floor. He had some impression of moving — of being upright among the benches, of the candle guttering, of his own hands rising in front of him as if to ward something off, and his hands, his traitorous gifted hands, were *shaping* as they rose, fingers curling around invisible tools, and somewhere very far away he understood what was happening: the tide wanted a door, and he was the closest thing to a door it had found in forty years, and doors open, that's what doors are *for*—

The door of the workshop banged open.

"—told you he wasn't at the tavern, Brannick owes me a copper, honestly, does the man think I sleep standing up—" Kesh stopped.

She stood in the doorway with a lantern in one hand and a heel of bread in the other, hair escaping its braid, thirteen years old and built entirely of elbows and indignation. She looked at Noor. She looked at his raised hands. She looked at the candle, which had burned down to almost nothing, and at the shavings scattered across the floor, which — he saw it as she saw it — had arranged themselves, somehow, in spirals. Neat concentric spirals, like fingerprints, like the rings of a tree.

Kesh set down the bread.

"Noor," she said, in the voice she used on goats that were considering escape. "Sit down."

"I'm—"

"You're standing in the middle of the floor making shapes with your hands and the wood shavings are doing *that*," she said, pointing at the spirals with her chin, "and you've gone the color of porridge, so sit *down* before you fall down, because if you fall down I am not carrying you to bed, you weigh as much as the anvil."

There was no arguing with that tone. There never was. Noor sat down, mostly because his legs made the decision independently, and the moment his weight settled onto the bench the spirals of shaving trembled — and held. The whispering receded by a single degree, the way a fever breaks by degrees, still there but no longer climbing.

Kesh crossed the room, picked up the little fox, and looked at it with deep suspicion.

"What did this one do?"

"It's a birthday present," Noor managed. "For Tam Wicker's girl."

"It's creepy," Kesh decided. "It's looking at me." She turned it to face the wall. "Everything in here is looking at me. Have you noticed your workshop looks like a jury?"

"That's — most carvings face outward, it's a matter of proportion—"

"They're facing *me*, Noor." She planted herself in front of him, arms crossed, lantern light throwing her shadow huge up the wall behind her, so that she appeared to have the shoulders of a giant and the scowl of a much older woman. "Talk. What happened. And don't say 'nothing,' because I've seen 'nothing' before, and 'nothing' doesn't leave shavings in spirals."

And there it was — the wall he'd kept between them for two years, the wall he'd told himself was kindness. She was thirteen. She was an orphan the war had chewed up and spat out at his stall one market day, all sharp tongue and sharper hunger, and he'd taken her on because she could haggle a smuggler down to tears and because feeding one more mouth had seemed like the smallest possible good deed. She did not know about the gift. Nobody living knew about the gift. Knowing about the gift got people killed — his mother was proof of that, carved into his memory in letters of smoke.

But the wall had a tide pressing against it too, tonight, and he was so tired, and she was looking at him with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"The work talks to me," he said.

Kesh waited. "I know. You mutter at it. You argue with your own knives. Brannick says you're touched, but Brannick thinks cheese is a personality, so."

"Not like that." His hands had started shaking again. He put them flat on his knees. "Like this. Like — voices. When I carve. Sometimes the things I carve hold on to — people who are gone. Pieces of them. And I can hear them. And tonight—" He gestured weakly at the spirals, the wall-facing fox, the whole crowded midnight jury of the workshop. "Tonight they all started talking at once."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

Kesh looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked around the workshop, slowly, at every bench and shelf and half-finished thing, and Noor watched her do the arithmetic she was always doing — the same quick ruthless arithmetic she applied to smugglers' ledgers and ration counts — except this time the numbers were *every carving he had ever made*, and where he had sold them, and who had carried them over which passes, and how many homes in how many valleys held a piece of Noor Nystrom's work on a mantel or a windowsill or a grave.

"Huh," she said finally.

"*Huh?*"

"Well, what do you want me to say?" She threw up her hands. "That it's impossible? Noor, I've watched you find a smuggler's road through a mountain fog so thick the mules walked into each other. I've watched you take one look at a stranger and know his grandmother's name. I assumed you were either cursed or extremely well-informed, and honestly this explains more than it shocks." She paused. "It does explain the talking to the knives, though. I feel vindicated about the knives."

Despite everything — despite the tide still pressing at the edges of him, despite the whispering that hadn't stopped, only gentled — Noor felt something in his chest loosen. It was absurd. It was completely absurd, and it was also the most Kesh response imaginable, and it anchored him to the world the way a peg anchors a tent.

"They want to go home," he said quietly. "All of them. That's all any of them say. Thornfell — they want to go back to Thornfell, and there isn't any Thornfell anymore, and they don't seem to know that, or they know it and won't accept it, and they keep asking *me*, and I don't—" His breath hitched. "Kesh, there are thousands of them. Tonight there were *thousands*. Like every unfinished thing I've ever carved opened at once. Like something called them."

Something flickered across her face — quick, quickly hidden. He'd taught her card games; he knew her tells better than she knew them herself.

"You know something," he said.

"I know you look terrible and should drink something." She fetched the water jug, poured a cup, and pushed it into his hands with the brisk authority of a field medic. "Drink. All of it."

He drank. The water was cold and tasted of the clay jug and the mountain, and it was, absurdly, the most real thing that had happened all night. Around them the workshop sat in its lantern-lit quiet, the whispering reduced now to a murmur at the far edge of hearing — still there, still patient, but no longer a tide. More like rain on a roof. He could live under rain on a roof. He'd been doing it for years.

Kesh dragged a stool over and sat across from him, elbows on knees, studying him with her chin propped on her fists.

"So," she said. "Rules. Because clearly nobody's made rules for this and you're the type to suffer politely instead. One: you don't work alone at night anymore. If the voices come, you wake me. I sleep like a cat and I'm meaner. Two: we figure out what called them tonight, because things don't happen for no reason, and 'all the dead in the mountains woke up at once' is not a thing that happens for no reason. Three—" she pointed at the fox, still facing the wall, "—we finish the fox first thing tomorrow and get it out of here, because it's giving me a bad feeling, and my feelings are never wrong."

"They're frequently wrong."

"My feelings are *never* wrong," Kesh repeated, with the serene confidence of youth, and then, quieter, in a different voice entirely — the small voice she used maybe twice a year: "You should have told me. Before. Not because I'd have understood it. Because you shouldn't carry things alone. That's how people break. I've seen it. I *was* it."

Noor looked at his apprentice — this fierce, ragged, unbreakable child the world had already broken once and who had reassembled herself with the cracks showing and dared anyone to mention it — and felt the last of the tide's grip slide off him. Not gone. Never gone. But held at bay, tonight, by the simple fact of her sitting there, solid and alive and annoyed, guarding the door of him without knowing she was doing it.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't make it weird." She stood, retrieved her bread from the floor, tore it in half, and shoved the larger half at him. "Eat. Dead people can't nag you if you've got your mouth full. Probably. Worth testing."

He ate. She banked the fire, righted the fallen stool, and paused at the doorway with the lantern, silhouetted against the dark street beyond.

"Noor? The spirals. The shavings." She hesitated. "They weren't random. I've seen that pattern before — on the old boundary stones, up past the pass. The ones nobody talks about." Her eyes met his, and in the lantern light they looked suddenly very old, and very awake. "Whatever called them tonight — I think it's still calling. The dogs wouldn't stop howling on my way over. All of them, facing the mountain."

Far off, beyond the walls, beyond the sleeping town, the mountains stood black against the stars — and beneath the murmur of the patient dead, beneath the rain-on-the-roof hush of the workshop, Noor heard it too, faint and vast and unmistakable:

A hum. Deep in the stone. Like a door remembering it had once been a door.

* * *

He did not sleep. Toward the gray hour before dawn, wrapped in a blanket on the workshop floor with Kesh snoring ferociously on the cot she'd claimed as her own, he heard hoofbeats in the empty street — unhurried, deliberate, far too controlled for that hour — and through the frost-feathered window he watched a rider in a gray cloak walk her horse past his door, slow as a reading eye.

She did not stop. She did not need to. On the doorpost beside his workshop, nailed neatly at the height of a man's heart, was a square of paper he was certain had not been there yesterday.

He would read it in the morning. He already knew, in the marrow the voices lived in, exactly what it would say.
