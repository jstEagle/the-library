## 11. The Gray Cloak's Bargain

Market day in Drenn's Hollow smelled of wet wool, frying onions, and the particular desperation of people trying to sell things nobody had coin to buy. Noor had claimed the same corner stall for three years now — a plank table propped on two barrels and one very patient crate — and in that time he'd learned the sacred rhythms of the place. The widow Brenna always haggled first thing, when her pride was still awake. The tanner's boys came at midmorning to steal apples and pretend they weren't going to buy anything. And by late afternoon, when the light went amber and everyone's feet hurt, even the stingiest customers grew generous.

It was, all things considered, a good life. A small one, but good. He carved keepsakes for the grieving, guided caravans through passes that existed on no honest map, and pretended — successfully, he believed — to be nothing more than a bone-carver with an unusual talent for remembering roads.

He was carving a small round token for a fisherman whose wife had died in the spring floods when the market went quiet.

Not silent. Markets never went fully silent; there was always someone arguing about the price of turnips. But it *hushed*, the way a room hushes when a certain kind of person walks in. Noor didn't look up right away. He finished the curve of the wave he was working, because a carver who abandons a line mid-stroke might as well abandon the whole piece, and then he looked up.

Gray cloaks. Three of them, moving through the crowd with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been asked to leave anywhere.

The inquisition had been disbanded forty years ago. Officially. Everyone knew this the way everyone knew the king of Vess was merciful and the border taxes were fair — as a story told by people who benefited from it being true. And yet here they were, gray wool and silver pins shaped like closed doors, walking through Drenn's Hollow as if the intervening decades were a minor scheduling error.

The tallest of them peeled away from the other two and stopped at his stall.

She was perhaps fifty, with iron-gray hair pulled back so tightly it looked painful, and a face like a ledger — every line recorded, nothing wasted. She looked at his carvings the way an auditor looks at accounts.

"You're the bone-carver," she said. Not a question. "Nystrom."

"Noor Nystrom, yes." He set down his knife and smiled the smile he used on difficult customers. "Tokens, charms, keepsakes. I do a lovely wolf if you've lost a dog, though I'll warn you it makes people cry."

"Inquisitor Serane Hallic." She did not smile back. Inquisitors, he suspected, were trained from birth never to smile back. "I'd like to discuss your inventory."

"My inventory?"

"Your inventory." Her eyes moved over the table — the tokens, the charms, the little carved birds Kesh kept trying to convince him to sell at a markup. "Every piece you've made in the last three years. Where you got the material. Who you sold them to. What those buyers reported hearing."

Noor's hands stayed very still on the table. Stillness was the first rule of smuggling: a man who moves is a man with something to hide, and a man who moves *slowly* is a man with something to hide and time to plan.

"I'm afraid my inventory is mostly bones and bad jokes," he said. "If your buyers are reporting anything, I'd check their ale."

Something flickered across Serane Hallic's face. It wasn't amusement. It was the expression of a woman cataloguing a lie for later review.

"The shrine on Cooper's Lane burned last week," she said. "You may have noticed."

"I noticed smoke. Smoke's hard to miss."

"It was consecrated to a dead god. The offerings inside included a prayer tablet written in Old Thornish." She leaned forward slightly, and up close her eyes were pale as river ice. "Old Thornish has been extinct for forty years, Master Nystrom. Extinct languages do not write themselves. Someone in this valley remembers. Someone in this valley is keeping doors open that were sealed for very good reasons."

Around them, the market resumed its noise, cautiously, the way a dog approaches a strange cat. The fisherman's token sat between them on the table, its little wave half-finished, and Noor became acutely aware that it was humming. Faintly. The way it had hummed since the night the prayer tablet answered him. He hoped — he prayed, absurdly, to gods he wanted nothing to do with — that it only hummed for him.

"That's terrible," he said. "About the tablet. Have you tried asking around the farms? Old folk remember all sorts of things. My grandmother used to sing songs in three languages and couldn't tell you what any of them meant."

"Your grandmother," said Serane Hallic, "was Maren Nystrom of Thornfell."

The name landed in his chest like a dropped stone.

"She was a doorkeeper," the inquisitor continued, conversationally, as if discussing weather. "One of the last. We have records. We have very thorough records, Master Nystrom. That is rather the point of us."

*We.* As if the inquisition had never gone anywhere. As if forty years of quiet had just been the organization catching its breath.

"I think," Noor said carefully, "you've mistaken me for someone interesting. I carve trinkets. I guide mules over hills. Last month I spent four days fishing a caravan wheel out of a bog, and I promise you, no god was involved. Only mud, and a great deal of it."

For a moment — just a moment — something almost human moved behind the ice of her eyes. Something tired.

"Meet me at the Wayfarer's Rest," she said. "Tonight. After the lamps are lit. Come alone." She straightened, and the auditor returned. "Or don't come, and I'll continue my inquiries publicly. Publicly tends to be less pleasant for everyone involved. Especially for associates."

"Associates," Noor repeated. "I'm a bone-carver. My only associate is a seventeen-year-old who steals my apples."

"Yes," said Serane Hallic. "Kesh. Clever girl. Terrible liar. You should work on that."

She turned and walked away, and the market breathed again around her wake, and Noor sat very still with his half-finished wave until his hands stopped wanting to shake.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Kesh said.

They were in the workshop behind the cottage, which was generous language for a lean-to with a stove, and Kesh was supposed to be sanding a batch of prayer beads for the temple in Lowmark. Instead she was watching him with the flat, unblinking assessment she'd perfected sometime around age twelve and never retired.

"What face?"

"The face where you're thinking about something awful and deciding whether to tell me." She held up a bead to the lamplight, squinting at it. "You get a line *here*—" she tapped the space between her own eyebrows, "—like the wood's got a knot in it."

"Charming. Sand the bead."

"I am sanding the bead. I can do two things." She set it down anyway, which told him how worried she actually was. "It's the gray cloaks, isn't it. The one with the face like a frozen river came by the stall while you were getting lunch. Brann saw her talking to you. Brann sees everything, it's his whole personality."

"Brann sees everything except when the tanner's boys take his apples."

"Don't deflect with humor, it's insulting to both of us." Kesh folded her arms. She was small and sharp-boned and had a scar through one eyebrow from a border patrol's boot three years ago, and Noor had long ago stopped being able to imagine his days without her in them. "What did she want?"

He considered lying. It would have been easy; he was good at it; it was practically his trade. But Kesh had a nose for his lies the way some dogs had a nose for truffles, and besides — the evening was coming, and the lamp was lit, and somewhere across town a woman with a ledger for a face was waiting.

"She thinks I'm something I'm not," he said. "Or something I stopped being. Or maybe something I never properly started being. It's complicated."

"Everything about you is complicated. You alphabetize your chisels."

"There's a *system*—"

"Noor." And there it was, under the sharpness: fear. She was seventeen and she'd already lost one family to a fire and one country to a war, and she knew exactly what gray cloaks meant, better than he did sometimes. "Just tell me we're not in trouble."

And that was the question, wasn't it. He looked at her — at the sawdust in her hair, at the bead forgotten under her hand, at the whole small stubborn architecture of the life they'd built out of smuggled goods and secondhand furniture — and he could not make himself say *no*.

"Go finish the beads," he said gently. "I have an appointment."

* * *

The Wayfarer's Rest was the kind of inn that survived by being unremarkable: thin ale, thick stew, and a landlord who asked no questions because questions were bad for business. Noor arrived after the lamps were lit, as instructed, and found Serane Hallic in the corner booth with a pot of tea and two cups.

Two cups. He noted that. It was either courtesy or theater, and with inquisitors it was usually theater.

"You came," she said.

"You threatened my neighbors. Coming seemed faster than cleaning up afterward."

"Efficient. Good." She poured the second cup and pushed it toward him. He didn't touch it. She didn't seem offended; possibly she hadn't expected him to. "Then let's be efficient too. Sit."

He sat. Outside, rain had begun, soft against the shutters, and the inn's noise wrapped around their booth like a curtain.

"Forty years ago," Serane said, "the kingdom of Thornfell ended. The histories say plague and invasion. The histories are wrong, or rather, incomplete. What ended Thornfell was the gods."

Noor kept his face still. Inside, something old and cold turned over.

"The old gods of that country fed on their worshipers," she continued. "Not metaphorically. There is a reason the old rites required memory-tithes, why the priesthood kept ledgers of what each family had given. Devotion cost memory. Generations of it. By the end, whole villages had forgotten their own names, their children's faces, how to farm land they'd worked for centuries. They remembered only the gods. Perfectly. Lovingly. To the exclusion of everything else." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, precise as a clock hand. "My order sealed the border between the living world and theirs. It cost us dearly. It cost Thornfell everything. But it stopped the feeding."

"And now?" Noor said. His voice came out level, which he counted as a professional triumph. "Now what? The seals are old. Things leak."

"Now things leak." For the first time, something in her posture admitted weight. "Prayers in extinct tongues. Shrines regrowing overnight. Bones that sing, if our reports are accurate — and our reports are accurate, Master Nystrom, because I wrote half of them myself." She looked at him directly. "A doorkeeper's blood answers the old rites. A doorkeeper can open what we sealed. And a doorkeeper can seal it again — permanently, this time, so that no exile, no zealot, no grieving fool ever pries it open again."

The rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan clattered, gloriously ordinary.

"You want me to help you," Noor said slowly, "seal the gods away forever."

"I want you to help me save lives. Again. The way my order did before, badly, at terrible cost, but effectively." She leaned forward. "In exchange: the smugglers' amnesty continues. Every route, every driver, every orphan with a forged pass. The Kingdom of Vess has been looking for a reason to close those passes for years, Nystrom. One word from my office and the roads go dark, the caravans stop, and a great many people who feed their families off your routes find themselves unemployed and arrested. One word, and a certain sharp-tongued apprentice finds her papers reviewed very, very closely."

There it was. Laid out cold and clean as a dissected frog.

"And if I refuse?"

Serane Hallic did not raise her voice. He suspected she never had in her life.

"Then I do my duty," she said. "I investigate a bone-carver of doorkeeper descent whose mother was executed for heresy, whose stall sits at the center of a web of illegal crossings, whose apprentice carries false documents. I investigate thoroughly. Publicly. And everyone whose survival depends on your silence discovers precisely how much of their safety rested on it." She paused. "I want to be clear that I derive no pleasure from this. I have read your file. You guide people to safety for a living. So did I, once, in the way available to me. We are not enemies, you and I. We are simply both standing in front of the same door, wanting different things done with it."

"And what happens to the people who want it opened?" Noor asked. "The exiles. The ones who pray to these gods. The ones who've spent forty years with nothing but memory."

"Grief makes excellent fuel," Serane said quietly. "I know. I burn some myself most mornings. But fuel burns out, and what's left is ash, and the gods will still be feeding." She rose, smoothed her cloak, and left a coin on the table for the tea neither of them had drunk. "You have until the new moon. Eleven days. Think about who you're protecting, Nystrom. Then think about who I can reach."

She walked out into the rain without hurrying, and the door swung shut behind her, and Noor sat alone in the corner booth with two untouched cups of tea and the sudden, vertiginous understanding that the small good life he'd built had been standing on a foundation he'd never inspected — and the inspector had arrived.

* * *

He walked home the long way, through the rain, past the blackened skeleton of the shrine on Cooper's Lane, where someone had already laid fresh flowers in the ashes. Small white ones. He didn't stop. If he stopped he'd look at them, and if he looked at them he'd hear them, and tonight of all nights he could not afford to hear anything.

The workshop lamp was still burning when he got in. Kesh was asleep at the bench, cheek on her arms, sandpaper still curled in one fist, a row of finished beads lined up in front of her with the fussy precision of someone who'd been stalling for his return. Sawdust haloed her hair. One of the carved birds — the wren he'd made her last winter, the one she was forbidden to keep on the workbench and kept on the workbench — sat watch beside her elbow.

Noor stood in the doorway a long moment, rain dripping off his coat, looking at the whole small stubborn architecture of it: the girl, the beads, the bird, the stove ticking as it cooled.

*Everyone whose survival depends on your silence.*

Eleven days.

He hung up his coat, banked the stove, and lifted Kesh's feet onto the stool so she wouldn't wake with a cricked neck. She murmured something that sounded suspiciously like *"alphabetize this"* and burrowed deeper into her arms.

At the bench, in the low light, his half-finished fisherman's token waited where he'd left it that morning. The little wave, half-carved. As he passed, faint as breath, it hummed.

And beneath the hum — for the first time, unmistakable, patient as tide — something else. Not the widow's son. Not any voice he knew. A chorus, far off, like wind finding a crack under a door.

*Doorkeeper,* it said. *Doorkeeper. Doorkeeper.*

Noor covered it with a cloth, the way you cover a cage at night, and sat down heavily on the stool, and did not sleep.

Across the room, unnoticed in the dark, the little carved wren tilted its head — and listened.
