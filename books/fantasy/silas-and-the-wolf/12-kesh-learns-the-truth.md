## 12. Kesh Learns the Truth

Kesh had stolen three things in her life that she regretted, and one of them was currently sitting on Noor's workbench, looking at her.

It wasn't looking at her in any way a normal bird would look at anything. It wasn't looking at all, technically, because it was a carving — a wren no bigger than her thumb, whittled from a pale knot of antler with wings half-folded and its head cocked at an angle that suggested it had been interrupted mid-thought. But there was something about the tilt of it. Something about the little dark notch of its eye.

She had stolen it an hour ago, while Noor was out back arguing with a tanner about the price of hide bindings. She hadn't even wanted it, particularly. That was the thing about Kesh and stealing: sometimes her hands just did it, the way other people's hands drummed on tables. The wren had been sitting near the edge of the bench, small and perfect and unattended, and her fingers had simply made a decision her brain hadn't been consulted on.

Now she sat cross-legged on the floor of the workshop with it cupped in both palms, and she couldn't put it down, and she couldn't put it *back*, because putting it back meant admitting she'd taken it, and admitting she'd taken it meant a conversation, and conversations with Noor about his work were like trying to have conversations with a locked door that occasionally sighed.

"Right," she told the wren. "You're going back. I'm just — admiring you first. That's not a crime. Admiring is free."

The wren said nothing, because it was a bird made of antler, and Kesh felt briefly, enormously stupid, and decided to blame the workshop. There was something about the place that made a person talk to objects. It was full of objects worth talking to: half-finished combs and buttons and little animals, bones and antlers sorted into trays by color and grain, shavings drifting across the floor like snow that had given up halfway down. It smelled of dust and oil and something faintly sweet, like the inside of an old violin.

She turned the wren over. On its belly, so tiny she'd missed it at first, someone had scratched two letters in Old Thornish. She knew them now — Noor had been teaching her the alphabet in the evenings, claiming it was practical for reading old trail markers, though the trail markers they actually encountered were written in Vessian and mostly said things like TURN BACK and THIS MEANS YOU.

"Vell," she read aloud, sounding it out. "Huh."

The workshop went quiet.

Not ordinary quiet. Kesh had grown up in loud places — orphanage dormitories, market squares, the backs of caravans — and she knew every flavor of silence the world sold. This one was different. This was the silence of a room holding its breath.

The wren grew warm in her hands.

Not hot. Warm, the way a stone is warm if someone has just been holding it. And then — soft as a moth landing, so quiet she would have missed it if every hair on her arms hadn't stood up at once — the wren spoke.

*"You said my name."*

Kesh did not scream. She wanted the record to show, forever, that Kesh Nystrom-who-had-no-other-name did not scream, did not drop the bird, did not run. What she did was sit very still on the cold floorboards and say, in a voice that only shook a little:

"No, I didn't. I said *your* name. Vell. That's you, isn't it? Written on your belly."

A pause. The warmth pulsed, once, like a heartbeat deciding whether to bother.

*"Wrong name,"* said the wren, sounding tired in a way that no bird, wooden or otherwise, should be able to manage. *"Old name. Everyone gets the wrong name eventually."*

*

Noor came back from the tanner's argument to find his apprentice sitting on the floor of his workshop in the exact center of the room, holding a stolen wren at arm's length, staring at it with the expression of someone negotiating with a snake.

He set down the hide bindings. He looked at the wren. He looked at Kesh. He closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when the gods are testing him and he wishes they'd send a letter instead.

"Kesh."

"Don't," she said.

"That's a commission piece."

"I know what it is."

"It's due to a merchant in Threeford by the week's end."

"*I know what it is,* Noor." Her voice cracked in the middle, which ruined the effect somewhat. "It talked to me."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the one she'd felt alone. This one was heavy, and guilty, and entirely his.

"You heard it speak," he said carefully.

"It said I got its name wrong. It sounded — " She stopped, started again. "It sounded sad, Noor. Birds aren't supposed to sound sad. Carved birds especially aren't supposed to sound anything."

Noor crossed the room slowly, the way you'd approach a frightened animal, except Kesh was fairly sure she was supposed to be the animal and he was the one doing the frightening. He crouched down in front of her, joints cracking, forty-some years of road and craft announcing themselves, and held out one hand. She gave him the wren. It lay inert in his palm, just antler again, just craft.

"When did it start?" he asked.

"When I read the name off its belly. Out loud."

He exhaled through his nose. He turned the wren over, looked at the scratched letters, and pressed his thumb over them as if he could take the words back by force.

"There are things about this workshop," he began, in the tone of a man starting a speech he'd rehearsed for years and hoped never to give, "that I haven't — "

"Noor." Kesh scrambled to her feet. She was fifteen and all elbows and she used every inch of both. "Don't do the speech. Don't do the *there are things you don't understand* speech. I've been living in your pocket for three years. I've watched you talk to bones when you think nobody's looking. I've seen you stand in the pass at Hollow Ford with your hand flat on a rock like you were listening for footsteps. I found a comb last month with a name scratched on it that isn't anyone's name, and when I asked, you said it was a maker's mark, and I let you lie to me because I figured you'd get around to the truth eventually." She jabbed a finger at the wren. "It got around. So. Truth. Now. All of it."

For a long moment Noor just looked at her, and Kesh watched something move behind his eyes — fear, yes, but underneath the fear, something almost like relief, the way a man carrying a heavy pack feels when someone finally offers to help set it down.

"The dead talk through the carvings," he said.

Kesh blinked. Of all the sentences she'd braced for, she realized afterward, she hadn't braced for that one, because she'd been expecting something explainable. Smuggling. Spycraft. A second identity. Not this.

"What, all of them?"

"All the ones I make from certain bone. Antler especially. Ivory. Some horn." He set the wren gently on the bench, face-down, like closing a book. "When I carve, I hear them. Whatever's left of a person — or a thing — after it dies, if it's still hanging around, it comes through the work. I've never known why. My mother could do it too. It's why we're alive and Thornfell isn't, more or less, and it's why I don't talk about it, and it's why you're going to forget the word *Vell* by morning."

"I'm absolutely not."

"Kesh —"

"Who's Vell?"

"A story. An old, bad story." He picked up the hide bindings and began coiling them with tremendous concentration, which was what Noor did instead of having feelings. "The point is, the less you know, the safer — "

"Stop." Kesh planted herself between him and the coil of leather. "Do you remember the night we met? The orphanage at Marrowgate? You were buying rope off the fence and I picked your purse and you caught my wrist and instead of turning me in you bought me dinner. Do you remember what you said?"

Noor's jaw tightened. "I said a lot of things."

"You said, *you've got quick hands and quicker eyes, girl, and eyes like yours shouldn't go to waste on empty pockets.*" She stepped closer. "So here's my eyes being quick: you've been scared since the day I met you. Not smuggler-scared. Deeper. Border-patrol-scared doesn't make a man flinch when a widow says thank you too kindly. And today a bird talked to me and instead of telling me why, you're going to hand me another empty pocket." Her voice dropped. "I'm not asking as your apprentice. I'm asking as family. Which is what we are, whether you've got the nerve to say it or not."

The coiled bindings hung forgotten in his hand.

Outside, the market noise of Little Fenwick went on — cart wheels, a fishmonger's bell, children shrieking over some game whose rules changed hourly. Inside, Noor Nystrom stood very still for a long time, and then he did something Kesh had never seen him do. He sat down on his own stool like a man whose legs had been quietly removed without his consent.

"Her name was Vell," he said. "And she was a god."

*

He told her all of it, or nearly all — the prayer in the dead tongue left at his stall, the voice that answered through the antler, the goddess who had forgotten herself, begging to be remembered. He told her about his mother, in fragments, the way a man handles broken glass: quickly, and by the edges, and with blood anyway. He told her that carving gods was how she died, and that he had sworn an oath over her grave that he would never do it, and that lately the oath had begun to feel like a door with something enormous pressing against the other side of it.

Kesh listened without interrupting, which for Kesh was roughly equivalent to a saint performing miracles.

"So the wren," she said finally. "That wasn't Vell. It said it had the wrong name."

"No. The wren's antler came from a stag shot two winters back up near Graywater. Sometimes the animal itself lingers a little — animals are simpler, their ghosts are mostly just habits. Habit of listening. Habit of answering when spoken to." He rubbed his eyes. "But the same door lets them through, whatever they are. And the door is getting wider. Every month it's wider. That prayer at my stall woke something up, and now everything I carve is — louder."

"And you were just going to let me find out by accident."

"Yes," Noor admitted. "Preferably never."

Kesh thought about being angry. She got as far as the front porch of anger, admired the view, and found she couldn't move in. Because she understood it, was the terrible thing. She understood keeping the one true thing about yourself locked in a box, because the moment people saw it they either wanted to use it or burn it, and neither option left much room for being a person.

"Okay," she said.

Noor looked up, visibly braced for something other than *okay*.

"Okay," she repeated. "You should've told me sooner, and you're an idiot, and also I get it. But Noor —" and here her voice did something she didn't authorize, going soft in a way she hated, "— if a carved bird will talk to me, will it carry a message? If I asked it to?"

His face changed. Slowly, like weather.

"Kesh."

"My mother." She crossed her arms, defensive, daring him to make it a big thing. "She died when I was six. Fever, at the Marrowgate foundling house, before they even bothered to learn my name. I don't remember her face. I remember she hummed. That's it. That's the whole inheritance — humming. And you're telling me there's a door, and things come through it, and you've known me three years and it never once occurred to you that I might want to knock?"

"Kesh, it doesn't work like — the dead who linger are rare, and ones gone thirty years —"

"You don't know until you try." She was crying now, furiously, wiping her face with her sleeve like the tears were a personal insult. "That's your whole philosophy, isn't it? Try the pass, try the road, try the other road when the first one's guarded. So try. Please. One message. Just — tell her I kept the humming. Tell her I turned out fine. Mostly. Tell her —"

She stopped, because Noor had gotten up, and crossed the room, and done something he had never once done in three years of shared roads, shared fires, shared danger.

He hugged her.

It was brief and awkward and smelled of bone dust and linseed oil, and it undid her completely.

"One message," he said into the top of her head. "Tomorrow. We'll do it properly — good antler, unhurried work. And Kesh? Whatever answers, or doesn't answer — it changes nothing between us. Understood? You're stuck with me either way."

"Same," she managed, into his shirt.

*

They worked by lamplight long after the market slept. Noor chose a piece of elk antler from the oldest tray, dense and honey-colored, and carved slowly, and Kesh sat across from him with her chin in her hands and watched the shape of a small round bird emerge under his knife — a thrush, because thrushes sing at dusk, and her mother had hummed at dusk, she remembered that suddenly, the memory arriving like a letter delayed thirty years.

Halfway through, Noor paused, knife resting, and looked at her across the lamp.

"For the record," he said, "the wren. When you took it."

Kesh froze. "I was going to return it."

"I know." His mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile. "I counted the tray before I left. I left it near the edge on purpose. Been waiting weeks for you to be curious enough."

Kesh stared at him. "You *thief.*"

"Takes one to apprentice one." He returned to the carving. "Now. Tell me exactly what you want it to say. Word for word. The dead are literal — it's one of their few virtues."

Kesh thought for a long moment, watching lamplight pool in the hollows of the unfinished thrush.

"Tell her," she said at last, "her daughter kept the humming."

Outside the workshop window, far off toward the Vess border, a light flickered where no light should be — brief, white, deliberate, like a signal. Neither of them saw it. But the antler shavings on the floor stirred, though the window was shut, and somewhere in the dark a shrine that had burned cold for forty years felt, faintly, the first warmth of being needed again.
