## 13. The Message Delivered

Noor had carved a hundred keepsakes in his life — lockets of bone for lovers, name-stones for the dead, a tiny lute once for a musician who'd lost his fingers to frostbite and wanted, absurdly, to keep playing anyway. He had never carved anything like this.

Kesh sat across the workbench from him, arms folded, watching his hands with the expression of someone who suspected she was being swindled but couldn't yet prove how.

"You're sure," she said. It was the fourth time she'd said it.

"I'm sure."

"Because if you're doing this out of pity, I'll break your thumbs."

"That's a very specific threat."

"I've thought about it." She unfolded her arms, then folded them again, then picked up a wood shaving from the bench and began tearing it into smaller and smaller pieces, which was what Kesh did instead of crying. Noor had learned that early. The first winter she'd been with him, she'd shredded an entire broom into kindling over the course of one bad night, and in the morning she'd swept up the wreckage herself and never mentioned it again.

He turned the bone over in his hands. It was a knuckle-bone from an ox, smooth and heavy, bought fair from the butcher in Lowmark because he refused to carve grief out of anything stolen. He'd been shaping it since sundown. It was nearly midnight now, and the workshop smelled of lamp oil and bone dust and the rosemary Kesh insisted on burning because it reminded her of bread.

"Tell me again what you want to say," he said gently.

Kesh glared at him. "You already know."

"I want to hear you say it. That's part of it. The words have to come from your mouth before they can go into my hands. Otherwise it's just my message wearing your face."

She scowled at the ceiling as though it had personally wronged her. Then, quietly, in a voice he'd only heard from her maybe twice in three years:

"Mama. It's me. It's Kesh." A breath. "I know you didn't leave on purpose. I know now. The fever took you fast and nobody told me because they were afraid I'd catch it too, and I understand that, I do, I'm not — I'm not angry anymore. Mostly not angry." The wood shaving in her hand was confetti now. "I just wanted you to know I turned out all right. Better than all right. I've got a trade. I've got—" She glanced at Noor and seemed to physically wrestle with the next word. "—a person. Who's annoying but reliable. And I wanted to ask if you were — if it doesn't hurt, where you are. That's all. That's the whole thing. If it hurts, don't tell me. Lie to me. You were always good at that."

The last sentence came out sharper than she'd meant it, and she flinched at her own voice.

Noor set down his knife. "Kesh."

"Don't."

"She'll know it wasn't cruel. The dead hear what's underneath the words. That's the whole trouble with them, actually."

Kesh wiped her nose with her sleeve, fast and furious, as if the nose had betrayed her too. "Fine. Carve it. And if she says anything embarrassing about me, I deny everything."

* * *

Carving a message for the living dead was nothing like carving a keepsake. A keepsake held an echo — a warmth, a shape of a person, the way a cup holds the shape of water after the water's gone. But a message needed a door, and doors were dangerous, and Noor built them carefully.

He worked the ox-knuckle slow, letting Kesh's words settle into the grain the way rain settles into dry ground. *It's me. It's Kesh.* The bone warmed under his blade. Not the ordinary warmth of friction — this was the other warmth, the one that made the hairs on his arms stand up, the warmth of something leaning close from very far away.

By the time he cut the last curve, the workshop had gone quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when a third person has entered it without opening the door.

Kesh felt it too. He saw her shoulders rise. "Is she—"

"Hush. Let her arrive."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. The rosemary smoke curled into a shape almost like a braid. And the bone in Noor's hands began, very softly, to sing.

Not words at first. It never started with words. It started with feeling, the way a letter starts with handwriting before you read it: a wash of something enormous and gentle, like standing in a kitchen doorway as a child and smelling bread. Kesh made a small sound and pressed both hands over her mouth.

Then the voice came, thin and clear through the bone, through Noor's palms, into the air of the little room.

*"My girl. My tall girl. When did you get so tall?"*

Kesh made another sound behind her hands, half laugh, half sob. "I was eleven when you died, Mama. I've been tall for ages."

*"Eleven? No. You were small. Small enough to fit under my arm when the storms came."* The voice wavered, catching on itself like a skipped stitch. Memory came strangely to the dead, Noor knew — time pooled rather than flowed, and some of them arrived at their deaths still carrying years that hadn't happened yet. *"You used to hide under my arm and count my heartbeats until you fell asleep. You said my heart sounded like a horse trotting."*

"A lame horse," Kesh whispered. "I said you had a lame horse in your chest and the vet should look at it."

The ghost laughed. It was a real laugh, round and warm, and Noor felt it pass through the bone and through his own ribs and out the other side, and he had to blink hard and keep his hands steady. This was the cruelty and the mercy of his gift, braided together so tightly you couldn't tell one from the other: the dead were so *alive*. That was the thing no one believed until they heard it. Grief told you the dead were gone. The bone told you they were right there, just on the far side of a wall you couldn't climb.

*"Doesn't hurt,"* the mother said, answering the question Kesh hadn't dared repeat aloud. *"You asked. It doesn't hurt. It's like the minute after you put down something very heavy and you keep waiting for your arms to remember they're empty."* A pause, and then, softer, with an edge of mischief that made Noor think, absurdly, *so that's where Kesh gets it*: *"And I would never lie to you about pain. I lied to you about plenty. Never about that."*

"What did you lie about?" Kesh demanded, and then immediately, "No — wait — actually, don't answer that, I don't want a list—"

*"Your father didn't die before you were born."*

The room went absolutely still.

Kesh lowered her hands slowly. "What?"

Noor's stomach dropped. He knew — he *knew* — what was coming, the way you know a storm is coming by the smell of the air, and every instinct in him said *stop the carving, close the door*, but his hands wouldn't move, because this was hers to hear. Whatever it was. It was hers.

*"His name was Davorin Hale,"* the ghost said, and each word came slower than the last, dragged up from somewhere deep, the way a drowning woman drags herself toward air. *"He was a caravan guard. Brown coat, crooked smile, sang badly on purpose because it made me laugh. He stayed until you were born. He held you once. Once."* Something in the voice cracked, old anger preserved like amber. *"Then he heard the fever was in the quarter and he ran. Not from the fever. From the staying. Some men can carry a sword through any battle but can't carry a breakfast plate across a room twice."*

Kesh had gone white. "I was told he was dead. Grandda said he died in Vess. There's a story. There's a whole *story*—"

*"Grandda was protecting you. So was I. What does a child want with a father who chose to be dead?"* The gentleness in the ghost's voice was ferocious. *"But you're not a child now, and you asked me to stop lying, and I am so tired, my love, I am so tired of the shape of that lie."*

Kesh stood up. Sat down. Stood up again. She looked at Noor with an expression he'd never seen on her face — stripped bare, fifteen years old, the orphan under all the armor.

"He's alive?"

*"Was, the last I could see. Men like that live forever. They're too light to sink."* A pause, and when the voice came again it had softened into something almost shy. *"I didn't tell you to hurt you. I told you because you deserve the whole truth of yourself. Even the ugly parts. Especially those. You come from a woman who loved you more than breathing and a man who couldn't stay, and you became someone who stays. Do you understand what a miracle that is? You built the thing he couldn't. Out of nothing. Out of less than nothing."*

Kesh was crying now, properly, messily, without shredding anything, and Noor sat very still and let her, because some weeping is private even in company.

"I hate him," she said finally, thickly.

*"Of course you do."*

"And I want to find him and ask him why, and I hate that I want that."

*"Of course you do. Both things can live in you. They've been sharing the house for years; they've just never met."* The laugh again, fainter now. The bone was cooling in Noor's hands. Doors didn't hold long. *"My tall girl. My lame-horse girl. Turned out better than all right. Better than I ever managed. Now put that nice man out of his misery — he's been holding that bone like it's a hot coal, and he loves you like a daughter and he'd rather die than say it."*

"Mama!" Kesh yelped, scandalized, cheeks flaming.

Noor cleared his throat and studied the ceiling with tremendous professionalism. "I have no idea what she's talking about."

*"Liar,"* said the mother and the apprentice, in perfect unison, and for one impossible moment the dead woman and the living girl laughed together in the same small room, and Noor thought: *this. This is what the gift is for. Forget the murders and the warnings and the dread. This.*

The voice was fading now, going thin as smoke. *"I have to go where the road goes, my love. But listen — listen —"* urgency flared in it one last time, and Noor felt the bone pulse against his palms, felt something vast lean in behind the small voice, something that made the lamp gutter and the rosemary smoke bend — *"the roads are waking. Tell the carver. The roads remember him, and they are not the only ones. Someone is asking for him by name, and she is older than the fever, older than the kingdom, and she is very, very patient—"*

"Mama, what? Mama—"

But the voice was gone. The bone went cool and silent in Noor's hands, just bone again, just an ox's knuckle with some curves cut into it. The lamp straightened. The room exhaled.

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Then Kesh crossed the workshop in three strides and hugged him — a full, fierce, unannounced hug, the first in three years, brief as a lightning strike and gone before he could return it.

"If you ever tell anyone I did that," she said, red-eyed and deadly, "I'll break your thumbs."

"Noted."

She snatched the finished carving off the bench and turned it over in her fingers, frowning at it as though it might confess further secrets. "Davorin Hale," she muttered. "Brown coat. Crooked smile." Her jaw worked. "I'm going to find him someday. Not to forgive him. To make him look at what I built without him."

"That," Noor said, "is the healthiest reason to find anyone I've ever heard."

She almost smiled. Then stopped. "Noor. What she said at the end. About the roads waking. About someone asking for you by name." She looked up at him, and beneath the tear-swollen eyes her gaze was sharp as a filleting knife. "You already knew something was happening, didn't you? Since the stall. Since the prayer in the dead tongue. You've been walking around all week looking like a man counting the exits."

He weighed the lie. It would have been easy. He was good at lies; he'd kept his gift secret for thirty years, and lies were just doors you painted shut.

But she'd just given him the truest thing she had. It seemed a poor time to hand back a painted door.

"There's a goddess," he said quietly, "who's forgotten her own name, and she thinks I can help her remember. And I think she's not the only one coming back. And I think whatever's happening, it's going to need a doorkeeper." He turned his hands over, palm up, the way he did when he was telling the truth and hated it. "That's me, Kesh. Apparently I'm the last one."

Kesh stared at him. Outside, far off in the direction of the market square, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm bell, but the deep old bell in the tower that hadn't rung since Thornfell fell, the one everyone said was cracked beyond use.

It rang twice, and stopped.

Kesh looked at the window. Then at him.

"Noor," she said slowly, "tell me that's a coincidence."

He honestly wished he could.
