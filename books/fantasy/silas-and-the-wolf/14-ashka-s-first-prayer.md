## 14. Ashka's First Prayer

Noor knew something was wrong before he knew what it was.

He was mending a net down by the lake at Hollowmere — an honest task, the kind of work that kept his hands busy and his mind quiet and gave him no excuse to think about the king's femur sitting in General Ashka Vey's tent like a guest of honor. The net belonged to a fisherman named Odo who had lost it to a snag, and Noor had offered to fix it partly out of kindness and partly because knots were one of the few things in the world that never asked him anything.

The bone-carver's gift had been quiet for days. That should have been a relief. Instead it felt like the hush before a storm that everyone else can smell coming.

Then the birds went up off the eastern ridge all at once — not scattering, but *rising*, a dark sheet of them peeling away from the pines as if the mountain itself had flinched.

Kesh came running along the shore path, breathless, her braid half-undone, which meant she'd been sleeping and woken badly.

"Noor." She skidded to a stop on the wet stones. "The general's tent. It's — you have to see it."

"See what?"

"Just come."

* * *

They heard it before they saw it: a sound like a choir singing through a wall of water, low and vast and wrong, every voice a half-step out of tune with its neighbor. The camp was already emptying toward the ridge, exiles stumbling out of their tents with blankets around their shoulders, faces turned eastward the way flowers turn to sun.

Ashka's command tent stood at the top of the rise, and it was glowing.

Not burning. Glowing — a pale gold light leaking through the canvas seams like the tent had become a lantern, and from inside came the chanting, dozens of voices, Ashka's rising above them all in Old Thornish. Noor understood maybe one word in ten, but he didn't need more than one.

"*Vellath.* Open."

"No," Noor said, and started up the hill at a run.

Kesh kept pace. "She said she was going to study it. She said —"

"She said a lot of things." His lungs burned. "I told her the femur wasn't ready. I told her I hadn't carved it. A key-bone has to be carved by hand, by bloodline, with the right words cut into it — you can't just *pray* at a dead man's leg and expect —"

The light went white.

It wasn't an explosion. Explosions push outward, fast and finished. This was slower and infinitely worse: a soundless unfolding, like a book being opened by someone with enormous hands, and then the ridge above Ashka's tent simply *wasn't there anymore*.

Where the granite shoulder of the mountain had been, there was a gap. A wound. And from the wound poured fog — not drifting in from the valleys the way morning mist did, but pouring *out*, upward and sideways, thick as milk, and inside the fog Noor could hear whispering. Hundreds of whispers. Thousands. All of them saying the same thing in forty-year-old voices:

*Home. Home. Home.*

* * *

The ritual circle was still intact when they reached it — a ring of kneeling exiles, eleven of them, swaying like reeds, their eyes rolled back and their lips moving. The femur lay in the center on a cloth, and it was blackened along one side, charred as if it had been held in a fire, though nothing near it was burned.

Ashka stood over it. Her arms were raised. Her face —

Noor had seen Ashka Vey angry, and he had seen her devout, and he had seen her look at the map of old Thornfell the way some men looked at lovers. None of it prepared him for this. She was weeping without seeming to know she was weeping, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her cheeks, and she was smiling, and the combination was the most frightening thing he had witnessed since Thornfell fell.

"It worked," she breathed. "Noor. *Doorkeeper.* Do you see? Forty years and the door remembers us. It opened for us."

"That's not a door." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Doors don't bleed."

And it was bleeding. From the wound in the mountain, along with the fog, came something darker — threads of shadow winding down the rock face like sap from a cut tree, and where they touched the ground, the ground changed. Grass grayed. A pine tree at the edge of the wound had gone silver, needles and bark both, standing stiff as a carving of a tree rather than a tree.

One of the kneeling exiles slumped forward. Kesh caught her — a woman, sixty perhaps, her face slack, breathing but absent, her eyes moving under closed lids as if watching something urgent.

"Aunt Sefla?" Kesh shook her. "Sefla, wake up —"

"She's fine," Ashka said, and there was such serene certainty in it that Noor wanted to scream. "She's *witnessing*. They're all witnessing. The dead are showing them Thornfell as it was. Look at them — they're home, Noor. For the first time in forty years, they're home."

"They're unconscious," Noor said. "There's a difference between a door and a drain, General, and your people are what's going down it."

For just a moment, something flickered across Ashka's face — doubt, or its ghost. Then she straightened, wiped her cheeks with the back of one hand, and became the general again, brisk and certain and terrible.

"You're frightened," she said, almost gently. "That's understandable. But this is what the prophecy promised. The land itself opens for the bloodline. You felt it, didn't you? When you walked our roads. The country knows you."

"The country isn't supposed to *know* anyone," Noor said. "That's the point of a country. It's supposed to just be there, quietly, like a good chair. When furniture starts talking to you, you've either been drinking or something's very wrong."

Someone behind him made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Ashka did not laugh.

"Carve the key," she said. "Finish what I've begun. With a true key-bone, the wound becomes a gate — controlled, wide, permanent. Without it—" she gestured at the fog, at the silvered tree, at the whispering, and for a heartbeat her certainty trembled like a candle flame, "—without it, I don't know. Which is why you must carve it. *Now.*"

"And if I say no?"

The temperature of her gaze dropped ten degrees.

"Then I will pray again," Ashka said. "Tonight. Tomorrow. As many times as it takes. And each time, the mountain will pay for my patience." She spread her hands, reasonable, almost kind. "I would rather not. I have watched enough of this land burn. Help me open it *properly*, and no one else gets hurt."

Noor looked at the femur, blackened and humming faintly, at the eleven sleepers, at the fog rolling down toward the tents of two hundred people who trusted him — who had cheered when he arrived, called him *doorkeeper* like a title instead of a diagnosis.

"I need to think," he said.

"Think quickly."

* * *

Kesh found him an hour later at the lake's edge, where he'd gone because water was the only thing loud enough to cover the whispers. He had Odo's net in his lap, mended twice over, the knots so tight they'd probably outlive them both.

"So," Kesh said, dropping down beside him. "On a scale of one to 'we're fleeing in the night,' how bad?"

"Fleeing in the night is tempting," Noor admitted. "But there are two hundred people up that hill, and half of them think I'm the answer to a prayer their grandparents wrote. If I run, Ashka prays again, and the next prayer might crack the whole range open."

"So we stay and fix it."

"We stay and try not to make it worse." He fed a knot through his fingers without looking at it. "Kesh, I want you to understand what happened up there. She used a bone that was never carved. It's like — imagine someone found a violin in a tomb, four hundred years old, strings rotted, wood cracked, and they dragged a bow across it anyway. You wouldn't get music. You'd get a scream. And the mountain screamed."

"And now it won't stop."

"Now it won't stop." He glanced at her. She was fourteen-going-on-forty, all elbows and opinions, and she was watching the wound on the ridge with an expression he recognized because he wore it daily: fear held together with stubbornness and twine.

"My ma used to say," Kesh said slowly, "that zealots are just people who found one true thing and decided everything else was allowed to break." She picked up a flat stone and skipped it — three hops, decent form. "She said it about priests. Never thought I'd say it about a war hero."

"Your mother sounds like she missed her calling as a philosopher."

"She sounded like a lot of things." Kesh's voice went carefully casual, the way it always did when her mother surfaced. "Anyway. The sleepers. Aunt Sefla woke up about twenty minutes ago."

Noor's hands went still. "Is she all right?"

"She's — talking." Kesh hesitated. "In Old Thornish. Words nobody taught her. She keeps asking for someone named Brenna, says Brenna's waiting at the mill, says the bread will go stale. Except Sefla's never been to Thornfell. She was born after the fall. In Vess. She's never even *seen* a mill like the one she's describing."

The whispers from the ridge seemed suddenly much closer.

"It's not showing them visions," Noor said quietly. "It's pouring the dead *into* them. There aren't enough doors left, so it's making new ones out of whoever's nearest." He stood, net forgotten. "Get me the list of everyone who knelt in that circle. Every name. And Kesh —"

"Yeah?"

"If anyone starts speaking a language they shouldn't know, or asking for people who died before they were born, you come get me. Immediately. Don't wait for permission from the general. Don't wait for anything."

"What are you going to do?"

Noor looked up at the wound, bleeding fog into a sky that had been clear that morning.

"I'm going to do the thing I swore I'd never do," he said. "I'm going to listen."

* * *

Listening, it turned out, required getting closer, and getting closer required walking past Ashka's guards, and Ashka's guards required a reason.

He found one in the form of honesty, deployed sparingly, like strong spice.

"I'll carve the key," he told Ashka, standing in her tent with the blackened femur between them like a truce table. "Tomorrow. Tonight I need to sit with the wound and hear what it wants. A key cut blind breaks locks, General. I need to know the shape of the mechanism first."

It was mostly true, which was the best kind of lie. Ashka studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw — exhaustion, sincerity, the particular grayness of a man who has stopped arguing — satisfied her.

"One night," she said. "Dawn tomorrow, you begin the carving."

"Dawn," Noor agreed, and took his lie home with him like a stone in his boot.

The walk up to the wound took an hour, most of it uphill through fog that grew thicker and warmer the higher he climbed, until it felt less like weather and more like breath. The whispers resolved, gradually, from a crowd-murmur into distinct voices, and the distinct voices into distinct *people*, and by the time he reached the raw edge of the wound he was walking through a market square that no longer existed, hearing a fishmonger complain about prices, a child counting skips, an old man arguing cheerfully with his wife about whether the shutters needed painting.

All of it forty years dead. All of it utterly alive.

Noor sat down on a stone at the wound's lip, took out his knife and a blank piece of antler — not to carve, just to hold, the way a nervous man holds a rosary — and opened himself the way his mother had taught him, once, in a kitchen that smelled of bread and bone-dust, before he understood what she was teaching him and why she always looked tired afterward.

*Listen,* she'd said. *But never answer first. Whoever speaks first owes the other the truth.*

So he listened.

And beneath the market chatter, beneath the fishmonger and the skipping child, he heard the other thing. The thing under the wound. Not many voices but one, deep and slow, groaning up through the roots of the mountain like a ship settling onto rocks.

A woman's voice. Vast and frayed, worn thin as old cloth, and it was saying — over and over, patient as erosion:

*Not this way. Little doorkeeper, not this way. She is opening me wrong. Tell her. Tell her before the seam gives. Tell her —*

The voice broke apart into static, into fog, into the fishmonger complaining about herring prices, and Noor sat very still on the cold stone with his heart slamming against his ribs, because he knew that voice.

He had refused it once already, through a carved antler, in a workshop that smelled of shavings and fear. *Remember me,* it had begged. *Please. I've forgotten myself.*

The goddess. The mad, fragmented goddess who had spoken to him in the dark and whom he had turned away from, because carving gods was how his mother died.

And now Ashka had torn a hole in the mountainside, and the hole went *through* her.

Noor looked at the wound — at the fog bleeding upward, at the silvered trees, at the dark threads seeping down the rock like the world's slowest hemorrhage — and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that this was not a door being opened.

It was a body being cut.

And somewhere on the other side of the cut, a goddess who couldn't remember her own name was holding the seam together with her bare hands, and she was losing.

He was halfway down the hill, moving at a pace that would have alarmed any passing physician, when he heard the second thing.

From the direction of the camp, thin and high and carried on the wind: screaming. Not one voice. Many. And underneath the screams, rising through the fog like smoke through floorboards, a sound he had hoped never to hear again outside of nightmares — the deep, grinding groan of stone moving against stone, as if the mountain were turning over in its sleep.

Or waking up.

Kesh met him at the treeline, white-faced, out of breath, grabbing his sleeve with both hands.

"Noor — the sleepers. They're all awake now. All eleven. And they're standing in a line at the edge of camp, facing the wound, and they're *singing*." Her grip tightened. "Aunt Sefla looked right at me. Right through me. And she said —" Kesh swallowed. "She said, *Tell the little doorkeeper: she is opening me wrong.*"

The mountain groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere far below their feet, Noor felt it through the soles of his boots — a vibration like a heartbeat, quickening.

"Get Ashka," he said. "Wake everyone. Get them away from the east ridge, down to the lake, as far as the fog hasn't reached. Go *now*, Kesh."

"And you?"

Noor turned back toward the wound, toward the bleeding fog and the whispering and the goddess holding the seam with her failing hands.

"I'm going to go talk to a god," he said. "Pray for me. Apparently that's how all this works now."
