## 15. The Wound in Thornfell

The road to the wound did not exist on any map, which was fitting, because neither did the wound.

Noor had named it that in his head three days ago, when he'd first felt it through the soles of his boots — a wrongness in the mountain's rhythm, like a heartbeat skipping. Kesh had laughed at him for talking about mountains having heartbeats, and then she'd felt it too, and stopped laughing, and hadn't said much since except to ask twice whether they were nearly there and once, quietly, whether he was scared.

He'd told her the truth: "Terrified. But terrified is just a word for paying attention."

"That's not what terrified means," she'd said.

"It is where I'm from."

Where I'm from. He kept saying it without thinking — the old smuggler's habit, meaning the passes, meaning Thornfell, meaning a country that had been erased so thoroughly that cartographers in Vess now drew the region as "the Unclaimed Reaches" with a little decorative flourish, as if it were a marginal note rather than forty years of graves.

They climbed through fog that behaved strangely. Fog was supposed to drift. This fog *leaned*, pressing against them like a curious animal, and once — Noor would swear this later, and did swear it, loudly, at supper, to anyone who'd listen — it shaped itself briefly into a doorway before collapsing back into weather.

"Did you see that?" Kesh whispered.

"I saw fog."

"You saw fog do a thing."

"Fog does things. It's an overachiever."

But his hands were shaking, and Kesh saw, because Kesh always saw, and she didn't tease him about it, which was somehow worse than teasing.

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined waystation — one of the old Thornish marker-stones, carved with the faces of travelers who had died on the road, so that no one walked alone. The faces had been chiseled off during the Sealing, every one of them, methodically, by people who believed that remembering was a disease. Noor ran his thumb along the blank stone where a face should have been.

"They even took their faces," Kesh said.

"They took everything they could reach." He fed the fire another branch. "That's why we're going. They couldn't reach far enough."

* * *

The first village appeared on the second morning, sitting in a valley that Noor remembered being empty.

It wasn't a large village — maybe thirty houses, slate roofs, smoke rising from chimneys in thin gray threads. It looked, at a distance, like any mountain village anywhere. It looked like home, if you squinted, if you wanted it badly enough.

Except the smoke didn't rise. It hung above the chimneys, motionless, like paint.

"Noor." Kesh had gone very still beside him. "Tell me you see that."

"I see it."

"And the houses. Look at the houses."

He looked. The nearest house was solid enough — stone walls, a door, a window box with dead flowers. But the house behind it flickered. Not vanished; *flickered*, the way a candle gutters, there and not-there and there again, and in the not-there moments Noor could see the hillside through its walls, unbroken, untouched, as though the house had never been built at all.

And the third house was worse. It was two houses. It stood in both shapes at once — one with a steep gabled roof, one flat-roofed — overlapping like a double-exposed photograph, and looking at it made Noor's eyes water and his teeth ache.

"The land remembers," he said slowly, "and the land forgot, and both of those are true at the same time, and the mountain's wound has torn the seam between them."

"Say that again but in words."

"The country can't decide if it exists."

Kesh considered this. "Right. Yes. Horrifying. Good."

They went down into the valley anyway, because the road to the wound went through it, and because Noor had heard something — faint, threading up through the fog — that might have been singing.

* * *

The people of the village were milking goats.

This was the detail that undid him later, more than anything else. Not the horror of them — the ordinariness. A woman on a stool, working the teats of a brown goat into a wooden pail, humming. Two children chasing each other around a well. An old man mending a net, though there was no river within twenty miles.

They were flickering too.

The woman was worst. She'd be solid, warm, real — Noor could see the chapped red of her knuckles, hear the wet rhythmic splash of milk — and then she'd gutter, and for half a heartbeat there would be nothing on the stool at all, just the goat standing patiently beside empty air, and then the woman would be back, mid-hum, as if no time had passed. As if she herself didn't know she'd been gone.

"Hallo!" Noor called, because what else did you do. "Good morning!"

The woman looked up and smiled, and her smile was the kindest thing he'd seen in weeks, and then her face flickered and for an instant there was a skull beneath the skin — not menacing, not rotting, just *unfinished*, a sketch of a person — and then she was whole again.

"Morning, carver," she said. "You'll take breakfast? We've bread, and honey, if the bees are still being real today."

"If the bees are—" Kesh started.

"Some days they're not," the woman said placidly, as though discussing weather. "Some days nothing is. You get used to it. Or you don't, and then you're used to that instead." She patted the stool beside her. "Sit. You've got the look of men walking toward the Wound."

Noor sat. The stool held him, mostly. "You know about it?"

"We're *in* it, dear." She gestured with her chin at the flickering rooftops, the hanging smoke, the children who were sometimes two children and sometimes four and sometimes a smear of laughter-colored light. "When the mountain tore, everything between got pulled halfway out. Some of us hold better than others." She looked down at her own hands, which were currently translucent to the wrist. "I hold poorly. But the goats need milking either way, and the goats don't care what I'm made of."

"What's your name?" Kesh asked.

The woman smiled again, and this time the smile flickered too, appearing and disappearing like a lamp behind a curtain. "That's the trouble, isn't it. I keep reaching for it and finding a hole. I know I had one. I know it was a good one. My mother chose it and she never chose anything badly." Her translucent hand pressed against her chest. "There's a name-shaped space right here. It aches like a missing tooth."

Noor's throat tightened. He knew that ache. He'd spent forty years watching a whole country wear it.

"Vell might be able to help," he said, and immediately regretted it, because the woman's head came up sharply.

"The Lady of Antlers?" The old man with the net had drifted closer, silent as fog himself. "She walks here sometimes. Since the tearing. She stands at the edge of the village at dusk and looks at us, and she weeps, and none of us remember why that should frighten us, but it does. It does frighten us." His voice was gentle and absolutely certain. "A god weeping at you is worse than a god angry at you. Anger means you matter enough to punish. Weeping means—" he paused, searching, "—means you're already lost."

"She's coming tonight," the woman said. "She comes every night. Stay and see, if you're going up the mountain anyway. Maybe she'll speak to you. She spoke to a stone once, my grandmother swore it. Spoke to a stone for a whole hour, very politely, about the price of wool."

"A god talked about wool prices?"

"How do you think gods learn manners?"

* * *

Dusk came down purple and strange, and the goddess came with it.

Noor saw her first as a wrongness in the treeline — a shape the eye kept sliding off, the way it slides off the moon in daylight. Then the fog leaned aside for her, actually parted, bowing, and she stepped into the village square and she was *bodily* there, and Noor's whole body reacted before his mind caught up: knees softening, heart slamming, the ancient mammalian knowledge that something enormous was standing very close while wearing a manageable shape.

In the antler, all those months ago, she had been a broken voice, fragmented, half-mad, begging him to remember her. Bodily, she was worse. Bodily, she was beautiful the way winter is beautiful — vast, precise, indifferent to your comfort. She was tall as two women, crowned with a rack of antlers that branched like a frozen forest, and her skin was the gray-white of birch bark, and where her eyes should have been there were only deep, patient hollows, and inside the hollows, small lights moved like fish under ice.

The villagers did not run. That was the terrible part. They turned toward her the way flowers turn toward light, flickering faster, hope and terror indistinguishable.

"Lady," the old man whispered, and knelt, and his knees passed through the ground and back out and he didn't seem to notice.

Vell — though she had not yet given Noor her name, though he knew it only as a pressure behind his teeth, a word waiting — Vell crossed the square and stopped before the milking woman, and the great antlered head bowed, and the hollow eyes regarded her.

"You hold poorly," the goddess said. Her voice was the sound wind makes moving through a forest of antlers, if forests of antlers existed, which Noor was beginning to suspect they did somewhere.

"I hold as well as I can, Lady."

"It is not your fault. Holding was never meant to be done alone." A birch-bark hand rose, hesitated, and then — with a tenderness that cracked something open in Noor's chest — touched the woman's forehead. "I cannot give you back your name. It is not mine to give. It lives in the part of you that was sealed away, and the seal is strong, and I am—" the lights in her hollows dimmed, flickered, matching the villagers' flicker, contagion of grief, "—I am less than I was. Less than I ever was. Perhaps less than I should have been."

"Then what can you give?" the woman asked, and there was no bitterness in it, only the plain arithmetic of the desperate.

"Company," said Vell. "While you flicker, I will flicker with you."

And she knelt — a goddess, kneeling in goat-muck and guttering light — and she stayed.

Noor turned away, because some things were private even when performed in a village square, and found Kesh staring at him with an expression he'd learned to dread, the one that meant she'd noticed something about him that he hadn't noticed yet.

"You know her," Kesh said. "Not just of her. You've *heard* her. Before. In the carving."

"Yes."

"And she asked you for something, and you said no."

"Kesh—"

"You said no to a *god*, and now she's kneeling in the dirt comforting a woman whose name has a hole in it, and you're standing here with your jaw doing the thing it does when you're swallowing guilt." Kesh folded her arms. She was fifteen and five feet nothing and she had the moral authority of a magistrate. "So. What did she ask?"

Noor looked back at the square. Vell was holding the woman's translucent hands in her own birch-bark ones, and the old man had begun, softly, to sing — an old song, Old Thornish, the words worn smooth as river stones — and the flickering slowed, just slightly, just barely, as though the song were thread and the village were a tear in cloth.

"She asked me to remember her," Noor said. "Her name. Who she was. What she—" He stopped.

"What she what?"

"What she did." He met Kesh's eyes. "Carving gods is how my mother died, Kesh. I told you that much. I didn't tell you why. When you carve a god, you don't carve a statue. You carve a *door*. And doors open from both sides, and what came through ours, the night she finished it—" His hands, of their own accord, curled closed. "I said no. I'd say it again. I'd say it a thousand times."

"Would you?" Kesh asked quietly. She nodded at the square, at the kneeling goddess, at the woman whose name had a hole in it. "Looking at that? Same answer?"

Noor opened his mouth to say yes, because the answer was yes, because it had always been yes, because fear was a wall he'd built brick by brick over forty years and walls keep you alive—

And that was when Vell turned her head.

Not quickly. Nothing about her was quick. But the hollow eyes found him across the square with the certainty of a compass needle, and the small lights inside them stilled, and she rose, unfolding upward and upward, and walked toward him through the flickering dusk, and every villager watched her go with expressions of dawning, dreadful hope, because she had never once, in all her visits, looked at any of them the way she was looking at Noor Nystrom.

She stopped an arm's length away. Up close, the birch-bark texture of her skin resolved into something finer — script, maybe, or scars, or both, a history written on her in a language he almost read.

"Doorkeeper," she said.

"Don't call me that."

"You are the last one. The blood runs thin in you, thinner than in your mother, thinner than in hers, but it runs, and the door knows its own." The hollows tilted, considering him. "You refused me. I have thought about why, these long nights, kneeling in your mud among your flickering people. I thought perhaps cowardice. I thought perhaps hatred. Both would have been fair."

"And now?"

"Now I think you were protecting them." She spread one hand toward the village, the guttering houses, the woman with the hole where her name lived. "From me. From what I might be. From what came through your mother's door." The lights in her eyes dimmed to embers. "You were right to refuse me, doorkeeper. That is the horror of it. You were right, and it changes nothing, because the seal is breaking anyway — Ashka's ritual tore it, and every hour it tears wider, and when it fails completely, everything sealed behind it comes through at once. Everything. Including the one who wore my sister's face and ate a village's memories down to the babies' first steps. Including me, and whatever I was, before they took it from me."

The wind moved through her antlers and made a sound like distant mourning.

"I do not ask you to remember me anymore," Vell said. "I ask you to come up the mountain and *see*. See what your refusal protected, and what it failed to protect, and decide — with your eyes open, knowing what I am and what I was — whether the door should open, and who should walk through it, and whether anything that comes out deserves mercy."

Behind her, the milking woman flickered, and guttered, and for three full seconds was not there at all, and the goat waited patiently beside the empty air, and then the woman returned, mid-hum, not knowing she'd been gone.

Noor looked at Kesh. Kesh looked at Noor. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the mountain wasn't already saying, in its wounded, skipping heartbeat, louder every hour:

*Come and see.*

"All right," Noor said at last, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the only kind of brave he'd ever owned. "We'll come. But I'm bringing my apprentice, and she asks questions, and if you eat her I will find a way to make that a problem for you."

Something happened to Vell's face then — a movement around the hollow eyes, a crease in the birch-bark — and it took Noor a moment to recognize it, because he had never once imagined seeing it there.

She was trying not to laugh.

"Eat her," the goddess repeated, tasting the words. "Forty years of silence, and the last doorkeeper threatens me with a fifteen-year-old."

"She's very threatening."

"I believe it." Vell turned toward the mountain, toward the wound bleeding fog into the darkening sky, and the humor drained out of her like color out of a dying leaf. "Come, then. Come and see. And doorkeeper—" the hollow eyes slid back to him, and the lights within them had gone cold and bright, "—when you see what waits behind the seal, try to remember that I begged you, all those nights in the antler, and you could not hear me over the sound of your own grief. Whatever you decide up there — decide it *knowing* that."

She walked into the fog, and the fog received her like a host receiving an old friend, and Noor stood in the flickering village with his heart hammering and Kesh's hand suddenly small and fierce around his wrist.

"So," she said. "Mountain?"

"Mountain."

"And if the thing behind the seal is as bad as she says?"

Noor watched the fog close over the place where the goddess had been, and thought of his mother's door, and the thing that had come through it wearing a borrowed face, and the forty years of silence he'd built on top of that night like snowfall burying a house.

"Then," he said, "we'd better find out exactly how bad, before someone opens it for us."
