## 17. Serane's Ledger

The ledger had forty years of entries, and Serane Hallic had read every one of them so many times she could recite the margins.

She kept it in a locked case beneath her bunk, which was itself locked, in a wagon that traveled under guard of six soldiers who believed the case held maps. It did not hold maps. It held names. Four thousand one hundred and six names, written in the careful hand of Inquisitor Dannel Ostrava, who had died at Thornfell's border with ink still on his fingers, and continued since in Serane's own hand, which was less careful every year because her eyes were going and she refused to admit it.

Each name had a date. Each date had a symptom. And each symptom, when you laid them all out across four decades like bones on a table, made a shape that no one else seemed willing to look at directly.

*Madness*, the old reports called it. *God-sickness*. The plague of whispers that had swept through Thornfell in its final decade, when the old gods — hungry, half-forgotten, starving for worshipers who no longer remembered how to feed them properly — had begun taking what they needed by force.

Serane poured herself tea she would not drink and opened the ledger to the page she always opened first.

*Marta Ellin Voss, age nine. Began speaking in a voice not her own on the third of Harvestmonth. By the seventh, she had forgotten her mother's face. By the eleventh, she had forgotten her own name. The god speaking through her identified itself as "the Shepherd of Small Sorrows" and demanded offerings of memory. The family complied for six days. On the seventh day the child stopped breathing mid-prayer, smiling.*

Serane had been eleven years old when Marta Voss died. She had lived two villages over. She had not known the girl, but she had known the fear — the way it moved through the valley like weather, mothers holding their children's faces at night as if checking whether they still recognized them, fathers whispering prayers to gods they were suddenly afraid to pray to because attention was food and they could no longer afford it.

Her own parents had died three years later, in the last wave before the sealing. Her father had forgotten her entirely in his final week. He had looked at her with the polite blankness of a man greeting a stranger at a well and asked if she was the one who brought bread. She had said yes. She had brought bread every day after that, and he thanked her every day, kindly, courteously, for the bread, and never once again called her daughter.

So no. Serane Hallic was not cruel. Cruelty required a certain looseness, a willingness to let things be complicated, and Serane had spent forty years making certain that nothing about this was complicated at all.

The sealing had worked. That was the whole of it. The Gray Cloaks — her order, founded in panic and perfected in grief — had found the doorways between Thornfell and its gods and closed them, and the whispering plague had ended within a season, and children had stopped forgetting their own names, and the country had been allowed to die a natural death instead of being eaten alive from the inside by things that wore its dead like coats.

That Thornfell no longer existed on any map was a consequence of politics, not of the sealing. That its people were scattered and grieving was the fault of kings and treaties and the ordinary cruelty of borders. Serane's order had not destroyed a nation. Her order had performed surgery, and the patient had survived, and everyone who blamed the surgeon for the scar had never watched a child forget her mother's face.

She believed this the way she believed in the weight of stone: completely, without effort, without ever once having to remind herself.

Which was why the bone-carver troubled her so deeply.

* * *

"He's not what I expected," said Corporal Dennet, who was young enough to still have expectations.

They stood at the edge of the market in Marrowgate, watching Noor Nystrom's stall from a distance that was respectful rather than covert, because Serane had long ago decided that skulking produced worse intelligence than standing in the open and letting people lie to you naturally. The carver was showing a piece to an old woman — some trinket, some carved animal — and Kesh, the sharp-mouthed apprentice, was haggling with a farmer over something with the ferocity of a girl defending her own funeral goods.

"What did you expect?" Serane asked.

"Dunno. Something more..." Dennet waved a hand. "Sinister."

"The most dangerous people I've ever arrested were kind to animals and good with children." She sipped her terrible tea. "Look at his hands."

Dennet looked. "Carver's hands. Calluses."

"Left hand. The thumb." Serane had watched him work for two days now, from distances, through windows, once from a rooftop that had made her knees file formal complaints. "He holds the bone against the grain when he smooths it. Everyone else cuts with the grain. He goes against it."

"Why does that matter?"

"I don't know yet. But it's the same grip in the old inquisition sketches of doorkeepers. Forty-year-old drawings by men who watched doorkeepers work, drawn by people who didn't understand what they were seeing and drew it anyway, faithfully, because terror is very good at detail." She folded the ledger shut inside her mind. "He works against the grain because he isn't shaping the bone. He's listening to it. And you can't listen and cut in the same direction."

Dennet considered this. "That's either brilliant or mad, ma'am."

"In my experience those are usually the same thing wearing different coats." She finished the tea, grimaced at it — she had been grimacing at her own tea for twenty years out of stubbornness, having decided long ago that admitting she disliked it would mean admitting she'd wasted two decades drinking it — and stepped forward into the market.

Time to meet the last doorkeeper.

* * *

He was polite. That was the first thing. He stood when she approached, offered her a stool, offered her tea — *his* tea, which was better than hers, which annoyed her disproportionately — and answered her questions with the calm of a man discussing weather.

Yes, he carved keepsakes. Yes, sometimes families said strange things about the pieces, felt their dead near them, heard comfort in the curves. Grief did that. Grief made music out of anything.

He lied beautifully. Not fluently — fluency was for amateurs. He lied slowly, with small hesitations placed exactly where a truthful man's doubts would sit, and Serane found herself almost admiring it, the way one admires a well-built bridge even while planning to cross it with an army.

"You knew Thornfell," she said. "Before."

"Everyone my age knew Thornfell before."

"You guided smugglers through passes that don't appear on any map. Passes that shouldn't exist anymore."

"Smugglers exaggerate. It's how we advertise."

"And the notices posted this morning." She slid one across his workbench — the Gray Cloak seal, the word *DOORKEEPER*, the description that fit him the way a key fits a lock, which was of course the entire problem. "You've seen these."

"I can't read," he said pleasantly.

"You signed your mark on the customs register in Marrowgate in 731."

"Someone taught me to write my name. They didn't stick around for the rest of the alphabet."

Behind him, the apprentice — Kesh, thirteen or fourteen, all elbows and insolence — went very still over her whittling. Stillness, in a child that age, was louder than screaming. Serane noted it, filed it, and smiled her gentlest smile, the one that had unnerved hardened criminals into confessions because it contained, visibly, nothing at all.

"Master Nystrom," she said, rising, "I want you to know that I take no pleasure in any of what may come. If you are what the notices describe, then you are a danger to yourself and everyone around you, and everything I do next will be aimed at preventing a catastrophe that you cannot imagine because you were too young to watch the last one."

"And if I'm just a carver?"

"Then I'll owe you an apology and a very good dinner." She paused at the edge of the stall. "I make excellent apologies. My dinners are mediocre. Ask anyone."

She left him there and felt his eyes on her back the whole way down the lane, and thought: *he's deciding right now whether to run.* And thought: *he won't run. Men who run leave their apprentices behind, and he won't leave the girl.* And thought, with a weariness that surprised her: *I wish to every silent god that I were wrong about him.*

Because here was the thing Serane Hallic had never told anyone, the thing she kept in a smaller ledger, unwritten, behind her ribs:

She hoped she was wrong.

Every time. Every suspect, every whisper, every half-glimpsed carving that hummed at the edge of hearing — some tired, buried part of her hoped to be proven a fool. Because being wrong about the carver meant being wrong about the world, meant the sealing had been unnecessary, meant her parents' deaths had been meaningless rather than tragic, meant forty years of certainty had been forty years of comfortable lies.

And Serane Hallic did not know how to be that person. She had tried, once, briefly, in her thirties, after a bottle of wine and a conversation with a philosopher-priest who'd argued that certainty was just fear wearing armor. She had woken the next morning, read Marta Voss's entry again, and put the armor back on. It fit. That was the horror of it. It fit perfectly.

* * *

That night she wrote in the working ledger — the public one, the one her superiors would eventually see — the following entry:

*Nystrom, Noor. Bone-carver, Marrowgate. Denies literacy; evidence contradicts. Apprentice female, approx. 14, protective, observant — possible second subject or leverage. Subject displays behavioral markers consistent with doorkeeper training: counter-grain carving technique, familiarity with unrecorded passes, composure under direct inquiry exceeding civilian norms. Recommendation: approach with offer of cooperation before detention. A living doorkeeper who consents is worth ten who resist.*

She sat back and read it over and hated the last line, because it was true, and because truth in her profession was almost always a euphemism for threat. *Cooperate or we make you wish you had.* She had written variations of that sentence for twenty years and had told herself each time that the alternative — the plague, the forgetting, the children smiling as they stopped breathing — justified it.

Had it? She turned back through the ledger, past her own entries, into Dannel Ostrava's careful hand, into the numbers. Four thousand one hundred and six cases of god-sickness recorded in the final decade alone. Projected spread, per the mathematicians of the old order: the entire eastern seaboard within fifteen years. Children first. Children always first; the gods preferred fresh memories, unmixed ones, and a child's life was nothing but memories stacked neatly waiting to be eaten.

The sealing had saved hundreds of thousands. Possibly millions. The ledger proved it. The ledger proved everything.

Except.

Except there was a new entry she hadn't written yet, and it sat in her mind all night, refusing to be filed: the report from the northern pass. Fog where fog should not be. Whispers in a wound in the mountains. Villages flickering. Something had torn open — not because of the carver, but because of zealots using relics they didn't understand, exiles playing at resurrection. And the fog, the survivors said, spoke in voices. And the voices, the survivors said, sounded *sad*.

Sad. Not hungry. Not demanding. Sad.

Serane lay awake in her bunk with the ledger case beneath her and tried to make the word fit the architecture of her certainty, and for the first time in twenty years, it wouldn't go in cleanly. Sad voices in a wounded mountain. A goddess, maybe, or a fragment of one, bleeding through Ashka Vey's botched ritual — and sounding not like the Shepherd of Small Sorrows, not like the devouring things of the plague years, but like something hurt.

*They sealed us out,* the fog-voices reportedly said. *We only wanted to come home.*

Home. Serane stared at the ceiling of her wagon. Her parents had died in Thornfell. By the logic of the fog-voices, her parents' ghosts were on the wrong side of a door, wanting home, and she was the woman guarding the lock.

She got up, lit the lamp, and did not open the ledger. She looked at the case instead, for a long time, the way one looks at an old dog that has begun, unmistakably, to fail.

"There's another way to do this," she said aloud to the empty wagon, testing the words. "A lock that opens for the ones who need leaving. A sealing with mercy built into it."

The words hung there, absurd, treasonous, impossible. Her superiors would call it heresy. Her order would call it weakness. Dannel Ostrava's ghost would call it the unraveling of everything he'd died writing down.

But the carver — Nystrom, the last doorkeeper, the man who lied beautifully and worked against the grain — might call it something else. Might, if she offered it honestly, without the knife hidden in the word *cooperation*, show her a third path that neither her certainty nor Ashka Vey's fanaticism had room for.

She snuffed the lamp. Outside, far off toward the mountains, the fog glowed faintly along the ridge line like a bruise healing wrong, and the wind carried something that might have been whispering and might have been weather.

In the morning, she decided, she would find the carver alone. No soldiers. No notices. Just the ledger's arithmetic and an honest question: *help me build a better lock, or help me prove the lock should never have existed — but choose, and choose quickly, because the mountain is bleeding and I am not the only one marching on it.*

She slept, at last, badly, and dreamed of bread, and of her father thanking her kindly, courteously, forever, for the bread.

And thirty miles north, in a camp she did not yet know had been marked for burning, exiles gathered around fires and sharpened their grief into weapons, and the orders that would reach her by dusk were already moving — orders that would make her careful offer of partnership look, to every survivor of Hollowmere, like the coldest betrayal imaginable.
