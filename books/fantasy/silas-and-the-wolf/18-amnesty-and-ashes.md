## 18. Amnesty and Ashes

The smoke was visible from three ridges away.

Noor had been watching it grow for the better part of an hour — a gray column rising off Hollowmere like a crooked finger pointing at heaven, as if to say *here, here is where your hope lived, and look what we did with it.* He had told himself, in the way a man tells himself anything when the alternative is unbearable, that it might be a cookfire. A big one. A festival.

Kesh didn't bother with such fictions. She stood at the ridge crest with her knuckles white on her walking staff and said, flatly, "That's not a festival."

"No," Noor said.

"That's our camp."

"I know."

They ran anyway. It is a strange thing, running toward a thing you already know is ruined, but grief makes people do irrational things, and besides — there was always the chance. There is *always* the chance. That is the cruelest trick hope ever learned: it doesn't need to be likely. It only needs to be possible.

By the time they came down through the birch scrub into the hollow, the outer camp was a field of black skeletons. The tents of hide and patched canvas were gone. The long cooking lodge was gone. The shrine — the little shrine of stacked stones and river-worn offerings that someone had built in the first week, back when Hollowmere still felt like a beginning instead of a waiting room — was a smear of char and cracked rock. Ash lay over everything like dirty snow, and the snow itself, where it survived in the shadows, had melted into gray slush that stained their boots.

People moved among the ruins like ghosts who hadn't yet been told they were dead. A woman Noor knew by sight — Marta, who mended nets and sang off-key — sat on an overturned cart wheel holding a child's boot that had no child attached to it. Two men were digging through the ashes of the supply tent with bare hands, pulling out half-melted pots and setting them down again as if they couldn't decide whether anything was worth keeping.

And near the center, standing very straight in the middle of the ruin as though she could hold it up by posture alone, was General Ashka Vey.

She turned when they entered the hollow, and Noor saw her face change — saw something move behind her eyes that he recognized, because he'd seen it before, in mirrors, in his own worst moments. It was the look of a person who has already decided where to put the blame, and has found a place for it, and the place is convenient, and the place is *you*.

"You're back," Ashka said.

"We saw the smoke from the pass," Kesh said. "What happened? When?"

"Two hours ago. Maybe three." Ashka's voice was level, which somehow made it worse. "A Vess patrol column. Forty soldiers, maybe fifty. They came up the shepherd's track — the one we thought nobody remembered." Her eyes went to Noor when she said it. Not accusingly. Accusingly would have required effort. This was just a fact being placed on a table, gently, where everyone could see it.

Noor felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped into a well. "I didn't—"

"They burned the outer camp first," Ashka continued, as if he hadn't spoken. "Deliberately. They left the inner ring untouched." She gestured, and Noor followed her hand to the cluster of deeper shelters up against the cliff wall — the ones where the wounded slept, where the children slept, where *Ashka* slept. Untouched. Unburned. A message written in fire, addressed to someone specific.

"They wanted us to know they could have taken everything," Ashka said. "They wanted us to know they were being *merciful*. And they left this." She held out a sheet of heavy paper, the kind Vess used for official notices, its corner scorched but legible.

Noor took it. The seal of the Kingdom of Vess stared up at him — the mountain-and-crown, stamped in black wax. Beneath it, in careful bureaucratic script:

*By order of the Office of Inquisitorial Affairs, the encampment known as Hollowmere is declared an unlawful assembly harboring persons of interest. This notice serves as warning. Further assembly will be met with further measures.*

At the bottom, in different ink, added by hand:

*We know the doorkeeper dines with you. Ask him what he discussed with Inquisitor Hallic. Ask him what he promised.*

Noor read it twice. The second reading changed nothing except how cold his hands felt.

"I was negotiating," he said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "With Serane. For amnesty. For all of you."

"Yes," Ashka said. "So the notice says."

"So the notice *says*," Kesh repeated, and there was the sharp edge coming into her voice, the one Noor usually loved and right now wanted to hug close like a shield. "You think he sold us out? You think he walked into a Gray Cloak's parlor and — what, drew them a map while he was at it?"

"The patrol used the shepherd's track," Ashka said. "Nobody living remembers the shepherd's track. It fell out of use forty years ago. It exists now only in—" she stopped herself, but everyone heard the end of the sentence anyway. *In the memory of a doorkeeper.*

"I never told Serane about Hollowmere," Noor said. "Not the location. Not the name. Nothing."

"Then she guessed."

"People guess."

"Not *that*, not *there*, not with *those* roads." Ashka stepped closer. Up close, Noor could see that she was more tired than angry — that under the iron of her there was ash too, the same ash, and maybe that was why she needed somewhere to put hers. "Noor Nystrom. I have fought beside people my whole life. I know what betrayal looks like, and I also know what it looks like when a good man gets played by someone smarter than him. I'm not saying you meant this." She paused, and the pause was almost worse than an accusation. "I'm saying it happened *while you were having tea with her*. Whatever you think you were doing in that room, she was doing something else."

* * *

They buried what needed burying in the late afternoon.

There wasn't much. The soldiers had come at dawn, when most of the camp was asleep, and they had been — Noor heard the accounts over and over that evening, each teller adding a detail, subtracting another — they had been *correct*. Professional. They hadn't looted much. They hadn't beaten anyone who didn't run. They had given the camp one hour to clear the outer ring, and then they had burned it, and they had marched away singing, which was the detail that made old Tammas weep into his hands. "Singing," he kept saying. "Like it was a fair-day. Like it was nothing."

Marta found Noor by the remains of the shrine. She was still holding the boot.

"My boy's," she said, when she noticed him looking. "He's six. He was up at the spring when they came, thank all the quiet gods. But his boots were by the fire." She looked down at the little boot as if seeing it for the first time. "They burned his boots, carver."

"Marta—"

"Where were you?" She asked it simply. Without venom. That was the terrible part — there was no venom in it at all, just the plain arithmetic of a mother whose child's boots had burned while the man who could walk any road in creation was off drinking tea with the people who lit the match. "They say you were with the Gray Cloaks. Is that true?"

"It's true I met with her. Inquisitor Hallic. I was trying to buy safety for this camp. For everyone here."

"And did you?"

Noor opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was *I don't know*, and the more honest answer was *it doesn't look like it*, and the most honest answer of all was one he couldn't say out loud: *I don't know what I agreed to, because agreeing to anything with Serane Hallic is like signing a contract written in water.*

Marta nodded slowly, as if he'd answered anyway. "My husband used to say, when you sit down at a table with wolves, bring your own chair. So you've got something to stand on when it goes wrong." She tucked the boot inside her coat, against her heart, and walked away, and Noor stood alone among the cracked offering-stones feeling every year of his life.

Kesh found him there at dusk. She didn't say anything at first — just sat down next to him on a fallen stone, shoulder to shoulder, the way she did when words were optional and presence wasn't. Somewhere across the hollow, someone had gotten a fire going in the inner ring, and the smell of soup was fighting its way through the smell of ash, which Noor chose to take as a small, stubborn victory of the human spirit.

"You know it wasn't your fault," Kesh said eventually.

"Do I?"

"Well. No. Probably not. You're doing the thing where you take responsibility for weather." She nudged him. "But listen. Even if it *was* your fault — which it isn't — sitting out here freezing won't fix it. Come eat. Tammas made the soup with the good barley, the smuggled barley, the *felony* barley. It'd be a crime to waste it. A second crime today, technically."

Despite everything — despite the ash and the notice and Marta's boot — Noor laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, like a bird startled out of a hedge, but it was real. "You're a horror, Kesh."

"I'm a comfort," she said. "A deeply unsettling comfort. Come on."

* * *

But the evening did not stay gentle.

The whole camp gathered in the inner ring around the big fire — what remained of the whole camp, which was perhaps sixty souls where there had been nearly two hundred. People sat close together against the cold, and the firelight made moving shadows of them, and the mood in the circle was the mood Noor had dreaded all day: not grief, exactly, or not only grief, but grief looking for a shape. Grief wanting a name. Grief doing what grief does when it isn't given honest work.

Ashka stood.

Noor watched her rise and felt the circle tighten around him without anyone moving. It was a strange talent she had — the general's gift, the one that had once made ten thousand soldiers charge uphill singing. She didn't point at anyone. She didn't raise her voice. She simply stood, and the fire caught the gray in her hair, and she said:

"Today we lost our homes. Our stores. Our shrine. We lost the work of two years, and we lost it to soldiers who sang while they burned." Murmurs ran around the circle like wind through dry grass. "And I want to be honest with you, because you deserve honesty and we have too little time left for anything else. Those soldiers knew roads that are forty years dead. They knew where we slept and where we stored our grain. They knew things about this camp that only people *inside* this camp should have known." She let that hang. Then, quietly: "Or people the inside of this camp trusted."

Every eye in the circle turned to Noor.

It was not universal. He counted, in the way a drowning man counts the heads above water — Marta looked away, ashamed of herself or of him, he couldn't tell. Old Tammas scowled at Ashka rather than at him. A handful of the younger exiles — the ones who had never known Thornfell, who had only inherited its loss like a debt — stared at him with open, hungry suspicion. And Kesh was on her feet before Noor could even draw breath.

"Oh, that's filth," she said. "That's beautiful, clean filth, that is. Burned out of your beds, so you go hunting for someone to blame, and who's handy? The outsider. The bone-man. The one who was — let me check my notes — *miles away*, risking his neck trying to get the people who did this to leave us *alone*."

"He met with the inquisition," said one of the young exiles, a lean man named Dorren whom Noor had once carved a whistle for. "In secret. And the next morning the soldiers come."

"The *next morning*," Kesh snapped. "Soldiers march for days, you absolute turnip. If he'd sold you out, the meeting would've been last *week*."

"Dorren," Noor said, and his voice was quiet enough that it cut through the noise better than shouting would have. The circle hushed. He stood, slowly, knees aching, and faced them all. "I met with Inquisitor Hallic. That's true. I'll tell you everything that passed between us, every word, tonight, in front of anyone who wants to hear it. But I didn't give her Hollowmere. I didn't give her the roads. I swear it on—" he stopped. Swearing on the gods felt obscene lately; they were too loud in his bones, too present, too *complicated*. He started again. "I swear it on my mother's grave, and on every bone I've ever carved. Which, if you know anything about me at all, is the strongest oath there is."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere a child started crying and was hushed.

And then Ashka said, softly, in the voice of a woman laying down a card she'd been holding all evening:

"Then you won't mind answering one question, carver. Just one." She stepped forward into the firelight. "When you met with Hallic — what did you promise her?"

And Noor, who had been turning the question over in his own skull for two days like a stone with something underneath it, found that he could not answer quickly enough. One heartbeat. Two. Three.

Because the truth was that he *had* promised her something. He had promised her the door. He had promised that when the time came, he would help her seal the old gods away forever — and he had told himself it was a lie, a gambit, a coin spent to buy time, but promises have a way of not caring what you intended when you made them, and Serane Hallic struck him as a woman who kept excellent records.

Three heartbeats of silence, in a circle of frightened people, is a very long time.

Ashka nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed everything. "That's what I thought," she said, and turned to the circle, arms open, magnificent in the firelight. "This is what happens when we put our faith in one man's secrets. Tomorrow we stop waiting on prophecy and bone-whispers. Tomorrow we talk about what *we* do — all of us, together — before Vess comes back and finishes the job."

The circle murmured agreement, and broke apart into knots of whispered conversation, and Noor stood in the firelight feeling the ground shift beneath him — not the waking ground of the passes, not the living country that knew his name, but the older, crueler ground of human trust, which gives way so much faster.

Kesh stayed beside him. Of course she did.

"For what it's worth," she muttered, "I believe you."

"I know," Noor said. "Thank you."

"But also?" She looked up at him, firelight in her eyes, and her voice dropped low enough that only he could hear it. "You *did* promise her something. Didn't you. I can see it all over your face, and I've been reading that face since I was nine years old."

Noor looked into the fire, where the flames had gone low and blue at their hearts, and said nothing at all.

Across the hollow, Ashka was speaking quietly with Dorren and two others, their heads bent together over something — a map, maybe, or a plan, or a list. And Noor understood, with the cold clarity that comes after shock wears off, that the burning of the outer camp had done something worse than destroy tents and grain.

It had given her her argument. *One man's secrets.* And tomorrow, unless he found a way to change the story, the whole camp would follow her wherever she led — even into the wound in the mountain. *Especially* into the wound in the mountain.

"Kesh," he said quietly. "How fast can you walk?"

She followed his gaze to Ashka's huddled knot of advisors, and he watched her understand — watched the sharpness in her sharpen into something like fear.

"How fast do we need to?" she said.
