## 20. Two Keys

Noor didn't sleep. He sat in the wreck of Hollowmere's great tent — half the canvas scorched, the center pole leaning like a drunk uncle at a wedding — and turned Kesh's words over and over like a stone he was trying to find the heart of.

*She's of the line too. Distantly. Her grandmother was a Thornish woman who married out before the fall.*

Two carvers. Two of the bloodline. The thought had arrived whole, the way his best insights always did — not through reasoning but through the bones themselves, whispering up through the floorboards of his mind even now that he'd sworn to stop listening.

Except he hadn't sworn anything, had he? He'd only stopped carving gods. And the dead were patient. The dead were *always* patient.

"You're doing the face," Kesh said from the doorway. She had a bowl of something gray in each hand and the expression of someone who had lost the argument about whose turn it was to cook. "The one where you stare at nothing and your eyebrows do all the talking."

"My eyebrows are having a private conversation."

"Well, tell them supper's ready." She set a bowl beside him and folded herself down onto an overturned crate, cross-legged, elbows on knees. She was sixteen going on forty, all sharp angles and sharper tongue, with her mother's dark hair cropped short for practicality and her unknown father's stubborn chin. "You've been sitting here since Ashka stormed off. That was four hours ago."

"Was it?"

"Time moves when you're not brooding. It's one of its features." She blew on her soup. "So. Are you going to say it out loud or make me guess?"

Noor looked at her. Really looked. At the orphan who had followed him out of Vess's border towns because he paid in stew and stories; at the girl who had stood between him and General Ashka Vey's drawn blade and called treason *treason* to the most feared woman in the exile camps; at the girl who, as of yesterday, was apparently the second key to a door Noor had spent his whole life pretending didn't exist.

"Kesh," he said carefully, "how would you feel about learning to carve?"

She snorted into her soup. "Badly. I once whittled a duck so ugly a dog apologized to it."

"I'm serious."

"You're always serious. It's your worst quality after the eyebrows." But she set the bowl down, and he saw the wariness come into her eyes, the quick animal alertness she'd never quite unlearned from the streets. "Why?"

Because the door needs two of us. Because Ashka wants to use you as fuel and Serane wants to use you as a lock and I want — what did he want? He wanted her safe, and safety had just become the one thing he couldn't offer anyone.

"Because," he said instead, "you stood up for me when you didn't have to. And because if I'm right about what's coming, you're going to need every tool I can give you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest part of one."

Kesh studied him for a long moment. Then she picked up her bowl again and drank the rest of the soup in one unbroken motion, the way street kids drink everything, like someone might take it. "Fine. Tomorrow. Dawn. And if I carve another duck, we never speak of it."

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold over Hollowmere, and Noor discovered that teaching was harder than carving.

"No, no — *with* the grain, not against it. You're fighting the bone. The bone has been dead longer than either of us has been alive; show some respect."

"The bone started it," Kesh muttered. Her knife skidded again, and a curl of antler spun off across the workbench. They were using antler scraps, shed pieces Noor had carried for years without purpose — safe material, inert, silent. Even so, he kept his voice low. Old habits. Old fears.

They worked in the lean-to behind the rebuilt cook-tent, away from the camp's main paths. Word had spread since Kesh's stand against Ashka: the smuggler-carver and his apprentice, the two of the bloodline. Exiles watched them pass now with an attention that felt less like reverence than hunger, and Noor hated it. Hunger made people do arithmetic with other people's lives. He'd seen Ashka do it. He'd seen Serane do it, in her colder, cleaner way.

"Again," he said.

"This time I'll aim for beautiful. Or at least recognizable."

The third attempt was a lopsided bird. Not a good bird. A bird designed by committee, if the committee hated birds. But when Kesh set it down and wiped her hands, Noor felt something flicker along the edge of his senses — faint as heat off a stone at dusk. Not a voice. Not yet. Just... an opening. A door cracked a finger's width in a wall he'd thought solid.

His breath caught.

"What?" Kesh said instantly. "What is it? Is it singing? Please don't let it be singing, I'll feel terrible about the bird."

"It's not singing." He turned the little carving over in his palm. "But it's *listening*. Do you understand what that means? Every piece I've ever carved alone just — receives. Voices come through. But this—" He shook his head, marveling despite himself. "This is a channel. Two ends. Yours and mine. The bloodline doesn't work like one voice calling into the dark. It works like two people holding a rope between them."

Kesh frowned. "Meaning what, practically?"

"Meaning the door needs both of us. Together. Willing." He set the bird down between them like evidence. "Ashka can't force it open with the king's femur and a thousand conscripted prayers. Serane can't seal it shut with one carver, no matter how many Gray Cloaks she marches up the mountain. All her certainty, all her soldiers — useless without a second of the blood. And all of Ashka's zealotry too."

Something loosened in his chest as he said it. For days he had felt like a coin being fought over, heads to Ashka, tails to Serane, spending him either way. But coins could only be spent by their owners. And he had just learned he wasn't a coin. He was half a key — and the other half was sitting across the workbench, sucking antler dust off her thumb.

"So they need us," Kesh said slowly. "Both of them. The zealot and the inquisitor. They need *us* more than we need them."

"Now you're doing arithmetic."

"I learned from watching you count smuggled brandy barrels. Everything's arithmetic if you're brave enough." She grinned, and then the grin faded into something more careful. "Noor. If they need us together — what happens if we just... don't? Pick a side, I mean. What if there's a third option?"

He had been circling that question all night, the way his knife circled a knot in the grain, looking for the angle where the wood would finally yield. Trust Ashka, and the door tore open on a rite built from exile lives, and whatever came through would come through mad and starving. Trust Serane, and the door sealed forever, and the dead stayed locked in the gray country, unfinished, screaming politely for eternity.

Or.

"There's a library," he said. "Under the ice. Vell told me about it — the goddess, the one in the antler. Records from before the fall. Why the first inquisition really sealed the gods away. What they were afraid of." He met Kesh's eyes. "If we're going to choose a third way, we should know what the first two ways were actually about. Everyone keeps telling me the truth like it's a weapon. I'd like to read it myself."

"A frozen archive under a mountain. In winter. Past Vess patrols, past Ashka's scouts, past whatever's leaking out of that wound she tore in the pass." Kesh ticked the hazards off on her fingers. "And you're proposing this like it's a picnic."

"I'm proposing it like it's the only plan I have."

"That's how all your plans start." She was already reaching for her pack. "Fine. But I'm bringing the ugly bird."

"Why?"

"For luck. And because if a goddess speaks through it, I want to watch her apologize to the duck."

* * *

They left at midmorning, when the fog off Hollowmere was thinnest, and Noor went first to the one person he'd promised himself he wouldn't consult.

Ashka Vey received them in what remained of her command tent, standing over a table map weighted with knives at the corners. She looked as though she hadn't slept either; there was a rawness around her eyes that might have been grief wearing armor. Since the burning of the outer camp, since Kesh's defiance, since Noor's refusal, something had gone brittle in her — and brittle things, Noor knew from bone, held their shape right up until they didn't.

"You're leaving," she said. Not a question. Her scouts were good.

"We're traveling," Noor corrected. "There's a difference."

"There is not. Not anymore." She planted both fists on the map. "Every day that door stays shut, my people dissolve a little further into what they lost. You've seen them, carver. The ones who've stopped speaking Thornish because it hurts. The ones who pray to gods who can't hear. You hold the bloodline in your hands like it's yours—"

"It isn't mine," Noor said quietly. "That's rather the point. It's hers too." He nodded at Kesh. "And it's not a hammer, General. It's a hinge. Hinges don't work alone."

For a moment something moved behind Ashka's eyes — hope, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Then open it. Both of you. Now. Today. I'll give you everything — guards, supplies, the femur—"

"And the ritual you planned," Kesh cut in, arms crossed. "The one with the volunteers. The ones who'd 'lend their strength.' How much strength were they lending, exactly? All of it?"

Ashka's jaw tightened. "Sacrifice is the price of nations."

"Sacrifice is what you call it when it's other people," Kesh said, and Noor felt a fierce, inappropriate pride rise in his chest like spring water.

Silence stretched. Then Ashka straightened, and the general came back into her spine, and the hope — if it had been hope — was folded away somewhere soldierly. "Go, then. Read your frozen books. But know this, Noor Nystrom: while you travel, I am not idle. The wound in the pass widens. Something on the other side of it is *pushing*. When it breaks, and it will break, I will be there with a plan. Make sure yours is better."

It was as close to a blessing as she had left in her, and Noor took it the way you take a knife offered handle-first: gratefully, and without turning your back.

* * *

Serane Hallic found them at the tree line, which was exactly like her. No ambush, no squad of Gray Cloaks melting out of the pines — just the woman herself, hood back, hands visible, standing in the snow like a merchant waiting for a fair price.

"You're difficult to meet privately," she said by way of greeting. "Your apprentice posts lookouts like a professional."

"She is a professional. I pay her in stew."

"One day I'll learn your routes, carver, and then we'll talk about what you owe me in back taxes." But there was no weight behind it, and Noor noticed — had been noticing for weeks — how tired she looked. Certainty, he was beginning to understand, was heavy luggage. She carried hers everywhere, and it showed in her shoulders.

"You heard we're leaving," he said.

"I hear everything in these mountains. It's my least favorite talent." She glanced at Kesh, then back. "You're going to the archive. Under the ice."

Noor went very still. "How—"

"Because I've known about it for twenty years, and my predecessors for twenty before that, and none of us ever burned it, though we burned everything else." Something flickered across her face — not guilt exactly; guilt would have been simpler. "Do you know why? Because the archive contains the full accounting of the god-plague. What the old gods did before the sealing. What one of them *did.* I have read those records, Noor. I have read them until the words wore grooves in me. And I sealed the door in my heart long before I ever proposed sealing it in stone."

"Then why haven't you stopped us?"

Serane was quiet for a moment. Snow settled on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off. "Because last month I would have arrested you both. Last week I would have followed you. This morning I realized I'm curious what *you'll* conclude." Her mouth twisted, wry and unwilling. "I'm beginning to suspect that's dangerous. In myself, I mean. Curiosity. It's how inquisitors go wrong."

"Or how they go right," Kesh said.

"Pray it's the first. I'm much more useful wrong." Serane reached into her cloak and produced a small oilskin packet, holding it out. "Maps. The southern approach to the ice field avoids the wound in the pass entirely — mostly. And a token. Show it to any Gray Cloak patrol and they'll report your passage north as sanctioned reconnaissance. It buys you perhaps six days before the reports reach my superiors and I'm forced to explain myself."

Noor didn't take it at once. "Why?"

"Because you asked me *why*, and it's the first question anyone's asked me in years that I couldn't answer with a doctrine." She pushed the packet into his hands anyway, briskly, as if embarrassed by the whole transaction. "Read the archive, carver. Read all of it. Including the parts that will make you hate the gods. Especially those parts. Then come find me, and we'll see whether an inquisitor and a doorkeeper can build something neither of us has a word for yet."

She turned to go, then paused. "The girl. Kesh."

"Yes?" Kesh said, wary.

"Your grandmother's name was Sela Nystrom. She was my grandmother's midwife, before the fall. Small world, these mountains. Smaller every year." And Serane Hallic walked away into the pines, leaving Noor staring after her with the packet heavy in his hands and the whole shape of the world quietly rearranging itself.

* * *

That evening, packed and provisioned, Noor sat with Kesh at the fire while the camp settled around them — the murmur of exiles, the smell of barley and woodsmoke, someone's fiddle playing a Thornish air with three notes missing, the way all their songs were played now.

"Two keys," Kesh said, poking the coals. "One zealot who wants the door blown open, one inquisitor who wants it welded shut, and both of them suddenly being almost reasonable, which is somehow scarier." She glanced sideways at him. "And a frozen library full of reasons to hate the gods we're supposed to save. You realize whichever way we walk tomorrow, somebody's going to feel betrayed."

"Somebody already feels betrayed. That's the family business lately." Noor fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "But here's what I keep coming back to. Ashka wants to restore a country. Serane wants to bury one. Neither of them is asking the question I should have asked from the beginning."

"Which is?"

"What the dead actually want." He said it simply, and the fire popped, and somewhere far off in the dark the wounded mountain gave a low sound like a whale breathing. "We've got a zealot's answer and an inquisitor's answer and a goddess's answer, and nobody's asked the dead. So that's the third way, Kesh. We go to the ice, we read what's written, and then — before anyone opens anything or seals anything — we ask."

Kesh was quiet a moment. Then she reached into her pack, pulled out the ugly antler bird, and set it on a flat stone between them, facing the fire like a small, lopsided guest of honor.

"Together, then," she said. "Bloodline answering bloodline."

"Together." Noor smiled despite everything — despite the wound in the mountain, the whispers in his workshop, the mother's voice he still hadn't dared listen for. Hope, he was rediscovering, was not a feeling. It was a decision you made daily, usually over bad soup, usually with company.

Above the fire, the stars came out hard and cold over the high passes, and beneath them, deep under the ice, a library waited with its terrible, patient pages.

They broke camp before dawn.
