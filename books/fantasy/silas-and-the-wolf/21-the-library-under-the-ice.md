## 21. The Library Under the Ice

The goddess had given them directions the way a drunk gives directions to her own house — with great confidence, several contradictions, and one crucial detail delivered at the last possible moment.

"Follow the river until it forgets it's a river," Vell had said, her voice still strange and layered, like three women speaking in unison and only occasionally agreeing. "Then climb where the mountain's shoulder meets its neck. The door is where the ice keeps a secret."

"Which river?" Kesh had asked.

"The one that forgets."

"That's every river eventually," Kesh said. "That's what rivers do."

But she'd meant the Skaldva, as it turned out — the gray ribbon of meltwater that ran out of the high passes and vanished into a limestone sink two days north of Hollowmere, swallowed whole by the earth without so much as a splash. The smugglers called it the Thirsty River and avoided it on principle, because water that went somewhere underground could come back up somewhere else, usually under your camp in the middle of the night.

Noor stood at the edge of the sinkhole now, watching the river pour itself into darkness, and felt the bone tokens in his coat pocket humming like bees in a jar.

"She's down there," he said.

"Or she's lying," Kesh said. She crouched at the lip, peering down into the blue-black gloom where the water disappeared. "Gods lie. You told me that yourself. Chapter four of your very depressing life story."

"I said gods *forget*. Lying requires you to remember what the truth was."

"Comforting. Truly."

He almost smiled. That was Kesh's gift, though she'd deny having any gifts at all: she made the terrifying feel merely annoying. Two weeks ago he had been drowning in whispers, and she had sat with him through the worst night of his life with a cup of barley tea and a running commentary on how if all these dead people were so eager to talk, they could at least take turns like civilized folk.

Now she shouldered her pack and looked at him expectantly, this sharp-boned girl of sixteen who had crossed half a mountain range alone to save him, and who would rather die than be thanked for it.

"The shoulder meets the neck," Noor said, pointing up-slope, where a ridge of black stone jutted from the glacier like a collarbone. "That has to mean the ridge. Come on."

* * *

The climb took most of the day, and the cold took the rest.

It was not winter — spring was arguing its case in the valleys below, green shoots bullying up through old snow — but up here the seasons had never signed any treaties. The glacier held the ridge in a grip of blue ice, ancient and groaning, and the wind came off it with the particular malice of weather that has never been loved by anyone.

Noor's hands ached inside his gloves. Carver's hands, his mother had called them, long before he understood what she'd meant — hands that felt everything, that remembered every shape they'd ever held. Right now they remembered warmth with a bitterness that bordered on grief.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Kesh shouted over the wind. She was ahead of him on the rope, moving with the easy confidence of someone who had grown up climbing things she wasn't supposed to climb.

"Because a goddess asked."

"A goddess *begged*. There's a difference, and I don't love either one." She hauled herself over a shelf of ice and reached back for his arm. "You don't trust her. You've said so six times since Hollowmere."

"I don't trust what she wants from me. Those are different problems."

"And what does she want?"

Noor considered the question honestly, which was more than he usually managed. The bone tokens hummed against his chest. Somewhere beneath the ice, something vast and patient was waiting, and every step closer made the humming louder, warmer, almost eager.

"To be remembered," he said finally. "And I'm afraid of what we'll find out about why everyone stopped."

Kesh was quiet for a moment. Then: "My mother used to say that about my father. That people didn't leave because they were cruel. They left because remembering hurt too much." She turned and kept climbing. "She was wrong about him, by the way. He was just cruel. But it was a nice thing to say."

* * *

They found the door at dusk, when the light went copper and the glacier's shadow swallowed the ridge whole.

It was not a door, exactly. It was a seam — a vertical line in the ice where the blue deepened to something older, darker, shot through with threads of silver that might have been frozen water or might have been writing. Noor's fingers found it before his eyes did, drawn along the rock the way water finds a crack, and when his bare skin touched the seam, the humming in his pocket rose to a single clear note.

A word surfaced in his mind in Old Thornish. He didn't know how he knew it; the language had died forty years before he was born, and yet it lived in his blood the way songs live in birds.

"*Open for the keeping,*" he translated aloud, wondering.

"Don't say it out loud if you're not going to do it," Kesh said. "That's how curses start. Everybody knows that."

He pressed his palm flat against the seam and spoke the word anyway, because he was tired of being careful, and the ice sighed.

Not cracked. Not shattered. *Sighed* — a long exhalation of frost-smoke — and the seam widened into an archway, and beyond the archway there was air that did not move, and stairs cut from black stone descending into a blue so deep it seemed less like color than like memory.

"Well," said Kesh. "That's either a library or a tomb."

"In Thornfell those were often the same building," Noor said, and stepped through.

* * *

The cold inside was different from the cold outside — dry, still, respectful, like the cold of a church. Their breath hung around them in slow clouds that refused to dissipate. The stairs went down farther than stairs had any business going, each step carved with a word worn nearly smooth, and Noor trailed his fingers over them as he descended and caught fragments: names, dates, titles. *Keeper of the Western Door. Keeper of the Salt Road. Keeper of Names.*

Keeper of Names. His hand paused on that one longer than he meant to.

At the bottom, the stairs opened into a hall that took his breath away even after the breath had already been taken by the cold.

Shelves. Thousands of them, carved directly from the living rock, rising into darkness overhead, and every shelf packed with bones.

Not skeletons — he'd braced himself for that, and Kesh had made him promise not to scream like a first-year apprentice. These were worked bones. Carved bones. Tens of thousands of them, each one etched with lines of script finer than anything Noor could produce on his best day, arranged in rows and bundles and coils, labeled with small tags of antler. A library where every book was a bone, and every bone was a book, and the silence of them was not empty but *full* — the silence of ten thousand voices holding their breath at once.

"Oh," Kesh whispered. For once in her life, she had nothing clever to add.

Noor walked forward slowly. The nearest shelf held femurs, dozens of them, each carved head to foot with text. He lifted one — it came free easily, as if it had been waiting — and the moment his skin touched it, the whispering began.

Not the drowning chorus of his workshop. This was orderly. One voice, calm and dry as dust, reading itself to him:

"*Record of the Sealing, third day. Present: the Nine Keepers, the King's Voice, and the delegates of the Quiet Concord...*"

"Noor." Kesh's voice, tight. "Noor, look at the tags."

He looked. Each antler tag bore a title, and the titles were not what a library of a dead nation should have held. Not ledgers of grain or records of law, though those were here too, he could feel them further in. The nearest shelves read:

*Testimony of the Drowned of Merrow Bay.*
*Last Words of the Plague Year, Collected.*
*What the Gods Said Before We Stopped Listening.*

"They recorded everything," Noor breathed. "Every death. Every testimony. They carved their dead into the archive so nothing would be lost." His throat tightened. "This is what I do. This is what I've always done. It wasn't a curse. It was a *profession*."

"Your whole tragic backstory is just a family trade," Kesh said. "Devastating. My condolences." But her voice wobbled on the last word, and she turned away quickly, pretending to examine a shelf of rib bones, and Noor pretended not to notice that she needed the pretending.

* * *

They worked by the light of Kesh's lamp and the faint blue glow that seeped from the ice above, following the tags deeper into the hall, past testimony and testament, past the collected grievances of a nation that no longer existed, until they found the section that Vell had sent them to find — whether she'd known she was sending them or not.

The tag read: *Why We Sealed Them. The Full Record. Do Not Open Lightly.*

"Somebody carved 'do not open lightly' on a tag," Kesh said. "Four hundred years ago. That's the historical equivalent of a wet floor sign, and we're both about to slip on it."

"We're already wet," Noor said. "Metaphorically."

"You've been spending too much time with me."

He lifted the first bone from the rack. It was a skull — human, old, the carving so fine it looked like lace laid over bone — and the voice that spoke through it was a woman's, clipped and formal, the voice of someone accustomed to being believed.

"*I am Sera Ilvane, Fourth Keeper of the Western Door, and I make this record so that no future keeper opens the way out of mercy alone. Hear me.*

*"You will have heard that the gods were sealed out of fear, or spite, or the ambition of kings. Some of that is true. Kings are always ambitious. But hear the rest of it, the part the priests burned and the soldiers forgot.*

*"One of the Nine fed on worship.*"

The hall seemed to lean closer. Even Kesh stopped fidgeting.

"*Not their bodies. Not their blood. Their memories. The god my grandmother's generation called the Tender One — the Comforter, the Unforgotten — took worship in the form of remembrance. Pray to it, and it would hold your grief for you. Your worst day. Your shame. The face of everyone you'd lost. It would carry these things so you need not, and its worshipers loved it for that, and who can blame them? Grief is heavy. Here was a god who offered to carry it.*

*"But it did not carry them. It ate them. And when a worshiper had given enough — when the god had drunk down every memory that made them who they were — what remained was a smiling, hollow thing that loved the Tender One with its entire emptied heart, and could not remember why, and did not care.*

*"We found villages of them. Whole valleys. People who had given away their children's faces, their own names, the taste of bread. They were happy. That is the horror I need you to understand, whoever you are, whenever you are reading this. They were *happy*, and they begged us to let them keep giving, and the god behind them smiled with a mouth that had learned smiling from ten thousand stolen lives.*

*"So we sealed the Nine. All nine, because we could not be certain which of the others had learned from it, or aided it, or simply failed to stop it while it grew fat. Perhaps that was unjust. I have prayed about it every day of my life and received no answer, which is itself an answer of a kind.*

*"Mercy opened the door the first time. Let certainty seal it. And let whoever comes after me weigh both, and forgive us, and do better if they can."*

The voice stopped. The hall was silent except for the slow tick of freezing water somewhere far above.

Kesh sat down abruptly on a shelf ledge, lamp trembling in her hand. "The Tender One," she said. "That's — Noor, that's what Ashka wants to let *back in*. She wants to open the door for *that*."

"For all of them," Noor said quietly. "Including it. Including whatever else is in here that we haven't found yet." He set the skull down with a care that surprised him, the care you give a sleeping animal. "Serane Hallic spent her whole life trying to keep this door shut, and I thought it was grief and fear. Some of it is. But some of it—" He touched the tag. *Do Not Open Lightly.* "Some of it is this woman, four hundred years dead, standing in a valley full of hollow, happy people, deciding to lock the sky."

"So she was right?"

"I don't know." He laughed, and it came out ragged. "That's the problem. I don't know. Vell sent me here to prove the sealing was a crime. Instead she handed me the evidence for the defense."

"Maybe she doesn't know what's in here," Kesh said. "She's missing forty years and her own name. Maybe her memory got sealed along with her."

"Or maybe," Noor said slowly, "she knows exactly what's in here, and she sent us anyway, because she wanted us to see it. Because she's been carrying something for four hundred years that she needs forgiven for, and she couldn't ask until someone understood what forgiveness would cost."

Kesh looked at him for a long moment. In the lamplight her face was all angles and stubbornness, and underneath that, briefly visible, the frightened child she'd been before the roads adopted her.

"You think she's the Tender One," Kesh said. "You think Vell ate somebody."

Noor didn't answer. He didn't have to. Down the length of the hall, on a shelf apart from all the others — set aside, isolated, wrapped in chains of carved iron that had rusted to lace — sat a single bone, glowing faintly, humming a tune he recognized.

It was the melody Vell had sung to him through the antler. Her name-song. Her true name, the one she'd begged him to remember.

Someone had quarantined it here, four hundred years ago, and locked the door, and written *do not open lightly* — and Noor's feet were already carrying him toward it, and Kesh was saying his name in the voice you use when someone walks toward a cliff edge, and the bone sang sweeter the closer he came, sweet as absolution, sweet as forgetting.
