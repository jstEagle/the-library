## 22. Which Gods Deserve Saving

The library under the ice did not want them to leave.

Noor felt it in the way the cold settled into his joints, patient as a creditor, and in the way the frost on the archive walls caught the lamplight and held it, so that every step they took away from the reading alcove seemed to circle back toward it. The shelves rose around them like the ribs of some enormous drowned beast — bone-white ice shot through with darker veins, and inside those veins, pages. Thousands of pages. Tens of thousands. Frozen mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-prayer, preserved by a winter that had come down from the mountains forty years ago and simply never left.

"They're watching us," Kesh said for the third time.

"They're not."

"The books are watching us, Noor. I've been a thief since I was six. I know when something's keeping an eye on its valuables." She stood with her arms crossed, breath steaming, wearing three of everything because Noor had insisted and she had complained about it the entire climb up from Hollowmere. "Also my toes are gone. Just so you know. Whatever you find in there better be worth eight toes."

"Ten toes."

"That's what you think now."

He almost smiled. It was getting harder to do that lately, and he noticed himself hoarding the almosts. They had come down out of the wound in the mountain with Vell's directions still ringing in his skull — *the library remembers what I cannot* — and every hour since, the goddess's voice had grown quieter in his carvings, which should have been a mercy and instead felt like a held breath. Somewhere behind them, Ashka was consolidating her grip on Hollowmere. Somewhere ahead of them, Serane Hallic was doing whatever certainty did when it wasn't burning shrines. And here, in between, under forty years of ice, sat the answer to a question Noor hadn't known how to ask until now: *why had the first inquisition sealed the gods away?*

Vell had told him the doorkeeper bloodline was meant to free them. She had wept antler-light and called herself forgotten. She had made him feel, for one terrible afternoon, like an heir to a purpose.

But Vell had also admitted her memory was taken. Taken, not lost. And taken things, Noor knew better than anyone, were usually taken *from* someone.

"Start here," Kesh said, pulling a shelf free of the ice with a crack that echoed down the frozen aisles. She'd gotten good at this — the careful violence of extraction, warming the ice with bare palms until it surrendered the page inside without tearing it. "This whole row is administrative. Ledgers, grain counts, someone's very passionate opinions about bridge tolls." She squinted at the Old Thornish script. "I can read maybe half of it. You read the other half and lie to me about what it says, and I'll know."

"You always know."

"It's my best quality."

They worked. That was the thing about Kesh no one expected, looking at her — the sharp tongue and the quick hands and the orphan's habit of taking up space like she owned it — underneath all of that she could work for hours, silent and steady, the way only people who had learned young that idleness was dangerous ever could. Noor unrolled page after page against the lamplight. Grain ledgers. Census rolls. A tax dispute between two villages that had apparently hated each other for a century over a millpond. The ordinary sediment of a dead country, and it ached to read it, the way all ordinary things ache once they're gone.

It was Kesh who found the trial records.

"Noor." Her voice had changed. He looked up. She was holding a page close to the lamp, and the light through the thin vellum turned her fingers red as if she were holding them over a flame. "These aren't ledgers."

She passed it to him, and he began to read, and the library got colder, or he did.

---

The records were judicial. That was the horror of them, later — not their content, at first, but their *form*. Someone had written these carefully. Someone had numbered the exhibits. Someone had ruled lines in neat margins and dated everything, because even at the end, even with the sky coming apart, Thornfell had been a country that believed in paperwork.

*Inquiry into the events at Marenholt, being a full account of the worship of the Lady of Antlers, taken before the Circuit Court of the Eastern Reaches.*

Marenholt. Noor knew the name the way you know a scar's shape without remembering the fall. A river town in the eastern valleys. His mother had mentioned it once, in the flat voice she used for places that had stopped meaning anything.

He read.

The Lady of Antlers. Vell. Of course it was Vell; he had known it would be before he finished the first line, the way you know a knock at the door is bad news by the quality of the silence after it. But knowing did nothing against the slow accumulation of the record, exhibit by exhibit, witness by witness.

A priestess named Odile Harn, who testified that the Lady had asked her congregation for their memories — not demanded, *asked*, gently, the way a friend asks to borrow something — and that those who gave had grown serene and empty and wonderfully easy to lead. Exhibit four: a list of one hundred and six names. One hundred and six people who had walked into the Marenholt river in a single winter night, smiling, because the Lady had shown them a memory of drowning that was so beautiful they wanted to keep it forever, and the only way to keep a memory forever is to die inside it.

Exhibit nine: testimony from a man whose daughter had given the Lady her memory of him. All of it. Every day of her childhood, every kindness, every quarrel, every time he'd carried her on his shoulders through the barley fields. She had looked at her own father afterward with the polite blankness of a stranger, and thanked him for his hospitality, and gone back to the shrine. He had come to the court not to accuse anyone, he said. He had come because he wanted someone official to write down that his daughter had existed, that he had loved her, that the love had been real even if she couldn't find it anymore. Someone should write it down. That was all.

Noor set the page down on the ice shelf with hands that did not feel like his own.

"Noor?" Kesh's voice came from far away. "You've gone the color of the walls."

"She drowned them," he said. His voice sounded strange in the frozen air. "Not — not all of them. A hundred and six. In one winter. And there are more records, Kesh, look—" He pulled another page from the vein of ice, and another. Harrow Fen. The cult of the Quiet Shepherd, who had been Vell's sibling-god or her lover or both, depending on the account, and who had eaten memories too, but without Vell's gentleness. Without asking. A village where nobody remembered their own names and the Shepherd had answered prayers in all their voices at once. A god-war, in the last decade of Thornfell, fought not with armies but with congregations hollowed out and pointed at each other like weapons.

And threaded through all of it, everywhere, the same refrain: *the Lady of Antlers, who takes memory as offering.*

"No," Noor said aloud, to the page, to the ice, to the goddess whose voice had been growing quiet in his carvings for three days. "No, she told me—"

"What did she tell you?"

"That she forgot. That her memory was *taken*, as a punishment, as a mercy, she didn't know which. She begged me to remember her. She said the gods didn't abandon Thornfell, that they were sealed out, that my bloodline was meant to open the door." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "She never said what she'd done to get sealed."

Kesh took the page from his hand — gently, the way she took things when she thought he might break them or himself — and read it through, lips moving over the Old Thornish. When she looked up, her face was doing something complicated.

"So the inquisition," she said slowly, "the first one. The one that burned everything and froze the passes and killed your mother's whole world. They weren't just — what did Ashka say? Tyrants? God-haters?"

"They were stopping this." He gestured at the shelves, at the tens of thousands of pages, at the whole frozen weight of a country's evidence against itself. "Or they believed they were. Serane's family died in the god-plague, she said. The old wars. I thought she was talking about something distant. Something historical." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Kesh, this isn't history. This is a *case file*. This is why the Gray Cloaks exist. They've been right this whole time, or right enough, and I've been feeling sorry for myself about my gift while they were carrying the memory of *this*—"

"Stop." Kesh said it quietly, which from Kesh was louder than shouting. "Stop doing the thing where you take the worst possible reading and wear it like a coat. Sit down. You look like a fence post in a windstorm."

"I don't want to sit down. I want—"

"You want it to be simple." She pushed him, not hard, onto an overturned crate that some long-dead archivist had probably also used for sitting and despairing. "You want Vell to be the sad forgotten lady and the inquisition to be the villains and your gift to mean you're the hero of a story somebody planned out. And now the papers say otherwise and you'd rather drown in them than sit in the ugly middle where everything actually lives." She crouched in front of him, eye level, and her expression was fierce and young and older than either. "My mum used to say the truth's like a fish. You can gut it fast or gut it slow, but either way there's blood on your hands, so you might as well eat."

"That's disgusting."

"She was a fishwife's daughter. Everything she taught me was disgusting and true." Kesh folded the trial record with unnecessary care and put it in Noor's breast pocket, over his heart, like a warrant. "So. Eat. Tell me the ugly middle."

He ate. He talked, haltingly at first and then in a flood — about the exhibits, about the hundred and six, about the father who only wanted his daughter written down. About the fact that Vell's memory had been taken *by the other gods*, according to one marginal note in a different hand, a desperate annotation: *if she will not stop, she must forget — let her forget herself first.* The gods had tried to police their own. It hadn't worked, or hadn't worked enough, and then the mortals had done what mortals always do when heaven fails: they had built a door and locked it, and posted guards, and called the guards holy.

"And now Ashka wants to open that door," Kesh said, "and put *that* back in the world."

"And Serane wants to seal it tighter. And both of them are standing on real ground." Noor stared at the lamplight. "That's the vertigo of it, Kesh. I keep waiting for one side to turn into monsters so I'll know what to do. And neither one will. Ashka's exiles really did lose everything. Serane's order really did save people. And Vell—" His voice snagged. "Vell wept. I *heard* her weep. Can a thing that weeps like that still be the thing in exhibit four? Can a god be forgiven what a mortal would hang for?"

"Depends," Kesh said.

"On what?"

"On whether forgiveness is a thing you give or a thing you build." She shrugged, uncomfortable with her own wisdom, deflecting before it could settle. "My mum again. Don't look at me like that. Look — a man steals bread, you fine him. A man burns a village, you hang him. What do you do with a thing that burned a hundred souls and then had the memory of burning *taken away*? Is she the one who did it, or the one who woke up in the ruins of herself with no idea why everyone flinches?" Kesh shook her head. "There's no law for that. There's no gallows tall enough and no fine big enough. Which means it's not a law question."

"Then what is it?"

"A question for whoever's holding the key." She tapped his breast pocket, over the folded page. "Which is you. Congratulations. Terrible job. No pay."

Noor laughed despite everything — a small, cracked, real laugh — and for a moment the library was just cold and vast and full of paper, and Kesh was just Kesh, crouched in front of him with icicles melting in her hair, and the world had edges again.

Then the humming started.

It came up through the floor first, through the ice, a vibration Noor felt in his teeth before he heard it. The lamps guttered. Down the frozen aisles, deep in the stacks, the veins of dark ice began to pulse — slowly, steadily, like a heartbeat made of shadow — and from somewhere far below them, beneath the archive, beneath the mountain's roots, came a sound that Noor's bones recognized before his ears did.

Singing. Thousands of voices, singing in Old Thornish, in a hymn he had never learned and knew completely.

Kesh grabbed his arm. "Tell me that's the wind."

"It's not the wind."

"Tell me anyway. Lie to me. It's your turn."

But Noor was already on his feet, because he had understood — all at once, the way ice understands spring — what the singing was. Not the dead gods. Not the dead of Thornfell. It was the *archive itself*, the prayers frozen mid-word forty years ago, waking. The library under the ice had been a prison as much as a preserve, and something had just knocked the lock loose — something up at the wound in the mountain, something Ashka was doing with the king's femur and a thousand exile lives — and every sealed prayer in the dark was beginning, syllable by syllable, to finish.

And the nearest prayer, the one rising fastest through the ice toward their feet, was addressed — he could feel it through his boot soles, through his marrow — to the Lady of Antlers.

To Vell.

Who was, as far as Noor knew, alone and bodiless somewhere near the wound, gathering herself out of fog and whispers, growing stronger with every hour that Ashka's rite tore the border thinner.

"Kesh," he said. "We have to get to the mountain."

"Yeah," she said, already moving, already stuffing pages into her coat with a thief's reflexes. "I figured. Any chance it's for a fun reason?"

Noor looked back once, at the pulsing dark veins, at the hymn climbing through the cold.

"No," he said. "I think she's about to remember."
