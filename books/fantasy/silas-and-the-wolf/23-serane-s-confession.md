## 23. Serane's Confession

The meeting place Serane chose was a mill, long abandoned, three miles downriver from Hollowmere where the water no longer turned anything but itself. Noor arrived early because he had learned that arriving early to a meeting with an inquisitor was the only way to arrive at all — she had a talent for being somewhere before you agreed to meet her there.

Kesh had argued against coming.

"You're walking into a barn alone with the woman who wants to nail your skull to a notice board," she'd said, arms crossed in the gray light before dawn, her breath steaming like a small angry kettle.

"She wants my help, not my skull."

"People say that right up until the nailing."

He'd almost smiled at that. Almost. "If I'm not back by moonrise, take the widow's letters and go east. Don't come looking."

"I never look for you," Kesh said. "I *find* you. There's a difference, and it's mostly that I complain less than people who look."

Now he stood in the ruined mill with his hands in his pockets and watched the wheel turn uselessly in the current, creaking its one note over and over, and tried not to listen to the bones in his satchel, which had been murmuring since morning. Not words. Not yet. Just the restless rustle of the dead sensing something on the wind — the way dogs know weather.

Serane Hallic came through the door without ceremony. No escort. No gray cloak even — she wore a plain traveler's coat, brown as river mud, and for a moment Noor genuinely did not recognize her. Without the uniform she was simply a woman of perhaps fifty, tall, narrow, with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who had spent decades sitting very straight while terrible things were decided around her.

"You came," she said.

"You sound surprised."

"I gave you every reason not to be here." She set a small canvas bag on the millstone between them. "I brought bread. The kind from the baker on Cordwainer Row, the one with the burnt bottom crusts. I noticed you buying it twice."

Noor stared at her. "You've been watching me buy bread."

"I watch everything. It's the job." Something flickered across her face — not quite embarrassment, but its distant cousin. "The watching, I mean. Not the bread. The bread is just bread."

* * *

They ate sitting on the millstone, legs dangling, which struck Noor as so absurd that he nearly laughed. An inquisitor and a smuggler, sharing a loaf with burnt bottoms in a dead mill, while somewhere behind them a mountain bled fog into the sky.

"The wound is worse," Serane said, when the silence had gone on long enough. "My surveyors measured it this week. It's grown forty paces wider since Ashka's ritual. The villages inside it — Marrowdale, Little Sennet — they're not villages anymore. They're *memories* of villages, flickering. A woman waved at my scout yesterday and then wasn't there, and then was there again, older, and then wasn't."

"I know. I've walked it."

"Then you know better than anyone what happens if Ashka forces the door open at that scale. Half of Vess's northern provinces could end up flickering." She brushed crumbs from her coat with precise, economical movements. "So. You've met my terms. You've refused them. You've ignored them. I thought perhaps we should try conversation instead. I'm told it's what civilized people do."

"Who told you?"

"A book. I didn't care for it."

Despite himself — despite everything — Noor felt the corner of his mouth move. "You're not what I expected, Inquisitor Hallic."

"No," she agreed. "People expect cruelty. Cruelty is easier to hate. Certainty is much harder." She looked out at the turning wheel. "May I tell you why I'm certain? Not to persuade you. I've given up persuading you — you have your mother's stubbornness, and I knew your mother, a little, though she wouldn't remember me. I'm telling you because you deserve to know who you're negotiating with. That's all. Afterward you can refuse me again, and we'll both feel we've done our duty."

The wheel creaked. Somewhere upstream, a bird complained about the weather. Noor found that his hands had gone still in his lap — truly still, for the first time in weeks, no carving, no fidgeting, no listening.

"Tell me," he said.

* * *

"I had a brother," Serane said. "Ollen. He was four years younger than me and better at everything, which I resented enormously and would give my remaining eye to resent again." Her voice was level, unhurried, the voice of someone who had rehearsed this not for others but for herself, many times, in the dark. "We grew up in Candlemere, on the old border, back when Thornfell still existed and everyone pretended the border was a friendly thing rather than a scar."

She turned her hands over, looked at them as if checking for something.

"When the god-plague came through — you'd have been a child, you may not remember it clearly — it didn't kill the way plagues kill. It didn't take bodies. It took *selves*. People caught it from their gods, or their gods' relics, or their prayers. One day your neighbor was your neighbor. Three days later he was a mouth repeating the same devotion over and over, faster and faster, until the words wore through him like water through stone, and what was left wasn't him anymore. It was the god wearing him, badly. Like a coat cut for someone larger."

Noor's throat tightened. He thought of the voices in his workshop, thousands strong, asking to go home. He thought of how easily *asking* could become *insisting*. Could become *taking*.

"My parents caught it from a shrine relic," Serane went on. "A saint's fingerbone, if you can believe the irony. My father first. I came home and he was kneeling in the kitchen, praying in Old Thornish — a language he didn't speak, Noor, he was a tax assessor, he spoke Vessish and bad poetry — and he looked up at me and smiled with such *joy*, and it wasn't his smile. It was too big for his face. Ollen caught it two days later. He was nine." 

For the first time, her voice bent. Only slightly. A hairline crack in fired clay.

"He prayed for six days. On the seventh, the Gray Cloaks came through with their censers and their ledgers, and they did what the protocol required." She met Noor's eyes. "You know what the protocol required."

"I know," Noor said quietly. Everyone on the border knew, in the way you know a thing you've agreed never to say aloud.

"They were gentle," Serane said. "That's the part I've carried for thirty years. The inquisitor held Ollen's hand while he did it, and Ollen squeezed back, and for one moment — one — my brother was in his own eyes again, and he was *grateful*. Do you understand? The sealing saved him from whatever was eating him, and it cost him everything else, and in that last second he knew it and chose it." She looked away, at the wheel. "Or I've told myself that for thirty years because the alternative is unbearable. I honestly don't know anymore which it was. That's what grief does. It doesn't make memories. It makes arguments."

The mill creaked. Noor realized his eyes were wet and did nothing to hide it.

"Afterward," Serane continued, steadier now, "the inquisitor — a woman named Doss, who smelled of lamp oil and lavender — sat with me in the kitchen and explained that the plague had come *through* the old gods. That Thornfell's pantheon had rotted from the inside, that some of them had begun feeding on worship instead of receiving it, and that the only mercy left was a wall. She said: *someone has to hold the wall, child, or it happens again.* And I was eleven years old, and I said, *I'll do it.*" 

She laughed — short, dry, entirely without humor. "Eleven-year-olds should not be allowed to make vows. But I made it. Forty years I've made it, brick by brick, ledger line by ledger line. Every shrine burned, every relic confiscated, every doorkeeper hunted — I have told myself it is a wall, and walls keep out wolves. And then your mountain tore open, and I stood at the edge of that wound, and I heard what was on the other side of my wall." 

Her jaw worked.

"It wasn't wolves, Noor. It was *people*. Frightened, unfinished people, knocking politely. And some of them sounded like my brother might have sounded, if anyone had ever asked him what he wanted instead of deciding for him."

* * *

Noor sat with that for a long time. The wheel turned. The bread sat forgotten between them.

"You could have led with any of this," he said finally. "Months ago. Instead of notices and threats."

"Notices and threats work," Serane said. "Confessions are slower, and I had a schedule." She winced at her own words. "That was a joke. Mostly. I'm told my humor is an acquired taste, like the bread."

"The bread's burnt on the bottom."

"So am I, apparently." She sighed, and something in her seemed to settle — the way a house settles, walls finding their true weight. "Here is what I actually believe, stripped of protocol. I believe the sealing was necessary once. I believe it has become a habit, and habits performed for forty years stop being choices and start being *costumes*. I believe Ashka Vey will kill thousands trying to tear the wall down, and I believe that if we simply patch the wound and walk away, the wall stays a prison for things that may deserve better than a prison." She turned to face him fully. "And I believe you are the only person alive who can hear both sides of it — the living and the dead — which makes you either the most dangerous man in Vess or the most useful one, and I would very much prefer useful."

"And what are you offering?" Noor asked carefully. "Not amnesty. Not threats. What's the actual shape of it?"

"Partnership." She said the word plainly, without ornament. "You open nothing and seal nothing without hearing the dead first — really hearing them, the way you described to my scouts at the wound. I bring the archive access, the legal machinery, the authority to stand between you and every Gray Cloak in the north. If the dead want release, we find a way to give it that doesn't dissolve villages. If some of them want what my brother's god wanted—" her voice hardened, "—then we seal *those*, specifically, precisely, instead of everyone. Surgery instead of a wall. I can't promise my superiors will accept it. I can promise I'll spend my career making them."

Noor looked at his hands. Carver's hands, scarred, steady. Hands that had spent a lifetime listening to the dead alone, in secret, carrying every voice like stones in his chest because there had never been anyone to share the weight with.

It would be so easy. That was the treacherous part. Here was a woman who understood — who had lost what he had lost, who feared what he feared, who was offering to carry half. All he had to do was say yes, and the hunting would stop, and Kesh would be safe, and the whole impossible tangle would resolve into order.

And order, he thought, was exactly what Ashka offered too. Just a different flavor of it. *Open the door and go home,* Ashka said. *Seal the door and stay safe,* Serane said. Both of them holding out a finished country, a settled answer, a road already paved.

And both of them, he suspected, were more frightened than they would ever admit.

"If I say yes," Noor said slowly, "it isn't surrender, and it isn't alliance. It's a wager. We find out together what the dead want. And if it turns out they want something neither of us can give — you don't burn it, and I don't open it. We come back to this mill and argue again. Agreed?"

Something eased in Serane's shoulders — a tension so old it had probably forgotten it was tension. "Agreed." She extended her hand, then paused, glancing at his satchel with wary curiosity. "Does it... does it bother them? The shaking of hands. The dead. Are they watching?"

Noor considered the question seriously, because she had asked it seriously. "They're always watching. But mostly they gossip. Right now I believe your grandmother is telling them you eat too fast."

Serane went very still. "My grandmother has been dead twenty-two years."

"I know," Noor said gently. "She says you got the stubbornness from her, and she's not entirely proud of it."

For a moment — one unguarded moment — Inquisitor Serane Hallic, terror of the northern shrines, looked like an eleven-year-old girl in a kitchen, being told that someone she loved was still, in some fashion, reachable. Then she mastered it, the way she mastered everything, and shook his hand with a grip like a well-oiled hinge.

"We should return," she said. "Before your apprentice organizes a rescue and gets us all killed. She's been following us since the bend in the river, by the way. Behind the alder stump. She's very good."

"She's the best," Noor said, with fierce, uncomplicated pride.

"One more thing." Serane gathered the bread bag, hesitated. "My surveyors reported movement at the pass this morning. Torches. A great many torches, arranged in a pattern my people say matches nothing in any rite they know — but it matches something in *yours*. Old Thornish funerary geometry." Her eyes were sharp again, the softness folded away like a knife. "Ashka isn't waiting for you anymore, Noor. Whatever she means to do, she's begun."

The wheel creaked. Far off, toward the mountains, the sky had taken on a strange pearlescent glow, like the underside of a shell — and in his satchel, every bone began to sing at once.
