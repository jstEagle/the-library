## 24. Ashka's Coup

The trouble with zealots, Noor had learned over a long life of carving other people's grief, was that they were almost always kind first. Ashka Vey had fed him stew. She had mended the tear in his coat sleeve herself, with small neat stitches, humming an old Thornish marching song under her breath. She had learned how Kesh took her tea — bitter, no honey, "like punishment," Kesh said approvingly — and had it waiting every morning.

That was the trap of it. You could not hate someone who remembered your tea.

Noor stood at the mouth of Hollowmere camp on the morning everything changed and watched the exiles gather in the gray light, and he understood, with the slow cold certainty of a bone cracking in the hand, that he had waited too long to be afraid.

They came out of their tents in silence. Not the sleepy shuffle of a normal morning — this was purposeful, dressed, armed where they could be. Ashka stood on the flat rock at the camp's center, and beside her, wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping child, lay the king's femur.

"Noor." She beckoned him forward without looking away from her people. Her voice carried the way a bell carries, clean and unavoidable. "Come stand with me."

He went, because refusing in front of three hundred desperate people felt like a kind of cruelty, and because some traitorous part of him still wanted to believe there was a version of this morning where nothing broke.

"You've all felt it," Ashka said. "The mountain bleeding fog. The whispers in the stone. The wound our enemies made when they sealed our gods behind the skin of the world." A murmur ran through the crowd, low and hungry. "For forty years we have prayed into a locked room. For forty years we have been told to wait. To grieve quietly. To scatter like ash and call it peace." Her jaw tightened. "I am done calling it peace."

She pulled the oilcloth back.

The femur gleamed in the dawn light, pale as a peeled willow branch, and even from ten paces Noor could feel it — a hum in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his palms, in the place behind his sternum where the voices lived. It was louder than anything he had ever held. Louder than the widow's son. Louder than Vell in her broken antler. It did not whisper. It *waited*.

"Tonight," Ashka said, "we march for the wound in the pass. And tomorrow, Thornfell opens."

The cheer that went up should have sounded joyful. It sounded instead like hunger — three hundred throats discovering they had been starving.

Kesh found his elbow in the crowd and gripped it hard enough to bruise. "Tell me you're not going to do whatever she just asked you to do," she muttered.

"I haven't been asked yet."

"That's not comforting. That's the pause before the asking."

* * *

The asking came an hour later, in Ashka's tent, which smelled of tallow and old leather and, faintly, of the map — the great map of what-was spread across her table, weighted at the corners with river stones, its borders drawn in ink so faded it looked like a memory of a border rather than a border itself.

"I need you to carve the key," Ashka said. No preamble. She had never wasted words on him; that too had felt like respect, once.

"Ashka—"

"The ritual requires a doorkeeper's hand. Mine is enough to open the way partway — I proved that at the pass. But partway is a wound. Partway is fog and whispers and villages flickering like bad lanterns." She laid her palm flat on the map, over the mountains, over the wound Serane's people called a disaster and her people called a beginning. "You saw what half a door does. Full open, and the land knits itself whole. Our dead come home. Our gods come home. Thornfell breathes again."

"And what feeds it?" Noor asked quietly. "Vell told me what the rite costs. Bloodline power, she said. Life given freely, or life taken."

Ashka's face did something complicated and then settled into resolve, the way ice settles after a crack. "Given freely," she said. "There are three hundred people in this camp who would give anything to go home, Noor. Some of them literally. They've told me so. Old Marta. The brothers Fen. Half the wounded from the last patrol. They *asked*."

"They asked to die?"

"They asked to *matter*." For the first time her voice roughened, and beneath the roughness Noor heard the thing that had made her a war hero: not cruelty, never cruelty, but an arithmetic of sacrifice so total it had eaten everything else. "I have buried more of my own soldiers than I can carve names for. I will not bury one more death that bought nothing. This buys everything."

"It doesn't buy everything." He kept his voice gentle, because shouting at her would only harden her, and gentleness might — might — find the person underneath the general. "It buys a door into a gray country full of unfinished dead, most of whom don't want to come back. I've listened to them, Ashka. Thousands of them. They want release. They want to be *finished*. Opening the door wide doesn't bring Thornfell back. It just makes the dying louder."

Something closed behind her eyes. He watched it happen — the exact moment the general replaced the woman who mended coats.

"You sound like the Gray Cloaks," she said.

"I sound like the dead."

"The dead are confused. Forty years sealed away from their gods — of course they're confused. Vell will guide them true once the door is open." She straightened. "Carve the key, Noor. From the king's bone. Tonight, before we march."

"No."

The word fell into the tent and lay there between them.

Ashka looked at him for a long moment. Then, terribly, she smiled — a warm, rueful, understanding smile, the smile of a commander being patient with a frightened recruit.

"I know you're afraid," she said. "Your mother was afraid too."

The floor of the world tilted slightly. "What did you say?"

"She refused as well. At the end." Ashka began rolling the map, unhurried, not looking at him. "She thought the gods should stay sealed. She thought the cost was too high. Brave woman. Wrong, but brave." The map rolled shut with a soft thump. "Her faction disagreed with mine. There was a disagreement."

"There was a *murder*," Noor said. His voice came out very quiet and very steady, which surprised him, because inside he was sixteen years old again, standing in a burned doorway, holding a half-finished bird that would never sing.

"There was a war," Ashka corrected, "and everyone chose a side whether they meant to or not. You're choosing now." She stepped close, and up close her eyes were not hard at all; they were tired, and sad, and absolutely immovable. "I won't force your hand, Noor. I'm not the Inquisition. But I'll tell you what I told my officers: the door opens tomorrow, with your key or without it. Without it, the rite is cruder. Cruder means more fuel. More fuel means more of my people burn to light the way." She touched his shoulder, briefly, almost tenderly. "Every hour you refuse, I add names to the list. That's not a threat. That's just the shape of the arithmetic."

She left him alone with the map and the fading smell of tallow, and Noor sat down slowly on her cot, and put his head in his hands, and for the first time since Thornfell fell, wished sincerely that he had never heard a voice in his life.

* * *

He tried to run at dusk.

It was not a dramatic decision. It was Kesh, appearing at his elbow as the camp lit its fires, saying in a low voice, "So. We're leaving tonight, yes? Because I've packed already, and if you say no I'm going to point out that you taught me to pack before you taught me to carve, so really this is your fault."

"We're leaving tonight," he agreed.

They nearly made it. The north path out of Hollowmere was a smuggler's path, one of the roads that remembered him, and it opened for them like a hand uncurling — until it didn't. Until the stones underfoot went cold and dumb, and torchlight bloomed through the pines ahead, and Ashka's voice came out of the dark, calm as a hearth:

"The roads listen to me now too, Noor. I've been learning the words."

They came out of the trees on all sides — not soldiers exactly, exiles with hunting bows and farm implements sharpened into weapons, and among them faces he knew, faces that had shared his fire, and none of them would meet his eye except old Marta, who looked at him with an apology so naked it was worse than hatred.

Ashka walked out of the torchlight and stopped in front of him. Behind her, two of her people held Kesh by the arms, and Kesh was saying things to them in a tone that would have gotten her killed anywhere less desperate.

"I gave you the day," Ashka said. "I'm sorry. I truly am. But the rite needs a doorkeeper present — the bloodline anchors the working, Vell was clear on that — and I can't risk you deciding to walk to the Gray Cloaks tonight."

"I'd never—" He stopped. Because he would. He was going to. He was going to walk to Serane Hallic, enemy of everything Thornfell had been, and put himself in her certain cold hands, because she was the only person left whose certainty pointed somewhere other than off a cliff.

Ashka read all of that in his face, because she was not a fool, and her expression softened with genuine regret.

"Take him to the store-tent," she said. "The one with the confiscated carvings. Guard him well. Feed him. He's not a prisoner, he's..." She searched for the word and did not find a kind one, so she used a true one. "He's the key. And keys don't get to choose which locks they open."

They marched him back down through the camp, past fires where people turned away from him, past Marta's tent where Marta stood watching with her hands pressed over her mouth. Kesh fought the whole way — he could hear her, a bright furious thread of cursing woven through the dark — and then her voice cut off abruptly, and the fear that hit him then was worse than anything Ashka could have done.

"What did you do to her?" he demanded, twisting against his escorts. "Kesh! What did you—"

"She's confined to her tent," Ashka's voice said, somewhere above him, weary. "Alone, unharmed, with a guard outside who has orders to let no one harm her. I'm a monster, Noor, not an idiot. There's a difference, and history will eventually notice it."

They put him in the store-tent and tied his wrists with soft leather, considerately, the way you tie something valuable, and the last thing he saw before the flap closed was Ashka standing in the firelight with the king's femur across her arms, looking down at it the way a mourner looks at a grave.

Then the flap fell, and the dark came in, and with the dark came the voices.

* * *

The confiscated carvings hung from the tent poles in rows — dozens of them, hundreds, bones and antlers and river-ivory taken from exiles over the years, keepsakes and grave-goods and little carved wolves, each one a throat, each one a mouth. In daylight they were silent. Ashka's people had learned to keep them apart, spaced out, muzzled by distance.

But there were so many of them, and they were so close together now, and the night was long.

They began quietly, the way rain begins. A grandmother's voice from a carved spoon handle, murmuring a lullaby in Old Thornish. A boy from a button, asking over and over whether anyone had found his dog. A soldier from a knife-grip reciting the names of his squad, all of them, endlessly, a rosary of the dead. And underneath them, deeper, the hum of the king's femur somewhere across the camp, vast and patient, a bell that had not yet decided to ring.

Noor pressed his bound hands against his ears and discovered that the voices did not live in his ears. They lived in the gift, and the gift lived in him, and there was nowhere in his own skull he could go to be free of it.

*Please,* said a voice he didn't know. *Please, it's so gray here.*

*My daughter. Has anyone told my daughter.*

*I was only nineteen. I was only nineteen. I was only—*

And then, threading through the chorus, familiar as a scar:

*Noor.*

His mother's voice. From a carving somewhere in these rows — of course she was here, of course Ashka's faction had taken her work when they killed her, of course it hung in this tent like all the rest. He had not heard her voice in twenty years except in memory, and memory had worn it smooth and wrong, and this was not memory. This was her, or the shape her death had left in the world, and she was saying his name the way she used to say it when he'd had a nightmare: once, gently, and then waiting for him to come to her.

"No," he whispered into the dark. "No, no, I can't, I can't listen, if I listen—"

Because that was the thing about the gift that no one understood, that he had never told anyone, not even Kesh: listening was a door. Every voice he answered, he opened a little wider. And there were hundreds of carvings in this tent, thousands of dead behind them, and if he opened all those doors at once—

*Noor,* his mother's voice said again, closer now, or perhaps just clearer. *Listen to me. You have to listen to me. She's going to use the bone wrong, and when she does—*

The tent flap rustled. Boots. A guard checking on the key, making sure the lock hadn't rusted.

Noor lifted his head from his knees. His eyes had adjusted enough to see the rows of carvings swaying faintly on their cords, though there was no wind, turning toward him like flowers toward a sun that had finally risen after forty years of night.

Somewhere beyond the canvas walls, he could hear the camp waking — the tramp of feet, the creak of wagons, Ashka's voice raised in the old marching cadence. They were breaking camp early. They were moving for the pass.

And somewhere in the dark, small and fierce and unmistakably alive, a whistle — three notes, sharp, sharp, flat. Kesh's signal. The one she'd invented their second year on the road, meaning *trouble, but I have a plan.*

Noor closed his eyes, and for the first time in his life, deliberately, reached out and began to listen.
