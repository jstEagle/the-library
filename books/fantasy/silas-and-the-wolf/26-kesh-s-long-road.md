## 26. Kesh's Long Road

The trouble with escaping a camp of zealots, Kesh decided somewhere around the third hour of crawling through frozen bracken, was that zealots kept watch.

Not good watch — she'd seen better from drunk smugglers on festival nights — but *constant* watch, which was worse in its own way. You could time a drunk's rounds. You could not time a true believer, because a true believer didn't get bored, didn't wander off to piss behind a tent, didn't stop to argue about whose turn it was to feed the mules. A true believer just stood there in the freezing dark, staring at the pass with shining eyes, waiting for the world to end in the correct direction.

Kesh lay flat against a boulder that smelled like sheep and old lichen and thought very uncharitable thoughts about General Ashka Vey.

Below her, Hollowmere's inner camp glowed with cookfires. Too many cookfires for the number of mouths, because Ashka had ordered every fire fed high — "so they can see us from the mountain," she'd said, meaning the dead, meaning the gods, meaning whatever lived inside the wound in the pass where the fog still bled out at night like smoke from a house fire nobody had put out yet.

And in the center of it all, under guard, in a tent that wasn't a tent so much as a cage made of canvas and certainty: Noor.

Three days now. Three days since he'd refused her, since Ashka's face had gone smooth and terrible and she'd said, in the voice of someone discussing grain stores, *"He'll come around. Put him somewhere quiet."*

Quiet. As if Noor Nystrom had ever been quiet a day in his life. The man hummed when he carved. He argued with his own hands. He talked to bones like they were stubborn relatives.

Kesh pressed her cheek to the cold stone and did the arithmetic she'd learned at his elbow over four years of illegal roads: two sentries at the north gap, rotating badly; a dog line on the east slope that she'd spent two evenings feeding dried fish until the dogs now considered her a minor deity; and the west ravine, which everyone avoided because everyone believed it was haunted.

It wasn't haunted. It was just cold, steep, and full of scree that announced your arrival to the entire valley. Which, honestly, amounted to the same thing.

She checked the bundle strapped inside her coat one more time. Ashka's ledger pages — stolen from the general's own map table while the woman prayed — with the ritual numbers written in Ashka's tight, pious hand. How many exiles. How much bloodline ink. How many lives, measured out in neat columns like sacks of flour.

Proof. That was what Noor had whispered to her through the canvas wall of his cage on the second night, when she'd crept close enough to touch the rope and he'd pressed his mouth to the gap and said, barely breathing the words: *"They'll never believe me. I'm the doorkeeper, I'm the prophecy, I'm whatever story they need. But you — you're nobody important, Kesh. Nobody important can walk out of here."*

*"Charming,"* she'd whispered back.

*"The highest compliment a smuggler can give,"* he'd said, and she could hear the smile in it, cracked though it was. *"Find Serane Hallic. She'll want to hate me, but she won't want this. Not this. Take the ledger. Take my knife — the one with the wolf on the hilt. If anyone asks why you have it, tell them I gave it to you years ago and I'm sentimental about debts."*

*"You're not sentimental about anything."*

*"I'm sentimental about you. Now go before I say something embarrassing."*

He'd said something embarrassing anyway — something in Old Thornish, soft and quick, that she hadn't understood but had felt settle into her chest like a warm coal. She'd carried it up the ravine path with her, and she was carrying it still.

*Right,* she told herself. *West ravine. Then the high road. Then find the most dangerous woman in Vess and ask her nicely for help.*

Simple. Practically a holiday.

* * *

The scree held its peace for exactly as long as it took Kesh to feel confident, and then betrayed her with the enthusiasm of an old friend who talks too loudly in public.

A single stone clattered. Then, in the way of scree, it invited three hundred of its closest companions to do the same, and the whole west slope rang like a dropped pot.

Kesh froze, half-crouched, arms out, doing the statue impression that had gotten her out of trouble since she was six years old and caught stealing bread off a Vess supply cart. Below and behind her, torchlight swung toward the noise. A voice called out — one of Ashka's faithful, young-sounding, frightened-sounding.

"Who's there?"

Kesh considered her options. There were none worth considering. So she did what she always did when there were no options: she invented one.

"It's the ghost!" she called back, pitching her voice low and hollow, letting it bounce off the rocks the way Noor had taught her sound moved in the passes. "The ravine-ghost! Go back to your fires!"

Silence. Then, uncertainly: "...Is it really?"

*Oh, you sweet summer idiot,* Kesh thought. *Never change.*

"The ravine does not suffer the unbelieving!" she intoned, deepening her voice and adding a wobble, because ghosts apparently needed dramatic flair. "Begone!"

There was a scramble, a yelp, running feet. Either the sentry had fled or had gone to fetch someone less gullible, and either way Kesh intended to be several ridges away before the debate resolved itself.

She ran. Not well — the dark was total and the ground was out to get her — but she ran the way she'd been taught, weight low, steps short, reading the mountain with her boots the way Noor read it with his eyes. *The land remembers,* he always said. *Trust your feet more than your fear.* Her feet remembered a thousand smuggled miles. Her feet, at least, had never once let her down.

By the time gray light came, she was above the tree line with her lungs full of knives and the camp invisible below the shoulder of the mountain. She allowed herself ninety seconds of lying face-down in the snow, wheezing, before getting up.

"No rest for the important people," she told the wind, "and none for me either."

* * *

She traveled by day and hid by dusk, and by the second evening she had learned three things about being alone in the high mountains in late autumn.

One: it was cold in a way that stopped being weather and became a personality. The cold had opinions. The cold wanted to discuss them at length.

Two: her food math had been optimistic. She'd packed for four days. The pass would take six, if the weather held, and the weather showed no interest in holding.

Three: silence was a thing you could drown in, if you weren't careful.

That last one scared her more than the cold. Back at Hollowmere, even after everything went wrong, there had been noise — arguments, prayers, the endless murmur of the wounded wound in the pass. And before that, there had always been Noor's voice, needling and humming and narrating their crimes. Now there was nothing but wind and her own breathing, and she caught herself talking aloud just to fill it, telling the marmots about the ledger, telling the cairns about Ashka, telling one particularly patient boulder about the time Noor had fallen into a river trying to impress a customs officer.

"You'd have liked him," she informed the boulder. "Everyone likes him. It's infuriating."

On the third day she found the shrine.

It was small — a stacked-stone cairn with a niche cut into it, sheltering a carving so weathered it was nearly abstract. A figure with raised arms. Old Thornish work, forty years or older, left here by some exile who'd crossed this same pass with the same hole in their chest that Kesh carried in hers.

She should have walked past. Shrines meant pilgrims, pilgrims meant traffic, traffic meant questions.

Instead she sat down in front of it, took out the last of her dried plums, and ate half. Then, feeling ridiculous and doing it anyway, she set the other half in the niche.

"I don't know who you were," she said to the carved figure. "I don't know if you're listening, or if you're finished, or if you're one of the ones Noor says just want to be done. But if you've got any pull left — he's a good man. He's the best man I know, and I include myself in that ranking, which costs me something to admit. Don't let them use him as a key. He's not a key. He's a person who happens to be shaped like one."

The wind moved through the pass. The plum sat in the niche, going frosty at the edges.

Nothing answered, and somehow that was fine. That was almost better than fine. Because Noor had told her once — back when she'd learned his secret, back when the carved bird had spoken to her and the whole strange truth had come pouring out of him — that the dead didn't want worship. Most of them, he'd said, just wanted to be heard once, properly, and then left in peace.

"Well," Kesh said, standing, brushing snow off her knees. "Consider yourself heard."

She walked on, and if her eyes were wet, the wind took the blame, as winds reliably do.

* * *

The storm caught her on the fourth day, because storms are punctual creatures and she had been dreading exactly this appointment all week.

It came down the pass sideways, white and howling, and turned the world into a single screaming color. Kesh got low, found a hollow beneath an overhang of rock, and wedged herself into it with the practiced economy of someone who had slept in ditches, culverts, hay barns, and once, memorably, inside a hollowed-out smuggling barrel for two days while Vess soldiers played cards six feet away.

She wrapped herself around the ledger pages first. Proof stayed dry or none of this mattered. Then the knife with the wolf hilt. Then herself, last, shivering, doing sums to stay awake because sleep in cold like this was a door that didn't always open again.

*Sixty miles to the border forts. Two days past them to the garrison town where Serane Hallic kept her office, according to the smuggler gossip Noor pretended not to collect. One storm. Half a plum's worth of divine goodwill.*

The numbers were bad. Smuggler's arithmetic, Noor called it — the honest kind, where you counted what you actually had instead of what you hoped for. By smuggler's arithmetic, she was going to die on this mountain with a dead woman's name in her pocket and a message undelivered.

Then she thought about Noor's face when she'd asked him to carry word to her mother. The way he hadn't laughed. The way he'd carved that little bird and given her mother's voice back to her — and the name, too, the name of the man who'd left, the truth Kesh hadn't known she needed until it was handed to her wrapped in wood and kindness.

*Somebody delivered my message,* she thought. *So somebody's delivering mine.*

She laughed — a thin, mad, wind-torn laugh — and the storm screamed back, and she held onto the ledger and the knife and the coal of words in Old Thornish that Noor had pressed into her chest, and she stayed awake until dawn.

* * *

On the fifth day, the storm broke and the world reassembled itself, glittering and indifferent, and Kesh came down out of the high country into the borderlands of the Kingdom of Vess with frostbitten fingertips and a plan assembled entirely from desperation and audacity.

The plan was this: she could not cross the border forts as a smuggler, because she looked like a smuggler, walked like a smuggler, and had the documented history of a smuggler. But she could cross as something far more dangerous to her health.

A refugee.

It was almost funny. Almost. Four years of perfecting the art of invisibility along these roads, and now her best disguise was the truth: a ragged orphan girl stumbling down out of the mountains with frostbite and no papers, fleeing — and here the truth got to do all the work — *trouble.*

The corporal at the ford checkpoint was nineteen, bored, and chapped raw by the wind. He looked at her the way tired soldiers look at refugees: with pity he couldn't afford and suspicion he could.

"Name?"

"Kesh." True. Always start with truths; they're easier to maintain.

"From where?"

"Up north." Also true. "There was a camp. In the high valleys. Exiles." True, true, gloriously true. "There was a — an accident. A ritual. People died." Every word a coin straight from the purse. "I ran."

The corporal's boredom flickered into something sharper. "Exiles? What kind of ritual?"

"The kind that kills people," Kesh said, and let him see her shake. This required no acting whatsoever. "Please. I just want to get somewhere warm."

He waved her through with a form and a stamp and a warning not to cause trouble, and Kesh walked into Vess proper carrying forged nothing, smuggled nothing, and the single most incriminating document in the northern provinces folded against her heart.

Noor would have been proud. Noor would also have lectured her for an hour about unnecessary risks, and then bought her dinner, and then lectured her again.

*Hold on,* she thought at him across sixty miles of mountain. *I'm coming. Don't you dare be reasonable about this. Don't you dare decide to cooperate.*

Because that was the fear that rode with her now, heavier than the storm had been: that Noor, in that canvas cage, surrounded by people telling him he was holy, might finally start believing them. He'd spent his whole life hiding what he was. Ashka had built him a temple to it instead. And even good men, Kesh knew, could drown in flattery if it was the first water they'd ever been offered.

* * *

The garrison town of Marrow's Ford smelled like woodsmoke, horse, and official paperwork, and Kesh hated it instantly and completely.

Getting to see Inquisitor Serane Hallic proved harder than crossing the border. The garrison office had queues. The queues had forms. The forms had queues. Kesh spent half a day discovering that Vess bureaucracy was a maze designed by someone who genuinely loved mazes, and the other half discovering that saying "I have information about the Gray Cloaks' old case files" got you escorted to a side room with remarkable speed.

The side room was cold and bare. She waited in it for two hours, rehearsing.

When the door finally opened, Serane Hallic came in alone — no guards, no clerks — and closed it behind her, and Kesh understood immediately why Noor described her the way he did. She wasn't tall or imposing. She was precise. Everything about her was precise: her posture, her gray traveling cloak, the way her eyes went to Kesh's frostbitten fingers, then her face, then — briefly, professionally — the shape of the bundle under her coat.

"You're the girl from the checkpoint report," Serane said. "Refugee from an exile camp. Ritual casualties." She sat down across the table, unhurried. "Reports travel fast when they mention rituals. Talk."

"My name is Kesh. I'm a bone-carver's apprentice." She watched for the reaction and got it — a stillness, small but real. Good. The name meant something to this woman. "My master is Noor Nystrom."

Something moved behind Serane's eyes — calculation, recognition, maybe regret. "I know the name."

"He's being held by General Ashka Vey at a camp in the high valleys. Hollowmere. She means to force open the old border with a mass rite." Kesh pulled the ledger pages out and laid them on the table between them, smoothing each sheet flat with fingers that only shook a little. "Her own handwriting. Numbers, names, the sequence of the ritual. She's already tried it once without him — you've seen the wound in the pass, or you've heard of it. This time she's going to use people. Hundreds of them. They think they're going home. They're fuel."

Serane picked up the top page. Read it. Her face did not change, which was its own kind of eloquence — Kesh had grown up around card players and knew a person keeping their hand flat when the cards were enormous.

"These are accurate?" Serane asked quietly.

"I stole them off her table myself. I fed her dogs for two days to learn the watch rotations. I crawled through a haunted ravine." Kesh leaned forward. "I lied to a ghost-believer, crossed your border on the truth, and walked through a storm that tried to kill me twice. I did not come all this way to be doubted by the second-most-dangerous woman I know."

Something almost like a smile touched Serane's mouth. "Second-most?"

"I'm saving the top spot for someone currently in a cage."

Serane set the page down. For a long moment she looked at Kesh — really looked, the way you look at a lock to understand it — and then she exhaled slowly, and some of the precision went out of her shoulders, replaced by something wearier and more human.

"When I cornered your master in the market," she said, "he refused me. Told me the dead deserved better than my locks. I gave him terms that would have kept his people safe, and he chose the exiles over the amnesty." Her jaw tightened. "I have spent forty years making sure what happened in Thornfell never happens again. And now a war hero I once admired intends to tear the seal open with living souls, and the only man who can stop her is sitting in her prison because he wouldn't help *me.*"

"So help him instead," Kesh said. "You want the same thing. Both of you. Neither of you wants people burned up as kindling. You just—" she groped for it, Noor-style, hands moving, "—you both keep insisting there's only one right way to hold a door shut, and there isn't. He told me once: a good lock opens for the right reasons. Maybe that's the deal. Maybe that's the third way you're both too proud to say out loud."

Serane was silent for a long moment. Outside, garrison boots rang on frozen mud. When she spoke again, her voice was lower.

"If I march inquisitors into those mountains alongside smugglers and exiles, I am ending my career and quite possibly my life. If I'm wrong about what's behind that door, I am ending considerably more than that." She gathered the ledger pages, squared their edges with a tap, and looked up. "Why should I trust you? You're a smuggler's apprentice. Lying is your trade."

Kesh thought about a plum left in a stone niche. About a voice in a carved bird. About a man in a canvas cage whispering old words through a gap in the wall, trusting a nobody-important orphan girl with the fate of everything.

"Because I don't lie to people who matter," she said. "And because if you don't come, I'll go alone, and then you'll have to explain to your superiors how a fifteen-year-old beat the entire Kingdom of Vess to the biggest disaster in forty years."

"How old are you?"

"Fifteen," Kesh said, "and a half. The half matters."

Serane Hallic stood, tucked the ledger inside her cloak, and for the first time since entering, smiled fully — a tired, sharp, unexpectedly warm expression, like sun through a crack in a fortress wall.

"Get something hot to eat," she said. "We leave within the hour. And apprentice—"

"Yes?"

"What did Nystrom whisper to you, when you left him? You've been carrying it like a coal since you walked in. I'd like to know what we're fighting for."

Kesh blinked. She hadn't known she'd been carrying it visibly. She reached into her memory for the old words, rolled them over her tongue wrong and didn't care.

"He said it means *come back to me safe, that's an order,*" she said. "But I think it actually means something closer to *you're mine to look after, and I'm yours.*" She shrugged, suddenly fierce-eyed and grinning. "Family stuff. You wouldn't understand. You people file things."

Serane laughed — one short, startled bark of it — and opened the door onto the cold bright morning, and the long road back into the mountains began.
