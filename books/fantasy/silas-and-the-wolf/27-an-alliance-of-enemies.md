## 27. An Alliance of Enemies

Kesh had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road down from Hollowmere — usually while her boots were frozen and the proof of Ashka's plan was a lump of stolen papers against her ribs like a second heartbeat. In the imaginings, she always knew exactly what to say. She was eloquent. She was devastating. Serane Hallic wept, or saluted, or at minimum looked properly ashamed.

In reality, she stood in the doorway of an inquisition waystation outside Vess's border town of Corren Moll, dripping snowmelt onto a floor so clean it squeaked, holding a dead chicken.

The chicken had seemed like a good idea four miles back. A gift. Smugglers knew you never arrived anywhere empty-handed; it marked you as either friend or fool, and between those two, friend got fed. But the duty officer at the gate had looked at the chicken, then at Kesh — small, frostbitten, seventeen, and radiating the specific guilt of someone who has recently climbed through a window — and now two gray-cloaked soldiers were escorting her down a corridor while the chicken clucked with what Kesh felt was unhelpful serenity.

"You can't bring livestock in here," said the taller soldier.

"It's a diplomatic offering," Kesh said.

"It's a hen."

"Diplomacy is mostly hens, if you do it right."

The shorter soldier made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Gray Cloaks weren't supposed to laugh. Kesh filed that away as useful intelligence: the inquisition was still made of people, and people could be worked with.

They stopped before a plain door. The tall soldier knocked once and said, "Inquisitor. There's a girl with a bird."

"A what?"

"Never mind," Kesh said quickly. "I'll leave the hen outside."

She did, propping it against the corridor wall with the dignity of a state gift, and went in alone.

* * *

Serane Hallic was not what Kesh expected, though she couldn't have said what she'd expected instead. Thinner, for one. There were ink stains on her fingers and shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose a coin in, and she was standing at a table covered in maps, holding a cup of tea she clearly hadn't drunk in an hour. She looked less like the terror of Thornfell's survivors and more like a clerk who had been awake for nine days and knew exactly how many of them remained until something terrible happened.

"You're Nystrom's apprentice," Serane said. It wasn't a question. "Kesh. You went missing from Hollowmere three days ago."

"I went on an errand."

"To me." Serane set down the cup. "You crossed forty miles of winter mountains alone to reach an inquisition waystation. Either you're the bravest person I've met this year or the most desperate, and in my experience those are usually the same person wearing different coats."

Kesh liked her immediately and hated herself for it.

"I brought proof," she said, and pulled the folded papers from inside her coat. They were damp and creased and one corner had been chewed by something during the second night, but they were legible. Ashka's handwriting was beautiful, which Kesh had always thought was suspicious in itself. Nobody with handwriting that elegant should be trusted near large decisions.

Serane read. Kesh watched her read, and watched the color change in her face by degrees, like weather coming over water. Ashka's plan was laid out in General Vey's precise, lovely script: the seizure of the wounded pass at Cael Duren. The mass rite. The conscription of exile lives — six hundred souls from the outer camps, volunteers who believed they were pilgrims, fuel for a door that would be forced open whether it wanted to open or not.

When Serane finished, she sat down slowly, the way old people sit when their knees have opinions.

"She means to burn six hundred people," Serane said.

"They think they're going home," Kesh said. "That's the worst part. She's told them the door opens into Thornfell. Green fields. Their grandmother's kitchen. And maybe some of them will feel that, right up until the light takes them apart." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated that too, so she pushed through it. "I've seen what her rituals do. Half-done, with one bone and no bloodline. This one won't be half-done. It'll work. That's what I came to tell you. It will actually work, and everyone in that pass will die believing they're walking home."

Silence. Somewhere down the corridor, the hen remarked on the situation.

"Why bring this to me?" Serane asked quietly. "Your people hate mine. Your master has every reason to let my order burn with the rest of it."

"Because Noor is in that pass," Kesh said. "Because he refused her, and she took him anyway, and he's sitting in a cell right now listening to — " She stopped. She almost said *the dead*, and then thought about it, and decided that if she was going to ask this woman for an army she might as well spend all her coins at once. "Listening to thousands of confiscated carvings scream. He hears the dead, Inquisitor. He always has. That's why your notices call him a doorkeeper. And that's why Ashka needs him alive, and why she'll use him until there's nothing left, and why nobody else in the world except you has enough soldiers to get there in time."

Serane stared at her. "You just confessed your master's secret to the head of the order that destroyed his country."

"No," Kesh said. "I spent it. There's a difference. Secrets are things you keep. This was coin, and I bought something with it, and what I bought is you, sitting there, knowing that if you do nothing, six hundred people dissolve and a hole gets torn in the world that makes everything your order ever sealed look like a scraped knee." She leaned forward. "You want to seal the gods away forever? Fine. Argue it with me later, I'll even buy the drinks. But Ashka isn't opening a door. She's blowing out a wall, and what comes through a wall doesn't knock."

* * *

It took three days.

Three days of Serane sending riders, arguing with a regional commander named Ostrec who had a voice like a landslide and the imagination of one, and pacing her maps room at night while Kesh sat in the corner pretending to sleep and actually memorizing where everything was, because old habits.

"He says the pass is outside his jurisdiction," Serane reported on the second evening, dropping into a chair across from Kesh. "He says the exiles are a Thornish internal matter. He says — " she rubbed her eyes — "he says a great many things, all of which mean *not my problem until it's a problem with my name on it*."

"So we don't wait for him."

"With what army? I have thirty-one soldiers, most of them young, and a warrant signed by a ministry that would disown me by noon if I marched without orders."

Kesh turned the hen feather she'd been fidgeting with — a souvenir; the hen had been released into the waystation yard and had immediately begun running the place — between her fingers.

"When I was eight," she said, "a caravan master told me something I've never forgotten. He said, *the law is a road, girl, and roads are only good for as long as they go where you're going.*" She looked up. "Your law goes to jurisdiction. My road goes to Cael Duren. Six hundred people are going to come apart at the seams of the world in four days, and you're going to sit here because a man with a landslide voice used the word *internal*?" She shook her head. "Noor talks about you sometimes. Did you know that? Not kindly, mostly. But he says you're the only enemy he's ever had who he thinks might actually listen. He says certainty like yours is just grief that found a job. Well. Here's a job for your grief, Inquisitor. It pays in lives."

Serane was quiet for a long time. The lamp guttered. Down the hall, faintly, absurdly, the hen sang the song of its people.

"My family died in the god-plague," Serane said at last, not looking at Kesh. "Corren Moll, forty years ago. I was four. I remember my mother singing to me through a door, and then I remember a man in gray carrying me out, and then nothing for a year. Everything I've built — the order, the sealing, all of it — is a wall around that door." Her jaw tightened. "And you're telling me the wall is the wrong shape."

"I'm telling you walls are for houses," Kesh said gently. "Not for people. People need doors. Good ones. With locks that open both ways."

Something moved behind Serane's eyes — the crack Kesh had been aiming for since the first night, the place where certainty met the thing certainty was built to protect.

"On the third day of the god-plague," Serane said, "my mother stopped singing. Do you know why? Because the god the priests had called on ate memories, and hers went first, and she forgot she had a daughter. She walked past the door I was behind eleven times." She stood. "I am not doing this for you, girl. I'm doing it because if there is any chance — any — that a better lock exists, a lock with mercy in it, then I have to see it tried. If I'm wrong, I'll answer for it. But I will not answer for six hundred dissolving pilgrims because a commander liked his paperwork dry."

Kesh grinned so hard her cracked lips hurt. "So that's a yes."

"That's a *maybe* shaped like a yes. Get your coat. We march at dawn."

"What about Ostrec?"

"Ostrec," Serane said, gathering papers with sudden, terrifying efficiency, "will receive a report that I conducted a routine inspection of mountain shrines. By the time anyone reads it, either I'll be a hero or a heretic, and frankly I've been both before and the meals are similar."

* * *

They marched at dawn, thirty-one gray cloaks and one smuggler who had somehow, without discussion, become the column's guide.

It should have been stranger than it was. Kesh kept waiting for the wrongness of it to hit her — walking at the front of an inquisition patrol, calling turns to men whose predecessors had burned her master's country — but the strangeness never quite arrived, and after a day she understood why. On the mountain roads, rank dissolved. The gray cloaks were just people with cold feet. The tall soldier from the gate — his name was Bren — carried the hen, which had refused to be left behind and had been formally designated the expedition's mascot by unanimous vote Kesh hadn't been consulted on. A corporal named Ilse taught Kesh a marching song with lyrics that would have gotten her arrested in any town in Vess, and Kesh taught Ilse three ways to spot a smuggler's cache, and by the second evening they were trading insults with the easy rhythm of people who had decided to survive the same week together.

"You know what I keep thinking," Bren said on the third night, hunkered by the fire with the hen tucked in his cloak like a feathered conspiracy. "My whole career, exiles were the problem. Notices, patrols, reports. And now we're marching to save six hundred of them, and I can't remember why the paperwork ever mattered."

"The paperwork mattered," Serane said from across the fire. She said it without heat, the way you correct a child's arithmetic. "Order matters. It's just not the top of the list. It's the bottom. It's what holds everything up *after* you've decided what's worth holding." She glanced at Kesh. "Your master taught you that?"

"Noor doesn't teach," Kesh said. "He carves and complains and feeds you whether you deserve it or not, and somehow you learn things anyway. It's infuriating. You'll like him."

"I arrested him twice."

"He mentions it constantly. He's very proud of the second time — says he talked you in circles."

"He did not talk me in circles. I chose to release him for tactical reasons."

"That's what talking someone in circles looks like from inside, Inquisitor."

Bren laughed outright this time, and even Serane's mouth twitched, and Kesh sat back in the snow and felt something she hadn't felt since Hollowmere burned: hope with weight to it. Not the flammable kind Ashka dealt in. The slow kind. The kind you could build with.

* * *

They reached the shoulder below Cael Duren on the fourth morning, and the mountain told them they were late.

Fog poured from the pass like smoke from a wound — which, Kesh thought grimly, was exactly what it was. It bled down the slopes in ropes and sheets, and inside it, faintly, voices whispered in Old Thornish, words repeating and unraveling, prayers said by no living mouth. The horses wouldn't go higher. Even the hen went silent, pressing itself flat under Bren's arm.

Above, at the crown of the pass, the fog pulsed with light. Slow. Rhythmic. Like breathing. Or like a heart.

Serane stood beside Kesh at the tree line, and for once the inquisitor looked her age and then some.

"How long?" she asked.

Kesh counted the pulse of the light against her own heartbeat, the way Noor had taught her to count a caravan's pace. "It's already started. The rite. Those aren't soldiers' fires — that glow is people." Her throat closed. She made herself say it. "Six hundred people, giving themselves away piece by piece, and they're happy, Serane. That's the horror of it. Up there, right now, they feel like they're finally going home."

Serane drew a long breath. When she spoke again, her voice was steady, and it was the steadiest thing on the whole mountain.

"Then we go take it home from her." She turned to her thirty-one. "Gray Cloaks! We are past jurisdiction, past orders, past everything but the job in front of us. There are people in that light who cannot walk out on their own feet. So we carry them. Every one we can. Move!"

They moved. Thirty-one gray cloaks and one smuggler and one hen plunged up into the bleeding fog, into the whispering dark, toward the pulsing heart of the pass —

— and the fog parted ahead of them, deliberate as a curtain, and Kesh's blood went cold, because the mountain had noticed them.

Something in the light above turned, slow and vast, to look.
