## 28. The Ritual Begins

They took his boots.

That was the first thing Noor noticed when they dragged him up the switchback trail to the wound in the mountain — not the fog bleeding from the pass like smoke from a house fire, not the hundreds of exiles gathered in the gray light with their faces turned upward like sunflowers, but the absence of his boots. Someone had decided a prisoner's laces were a weapon. His feet were wrapped in strips of blanket, and the cold of the stone came up through them like a message.

Which meant the bone was gone too. The little finger-length of carved antler he'd kept tucked in the heel of his left boot for three years — a wolf's tooth, no bigger than a thumbnail, that sang so quietly even he sometimes forgot it — gone, along with everything else they'd confiscated in the cell at Hollowmere.

He should have felt despair. Instead he felt a strange, cold clarity, the kind that comes after you've been afraid for so long that fear wears a groove and stops being an event and becomes a road.

*You're getting slow, old man,* Kesh would have said. *Hiding your one good trick in footwear.*

He hoped she was far away. He hoped she had run the moment Ashka's people locked him in that cell, because Kesh Vey — no, Kesh *not-Vey*, she'd been very clear about that, whatever distant thread of doorkeeper blood ran in her — Kesh was many things, but loyal past the point of sense was one of them, and loyalty past the point of sense got people dissolved into light on mountainsides.

The wound opened before him.

It was worse than he remembered from Vell's descriptions, worse than the reports the exiles whispered. A gash in the world perhaps forty paces across, where Ashka's half-finished ritual had torn the mountain's skin away and never let it heal. Through it he could see — not Thornfell, not exactly, but something like Thornfell seen through water: rooftops that flickered between standing and ruined, a bell tower that existed and didn't, streets where figures walked who were only sometimes there. Fog poured out of it in slow ropes and pooled around the assembled crowd's ankles, and the whispers came with it, thousands of whispers, all saying variations of the same word.

*Home. Home. Home.*

"Doorkeeper." Ashka Vey stood at the edge of the wound on a platform of stacked flagstones, her gray general's coat immaculate against all that ruin, her voice carrying the way it always did — not shouted, but *placed*, each word set down like a stone in a wall. "You'll want to see this clearly. I've had them bring you forward for that reason."

Two guards held his arms. He didn't struggle. Struggling was for people with plans.

"You can still stop this," Noor said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. Three days in a cell full of screaming bones will do that to a throat. "Ashka. What you tore open here — it isn't a door. It's a wound. You don't open a wound wider and call it healing."

"I open what our ancestors closed," she said. "I finish what the prophecy started." She gestured, and the crowd stirred, and Noor saw what he had been refusing to see since they marched him up the trail.

Rings. Hundreds of exiles arranged in concentric rings around the wound, kneeling on stones painted with Old Thornish characters — real ones, mostly; Ashka had scholars, or at least people who could copy. And at intervals through the rings, on low altars of unworked granite, lay bones. Not carvings. Raw bones, old ones, dug up and brought here — femurs and ribs and skulls, the dead of Thornfell excavated from wherever forty years had hidden them, laid out like kindling.

"No," Noor said quietly.

"They volunteered," Ashka said.

"The *bones* didn't."

"The bones are doors, doorkeeper. That is the whole of your family's secret, isn't it? Every bone remembers the person it carried. Every bone is a small door." Her eyes found his, and there was such certainty in them, such terrible clean certainty, that for one absurd moment Noor missed Serane Hallic, whose certainty at least came wrapped in ledgers and procedure and the occasional human doubt. "And we have brought enough doors. Tonight we open them all at once, and Thornfell comes back, and everyone who died in the fall walks out of the fog and into the morning."

Noor looked at the rings of kneeling exiles. They weren't bound. They weren't drugged, as far as he could tell. They knelt because they wanted to kneel, because Ashka had spent years feeding them the one meal starvation leaves you desperate for: *you can go home.* An old man near the front had folded his hands over his chest like a man lying in state, already practicing. A young woman beside him kept touching the ground in front of her, patting it, the way you'd pat a horse to calm it, or yourself.

Some of them, Noor realized, thought they would die tonight and considered that a fair price. Some of them thought they wouldn't. Ashka had been careful, he suspected, never to fully clarify which.

"And if it works?" he said. "If you tear it wide — what comes out? Have you asked? Have you listened?" He leaned into the guards' grip, enough to make them tighten their hold, enough to make Ashka's gaze sharpen. "Because I listen, General. It's the whole of my gift, as you keep reminding me. And what's on the other side of that wound isn't an army waiting to come home. It's—" He stopped. He almost said it. *It's the unfinished. It's the ones who couldn't rest, including the one your ritual half-woke, the goddess who ate memories, the thing that—*

"It's Thornfell," Ashka said, with finality, and turned to her altar.

* * *

The rite began with a horn.

Not a war horn — a shepherd's horn, thin and wavering, played by someone at the outermost ring, and the sound of it went through Noor like a hook through cloth. Because he knew that melody. Everyone born in Thornfell knew that melody; it was the dusk-call, the tune shepherds sounded to bring the flocks down off the high pasture, and hearing it now, played by some exile who had learned it from a parent who had learned it from a parent, over a wound in the world instead of a green valley — it was cruelty disguised as reverence, and Noor didn't think Ashka even saw the difference.

Then the chanting began, and the chanting was Old Thornish, and the words were wrong.

Not mispronounced — wrong. Ashka's scholars had reconstructed a liturgy from fragments, and fragments lie. The grammar was sound but the heart of it was missing, the way a copied letter carries the information but none of the warmth of the hand that wrote it. Noor heard the dead gods' names spoken in those rings and heard them flinch. He felt it in his teeth, in the blank silence where his gift used to be accessible — no, not silent, he corrected himself. Muffled. Like voices behind a wall. The gift wasn't gone. It was *pressed down*, smothered under whatever Ashka's people had fed him in the cell, some bitter tea that sat in him still like wet ash.

But even muffled, he could feel the bones beginning to answer.

It started at the nearest altar, thirty paces to his left. A rib — just a rib, yellow-brown with age — began to hum. Then the skull beside it. Then the altar beyond, and the one beyond that, a spreading ring of resonance, each bone picking up the chant and giving it back changed, thickened, layered with the voice of whoever had once lived inside it. The fog churned. The wound brightened at its edges, a sickly dawn-colored glow, and through it the ghost-Thornfell flickered faster, rooftops stuttering between states, and Noor saw figures gathering on the far side now. Watching. Pressing against the inside of the wound like faces against a window.

*Home,* they whispered. *Home. Home.*

One of the kneeling exiles gasped.

Noor saw it happen — saw the light take her. It rose out of the painted stones beneath her knees, a soft gold-white, and climbed her like water climbing a wick. She didn't scream. That was the horror of it. She smiled. She spread her arms slightly, the way a person does stepping into a hot spring, and said something in Old Thornish that Noor's muffled gift couldn't quite catch but his heart translated anyway: *I'm coming, Mama.*

And then she wasn't a woman anymore. She was a column of light shaped briefly like a woman, and then she was light only, and then she poured — there was no other word for it — she *poured* toward the wound, streaming across the ground in a ribbon of gold, and passed through, and the wound swallowed her, and the whispering on the other side rose to something that might have been joy or might have been hunger, and Noor could not tell the difference and neither, he suspected, could Ashka.

"She's through!" someone cried from the rings. "She made it through! Blessed be — she's *home!*"

A ripple of ecstatic weeping moved through the exiles like wind through wheat. More of them pressed their palms flat to the painted stones, eager now, hungry for it. On the second altar, another bone began to burn with inner light.

"That is not going home," Noor said. He hadn't meant to speak aloud. "Ashka! Whatever you're feeding them to — look at what's left!"

Nothing was left. Where the young woman had knelt there was a scorch-mark on the stone in the shape of a kneeling figure, delicate as frost. No body. No ashes. Not even her clothes, really — a drift of fabric, a boot, a hairpin. As if she had been not transported but *spent*, burned as fuel, and whatever crossed through that wound wearing her shape was made of her, but was not her.

Ashka stood at her own altar with her arms raised, and she was radiant, and she did not look.

* * *

Noor had been in bad situations before. He had crawled through a Vess patrol line with fever, guided a caravan blind through a rockslide, sat with a widow while her son's murder poured out of a keepsake bone. He had a connoisseur's appreciation for bad situations.

This was the worst, and the worst part was how much of it was beautiful. The light was beautiful. The singing bones were beautiful. The exiles' faces, lifted and shining, certain they were being saved — beautiful, in the way that fires are beautiful when they are eating a house you love. Ashka had built a machine out of hope and grief, and it worked, and its working was indistinguishable from a massacre, and nobody in the rings would ever believe otherwise because they had all watched their friend go home smiling.

Think, Noor told himself. Think the way you think on a smuggler's road. What do you have?

He had: two guards, both young, both exiles, both currently watching the light with tears on their faces and grips gone loose with awe. He had: feet wrapped in blankets, which meant someone had assumed the boots were the danger and stopped looking. He had: a body that ached, a gift that was muffled but present, and —

Wait.

Blankets. Wrapped feet. And underneath the wrapping, against the sole of his right foot, something he had forgotten about because he had hidden it so well even from himself, three weeks ago, in the cell, when they took his boots: the splinter.

Not a carving. A splinter — a shard of the antler where Vell had first spoken to him, broken off during that terrible conversation at the wound's edge, small as a fishhook barb. He'd felt it dig into his heel that first night and had, in a moment of instinct rather than strategy, worked it free and pushed it deep into the wrapping of his bandage, telling himself it was nothing, telling himself it was sentiment. Carvers kept splinters of their material the way sailors kept knots. It meant nothing.

Except antler that a goddess has spoken through does not mean nothing. Antler that a goddess has spoken through *remembers*, and right now, pressed against the sole of his foot, warm as a live coal, it was humming.

Noor shifted his weight, casually, agonizingly, until his toes could work at the wrapping. The guards didn't notice. Nobody was looking at a prisoner; everybody was looking at heaven. He worked the strip loose, worked the splinter free, and got it pinched between his first two toes, which — thank every indifferent god — was a skill he'd developed in a misspent youth picking up dropped coins without bending down, to Kesh's eternal disgust and delight.

The splinter sang against his skin. One note. Small and clear as a bird in winter.

And through the muffle, through the ash-tea and the chanting and the roar of the wound, something answered.

*There you are,* said Vell, or the memory of Vell, or the piece of Vell that lived in antler the way people lived in bones. *Little carver. They are spending my children.*

*I know,* Noor thought, and it was strange, thinking at a splinter, but the strangeness slid off him easily now; he had long ago stopped ranking his problems. *Can you cut through what they gave me?*

*A splinter cannot unmake a draught. But a splinter can be loud.* And there was something in the voice that Noor had never heard from Vell before, or maybe he had and hadn't known what to call it: ferocity. The old ferocity, the pre-fall ferocity, the ferocity of a goddess who had done terrible things and knew it. *Put me in a bone, little carver. Any bone. Give me a mouth.*

His eyes went to the nearest altar. Thirty paces. Two guards. A skull sitting on granite, old and patient, its jaw wired shut with copper wire by some exile's reverent hands.

Thirty paces was a long way for a man in foot-wrappings.

But the guards had drifted. That was the thing about awe — it loosened hands. The younger one had taken two steps toward the rings, drawn by the light, and the older one was crying openly and gripping his spear like a walking stick. And between Noor and the altar lay the crowd's outer fringe, kneeling exiles lost in ecstasy, eyes shut, palms to the earth.

Noor did the most undignified thing available to him. He dropped his full weight backward, drove his heel into the older guard's instep, and ran.

Or — lurched, staggered, flailed, ran in the way that a man runs when his feet are wrapped in blankets on loose scree. It should not have worked. It worked because no one had ever planned for it. The guard went down swearing; the younger one spun back too late; and Noor was among the kneeling exiles, weaving between them like a man late for a ferry, and they barely saw him, because why would they look at anything but the light?

A hand caught his collar. Ashka's lieutenant, Halvard, the one with the scarred jaw — Noor twisted, dropped, the oldest trick in the smuggler's book, and the grip tore his shirt instead of his neck, and he rolled, came up, and threw himself the last five paces at the altar.

His wrapped feet skidded on granite. He crashed into it hip-first, hard enough to see stars, and his hand closed on the skull.

Cold. Heavy. And under the coldness, faint as a heartbeat under wool, a voice. *Who's there? Is it time? Is it finally—*

"Hush," Noor whispered, and pressed the splinter into the gap between the skull's wired teeth, into the nasal channel, anywhere it would sit against bone. "Hold still. This will either work or be extremely embarrassing."

He had no knife. He had no tools. He had ten fingers and twenty-nine years of carving, and so he did the thing his mother had forbidden and his gift demanded and his whole life had bent toward: he spoke to the bone directly, in Old Thornish, the true tongue, the living one, the one the land itself had taught him on roads that reshaped themselves at his words.

"*Wake,*" he said. "*Open. Lend her your mouth.*"

The skull's eye sockets filled with light.

It was not gentle. Nothing tonight was gentle. The light blew outward through the bone like breath through a reed, and the sound that came out was Vell — all of Vell, the fragment in the splinter amplified by a skull's hollow chambers, a goddess's voice given a mortal instrument — and it cut through the chanting, through the horn, through Ashka's rising liturgy, the way a blade cuts through rope.

"*STOP.*"

Every exile in the rings flinched. Several fell over. The chant collapsed into scattered coughs and silence, and into that silence the skull spoke on, in a voice like wind through a burned forest:

"*Children of Thornfell. Beloved, foolish, grieving children. Look at what remains of the one who went first. Look at the stone. That is not arrival. That is consumption. The thing on the other side of this wound is not your home. Your home is gone. What waits there is what was sealed away — and I was sealed with it, and I am telling you: RUN.*"

"Lies!" Ashka's voice cracked across the mountainside like a whip. She had come down from her platform, and her face — Noor would remember this — her face was not angry. It was *frightened*, and the fright made her magnificent and terrible. "The gods speak truth and the enemy speaks lies! That is a prisoner's trick, a—"

"Ashka." Noor stayed crouched at the altar, one hand on the burning skull, blood running from his scraped palm. His voice shook, and he let it. "Ask her name. Ask the goddess you're trying to free what her name was. Ask her what she did before the sealing. There's a library under the ice that will tell you. I've read it." He swallowed. "I can tell you. But you have to stop the rite, because every minute it runs, it eats another one of your people, and they go *smiling*, Ashka, and that's the worst thing I have ever seen."

For one heartbeat — one — something moved behind Ashka's eyes. Doubt, maybe. Or its cousin, grief.

Then the wound roared.

It was not a sound so much as a pressure, a vast exhale from the other side, and the fog surged, and the light in the rings reignited all at once — a dozen exiles lifting off their knees in columns of gold, streaming toward the wound, and the whispering swelled to a chorus, and through it, cutting through everything, Noor heard the skull-Vell say, urgently, terribly:

"*Little carver — the door is opening whether you will it or not. When it opens, LISTEN. Listen to what the dead actually want. It is not what any of you think.*"

The first of the shining exiles reached the wound's edge and poured through, and the mountain screamed, and somewhere below on the switchback trail, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a whistle — three notes, sharp, insolent, deliberately annoying.

Three notes Noor knew better than the dusk-call. Three notes that meant, in the private language of two people who had survived too much together: *I'm here, you idiot, and I brought help.*

Kesh.

Noor gripped the skull, and the wound tore wide, and the dead came through.
