## 29. What the Dead Actually Want

The light took people the way water takes ink.

Noor stood at the edge of Ashka's rite and watched it happen — exiles stepping forward with their arms out and their faces tilted up, dissolving into threads of gold that wound upward into the wound in the mountain. They were smiling. That was the worst part. They looked like travelers finally seeing home after a long road, and they came apart as they smiled.

"Stop," he said, to no one who could hear him. His wrists still ached from the ropes; the guard he'd knocked senseless lay behind him in the scree, breathing. The bone from his boot was warm in his fist — a small thing, a knuckle of antler he'd carved in a cell while the dead screamed through every other piece in the room. He had carved this one silent on purpose. Blank. A door with nothing behind it yet.

Now it hummed against his palm like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

He should run. Every sensible instinct said run — down the pass, through the fog-bleeding wound, back to Kesh and Serane and the column of very unlikely allies marching somewhere behind him at the speed of institutions. Kesh would tell him running was the smart play. Kesh would be right.

But the light was taking people, and Noor Nystrom had spent his whole life listening to the dead one whisper at a time, and he realized — standing there with his heart slamming and his hands shaking — that he had never once asked them what they wanted. Not really. People told him things. Griefs, confessions, last words for widows. He carried messages like a ferryman carries passengers: politely, professionally, without ever asking where the river went.

Ashka wanted to bring them home. Serane wanted to keep them locked away. And the dead themselves —

The dead themselves had never been consulted.

"No," Noor said aloud, to himself this time, and stepped into the light.

* * *

It was not like drowning. Drowning, he imagined, had the decency to be cold.

This was warm. Warmth everywhere, pressing in, and under the warmth a sound like ten thousand conversations held just below hearing — the sound of a market heard from three streets away, the sound of a country talking in its sleep. Thornfell's dead. All of them. Not the few voices that leaked through his carvings over the years, but the whole drowned congregation of them, forty years of unfinished lives, and they were all speaking at once.

He felt his knees go. Felt the antler-bone crack against stone as he caught himself. Somewhere far above, gold threads swayed like kelp.

*Listen,* said something that was not quite a voice. *You came all this way. Listen.*

"I'm listening," Noor whispered. "That's the entire problem."

And because he was a carver, because his whole life had been the slow patient work of putting his hands on hard things until they gave up their shape, he did the only thing he knew how to do. He stopped bracing. He opened — not his ears, which were useless here, but whatever door lived behind his sternum that his mother's blood had built — and he let the dead in.

They came like a tide.

* * *

The first voice belonged to a woman who introduced herself, with tremendous dignity, as Marta Osten, wheelwright, of Lower Fenwick, and she was furious.

"You'd think," she said, "that after forty years of standing around being dead, somebody would ask us. Forty years! The priests sealed us out of our own country, the inquisition sealed us out of everything else, and now this general woman comes along with her big ritual and her golden light, and does she ask? She does not. She just starts pouring us through a door like we're soup."

Noor blinked in the gold. "I — I'm sorry?"

"And you're as bad," Marta said. "All these years, carving your little bones, carrying little messages. 'Tell my wife I loved her.' 'Tell my son where I hid the deed.' Nobody ever says, 'Marta, how are you finding death? Is it everything you hoped?'"

"I didn't know I could ask."

"Well." A pause. The fury wobbled, and underneath it Noor heard something older and tireder. "Neither did we, at first. You get busy being angry. It passes the time."

* * *

After Marta came the others, and they came in their thousands, and Noor learned quickly that the dead of Thornfell were exactly like the living of Thornfell: opinionated, stubborn, prone to arguing about grain prices, and deeply divided on the question of what should happen next.

He had assumed — everyone had assumed — that they wanted to come back. That was the whole shape of the story Ashka told, the story the exiles sang, the story carved into half the shrines in Hollowmere: the gods cast out, the dead unquiet, the border closed against its own children, and someday, someday, the door would open and Thornfell would be Thornfell again.

But wanting to go home, Noor discovered, and wanting to *return* were two different animals entirely, and nobody had thought to check which one the dead were chasing.

A farmer named Aldis wanted to see the barley come up again — just once, just to stand in a field of it — but when Noor asked if he wanted a body back, a life back, chores and taxes and bad knees, Aldis was quiet a long moment and then said, "Gods, no. I did my years. I just want to know it kept growing without me."

A soldier of the old border guard wanted to apologize to a man he'd killed by accident in the last panicked days of the fall. Just the apology. Then he wanted, in his words, "to stop."

A child — Noor flinched away from that one, but the child found him anyway, a girl of maybe seven who had died in the crossing years ago — the girl didn't want anything except someone to tell that she'd found a dog on the other side, a good dog, and the dog had stayed with her the whole time, and could someone please tell her mother that she hadn't been alone?

Noor pressed his hands over his face inside the light. "I'll tell her," he managed. "I promise. I'll find her."

"She won't remember me right," the girl said anxiously. "People get you wrong when you're dead. Tell her I laughed a lot. That's the part she'd want."

* * *

By the hundredth voice, a pattern had emerged so clear that Noor felt foolish for missing it, the way you feel foolish for not seeing a shape in the stars until someone points.

Most of them didn't want resurrection. Most of them didn't even want return — not return as Ashka meant it, not bodies and borders and banners. What they wanted, almost universally, in almost the same words, worn smooth like river stones from saying it to each other for forty years in the dark:

To be finished.

To have the loose ends tied. The apologies made, the names remembered, the last messages delivered. To be *known*, completely, one final time — and then to be allowed to set it all down. To cross over, or fade, or whatever waited past the edge of even this gray country. They weren't unquiet because they were hungry. They were unquiet because they were *mid-sentence*, and had been mid-sentence for forty years, and no one had listened long enough to hear them reach the end.

"Oh," Noor said, to the gold-lit dark. "Oh, you idiots."

Somewhere above him, Ashka's ritual thundered on, pouring the willing and the desperate and the merely confused through a door none of them had been asked about, spending exile lives as fuel to force open a passage the dead had never requested. And somewhere beyond the light, an inquisitor marched with an army to seal that same door shut forever, certain that what lay behind it was madness and hunger.

Both of them, Noor thought, both armies, both certainties, fighting over a door — and the people on the other side mostly just wanted someone to hold it open gently while they walked *through* it and out the far side, finished, free, done.

Not opened. Not sealed. *Completed.*

"It was never a wall," he whispered. "It was a ferry landing. And we've been fighting over whether to burn the dock or rebuild the town on it."

*Now you've got it,* said Marta Osten, wheelwright, sounding insufferably pleased. *Only took forty years and one smuggler.*

* * *

There were others, though. He couldn't pretend otherwise, and the archive under the ice had already taught him better than to pretend.

Some of the dead did want to come back. A handful — a thin, hungry handful — wanted it with a ferocity that made Noor's teeth ache. He felt them at the edges of the crowd the way you feel a cold draft in a warm room: the ones who had refused to finish, who had spent four decades sharpening their grief into a point. They clustered near the wound in the mountain, drinking the golden light, growing denser and more solid and more *wrong*. One of them wore the shape of a priest Noor's mother might have known, and when it noticed Noor noticing, it smiled with too many years of practice.

"Doorkeeper," it said. "Open it wide. We've waited so long to be *warm* again."

Noor's mouth went dry. But beside him — beside all of them — Marta Osten stepped forward, wheelwright's shoulders squared, and the crowd of the finished dead moved with her, hundreds strong, placing themselves between the hungry ones and the light.

"Not with our door open, you don't," Marta said. "We buried you twice. We'll manage a third."

The hungry thing hissed — actually hissed, like a kettle — and withdrew into the fog, and Noor understood two things at once, with terrible clarity.

First: Serane Hallic was not entirely wrong. There were things behind the door that should never walk through it. The sealing had saved people. Some of the gods deserved it, and some of the dead deserved worse than sealing.

Second: that was not a reason to lock the door on all of them. That was a reason to stand at it. A doorkeeper's job had never been to bar the way or throw it wide. It was to *know the difference* — and to stand there, in the doorway, and do the knowing.

His mother had died doing the knowing. He was beginning to suspect she'd known exactly what she was dying for.

* * *

The warmth around him thinned. The ritual above was reaching some new pitch — he could feel it in the antler-bone, a rising whine like a sawblade finding its note — and the gray country shuddered, and far off across the dead fields he saw something that stopped his breath.

A figure walking toward him through the gold-flecked mist. Tall. Unhurried. Wearing a shape that flickered between a woman and a stag and a mountain and back again, and trailing behind her a wake of whispers that bent toward her like grass toward wind.

Vell. The goddess who had forgotten her name, then remembered it, then confessed what she'd done with it. Coming toward him across the land of the unfinished dead, and the hungry shapes at the edges of the crowd fell silent as she passed, the way dogs go quiet before a storm.

Noor's hand closed around the blank antler-bone until it hurt.

He thought of the archive under the ice. Of what Vell had fed on, before the first inquisition took her memory — worshipers' memories, whole lifetimes, swallowed. He thought of the villages flickering between worlds, paying for her kind's war. He thought of Kesh, waiting somewhere on the living side of all this, probably inventing new insults for him in his absence.

And he thought of the girl with the dog, and Marta with her forty years of complaints, and the soldier with his apology still undelivered, all of them standing between the hungry and the light because a dead wheelwright had decided, on the spot, that this smuggler's errand was worth guarding.

The goddess stopped an arm's length away. Up close she smelled like snowmelt and old incense, and her eyes were the exact gray of the country she was trapped in.

"You listened," Vell said. Her voice was quiet, and cracked, and human, which was somehow worse than if it had thundered. "All the way through. None of my priests ever managed it. None of my kings. They prayed *at* me. You listened."

"Somebody had to," Noor said. "Everyone else was too busy deciding for you."

Something moved across her face — too fast to name, too old to be young grief. Behind her, the wound in the world pulsed, and Ashka's ritual climbed another note, and the gold threads swayed like a field about to break.

"There is a price on listening," Vell said softly. "There always was. I am the proof." She held out her hand — a woman's hand, a stag's hoof, a mountain's shadow, all at once. "Come, doorkeeper. Walk with me to the door's heart, and I will show you what your mother saw before she died. And then you can decide what I deserve — because it seems someone finally has to."

Noor looked at the offered hand. He looked back, once, toward the living world — toward the pass, the fog, the distant sound of an army of enemies marching together to save him.

Then he took the goddess's hand, and the gray country closed over them both like water.
