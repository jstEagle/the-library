## 30. Confronting Vell

The light of the rite did not behave like light.

It moved like water finding a crack in a wall — patient, searching, everywhere at once — and where it touched the exiles kneeling on the mountainside, they came apart gently, like sugar stirred into tea. They were smiling. That was the worst part. Ashka had promised them home, and they believed her, and belief looked beautiful right up until it finished eating you.

Noor stood at the edge of it with his boots in the fog and his heart hammering somewhere up around his teeth, and he thought: *I have walked roads that remember me. I can walk this one.*

Beside him, Kesh had both hands fisted in his sleeve. "Tell me you have a plan," she said. "Lie if you have to."

"I have half a plan."

"Which half?"

"The part where I talk to a goddess," Noor said. "The other half is where she listens."

Kesh considered this. "That's not a plan, that's a prayer."

"Then we're in the right country for it."

* * *

He found Vell at the center of the wound.

She stood where the mountain bled its fog into the sky, and she was not the fragmented whisper he had met through the antler anymore. The ritual was feeding her — Ashka's great engine of stolen lives poured worship straight into her veins, and divinity filled her out like water filling a dry riverbed. She was tall now. Whole. Her antler crown had grown back, branching white against the gray light, and her eyes held the particular terrible calm of someone who has remembered everything.

Including, Noor suspected, the things she had spent forty years begging to forget.

"You came," Vell said. Her voice was the voice from the antler no longer — it was layered now, a chorus wearing one throat. "I felt you crossing the edge. Little doorkeeper. You always did come when called, even when you hated the calling."

"I'm not here because you called." Noor kept walking, one slow step at a time, the way you'd approach a spooked horse or a burning house. "I'm here because I finally read your file."

Something flickered across her face. Even gods, it turned out, could flinch.

"The archive under the ice," he said. "Kesh nearly froze off two fingers getting it. Worth it, though. Very thorough, your old priests. They wrote down everything. The harvests of memory. The village of Sennet Hollow, where three hundred people forgot their own children's names while you drank them down like spring wine. The man who knelt at your shrine every day for thirty years and died unable to remember why he'd come." He stopped an arm's length from her. Close enough to see that her skin, up close, was faintly translucent, lit from within like carved bone held before a candle. "That was you."

"Yes." No denial. No deflection. Just the word, dropped between them like a stone into a well, falling a long way before it landed.

"They sealed you out for it," Noor said. "The first inquisition. Serane's predecessors. And the world called it salvation, and mostly it was, and my mother died for the bloodline anyway, and here we all are, forty years later, standing in a hole in a mountain while good people dissolve into light because a general couldn't tell a door from a wound."

"Ashka is doing what must be—"

"Ashka is doing what *you told her* must be done," Noor said. "Through dreams. Through whispers in the femur. Don't look surprised — I've been listening to bones my whole life. I know what it sounds like when something wants out badly enough to lie." He gestured at the dissolving exiles behind him. "You didn't want the door opened so Thornfell could live again. You wanted it opened so *you* could live again. So the worship would flow and the hunger would wake up hungry."

Vell was silent for a long moment. Around them the fog churned, and somewhere in it, faintly, he could hear singing — the exiles' last thoughts rising like steam.

"And what would you do, little carver?" she asked at last, and there was something new in the chorus-voice now, something smaller. "Would you seal me again? Put me back behind the wall and let another forty years pass, and another? Do you know what it is to be a god with no one to be a god *for*? To be a name with no mouths to say it?" Her hands opened, pleading. "I was starving, Noor Nystrom. Worship is food. Memory is food. What would any creature do, starving?"

"Eat its own children?" Noor said quietly. "Some do. We hang them for it."

"We," she repeated, and laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through into something else. "You speak to me of hanging. Of law. I am older than law. I am older than the idea that anything can be *owed*."

"Then it's lucky," Noor said, "that I'm not here to collect a debt."

* * *

Kesh had, against every instruction he'd given her, followed him to the edge. She stood now at the boundary of the light with her arms crossed and her chin up, the way she did when she was scared and refusing to admit it, and Noor loved her so fiercely in that moment that it frightened him more than the goddess did.

"She's going to do something stupid," Kesh announced to Vell, jerking a thumb at Noor. "I know that look. He gets it before he does something stupid. I just want it on record."

"Noted," said Vell, and the absurdity of a goddess humoring a smuggler's apprentice almost made Noor laugh, which was probably the point. Kesh had always known when he needed laughing.

He turned back to Vell. "Here's what I learned," he said, "in the last few days, listening to the dead instead of the living. Everyone assumed they wanted what we wanted for them. Ashka assumes they want resurrection. Serane assumes they want silence. I assumed—" he stopped, swallowed, started again. "I assumed they wanted *me*. The doorkeeper. The purpose. The heir to something."

"And don't they?"

"No." He said it simply, and watched the word land. "They want to be finished. That's all. Not brought back, not restored, not worshipped. Finished. Held long enough to say goodbye, and then let go." He took a breath. "And I think — I think that's true for you too. Underneath everything. Underneath the hunger and the crown and the forty years of scratching at the wall. I think some tired part of you has been begging to be done."

"Do not," Vell said, very softly, "presume to know my heart."

"Somebody has to," Noor said. "You clearly don't."

The fog shuddered. For a heartbeat the whole wound in the mountain seemed to hold its breath, and Noor understood that he was balancing on a knife-edge of divine temper, and that one wrong word would unmake him as thoroughly as the rite was unmaking the exiles.

But he had spent his whole life talking to the dead, and the dead, whatever else they were, were honest. And he had heard ten thousand voices ask for the same thing, over and over, in ten thousand different words.

*Let us go. Let us rest. Let us be finished.*

"So here is my offer," he said. "Not freedom-with-power. Not the door thrown wide so the worship pours in and the hunger wakes up fed and we do all this again in another forty years, worse. I'm offering you what I'll offer every soul on this mountain." He held out his hand, palm up, the way he offered a carving to a grieving widow. "Forgiveness. Passage. An ending."

Vell stared at his hand. "You cannot forgive me," she said. "You weren't the one I hurt."

"No," Noor agreed. "But I'm the only one here who can open the door, and I'm telling you: it opens for the forgiven. All of us. Every soul who walks through walks through clean. That's the price of passage. Not punishment — I'm not sealing you in a box, I'm not making you kneel, I'm not even asking you to be sorry, though I suspect you are." His voice gentled. "I'm asking you to put it down. All of it. The divinity, the hunger, the crown, the name. Walk through as one of the dead. As *a* dead. One soul among thousands, going home to rest."

"You're asking me to stop being myself."

"I'm asking you to start," Noor said. "Being yourself. Not the thing the worshipers built. Not the hunger wearing a woman's shape. *Yourself.* Whoever that was, before the first altar went up. I don't think even you remember her. But I think she's in there. I think she's been in there the whole time, screaming, same as the ones in my workshop at night." He stepped closer. "Vell. You begged me through an antler to remember your name. I'm giving you better than that. I'm giving you the chance to *retire* it."

"It will hurt," Vell said. It wasn't a refusal. It was a question wearing a warning's clothes.

"Yes," Noor said. "Most true things do. Ask any widow I've ever carved for."

Behind him, Kesh made a small sound — half snort, half sob — and covered it with a cough.

* * *

Vell looked at her hands.

Noor watched her do it — watched the goddess examine her own divinity the way a miser examines coin, turning it over, weighing it, counting what surrender would actually cost. The light beneath her skin pulsed. The antler crown branched and shimmered. Somewhere in the fog, Ashka's chanting rose and fell, steady as a loom, weaving souls into power.

"If I refuse," Vell said slowly, "you will try to stop the rite. You and the child and the inquisitor marching up the pass with her soldiers. And you will fail, little doorkeeper, because I am being fed by a thousand faithful and I have not been strong in forty years, and I am *so tired of being weak*." Her eyes lifted to his. "And if I accept?"

"Then you walk through with the others," Noor said. "And Ashka's rite loses its god, and becomes what it actually is: a murder. And murders can be stopped."

"And what do I find, on the other side?"

Noor thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty. "I don't know," he said. "Nobody comes back to tell us. But I've listened to the dead my whole life, and in all those years, in all those voices, I never once heard regret about crossing over. Only about staying too long." He smiled, small and crooked. "For what it's worth — I carve their faces sometimes. The ones who've gone on. There's peace in them. Even the murderers. Even the ones nobody mourned. Especially, maybe, the ones nobody mourned."

"I am a murderer," Vell said.

"You're also the one who guided me to the archive," Noor said. "Did you think I didn't notice? You could have lied to me forever. Instead you sent me to the ice to find the truth that damns you. A hungry god doesn't do that. A tired one does."

Vell made a sound that no mortal throat could have made — grief and relief tangled together, a chord rather than a note. The fog around them dimmed. The chanting on the mountainside faltered, just slightly, as though the rite had lost a thread.

"When I was young," she said, and the chorus-voice thinned, and thinned, until only one voice remained, a woman's voice, ordinary and worn, "before the altars — I was a river spirit. Small things. Clean water. Children paddling in the shallows. That was all I ever wanted to be." She looked down at her blazing, terrible, magnificent hands. "They gave me crowns. They gave me names. They gave me *hunger*, without meaning to, because love with nothing to give back curdles into appetite. And by the time I understood what I'd become, I was too proud to say stop." Her shoulders shook. "Too proud. Forty years sealed outside my own country, and the lesson I finally learn is the one I could have learned in the shallows: *say stop.*"

"Say it now," Noor said.

"I don't know how."

"That's all right." He closed the last distance between them and took her blazing hand in his scarred, bone-dusted one. "Neither does anyone else, the first time. That's what forgiveness is for. It's the word we use when 'stop' isn't enough anymore."

* * *

What happened next, Noor would spend years failing to describe properly.

The divinity left her the way winter leaves a valley — not all at once but in layers, each one revealing something smaller and more human underneath. The antler crown crumbled into pale dust that the fog carried away like ash. The light beneath her skin guttered, steadied, dimmed to the plain warmth of a living body. She grew shorter. Or perhaps she had always been this height, and the godhead had been the exaggeration. Her hair went from silver to gray to brown. Her eyes, which had held galaxies, settled into holding only tears.

She weighed something. That was the detail that undid him. Gods, he realized, weigh nothing; they are made of everyone else's weight. But the woman swaying in his grip was heavy the way people are heavy, heavy with a body and a history and a grief, and when her knees buckled he caught her, and she was — astonishingly, ordinarily — warm.

"I'm sorry," she wept into his shoulder. Ordinary words. A mortal mouth. "Sennet Hollow. The man at the shrine. All of it. I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry."

"I know," Noor said, which was not forgiveness itself but the road leading to it, and he supposed that was what doors were for. "I know. Come on. This way. With the others."

He turned her toward the gray country opening in the heart of the wound — the door he and Kesh would hold, the threshold where the unfinished waited in their thousands. Vell looked at it for a long time. Then she looked back at the mountainside, at the exiles still dissolving in Ashka's light, and her ruined, human face hardened into something Noor recognized from every smuggler's campfire he'd ever sat at:

Resolve.

"She believes she's saving them," Vell said. "Ashka. She believes it with her whole heart, and her whole heart was always the problem." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, a gesture so mundane it made Noor's chest ache. "She won't stop when the god goes out of the machine, doorkeeper. She'll push harder. She'll pour herself into the rite. I know her kind. I *was* her kind."

"Then we'd better hurry," Noor said.

Kesh appeared at his elbow, breathless, holding the king's femur wrapped in sacking like the world's ugliest bundle. "The chanting's changing," she reported. "Faster. And Ashka's stopped shouting orders, which either means it's working or it's gone wrong, and honestly with her track record I can't tell which is worse." She squinted at Vell — at the small, gray, tear-streaked woman where a goddess had been standing — and her eyebrows climbed. "Huh. You look better. Less murdery."

"Thank you," Vell said gravely.

"Don't mention it. Literally ever." Kesh shoved the femur into Noor's arms. "Now come on. If we're stopping a massacre, I want it on record that I suggested it first."

Noor hefted the bone — the key that should never have been a key, the king's leg pressed into service one last time — and looked once more at the door waiting in the wound. On its far side, faint as starlight through fog, the dead were gathering. Waiting. Among them, he somehow knew, a small river spirit with clean water and children in the shallows, patient as weather.

"Hold it open," he told Kesh. "However it takes. However long."

"With what?" she demanded, but she was already reaching for his hand, bloodline answering bloodline, and the bone began to sing.

Behind them, up the mountainside, the chanting stopped altogether.

And Ashka Vey began to scream.
