## 31. Ashka Unmade

The mountain was screaming.

Noor felt it through his boots before he saw it — a vibration in the stone like a struck bell that would not stop ringing. The wound Ashka had torn in the pass had widened into a gash of white light and gray fog, and at its edge stood the General of Hollowmere, no longer entirely a woman.

She floated a hand's width above the rock. Her boots had stopped touching the ground somewhere in the last hour, Noor realized, and nobody had thought to mention it because there had been so many other things to mention — the exiles kneeling in rows around the wound, their bodies already going translucent at the edges, dissolving upward into threads of light they believed were roads home. Ashka's eyes had gone the color of old ice, and where her hair had been, something like smoke moved in slow spirals.

"Noor Nystrom." Her voice came from everywhere now, from the fog, from the bones in his pack, from the stone under his knees. "You came after all. I knew you would. The doorkeeper always comes to his door."

"I came to stop you," Noor said. His voice cracked on the second word, which he supposed was honest if not impressive.

"You came too late." She spread her arms, and the light bent toward her like wheat toward wind. "Look at them. Look how happy they are."

He looked. He wished he hadn't. The exiles in the front rows were smiling — genuinely smiling, faces tilted up into the glow, and one old man was singing something in Old Thornish, a harvest song, half-remembered, while his hands came apart into motes. They weren't screaming. That was the worst of it. Ashka's rite had wrapped them in such perfect certainty that they were walking into death the way children walk into a festival.

"They're not going home," Noor said quietly. "Ashka. Listen to me. I've listened to the dead my whole life, and I'm telling you — this isn't what they want. This isn't even what your dead want. You're feeding them to a door that doesn't lead anywhere yet."

"Then open it." Her head turned with a smoothness no neck should allow. "That was always the arrangement, carver. I provide the power. You provide the way. Stop fighting the shape of your own life."

Behind him, small and fierce and breathing hard from the climb, Kesh said, "He said no. He says it very well. I've heard him practice."

* * *

They had come up the smuggler's road in nine hours what should have taken two days, because the road remembered them.

Noor still didn't fully understand it. Somewhere past the third switchback, exhausted, Kesh limping on a twisted ankle, he had started speaking Old Thornish without deciding to — the words Vell had given him, the words from the archive under the ice — and the path had simply *cooperated*. Landslides unslid. A chasm offered up a bridge of living root that hadn't existed since before the fall. The country was waking, and it knew its doorkeeper, and it carried them like a current carries a leaf.

Now, standing at the wound's edge with the light trying to pull his marrow out through his teeth, Noor understood the shape of the trap. Ashka had built a rite out of stolen lives, but a rite is only a sentence, and sentences can be unwritten — by someone who knows the grammar.

By two someones.

"Kesh." He didn't look away from Ashka. "Your ankle."

"Is fine."

"It's the size of a plum."

"It's a *brave* plum." But she came to stand beside him, and when he held out his hand, she took it. Her fingers were cold and steady. "Tell me what you need."

"Bloodline answering bloodline," Noor said. "That's what the archive said. Two carvers of the line, working as one. I don't know exactly what that means. My mother never got far enough to teach me."

"So we'll invent it," Kesh said. "We're good at that. We invented an entire smuggling route out of a goat track and a lie about cheese tariffs."

Despite everything — the dissolving dead, the floating general, the mountain bleeding light — Noor laughed. It came out ragged and surprised, and he felt Ashka flinch at the sound of it, which told him something important: joy was not part of her rite. Joy was a language she'd forgotten.

* * *

He knelt and opened his pack.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs, was the bone he had hidden from Ashka's confiscators weeks ago, back when he was her honored guest and prisoner both: a length of antler, pale as morning milk. The same antler where Vell had first spoken to him. He had carved nothing into it. It was blank, waiting, humming faintly like a struck tuning fork laid on a table.

"Ashka," he called. "I'll do it."

The light stilled. Even the fog seemed to lean in.

"I'll carve the key," Noor said. "Right here, right now, with your rite behind me. Open your circle and let me approach."

Kesh's grip tightened on his arm. *What are you doing,* her fingers said.

*Buying us three steps closer,* his answered, or he hoped it did. Communication by hand-squeeze was imprecise. They'd work on it, if there was an afterward.

Ashka descended until her feet nearly touched stone. The smoke of her hair curled. "You yield?"

"I cooperate," Noor said. "There's a difference, and you're about to learn it."

She gestured, and the ring of kneeling exiles parted. Noor walked into the wound's edge carrying a blank antler and an apprentice with a brave plum for an ankle, and every step made his skin feel less like skin and more like a window left open in winter.

* * *

Here is what Noor knew that Ashka did not: a door has two sides, and a key has two functions. It opens. And it locks.

Ashka wanted an opening. She had poured a thousand lives into prying the seam apart, and the seam was indeed apart — gaping, raw, howling. What she had failed to grasp, in her certainty, was that an opening without definition is just a wound. She had built a mouth and forgotten to build anything behind it.

Noor sat down at the wound's edge, cross-legged, the way he sat at his stall in the market, the way he had sat as a boy at his mother's knee. He set the antler across his thighs. He took out his knife.

And he began to speak Old Thornish, low and conversational, the tone of a man chatting over a fence, while the knife began to move.

"This is the part," he said to Kesh, without pausing the carving, "where you cut too."

"With what? I have a belt knife and strong opinions."

"Opinions will do. Put your hand on mine. Not guiding — just *there*. The bloodline does the rest. Apparently."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan. Welcome to my entire life."

Her palm settled over the back of his hand, warm despite the cold pouring off the wound. And something happened that Noor had no words for and would spend years failing to describe afterward: it was as if a second heartbeat started underneath his own, younger, quicker, stubborn as a mule, and the knife suddenly knew things his hands alone did not. Lines appeared in the antler that he had never planned — spirals within spirals, a wolf running through a field of doors, and at the center, small and precise, the shape of two hands clasped.

Kesh gasped. "I can *see* it. Noor, I can see what it's supposed to be—"

"Don't talk, you'll jostle the miracle."

* * *

Ashka understood perhaps four seconds before it was too late, which for a woman who had once read a battlefield in a glance was a long time to be slow.

"You're *locking* it—" The light around her went from gold to a furious white. "Doorkeeper! You dare! I am the sword of Thornfell, I am the last wall, I am—"

"You're a woman who lost everyone," Noor said, still carving, tears freezing on his cheeks from the cold pouring out of the wound, "and decided grief was a strategy. I know. I almost did the same thing. It's the family disease."

The rite turned on her then.

This was the part Noor had gambled everything on and could not have explained to save his life: the power Ashka had gathered belonged, in truth, to the dead. She had borrowed it with promises of homecoming. When the key-bone began defining the door — when the wound began, slowly, agonizingly, to acquire edges, purpose, *shape* — the borrowed power remembered its creditors. The threads of light streaming up from the dissolving exiles shuddered, halted, and reversed.

The old man singing the harvest song solidified back into flesh, mid-verse, and looked down at his own restored hands with an expression Noor would carry to his grave: bewildered, disappointed, and then — as the true whisper of the unfinished dead reached him through the half-made door — understanding. He sat down heavily on the stone and wept, and beside him others woke the same way, coughing, whole, alive, cheated of a beautiful lie.

All of them turned to look at Ashka.

She screamed. It was not entirely a human sound anymore. The smoke of her tore loose from her scalp and the light poured backward out of her like water out of a broken jug, and she should have collapsed, emptied, finished —

But she had been fused too long. Some of it wouldn't leave.

* * *

What remained of General Ashka Vey rose ten feet above the pass, and she was magnificent and horrible, a silhouette of a woman filled with doorway. Through the place where her heart had been, Noor could see stars that were not the night's stars. Her voice, when it came, was layered — hers, and beneath it a chorus of every prayer ever shouted into the wound.

"THEN UNMAKE ME," she said. "IF YOU CAN. YOU HAVE ONE KEY AND HALF A BLOODLINE BETWEEN YOU. FINISH IT OR BE FINISHED."

"She's offering us a choice," Kesh whispered. "That's new. Usually she just lectures."

"Kesh."

"Yes."

"The lock needs both of us. All the way. Both hands, both hearts, whatever the archive meant by that. Are you—"

"Noor Nystrom." She turned and looked at him, this sharp-tongued orphan he'd fished out of a border town six years ago because she'd tried to pickpocket him and gotten offended when caught. Wind whipped her hair across her face. Her ankle was the size of a grapefruit. She was, he thought, the single most reliable thing in a world gone sideways. "I've been ready since the bird talked to me. Since my mother's name. Since you let me carry the message. Just — one thing first."

"Anything."

"If this kills us, I want it noted for posterity that I was right about the cheese tariff lie. It would have worked."

"Noted. Engraved on your memorial. Now *carve*."

* * *

They carved together, and the mountain helped.

Noor guided the deep lines — the grammar, the old tongue, the patient architecture his mother had died protecting. Kesh drove the wild lines, the ones no school would teach, all instinct and fury and the particular genius of someone who had survived by reading people and now read bone the same way. Where her knife touched, the antler sang. Where his touched, it *listened*. Bloodline answering bloodline, and between them, without either planning it, the design closed like two halves of a sentence meeting:

*A door is not a wall with a hole in it. A door is a promise that leaving and arriving can be the same act.*

The key-bone blazed white.

Ashka came down on them like a landslide wearing a general's face.

Noor had one heartbeat to register the descent — the vast shadow, the chorus-scream, the cold — and in that heartbeat he did the only thing a carver ever does with a finished piece. He lifted it up.

The key met the doorway-that-was-Ashka the way a word meets silence.

Light detonated outward, silent, total, gentle as snowfall. Noor felt himself lifted off the stone, felt Kesh's hand ripped from his, felt the world become white noise and white fire — and inside the fire, impossibly, he heard Ashka's voice alone again, stripped of the chorus, small and stunned and terribly young:

"Oh," she said. "Oh, it *hurts* to be a person again."

And then the light set them down, all of them, gently, the way a mother sets down a child who has fallen asleep in her arms, and the wound in the mountain was no longer a wound.

It was a door.

It stood free of the rockface, tall as a chapel arch, made of nothing anyone could name — gray as fog, silver as frost, quiet as the space between heartbeats. Through it was not Thornfell. Through it was a country of soft gray light where shapes waited, patient beyond patience, thousands upon thousands of unfinished dead, turning now toward the opening like flowers toward a sun they had stopped believing in.

At Noor's feet lay the antler-key, split cleanly in two halves, spent.

Beside it lay Ashka Vey — breathing, human, hollowed, her eyes ordinary brown and wide with a horror that had nothing to do with defeat and everything to do with clarity. She looked at the door. She looked at the exiles. She looked at her own hands.

"What have I done," she whispered. "What have I — oh, gods, what have I *done*—"

Kesh landed against Noor's side, wheezing, singed, alive. "Tell me that worked," she managed. "Tell me that's the door and we're done and I never have to fight a floating woman again."

Noor couldn't answer. Because the door was opening from the other side, and something was coming through it — something vast and sorrowful and familiar, a presence he had heard once through a mad fragment of carved antler, begging him to remember her name.

Vell stepped through her own doorway into the world of the living, and every dead thing behind her leaned forward to watch.
