## 34. The Price of the Carving

Noor woke to silence.

Not the silence of sleep — he had not known that in thirty years. Not even the silence of exhaustion, which had its own texture, a woolly hush like snow falling on wool. This was something else. This was a silence with walls around it, a silence shaped like an absence, and for three full breaths he lay in his cot at Hollowmere and could not understand why the world felt so enormous.

Then he understood, and he sat up so fast his ribs screamed.

He reached — it was not a reaching of hands but of attention, the way a man reaches for a tooth that has always been there — toward the small pile of carvings on the shelf above his cot. The sparrow. The knuckle-bone horse Kesh had made when she was nine and terrible at it. The antler, the antler where Vell had first spoken, wrapped now in linen because even after everything he could not quite bring himself to look at it directly.

Nothing.

No murmur. No half-heard sentence trailing off the edge of meaning. No dead man's complaint about the cold, no widow's son reciting the last thing he ever saw, no goddess humming fragments of a name through the grain of the bone. The shelf held objects. Wood and bone and antler, carved by a man's hands, and nothing more.

"No," Noor said aloud, to the empty room, in the reasonable tone of a man negotiating with weather. "No, I didn't agree to this part."

But he had. Somewhere in the gray country, holding the door open while ten thousand unfinished souls walked through it one by one, he had known — the way you know things in dreams, the way you know the price of bread without being told — that the door took its toll from the hinge. And he had been the hinge.

He got up anyway. He dressed anyway. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake. Outside, Hollowmere was waking in the ordinary way: smoke from cook-fires, someone's goat complaining about something, two of Ashka's former soldiers arguing over a card game with the passion of men who had very recently almost died and were determined to make up for lost pettiness. All of it arrived at his ears plainly, flatly, the way sound arrives for everyone else. Just noise. Just the world.

It should have been a relief. It *was* a relief. He had prayed for this once — kneeling in his mother's workshop at eleven years old, hands bleeding from a carving that would not stop whispering, praying to gods he did not yet know had been sealed away, *please, please, let me be deaf like other people.* He had prayed it again at fifteen, at twenty-two, at thirty-one, each time with more desperation and less hope. Every prayer unanswered. Every night the dead murmuring through his tools like wind through a keyhole.

And now the prayer had finally been answered, decades late, by a door instead of a god, and Noor stood in the middle of the camp with the morning sun on his face and discovered that grief does not check whether your wish has come true before it arrives.

* * *

Kesh found him at the cook-fire, staring into a bowl of porridge as though it might contain instructions.

"You look terrible," she said, sitting down across from him. She looked worse herself — hollow-eyed, singed at the edges, her left arm bandaged from the elbow down where Ashka's rite had burned her when she'd torn him free of the carving-circle. But she was upright, and she was eating, and she had stolen an extra honey-cake from somewhere, which Noor chose to take as a good sign. "You also look different. Did you wash?"

"I don't know how to say this," Noor said.

"Say it badly. You usually do."

He set down the spoon. "I can't hear them anymore."

Kesh stopped chewing.

"The dead. The bones. All of it." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if the answer might be written there. "It's gone. Spent. Whatever I was — whatever my family was — the door took it out of me when it opened. I'm just—" He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'm just a man who makes pretty shapes out of bone now."

Kesh put down the honey-cake. That, more than anything, told him how seriously she was taking it. In all the years he had known her, Kesh had never voluntarily set down food.

"Is that bad?" she asked carefully. "Noor. Is that — I mean." She was doing the thing she did when she was frightened, which was getting louder and quieter at the same time. "You spent your whole life drowning in voices. You told me. You told me you used to sleep with wax in your ears until your mother caught you and said the gift wasn't a punishment. You cried when the bird spoke to me. Happy crying. You said—" She faltered. "You said it was the first time anyone had ever asked you for something beautiful instead of something useful."

"I remember what I said."

"So which is it? Are you grieving or are you free?"

Noor opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the porridge, which offered no guidance whatsoever.

"Both," he said finally. "That's the horrible part. Both at once, in the same breath. Like losing a tooth that ached for thirty years. You're glad it's gone and you keep poking the hole with your tongue."

Kesh considered this with the gravity she reserved for smuggling arithmetic and moral questions, which in her mind were largely the same discipline.

"Well," she said. "At least now when you talk to yourself, people will stop assuming you're busy."

Despite everything — despite the ache, despite the hole his tongue kept finding — Noor laughed. It startled him, the laugh, coming up out of him unbidden, and Kesh grinned like she'd won a bet.

"There he is," she said. "The famous doorkeeper. Last of his line. Deaf as a post and twice as stubborn."

* * *

The trouble came at midday, when the widow Maren found him.

She had come back to Hollowmere with the exiles who survived the rite, carrying her son's keepsake bone — the first piece Noor had ever carved for her, the piece that had sung his son's murder into his ear and started all of this, though neither of them had known it then. She pressed it into his hands now with both of hers, and her eyes were wet, and Noor knew before she spoke.

"Say goodbye to him properly," she whispered. "One last time. Please. Before it's gone forever."

"Maren—"

"They say the mountain is quiet now. They say the fog is gone and the whispers have stopped and the wound is closed. They say—" Her voice broke. "They say you spent yourself doing it. So please. While there's still time. One word. Even one."

Noor held the little carved bird — a lark, he remembered, because her son had loved larks — and reached for it the way he had reached every hour of every day since he was four years old.

Nothing. Bone against skin. Warmth from his own hand and nothing else. A dead boy's name on a living woman's lips and no voice anywhere between them to carry it.

"I can't," he said, and it was the hardest sentence he had ever spoken, harder than any he had spoken to gods or inquisitors or zealots. "Maren, I'm sorry. It's gone. There's nothing left to spend."

She stared at him. And here was the cruelty of it: she did not understand. How could she? To her, the gift had been a well — deep, mysterious, but a well. Wells refill. Everyone knew wells refilled.

"But you'll get it back," she said. "Won't you? After you rest. After you're strong again."

"No." He said it gently. He said it the way Serane had once delivered verdicts, with that terrible clean certainty, except his certainty was of loss. "What I did at the door — it doesn't grow back. My mother's gift never grew back either, after she opened the way for the priests. I thought she was just tired. I thought she'd recover." His throat tightened. "She knew. That last year. She knew she was spending herself, and she did it anyway, and she never told me because she didn't want me to be afraid of the work."

Maren sat down slowly on the bench beside him. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Around them the camp went on with its business — hammering, arguing, a child shrieking joyfully about something involving mud — all of it oblivious, all of it blessedly, ordinarily silent.

"He crossed over," Maren said at last. "My Tam. At the door. You told me. You said he was one of the ones who chose to go."

"He was. He went smiling. He waited for the others — there was a girl, he wouldn't cross until she did —"

"Alys. Yes. He always liked her." Maren laughed wetly and wiped her eyes with her sleeve. "Then he's finished. Truly finished. Resting." She looked at the little lark in Noor's hands, and then, deliberately, she closed his fingers around it. "Keep it. Not because it sings. Because you made it, and because it was the beginning. Everything that happened — the door, the crossing, all those souls going home — it started with this ugly little bird."

"It's not ugly."

"It's extremely ugly, Noor. Your early work was dreadful." She patted his cheek, the way mothers do, the way only they can get away with. "Grieve it. Then get up. The living need carvers too. We just want the shapes. Turns out the shapes were enough all along."

She walked away, and Noor sat holding the lark, and found that his eyes were burning, and could not have said for the life of him whether it was mourning or gratitude. Probably both. Probably, he thought, it would always be both, and maybe that was not a wound. Maybe that was just what it cost to have been a door.

* * *

He tried, that evening, out of pure stubbornness, to carve.

The workshop Ashka had given him — given, taken, given back, the history of that room alone could fill a chapter — still smelled of shavings and linseed oil. His tools hung where they had always hung. On the bench lay the antler, unwrapped now; he had decided that morning that hiding from it was cowardice, and cowardice was for people with fewer scars.

He picked up a scrap of birch and a knife and began, mechanically, the first strokes of a wolf. Kesh had been dropping hints about wanting a wolf figurine for approximately six years, hints about as subtle as a rockslide, and if his hands were going to learn their trade all over again — plain trade, honest trade, the kind his father had practiced before the bloodline had revealed itself — then it might as well be for her.

The knife moved. The wood curled away in pale ribbons. And the silence—

The silence was the strange part. Always before, carving had been like working in a crowded market. Every stroke of the blade drew murmurs up out of the material: the memory of the tree, sometimes, faint and green; the dead who clung to bone like frost to a window; the great slow pressure of the gods underneath it all, humming in the marrow of the world. He had learned to carve inside that crowd the way a fish learns to swim in current. Now there was no current. Just him, and the knife, and the wood, and the small dry sounds of his own breathing.

It was terrifying. It was wonderful. He could not stop doing it.

Halfway through the wolf's haunches, he realized his shoulders had come down from around his ears for the first time since childhood. No voice in the grain asking him for anything. No name half-remembered by a goddess pressing through the antler behind him. No murdered boy, no weeping widow, no thousand-year-old king demanding his leg back. Just the work. Just the shape emerging under his hands, imperfect, human-sized, answerable to no one.

*So this is what everyone else gets,* he thought. *All this time, this is what it felt like to be Kesh.*

He understood, suddenly, why she was so loud.

* * *

Serane Hallic came at dusk, because of course she did. Former inquisitors, Noor was learning, had an unerring instinct for arriving precisely when a man was having a private emotional breakthrough.

She stood in the doorway of the workshop in her travel clothes — the gray cloak folded over her arm, retired along with everything it had stood for — and looked at the shavings on the floor with an expression Noor could not read.

"They told me you were carving again," she said.

"Practicing." He held up the half-formed wolf. "It's going to be dreadful. Apparently my early work was dreadful too, so at least I'm consistent."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Almost a smile. With Serane, that was practically a festival.

"I wanted to tell you," she said, "before I leave tomorrow. What you did at the mountain — opening it, letting them choose —" She stopped. Started again, in the flat careful voice of a woman dismantling her own certainty plank by plank. "Forty years ago, the inquisition sealed the gods away, and I built my life on the belief that it was the only mercy available. That some doors must be locked for the sake of the people on this side of them. And then you showed me a third thing. A lock that opens only outward. A door that is not a prison." Her hand went briefly to her collarbone, where, beneath the shirt, Noor knew, hung the small carved token he had made her — the first thing his hands had made in the new silence, a simple knot-work circle, no power in it at all. "I don't have a word for what you are anymore. Doorkeeper isn't right. You closed the office along with the door."

"Carver," Noor said. "Just call me a carver. It turns out it was always enough."

Serane nodded slowly, and for a moment she looked — not young, she was far too iron for that — but unburdened. A woman setting down a ledger she had carried for twenty years.

"The passes will be open soon," she said. "Vess is withdrawing the patrols. There will be roads again, real ones, and letters to carry, and people scattered across three kingdoms who think they're the last of their kind and aren't." She met his eyes. "I hear smugglers are good at finding roads."

"I hear the best one is unemployed as of this morning," Noor said. "Deaf as a post. Twice as stubborn."

"Then she'll manage." And this time the smile actually arrived, brief and crooked and human, before she turned and left him alone with the shavings and the dusk.

Noor picked up the knife again. Outside, the mountains stood dark and whole against the stars, closed now, sleeping, keeping their dead the way the earth keeps seeds — not gone, only waiting, and none of it any longer his to carry.

He carved. The wolf slowly became a wolf. And in the deepest quiet he had ever known, Noor Nystrom worked by lamplight, mourning and free, until footsteps came running up the path outside — Kesh's unmistakable stride, fast and urgent, followed by voices he didn't recognize, and her voice cutting through them, sharp with alarm:

"Noor! Noor, you need to see this — someone's coming up the valley, and they're carrying something that's *humming.*"
