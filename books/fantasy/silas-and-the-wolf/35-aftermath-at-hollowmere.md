## 35. Aftermath at Hollowmere

The fog took three days to leave the pass. Noor counted them from the ridge above Hollowmere, watching it thin like milk stirred into water until the mountains showed their gray faces again and the wound in Thornfell closed over with nothing worse than a scar of pale stone. Below him, the camp was already learning how to be a town.

Tents came down. Frames went up. Someone had dragged a cartload of timber from the burned outer camp — wood that had survived the Vess soldiers' torches — and now half a dozen people were arguing over where a meeting hall should stand, which was to say everyone was arguing and no one was building, which Noor had come to understand was simply how communities began.

"You're staring," Kesh said, arriving at his elbow with two bowls of barley porridge and handing him the less burnt one. "Staring's not building."

"I'm supervising."

"You're hiding." She sat down on the rock beside him, tucking her boots under her, and blew across her spoon. "There's a difference. I know all about it. I invented it."

He ate. The porridge was terrible. It was also the best thing he'd ever tasted, though he suspected that had less to do with the porridge than with the fact that he could sit here eating it in silence — true silence, ordinary human silence — without a single dead voice threading through his skull. He'd spent eleven years wishing for this quiet. Now that he had it, he kept catching himself listening for what wasn't there, the way an amputee's hand still itches.

"Any regrets?" Kesh asked, watching his face do whatever it was doing.

"About the porridge? Several."

"Noor."

He considered lying to her. He'd never once managed it, so he didn't start now. "It's like coming home to a house where every room has been emptied," he said. "I got exactly what I wanted. And the rooms are so *big* now."

Kesh nodded slowly, chewing. Then she leaned her shoulder against his, briefly, the way she did when words were too heavy to bother with. They watched the camp argue itself into existence below.

* * *

The reckoning, as it turned out, was harder than the rebuilding.

Ashka's followers numbered perhaps two hundred still living — those who had stood at the edge of her rite and been pulled back before the light finished taking them, those who had fled when they finally understood what the general intended, those who had simply been too far from the mountain to matter. They drifted back into Hollowmere over the first week like leaves returning to a puddle, and each of them carried the same look: the look of people who had volunteered to die and been told, afterward, exactly what they'd volunteered for.

Noor heard the confessions secondhand, because he couldn't hear anything firsthand anymore, and there was a strange mercy in that. Sera Dannel, who had held the eastern watch for six years, stood up in the first open assembly and said, "I saw the exiles dissolving and I thought it was beautiful. I thought they were going *home*. I would have walked into it myself if Ashka had asked me by name." She said it loudly, in front of everyone, and then sat down and put her face in her hands, and no one condemned her, because half the assembly had thought the same thing.

That was the sickness of it, Noor thought, watching from the back. Not cruelty. Longing. Two hundred people who had lost a country so thoroughly that they were willing to be unmade rather than keep being unhomed. Ashka hadn't created that hunger. She'd only found it and aimed it.

Some of them wanted penance. A woman named Orla — Noor remembered her from the exile camps, a weaver with forearms like ship rope — took it upon herself to dig graves for the ones who had dissolved entirely, though there was nothing to bury. She dug forty-one holes in the hard ground of Hollowmere and filled every one with a handful of mountain stone from the scarred pass, and marked each with a carved post, and when Noor passed her on the third day she was talking to the empty holes as she worked.

"I'd offer to carve you something proper," he said, stopping beside the forty-first grave. "But my knife's gone quiet, these days."

Orla looked up at him with red-rimmed eyes and, unexpectedly, smiled. "Good," she said. "Let the dead rest. We've spent forty years asking them to do things." She patted the fresh-turned earth. "This one's for my brother. I don't need a bone to tell me what he'd want. He'd want me to plant something here in spring."

"What would you plant?"

"Barley," she said. "Terrible barley. The kind that burns." And she laughed, wetly, and Noor laughed too, and it was the first laugh either of them had managed since the mountain, and it cracked something open in both of them that needed cracking.

* * *

On the fifth day, the assembly asked Kesh to lead them.

Noor wasn't there for the asking — he was down at the stream, washing bowls, having discovered that manual labor was a kind of prayer when your hands had nothing left to listen to — but he heard about it within the hour, because Kesh came storming up the bank with her ears scarlet and her jaw set in the particular way that meant someone had said something reasonable to her and she'd hated every word of it.

"They want me to be *general*," she announced, flinging herself onto a boulder. "General Kesh. Of Hollowmere. There are two hundred people down there and most of them are older than the fall of Thornfell and they want a seventeen-year-old orphan to tell them what to do."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd think about it, which is what you say when you're too polite to laugh in someone's face." She picked up a stone and hurled it into the stream with impressive venom. "Why me? Because I stood between them and Ashka one time? Because I rode to Serane Hallic through a blizzard? That was one good week. One. You can't run a town on one good week."

"You ran a smuggling route on considerably less."

"That was different. Smuggling has rules. Don't trust anyone, don't stop moving, always know where the patrol drinks. Leadership is just — " she made a gesture of profound disgust — "*talking*. To everyone. All the time. About their feelings."

Noor dried a bowl. Set it down. Picked up another. "You talked Orla out of walking into the pass after the rite collapsed," he said. "I heard about that. Four hours, in the wind, while she screamed at you."

"She was going to get herself killed."

"Yes. So you stayed." He looked at her. "That's the whole job, Kesh. Staying when staying is awful. Everything else is porridge distribution."

She glared at him. Then, treacherously, her glare wobbled. "I don't want to be her," she said quietly. "Ashka started out wanting to save people too. Everyone says so. She was a war hero, she fed refugees out of her own stores, and then one day the saving mattered more than the saved, and she — " Kesh's hand closed around another stone. "What if it's in me? The turning?"

Noor set down the bowl and crossed to her and took the stone gently out of her fist, because she was going to split a knuckle. "Then it's in everyone," he said. "Including me. Including the widow Marta, including Serane Hallic, including sweet old Orla digging graves. The turning isn't a curse some people catch. It's a door inside every person, and you walk past it every day and choose not to go through. That's all virtue is, as far as I've ever seen. Choosing not to go through. Daily. Boringly."

"And if I choose wrong someday?"

"Then you'll have two hundred people around you whose whole job is to say *Kesh, no*." He smiled. "That's the other thing leadership gets you that smuggling doesn't. People who'll tell you when you're wrong. Ashka made sure no one could. Look how that ended."

Kesh was silent a long moment, watching the water. Then: "I'm still saying no."

"I know."

"But — " she exhaled through her nose, annoyed at herself — "maybe I'll sit on their council. If they'll have me. Somebody has to make sure they don't build the meeting hall on the floodplain, because I've seen the creek in spring and they haven't, and they'll all drown being sentimental about it."

"There it is," Noor said. "Leadership. Porridge distribution and floodplain sense. You're a natural."

She threw the remaining stone at him. He let it hit his shoulder, because he deserved it, and because her aim had improved and it barely hurt at all.

* * *

Serane Hallic left on the seventh day, but before she left she came to find Noor at the grave field, where he'd taken to sitting in the evenings with Orla and whoever else wanted company among the forty-one posts. His inquisitor arrived in her gray cloak, which she had stopped wearing during the march on the pass and now wore again, though Noor noticed it had been cleaned of its scorch marks and its hem let down — mended, not replaced. A woman planning to keep using it, then. Just differently.

"The order won't dissolve officially," she said, standing beside him, hands clasped behind her back in parade rest even now, even here. "Vess will reassign my cell to border administration. But the doorkeeper hunts are done. I've written the recommendation myself. 'Threat neutralized by cooperative resolution; further enforcement unnecessary and contrary to crown interest.' They'll hate every word. They'll accept it anyway, because the alternative is admitting the gods were real, and bureaucracy would rather swallow glass."

"You wrote *cooperative resolution*," Noor said. "For the woman who cornered me in an alley and threatened to kill everyone I love."

"I did threaten that." She didn't flinch from it. "And then I marched my soldiers against my own order to stop a massacre, on the word of a smuggler's apprentice. I've been trying to decide which of those women I am. I think — " she paused, and for the first time since he'd known her, Serane Hallic seemed to genuinely not know the end of her own sentence. "I think I was certain for thirty years because certainty was easier than grief. My family died in the god-plague. I sealed my grief away the way we sealed the gods, and I called it justice, and it worked beautifully right up until it didn't." She turned to look at him. "Your apprentice told me something on the road. She said, *you can't lock grief in a box, it just gets louder.* She's seventeen. Where does she get this?"

"Terrible influences," Noor said. "Mostly me."

A ghost of a smile crossed Serane's face. From her belt she drew something wrapped in cloth and held it out: the small carved token he had made her — the first thing his hands had shaped since the silence, a simple knotwork circle, no voices in it, no magic, just craft. She'd worn it against her hip like a soldier's medal.

"I resigned this morning," she said. "Effective when I cross the border. After that I'm nobody, which is a rank I find I'm looking forward to." She pressed the token back into his hand. "Keep it. Or give it to someone who needs reminding that enemies can stop being enemies. Apparently I'm evidence of the concept."

"Where will you go?"

"North, initially. There are records in the Vess archives I mean to read — properly, this time, not as an inquisitor but as a woman whose grandparents lived under the old gods and never spoke of it. Someone should write the true history of Thornfell. The archive under the ice gave us pieces; the survivors give us the rest." She looked out over the forty-one posts, the evening light gilding them. "History written by the certain is how we got here. Maybe history written by the sorry will do better."

When she'd gone, Kesh appeared at Noor's shoulder — she had a gift for that, or perhaps he simply always knew where she was; some senses survive the loss of others — and looked after the departing inquisitor.

"Did she just call me *your apprentice*?" Kesh said. "To your face? Like it's official?"

"It's been official for years. You just refused to sign the paperwork."

"I refuse the *title*, not the — " She stopped. Scowled. "Shut up."

* * *

Spring came early that year, or perhaps it only seemed to, because everyone in Hollowmere was watching for it.

Orla planted her barley in the forty-one graves, exactly as promised, and by midsummer the grave field stood waist-high in grain that was, as forecast, terrible — thin-stalked and rust-prone and stubbornly alive. The children of the camp harvested it in autumn and it made bread that burned going down, and everyone ate it anyway, and Orla presided over the first loaf-cutting with the expression of a queen reviewing troops.

The meeting hall went up on high ground, well clear of the floodplain, because Kesh had stood in three separate assemblies and said *the creek rises, build higher* until they surrendered. It was lopsided and drafty and its roof leaked in exactly one spot, directly over the council bench, which Kesh declared to be nature's commentary on authority and refused to have fixed.

People came, in those months. Exiles from the far camps, hearing that Hollowmere had survived and was planting things. A few Vess citizens, quietly, the ones with Thornish grandmothers and questions they'd never dared ask. Even a pair of Gray Cloaks, former soldiers of Serane's cell, who arrived with their weapons peace-bonded and their hats in their hands, and were put to work hauling timber, because the surest way to be forgiven at Hollowmere was to be useful.

And sometimes, on market days, travelers stopped at the stall where Noor sold carvings — plain work now, spoons and buttons and little animals, nothing that sang — and asked him, hesitantly, whether it was true. Whether he had been the doorkeeper. Whether the stones really hummed.

"It's true the mountains are old," he'd say, and hand them a wooden wolf, or a bird, or nothing at all, and let them make of that whatever they needed.

Because the truth was stranger and quieter than any story: some nights, walking the high paths alone, Noor would feel it — not a voice, not a whisper, nothing so definite. More like warmth rising off a stone at dusk. A faint pressure, like a memory of music. The country, remembering itself gently instead of screaming. Vell was gone, crossed over into rest with the thousands of the unfinished dead, her divinity surrendered at the threshold. Whatever remained in the land was only echo. Only the way a house holds the shape of the people who lived in it.

He didn't mind. Echoes, he'd learned, could be lived alongside. Voices had to be obeyed.

* * *

It was Kesh who finally said it, one evening in late summer, when the two of them sat on the ridge with their boots dangling and the camp below them glowing with cookfires — dozens of cookfires now, where there had been tents and fear.

"I told the council no again today," she said. "Third time. Old Brenn keeps asking. He says I have *the instinct*." She snorted. "Then Marta's boy asked me to teach him the passes. Reading weather, reading stone, the arithmetic of loads and patrols. And I said yes before I even thought about it. So apparently I'll teach the roads, but I won't govern the town."

"Sounds about right."

"And then I thought — " she turned the stick she was whittling over in her fingers; she'd taken up carving lately, badly, with tremendous enthusiasm — "there are other settlements. Other camps, scattered all over the map, full of people who don't know what happened here. Who think the doorkeepers are monsters or myths. Who've got letters they can't send and kin they can't find." She glanced sideways at him, and there it was, the old sharp gleam, the smuggler's arithmetic already running behind her eyes. "Roads need walking. Letters need carrying. Maps need making — new maps, not Ashka's map of what was. Maps of what *is*."

Noor looked out at the cookfires, at the terrible barley rippling silver in the dusk wind, at the lopsided hall with its leaky roof, at everything they had built out of the bones of a country that no longer existed — built not to restore it but to grow past it, the way a tree grows past the fence post it was once tied to.

"You're proposing we leave," he said.

"I'm proposing we *go*," Kesh corrected. "Leaving's what you do when you're running. Going's what you do when you know the way back." She stood, brushing off her trousers, and stuck out her hand to haul him up, because she had never once in five years waited for him to rise on his own. "Pack light, old man. We leave when the passes clear."

He took her hand and let her pull him to his feet, and together they looked down at Hollowmere, lit and loud and alive, a town that would be fine without them — which was, Noor reflected, the entire point of everything.

"Where first?" he asked.

Kesh grinned, and somewhere far off in the dark mountains, so faint it might have been wind, the stones hummed.

"Wherever the road remembers us," she said.
