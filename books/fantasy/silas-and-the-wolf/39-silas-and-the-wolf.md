## 39. Silas and the Wolf

The dog had been Kesh's idea, which was fitting, because most of the good things in Noor's life had been.

"You need a companion," she'd said, seven years ago, dropping a bundle of fur and teeth into his lap outside a waystation in the Vessan foothills. "You talk to yourself when you walk. It's unsettling. People think you're haunted."

"I *was* haunted," Noor had said.

"Was," Kesh agreed, scratching the pup behind one enormous ear. "Past tense. Now you're just a man who mutters at rocks. A dog makes it look intentional."

The pup had been half wolf by every honest account and entirely wolf by his own opinion of himself. The shepherd who'd bred him swore the mother was only a large dog, but no large dog had ever looked at Noor quite that way — with the patient, ancient attention of something deciding whether you were worth its time. He'd named the animal Silas after the first bone he'd ever carved that sang: a little wolf figurine for a boy who died of fever in the last winter of Thornfell, whose ghost had asked him, very politely, to make it *run*.

Silas did not sing. That was the thing about him. In seven years of walking the high passes together, through weather that peeled paint and passes that forgot they were passes, Silas had never once done anything inexplicable. He chased marmots he never caught. He was afraid of goats. He slept with his nose under his tail like an ordinary dog and woke Noor each morning by breathing on his face with the dignity of a tax collector.

He was, in other words, exactly what Noor had needed: a creature with no ghosts in him at all.

* * *

They heard the stories the way you hear weather — secondhand, arriving from all directions, slightly different each time.

In a tavern in Lowmark, a wool merchant told them about the carver and his wolf who walked the Thornfell passes, the ones the maps still called impassable and the smugglers called home. The carver sold small figures at crossroads shrines, the merchant said. Wolves, mostly. Birds. Once, allegedly, a king's femur carved into a flute, though nobody could say what had become of it, and the merchant's cousin swore the man who played it wept for three days and then laughed for three more, and then bought everyone in the room dinner.

"That's not how it happened," Noor murmured into his stew.

"How *did* it happen?" Kesh asked, delighted.

"The flute was a shinbone, and I gave it to a shepherd whose daughter wouldn't stop grieving, and he played it once and then buried it where she couldn't find it." He shrugged. "Some songs are meant to be sung once."

"And the weeping?"

"That part's true. Mine, mostly. Carving's harder than it used to be." He flexed his right hand, where the old ache lived now, a dull companion that flared in cold weather and announced rain two days before the sky did. His gift was gone — spent, poured out, given away in a gray country between worlds on a night he still dreamed about — but the hands remembered their work. They simply made things now that kept their silence. It was, he'd come to believe, what craft had always wanted from him anyway.

The merchant went on: the carver and his wolf had once been seen standing at the Wound in the mountain — the pass that bled fog for a year after the mad general's ritual — and where the carver stood, the fog had parted like a curtain being drawn by a considerate hand.

"Fog parts for wind," Noor said.

"Mm," said Kesh, who had stopped humoring him years ago and now simply enjoyed watching him squirm.

A drover at another table leaned over. "My brother saw them at Hollowmere," he said. "Says the dog looked straight at him and he felt — judged. Thoroughly judged. Like the animal knew everything he'd ever done wrong."

"He does have a look," Kesh said solemnly, and Silas, sprawled across her boots, opened one eye and judged the drover thoroughly.

* * *

Spring took them up through the southern passes, the long way, because the long way was the point. They carried letters — that was the trade now, or half of it. Letters between scattered exiles, between mothers and sons separated by borders that had outlived the country that drew them, between old priests living quietly under Vessan names and the few remaining souls who remembered why the old gods had needed sealing and why some of them had deserved mercy anyway.

Noor had learned to read grief the way he'd once read bone. A letter from a woman in Grayharbor to a brother she hadn't seen since the fall: her handwriting started neat and ended ragged, and there was a smudge near the bottom that might have been a tear or might have been soup, and Noor sealed it with extra wax either way. A letter from a boy of maybe twelve to a grandfather in the exile settlements, containing nothing but a drawing of a goat and the words *I HAVE A GOAT NOW*. Noor carried that one against his chest, in the inner pocket, where the important freight rode.

Kesh ran the routes now. Everyone knew it; she'd stopped pretending otherwise around the time she'd talked three border garrisons, a toll collector, and one genuinely terrifying bridge troll of a customs officer into believing their caravan was a traveling theater troupe. She kept the ledgers, haggled the tariffs, chose the passes, and blamed Noor cheerfully whenever anything went wrong, which he accepted as his fair share of an arrangement that worked.

"You're slowing down," she told him on the third morning above the tree line, not unkindly.

"I'm savoring."

"You're slowing down *and* savoring. Both things can be true. My mother used to say that."

It was the first time in months she'd mentioned her mother without the careful blankness she'd worn around the name since the message, since the name of the man who'd left. Noor glanced at her. She was looking at the mountains with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of someone carrying something heavy who has finally found a way to set it down for a moment without it breaking.

"She'd have liked this route," Noor offered.

"She'd have hated this route. She hated cold." Kesh smiled at the horizon. "She'd have liked that I don't."

* * *

They crossed into old Thornfell on a Tuesday, because Kesh insisted borders should be crossed on ordinary days. There was no marker anymore. There hadn't been for forty years — no wall, no gate, no line on any living map. But Noor's feet knew the exact spot. Every doorkeeper's feet would have known it, once. Here the road changed under you, the way a house changes when you step from the entryway into the kitchen: same stones, different belonging.

These days the change was fainter. These days it felt less like crossing into a country and more like walking through a memory of one — which, he supposed, was what it was.

The villages along the high valley had come back slowly, and strange. Not restored — nothing here was restored — but reinhabited, rebuilt by exiles' grandchildren who'd never seen the old country and built it new instead, with Vessan rooflines and Thornish doorways, with names half in one tongue and half in the other. The shrine at the valley mouth had been raised by the widow Marta's granddaughter, who carved a little herself, badly, and didn't care. She left offerings of bread and small wooden animals, and if the old gods ever came for them, no one had reported it. Probably they didn't. Probably most of them were gone now, crossed over on that long night when the door stood open both ways, finished at last, released.

One or two, maybe, had stayed. Noor had his suspicions about the wind off the northern crags, which had a way of arriving exactly when you needed a push uphill and dying down exactly when you needed rest. But suspicion was as far as he let himself go. He'd learned the cost of listening too closely.

Still. On quiet nights, in certain places, the stones hummed.

Not voices. He wanted to be precise about this, even alone in his own skull, even with no one to correct. Voices had words, and demands, and griefs the size of nations. This was none of that. This was the sound a bell makes long after it's been struck — not ringing, just remembering how. You heard it best lying on warm rock in the hour before dawn, when the world held its breath. A hum in the bones of the mountain, in the marrow of the land. Memory, not speech. The country dreaming of itself, gently, the way old people dream of their youth: without urgency, without sorrow, just the pleasure of having been.

The first time he'd heard it, two summers ago, he'd sat bolt upright in the dark with his heart hammering, certain the gift had come back, certain something wanted something from him again. He'd lain awake until dawn waiting for the whisper to become a word.

It never did. By morning he understood, the way you understand things in the space between sleep and waking: nothing wanted anything. The land wasn't asking. It was just *there*, the way a grave is there, the way a song is there after the singer stops. And for the first time since he was a boy pressing his ear to his mother's carvings, Noor Nystrom heard the dead country speak and felt no fear at all.

Now he sought those places out. He'd never admit it to Kesh. She'd already caught him doing it twice.

* * *

They camped that night below the Wound.

It was almost healed. Seven years of weather and patience had done what rituals couldn't: grass grew in the scar, stubborn and bright green, and a stream ran through it now that hadn't existed before, cold and clean, as if the mountain had decided that if it must be cut open it might as well spring a useful leak. Travelers used the pass freely. Children from the new villages swam in the stream in summer and dared each other to touch the old ritual-stones, which were just stones now, lichened and dumb.

Noor touched one anyway, the way he always did. Habit. Greeting. *Hello, old friend. Still here?*

The stone said nothing. The stone was a stone. But beneath his palm, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the ground hummed.

"There it is," Kesh said softly. She'd built the fire and was feeding it twigs, not looking at him, giving him his moment the way she always did — privacy performed as indifference. "You've got your face on."

"What face?"

"The one where you're listening to something and pretending you're not. Your whole face goes polite." She pointed a stick at him. "It's very annoying. I've watched you do it for ten years."

"It's been seven."

"It's felt like ten."

Silas, who had spent the evening losing a philosophical argument with a marmot, came and lay down against Noor's side, warm as a loaf, smelling of dust and victory he had not earned. Noor scratched his ears. Below them the stream talked to itself in the dark. Somewhere far south, in a house in Vess with a garden she'd sworn she'd never keep and kept beautifully, a former inquisitor named Serane Hallic was presumably drinking tea and pretending not to wonder whether the letters she sent every solstice were too many. (They were. Noor answered every one.)

"Do you ever miss it?" Kesh asked. She asked it perhaps once a year, always at night, always sideways, watching the fire instead of him. "The hearing."

Noor considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he'd had seven years to practice the answer.

"Yes," he said. "And no. It's like — you know how you lost your mother's voice? The actual sound of it? You told me once you can't remember it anymore, just the words she said."

Kesh nodded. Her jaw did the small tight thing it did.

"It's like that. I miss the voices the way you miss a voice. But I don't miss what came with them. The weight. Every carving a door someone could knock on. Every silence suspicious." He turned a smooth stone over in his fingers. "I spent thirty years being a hallway. It turns out I prefer being a house."

"A house," Kesh repeated. "That's the worst metaphor you've ever made and I've heard you describe smuggling arithmetic as 'a stew with numbers in it.'"

"A stew with numbers in it is *good*, actually—"

"It is not. Nobody wants that stew." She threw a twig at him. He caught it, because his reflexes remained smuggler-grade even if his knees had begun filing complaints. "House," she muttered. "Honestly."

But she was smiling, and the fire cracked, and Silas sighed the deep sigh of a creature whose work for the day — napping, judging, failing to catch marmots — was complete. Above them the stars came out the way they did over Thornfell, thick and close, as if the sky here had never quite gotten the news that the country below it was gone.

* * *

Near midnight, Noor gave up on sleep and walked a little way from the camp, the way he did, and Kesh pretended to stay asleep, the way she did, and Silas trotted after him because a wolf-dog does not permit unsupervised wandering.

He climbed to a shelf of rock above the stream and sat, and the cold came up through his old bones, and the mountain hummed.

He listened the way he'd taught himself to listen now — loosely, the way you listen to rain. Not for messages. For company.

And the strangest thing happened, the small strange thing that happened sometimes, that he had never told anyone, not Kesh, not Serane, not the letters: for a moment — half a breath, no more — the hum shaped itself around something almost like a word. Not a command. Not a plea. Something warmer and simpler than either.

It sounded, if he was being generous with himself, and tonight he was, like *stay*.

Or possibly like *thank you*. The hum was imprecise. It had always been imprecise. That was the difference between memory and a voice, he thought: memory doesn't need to be understood. It only needs to be kept.

"Stay a while, then," Noor said aloud, to the mountain, to the country that no longer existed on any map and had never, in forty years, stopped existing everywhere else. "We're in no hurry."

Silas put his great head on Noor's knee and sighed, and far below the stream went on talking to itself, and somewhere in the world a traveler was telling someone a story about a carver and his wolf who walked the high passes, and the story was wrong in every particular and completely true, and the stones hummed on — not voices, never voices again, just memory, just the long low note of a country that had learned, at last and at terrible cost, how to rest.

Noor smiled, and scratched his wolf's ears, and stayed a while longer, because he could.

Then he got up, stiff-kneed and content, and walked back down to the fire, and to Kesh, and to sleep — and in the morning they took the road east, toward whatever letters needed carrying, and the mountain watched them go the way old friends watch from doorways: without calling after them, certain they'd be back.
