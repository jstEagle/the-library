## 40. Chapter 40

The first thaw came to Hollowmere in the shape of water.

Noor heard it before he saw it — not with the old hearing, which was gone and would stay gone, but with his ears, the ordinary ones, the ones he'd spent forty years ignoring in favor of voices that were never meant for them. Water moving under ice. A long, cracking sigh as the frozen pond behind the workshop let go of winter all at once, like an old man easing into a chair he'd been standing beside too long.

He stood at the door with his tea going cold and listened to it, and thought: *this is what everyone else has had all along.*

It was, he'd decided over the winter, rather a lot.

"Tea's stewing itself into tar," Kesh called from inside, where she was doing something to the stove that involved a poker and what sounded like a personal grudge. "If you want any, come argue with me about the honey."

"I always want honey."

"You always take too much honey. There's a difference between wanting and taking, carver. It's practically your whole biography."

He smiled and went in. The workshop smelled of woodsmoke and linseed oil and, faintly, of bone dust that no longer whispered anything at all. His tools hung on their pegs in order of size, because some habits outlive their reasons. On the bench by the window sat the last thing he'd carved — nothing, currently. The bench was empty. He'd gotten used to the emptiness the way you get used to a missing tooth: aware of it mostly when your tongue went looking.

Kesh had won the honey argument, as she won most arguments, by simply pouring while he was still forming his rebuttal. She slid his cup across the table and dropped into the opposite chair, boots up, hair still sleep-flattened on one side. Nineteen now, or twenty — she'd stopped being precise about it around the time she realized precision gave him ammunition for birthday gifts.

"Vess patrol came through the lower road yesterday," she said, in the tone she used for weather reports. "Two of them. Bought eggs. Paid fairly. One of them asked if we were the smugglers."

"What did you say?"

"I said smugglers don't sell eggs." She sipped her tea. "He looked very confused. I think we're safe until someone explains economics to the Vess army."

Noor laughed, and the sound surprised him a little — easy, unguarded, the laugh of a man who was not currently being hunted by anyone. It had taken two full years to trust it.

Outside, the pond finished its long surrender, and somewhere down in the village a bell rang the morning hour, off-key, because the bellringer was nine years old and enthusiastic rather than accurate. Nobody had fixed it. Nobody wanted to. The off-key bell had become, in the way of small communities, a point of pride.

* * *

Spring in Hollowmere meant the Founding Market, which was a strange name for a festival in a town founded by people fleeing a country that no longer existed, but names are stubborn things and so are festivals. Stalls went up along the mill stream. Someone strung lanterns between buildings that could not have held less structural agreement about the arrangement. Children ran feral and delighted through the gaps.

Noor kept a stall there too, though he no longer sold keepsakes. He sold spoons. It had been Kesh's idea, delivered with characteristic mercy: *"You can't just sit in that workshop staring at blank bone forever, and nobody needs another mourning token from you specifically, no offense, but you make a genuinely excellent spoon."*

He did, it turned out, make an excellent spoon. Word had spread. There was now a waiting list, which struck him as the single funniest development of his entire life, including the year he'd been briefly imprisoned by a zealot general.

He was wrapping a set of six for a farmer's wife when the child appeared.

She couldn't have been more than seven, gap-toothed, wearing a coat that had been her brother's and before that possibly a tent. She stood at the edge of the stall with the particular rigid stillness of a child working up to something enormous, and Noor knew that stillness. He had worn it himself, once, in a workshop much like this one, holding a carving he shouldn't have been able to hear.

"Master Nystrom?" she said.

"Noor," he said, as he said to everyone. "What can I do for you?"

She glanced left. Glanced right. Then she reached into her coat and produced a stone — a river stone, gray, smooth, unremarkable in every way except that she held it the way you hold a bird with a broken wing.

"It hums," she whispered.

The market noise went on around them — haggling, laughter, the criminal bell — and Noor felt the world tilt very slightly, the way it sometimes still did, like a floor remembering an earthquake.

"Hums," he repeated carefully.

"When I hold it. Not loud. Like—" she screwed up her face, searching, "—like when someone's in the next room and you can't hear the words but you know they're talking. And my mam says I'm making it up but I'm *not*, and Da says maybe I should ask you, because you used to do the bones, before."

Before. Before the door. Before Vell crossed over and thousands of the unfinished dead finally got to be finished, and the gift that had defined every miserable, holy day of Noor's life drained out of him through his own two hands like water through open fingers.

He looked at the stone. It was just a stone. He picked it up anyway, and it was warm from her pocket, and silent, utterly silent, and he felt — not grief exactly. Something adjacent to grief. Something that lived next door to grief and borrowed its cups.

"Where did you find this?" he asked.

"By the pass. The one with the scar in the mountain." She lowered her voice further. "I wasn't supposed to go up there. But it was singing first, before I even touched it."

Noor closed his hand around the stone and thought about mountains that remembered, roads that reshaped themselves at words in a dead tongue, a country that had never fully agreed to stop existing. He thought about the quiet nights travelers described, the stones that hummed faintly — not voices, just memory. Everyone assumed the humming was fading. Assumed the land was settling, the way ash settles.

But ash didn't sing louder near children.

"Tell you what," he said, and handed the stone back, and watched her whole face light with the relief of being believed. "I'm going to give you a spoon. The best one I have. And in exchange, you're going to tell me everything that stone ever said to you, and you're going to keep listening, and you're going to tell me if it ever says more than humming. Can you do that?"

She nodded so hard her hood fell down.

"And," Noor added, "you're not making it up. Your mam's wrong about this one. It happens. Mine was wrong about it too."

* * *

He told Kesh that night, sitting on the workshop roof with their boots dangling over the drainpipe, watching the festival lanterns gutter out one by one below.

"A listener," Kesh said. "A kid listener. In Hollowmere. Downstream of a mountain wound that a goddess tore open and we then politely asked to close."

"That's roughly the summary, yes."

"And your professional assessment?"

"My professional assessment is that doors don't really close. They just change what they're doors *to*." He turned the thought over. It had been ripening all day. "We sealed it gently. With consent. Serane's lock opens only for those ready to leave. But a lock implies a key, and a key implies a hand, and hands—" he wiggled his fingers, "—come in all sizes. Some of them seven years old."

Kesh was quiet for a while. Below, the last lantern died, and the village settled into that particular darkness that isn't empty — the darkness of people sleeping behind walls, dogs dreaming, bread rising somewhere for morning.

"You could ignore it," she offered. "You've earned ignoring it. You could carve spoons until you die, beloved and mildly famous for it, and some other fool deals with the singing stones."

"I could."

"But?"

"But I remember what it was like," Noor said, "being seven and hearing something impossible and having absolutely no one to tell. My mother knew, eventually, and look how that went. But before she knew — those years alone with it?" He shook his head. "I wouldn't wish that on anyone. Especially not on someone standing downstream of *that mountain*."

"So we're doing this."

"We're doing this. Gently. Slowly. With consent." He bumped her shoulder. "And with you doing most of the actual work, because I'm old and my knees have opinions."

"Your knees are fifty-two."

"They're *fifty-two and have opinions*, Kesh. Respect your elders."

She laughed — the sharp bark of a laugh she'd never quite smoothed into politeness, the orphan's laugh, the road's laugh — and then went quiet again, and he knew that quiet too. She only went quiet like that when she was deciding whether to say something true.

"Say it," he said.

"She'll need a teacher who isn't scared of it," Kesh said, to the dark. "Whoever she is. That kid. She'll need someone who can teach her the difference between a gift and a wound, because nobody taught you, and we both know how that went." She paused. "And she'll need someone who remembers that the dead aren't the point. The living are. You figured that out at the end, at the door. Somebody has to teach her that part *first*."

Noor looked at her. Firelight from the dying stove below caught half her face, and he thought — not for the first time, but with fresh astonishment every time — *I found you in a smuggling caravan arguing about tolls, and you turned out to be the finest person I know.*

"When did you get wise?" he asked.

"I've always been wise. You were too busy hearing ghosts to notice." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and headed for the roof ladder. Then she stopped. "Also, I'm keeping the wolf."

"The wolf is yours. It's always been yours."

"I know. I'm just formally announcing it. So there's no dispute later."

"There was never going to be a dispute later."

"There's always a dispute later. Ask anyone who's ever shared an inheritance." She went down the ladder, and a moment later he heard her inside, banking the stove, muttering at it with affection and hostility in equal measure.

Noor stayed on the roof a while longer. The stars over Hollowmere were thick tonight, the kind of sky the city folk paid money to pretend they'd seen. Somewhere north, past the passes, the scar in the mountain slept under snow, and the gray country beyond it kept its quiet, and the dead who had chosen rest were resting, and the dead who had chosen otherwise —

Well. That was the question, wasn't it. That was always the question.

He put his palm flat against the rooftop shingles, an old habit, listening with hands that heard nothing and never would again.

The shingles said nothing.

But far away, faint as breath, so faint he might have imagined it — might have *wanted* to imagine it, which amounted to the same thing — the mountain hummed.

Not a voice. Just memory. Just a country, remembering itself, patient as stone, waiting to see what its newest keeper would do next.

Noor smiled, and went inside, and slept better than he had any right to.

In the morning there would be spoons to carve, and a girl with a humming stone to teach, and roads worth mapping, and Kesh would burn the porridge, and the bell would ring wrong, and it would all be ordinary, and it would all be enough.

It already was.
