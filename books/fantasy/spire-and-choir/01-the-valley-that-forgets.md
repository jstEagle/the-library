## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The mist came up the valley walls at dawn the way it always did—slowly, without malice, like a tide that had forgotten what it was reaching for. Astrid Blackwood stood at the edge of it with their boots in the frost-stiffened grass and watched the white wall breathe.

It was not weather. They had learned that much in the first year, before they stopped counting years. Weather moved. This waited. It hung along the ridgeline of the valley in a gray curtain that never thinned and never thickened, and when the wind pushed against it, the wind went through and came out the other side unchanged while the mist simply declined to be disturbed. It was the oldest thing in the valley, older than the terraced fields below, older than the stone shrine with its faceless idol, older, perhaps, than the mountains that held them all like water in a cup.

Astrid checked the boundary stones. That was the morning's work, most days: walking the mistline from the split birch to the fallen arch, touching each of the nine markers, confirming that the world outside still could not see in.

The first stone was warm. They were all warm, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm long after the fire dies. Astrid pressed a palm flat against it anyway, out of habit rather than need, and felt nothing but stone and the ghost of some heat that had no source they cared to name.

*No names spoken aloud.* That was the first rule, though no one had given it to them as a rule. It had simply arrived one day, fully formed, the way you arrive at knowing not to touch a hot iron—not through instruction but through the body's own ancient wisdom. In the Valley of Hush, you did not say *I am Astrid*. You did not say *my mother's name was* or *I once loved a woman in* or even, when the loneliness got its teeth into you on the long winter nights, *hello* to the empty air. Names were hooks. The valley had taught them that without ever saying a word.

Below, the village was waking. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys in straight, unhurried columns. Someone was already at the millrace; Astrid could hear the wheel turning, a low wooden heartbeat under the birdsong. Good people, most of them. Quiet people. They had come here for the same reason Astrid had, though none of them would have phrased it that way. They had come here to forget, and the valley, generous in its strange way, had obliged.

Astrid touched the second stone. Warm. The third had a fox's pawprints pressed into the moss around its base, delicate as script, and for a moment Astrid crouched there studying them with an attention that felt almost devotional. The fox had come up from the orchard side in the night, circled the stone twice—why twice?—and gone down toward the river. A small life, going about its small business, unaware that it lived inside a wound in the world.

That was how Astrid thought of the valley sometimes. Not as a sanctuary. Sanctuaries implied choice, agency, a door you could walk back out of. The valley was a wound, and the mist was the skin grown over it, and everyone living beneath that skin was something the world had coughed up and then healed around.

They stood, knees cracking—they were not young anymore, though they could not have said how not-young—and walked on.

* * *

The fourth stone was missing.

Astrid knew it before they saw the empty socket, the way a musician knows a wrong note before the ear can locate it. Something in the morning's harmony had gone flat. They broke into a run, boots tearing through the frozen grass, breath smoking, and skidded to a stop at the place where the fourth marker should have been.

The socket was there. The stone was not. In its place: a churned patch of earth, a drag-mark running downhill toward the river, and—Astrid's stomach tightened—a single boot print, large, deep, pressed into the mud beside the hole. Recent. The edges of the print still glistened.

Someone had taken the stone. Someone who did not know what it was, probably. A farmer needing a boundary marker. A child playing at mason. The valley's invisibility did not depend on the stones—Astrid was nearly sure of that, nearly—but the stones were old, and old things in this place had a way of mattering more than they should.

Astrid followed the drag-mark down through the alders to the riverbank, where the story resolved itself into mundane truth: a raft of cut timber lashed together with rope, the stolen stone lashed on top of it like cargo, and a man standing waist-deep in the shallows, wrestling with a knot.

"Hey," Astrid called, and then, catching themselves, softer: "Hey. Leave that."

The man looked up. He was young, broad, red-faced with cold and effort—one of the Hendricks boys, maybe, though Astrid couldn't have said which Hendricks boy, because saying so would have required a name, and names were hooks. "Found this pretty rock up past the birches," he said. "Going to use it for my mother's garden wall. She's been wanting a—" He squinted. "Do I know you?"

"No."

"You've got a face like—" He frowned, working at it the way a tongue works at a loose tooth. "Like somebody. Somebody I should—"

"You don't." Astrid waded in, gasping—the water was snowmelt, knife-cold—and put a hand on the stone. "This stays here. Take any other rock in the valley. Not this one."

"Why? What is it?"

And there was the trap, laid so neatly Astrid almost admired it. *What is it?* To answer honestly they would have to say *it is a boundary marker on the edge of a forgetting*, and to say that they would have to remember what was being forgotten, and the valley did not permit that sentence to be finished. Astrid opened their mouth and felt the words dissolve like sugar on the tongue, leaving only a shape where meaning had been.

"I don't know," they said finally, and it was true, and it was a lie, and both truths sat in their chest like swallowed stones. "But leave it."

The Hendricks boy—no. *The young man.* The young man looked at Astrid for a long moment, and Astrid watched the forgetfulness take him in real time, watched his brow smooth over, watched the question die behind his eyes not because it was answered but because it was *unaskable*, the way you cannot remember a dream by noon. "Cold day for standing in the river," he said at last, agreeably, and waded out, and went home to his mother and her unbuilt garden wall, whistling.

Astrid hauled the stone back up the hill alone. It weighed as much as a dead man. By the time they set it back in its socket, their shoulders were burning and their hands had gone the color of tallow, and they stood there a while, breathing hard, looking at the mist.

"Thank you," they said to it. Saying things to the mist was another habit they didn't examine. The mist did not answer. The mist never answered. But the warmth of the stone seemed to deepen under their palm, very slightly, the way a sleeping animal leans into a familiar hand.

* * *

Midday found them at the fallen arch, the ninth and last marker, where the mistline dipped lowest and the world outside came closest. From here, on clear days, you could see a thread of the far road winding down from the pass—a brown stitch across green country—and once, years ago, Astrid had watched a column of travelers go along it, tiny as ants, and had felt something turn over in their chest that might have been longing or might have been grief, and had walked home and drunk half a bottle of plum brandy and not thought about it again.

Today the road was empty. Today the mist was quiet. Astrid ate bread and hard cheese sitting against the arch's broken curve, and let the sun find their face, and permitted themselves, as they permitted themselves every day at exactly this hour, ten minutes of remembering.

Not everything. Never everything. The valley wouldn't allow it—the memories came back soft-edged and incomplete, like letters read through frosted glass. But fragments. A campfire, and someone laughing. Hands teaching theirs to hold a sword properly, calloused and patient. A voice saying *you don't have to be what they made you*, though whose voice, and what *they* had made them, and whether anyone had ever actually said it—all of that was mist, was valley, was gone.

There had been a war. They were almost sure there had been a war. There was a scar along their ribs that said blade, and a stiffness in the left knee that said fall-from-height, and a flinch in them at the sound of singing, high voices in unison, that said something worse than either. And there was the oath.

Astrid did not think about the oath. Thinking about the oath was like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurt—it did, it always did, and the hurt came with a cold vertigo attached, a sense of standing at the lip of something vast and dark with their name written on the bottom. But every day at midday, for ten minutes, they let themselves stand at that lip and look down without falling in.

*I swore something,* they told the sky. *I swore something enormous, and it cost me, and I am alive, and I do not know why.*

The sky offered nothing. Below, the valley lay green and gold and utterly peaceful, and somewhere in it a child was shrieking with laughter over something, and the millwheel turned, and the smoke rose straight.

Weary contentment. That was the phrase Astrid had coined for it, privately, in the privacy of their own skull where rules about silence didn't reach. Weary contentment: the specific flavor of happiness available to a person who has survived by subtraction. Every day the valley took a little—not memories, mostly, those were already spent, but the *wanting*, the ambition, the sharp forward-leaning hunger that had presumably once driven them across whatever battlefields had left their scars. Every day it took a little, and every day what remained was enough. Bread. Sunlight. Work for the hands. The mist holding its line.

It was enough. It had to be enough. The alternative was the road beyond the pass, and whatever waited on the road beyond the pass, and Astrid's whole body flinched from that thought like a hand from a flame.

*No oaths renewed.* That was the second rule. Whatever vow lived coiled in them, sleeping, they fed it nothing. No promises aloud, not even small ones—not *I'll be there tomorrow*, not *I swear I locked the gate*. The words *swear* and *promise* and *vow* had a weight in Astrid's mouth that they did not have in other mouths; Astrid had learned to speak around them the way a man with a broken tooth learns to chew on one side. Once, three winters ago, a neighbor had asked them to *promise* to watch her house while she traveled, and Astrid had lain awake all night feeling the coil shift in their chest, feeling something old and enormous roll over in its sleep two valleys and a lifetime away, and in the morning they had told her, gently, that they couldn't, and she had been hurt, and Astrid had let her be hurt, because hurt feelings healed and oaths did not.

*No leaving.* The third rule, and the simplest, and the only one the valley itself enforced. Astrid had tested it exactly once, in the early years, walking up to the mistline with a pack on their back and fury in their throat. They remembered putting one foot into the white. They remembered the mist closing around them like a held breath. They did not remember the rest of that day, or the next, or how they had come to be back in their own bed with their boots cleaned and their pack unpacked and a taste in their mouth like copper and old incense. The valley did not keep prisoners. It kept something better than prisoners. It kept people who no longer wished to leave, and if wishing failed, it kept them anyway.

Astrid finished the bread. Brushed the crumbs from their coat. Stood, and rolled their bad knee until it loosened, and began the long walk back along the line, touching each stone as they passed. First. Second. Third. The fourth, newly returned to its socket, warm as a living thing.

It was on the fifth stone that they found the footprint.

Not the young man's—this one was small. Very small. A child's boot, maybe, or a bare foot, pressed into the moss just inside the mistline, pointing *inward*. And beside it, half-erased by the damp, a second print, and a third, a trailing scatter of small steps coming down out of the mountains—from *outside*, from beyond the pass, from the road where no one walked in winter and nothing human had crossed the mist in all Astrid's years of watching.

The steps led down into the valley. They did not lead back out.

Astrid stood very still. The wind moved the alders. Somewhere below, the child who had been laughing was laughing still, and the millwheel turned its slow wooden turn, and the mist breathed its long, patient breath along the ridge.

Then, faintly, carried up through the trees on the cold air, Astrid heard a voice. High. Young. Singing—or speaking, it was hard to tell—words in a language Astrid had never been taught and did not know and yet understood the way the body understands a fall: instantly, terribly, in the bones.

The words meant *I am coming to find you.*

Astrid ran.
