## 2. A Knock in Dead Winter

The snow came down like a verdict.

Astrid had learned to read the valley's weather the way other people read scripture—by rote, by repetition, by the slow accumulation of small betrayals. This storm was not one of the valley's usual mutterings. It was the kind of snow that erased. It fell in fat, silent sheets from a sky the color of old pewter, and it buried the fence posts one by one, and then the well-rope, and then the memory of what the world beyond the mistline had ever looked like.

Good, Astrid thought, standing at the window with a chipped mug of barley tea going cold in their hands. Let it bury everything. Let it bury the road, and the ridge, and the smoke of Ashford's chimneys if any of it dared show itself. Let it bury me too, while it's at it.

The tea was bitter. Everything in the Valley of Hush was a little bitter—the water, the bread, the company. That was the price of a place where magic could not follow: nothing grew quite right, nothing healed quite clean, and no prayer, however desperate, was ever answered. The godless lived here. The forgotten. People who had come over the mistline carrying names they never spoke again, oaths they let rot in their chests like teeth gone bad, and griefs so heavy that forgetting them was not a wound but a mercy.

Astrid had been here eleven years. Or twelve. The winters blurred together the way winters do when you stop marking them, and Astrid had stopped marking them on purpose. There was a notch-board above the hearth with thirteen scratches on it, and Astrid genuinely could not remember whether the thirteenth was this winter or the last one. That was fine. That was the arrangement. You gave up counting; you gave up being counted.

The house was small—one room and a loft, stone-walled, turf-roofed, built by hands Astrid did not remember building it. There was a bed, a stove, a table scarred with knife-marks that predated tenancy, and a shelf of books in languages Astrid could read but chose, most days, not to. On the wall opposite the window hung a sword wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth the blade slept the dreamless sleep of iron that has not tasted anything worth cutting in a very long time.

Astrid liked the sword best that way. Asleep. Wrapped. A thing that had happened to someone else.

* * *

The knock came at dusk, or what passed for dusk under that low, woolen sky—which is to say the dark simply arrived without ceremony, the way death arrives for the old.

Three knocks. Small ones. Almost apologetic.

Astrid did not move at first. Nobody knocked in the Valley of Hush. The valley had perhaps forty souls scattered along its length like seeds dropped carelessly from a torn pouch, and every one of them knew the rules as Astrid knew them: you did not visit uninvited, you did not ask questions about the before-times, you did not say your old name aloud even in your own kitchen where only the mice could hear. Neighbors waved across fences. They traded salt for turnips at the midwinter market with their eyes down and their words short. They did not knock.

Three knocks again. Weaker now. The rhythm of someone who was spending the last of something.

Astrid set down the mug. Every instinct they owned—and they owned good instincts, honed in years whose details were fog but whose lessons remained sharp as fishhooks—every instinct said *do not open that door*. Storms were cover. Storms were how things crossed borders that were watched. And though the valley's mistline turned back anything that carried magic in its blood, there were plenty of things in this world that were terrible in perfectly mundane ways: men with knives, men with warrants, men with faith.

But the knocks were small. That was the problem. Astrid had heard a great many knocks in a great many doors, in another life, in a country of fog, and they had learned the grammar of knocking the way soldiers learn the grammar of gunfire. Heavy knocks meant authority. Rhythmic knocks meant patience, which was worse. But small knocks, uneven, spaced too far apart, each one costing more than the last—

That was the knock of someone who would not survive the night outside.

"Damn it," Astrid said quietly, to the room, to themselves, to whatever dead god might or might not be listening. "Damn it all."

They opened the door, and the wind came in first like an invader, filling the room with white noise and cold that bit down to the marrow, and behind the wind, half-collapsed against the doorframe, was a child.

* * *

It was hard, in that first moment, to say what the child was. A drift of rags, mostly. Snow-crusted. Small—a head shorter than the door latch, which put them at seven years old, maybe eight, maybe younger, because famine children ran small and this one had famine written into the hollows of its face. Chapped lips gone gray-blue. Eyes squeezed shut against the wind. Hair frozen into a helmet of dirty brown ice.

And frostbitten feet, bare feet, Astrid saw with a lurch—bare feet on bare ground, blue-black at the toes, leaving a trail of half-melted prints up the path that should have been impossible. The nearest farmstead was six miles off. The mistline was farther than that. Whatever this child had walked through to get here, it had walked through it without shoes, in a killing storm, and was still standing. Had been standing. Was now, in fact, folding at the knees like wet paper.

Astrid caught them under the arms. The body was light in the horrifying way—lighter than a sack of grain, lighter than a corpse ought to be, all sinew and bird-bones and cold. Astrid hauled them inside, kicked the door shut against the screaming wind, and stood for a moment in the sudden hush with an armful of freezing child, feeling like a man who has just pulled a drowning stranger out of a river and realized, too late, that the river followed him in.

The child's lips were moving. Not shivering—moving. Forming words.

Astrid leaned close, and the words came out on a thread of breath, cracked and slurred by cold, and they were words Astrid did not know.

Except that wasn't true. That was the terrible part. That was the part that made Astrid's hands go still on the child's shoulders and made the fire pop in the silence like a held breath breaking.

The words were in no language Astrid had ever been taught. Astrid was certain of this. Astrid's education had been thorough—trade-tongue, high script, the border dialects, enough of the marsh cant to haggle and enough of the church liturgy to blaspheme convincingly. None of those. These syllables were older than all of them. They had a shape like riverbed stones, worn smooth and deep, vowels that opened like doors onto rooms Astrid had never entered.

And yet some chamber far down inside Astrid's chest, some locked and lightless cellar of the self, *recognized* them. The way a body recognizes a smell from a burning house. Not understanding. Recognition. The animal knowledge that these sounds meant danger once, mean danger still, that somewhere behind the fog of eleven or twelve or thirteen winters there was a version of Astrid Sterling who knew exactly what those words were and had bled to make them stop.

"*...vennath... sel... vennath ira...*" the child whispered, eyes still closed, snow melting off their lashes in beads. "*...dommarae... dommarae ven...*"

"Stop," Astrid said. Their voice came out wrong. Rough. "Don't—stop talking."

The child stopped. Opened their eyes.

They were pale eyes. Not blue, not gray—pale the way the sky goes pale just before something falls out of it. And they looked at Astrid with none of the terror a lost child ought to feel at being held by a stranger with a sword on the wall and blood in their history. They looked at Astrid the way you look at a door you have been walking toward for a very long time.

"You're the one," the child said—in trade-tongue now, ordinary, accented from somewhere in the mountains, a child's voice worn ragged. "You're the one the voice said about."

Astrid's mouth went dry. "What voice?"

"The one that walks with me." The child said it the way other children report the weather. Matter-of-fact. Slightly bored, even, as if explaining for the hundredth time to slow adults. "It got quiet when we crossed the white line in the air. But it said, go down, go down to the valley, find the one who swore. Find the one who forgot on purpose." Pale eyes blinked slowly. "You forgot on purpose, didn't you? I can see the hole where it used to be."

The fire crackled. Wind clawed at the eaves. Somewhere under the floorboards a mouse went about its small, indifferent life.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Astrid said, and heard, even as they said it, how much it sounded like a lie. Because it was one, probably. Because the child was right—there *was* a hole. There had always been a hole. Eleven or twelve or thirteen years of waking at dawn with the taste of ash in the mouth and no reason for it, of flinching from hymns in the market at Ashford, of standing at the mistline some nights without knowing why, watching the silver curtain ripple and feeling like a man standing at the grave of someone whose name he couldn't recall.

The child smiled. It was a sweet smile. It was the worst thing Astrid had seen in a decade.

"You'll remember," the child said. Then their eyes rolled back, and they went limp as a dropped cloak, and the whispering started again—unconscious now, deep in whatever dream the cold had given them, murmuring those smooth riverbed syllables over and over like a prayer, like a countdown, like a key turning slowly in a lock Astrid had sworn, in some life they could not remember, to keep shut forever.

* * *

Astrid worked. Work was mercy. Work was a place to put the shaking hands.

They stripped the rags off the child—found beneath them a thin shirt stamped with a mark that made Astrid's stomach drop before they consciously understood why: a spire rendered in seven vertical strokes, ink faded almost to nothing. They rubbed the small blue-black feet with snow until the flesh screamed back to pink and the child whimpered without waking. They wrapped them in every blanket the house owned and laid them by the stove, and fed melted snow between cracked lips drop by drop, and all the while the murmur went on underneath, faint as insects under floorboards:

"*Vennath. Dommarae ven. Vennath ira sel.*"

Astrid caught themselves mouthing along. Caught themselves halfway through the second phrase, lips shaping sounds they had never learned, and sat back on their heels with their heart slamming like a fist on a door.

No. No, no. This was the valley. Nothing crossed the mistline. Magic couldn't follow. That was the whole covenant of the place—that was the entire bargain, the reason people gave up their names and their pasts and their gods to live bitter and safe among the turnips. Whatever this child carried, it should have been stripped off at the border like mud from boots. It should have been left in the snow with the shoes.

Unless the child hadn't crossed the mistline at all.

Unless the child had come *out of* it.

Astrid looked at the small sleeping face—chapped, frost-scarred, absurdly young—and felt the two oldest forces in their nature grapple for the wheel. The first was the survivor, the animal, the part that had gotten them over the mountains and through the fog and into this valley of deliberate forgetting: it wanted the child back out the door, wanted the blankets returned and the storm to do its work, wanted the whole impossible knot of it dropped back into whatever darkness had spawned it. Self-preservation was not a flaw in Astrid. It was the load-bearing wall.

The second force was older than the survivor. It had no name Astrid could retrieve. It lived in the hands, apparently—the hands that kept feeding snow-water between gray lips long after the immediate danger passed, the hands that tucked the blanket higher, the hands that paused, without instruction, on the child's forehead to push back a curl of thawing hair.

"You're the one who swore," the child had said. "Find the one who forgot on purpose."

Outside, the storm thickened toward full dark. Inside, the murmur continued, patient as dripping water carving stone.

Astrid sat down at the table with their back to the child because looking at them was unbearable, and poured the last of the barley tea, and did not drink it, and listened to a language they had never been taught rise and fall in the small breathing dark behind them like a tide coming in over graves.

One night, Astrid told themselves. One night out of the cold, and in the morning, when the storm broke, they'd take the child to the mistline and send them back wherever they'd come from. Back to the voice. Back to whatever waited. It was the only sane course. It was survival, and survival was the one law Astrid had never broken.

Behind them, in their sleep, the child said a word Astrid's bones understood even while Astrid's mind scrambled away from it—a single syllable, soft and absolute, and every hair on Astrid's arms rose, and the wrapped sword on the wall seemed, in the firelight, to have grown very slightly heavier.

The word sounded, unmistakably, like a name.

Not the child's.

Astrid did not sleep that night.
