## 3. Wren

The voice had been with Wren for as long as Wren could remember, which was not very long at all.

That was the first strange thing, though Wren did not know it was strange. Memory was supposed to be like a river, the woman in the valley had said once—no. The woman hadn't said that. Nobody had said anything yet. Wren was still walking, and the snow was still falling, and the voice was still singing.

*Little bird,* it sang, in a language that was not a language, or was a language before languages got tired and put on clothes. *Little bird, keep walking. Little bird, the valley is close.*

Wren's feet hurt. That was a real thing, a solid thing, one of the only solid things left. Everything behind them was soft and white and full of holes, like cloth eaten by moths. There had been a mother—or someone who held Wren's hand crossing a frozen stream, laughing when they both slipped. There had been bread, dark and dense, torn into three pieces. Three pieces meant three people. But when Wren reached for the third person's face, the memory tore like wet paper, and all that remained was the taste of the bread, which was rye, and the feeling of being loved by someone whose name had gone where names go.

The voice said not to worry about that.

*You will remember what matters,* it sang. *I have kept the rest safe for you. I am keeping many things safe. That is what I am for.*

"Where are you?" Wren asked aloud, because talking made the walking easier, and because the voice liked to be talked to. Snow went into Wren's mouth when they spoke, which tasted the way the sky smelled.

*I am everywhere you are,* the voice said. *And soon I will be somewhere very specific. Keep walking, little bird. Left foot. Right foot.*

Left foot. Right foot. The mountain fell away behind them in long grey slopes, and the trees grew taller and closer together, and somewhere below there was a smell of woodsmoke that made Wren's stomach clench like a fist.

Wren could not remember deciding to leave home. That was another hole in the cloth. There was warmth, and then there was cold, and between the two there should have been a door, a goodbye, a bundle of food tied in cloth. Instead there was simply this: waking on a mountainside with frost in their eyelashes and the voice saying *walk*, and walking. It did not frighten Wren. Frightening things were things you didn't understand, and Wren understood the voice the way they understood hunger—completely, without needing reasons.

*You trust me,* the voice observed, warm as a hand on the head.

"Yes," Wren said.

*Good. Trust is a door. You are my door, little bird.*

Wren didn't know what that meant, but it sounded important, the way church-sounds were important, back when—back when there had been a church? A bell? Something bronze that hummed in the chest. Another moth-hole. Wren decided, as children do, with the terrible practicality of children, not to look at the holes anymore. Looking made the edges fray faster.

* * *

Night came down blue and enormous. Wren found a hollow beneath a spruce tree where the snow hadn't reached, and curled up small, knees to chin, hands tucked under arms. The cold was past hurting now. It had become a kind of company, the way pain does when it stays long enough.

*Are you lonely?* the voice asked.

"No," Wren said. "You're here."

A pause. Even the voice could pause, Wren had learned. When it paused, it was thinking, or remembering, or something like remembering, though its memories must be different from people-memories, bigger, older, with different-shaped holes.

*When I am awake,* the voice said, *you will never be cold again. No one will. I will make the fields heavy. I will make the winters short and gentle. I will hold every city in my hand like a cupped bird and nothing will fall.*

"That sounds nice," Wren said sleepily.

*It will be,* said the voice, and for just a moment its song slipped, the way a hand slips on ice—a note underneath the warmth, vast and cold and patient, a note like the inside of a mountain. Then it smoothed over. *Sleep now, little bird. I will watch.*

Wren slept. And dreamed—not of the mother with the rye bread, but of a tower, black stone against a burning sky, and voices raised in a language Wren somehow knew even though no one had ever taught it: words that landed in the world and *stayed*, words that bent things. In the dream, Wren stood at the tower's foot and watched the sky crack along a seam no one else could see, and heard a great silence fall like an axe.

Wren woke with tears frozen on their cheeks and did not know why.

*You dreamed of the old days,* the voice said gently. *Pay it no mind. Dreams are just smoke from fires other people lit.*

"Whose fire?"

Silence. Longer this time. Long enough that Wren counted their own heartbeats, eleven of them, twelve.

*A dead man's,* the voice said at last, and laughed, and the laugh was warm again, and Wren let it be warm because the alternative was too large to hold.

* * *

On the third day—the voice counted the days for Wren, since Wren's counting kept sliding—the trees opened onto a bowl of land wrapped in mist so thick it looked solid, a grey wall from earth to sky, curving away in both directions until it met the mountains.

*There,* the voice sang, and its song changed. Wren felt the change in their teeth, in the roots of their hair. The voice had been lullaby-soft for three days; now it went taut as a bowstring. *There, little bird. The quiet place. The forgetting place. Walk through the wall and do not stop, no matter what you hear.*

"What will I hear?"

*Regrets,* said the voice. *Everyone's regrets, hung in the mist like laundry. Pay them no attention. They are only words, and words are only wind—*

The voice stopped itself. Wren felt it gather the sentence back like a fisherman gathering line.

*Words are never only wind,* it corrected itself softly. *That is rather the point. Walk, little bird. And when you find the one who lives inside—when you find the keeper—you will know them by their eyes. They will look at you like a door they nailed shut.*

"A door?"

*You'll see.* A ripple of something that might have been amusement, or hunger; with the voice it was hard to tell where one ended. *Now listen. Before you go in, I will give you the words. The true words. You will not understand them, and that is good—understanding is a weight, and you must travel light. Just carry them. Carry them the way you'd carry a coal in cupped hands, carefully, and give them only when I say.*

And the voice gave Wren the words.

They poured in like cold water, syllable by syllable, and each one settled somewhere behind Wren's breastbone and glowed faintly, the way a swallowed ember might. Wren's mouth shaped them once, silently, testing—and the snow around them shivered, all at once, a thousand flakes reversing mid-fall for the space of a heartbeat.

*Not aloud,* the voice said sharply, and then, gentler: *Not yet. Soon. But not yet.*

"Why not yet?"

*Because the keeper must choose first,* the voice said. *That is the shape of the lock. It cannot be forced open from outside. Someone inside must turn the key—and the keeper is the key, little bird, the keeper has always been the key, though they do not remember it. I made sure they do not remember it.*

"You made them forget?"

*I made everyone forget,* the voice said, and there was such tenderness in it, such terrible tenderness, that Wren hugged their own arms and shivered though the voice meant it kindly. *Forgetting is mercy. Forgetting is how I love you. Now go. The mist is waiting, and so is your new home.*

Wren stood at the edge of the grey wall. Up close it moved—it breathed, slow tides rolling through it—and from somewhere deep inside came sounds: a crow, far off; a branch cracking under snow; and underneath, almost too low to catch, a human voice speaking words that curled and dissolved before they could mean anything.

*Do not listen,* the voice whispered. *Walk.*

Wren walked.

The mist took them the way water takes a dropped stone—no resistance, just a closing. Inside, the world went soft and soundless. Wren's own footsteps vanished. Their breath hung and refused to drift. And the regrets the voice had promised began, faintly, to murmur—not in Wren's ears but in the hollow behind the breastbone where the words sat glowing. Voices saying *I should have stayed*, saying *forgive me*, saying a name over and over, a woman's name, *Maren, Maren, Maren*, until Wren wanted to cover their ears and remembered they couldn't, because the voices weren't coming from outside.

Then, abruptly, the murmurs stopped—as if the mist had noticed Wren and, finding nothing to regret, let them pass.

*Good little bird,* the voice breathed. *Straight on. The cabin is ahead. Smoke from the chimney. Go and knock, and when the door opens, say the first thing that comes to you. Do not think. Thinking is for later.*

Wren came out of the mist into falling snow and lamplight, and there was the cabin, low and dark against the white, and smoke rising from its chimney in a thin straight thread. Warmth was behind that door. Bread was behind that door, maybe, and a fire, and a person.

Wren knocked.

The door opened on a figure tall and lean and wrapped in shadow, face half-lit by the lamp behind them, eyes catching the light like a cat's—and looking down at Wren with an expression Wren recognized instantly, because the voice had described it exactly: like a door someone had nailed shut, hearing a knock.

*Say it,* the voice urged, trembling now, urgent, hungry, joyful all at once. *Say the words, little bird. Open your mouth and let me hear the world again.*

Wren opened their mouth. And the words came—not chosen, not understood, pouring out in that older tongue, syllables that made the lamp-flame stand perfectly still and made the tall figure in the doorway go white as the snow behind them—

—and Wren smiled up at the stranger, trusting completely, the way only the very young and the very lost can trust, and said in plain valley speech:

"I'm cold. Can I come in?"
