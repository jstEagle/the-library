## 4. What the Oath Remembers

The child slept in the loft for three days.

Astrid told themselves it was practical. The storm had not broken; the passes were white and closed; turning Wren out would be killing them by slower means than the cold. That was all. Mercy by arithmetic. Astrid had always been good at arithmetic—it was what mercenaries did instead of praying, in the years when prayer still worked.

But on the third evening, when Astrid climbed the ladder with a bowl of barley porridge sweetened with a spoonful of stolen honey, they found the child sitting up in the blankets, cross-legged, talking to the dark.

Not muttering. Not the shapeless babble of fever dreams. The words came out measured, rounded, each one placed like a stone in a wall. And though Astrid had never been taught the language—had sworn, some part of them whispered, that they never would be—they understood it.

That was the horror of it. Not that the words were strange, but that they weren't. They rose up in Astrid's chest already translated, already known, the way you know the taste of your own blood before you've ever bitten your tongue.

*Vael-ith, vael-ith, orum kaeth,* the child said to the rafters, patient as a bell counting hours. *Orum kaeth, il vael.*

Wake, wake, or I will grieve. Or I will grieve, and you will wake.

Astrid's hand closed around the ladder rail until the wood creaked.

"Where did you learn that?"

Wren turned. Their eyes in the lamplight were the color of thaw-water, clear and shallow-looking over something very deep. "It's not learned," they said, in the valley's plain trade-tongue, and then, puzzled at themselves: "I don't know how I know it. It's like knowing how to breathe. You don't learn breathing."

"Say nothing else." Astrid heard their own voice go flat and hard, the voice they'd used on green recruits a lifetime ago. "Not one word of it. Not in your sleep. Do you understand me?"

The child's face did something complicated—hurt folding down into obedience folding down into that terrible, unshakable trust. "You're frightened," Wren said. Not an accusation. An observation, gentle as a hand on a wound.

"I'm careful. There's a difference."

"There isn't," said Wren, with the serene cruelty of children, and lay back down, and was asleep within moments, breathing slow and even, while Astrid sat on the bottom rung of the ladder with the cooling bowl in their hands and felt the first hairline crack open across the sealed vault of their mind.

* * *

It came back the way water comes through a dam—not all at once, but in a thin bright thread that widened every hour, impossible to stop, impossible to look away from.

Astrid was mending a harness by the fire when the memory arrived whole, without warning, like a blade slipped between ribs.

Snow. A different snow, older, redder at the edges. The pass above Harrowgate, eleven years ago—or twelve, or twenty; the valley had sanded the numbers off Astrid's life like weather off old carving. The company had made its last stand there, thirty-one of them against the Choir's rearguard, and the snow had been red because everything else was.

They remembered Maren's arm hanging wrong. They remembered Doss laughing with half a face and still laughing. They remembered the captain—Sera Ilven, who had carried the company's standard since before Astrid could grow a beard—kneeling in the drifts with a spear through her, her blood freezing in little black stars on the crust, and her hand closing around Astrid's wrist with the strength of the dying.

"You swore," she said. "You swore you'd carry it if it came to this."

And Astrid—who had been young, who had been bleeding from a dozen cuts, who had loved these people with the dumb total love that soldiers are permitted and no one else—had knelt in the red snow and said yes.

Yes. In the First Tongue, because oaths in the trade-tongue were only words, and this had to be more than words. It had to be a door barred from the inside.

*Ith vael-orum,* Astrid had said, kneeling over the bodies of everyone they had ever fought beside. *Ith vael-orum, sel kaeth-im, sel kaeth-im vorath.*

Let him sleep. Let him sleep, and let me forget, and let my forgetting be the lock.

Because that was the price. That was the part the god himself had set into the terms, or perhaps the language had set it, or perhaps Sera Ilven had known and not said: an oath spoken in the First Tongue bound both ways. To seal Vael's silence, Astrid had to give up every memory that tied them to the world the god had touched—and the god had touched everything Astrid loved. Every face. Every name. Every grave.

The vow had taken the war, and the company, and the captain's hand on their wrist, and folded them all away behind a door that Astrid themselves had built and locked and then, mercifully, forgotten the location of.

Until now. Until a child walked out of the mountains speaking the language of locks.

Astrid looked down and found they'd pushed the awl through the harness leather and through the pad of their thumb without noticing. Blood welled, ordinary and red, absurdly mundane. They watched it fall onto the floorboards and thought, with a vertigo so complete it was almost peaceful: *I have done this before. I have bled on snow exactly like this, saying exactly those words, and I chose it. I chose it and then I paid myself in memories so I wouldn't have to live knowing I'd chosen it.*

Eleven years in the Valley of Hush. Eleven years of careful, contented nothing—the patrol along the mistline, the unnamed neighbors, the deliberate smallness of every day. Astrid had thought it was hiding.

It wasn't hiding. It was the inside of the lock. The valley wasn't where Astrid had fled to escape the oath. The valley was the shape the oath had left behind—a hollowed place, a negative space, a life defined entirely by what had been carved out of it. No names spoken aloud, because names might call things back. No oaths renewed, because the tongue of oaths lived just under Astrid's skin like a splinter working toward the heart. No leaving, because—

Because somewhere outside, the thing they had buried was still buried, and the seal was their own forgetting, and forgetting did not survive distance. Or attention. Or the sound of a child's voice saying *orum kaeth* to the rafters as if reciting a lullaby.

Astrid bandaged their thumb with hands that did not shake, because shaking was a luxury and they had never been able to afford luxuries, and went to stand beneath the loft ladder in the dark, listening to a child breathe.

* * *

Sleep did not come. Instead, more of it returned, in pieces now, each one arriving with the soft terrible precision of a debt collector.

The spire. They remembered the spire—white stone, older than any kingdom, standing alone on the moor above Harrowgate where the company had mustered for the last march. They remembered the Choir singing from its steps, hundreds of voices in perfect unison, and how the harmony had been beautiful the way a landslide is beautiful, and how Vael had moved through that harmony like light through glass, filling his faithful with certainty so complete it left no room for anything else. Not doubt. Not grief. Not choice.

They remembered why the company had fought. Not for coin—though coin had been promised and never paid. Because Vael's order meant tithes that starved whole valleys, meant children given to the choir schools and never returned, meant a world where every voice was a note and every note belonged to the hymn. Sera Ilven had lost a brother to the choir schools. Doss had lost a wife. The company had lost, between them, almost everything worth losing, and so they had marched on the spire in winter, thirty-one against a thousand singing voices.

And when the battle broke them—when it became clear that you could not kill a god by killing his singers—Sera Ilven had dragged herself back to the standing stone on the moor, the old one, the one that predated even the spire, and she had known what had to be done, because the dying always know.

"Not a prison," she had gasped, blood on her teeth. "Prisons break. A bargain. He agrees to sleep—but someone has to hold the terms. Someone has to *be* the terms. Swear it, Astrid. Swear it and forget it, and live somewhere quiet, and never, ever let anyone speak to you in the First Tongue."

"And if someone does?" Astrid had asked, young and stupid and already grieving.

"Then God help us," she'd said, and laughed, and died, and Astrid had knelt at the standing stone and begun to speak.

Now, decades later, in a dark kitchen in a valley that forgot on purpose, Astrid pressed their palms against their eyes until colors bloomed and whispered the question they had spent eleven years not asking:

*What wakes a sleeping god?*

And the answer rose unbidden, in the First Tongue, in a child's voice, in the cadence Wren had used toward the rafters:

*A vessel. A throat clean enough for the waking-words. One born after the sealing, unbaptized in the old faith, unclaimed by any choir.* The god's own terms, written into the bargain like a clause in a contract: *and should a vessel come, the seal shall know its name.*

The seal shall know its name.

Astrid's hands dropped. The fire had burned down to embers, and in the ember-light the kitchen looked suddenly unfamiliar—the copper pots, the scarred table, the window with its view of the mistline—all of it revealed as scenery, a stage set dressed for a play whose ending had been written before the curtain ever rose.

The child hadn't found the valley. The child had been *sent*. Called across the mountains in dead winter, guided by a voice only they could hear, delivered to the one door in the world behind which the seal itself was living under a false name and a false peace.

*You knew,* Astrid thought, not to the god, not yet—there wasn't rage enough assembled for that. To the captain, maybe. To the snow. *You knew this day would come, and you sent me ahead of it like a coin paid forward.*

Outside, the wind had changed. It came down off the peaks carrying a smell that didn't belong to winter—woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke something sweeter, incense-thin, like a hymn burned rather than sung.

Astrid went to the window. Along the far edge of the valley, where the mist stood its eternal watch, there was a flicker. Small. Orange. Gone.

Then again, closer.

Torches. Someone was walking the mistline with torches, which no one did, which *no one had ever done*, because the people of Hush did not go to the mist and the mist did not come to the people, and that was the whole covenant of the place.

Behind Astrid, on the loft, a small voice spoke in its sleep—two words, soft and clear, in the language of locks:

*Il vael. Il vael.*

And you will wake.

Astrid took down the sword from above the lintel, the one they had sworn never to need again, and began, with the calm of a person watching their own life burn from a great distance, to pack.
