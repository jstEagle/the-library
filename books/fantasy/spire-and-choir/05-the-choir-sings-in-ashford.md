## 5. The Choir Sings in Ashford

Wren counted the days by the frost flowers on the window.

Astrid had shown them how the cold made pictures on the glass overnight—ferns and feathers and spiraling stars, all of them white, all of them dying when the sun touched them. Wren liked to press a fingertip against one before it melted, to feel the little bite of it, to know that something beautiful could exist for only an hour and not be sad about it. The frost did not grieve. It simply was, and then was not, and the window remembered nothing.

Wren wished they could be like the frost sometimes. There were things behind their eyes that hurt when they looked at them directly—snow, a long walking, a voice that sang in a language made of falling—and so they had learned to look at those things sideways, the way you look at the sun. Astrid said forgetting was a kind of mercy. Wren wasn't sure they believed that. Forgetting felt less like mercy and more like a room with the furniture covered in white sheets. You knew the shapes were still there. You just couldn't sit down anymore.

It was the ninth day of the frost flowers when the travelers came through Ashford.

* * *

Ashford wasn't really a town. That's what Astrid had told them, back when Astrid still talked in full sentences instead of the short careful ones they used now, like a man crossing ice. Ashford was a crossroads with ambitions—a scatter of houses around a well, a shrine nobody tended, a tavern called the Drowned Bell where the smoke always smelled of wet wool and burnt onions. The valley people came there to trade turnips for salt and gossip for nothing, because gossip in the Valley of Hush was a currency everyone hoarded and no one spent.

No names spoken aloud. That was the rule Wren had learned first, before even *don't touch the mist*. People here didn't say each other's names; they said *the cooper* and *the widow on the hill* and *the one who keeps bees.* At first Wren thought it was a game, like the hiding games they half-remembered from somewhere cold. Then they saw how the words sat in people's mouths—careful as eggs—and understood it was not a game at all. It was armor. Names were things you could lose, like teeth, like fingers, like children who walked out of mountains and never walked back.

So Wren had been given no name in Ashford. They were *the foundling*, which was a name shaped like an empty bowl. Wren didn't mind. An empty bowl could be filled with anything.

On the morning the travelers came, Wren was sitting on the low wall outside the Drowned Bell, mending nothing, watching everything. This was their other job besides carrying water and not asking questions: watching. Astrid had taught them without teaching them, simply by doing it—the way Astrid's eyes went to the treeline every few breaths, the way they counted strangers twice, once with their eyes and once with their hands resting near the knife beneath their coat. Wren had copied this the way children copy breathing. They watched the road the way other children watched birds.

The road came down from the northern pass, gray and rutted, disappearing into the mistline far off where the world went soft and uncertain. Nothing ever came down that road in winter. In winter the valley tucked itself in like a fox in snow, and the only visitors were wind and hunger.

But today there were figures on the road. Three of them. Walking in step.

Wren sat up straighter.

They were dressed alike—that was the first strange thing, the thing that made the watching-part of Wren go taut as a snare-wire. Travelers wore whatever the road left them: patched coats, mismatched boots, colors faded into the general brownness of winter. But these three wore gray cloaks, all the same gray, the gray of ash after a fire has finished being angry, and beneath the cloaks their clothes were dark and plain and identical. Their hoods were up. Their faces, when Wren squinted against the glare off the snow, were smooth and calm as eggs.

And they were singing.

That was the second strange thing. Nobody sang on the road in winter. Singing took breath, and breath in this cold was money you spent carefully. But the three of them sang as they walked, low and steady, and the song carried across the frozen fields the way smoke carries, flat and unhurried, and it reached the wall where Wren sat and slid inside their ears and—

Wren's whole body went strange.

It wasn't the melody. The melody was pretty enough, simple as a lullaby, the kind of tune that gets stuck in your head like a burr. But underneath the melody, wound through it, was something else. The singers' voices bent certain syllables in ways that made Wren's teeth ache and the backs of their eyes glow warm, and those bent syllables were—

*Words.*

Not the valley words, the soft round ones people used for bread and snow and *mind your own hearth.* Other words. Old ones. Words that felt less like sounds and more like small doors, and behind each door something enormous was holding its breath.

Wren knew them.

Wren did not know how they knew them, or where, or when, but knowing rose up in them like water rising in a flooded cellar, silent and unstoppable, and Wren's mouth opened before their mind could catch it, and they whispered along with the distant singers, tasting the syllables:

*"Vael-en-dorath. Vael-en-sommar."*

The words came out of them shaped wrong—they could feel that, feel the way a key feels when it doesn't quite fit a lock—but the lock noticed anyway. Somewhere very far away and also very close, behind Wren's ribs, behind the world, something turned over in its sleep.

The frost flowers on the Drowned Bell's nearest window, all of them, every fern and feather and star, melted at once.

Wren clapped both hands over their mouth.

* * *

The travelers stopped at the well.

Wren stayed on the wall, hands still pressed over their lips, heart going like a rabbit's. They watched the way Astrid had taught them to watch—not staring, but letting the eyes rest sideways, gathering everything while seeming to gather nothing.

There were two men and a woman. Up close, their sameness was even stranger. Not twins—nothing so explainable. They moved like one creature wearing three bodies, turning their heads at the same moments, blinking at the same rhythm. The woman carried a satchel against her chest the way other people carried infants, one hand cupped protectively over it even as she worked the well-rope.

The man with the gray beard drew up a bucket of water, drank, and then did the strangest thing yet. He poured a measure of the water out onto the snow—wasted it, in winter, in the Valley of Hush where every drop was counted twice—and bowed his head over the dark stain it made.

"An offering," he said, to no one, to everyone. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was a voice like a warm room, like bread, like safety. "For the god who sleeps. May his dreams be sweet until we wake him."

The woman laughed softly, a sound full of fondness, as if he'd said something charming about a child. "He hears even here, brother. Even in the Hush."

"Especially here," said the third, the younger man, and his eyes swept the street once, quick and light as a bird's, and Wren felt that glance pass over them like a hand over a candle flame—close enough to scorch. "Places that hide are places that have something worth finding."

Wren became very interested in the fraying hem of their sleeve.

The tavern door opened and the Drowned Bell's keeper, a broad woman everyone called *the bell-ringer* though no one could remember why, came out with her arms crossed and her chin set in the particular way valley people set their chins at outsiders. "Road's bad past the mistline," she said. "You'll want to turn back north."

"We're not going past," the bearded man said pleasantly. "We're going through. A little way. We've heard there's good hunting in the high valleys."

"There's nothing in the high valleys."

"No," the man agreed, smiling. "Nothing at all."

Something passed between him and the bell-ringer then, some current Wren couldn't see but could feel the shape of, the way you can feel the shape of a fist through a blanket. The bell-ringer's jaw tightened. The man's smile didn't move at all.

Then the woman opened her satchel, and the world tilted sideways for Wren.

* * *

She brought out books.

Three of them, small and square, bound in gray leather too fine for the road, and she began handing them out to the curious children who had drifted near the way children drift toward anything new. Wren didn't move from the wall. Wren had gone entirely still, the stillness of a hare that has seen the hawk's shadow and knows that running is the same as confessing.

But the woman came to them anyway. Of course she did. She came to the child sitting alone on the wall with no name, because a child with no name belonged to no one, and things that belong to no one can be given things.

"For you, little one," she said, and her voice was honey poured over iron. She held out one of the books. "Songs for the waking world. The old faith returns, and with it, abundance. No more hungry winters. No more counting drops of water. Only order, and light, and plenty."

Wren should have taken it. Any child would take it. Refusing was a flag planted in the snow.

Wren took it.

The book was heavier than it looked. Cold, too, colder than the air, as if it had been kept somewhere winter never ended. The cover was stamped with a symbol Wren's eyes slid off of every time they tried to look at it directly—a tower, maybe, or a throat, or a flame seen through falling snow.

"Open it," the woman said gently. "Look at the songs. They're beautiful."

Wren opened it.

The pages were filled with notation—lines and marks, the music-writing Wren had seen once in another life they couldn't reach, in a chapel or a schoolroom, somewhere with candles. But this wasn't music-writing. Or rather, it was, the way a drowned person is still, technically, a person. Underneath the notes, threaded through the stems and beams and rests, were letters. Small, sharp, angular letters, and Wren's heart dropped through the floor of their chest, through the frozen ground, down and down, because they *knew* those letters.

They were First Tongue. Almost.

It was like seeing a face you loved rebuilt by someone who had only heard it described. Every letter was *nearly* right and therefore exactly wrong. The word for *light* spelled with a stroke missing, so that it meant *light-that-fails*. The word for *come* inverted, so that it meant *be dragged*. The word for *wake*—and Wren's finger trembled above it, not touching, never touching—was written correctly, perfectly, the only perfect word on the page, and around it the corrupted letters crowded like moths around the one true flame.

And Wren understood, in the way you understand things in dreams, completely and without evidence: these songs weren't prayers. They were instructions. A net woven out of almost-words, and every soul that sang them sincerely, believing, added a thread—and the threads were pulling toward something. Toward a standing stone. Toward a thirteenth moon. Toward a child who had walked out of the mountains alone.

Toward Wren.

"You like it?" the woman asked. Her eyes were gray and kind and bottomless.

"It's pretty," Wren said, which was the truth wearing a disguise.

"Sing it sometime," she said. "Singing is how we make the world ready. Every voice matters." She smiled, and her smile was warm as a hearth, and behind her the two men stood with their heads bowed toward the wasted water, and beyond them the mistline hung pale and silent over the fields, and Wren thought: *they don't know where it is yet. But they're singing the map.*

"Thank you," Wren whispered.

"Thank *him*," the woman said, tapping the book's cover, over the symbol Wren's eyes refused to hold. "When he wakes, thank him. He gave everything for us once. Soon we'll give it back."

* * *

The travelers did not stay the night. They filled their waterskins, bought hard cheese with coins that were too bright and too new, and took the road south along the mistline—not away from the valley, Wren noted, with the watcher's cold clarity, but *along* it, the way a hunter walks a fence line looking for the gap.

Their singing faded slowly, syllable by bent syllable, until the wind took it, and even after the wind took it Wren could still hear it, faintly, under their own heartbeat. *Vael-en-dorath. Vael-en-sommar.* Come, dragged. Light, failing. Wake.

Wake.

Wren sat on the wall until the sun went down and the frost flowers began again on the windows, and then they carried the gray book home through the blue dusk, holding it away from their body like something dead.

Astrid met them at the door. Astrid always knew—Wren had never figured out how—when something had happened. They stood in the doorway with lamplight behind them, and their eyes went from Wren's face to the book and back, and Wren watched the color leave them like water leaving a sink.

"Where," Astrid said, and stopped, and started again, in the careful voice, the ice-crossing voice. "Who gave you that?"

"The singers." Wren held it out. "Astrid—I read it. I'm not supposed to know how, but I do. The words are wrong. All of them except one." Their voice went small. "Except *wake.*"

Astrid took the book the way you take a snake—firm, fast, at the neck of it. They opened it. Wren watched them read, watched their lips move around syllables they clearly wished they'd never learned, watched a muscle jump in their jaw like something trying to escape.

"Astrid?" Wren whispered. "What is it? What did I find?"

Astrid closed the book. For a long moment they stood in the doorway, and the lamplight made their shadow stretch long across the snow behind them, thin and wavering, like a person made of smoke deciding whether to stay solid.

"They're called the Choir," Astrid said finally. And then, quieter, in a voice Wren had never heard them use—a voice with graves in it:

"Wren. How many of them saw your face?"

Far off, past the fields, past the mistline, someone was singing.
