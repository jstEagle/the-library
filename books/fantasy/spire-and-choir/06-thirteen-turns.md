## 6. Thirteen Turns

The dog came first.

I heard it before I saw it—a thin, ragged barking from the direction of the mistline, the kind of sound an animal makes when it has run past exhaustion and is running still. The valley's dogs did not bark like that. The valley's dogs barked at foxes and at thunder and at the boy who stole eggs from the Kessler coop, and they stopped when you whistled. This was a different register entirely. This was a dog telling me that something wrong had crossed the border of my careful, quiet world.

I put down the knife I'd been using to split kindling and stood very still in the yard, listening the way I had learned to listen in another life, in another country, under another name I no longer owned.

Snow had come down heavy for three days and then quit all at once, the way winter does here—like a door closing—and the light off the drifts was so clean it hurt. Wren was inside, mending a sock with more enthusiasm than skill, humming something tuneless. I had left the door unlatched. I always left the door unlatched now, which was its own small surrender, though I told myself it was only because the latch stuck.

The dog broke from the tree line at the valley's western edge, a grey lurcher I knew by sight if not by name—in the Valley of Hush we did not speak names aloud, and after enough years of not speaking them, even the animals seemed to forget they had any. It ran straight at me, and only when it was close did I see why it had been barking: there was a man on its heels, half-crawling, dragging one leg through the snow, and he was wearing colors I had not seen in thirteen years.

Maren Voss. Sergeant of the Ninth Company. Dead these thirteen years, as far as the world knew, and dead as far as I had been permitted to know, which amounted to the same thing.

He fell twice between the fence and my door. Both times the dog circled him, frantic, and both times he got up again, and I watched this with my heart going slow and terrible in my chest, because I recognized the discipline of it. That was not a wounded man walking. That was a soldier refusing to die on open ground.

"Maren," I said, and the name came out of me like a splinter working loose, and the moment I said it I felt the valley shift around me—the faintest pressure change, like a held breath, like the mist itself turning its head.

No names spoken aloud. First rule. First rule of the valley, first rule of my survival, and I had broken it in the first three seconds because some buried part of me had seen him fall and refused to let him be *the man* or *the stranger* or anything less than what he was.

He looked up at me from the snow. His face was grey as the sky, and there was blood on his mouth, and he smiled anyway.

"Hello, Sterling," he said. "You look terrible."

"You look worse," I said, and went to carry him inside.

* * *

Wound fever has a smell. I had forgotten that, or made myself forget it, the way I had made myself forget most things: the sweet-rot stink of flesh going bad under bandages, the copper underneath, the tallow and smoke. I knelt by the hearth boiling water while Wren hovered at the edge of the room with wide eyes, and Maren lay on my bed—I gave him the bed; old habits, old hierarchies—and talked, because talking kept him above the waterline.

"The Choir found the archive at Karst," he said. "You remember Karst."

"I don't," I said, and it was true, and it wasn't. There was a shape where the memory should have been, the way there's a shape where a tooth was. "Tell me anyway."

"Monastery in the passes. Burned eleven years ago, but the Choir only finished translating what they pulled out of it last autumn." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought up blood, and I pressed him back down against the pillow with one hand while I cleaned the wound with the other. His side was a ruin—a spear thrust, angled up under the ribs, badly stitched somewhere on the road. Whoever had stitched it had worked fast and dirty, by firelight, with shaking hands. My hands, I noticed, were steady. Thirteen years of splitting kindling and hauling water and pretending to be a farmer, and my hands still remembered everything.

"They found the vessel's name," Maren said.

The water in the pot shivered. Or perhaps that was me.

"Not your name," he added quickly, watching my face. "Not yours. The child's. The one who walked out of the mountains in the dead of winter and vanished into the Hush like smoke." He turned his head on the pillow and looked at Wren, who had gone very still by the wall, the mended sock forgotten in their lap. "Hello, little bird. You've caused rather a lot of trouble."

"What do you mean, found the name?" I asked. My voice came out flat, which is what my voice does instead of shaking. "Names can be changed. Names can be buried."

"Not this one. Not spoken in the First Tongue." He closed his eyes. "Astrid. Listen to me. The Choir doesn't want the child dead. They never wanted the child dead. They want the child *crowned*. They've been singing the waking-liturgy since midwinter, every cantor in every chapterhouse, building toward—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Toward the thirteenth turn."

I sat back on my heels. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the grey lurcher was barking again at nothing, or at the mist, or at the thirteen years of silence pressing against the valley's borders.

"The moon completes its thirteenth turn at spring equinox," Maren said quietly. "You know what that means. You swore the oath. Some part of you knows what that means even if the rest of you burned it out of yourself."

And the terrible thing, the thing I will carry however many years are left to me, is that he was right. Something in me did know. When he said *thirteenth turn*, a cold moved through my chest like water finding a crack in a wall, and behind my eyes a picture tried to surface—stone, moonlight, voices raised together, my own voice among them—and then it was gone, sluiced away, leaving only the ache of its absence. The oath had taken my memories and left the wounds they'd carved. I lived in a house built out of holes.

"How long until equinox?" I asked.

"Nine days."

* * *

I worked on his wound in silence after that, and he let me, and Wren crept close and then closer, the way children do, until they were sitting cross-legged at the foot of the bed watching my hands work with an attention that was almost clinical. There was something in the way Wren watched suffering—not fear exactly, not fascination either. Recognition. As if pain were a language the child had been born fluent in and was only now hearing spoken by others.

Later, when Maren slept—or lay with his eyes shut, which for men like Maren amounts to the same prayer—I went out to the yard to think, and Wren followed me, and I did not send them back inside.

The mistline glowed faintly along the western ridges, the way it always did at dusk, a pale seam between the valley and the world. For thirteen years that seam had been a wall. Things crossed into the Hush and forgot why they had come. Magic could not follow over it; oaths could not reach across it; the god's silence pooled in the valley like deep water, and anything that swam in it drowned gently and went home. That was the bargain. That was the whole architecture of my survival. I had hidden inside the wound I myself had made, the way a thief hides in the vault.

"Wren," I said. "When the travelers came through last month. The ones with the songbooks."

The child looked up at me. Snow-light and firelight, and those eyes too old for the face they lived in.

"You recognized the notation," I said. "In the hymnals. Tell me again what you saw."

Wren hugged their knees. "It was our words," they said. "But spelled wrong. Like when Tam writes letters backward. The shapes were almost right but they leaned the wrong way, and when they lean the wrong way they mean the opposite, or they mean nothing, or they mean—" The child frowned, hunting for it. "They mean *hungry*. Words that are hungry. They eat the meaning next to them."

"And the singers. The Choir. What were they singing?"

"That the sleeper should wake. That the thirteenth turn is the key turned thirteen times. That the vessel will open its mouth and the god will pour back into the world like—" Wren spread their small hands, searching the sky for the image. "Like water into a cracked cup. And everything the water touches belongs to it again."

I looked west, at the mist, and the mist looked back, and for the first time in thirteen years I thought I saw a thinness in it. A worn place. Perhaps it was imagination. Perhaps it was the same instinct that had made the dog bark at nothing all afternoon. But I thought of scouts' deaths tearing holes—I had seen the mist seal itself after intruders dissolved, always, always, a self-mending fabric—and I wondered how many times a fabric can mend before the weave gives out.

The Choir had been singing since midwinter. Every cantor, in every chapterhouse. Song was the oldest magic there was, older than oaths, older than names. And they were singing at a silence, and silences, however vast, are only walls, and walls can be sung down. Ask any siege engineer. Ask any widow.

"Are they coming here?" Wren asked.

"Yes," I said, because lying to the child had become impossible somewhere along the way, and I no longer examined why. "Some of them are already trying."

"The mist eats them."

"It does."

"But it's getting tired." Wren said it simply, without drama, the way children state the weather. "I can feel it. At night. It's like a blanket with a hole in it. Cold comes through."

I crouched down to the child's level. Up close, Wren smelled of woodsmoke and wool and something else underneath, something I had no honest name for except *the mountain*, except *before*. The voice that had guided them down out of the peaks was silent now—I had asked, more than once, carefully—but its fingerprints were all over the child. In the stillness. In the patience. In the way Wren waited for answers the way stone waits for rain.

"When the cold comes through," I said, "what do you feel? Exactly. Don't soften it."

Wren considered this with grave seriousness. "Like someone knocking," they said finally. "Very politely. From a long way away. Knocking and knocking. And it says, *let me in, let me in, I only want to see your face.*"

My mouth had gone dry. "And do you want to let it in?"

The child looked at me for a long moment, and whatever passed behind those eyes was older than the war, older than the oath, older than the god himself, maybe. Then Wren reached out and took my hand—my scarred, steady, murderous farmer's hand—and held it the way you hold a rope.

"No," the child said. "I want to stay here. I want to stay with you."

* * *

Maren's fever broke near midnight, or broke enough. I sat by the bed with a lamp turned low and listened to him breathe, and I did my accounting, because accounting was the one discipline the oath had never managed to burn out of me.

Nine days to equinox. The Choir singing in a hundred chapterhouses, wearing grooves in the world. The mist thinning. The vessel asleep under my own roof, trusting me absolutely, which was the most dangerous weapon anyone had ever pointed at my heart. And Maren dying by inches on my bed, having crawled out of the winter to bring me the only gift he had left: the truth, delivered early.

There was a version of me—the valley's version, the farmer's version—who would have packed at dawn. Take the child, take the dog, take the road south over the pass before the mist failed, find some other forgetting-place, some other valley, start the arithmetic of hiding all over again. It was what I had done before. It was the whole of my strategy for thirteen years, and it had worked, and working things should not be abandoned lightly.

But I sat there in the lamplight and I understood, with the cold clarity that comes only at the worst hours, that the arithmetic had changed. There was nowhere on this earth the Choir's song could not reach. There was no valley so deep that a god's faithful, armed with a true name and a liturgy and nine days of chanting, could not dig down to it. Running would not save Wren. Running would only decide where the war began.

And beneath that understanding, deeper, in the flooded cellar of myself where the oath had sealed everything I used to be, something stirred. When Maren had said *thirteenth turn*, something had knocked. Politely. From a long way away. *Let me in. I only want to show you your face.*

I had spent thirteen years believing my amnesia was the price of survival. Sitting there, listening to a dying man breathe, I began—for the first time—to wonder if it might have been the purpose. If the silence-oath had needed a keeper, and had hollowed me out to make one, and if everything I called my hidden life was really just the lock, holding.

Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and moved through the valley, and the mist along the western hills glowed and guttered like a lamp running low on oil.

Nine days.

Behind me, in the dark of the room, Wren spoke in their sleep—one word, soft and clear, in the First Tongue, a word I almost understood and had never been taught—and the lamp flame bent toward the sound like a listener leaning in.
