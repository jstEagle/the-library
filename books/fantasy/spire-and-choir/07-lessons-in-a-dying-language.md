## 7. Lessons in a Dying Language

The lessons began on the ninth day, though neither of us called them that at first.

It started the way most dangerous things start: small, domestic, almost accidental. Wren sat cross-legged on my floor by the hearth, turning one of my old mercenary tallies over in their small hands—a strip of leather scored with marks I no longer understood myself—and said a word. Just one word. *Sethren.* It came out of the child soft as breath, and the fire leaned toward them like a dog hearing its master's step.

I felt it in my teeth before I felt it anywhere else. A hum. An ache behind the eyes, deep as marrow.

And then—unbidden, unwelcome, sudden as a blade between ribs—I remembered rain.

Not valley rain, which falls gray and apologetic. Real rain, warm rain, the kind that hammers canvas and turns parade grounds to soup. I remembered standing in it, twenty years old maybe, laughing at something, someone's hand heavy on my shoulder. I couldn't see the face. But I remembered the weight of that hand, and the smell of wet leather and woodsmoke, and the taste of cheap wine shared from a dented cup.

I had not remembered tasting anything before the valley. That was the whole point of the valley.

"Sethren," Wren said again, curious now, watching me. "What is it? It means—warm? No. It means *together-warm*. Like when people are close in the cold."

I knew they were right. I knew it the way you know your own name, which is to say: I knew it, and I could not say how.

"Where did you learn that word?" My voice came out rougher than I intended.

"The voice gives them to me." Wren shrugged, the way children shrug off miracles. "But it drops them. Like I'm carrying water in my hands and it leaks out between my fingers. You have some of the spilled parts. I can tell. When I say them near you, you go all—" they waved a small hand at my face— "*leaky*."

I should have ended it there. I want that written down somewhere, if anything of me survives: I knew what was happening, or I guessed close enough, and I should have stopped it in that moment, sent the child out into the snow, sealed my door, gone back to my careful gray life of patrols and unspoken names.

Instead I heard myself say: "Say it again."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about a dying language: it does not die because the words rot. The words keep perfectly. They die because the people who carry them stop being able to afford them.

I learned this over the weeks that followed, in the blue hour before dawn, when the valley slept deepest and even the mist seemed to hold its breath. Those were our lessons. I chose the hour deliberately—if remembering was going to cost me, I would pay in private, where no one could watch Astrid Blackwood come apart and reassemble, come apart and reassemble, night after night like a tide against a seawall.

We worked from fragments. That was all either of us had. Wren carried words the way a storm cloud carries hail—hundreds of them, rattling loose, half-formed—and I carried, buried under twenty years of engineered silence, whatever the oath had left me. Between us we assembled the First Tongue the way beggars assemble a meal: scrap by scrap, each piece questionable, the whole of it barely enough to live on.

I taught with charcoal on slate, mostly. Writing the words was safe. It was speaking them that drew blood.

"*Vaelir,*" I wrote one night. "Do you know it?"

Wren's whole body went still in that uncanny way of theirs, the way that made them seem less like a child and more like a door something stood behind. "That's His name," they whispered. "The voice says it sometimes, but quiet, like it's afraid of waking Him up on accident."

"It's not His name exactly." I surprised myself with the correction. "It's—the calling of His name. The name is just Vael. *Vaelir* is what you do when you call Him. It's an action. In the First Tongue, names aren't things. They're doors."

I didn't know how I knew that. I felt the knowledge surface like a drowned body rising, bloated and pale, and with it came the memory: a chapel, or what passed for one in a marching camp—a tent with a standard planted in the center, and men kneeling around it, and a young officer with a burn-scarred jaw leading the call. *Vaelir. Vaelir.* Forty voices. The tent flaps straining inward though there was no wind.

My nose started bleeding at the second memory. I wiped it on my sleeve without breaking the lesson, because Wren was watching, and I did not want them to learn that this language frightened me.

They learned anyway. Children always know.

"You bleed when we do this," Wren said, not a question.

"I'm old. Old people leak."

"That's not why." Their eyes were too serious for their face. "The words are coming *out* of you. I can feel it. Like when I pull a thread on my sleeve and the whole hem starts walking."

"Wren."

"Yes?"

"Don't pull the hem."

* * *

But here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I will set down now only because these pages go nowhere and are read by no one: I kept teaching.

Every night. Even knowing. Even after I understood the shape of the bargain—that every word spoken truly cracked the seal a little wider, and through the crack poured pieces of Astrid Sterling, the person I had been before I paid everything I was for everyone else's sake, and that the payment collected interest, and the interest was blood.

Because—and this is the shameful part, the part I can barely write—it felt like *coming home*.

You cannot understand this unless you have been hollowed. Twenty years in the Valley of Hush is twenty years of being a shape with no contents. I ate. I patrolled. I mended my roof and counted my stores and watched the seasons turn with the mild disinterest of a hired hand. I was content, I told myself. Weary contentment, the kind you build on purpose, stone by deliberate stone, over the grave of everything you used to be.

Then Wren said *sethren*, and I remembered a hand on my shoulder in the rain, and I would have walked into fire for the next word.

That is what the godless years actually were, I think—not peace but starvation, so slow and so total that the starving forget food exists. And the First Tongue, that terrible binding tongue where every true word is a hook in the meat of the world, was the first meal I'd had in two decades. Each memory it returned was a kind of resurrection. Each one cost me a nosebleed, a migraine that split my skull like green wood, a night spent shaking on the cold floor while Wren slept and I cursed myself for weakness.

I kept teaching.

* * *

By midwinter's end, Wren could form sentences. Small ones. Clumsy ones, with the grammar wrong in ways that made me wince—but wrong in human ways, and I want to be clear about that distinction, because it turned out to matter more than anything else I ever taught anyone.

The First Tongue, properly spoken, does not permit error. That is its nature and its horror. A vow spoken in it is not a promise; it is a law enacted upon reality, and reality does not accept amendments. Every word must be perfect or the sentence fails entirely. There is no *almost* in the First Tongue. No *I meant well*. No mercy of intention.

Which is why, I began to suspect, the voice inside Wren spoke to them in fragments. Why it leaked. Why the child carried hundreds of words and could hold none of them steady. Something in Wren resisted the language's perfection—not stubbornly, not consciously, but the way water resists a fist. Humanly.

"Try it again," I said one night. "The sentence. Slowly."

"*Thera vaelin su-merath,*" Wren recited, tongue stumbling on the third word. "The light remembers the—hand? What's *su-merath*?"

"Open hand. The hand that gives rather than holds." I wrote it out. "Your accent is atrocious."

"What's 'atrocious'?"

"Terrible beyond redemption."

"Oh." Wren considered this. "Like the Choir?"

I laughed before I could stop myself—a real laugh, startled out of me, the first in longer than I could measure. It hurt my ribs. It hurt other places too, places I hadn't used in years.

"No," I said. "Not like the Choir. Nothing is like the Choir. Again. From the top."

They went again. And on the third word—the same stumbling word, *su-merath*—they got it right. Perfectly right, pitch and weight and meaning aligned the way the language demanded, and the hearthfire stood straight up like a spear, and every hair on my arms lifted, and the memory that came through me was not rain this time but faces.

A dozen faces around a table. Cards and candle smoke. Someone cheating outrageously and getting away with it. Someone singing badly. Someone—Maren, younger, her hair still dark, her laugh still whole—raising a cup and saying my name.

*Astrid.*

She said it twice. The second time softer. And I understood, sitting there with blood running freely over my lip and dripping onto the slate, that I had just been given back my own name, and that I had not known I'd lost it.

Wren grabbed my wrist. "Stop. Stop, you're—"

"I'm fine." I wasn't. The room was tilting in slow, gentle arcs. "Lesson's over. Bed."

"Astrid?" They tried the name themselves, tasting it. "Is that yours?"

"It was." I pressed my sleeve to my nose. "Go to sleep, little bird."

* * *

There was one more thing, and I found it out the worst way: by accident, at the bottom of a memory I hadn't asked for.

It was near the turn toward spring. Wren had caught a fever—nothing serious, a child's fever, but it kept them abed and restless, and they begged for a story instead of a lesson. So I gave them one, or the bones of one, and somewhere in the telling I used a phrase I didn't know I knew: *thera vaelin sumerath*, the light remembers the open hand. I said it gently, story-soft, imperfectly, the way you'd sing a lullaby.

Nothing happened. No fire standing to attention. No blood.

And I sat there in the dark beside a sleeping, feverish child and understood, with the cold patience of a soldier reading a battlefield, what that meant.

Imperfect speech was safe. Imperfect speech was *only speech*—sound and meaning and love, with no hooks in it, no law, no power except the ordinary power of one person talking to another. The language only bound when it was flawless. Which meant the god's whole architecture—every oath, every seal, every silence including mine—was built on a foundation of perfection that human mouths were never meant to sustain.

Which meant the seal on Vael was not unbreakable.

Which meant my oath was not permanent.

Which meant that every lesson, every fragment, every word Wren and I assembled together in the blue hours was mortar pulled from a wall that was already leaning—and that somewhere, on the far side of the mountains, other people were pulling at the same wall with both hands, and they were not doing it for love.

I looked at Wren's flushed face in the ember-light. Small. Fever-bright. Trusting me absolutely, the way they trusted everything, which was the most frightening thing about them.

"They're going to come for you," I said quietly, to the sleeping child, to the empty room, to the god who had gone quiet rather than watch what his faithful became. "And I don't know yet whether I'm building you a weapon or a shield. God forgive me. I don't know."

The embers settled. Somewhere far off, past the mistline, past the mountains, I thought I heard—though I told myself I imagined it—a sound like many voices, very faint, very far away, finding a note and holding it.

Holding it perfectly.

I put another log on the fire and sat up until dawn, memorizing the shape of the child's breathing, and began, for the first time in twenty years, to plan for war.
