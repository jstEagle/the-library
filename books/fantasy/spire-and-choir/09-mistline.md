## 9. Mistline

The mist had a sound.

Most people never heard it. They saw it—white and endless, rolling down the mountain shoulders like something poured—and they assumed silence, because that was the valley's whole promise: hush, forgetfulness, the soft erasure of everything loud in the world. But Astrid had walked the mistline every morning for thirteen years, and they knew better. The mist hummed. A note so low it lived more in the teeth than the ear, a held breath of a sound, as if the valley itself were a throat deciding whether to swallow or sing.

Astrid had stopped asking which.

They walked the border at first light, as they always did, boots crunching on frost that would be gone by midmorning. The ritual was older than their memory of inventing it—thirteen years of the same path, the same stones, the same counting. Seven cairns marked the mistline where it bent around the eastern fields. Astrid touched each one in passing, palm flat against cold rock, an act with no meaning left attached to it. Perhaps there had once been words to say over them. If so, those words were gone, scooped out of them like pulp from a fruit, and what remained was only the gesture: touch, walk on, touch again.

Behind them, smoke rose from the cottage chimney. Wren was awake. Astrid could feel it the way you feel weather coming—not knowledge exactly, but pressure. The child had been sleeping badly for three nights now, murmuring in that language that made the air go strange, and each morning the frost around the windows had melted into patterns too deliberate to be accidental. Spirals. Circles within circles. Once, unmistakably, an eye.

Maren's warning sat in Astrid's chest like a swallowed stone. *The thirteenth turn ends at equinox. They know the vessel lives.*

*Run*, Maren had said. *Take the child and run while the mist still hides you.*

But run where? That was the question Astrid turned over now, walking the line, touching the cairns, listening to the hum. The world outside the valley was a map of wounds. Astrid remembered none of it personally—the wars, the famine years, the godless decades Ilse's Choir sang about—but the body remembered what the mind had been made to surrender. When Astrid thought of open country, their shoulders tightened. When they imagined cities, their hand drifted to the knife at their hip without instruction. Whatever life they'd lived before the vow, it had taught them this much: there was no running far enough from a thing that lived inside a person. And whatever Wren carried, it lived inside the child like a second heartbeat.

So Astrid walked, and counted, and did not decide.

The seventh cairn stood where the treeline broke against the high pasture, and it was there, on a morning gray as unpolished silver, that Astrid saw the figures in the mist.

Three of them. Distant still—a hundred paces, maybe more, shapes without detail, the way things look through falling snow. But they were moving wrong. Everything that belonged outside the mist moved slowly when it approached the valley, hesitating at the border the way animals hesitate at deep water. These three came on at a march. Steady. Deliberate. Shoulders squared against the white.

Choir scouts. It had to be. No shepherd wandered into the Valley of Hush; no pilgrim stumbled across its borders by accident. The mist did not permit accidents. For thirteen years it had turned back the lost, the curious, the desperate, folding them gently around and sending them home with hours missing and no memory of why their feet ached. The mist was not a wall. It was a courtesy. And courtesies, Astrid understood with a cold settling in the gut, could be refused by anyone willing to pay the cost of refusal.

These three were willing.

Astrid dropped behind the cairn, low, knife already loose in its sheath, though the blade felt absurd. What did steel matter against people who could not be fought, only mourned? The figures resolved as they came closer: gray robes, travel-stained, hoods back despite the cold. Young. All three young—the eldest perhaps twenty-five, the youngest barely older than Wren would be in ten years. The girl in front carried a hymnal bound in ash-wood, clutching it to her chest the way children carry talismans against nightmares.

She was singing.

That was the worst of it. She was singing as she walked into the mist, some thin thread of melody in the corrupted notation Maren had described, notes bent toward a language they were never meant to carry. And the mist let her. Astrid watched the white part before her—not opening, exactly, but thinning, the way flesh thins over a wound. The hymn was doing something to the border. Unpicking it. Stitch by stitch, note by note, the song was teaching the mist to forget itself.

*No names spoken aloud,* Astrid thought, pressing flat against the frozen stone. *No oaths renewed. No leaving.* The old rules, the survival rules. There had never been a rule for this, because in thirteen years no one had ever chosen to come in.

The girl crossed the line first.

For a moment nothing happened. She stood one stride inside the valley, singing, her breath pluming white, and Astrid let hope rise stupid and brief—maybe the mist would take her anyway, maybe the hymn was only a key to the door and not to what waited behind it—

Then the mist took her.

It did not turn her around. It did not fold her gently back the way it had turned back lost travelers for thirteen years. It simply closed, the way water closes over a stone, and where the girl had been there was a sound Astrid had heard before and hoped never to hear again: a wet, collapsing sigh, the noise a body makes when it stops being a body. The hymnal hit the ground. The song stopped.

The two behind her did not falter. That was the horror of it—they did not falter. The boy stepped over the place where she had been, still marching, his own voice rising to fill the gap in the melody, and the second girl followed him, and the mist closed over him the same way, and then over her, and then there was silence except for the hum, which had changed pitch. Higher now. Strained. Like a throat holding a note too long.

And where the three had entered, the mist did not knit closed.

Astrid stayed behind the cairn a long time, watching the hole. It was small—a gap the width of a doorway, maybe less, hanging in the white like a tear in cloth. Through it, faintly, Astrid could see the mountains beyond the valley: real mountains, sharp-edged, indifferent, wearing snow like armor. The world outside. The world Astrid had spent thirteen years pretending did not exist.

The hole did not close. The mist pressed at its edges and failed, the way fingers fail against a wound that wants to gape. Somewhere in the fabric of the sanctuary, three deaths had been paid, and the price had bought a door.

Astrid made themselves stand up. Made themselves walk to the hymnal lying in the frost. It was warm—body-warm, though its owner had been dead for minutes—and the ash-wood cover was carved with the same spirals that melted into Wren's window-frost, the same concentric rings, the same eye. Inside, the notation crawled across the pages in ink that shifted color when Astrid wasn't looking directly at it. Corrupted First Tongue. Maren had said the word *corrupted* like it was a comfort, like corruption meant weakness. Looking at the pages now, Astrid understood the opposite. The Choir hadn't broken the language. They'd broken it *open*. They'd taken a tongue in which oaths were literally binding and hammered it into a shape that could bind anything—mist, borders, the patience of gods.

The last page bore a single line in a careful, clerkish hand:

*The vessel is found. The thirteenth turn wanes. Sing until the way is open, and count no cost.*

Count no cost. Astrid looked back at the three shallow depressions in the frost where bodies should have been and found nothing, not even ash. The mist had taken even that. Count no cost—except the cost was being counted somewhere else, in a ledger Astrid couldn't see, and the balance was tipping toward the valley with every hymn the Choir taught its children to sing.

Astrid burned the hymnal in the cottage stove that evening, and Wren watched the pages curl with an expression no child should own: recognition, and grief, and a terrible kind of homesickness.

"They were singing to me," Wren said. "Weren't they."

"Yes."

"It hurt them." Not a question. The child's hands were folded in their lap, small and still. "The song. It hurt them to carry it."

"The song killed them," Astrid said, because lying to Wren had become impossible sometime in the past month, and because the child deserved the weight of it. "They knew it would. They came anyway."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved on their face. "The voice says they'll keep coming," the child said finally. "It says they'll come until the way stays open. It says—" Wren's brow furrowed, listening to something Astrid could not hear, and their voice went distant, reciting. "*The mist is a mercy, and mercies end.*"

"The voice says a lot of things." Astrid kept their tone level. Thirteen years of practice, keeping tone level. "Do you believe it?"

"I believe it doesn't lie." Wren looked up, and their eyes caught the firelight wrong, twin embers in a pale face. "It just doesn't tell you what the truth costs."

*No,* Astrid thought. *It never does. That's how you know it's divine.*

* * *

Maren arrived two days later, limping, with snow in her hair and blood freezing black along her left sleeve, and she did not bother with greetings.

"Scouts went in at the eastern line four days ago," she said, before Astrid had finished barring the door behind her. "Three of them. We found their tracks going in and nothing coming out. Cantor Ilse declared them martyrs at dawn and sang the Requiem of Opening over their names." She laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "Thirteen years I told myself the valley would hold. Thirteen years I told myself you'd both died quiet and lucky."

"You tracked me?"

"I've always known where you were." Maren lowered herself into the chair by the fire like a woman twice her age, and Astrid saw properly, in the firelight, how bad the wound was—a slash from shoulder to elbow, bound with field dressings gone stiff and brown. "You think the oath erased everyone's memory but mine? Someone had to remember where the seal-bearer slept. Someone had to watch. That was my penance. Watching you plant cabbages." Her mouth twisted. "I used to pray you'd gotten ugly. You didn't even do that."

"Who else knows?"

"About the valley? The Choir's inner circle, now. About you?" Maren's eyes went to the loft ladder, where small feet padded overhead, deliberately loud. "Ilse has suspected since Ashford. The hymns have been pulling north for a month like iron filings to a lodestone. She doesn't need your name, Astrid. She needs the vessel, and the vessel is a lit candle in a dark room."

Above them, Wren descended the ladder with the exaggerated care of a child pretending not to eavesdrop, and Maren's face did something complicated—grief and tenderness and guilt all fighting for the same ground.

"This is Wren," Astrid said. "Wren, this is Maren. She's—" *Friend* seemed insufficient. *Comrade* belonged to a life Astrid couldn't remember. "She's ours," Astrid finished, and was surprised to find it true.

Maren studied the child for a long moment. Whatever she saw, it made her jaw tighten. "The mist is failing," she said. "You know that. The hole at the eastern line—it hasn't closed. My people have watched it for four days. It's widening. Slowly, but it's widening, and every scout they send through buys another arm's length of open road." She leaned forward. "Ilse doesn't need to breach the valley anymore. She needs to wait. Equinox is nineteen days off. The thirteenth turn completes, the waking-window opens, and she'll bring the full Choir through a hole her martyrs already paid for. Hundreds of them. Thousands."

"So we run." Astrid heard their own voice say it, flat and practical, a soldier's voice from a life they couldn't recall. "Before the hole widens. Before equinox. Take Wren south through the low passes—"

"South is Ashford. West is the coast, and the coastal chapters sing Ilse's harmony. North—" Maren spread her wounded arm, encompassing the map of the world and its failures. "There is no south or west or north, Astrid. There's the valley, and there's the Choir, and the space between them is shrinking by the day. Running now means running forever, with the thing they want asleep in your arms and waking anyway. Every mile you buy, the voice in that child gets louder. You know it. You've heard the frost on the windows."

Silence. Fire crackle. Overhead, wind worried the thatch.

"Then we stand," Astrid said.

Maren blinked. "Stand. With what? You, me, a wounded arm, and a child who talks to gods in her sleep. Against the full Choir."

"With the mist," Astrid said, and the plan assembled itself as they spoke, cold and clear, the way plans had once assembled in a life they couldn't remember—piece by piece, cost by cost. "The mist isn't failing, Maren. It's bleeding. Three deaths tore one hole. Which means the mist can be torn, and anything that can be torn can be mended—or widened. The Choir thinks they're opening a door one body at a time. Fine. Let them. And when the door is wide enough, when Ilse walks her thousands through it in triumph—" Astrid fed another split log to the stove, and the flames leapt, and their shadow climbed the wall huge and wavering. "We'll be standing at the narrow place. Where the door is thinnest. Where one person with a knife and thirteen years of knowing every stone in this valley can do what armies can't."

"That's not a plan," Maren said quietly. "That's a siege. And sieges are won by whoever starves last. They have thousands. We have cabbages."

"We have something they don't." Astrid glanced at the loft, at the small shadow moving behind the railing, listening. "We have the reason they're coming. And reasons can choose whose side they're on."

"You'd put that on the child?" Maren's voice cracked sharp. "You'd ask—"

"I'd ask nothing." Astrid met her eyes, and held them. "But when the choice comes—and it will come, Maren, at the standing stone, at equinox, with the valley burning—I intend to be the one standing between Wren and everyone who wants to make that choice for them. Including the god. Including the voice. Including you."

Maren held the look for a long moment. Then she laughed again, that same hollow sound, and pressed her good hand over her eyes.

"Thirteen years," she said, "and you're still the same stubborn bastard I bled beside at—" She stopped. Frowned. Her hand came away from her face slowly. "At..."

"At what?" Astrid asked. And realized, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with the wound in Maren's arm or the hole in the mist, that they genuinely did not know—that somewhere behind Maren's eyes was a battle, a field, a name, a shared history, and that the same absence yawned in Astrid's own skull, smooth-walled and silent, shaped exactly like the missing piece of a life.

"At—" Maren tried again, and her face went strange, grief arriving without an address. "I don't remember," she whispered. "Gods' mercy, Astrid, I *remember you*, I remember your face, I remember that I'd follow you into a burning building, and I cannot remember *why*."

Outside, the wind rose. Far to the east, beyond the darkened fields, the mist hummed its strained and holding note, guarding a door that was wider tonight than it had been that morning, and would be wider tomorrow still.

Nineteen days, Astrid thought. Nineteen days to mend a sanctuary, arm a siege, and teach a child to choose.

Nineteen days, and the mist was already learning how to die.
