## 10. Wren's Choice

The voice came back on the night the snow turned to rain.

Wren knew it was coming the way they knew storms—first a pressure behind the eyes, then a taste like coins at the back of the throat, then the warmth. The warmth was the worst part. It felt like being held by someone enormous, someone whose arms went all the way around the world, and Wren had learned that the things which held you warmest were also the things that never let go.

They lay in their pallet by the hearth and listened to Astrid's breathing from the other room. Slow. Even. The breathing of a person who slept like a soldier even when they pretended not to—a person who slept with one hand curled near the knife under the blanket, though Wren was not supposed to know about the knife.

*Little bird,* said the voice.

It did not use words, exactly. It used something older than words, something that arrived already understood, the way you understand falling or hunger. But sometimes it borrowed words from Wren's own head, and those always sounded slightly wrong, like a song hummed by someone who had only heard it once.

*I'm sleeping,* Wren thought back.

*You are not.*

That was true. Wren hadn't slept properly in three nights. Not since Maren came bleeding through the door with her arm wrapped in dirty linen and her face gray as old ash, saying words like *thirteenth turn* and *equinox* and *they know.* Since then the house had smelled of boiled willow bark and fear, and Astrid had stopped humming while cooking, and the silence where the humming used to be was louder than any sound.

*The valley is failing,* the voice said. *The mist grows thin. You have seen it.*

Wren had. Two days ago they had walked to the well and looked up at the ridgeline and seen, for the first time since arriving, the shape of trees beyond the mist—sharp-edged, detailed, *near*, as if someone had wiped fog from a window. The valley had always been a bowl of softness, edges blurred, distances uncertain. Now it was becoming a place with borders, and places with borders could be crossed.

*The ones who hunt you are gathering,* the voice continued. *They wear white and carry books. They sing in a tongue broken into pieces, and broken songs cut worse than whole ones. In forty days they will come over the ridge, and this house will burn, and the woman who took you in will die in it.*

"Stop," Wren whispered aloud, into the dark.

But the voice didn't stop. It never stopped. That was the thing no one understood—not Astrid, who spoke about it in careful, distant words like a person describing an animal they'd only seen caged; not Maren, who spat when she talked about it. Everyone thought the voice was a stranger's voice. It wasn't. It was a voice that lived inside Wren's own skull, that knew the shape of every thought before the thought finished forming, and you could not slam a door on something that was already in the room.

*I can save them,* it said.

Wren pulled the blanket up to their chin. The fire had burned down to orange coals, and their breath made little ghosts in the cold air. "How?"

*You know how.*

And Wren did know. That was the terrible thing. They had known for weeks, ever since the night they'd woken screaming from a dream of a mountain opening its mouth, and the words had been sitting on their tongue ever since like stones they couldn't spit out. The waking-words. Nine of them. The voice had given them over slowly, patiently, one per night, the way you might feed crumbs to something small enough to trust you.

*Speak them,* the voice said, *and I will fold this valley in my hand. I will make the mist a wall of iron. I will turn the snow against the white-cloaks and the hymnals to ash in their packs. No harm will touch the woman. No harm will touch the scarred one. No harm will touch you. Speak, and everything you love will be safe forever.*

Forever. The voice liked that word. Wren had noticed that. It used *forever* the way other people used *please*—constantly, and without meaning anything by it.

Wren sat up.

The floorboards were cold through the thin blanket. Moonlight came through the shutter-cracks in thin silver lines, laying stripes across the floor like the bars of some enormous cage, and Wren thought: *that's what it would be. A cage with warm bars.*

Because here was what Wren knew, the thing they knew down in their bones, deeper than the nine words, deeper than the voice itself:

Astrid was afraid of it.

Not the way you're afraid of wolves or burning houses. Afraid the way you're afraid of water when you've almost drowned—afraid of the *loving* part of it, the way it holds you, the way it fills your mouth and lungs and doesn't leave room for anything else. Wren had watched Astrid flinch at the smallest fragments of First Tongue, watched them go white and silent when Wren hummed without thinking, and at first Wren had believed it was guilt. But it wasn't guilt. Or it wasn't only guilt.

It was memory. Astrid remembered what the god's love felt like, and they remembered what it cost, and their body remembered both even when their mind had been scooped clean.

*If I speak the words,* Wren thought carefully, *you'll wake up all the way. Won't you?*

Silence. The particular heavy silence of something deciding whether to lie.

*The world was better when I was awake,* the voice said at last. *There was order. There was plenty. There was purpose. The years since I slept have been years of starving and wandering and forgetting. You yourself—you walked out of the mountains with nothing, little bird. No name you can trust, no mother, no past. Who gave you that emptiness? The godless world did. I offer you fullness.*

"That's not answering," Wren whispered. "If I speak the words, you'll wake up all the way."

*Yes.*

"And you'll be loud again. Like before. Like in Maren's stories."

*Order requires volume.*

Wren hugged their knees. In the other room, Astrid shifted in sleep, murmured something too low to catch, and settled again. Wren listened to that small human sound and tried to imagine it replaced—tried to imagine Astrid's voice filled up with the god the way a cup is filled with water, until there was no cup anymore, only water.

Because that was the other thing Wren knew. The thing nobody had said out loud, maybe because none of the grown-ups could see it, or maybe because seeing it hurt too much:

The voice didn't want to save Astrid. The voice wanted to save *through* Astrid. It wanted Wren to speak, and it wanted Astrid to stand beside the standing stone when the words finished, because Astrid was the seal and the seal had to be present when it broke. And when it broke—

Maren had told the story twice now, both times with her good hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding something shut. How the company had stood in a ring on the equinox. How each of them had spoken a piece of the binding so that no single soul carried the whole sin. And how, at the end, one of them—the youngest, the one nobody suspected, the one who volunteered when everyone else was weeping or praying or both—had taken the entire remainder into themselves. Every word. Every name. Every memory of everyone they had ever loved, poured out like water to make room for the silence.

*She doesn't remember us,* Maren had said, staring into the fire. *That's the price she paid. She looks at me and sees a stranger with a familiar scar. Do you understand what that means, child? Every day she's spent with you, she's spent with someone she cannot remember loving—and she loves you anyway. Whatever's left of her, underneath the hole, chose you before she ever met you.*

Wren hadn't cried then. Wren was crying now, quietly, the way you cry when you don't want to wake anyone, tears running hot and fast into the corners of their mouth, tasting of salt and the coin-taste of the god.

Because if Wren spoke the waking-words, the seal would break. And when the seal broke, whatever was left of the bargain would come due. Maybe Astrid would die outright—the oath dying with its keeper, Maren had guessed, though she didn't know, nobody knew. Or maybe worse: maybe Astrid would live, awake and aware, and feel for one horrible moment everything they had traded away, every face and every name flooding back in a single instant before—

Before what? Nobody knew that either.

But the voice knew. The voice always knew things it pretended not to.

*If I speak the words,* Wren thought, *will Astrid survive it?*

The warmth pulsed. Gentle. Soothing. The warmth of a hand on a fevered forehead.

*Answer me,* Wren thought, harder, putting all their small weight behind it. *Will. Astrid. Survive.*

*All things survive me,* the voice said. *I am not death. I am order.*

"That's not answering," Wren said out loud, and their voice cracked on the last word, and somewhere in the walls a mouse went still.

The voice was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Wren began to hope—foolishly, the way children hope—that it had gone, that it would leave them alone tonight, that tomorrow would just be porridge and lessons and Astrid teaching them the shapes of letters in the ash at the hearth's edge.

Then, softly, with something almost like tenderness:

*Little bird. I have waited a generation in the dark. I have worn away mountains with my patience. I can wait for your answer too—but not long. The moon turns, and the hunters climb, and soon there will be nothing left to save. Choose quickly. Choose wisely. Choose me.*

*Choose me,* and beneath it, so faint Wren might have imagined it, something else: *choose me, or watch them burn.*

Wren got up.

Their feet were cold on the boards. They padded past the striped moonlight, past the coat rack where Astrid's sword hung wrapped in oilcloth, past the shelf with the three clay cups—one chipped, one Maren's, one that had belonged to nobody and had become Wren's by the simple fact of being set aside for them. Small mercies. Small belongings. The architecture of a life built in a hiding place.

They stood in Astrid's doorway.

Astrid slept on their back, one arm thrown over their eyes, the other hand—as Wren had known, as Wren was never supposed to know—curled loosely around the knife's grip. Even asleep, their brow was furrowed, as if sleep were a battle they fought nightly and only barely won. The firelight from the main room touched the edge of their jaw. There was a scar there, thin and old, running from ear to chin, and Wren knew—it knew, the voice knew, and it leaked into Wren whether Wren wanted it or not—that Astrid had gotten it the day the company died. Knew the mud of that field. Knew the smell of the blood freezing. Knew the exact weight of the dying man whose last words had been the first half of the oath.

Wren squeezed their eyes shut against the knowing.

"I'm not going to say them," they whispered.

Astrid didn't stir. The knife-hand stayed loose. Trusting, even in sleep, in a house that was about to stop being safe.

*Think carefully,* the voice said, and the warmth in it had gone cool at the edges, the way a hearth goes cool when the wind finds the chimney. *I am offering you everything. Safety. Wholeness. An end to running. You are a child with nothing. Where will you be without me?*

"Here," Wren said. "I'll be here."

*Here is burning.*

"Then I'll burn here."

The words came out steadier than Wren felt. Inside, they were shaking like a leaf in rain. But underneath the shaking was something hard and bright and small, a pebble of certainty at the bottom of a rushing stream, and Wren held onto it with both hands.

Because here was the thing the god couldn't understand, the thing that was too small and too human for something that big to see:

Astrid had forgotten everyone they ever loved. Their mother's face. Their comrades' laughter. The names of people who had died for them. All of it gone, traded away in the dark on a mountain, and Astrid had woken up empty and *stayed* anyway—had built a life out of the hole, had learned to smile at strangers, had opened their door to a half-frozen child speaking a language that should have made them run. Astrid had paid the whole price already and kept choosing kindness with what was left.

And the god wanted Wren to unmake that. To crack the seal and pour the war back in and call it salvation.

"You say you ask," Wren whispered. "You keep saying you'll ask permission now. Ask instead of command." They wiped their nose on their sleeve. "So I'm answering. No."

*Nine words,* the voice breathed. *Nine small sounds between you and a saved world.*

"No," Wren said again, louder, and heard Astrid stir behind them, and didn't turn around. "Find another bird."

The warmth withdrew—not gone, never gone, but retreating like a tide, and in its wake it left one sentence, quiet and cold and precise as a needle slipped between ribs:

*Then I will find another way. And when the white-cloaks come over the ridge, little bird, remember that you chose this fire.*

Then it was just Wren in the dark, shivering, crying without sound, standing guard over a sleeping stranger who had once loved them enough to forget everything else.

"Wren?"

Astrid's voice, rough with sleep. Wren turned. Astrid had pushed up on one elbow, hair wild, eyes finding them in the dark with that soldier's quickness, already reading the wet cheeks, the clenched fists, the stance of someone who has just survived something.

"Bad dream," Wren said.

It was the first lie Wren had ever told them. It sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

Astrid held out one arm—an awkward, gruff gesture, the kind of comfort offered by someone who had forgotten how comfort worked and was doing it anyway from somewhere deeper than memory—and Wren crossed the room and climbed onto the narrow bed and let themselves be held, cheek against the rough wool of Astrid's shirt, listening to the heart underneath beat its steady, mortal, forgetful rhythm.

Outside, far off past the failing mist, something sang.

It was very faint. Three notes, rising, repeated—the opening phrase of a hymn Wren had heard in the travelers' corrupted book, sung now from the dark side of the ridge by voices that had no business being that close, that many, that patient.

Astrid heard it too. Wren felt them go rigid, felt the arm tighten, felt the heartbeat speed beneath their ear.

"They're early," Astrid whispered, to no one, to the ceiling, to the dark.

Below the ridge, the singing swelled, and the first light of false dawn found the mistline, and the mist was thinner than it had ever been.
