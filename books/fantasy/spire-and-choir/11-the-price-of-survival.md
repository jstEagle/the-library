## 11. The Price of Survival

The standing stone had been in the valley longer than the valley had a name.

Astrid had walked past it for eleven years without once touching it. That was discipline. That was survival. The stone stood at the head of the eastern meadow where the mist thinned to gauze, a grey finger of older rock thrust up through the turf like something buried that had not finished dying. Lichen had taken it in slow green continents. Sheep grazed around its base and would not come within a stride of it, though nothing marked the boundary except the stone's own silence.

Tonight Astrid touched it.

The rock was colder than the night deserved. Not winter-cold—*vow*-cold, a cold that lived in the grain of the thing, the way a bell holds the shape of every note it has ever rung. Astrid pressed both palms flat against it and felt the old words stir beneath the skin of the world, faint as a pulse under bandages.

"You're sure this will work," Maren said behind her. She sat propped against the fence post she'd insisted on being carried to, her wounded side bound in linen gone rust-brown at the center. She'd lost blood and color both, but her eyes were still the sharp grey ones that had counted arrows across a dozen battlefields. "You're sure renewing it won't just—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. *Won't just kill you.*

"It held once," Astrid said. "It can hold again."

"It held once because you paid for it." Maren's voice cracked on the word *paid*, and Astrid saw her bite down on whatever came next—the whole truth of what the payment had been, which Maren remembered and Astrid did not, which was its own particular cruelty. Maren knew the shape of the hole in Astrid's life. Astrid only knew there was a hole.

"I'm paying again, then." Astrid turned. "Bring her."

Wren came across the meadow barefoot, because Wren was always barefoot now, no matter how many times Astrid set out boots. The child moved through the frost-silvered grass as if the cold were a rumor they'd declined to believe. Eleven years old, maybe twelve; hair the brown of river stones; eyes that caught light strangely, the way water catches the moon—not reflecting it so much as keeping a little of it.

Wren stopped at the edge of the stone's shadow and looked up at Astrid with that terrible, total trust.

"Is it time?" Wren asked. "For the quieting?"

"Yes, little bird."

"The voice doesn't want to be quieted." Wren said it simply, the way one reports weather. "But I told it no."

Astrid's chest did something complicated and painful. "You told a god no."

"He asks," Wren said. "So I can say no." A pause, and then, with the gentle precision children use when they are being kinder than they know: "He says he's sorry about your name."

Astrid did not ask what that meant. There were too many things Astrid did not ask about anymore; the questions stacked up inside like stones in a cairn, and someday, Astrid suspected, the cairn would fall.

* * *

They built the ritual from fragments, because that was all anyone had left.

Maren remembered the order of the words—she'd stood witness at the original swearing, though her memory of it came to her in pieces too, the vow having taken its tithe from everyone present. Astrid remembered the geometry: salt from the valley floor, ash from the hearth, water drawn at midnight from the spring that never froze. And Wren—Wren was the anchor, the living knot at the center of it, because a seal made on a vessel held twice as fast. The god could not wake through a door that was nailed shut from both sides.

They worked by lantern-light in the freezing dark. Astrid drew the circle in salt, wide enough for three, and set the four candles at the quarters—north for the oath, east for the debt, south for the silence, west for the price. Old liturgy. Older than the Choir, older than Vael's churches with their gold spires and their hymns that had once been commands. The candles' flames leaned toward the stone even though there was no wind.

"Say it plain before we start," Maren said. She'd gone very still, the stillness of a soldier who has decided how she wants to die if it comes to that. "What happens when you speak the renewal?"

"I re-bind the silence. The god stays sleeping past the thirteenth turn. The valley stays hidden. Wren stays free of him." Astrid kept her voice level. "And I keep my life, because the oath is anchored in Wren now instead of in me alone. Shared weight."

"And the cost?"

Astrid looked at the salt circle. At the candle flames bowing toward the stone like courtiers.

"The cost," Astrid said, "is that I have to remember everything I'm sealing away. All of it. Before the words take hold. The vow can't bury what isn't dug up first."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere out in the dark, a fox screamed, high and human-sounding, and fell silent.

"That's why you forgot in the first place," Maren said finally. "Not a side effect. The mechanism."

"Yes."

"You'll remember the company. The war. Him." Maren's jaw tightened. "Everything you spent eleven years not knowing."

"Everything," Astrid agreed. "And then the words close, and it goes under again, and I wake tomorrow not knowing I ever knew." Astrid met her old comrade's eyes. "That's the mercy of it, Maren. That's the design. I dig the grave and lie down in it and forget I own a shovel."

"And if the remembering breaks you before you finish speaking?"

"Then finish it for me."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them she nodded once, the way she used to nod before a breach—*go, I've got the wall*—and Astrid felt eleven years compress into that single motion until they were young again and stupid and certain, two soldiers in a company that no longer existed, about to do a thing that could not be undone.

"Wren." Astrid crouched to the child's height. "Once we start, you must not let go of my hand. No matter what I say. No matter what I look like. Do you understand? What you hold, the vow cannot take."

Wren considered this with grave attention. Then: "If I hold on, does it hurt less?"

Astrid's throat closed. "Yes," she managed. "I think it does."

"Then I won't let go." Wren took Astrid's hand in both of theirs, small fingers laced tight, warm as coals. "Even if you forget me. Even if I stop being real to you. I'll hold on until you're done."

*Even if I stop being real to you.* The child understood the arithmetic better than any adult in the circle. If the silence renewed, Wren would become, by morning, a stranger sleeping in Astrid's house—a foundling taken in out of charity, nothing more. Every lesson, every shared meal, every night of nightmares soothed and stories told: sealed, sunk, erased. Astrid would look at this child and see a duty, not a—

Not whatever it was. Astrid had not let herself name it. Naming things gave vows something to grip.

"Begin," Astrid said.

* * *

The words went into the dark like knives into deep water.

Astrid spoke the First Tongue as she had been taught it—correctly, precisely, each syllable landing with the weight of law—and with every phrase the stone drank the sound and gave something back. Memory came up out of her like blood from a reopened wound.

The first phrases brought faces. Not names—names stayed stubbornly below the surface—but faces, dozens of them, flickering behind her eyelids with every syllable: a woman laughing with a pipe clenched in her teeth; a boy of sixteen sharpening a blade wrong and being corrected, gently; a broad-shouldered man singing off-key around a fire while snow came down sideways. The Ironbound Company. *Her* company. She knew it the way you know your own hands.

"*Vael-ith sorren,*" she spoke—*let the silence be laid*—and the second layer opened.

Fire. A city burning, but not the enemy's city—theirs. The spire of the great temple coming down in a slow golden avalanche while people ran beneath it like ants under a collapsing loaf. She was running too. Someone had her by the arm, hauling her along, screaming her name—her true name, the one she hadn't heard in eleven years, and it was right there, right at the surface, a fish rising toward the ice—

"Hold," Maren said sharply, and Astrid realized she'd swayed, that Wren's grip was the only reason she was upright, that blood was running warm from her nose over her lips and dripping onto the salt, where it hissed.

"I'm holding." Her voice came out sanded raw.

"You're bleeding."

"I noticed." She wiped her face with her sleeve and kept speaking. "*Vael-ith korran, vael-ith domm.* Let the silence be laid, let the debt be carried."

The third layer was the worst, because the third layer was not images. It was feeling, undiluted, the way it had actually been—not memory's softened retelling but the raw event itself, poured directly into her nervous system.

Terror. The specific terror of the last night of the war, when Vael had stopped asking and started taking—when the faithful had marched on Ashford not as soldiers but as instruments, their eyes gone smooth and shining, singing as they killed, singing as they died, singing as they burned their own homes because the god's harmony required it and harmony was all. Astrid remembered standing on a ridge above the burning valley with forty survivors of the Ironbound and understanding that they could not win, could not flee, could not negotiate, because you cannot make terms with a voice that lives inside your skull.

She remembered proposing the alternative.

Oh, she remembered that. She remembered standing before the dying company—dying because Vael had begun reclaiming his oath-bound soldiers, heart by heart, and the Ironbound's oaths were the oldest and therefore the first claimed—and saying the words that would damn her: *There is a rite. It needs a volunteer. It needs someone bound to him already, someone whose oath is strong enough to hang the silence on. It will cost everything they are. Everything they remember being.*

And she remembered thirty faces turning toward her, and she remembered volunteering before anyone else could, because she was the strongest, because she was the most bound, because—because—

Because of a reason. There was a reason underneath, a reason that made volunteering not courage but penance or love or guilt, some root-system of motive spreading down into darkness, and the ritual wanted that too, the ritual demanded *everything*, and it pulled—

"No." Astrid wrenched against it like a swimmer fighting an undertow. Her knees hit the frozen ground. Wren went down with her, hands never loosening, small arms wrapped around Astrid's forearm now, cheek pressed to her knuckles. "No—not that part—I don't need that part—"

"Astrid." Maren's voice, very far away. "Astrid, breathe. Finish the line. *Korran*, the debt line, say the debt line—"

"*Vael-ith korran.*" She forced it out through her teeth. Blood filled her mouth now, copper-warm; she spat and kept speaking. The pulling eased, but it did not stop. It waited, patient as tide, at the edge of the light. *I will come back for you,* it seemed to say. *I am the price. You cannot pay me partially.*

The fourth quarter of the circle burned low. The candles had been tall when they started; the southern one was already a guttering thumb. Time was going strange, stretching and pooling. Astrid became aware that she was crying without any accompanying emotion, tears simply falling from her eyes the way rain falls from a cloud that has forgotten why.

"Almost done," Maren whispered. "Two lines left. Two lines and it closes."

Two lines. Two lines and the grave closes over it all—the company, the fire, the terror, the volunteering, the reason, the name. She would wake tomorrow whole and hollow, the way she'd woken for eleven years, content in her careful forgetting, and this night would join the others in the drowned dark.

Wren shifted against her arm. The child was looking up at her, face luminous in the last candlelight, and there was something in that face that stopped Astrid's breath—grief, yes, and fear, but underneath them a fierce, deliberate tenderness, the look of someone memorizing.

"What are you doing?" Astrid rasped.

"Remembering you," Wren said. "While you still do. In case it helps later. Someone should know what your face looks like when you're brave."

The last candle flared. The stone beneath Astrid's palm went from cold to *hungry*, drinking heat, drinking sound, drinking her.

"One line," Maren said. "Speak it and it's done."

Astrid opened her mouth to speak the closing words—*the silence be sealed, the sleeper stay sleeping*—and the undertow took her whole.

Not a fragment this time. Not a face or a feeling. The *reason*. The root-system, torn up entire, slamming into her consciousness with the force of a gate swinging open after eleven years:

She remembered why she had volunteered.

She remembered whose death she was paying for. Whose oath she had broken first, years before the war ended, in a moment of cowardice she had never confessed to anyone—how her broken word had given Vael his foothold in the company, how the fire in the temple city traced back to her, how every death since was a debt signed in her name. She remembered that the silence-oath was not sacrifice.

It was sentence. Self-imposed, self-served, and she had dressed it as heroism because the truth was unbearable, and now the truth was here, enormous, filling her skull like the god's own voice—

"—Astrid!" Maren was shouting. "The line! Speak the line or you drown in it!"

Blood poured freely from her nose. The salt circle steamed where it fell. Wren's hands were iron around hers, impossibly strong for such small fingers, and the child was speaking too—low, rapid, in the First Tongue, words Astrid had never taught: *hold, hold, I have you, hold*—and the stone waited, and the last candle bent nearly horizontal, and the closing words sat in Astrid's mouth like a stone she could neither swallow nor spit.

One line between the buried past and the buried future.

One line, and she would never again know what she had done.

Astrid Blackwood—whoever that woman had been, whoever she was about to stop being—looked down at the child anchoring her to the world, and began to speak.
