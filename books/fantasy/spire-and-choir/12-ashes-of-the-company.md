## 12. Ashes of the Company

The ritual broke me open like a dropped egg.

I had thought I understood what it would cost. I had stood in the circle of salt and ash with Wren's small hand pressed against my sternum, and I had told myself the price was knowable: a few faces, a few names, the warm weight of Maren's shoulder against mine on some forgotten night march. A tithe. A fair one.

I was wrong. The silence-oath had never been a lock on a door. It was a door bricked over, and behind the brick was not a room but a country—the whole remembered landscape of who I had been—and when Wren's imperfect syllables cracked the first seam in that wall, everything came through at once.

* * *

It began with rain.

Not valley rain—valley rain is soft, apologetic, a mist that never quite commits to falling. This was hard rain on canvas, drumming, and the smell of wet wool and horse sweat and woodsmoke, and I was twenty-six years old and my name was not Astrid Sterling but something older, something I had set down like a heavy pack at the end of a long road, and for a moment I could not remember what it was because I was too busy remembering everything else.

The company. *There* they were.

Thirteen of us around a fire under a black sky, the kind of sky you only get in the high passes where the stars come down close enough to cut yourself on. Brannick turning a spit with the patience of a man who believed all problems could be solved by rotation. Sefa braiding and unbraiding the same strand of hair, her tell, her tell for grief. Old Hollis asleep sitting up, as he always did, his sword across his knees even in company, even among friends—*especially* among friends, he'd say, because friends were the ones whose funerals would break you.

And Vael above us. Not silent then. Never silent then.

You have to understand what it was like, serving a god who spoke. People imagine it as thunder, as commandments carved in stone. It wasn't. It was a voice in your blood, low and constant, like a second heartbeat that occasionally offered an opinion. *March east.* *Spare the village on the hill; they will feed you.* *This man lies.* And we obeyed, because the voice was never wrong, and because obedience felt—gods forgive me, it *felt* like love. Like being held. Like being a note in a chord so vast you could live your whole life inside it and never hear its edges.

We were the Company of the Spire. We kept the high places. We killed what needed killing, which in those years meant the things that crawled out of the war-torn borderlands wearing the shapes of the dead, and sometimes it meant men, and I do not pretend we were clean. But we were held. Every one of us could feel the chord beneath our days, and none of us knew—we *could not have known*—what it cost to be a note.

* * *

The memory turned, the way memories do when you finally look at them straight, and showed me the part I had buried deepest.

The winter of the Red Tithe. The year the god's demands changed.

It started small. A village that had always been spared was suddenly to be burned, because its headman had doubted aloud. Then a tithe—not grain, not gold, but names. Vael wanted names spoken into the fire, the names of the faithless, and the fire would take them, and the people who loved those names would simply... forget them. Cleanly. Painlessly. *Mercifully*, the priests said. Grief was disorder, and Vael's whole nature was order. A world without grief. A world where every loss was sanded smooth before it could bleed.

Brannick refused first. You'd have expected it to be Hollis, old and flinty, but it was Brannick, gentle Brannick with his spit and his rotations, who looked at the priest's brazier and said, "No. My brother died at Cael Ford and I will not unlove him to please a god."

The fire took him that night. Not his body—his name. By morning none of us could say who had sat across from us at supper. There was a space by the fire shaped like a man, and thirteen bowls when there should have been twelve, and a grief in us with no object, like a toothache in a tooth that isn't there.

That was when Sefa found the texts. She was our reader, our scholar, the one who'd learned scraps of the First Tongue from a heretic in the southern ports. She came to us white-faced in the dark with a stolen hymnal open on her knees and she said the words I have spent a lifetime not remembering:

"It isn't a gift. The forgetting. He eats it. Grief is the price of the chord, and the chord is how he keeps his shape. He is starving the world to fatten himself."

Silence around the fire. Real silence, for the first time in any of our lives—because Vael heard everything spoken in his Tongue, and most things spoken in any other, and we had all learned to think in murmurs.

Then Hollis, awake now, sword across his knees, asked the only question that mattered: "Can he be quieted?"

Sefa's answer took three nights to assemble, pieced from heretic fragments and half-burned pages. Yes. Not slain—a god of that size could not be slain, only sealed. But the seal required an oath in the First Tongue, sworn at the standing stone where his voice entered the world, and the oath required an anchor: a living mind to hold the silence, to be the brick in the door. And the anchor could not merely witness the sealing. The anchor had to *carry* it. Everything the anchor loved, everything the anchor was, would go into the wall alongside the god—because a seal made of anything less than a whole life would crack in a season.

"Which is why," Sefa said, and her voice shook, "it has to be someone with nothing left to lose."

And I raised my hand.

I remember the raising of it. I remember the weight of my own arm, the way the firelight slid along my knuckles. I remember thinking—I can still feel the exact shape of the thought, thirty years old and fresh as a wound—*I have no children. No land. My mother is dead and my father was a rumor. What am I, but what the company made? And if the company ends, better that I end with it and something kinder survives.*

They argued. Of course they argued. Sefa wept, which she never did. Hollis put his hand on my shoulder and said, "You'll forget us," and I said, "Yes," and he said, "All of us?" and I said, "All of it," and he nodded slowly, the way soldiers nod at orders they hate, and that was the last real conversation of my old life.

But here is the thing I did not know until this moment, here in the wreck of my own skull with Wren's hand against my chest and my blood running warm down my lip:

I didn't just volunteer to carry the seal.

*I wrote it.*

Sefa gave me the fragments and I assembled them, because I had the best ear for the First Tongue in the company, because the heretic had liked me, because I was the obvious hand. Word by word, night by night, I built the vow that would eat my life. I chose the clauses. I chose the mercy-clause—*let the bearer keep their hands, their craft, their courage; let them keep the shape of skill without the story of it*—because I wanted, gods help me, I wanted to wake up as someone who could still swing a blade and walk a border and not spend eternity weeping over faces she couldn't name. I engineered my own amnesia the way a carpenter engineers a joint, and then I signed it in the oldest language there is, where every word is a nail.

And the last clause, the one I hid even from myself, folded so deep in the syntax that thirty years of silence couldn't surface it until now:

*When the vessel comes to speak the waking-words, the bearer shall remember all, that they may choose with open eyes.*

I built myself a key and forgot it in the lock.

* * *

I came back to the present on my knees in the ash-circle, blood on my chin, Wren's eyes enormous and frightened an inch from mine.

"You cried," Wren said. "While you were sleeping-with-your-eyes-open. You said names."

"How many." My voice sounded dredged up from a well.

"Eleven. Then you stopped."

Eleven. Thirteen of us, minus Brannick already eaten, minus me. Eleven names, and I could feel all eleven of them sitting in my mouth like stones I'd finally been allowed to set down. Sefa, who taught me letters. Hollis, who taught me when to kill and when to wait. Tam, who sang badly. Osk, who owed everyone money and repaid it in loyalty. The twins, Rill and Rass, who finished each other's sentences and each other's rations. Yeva, who kept the maps. Doss, who feared nothing except moths. Little Corvin, youngest of us, who called me *Ash* because I swept the campfires and because my true name had ash in it somewhere—

My true name.

I reached for it and found it, and it fit my mouth like a key fits a lock I'd been leaning against for thirty years, and I did not say it aloud. Some things you keep. Even now. Even knowing what keeping costs.

Wren was still watching me. "Was it bad? Remembering?"

I almost lied. The instinct was old and smooth with use. But the child had walked out of mountains for me, had bled salt and snow for me, had pressed their hand to my heart and paid for my past in their own small strength.

"Yes," I said. "And no. It was—" I searched for it. "It was finding out that the person I've been for thirty years is a room someone built to protect something. And the something is me. And the someone is also me. And I'm angry at both of us."

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. "That's like when I couldn't remember if the voice in the mountain was kind or just loud."

"Yes," I said. "Exactly like that."

Because that was the clarity that had come through the broken wall, cold and total as meltwater: I had not survived. I had been *spent*. Thirty years in the Valley of Hush, and I had called it peace, and it was not peace—it was the inside of a vault, comfortable as a tomb, and every soft gray day had been a coin from a purse I'd emptied before I ever arrived. The weary contentment I'd worn like a coat was tailor-made. I had stitched it myself, clause by clause, so that the anchor wouldn't chafe. So the brick wouldn't dream of being a door.

And the worst of it, the part that made me press my palms flat against the frozen ground and breathe until the shaking stopped: it had worked. The sealing had worked. Vael went quiet, the Choir scattered, the godless years came on—and the world did not end. Villages starved, yes. Faith curdled into Cantors and hymnals, yes. But no more tithes of names. No more fires that ate love. Brannick's grief stayed his own, wherever Brannick had gone. I had bought a generation of ordinary human sorrow, the kind you can carry, the kind that makes songs instead of ashes.

It was worth it. That was the devastating part. Not the sacrifice itself—I'd known about that since I was twenty-six—but the arithmetic. Standing here with my whole self restored, every name accounted for, every debt tallied, I could find no flaw in the young mercenary's math. Given the same fire, the same choice, I would raise my hand again.

Which meant the question was never whether the price was right. The question was whether I could bear to pay it twice.

* * *

Maren found me at dusk, sitting on the woodpile behind the house, watching the mistline glow the strange bruised orange it had glowed since the scouts dissolved.

"You're different," she said. She lowered herself onto the chopping block, wincing at her bandaged side. "Been trying to place it all day. You sit like someone now. Before, you sat like nobody."

"I remember," I said.

Her breath stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the eaves. Then, very carefully, the way you approach a wounded animal: "How much?"

"All of it. The company. The Red Tithe. The stone." I looked at her—at the girl she'd been and the woman she was, both of them overlaid now like two sketches on one page. "You argued loudest against letting me do it. You said you'd tie me to a tree first."

"I said I'd *try* to tie you to a tree." Her mouth twitched, then failed. Her eyes went bright and she stared hard at the woodpile. "Osk bet me a week's wages you'd still do it. I never collected."

"He'd have welched anyway. He owed everyone." I said it gently, and watched thirty years fall off her face, watched her become nineteen years old and grieving in a way she hadn't been permitted to grieve, because the valley forbade names and the oath forbade tears and the dead deserved better than anonymity.

"Ash," she whispered. Just that. Just the old name, the campfire name, and it went through me like a blade through wet canvas.

"Maren." I took her hand. Her fingers were cold. "Listen to me. The seal is cracking—you've seen the mist. And I remember the terms now, all of them, including the ones I hid. When the vessel speaks the waking-words, the bearer chooses with open eyes. Renew the silence, or let it break. There's no third door. I checked. I *wrote* it."

"And?"

"And renewing it means going back in the vault. All of this—" I gestured at the valley, the house, the garden rows sleeping under snow, Wren's mended coat drying by the fire inside, "—all of it becomes a room I built to protect something, again. Another thirty years of weary contentment, purchased with a child's whole life, because Wren stays anchored to me or dies with the seal. I'd be building the tomb around *them* this time."

Maren was quiet. Beyond her, far off at the valley's edge, the mist pulsed once, slow, like the throat of something swallowing.

"But if you don't renew it," she said, "Vael wakes. And the Choir gets their tyrant back, and the tithes start again, and everything you bought—"

"Unless the words are spoken wrong." I heard myself say it, and marveled at it, this plan that had been assembling itself in the wreckage of my memory like bones knitting. "Sefa's texts were clear: the First Tongue binds absolutely. Perfect speech, perfect power. But Wren doesn't speak it perfectly, Maren. Wren speaks it like a child—which means every waking-word comes out bent, human, *asking* instead of commanding. If the god wakes through a flawed mouth, maybe he wakes flawed. Maybe he wakes *smaller*. Kinder. Or at least negotiable."

"That's a hope, not a plan."

"It's the only one I've got. And there's a price on that road too. The broken oath collects." I flexed my fingers, feeling already, faintly, at the edges of them, a numbness like frost coming on. "It takes the bearer's memories of everyone they ever loved. Not sealed. *Gone.* Spent, the way I spent the first life. I'd stand in front of you and not know your name."

Maren looked at me for a long time. Wind moved the smoke from the chimney sideways. Somewhere inside, Wren was singing—badly, tunelessly, in a language older than sin—and the sound of it should have been eerie and was instead the most hopeful thing I'd heard in thirty years.

"Then we write everything down," Maren said at last, gruff, practical, her jaw set the way it used to set before a fight she intended to win ugly. "Every name. Every story. Tonight, while you still have them. And the girl learns them, and carries them, and if the day comes when you look at her like a stranger—" her voice cracked and she drove through it, "—she'll know you anyway. That's not nothing, Ash. That's a rope across a chasm."

"No," I said, and stood, and felt the old company rise in me like heat from coals—Hollis's patience, Sefa's scholarship, Brannick's gentleness, all of them alive again in my hands for whatever time remained. "It's better than a rope. It's a relay. Memory passed hand to hand so the darkness never gets all of it." I looked north, toward the pass, toward the faint smudge of torchlight that had no business being visible through the mist and was visible anyway, growing. "And we'd best work fast. Because the Choir isn't sending scouts anymore."

Maren followed my gaze. Her hand went to her sword.

"How many?" she asked.

I counted the lights, and my stomach went cold and calm, the way it always did before the worst.

"All of them," I said. "They're coming at equinox. And they're bringing the thirteenth turn with them."
